## 31. Uneasy Hands

The sentry's whistle came at dusk—two short notes and one long, the signal for *stranger approaching alone*—and by the time Oscar reached the edge of camp, every knife in Duskhollow's remnant crew was already out.

She counted them without meaning to. Brannoc at the fire with his hatchet across his knees, the empty socket where his eye had been turned toward the tree line like a compass finding north. Petra half-hidden behind the woodpile, a skinning blade held low against her thigh where it could do the most work. Cassian stood closest to the path, and though his hands were empty, Oscar knew the weight of the prayer-knife he'd carried since Winterhold, hidden in the small of his back like a sin he couldn't stop confessing.

And down the smuggler's trail, limping out of the blue shadow of the pines, came Inquisitor Seraphine Locke.

Oscar had seen her only once before—from across a burning square in Thornfell, gold-trimmed coat immaculate while a shrine crumbled to ash behind her. She had looked then like something cast in bronze, untouchable, certain. Now she looked like a woman who had walked out of her own funeral. Her coat was scorched along one sleeve. A brand seared into her cheek—the Church's mark of heresy, still raw at the edges, still weeping—had been dressed with rough linen that didn't match the fine cloth beneath. One hand pressed against her ribs as she walked. The other held up, palm out, empty.

In it, wrapped in oilcloth and chained to her wrist with a thin silver tether, something glowed faintly through the fabric. A light Oscar felt in her teeth before she saw it. The seal-fragment.

"Nobody moves," Brannoc said quietly. It wasn't a warning to the crew. It was a courtesy to the enemy—a last moment of ignorance before the knives found her.

Locke stopped ten paces from the firelight's edge, exactly far enough to show she understood the arithmetic. Ten paces meant even a sprinting woman died twice over before she closed the distance. She had chosen the ground the way she'd once chosen interrogation rooms: deliberately, with full knowledge of what happened next.

"Oscar Dravenmoor," she said.

The name landed in the camp like a dropped lantern. Cassian's breath went sharp. Petra's blade rose an inch. Wren, who should have been asleep in the supply tent, appeared at its flap with her blanket around her shoulders and her ember-brand glowing sullenly through the fabric, and Oscar thought, *of course*, because the child had developed an unerring instinct for the moments when the world might end.

"That name is dead," Oscar said. "You can use it or not. It won't answer."

"It answered me well enough in the Church archives." Locke's voice was steady, but Oscar heard the hairline cracks in it now, the places where certainty had burned away. "Three weeks ago I would have knelt on this ground and recited the Rite of Unmaking over your body. I want you to know that. I want you to know what you're deciding about when you decide whether to hear me."

"Then say what you came to say."

"I brought you the third piece of the covenant seal." Locke lifted her wrist; the oilcloth shifted, and the glow strengthened, throwing pale amber light across her branded face. "Stolen from the Reliquary under Vespergate. I burned my name off the Church rolls to carry it here. And I am standing in your knife-range asking permission to come closer." A pause. Her jaw tightened. "I don't ask permission. That's new. I'm told it's called humility. It tastes like ash."

Brannoc laughed once, harsh and humorless. "The Gilded Inquisitor learns humility. Next you'll tell me the Maw says its prayers."

"The Maw doesn't pray." For the first time, something moved behind Locke's eyes that wasn't calculation. "It eats. I've seen it eat. I watched it take Hallowmere in forty minutes, and I read the reports afterward that described it as a 'localized weather event.' Forty minutes, cartographer. Nine hundred souls. And my superiors filed it between a grain shortage and a border dispute." She let that sit in the cold air. "I have spent eleven years believing the Church held the line between the mortal world and the hunger behind it. Last month I learned the Church built the hunger. Or broke the thing that would have kept it fed. The rites that caused the Sundering are written in our own liturgy, in cipher, in the margins of the oldest psalters. Ilsa—" she caught herself, and Oscar saw the flicker of genuine grief cross the woman's face, quickly caged, "—Ilsa's body told us the same story. Your forger died holding a pass against men carrying orders signed by the Lord Vicar himself. Orders to erase a bloodline. *Your* bloodline."

"You expect grief from us," Cassian said. His voice was low, controlled, the seminary-trained baritone he used when he wanted to sound reasonable and rarely did by accident. "You come wearing her death on your conscience and a stolen relic on your wrist, and you expect a chair by the fire."

"No." Locke's gaze moved to him, and something passed between the two of them—two people who had both served the same god and been failed by Him, recognition without warmth. "I expect a knife. I'd deserve it. But I'd rather be useful first. Kill me after, if you must. But the fragment comes to the altar whether I do or not. Cut my hand off and take it. I've considered offering."

"Don't tempt them," Oscar said dryly, and heard Petra snort despite herself.

*

They did not give her a chair by the fire. They gave her a place at its outermost ring, on a flat stone, with Brannoc's hatchet within his reach and hers nowhere near anything. Mother Quill's absence sat in the middle of the camp like a cold draft no one acknowledged. They had buried her under a cairn on the ridge two days after Locke's interrogators finished with her, and the ground there was still disturbed, and everyone's eyes went to it once, involuntarily, when they looked at the woman who had put her there.

Oscar made herself look longer.

"She talked to you," Oscar said. "Before she died."

"Yes."

"What did she tell you?"

Locke met her eyes without flinching, which Oscar resented, because she wanted the woman to flinch. "Nothing. She quoted smuggling tariffs at me for four hours. Then she told me my mother would be ashamed of me—which was accurate, incidentally—and then she told me that mercy is a muscle and mine had never been exercised, and that she pitied me more than she hated me." Locke's hands were folded in her lap, and Oscar noticed they were trembling very slightly, and that the woman was letting them tremble rather than hiding it, and understood that this too was a kind of discipline. "Then she died. She never broke. I have broken harder people with less effort. I will remember her longer than any of them."

"She was teaching you," Wren said suddenly, from the tent flap. Everyone turned. The girl's voice was thin but certain. "Mother Quill. That's how she taught. She'd insult you until you got smarter just to argue back." Wren's chin lifted. "She was doing it right up to the end. You're the lesson."

The silence that followed was long and strange. Locke looked at the child—at the brand glowing faintly at her collar—and Oscar watched the Inquisitor's expression do something complicated: horror, recognition, guilt, and underneath all of it, a scholar's helpless fascination with the theology of the thing. Eleven years of hunting god-touched heretics, and here was one sitting in firelight, alive, marked by a god who had eaten a town, wearing her mark like a question no catechism answered.

"Get some sleep, Wren," Oscar said gently. "That's not yours to carry tonight."

Wren went, but slowly, watching Locke over her shoulder the whole way, the way children watch large animals.

*

Oscar did not sleep either. She took the fragment examination to the map table instead—her father's old campaign table, salvaged from the waystation's ashes, its surface scarred with candle burns and compass points—and unwrapped the oilcloth by lamplight while the camp pretended not to watch.

The seal-fragment was smaller than she'd imagined. A disc of dark metal, older than any kingdom she'd mapped, worked with a spiral of script that predated the Dravenmoor alphabet and yet was somehow legible to her, the way a river is legible to a fish. When her fingers touched it, the mountain hummed. Not sound—pressure. A pulse through bedrock, patient and vast, like a heart beating somewhere below the roots of Thornfell.

Beside her, uninvited, Locke set down the second piece—the one the Church had held for three centuries, pried from the original altar site and locked in the Reliquary like a tooth pulled from a saint's skull.

When the two fragments came within a handspan of each other, they both began to glow, and the script crawled, and Oscar felt the mountain's heartbeat quicken.

"They fit," Locke said quietly. "All three. Yours—" she nodded at the journal open on the table, at the diagrams Oscar had spent a week deciphering, "—the heir carries the third. It's not a relic. It's blood. The seal was always three parts: stone, stone, and keeper. That's why the binding thinned. Two stones in boxes, and the keeper's line scattered and hiding." She paused. "Hiding so well that even I believed the Dravenmoors extinct. My instructors taught your family's extinction as a triumph. The final victory of the Covenant Purges." Her mouth twisted. "We celebrated it. There's a feast day."

"There's a *feast day*," Oscar repeated flatly.

"The Feast of the Quiet Sky." Locke said it like a confession. "I danced at it as a girl."

Oscar looked at the woman—at the heretic's brand on her cheek, the stolen relic on her wrist, the eleven years of faith lying around her like the wreckage of a ship—and felt the anger in her chest do something she hadn't authorized. It softened. Not into forgiveness. Into something colder and more useful: understanding. She hated that she understood. Understanding was the first step down a road she wasn't sure she wanted to walk with this woman.

"Here's how this works," Oscar said. She kept her voice level, the cartographer's voice, the one she used for negotiating with warlords and ferry-things. "You eat at the fire's edge. You sleep where Brannoc can see you. You touch nothing without asking, you go nowhere alone, and if the Church sends word that this defection is theater—if there's a single hidden sigil, a single coded phrase in anything you carry—I will know. I've spent ten years reading what people hide in maps and ledgers and letters. You are, functionally, a document now, Inquisitor. And I am very good at reading documents."

"And if I pass your reading?"

"Then you get to keep helping. Until the solstice. After the solstice—" Oscar met her eyes. "After the solstice, we'll see what's left to forgive. Maybe nothing. Maybe everything. I genuinely don't know. Forgiveness isn't a rite I can perform on schedule."

"That," Locke said, "is the first honest theological statement anyone has made to me in eleven years."

From the darkness beyond the fire, Cassian's voice: "Don't encourage her."

*

He found Oscar later at the map table, long after the camp had banked its fires and the fragment had been wrapped and stowed in separate halves—Locke's piece with Brannoc, the journal with Oscar, so that no single betrayal could gather the whole.

Cassian lowered himself onto the bench across from her, wincing as his bandaged shoulder reminded him of the Weeping Stair. The wound was healing clean, but slowly, and he favored it the way he favored all his damage: silently, resentfully, as though his own body were an unreliable subordinate.

"You're going to let her stay," he said.

"I already did."

"I heard. I was hoping you'd come to your senses overnight." He rubbed his eyes. "Oscar. She interrogated Quill to death. She burned shrines. She hunted you by name. If she's genuine, she's a monster having a crisis. If she's not genuine, she's a monster doing her job. Either way—"

"Either way, the fragment is essential, and you know it." Oscar turned the journal toward him, to the diagram of the Ember Altar's mechanism, the three-part lock drawn in her father's careful hand. "Two stones and a keeper. Without her piece, the rite fails. Without the rite, the veil tears, the Maw comes through whole, and everything we've lost—Quill, Ilsa, Brannoc's ship, your shoulder—we've lost for nothing." She leaned back. "I don't trust her, Cassian. I want to be clear about that, because I think you think I've gone soft. I haven't. Trust is a road I build one stone at a time, and she gets one stone, and she has to stand on it and ask nicely for the next. That's not softness. That's engineering."

Something shifted in his expression—not agreement, exactly, but the grudging respect of a man watching someone else hold a line he'd have held differently. "One stone," he said finally. "And if she steps off it?"

"Then the mountain's full of deep holes, and I know where every one of them is." Oscar smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "I told you. Ten years of mapping. Nobody disappears better than a cartographer."

Cassian huffed—almost a laugh—and reached across the table to cover her hand with his. His fingers were warm, ink-stained, familiar. "You know what frightens me? Not her. You. You've started sounding like someone who expects to survive this."

"Is that bad?"

"It's terrifying." He squeezed her hand. "Surviving means there's an after. I haven't planned for an after since the night my prayers went quiet." He looked at the banked fire, at the distant shape of Locke's bedroll at the camp's edge, alone, awake—he could see the faint gleam of her open eyes. "She's not sleeping either."

"No."

"She's listening to us."

"I know."

"Do you mind?"

Oscar considered it. "Let her listen," she said. "Maybe she'll learn what trust sounds like. God knows the Church never taught her."

*

But sleep wouldn't come for Oscar either, and near midnight she rose and walked the camp's perimeter, as she did most nights, checking the sight lines, the escape routes, the old smuggler paths that stitched through the pine shadow like veins through a wrist. Habit. Armor. The decade of invisibility that had turned out, impossibly, to be preparation.

At the eastern edge, she found Locke standing watch without being asked to—standing where a guard would stand, arms crossed against the cold, the heretic's brand pale in the starlight.

"You should sleep," Oscar said.

"So should you."

"I asked first."

A ghost of a smile touched the ruined cheek. "Inquisitors don't sleep well in camps we once burned. Every tree looks like kindling waiting for a decision." Locke was quiet a moment. Then, without turning: "The girl. Wren. The brand on her—it's the same sigil as the third seal-piece. I saw it in the Reliquary diagrams. The keeper's mark." Now she did turn, and her eyes in the dark were sharp and troubled. "The Maw marked her because she's part of the lock, Dravenmoor. Not collateral. Component. Whatever happens at that altar, the child is inside the mechanism."

"I know."

"Do you?" Locke's voice dropped, urgent, stripped of all its gilding. "Because I've read the old rites. The *original* ones, the pre-Sundering texts the Church suppressed. The reforging demands a witness. Someone bound to both sides—branded by the hunger, named by the keeper. If the rite goes wrong, the witness pays the price first. Before the heir. Before anyone." She stepped closer, close enough that Oscar could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes. "I'm telling you this because it's true, and because no one else will, and because I have decided—against all evidence and all sense—that you're the first person in eleven years worth telling the truth to. Your little witness is standing in the doorway of something terrible. And the Maw knows it. And it's coming for her faster than we're walking toward the mountain."

The words settled over Oscar like snowfall, silent and suffocating. She thought of Wren asleep in the supply tent, blanket to her chin, the brand glowing faintly through the wool like a coal refusing to die. She thought of the Ferryman's toll, the mother's face she'd paid, the altars bled upon, the friends buried under cairns. Everything had cost. Everything would cost more.

"How fast," Oscar said.

"Faster than us." Locke glanced east, toward the black shoulder of Thornfell rising against the stars, toward the heart of the mountain where the Ember Altar waited. "Unless we stop being careful."

Oscar followed her gaze. Somewhere beneath that mass of stone, her inheritance pulsed like a second heart. Somewhere behind them, a hunger the size of a god was learning their names.

"Break camp at dawn," she said. "We run the Teeth."

Behind them, in the supply tent, unnoticed by either woman, the ember-brand flared bright enough to shine through canvas—and far away, in the dark between the mountains, something vast turned its attention homeward.
