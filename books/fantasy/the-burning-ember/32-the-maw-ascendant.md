## 32. The Maw Ascendant

Wren had learned to read the brand the way other children read weather.

When it slept, it was a dull warmth beneath the skin of her forearm, a coal banked under ash. When it stirred—when gods came near, or when the veil thinned over some drowned shrine—it woke in slow pulses, like a second heartbeat that did not belong to her. And when it *screamed*, she felt it in her teeth, behind her eyes, in the soft hollow of her throat where her voice lived.

It was screaming now.

She sat up in the dark of the waystation loft, blankets pooling around her waist, and pressed both hands over the ember-mark as though she could smother it. The others slept below—Oscar bent over her father's journal even in sleep, Cassian with his back against the door out of old seminary habit, Brannoc snoring like a landslide. Above them, through the gaps in the shingles, the stars were wrong.

Not gone. Not dimmed. *Wrong*—arranged as if someone had taken the night sky and shaken it like a rug, so that constellations leaned at angles no sky should hold. Wren had seen that once before, on the night the black rain fell on Harrowfen and whispered names into the gutters. She had been eleven years old and full of wonder then. She was not full of wonder now.

"Key," she whispered to herself, testing the word the way you test a loose tooth. "You're not a curse. You're a key."

She didn't believe it yet. But Oscar had said it, three nights ago, crouched beside her while the Maw's shadow passed over the valley like a flock of carrion birds made of one enormous bird, and Oscar Dravenmoor did not say things she hadn't weighed first. Oscar measured everything—distances, risks, words. If she'd said *key*, she'd meant it.

The brand pulsed again, harder, and this time Wren felt something answer it from far away. A pull. Southward, toward the Greywater—the wide, slow marsh-river where the Ferryman kept his crossing, where they had paid a memory for passage what felt like a lifetime ago. Something was calling the brand home.

Or something was calling the brand to *dinner*.

* * *

She went down the ladder quietly, barefoot on cold boards, because she could not have slept if she'd tried and because some part of her—some part she didn't examine too closely—wanted to be near people when the world ended. She found Oscar awake anyway, of course, sitting by the banked fire with the journal open on her knees, ink-stained fingers tracing lines of dead handwriting.

"You felt it too," Wren said.

Oscar looked up. In the firelight her face was all sharp planes and tired eyes, and for once she didn't bother hiding how tired. "The map's been redrawing itself since midnight." She turned the journal around. On the final page, the living map shifted like water finding its level, roads crawling, mountains breathing—and at its southern edge, where the Greywater wound silver through the marshes, a blot of darkness was spreading across the parchment like spilled ink. Like rot. "Something's eating the map itself. I've never seen it do that."

"It's not eating the map." Wren's voice came out small. She sat down across from Oscar, hugging her knees, and made herself look at the spreading stain. "It's eating what the map shows. Look—the Ferryman's crossing is gone. The whole reach of river. It's just... blank now."

Oscar went very still. Wren had learned to recognize that stillness: it was what happened when the cartographer inside her met a fact she couldn't chart. Blank space on a map wasn't nothing. Blank space was a place where knowledge ended, and knowledge ending meant death, usually somebody's.

"The Ferryman," Oscar said slowly, "is older than the Sundering. He's not a god exactly—he's what's left when a god forgets everything except its job. He ferries memories. He ferried *mine*." Her hand drifted to her temple, the way it always did when she thought about her mother's face, which she had paid away and could not grieve properly because grief needs a face. "If the Maw has reached the Greywater—"

The brand flared white-hot.

Wren cried out before she could stop herself, and the sound brought the whole house awake—Cassian on his feet with a knife he hadn't remembered drawing, Brannoc thundering up from below decks of his own bad dreams, Ilsa and Petra tumbling into the room with their hair loose and their eyes sharp. By the time the flare faded, Wren was shaking, and everyone was looking at her, and the fire had burned strangely blue.

"It's showing me," she gasped. "I don't—I can't stop it—"

And then she wasn't in the waystation anymore.

* * *

She stood on the Greywater at dusk—or what had been dusk, once. The marshes stretched in every direction, but the water was wrong: black and utterly still, holding no reflection of the bruised sky above it. The reeds stood dead and grey as old bone. And in the middle of the river, where the Ferryman's lantern had always hung—a green light that travelers steered by for a thousand years—there was only a hole.

Not darkness. A *hole*. A place where the world had been scooped out, edges ragged, and beyond its rim Wren could see something else: vast, shifting, a country of hunger wearing the shapes of everything it had ever eaten. Faces moved along its inner surface like fish under ice. Some of them were still talking. Still pleading. The Maw did not kill quickly; it kept you, the way a miser keeps coins.

The Ferryman stood on his barge at the edge of the hole, pole in hand, and Wren understood with a child's terrible clarity that he was not fleeing because he could not flee. His nature was to carry things across. There was nowhere across from this.

"Maw," the Ferryman said, and his voice was the voice of every ferryman who had ever asked for a coin, layered and echoing. "You are not welcome in my boat."

"I am not asking for your boat." The Maw's voice came from everywhere, from the dead reeds, from the water, from inside Wren's own skull. "I am asking for your name. You have forgotten everything except your work, old man. Let me remember it for you. Let me *have* it."

"No one may take a god's name," said the Ferryman. "That is the covenant."

"The covenant is a fence," said the Maw, "and fences are for breaking."

Wren watched—she could do nothing else, her body a thousand miles away and her self pinned here like an insect in a book—as the hole widened. The Ferryman raised his pole, and light ran down it, green and ancient, and for one moment the marshes blazed with it and the Maw recoiled and Wren thought, *he can win, he can—*

Then the Maw showed him his name.

Wren couldn't see it. It wasn't a word, exactly; it was more like a weight, a shape of meaning that bent everything around it. But she saw the Ferryman bend under it. She saw a thousand years of forgetting lift all at once, every memory he had surrendered to keep himself simple and functional flooding back into him in a single instant—every passenger, every coin, every face, every prayer, every century of standing alone in the mist. It was too much. It would have been too much for anything.

"Oh," the Ferryman said, very quietly, in a voice that was suddenly just one voice, one tired old man's voice. "Oh, I remember. I remember *everything*."

And the Maw took him.

There was no scream. That was the worst of it. The Ferryman simply folded inward, like a coat falling off a hook, and the green light of his lantern guttered and went out, and the hole in the middle of the Greywater tore wider—not wider in the way a wound widens, but wider the way a door opens. Beyond it, the god-realm of Vaelith poured through like light through a cracked shutter, and Wren saw diminished gods scatter before it like startled birds, and she heard the veil itself make a sound she would hear for the rest of her life: a long, low groan, like ice on a warming lake, like a great structure deciding whether to fall.

Then the vision snapped shut, and she was on the floor of the waystation with blood running from her nose and five people shouting her name.

* * *

They gave her water and honey and Petra's blanket, the good wool one, and they sat her by the fire while the argument raged over her head like weather.

"—the Greywater is two days south," Brannoc was growling. "Two days *south*, girl, and we're going north, and if the thing ate the Ferryman then there's no crossing anywhere in the marshlands, which means half our routes are dead, which means—"

"—which means the veil is tearing," Cassian finished, quiet, and the quiet cut through the noise better than any shout. He was staring at the fire. "A remnant god consuming another entity—that's not feeding, that's *growth*. The Maw isn't a god anymore. It's becoming a wound. And wounds in the veil don't close on their own. They widen."

"Then we run faster," Ilsa said flatly. "North, to the mountain. To the altar. We let Oscar do what she came to do and we never look south again."

"We can't." Everyone turned to Wren. Her nose had stopped bleeding; her voice, when she found it, surprised her by being steady. "Don't you understand? It's not going to stay south. It ate the Ferryman because the Ferryman was *old*—old power, old name, easy to swallow. But it's tasted a name now. It knows what names taste like." She pulled back the sleeve of her nightshirt, and the brand lay on her forearm, glowing faintly, patient as a lit wick. "And it already knows mine."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, an owl called, ordinary and mortal, and Wren loved it fiercely.

"That's why it marked you," Cassian said softly. He crossed the room and knelt in front of her, and his eyes—those gray, guilty, kind eyes—searched hers. "In Harrowfen. When you prayed and the rain answered. You didn't call the Maw, Wren. You called *anything*, and the Maw found you first, and it put its mark on you the way a thief marks a house he means to come back to."

"A key," Wren said. "Oscar said it was a key."

Oscar had not spoken since the vision. Now she set the journal aside and came to sit beside Wren, close enough that their shoulders touched, and Wren realized the older woman was shaking too, just slightly, just enough to feel.

"When I renewed the first altar," Oscar said, "with my blood—something noticed me. I told you that. I felt it turn toward me across the veil, the way you feel someone watching in a dark room." Her jaw tightened. "I thought it was curiosity. It wasn't. It was recognition. The Maw doesn't want me, Wren. My blood is the lock. Your brand is the key. It marked you because through you, it can reach what's left of the covenant without ever touching a Dravenmoor. You're not bait." She stopped. Started again, gentler. "You're not *only* bait. A key works both ways."

Wren stared at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Oscar said, "that whatever door the Maw thinks it's opening—you're the handle. It comes to you, it comes on your terms. It follows your brand, which means your brand can lead it. Away from towns. Away from people. Into ground of our choosing." She looked around the room, at each of them in turn—Cassian, Brannoc, the twins—and Wren watched the plan assemble behind her eyes like roads assembling on a living map. "It means we stop running."

Brannoc's laugh was short and disbelieving. "You want to use the child as *bait*."

"I want to use the key as a *key*," Oscar said. "And I want everyone in this room to tell me honestly if they see another road. Because I've spent ten days doing nothing but look for roads, and this is the only one that doesn't end with the Maw tearing the veil open over a city."

No one spoke. The fire burned. Wren looked down at her arm—at the little ember that had branded her in a moment of desperate, childish hope, that had made her village call her witch, that had cost her everything and dragged her halfway across the world into the company of smugglers and exiles and one fallen priest who knelt in front of her like her sins were worth absolving.

Everyone in Harrowfen had told her the brand was a punishment. The priest who'd held the trial had said so. The neighbors who'd thrown the stones had said so. Even Wren, in the small hours, had believed it: *you prayed wrong, you wanted too much, this is what wanting gets.*

But keys weren't punishments. Keys were given to people who were trusted with doors.

"It led me to you," she said suddenly, aloud, and everyone looked at her. "The brand. After Harrowfen, I ran up the pass, and I was dying, and it burned and burned—and that's how you found me, Oscar. You said so. You said you saw a light on the snow." Her throat tightened. "It wasn't calling the Maw that day. It was calling *help*. It's been trying to talk to me this whole time and I've been treating it like a disease."

Cassian made a small sound, almost a laugh, almost a prayer. "God-touched," he murmured. "The Church uses it as an accusation. They never stop to ask what the touching might be *for*."

Outside, far to the south, the horizon flickered—a pulse of wrong-colored light, there and gone, like heat lightning in winter. Everyone saw it. Nobody pretended it was weather.

The veil, groaning.

"How long?" Petra asked quietly. "Before it tears all the way?"

Oscar was silent for a long moment. Then she picked up the journal, and the living map crawled across its page, and Wren watched her measure the distance between the spreading southern stain and the heart of Thornfell, and Wren watched her arrive at the same number Wren had already guessed.

"Days," Oscar said. "Maybe four. Maybe fewer." She closed the journal and looked at Wren, and there was something in her face that Wren had never seen there before—not pity, not fear, but a fierce, deliberate respect, the look of a cartographer regarding a landmark she intended to build her map around. "So we go north tonight. All of us. And Wren—" she hesitated, and Wren saw how much it cost her, the woman who paid memories without blinking, to ask for anything, especially from a twelve-year-old, "—I need you to listen to it. The brand. Not fight it, not fear it. Listen. If it's a key, I need to know every sound it makes."

Wren nodded. Then, because she was twelve and honest: "It's scared, you know. The brand. It's been scared since the Ferryman died. Whatever the Maw is—it's scared of it too."

"Good," said Brannoc grimly, hauling himself up and reaching for his boots. "Means it can bleed. Everything that's scared can bleed."

They moved to pack, the room filling with purposeful noise—straps cinching, maps rolling, Cassian murmuring to the twins—but Wren stayed by the fire a moment longer, her hand curled over the ember-mark, feeling it pulse against her palm like a small stubborn heart.

*Not a curse,* she told it silently. *All right. I believe you now.*

Far to the south, the horizon pulsed again—closer this time, hungry, patient—and the brand pulsed back, and Wren understood, with a clarity that settled over her like snowfall, that the Maw was already coming for her.

And that, for the first time since Harrowfen, she was glad.
