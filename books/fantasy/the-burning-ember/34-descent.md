## 34. Descent

The mountain's roots were older than its skin.

Cassian had thought he understood darkness. He had spent three years in a seminary cell with the shutters nailed closed, doing penance by candlelight; he had spent one worse night in a smuggler's hold beneath the Greywater, listening to the river press against the hull like something patient and enormous wanting in. He had believed, in the arrogant way of men who have only ever borrowed their fears, that he knew what dark was.

Then Brannoc pulled the last hatch shut behind them, and the world above ceased to exist.

The tunnel mouth swallowed the grey light of the high pass in a single breath. Behind them, wind moaned across the scree fields where Ilsa's cairn stood—Petra had stacked it herself, stone by stone, refusing help, her knuckles split and bleeding and her face carved out of something harder than grief—and ahead of them lay the under-mountain: the unmapped country beneath Thornfell that existed only in Oscar Dravenmoor's ink-stained memory.

"Torches," Oscar said quietly. "Stay close to the walls. The air moves down here in ways it shouldn't."

Cassian lit his torch from hers, and flame bloomed against stone so black it seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. The rock here was not the grey granite of the peaks above. It was veined—shot through with threads of dull red that pulsed, faintly, like veins beneath skin. Like embers banked under ash.

*Like a heart,* he thought, and did not say it aloud.

Wren pressed close against his side. The girl had not let go of his sleeve since they'd entered, her small fingers twisted into the wool hard enough to pull the seam crooked. Her brand was dim tonight—a faint orange glow at her wrist, banked low—but Cassian had learned in the weeks since Duskhollow that dim did not mean quiet. It meant listening.

"It's warm," Wren whispered. "Why is it warm?"

"Because we're inside something old," Cassian said. He kept his voice even, the way he'd once kept it steady over funeral rites, back when he still believed the words he spoke meant anything to anyone but the grieving. "Old things keep their heat."

Ahead, Oscar had already begun to walk. She moved through the dark the way she moved through everything—with that terrible competence that both drew him and made him feel, some days, like a man carrying a candle through a cathedral built by someone else's hands. But even she paused at the first junction, her torch raised, her brow furrowed at walls that bore no marks she recognized.

"This isn't on any map," she said. Not frightened. Worse than frightened: *revising*. "I mapped every smuggler road through Thornfell's roots. Every shaft, every flooded gallery, every rat-run the clans used before the Crown sealed them. This passage—" her torch traced the smooth, veined stone, "—wasn't here when my father walked these tunnels. Or it was sleeping."

"The mountain's waking up," Petra said from the rear. Her voice was flat as slate. Since Ilsa, she had spoken perhaps forty words, and each one landed like a coin dropped on stone. "Everything's waking up. That's the whole problem, isn't it."

No one answered her. There was nothing to answer with.

They descended.

* * *

Hours passed—or what Cassian assumed were hours. Time moved strangely in the roots of the world, stretching and pooling like water finding its level. They ate cold bread and dried fish without stopping, walking while they chewed because stopping felt like an invitation. The red veins in the stone grew brighter as they went deeper, until the torches became almost redundant, until they were walking through the slow circulatory system of the mountain itself, and Cassian found himself thinking of scripture again—the old forbidden verses about Vaelith, about how the god-realm was not above them but *within*, folded through the mortal world like marrow through bone.

He had memorized those verses as a boy. He had recited them at his ordination, voice cracking with joy. *And the gods walked among the roots of things, and their light was in the deep places, and man was never far from heaven though heaven forgot his name.*

He had prayed for thirty years after heaven stopped answering. He knew exactly how far from heaven a man could be.

"Cassian." Oscar fell into step beside him, letting the others draw ahead around a bend. Her voice dropped low. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you recite dead prayers under your breath and pretend it's breathing exercises."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Habit."

"I know." She glanced back at Wren, then at him, and in the vein-light her eyes looked like they held small fires. "When we stop, you'll take first watch with me. And Cassian—if it comes, if the Maw finds us down here—you get Wren out. Whatever happens to the rest of us, whatever happens to *me*, you take her and you run and you don't look back. Swear it."

"You know I won't swear that."

"You'll swear it anyway."

"I watched Ilsa die holding a pass," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "I watched Brannoc carry her body three miles because there wasn't room to bury her. I am not writing anyone else's eulogy in advance, Oscar. Least of all yours."

For a moment she said nothing. Then her hand found his in the dark—not tenderly, not gently, but with the fierce grip of a woman anchoring herself to something real. Her fingers were cold and ink-stained and shaking, just slightly, and he realized with a jolt that she was afraid. Not of the Maw. Of this. Of asking.

"If it comes to that," she said finally, "you'll choose her. Because you're the kind of man who chooses the child over the woman, and that's why—" She stopped. Started again. "That's why I love you. So don't make me say the rest of it in a tunnel full of listening stone."

The word landed between them like a struck bell. She had said it once before, in blood and panic on the Weeping Stair, and neither of them had dared speak it since, as if naming it twice might spend it.

Cassian lifted her hand and pressed his mouth to her knuckles, brief and hard. "Then walk faster," he said, "and don't make me choose at all."

* * *

They heard the Maw for the first time two hours past the third junction.

It began as a sound in the stone itself—a vast, wet shifting, like something turning over in sleep. Then came the voices. That was the worst of it, Cassian thought, gripping his torch so hard the wood groaned. The Maw wore the faces of everyone it had eaten, and it had eaten so many, and it used their voices the way a musician uses instruments.

*"Cassian?"*

His mother's voice. Dead twenty years, buried under the chapel floor at Vale's Hollow, and it came drifting up the tunnel behind them, warm and puzzled and loving. *"Cassian, why did you leave the light on? You know how the dark frightens me."*

"Don't answer," Oscar breathed. "Whatever you hear—don't answer. It's fishing."

*"Brother Vale,"* another voice now, deeper, male, familiar in a way that made his stomach drop—Father Aldous, his old mentor, who had died in his bed believing the Church was good. *"Brother Vale, the seminary letters were forged, you misread them, come back, come back and see—"*

"It lies," Cassian said aloud, to himself, to Wren, whose fingers had gone white on his sleeve. "It lies the way drowning men wave. It wants us to turn around. Keep walking."

But the voices grew more clever. They stopped being the dead and started being the living—Brannoc's laugh from a side passage where no one stood; Mother Quill's rasp, saying *good lad, good lad, you were always my best mistake*; and then, unbearably, Ilsa's voice, bright and sharp-edged, calling Petra's name from the dark ahead.

Petra broke.

She turned toward it with her knife already drawn, and it took Brannoc's whole arm around her waist to stop her, hauling her back as she screamed her sister's name into stone that swallowed it whole. She fought him for ten full seconds—an eternity—and then went limp, and then silent, and then she walked again, staring straight ahead, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face.

The Maw's voices stopped. In the silence that followed, Cassian understood, with cold certainty, that it hadn't given up.

It was savoring.

* * *

They reached the great gallery near dawn-or-what-would-have-been-dawn, a cavern so vast their torches couldn't find its ceiling, and there Oscar called the halt they could no longer avoid. Wren was swaying on her feet. Petra's silence had gone from grief to something worrying. Even Brannoc, who could march for two days on stubbornness alone, was gray-faced.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar the size of a chapel, backs to stone, torches ringed around them. And it was there, in the thin rest between terror and exhaustion, that Brannoc took Cassian aside.

The old pilot's remaining eye caught the firelight like a chip of amber. He looked back the way they'd come, down the long throat of tunnel, and when he spoke his voice was quiet, almost conversational.

"She's gaining," he said. "Your map-woman knows it too, she's just too polite to say. We've been running downhill for hours and the voices are getting louder, not softer. That means it's not following our trail." He tapped his own chest. "It's following the girl's brand. Straight line. Through rock, if it has to. We can't outrun a straight line, priest. I've sailed enough to know—you can't outrun a heading."

"So what do we do?"

"Well." Brannoc scratched his beard, and Cassian saw that his hand was not quite steady. "There's an old clan trick. My grandfather worked the deep mines before the Crown closed them. When the seam catches fire below, you don't run from it—you bring the mountain down on it. Seal it in. Costs you the mine, costs you maybe the men closest to the charge, but it saves the rest." He smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "There's a support gallery back a ways—saw it at the last junction. Old timbering, dry as tinder, holding up half a hillside. A man with a torch and a few minutes could bring the whole throat of that tunnel down. Bury it. Buy the rest of you a day, maybe two. Maybe enough."

Cassian felt the understanding arrive like cold water. "No."

"Aye," Brannoc agreed pleasantly. "That's about the size of it."

"No. We find another way. We double back, we—"

"With what speed, lad? With what luck? The Maw is behind us and Locke's lot may be ahead of us and the altar's still a day's climb below. There's no other way. There's just this way, and whether I'm the one who walks it or we all die strung out along this tunnel like beads on a string." He clapped Cassian on the shoulder, grip firm as an anchor chain. "I'm sixty-one years old, Brother Vale. I've buried a wife, a ship, and now a girl who deserved fifty more years. I'm owed a death that does some good, and I mean to collect."

"You stay," Cassian said, "and I stay with you. Two men can set the charges faster and—"

"And then who carries the child when her legs give out? Who watches the map-woman's back when she starts hearing her father's voice offering her power? You think I don't see it? You've got the only pair of hands down here that everyone else believes in." Brannoc's eye glittered. "That's not a burden, lad. That's a commission. Take it."

They argued for an hour. Cassian lost.

* * *

They told the others at the edge of the firelight, and it went the way such tellings go—Oscar rigid with refusal, Wren crying without sound, Petra saying nothing at all until she crossed the camp and embraced the old pilot fiercely, her face hidden against his shoulder, and whispered something Cassian was not meant to hear and would carry anyway:

*"Tell her I'm sorry about the cairn. Tell her it wasn't straight. Nothing I build will ever be straight again."*

Brannoc held her a long moment. "Nothing you build needs to be, little forge," he said. "That's what sisters are for. One of you holds the hammer. One holds the nail."

Then he shouldered his pack, checked his torch, and rolled his sleeves like a man preparing for ordinary work, and somehow that was the worst of it—that he made dying look like a chore he'd been meaning to get to.

Oscar walked him to the junction. Cassian followed, close enough to witness and far enough to give them privacy, and heard her say, "You come back. You hear me? You collapse that tunnel and you *run*, Brannoc. Run faster than you've ever—"

"Lass." He turned, and in the vein-light his weathered face looked carved from the same stone as the mountain, patient and ancient and warm. "I've been running my whole life. Ships, debts, husbands of women I shouldn't have loved. Let an old man stand still, just once, for something worth standing on." He touched her cheek with two fingers, gentle as a benediction. "Map the rest of it well. That's your penance and your gift both. Make sure none of this was for nothing."

Then he winked at Cassian. "Keep her alive, priest. She's the best cargo I ever carried."

And he turned and walked back down the tunnel with his torch held high, an old man's silhouette shrinking along a vein of ember-light, until the dark folded over him like water closing.

They ran.

They ran the way the damned run, downhill through the pulsing dark, Wren half-carried between them, Petra keeping pace with a face like flint, and behind them—minutes later, ten minutes, fifteen—the mountain spoke. A single deep concussion rolled through the stone, then another, then a long grinding roar as tons of rock came down where an old man stood holding a torch, and the floor bucked beneath their feet, and dust rained from seams overhead like grey snow, and Cassian did not look back because he had promised, because looking back would have been a lie about what Brannoc deserved.

The roar faded. The air settled. Somewhere behind them, sealed under a hundred fathoms of Thornfell, the Maw's voices went silent—or muffled, or merely patient.

They did not stop running until Oscar's map-memory brought them to a narrow stair cut into living rock, spiraling down into a warmth that rose to meet them like breath.

There, gasping, Wren suddenly grabbed Cassian's arm. Her brand had flared—bright, urgent, awake—and her eyes had gone wide and fixed on the darkness below.

"Cassian," she whispered. "It's not angry anymore."

"What do you mean?"

Her small hand tightened. "It's *thanking* him."

Below them, far down in the heart of the mountain, something vast exhaled—and every ember-vein in the stone around them pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat quickening, like a door beginning to open.
