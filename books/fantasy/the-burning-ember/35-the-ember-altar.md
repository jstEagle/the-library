## 35. The Ember Altar

The mountain's heart was warm.

Oscar had expected cold—had braced for it, the way she'd braced for every dark thing this road had shown her—but the deeper they descended through Thornfell's roots, the more the stone gave back heat like a sleeping animal. By the time the smuggler tunnel opened into the last gallery, sweat ran down her spine beneath her coat, and the air tasted of iron and hot sand and something older underneath: a sweetness like incense burned in a room sealed for centuries.

Cassian's lantern threw their shadows long against walls veined with glowing seams. Not torchlight. Not any fire she could name. The veins pulsed faintly, slow as breath, and when Oscar pressed her palm to one she felt it answer—a rhythm not quite her own heartbeat, but keeping time with it.

"It knows you're here," Cassian said quietly.

"I know." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "It's been waiting ten years. Maybe three hundred."

Behind them, Wren made a small sound. The girl had insisted on coming—had refused, in fact, with a stubbornness that would have been funny under any other circumstances—and now she stood pressed close to Brannoc's side, one hand curled around the pendant of salt-iron Mother Quill had given her before the end. The brand on her wrist glowed through its wrappings, a dull coal-colored light that brightened as they went deeper, as if it were being called home.

"Stay behind me," Oscar said, though they both knew the order was useless. If the Maw came, no arrangement of bodies would save them. They had all known that since the Greywater, since the Ferryman's lantern went out and something vast drank the river itself.

They had lost hours buying those hours. Brannoc was still somewhere above them in the dark with a fuse of blackpowder and a wall of stone he meant to bring down on a god's head. No one had said goodbye properly. There hadn't been time, and Brannoc would have hated it anyway—he'd have made a joke, spat over his shoulder, told them to run faster than grief. So instead Oscar had gripped his forearm once, hard, and he had grinned with his one eye and said, *Go on then, map-witch. Draw us a way out.*

She hadn't managed it yet. But there was still time. There had to be.

* * *

The last passage narrowed to a crack, then widened without warning into a space so vast the lantern couldn't find its ceiling. Cassian raised the light and the darkness simply took it, swallowed the beam whole, and for one vertiginous moment Oscar thought they'd walked into open sky underground—until the glow from the floor resolved.

Steps. Thousands of them, cut from black glassy stone, spiraling down toward a center that burned.

The Ember Altar did not look like anything the old maps had promised. It was not a shrine, not a table of sacrifice, not the ring of standing stones her father's journal had sketched in a hand gone shaky with awe. It was a wound in the world—a bowl of molten gold light perhaps thirty paces across, held in a cradle of fused rock, and rising from its center, a single shape: an anvil of black iron, unmarked, unadorned, older than the mountain around it.

And above it, hanging in the air like a held breath, a fragment of light. A shard. A piece of something that had once been whole—the seal, or what remained of it after three hundred years of thinning blood and lying priests and a family that had chosen power over payment.

Oscar stood at the top of the steps and could not move.

"Oscar." Cassian's hand found her shoulder. Warm. Human. Calloused from rope and oar and one bad winter hauling nets he never talked about. "Talk to me."

"I'm trying to remember why I came," she said, and it was almost a joke, and they both let it be one for exactly two seconds.

Then she started down.

* * *

The heat grew with every step, but it wasn't the heat that slowed her. It was the whispering.

She'd felt it before—in the Court of Cinders, in the thin place where the diminished gods had offered her everything with voices like silk drawn over knives. *Take it,* they'd said. *You were born to it. Why crawl when you were made to fly?* She had refused them, and Wren had paid for that refusal with a mark that deepened, and Oscar had carried the guilt of it up out of Vaelith like a stone sewn into her chest.

But this was different. This wasn't the gods' voices. This was the inheritance itself—the power that had lived dormant in her blood since birth, waking now because it was home. Because it recognized the altar the way a hound recognizes its master's door.

*You could save them all,* it murmured, in a voice that sounded terribly like her own. *Wren. Brannoc in the dark. The crew. The villages. One word of will and the Maw is ash. One act of dominion and no Inquisitor ever burns another shrine. You've crawled and hidden and starved your whole life for a cause you didn't choose. Take what's yours and never hide again.*

"You're not me," Oscar whispered. "You're what my family stole."

*We are what your family kept alive,* it answered, patient as geology. *Same thing, from where we sit.*

At the bottom of the spiral, the heat became a wall. Oscar stopped at the altar's edge, close enough to see the molten light was not liquid at all but countless tiny motes—embers, each one drifting, each one humming a note too low for human ears. The seal-fragment hung above the anvil, cracked down its middle, leaking light like a breached hull leaks sea.

Her father's journal had ended here, at the final page, in handwriting that shook: *The rite demands what we never paid. God forgive us, we took instead of gave. Let my daughter be wiser.*

She set the journal on the altar's rim. It seemed right that it should witness this.

"What does it want?" Wren asked from the top of the steps. She'd come down despite everything, small and fierce, her brand blazing now so brightly it lit her face like a hearth. "The rite. What does it demand?"

Oscar looked at the anvil. At the crack in the seal. At the ember-light swirling below, hungry and beautiful and utterly indifferent to her.

"Everything," she said. "It wants everything I am."

* * *

She understood it then—not through study, not through the journal, but the way you understand falling, instantly and completely. The old Dravenmoors had bound the gods by taking their power and anchoring it in their own bloodline. Every renewal had been a renewal of the theft: heir's blood poured on the altar, heir's claim restated, the stolen goods re-signed in the family name. That was why the binding thinned as the blood thinned. That was why the Sundering's wounds never healed. You cannot mend a covenant built on robbery; you can only keep recommitting the crime until the victims rise.

The Maw was not a monster. The Maw was a creditor.

And the only payment left—the only thing the old family had never surrendered—was the inheritance itself. All of it. Forever. Not spent, not shared, not wielded in some final glorious battle. Renounced. Given back freely, which was the one thing stolen goods could never be unless the thief's heir chose to make them gifts.

"If I do this," she said aloud, to Cassian, to Wren, to the listening dark, "I won't just lose the power. I'll lose the *claim*. Whatever I am after this—I'll be mortal until I die. Ordinary. No covenant-heir. No blood the gods answer. Just—" she laughed, short and unsteady, "—just a mapmaker."

"That was always the job description," Cassian said. He had come down the steps to stand beside her, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. His eyes were wet and steady. "I watched you spend ten years being ordinary on purpose. You were magnificent at it."

"The Maw is coming," Wren said. "Brannoc bought us hours, not days. If you give it back—if you empty yourself—what stops it?"

Oscar looked at the cracked seal. At the embers circling below like a congregation awaiting a sermon.

"Honestly?" she said. "I don't know. Maybe nothing. Maybe the whole thing fails and we die in the dark and the world gets eaten by a god-shaped debt collector." She turned to face them both. "Or maybe a covenant reforged by choice holds stronger than one forged by theft ever did. My father believed it. I think—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—I think I believe it too."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll be wrong with everyone I love standing next to me. That's the best odds anyone's offering tonight."

Cassian exhaled something that was half laugh, half prayer. Then he did the thing he'd done at the Weeping Stair, bleeding, blade in his ribs, when he'd looked at her like she was the only fixed point in a spinning world: he nodded. Once. *I'm here. Do what you have to.*

Oscar Dravenmoor rolled up her sleeves.

* * *

The rite required no words she knew, no gestures from any surviving text. It required only truth, spoken into the fire, and hands willing to burn.

She knelt at the altar's edge and plunged both arms into the ember-light to the elbow.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it—it felt like coming home, like sinking into a bath drawn at exactly the temperature of her own blood, and the power rose to meet her gladly, eagerly, pouring up her arms in rivers of gold, showing her everything it could be. She saw herself crowned in the Court of Cinders. She saw Thornfell's peaks bending like grass before her will. She saw Locke's Church broken, the Maw unmade with a word, Wren's brand erased, death itself negotiated down to a formality. She saw a thousand years of peace purchased with a single act of ownership, and it was gorgeous, it was effortless, it was—

—her mother's face, which she had sold to the Ferryman and could no longer remember, smiling at her from a memory that belonged to someone who had never existed.

"No," Oscar said.

The power paused. Listened.

"You were stolen," she said, and her voice rang off the glass-black walls, and every ember in the bowl turned toward her like an eye. "Three hundred years ago my ancestors took you from beings who could not refuse, and they called it salvation, and they made their children into locks on a treasure chest. I am the last lock. And I am opening it."

She began to pour.

There was no other word for it. The inheritance moved through her and out, through her palms, into the molten heart of the altar, and each thread of it tore a little as it left—not pain, exactly, but the grief of parting, the specific ache of setting down something heavy you have carried so long you forgot it wasn't part of your body. She gave back the dominion. She gave back the fire-sight. She gave back the whispers, the appetites, the gorgeous temptation, the crown she'd tried on in Vaelith and hated how well it fit. She gave it all, freely, deliberately, naming each gift as it went the way her father had taught her to label every line on a map: *this was taken, this was taken, this was taken, and I return it.*

The cracked seal above the anvil began to knit.

Light flowed into the fracture like water into a dry riverbed. The hum of the embers rose from a drone to a chord to something that was unmistakably, impossibly, *singing*—and far above them, through miles of stone, Oscar felt the veil between worlds flex and soften and begin, for the first time in three centuries, to heal rather than tear.

She was crying. She noted it distantly, the way she noted weather. Most of her was elsewhere, dissolving into the giving, becoming lighter with every breath, and the last threads of godhood-inheritance slid out of her like a splinter leaving flesh, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like mercy: *so that's what I weigh without it. So little. So wonderfully little.*

The seal closed. Whole. Bright. Held together not by a bloodline's grip but by a single mortal woman's open hands.

Oscar slumped forward against the altar's rim, emptied, human, shaking.

And that was when the mountain screamed.

* * *

It came up through the rock itself. Not through the tunnels—through the *stone*, a pressure like the whole massif above them turning soft, and the far wall of the chamber bulged inward, glass-black surface rippling, and then split along a seam that had not existed a moment before.

Through the breach poured the dark.

Not shadow—shadow was merely absence, and this was presence, a hunger given geography, a vastness that filled the enormous chamber in seconds and smelled of every mine it had swallowed, every town, every mouth. Faces moved in it. Hundreds of faces, the eaten, surfacing and sinking like the drowned in grey water, and among them—newly added, still fresh—she saw the Ferryman, lantern-less, his pole snapped, his patient eyes wide with an astonishment that had not yet had time to become fear.

The Maw had found them anyway. Hours bought with Brannoc's ship and Ilsa's grave and Quill's life, and it had spent them all and arrived regardless, because hunger is patient in a way loyalty cannot afford to be.

Wren's brand ignited. The girl cried out and dropped to her knees as the mark blazed white-hot through its wrappings, and the Maw turned toward her with the terrible tenderness of a thing regarding its key, its missing piece, the lock it had crossed a world to open.

"*No.*"

Oscar pushed herself upright. Her arms were bare and ordinary and trembling. She had nothing left—no inheritance, no fire, no dominion. She was a cartographer kneeling at the bottom of a mountain with a dead man's journal and two people she loved more than the world, and the world's oldest debt had come to collect.

The Maw's attention shifted. Those drowned faces arranged themselves into something like curiosity.

It knew. It could feel it the way she could feel it: the emptiness where the power had been. The heir who had renounced everything. It should have been furious. Instead the vastness quivered with something worse—*interest*, the interest of a creditor examining a debtor who has just burned the contract.

Because if the covenant was remade, then the rules had changed. And the Maw, above all things, understood rules.

It surged forward—not at Wren, not yet—but toward the altar, toward the newly mended seal, and Oscar understood in one lightning-stroke of clarity what it meant to do: test it. Break it while it was young. Prove that consent was just another kind of weakness, that the old ways, the strong ways, the *hungry* ways, were the only ones that had ever held.

"Cassian." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Get Wren behind the anvil."

"And you?"

Oscar looked at the Maw filling the chamber like a second night, and at the seal burning whole and new above the black iron, and she reached down and picked up her father's journal, and held it against her chest like a shield that had never been a shield at all—just a story, waiting for someone brave enough to finish it differently.

"I'm going to offer it its name back," she said. "And I'm going to pray it's listening."

The Maw opened every one of its borrowed mouths, and the chamber filled with the sound of everything it had ever eaten, screaming in chorus.

And Oscar Dravenmoor—who was, from this moment forward, only and entirely and gloriously mortal—took one step forward to meet it.
