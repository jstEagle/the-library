## 37. Aftermath of Ash

The road down from Thornfell smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and Seraphine Locke walked it alone.

She had given her horse to a clanwoman with three children and a limp, on the grounds that the Church would take everything else from her soon enough and she might as well practice poverty while she still had the dignity of choosing it. The seal-fragment rode in a leather case against her sternum, wrapped in linen, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warm for six days now. Ever since the mountain's heart had opened and closed like a lung.

Ever since Oscar Dravenmoor had poured a godhood into an altar and walked away from it.

Seraphine turned the memory over the way she once turned evidence—carefully, by lamplight, looking for the flaw that would let her dismiss it. She could not find one. That was the trouble. Ten years of Inquisition work had taught her that every miracle was a trick of light, every vision a fever, every god a hunger wearing borrowed faces. She had built her life on that certainty the way masons build on bedrock. And then a mapmaker with ink-stained fingers had knelt at the Ember Altar and *given the power back*, freely, asking nothing, and the mountain had filled with light that did not burn, and the Maw—the thing that had eaten Greywater Ferryman and half a mining town and Mother Quill's patience and Ilsa's life—had come apart into rain.

Rain. It had rained for two days afterward, ordinary rain, gray and clean and smelling of nothing but water. Wren had stood out in it with her arms spread and her brandless hand open, laughing and crying at once, and Seraphine had watched from the shelter of a broken archway and felt something crack in her chest that no amount of doctrine could mortar over.

*It was real.* All of it. The gods, the covenant, the Sundering—and the Church's own rites, the ones she had recited since childhood, the ones written into the liturgy in gold leaf: they had caused it. She had held the proof in her hands, lifted from Ilsa's body among the weeping stones of the third shrine. The old rites of severance, the Church's founding sacrament, drawn in a dead priest's blood on a page three centuries old. The Church had not merely known the covenant was failing. The Church had broken it, deliberately, and then spent three hundred years hunting anyone who remembered why.

And she had burned shrines for these people. She had burned a shrine in a border town with a girl watching, a girl who had prayed for rain and gotten black rain and whispers, and Seraphine had called it mercy—a quick fire instead of the question-room. She had told herself she was saving the world from hungry divinity. Some nights, in the honest dark behind her eyes, she still believed that was true, or had been true, right up until the moment it stopped being the whole truth and became only the comfortable part of it.

The road bent. Below her, through a break in the pines, she could see the lowlands: brown fields going green at the seams, a river carrying the last of the snowmelt, and far off, a smudge of white walls and slate roofs that she knew better than her own heartbeat. The Seminary city of Corvale. Home of the Choir Eternal. Seat of the Grand Inquisitor, Anselm Voss, whose ring she had kissed at her ordination and whose orders she was about to put in front of a tribunal like a corpse on a table.

They would destroy her for it. She had done the arithmetic a dozen times and it never came out any other way. Testimony against a Grand Inquisitor required corroboration from two sworn officers of equal rank; she had none. It required the seal-fragment be surrendered intact to the Reliquary; she intended to do that, which meant surrendering her only bargaining chip. It required, above all, that the tribunal want the truth—and the tribunal answered to Voss, and Voss had signed the order that read *the Dravenmoor line is to be erased root and branch*, and Voss had sent her to do the erasing, and Voss had been reading the same severed rites she had found in Ilsa's pack, because she had recognized his marginalia. His neat, patient, scholarly hand, annotating the destruction of the world.

He would call her mad. Or worse—he would call her seduced, corrupted, god-touched herself, and there were cells beneath Corvale where women went who were called that, and they did not come up again. She knew the cells. She had escorted people into them.

Her boots kept moving anyway. That was the strange thing about conviction, she thought as the pines thickened again and the city sank out of view. When it broke, it did not leave you free. It left you with the pieces, and the pieces still pointed somewhere, and you could not stop walking in the direction they indicated even when you knew the direction ended at a pyre.

* * *

She almost didn't stop at Duskhollow.

It lay a half-day off her route, down in its folded valley, and every practical instinct said pass it by—say your goodbyes on the road, keep the severance clean, don't give them the chance to spit at you or, worse, to be kind. But her feet took the side path the way her feet had been taking unauthorized paths all month, and by late afternoon she was standing at the mouth of the haven, looking at what spring was doing to it.

Rebuilding. That was the word for it, though the place had never looked like this even before the Maw and the Inquisitors between them had chewed its edges off. New timber frames stood where Brannoc's shipyard had been, pale as bones in the green light. Someone had planted a garden in the shell of the old chapel—not a shrine, she noted, just vegetables, though she supposed in Duskhollow the distinction had always been thinner than the Church liked. Smoke rose from chimneys. Children—actual children, two of them, clan-kin from Winterhold—were chasing a dog between the stacks of lumber.

And there, at the center of it, directing the raising of a wall with a charcoal stub behind her ear and ink to the second knuckle, stood Oscar Dravenmoor.

She looked different. That was the first thing Seraphine noticed, and it annoyed her, how quickly she noticed it. The woman she had hunted across three provinces had always seemed coiled, watchful, a person perpetually braced for the door to be kicked in. This one stood loose. When a beam slipped and the workmen shouted, she laughed—an unguarded, undignified laugh—and waved them back to it. There was a faded scar on her forearm where the Trial of Embers had marked her, and she wasn't hiding it under her sleeve.

Cassian Vale appeared beside her carrying a plank, said something that made her shove him, and Seraphine watched the fallen priest grin like a man who had forgotten he was allowed to.

Well. Someone had to ruin it.

"I need a word," she said, "before I lose my nerve."

The effect was immediate and satisfying. Cassian dropped the plank on his own foot. Two of the twins—Ilsa's sister Petra, and for one horrible second Seraphine's chest seized because she'd thought the dead twin lived—came around the corner with knives already in their hands, casual as drawing breath. And Oscar turned, and the warmth went out of her face by degrees, replaced by something older and harder, and Seraphine understood that this was the face the maps had been drawn to hide.

"Inquisitor." Oscar set down her charcoal. "You're a long way from your pyres."

"Ex-Inquisitor. As of roughly an hour ago, when I decided not to report to my regional superior and instead to commit treason." She held up both hands, palms out, the way you'd calm a horse—or show a court you carried no weapon. "I'm not here to arrest anyone. I'm here to say goodbye. And to give you something."

"You gave us enough on the mountain," Cassian said slowly. He hadn't picked up the plank. His eyes hadn't left her hands. "The fragment. The warning about Voss's riders."

"The fragment isn't yours to keep." She unslung the leather case. Her fingers hesitated on the buckle—one last moment of holding the thing that had defined her, the piece of the covenant seal the Church had hoarded for three centuries, proof that the binding had been made and therefore could be remade. "Neither is mine. It belongs to the covenant, and the covenant apparently has a keeper again, even if she resigned." She crossed the mud and held it out. "Oscar Dravenmoor. Last heir of the Ember Covenant. Keeper of the reforged seal."

Oscar didn't take it at once. She looked at the case, then at Seraphine's face, with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and whatever she saw there made her reach out slowly and accept it like accepting a surrendered blade.

"Why?" she asked simply. "You could sell this back to them. Buy yourself out of whatever's coming."

"They'd melt it down within the year and pretend it never existed. And I'd have to live in the same world as the pretending." Seraphine heard her own voice go flat and precise, the courtroom voice, and made herself soften it. "I'm going to Corvale. I'm going to stand in front of the Tribunal of Nine and read aloud the order Voss signed, and present the severed rites we found at the third shrine, and name every village I burned in service of a lie. They will strip my rank, my name, probably my freedom. Possibly my life. I've made peace with the probabilities. What I haven't made peace with is doing nothing, and I've discovered recently that I'm incapable of it. You've infected me. I hope you're proud."

"We're devastated," said Cassian, but softly, and without malice.

Petra had not put her knife away. "Ilsa died running from your people," she said. "Mother Quill died in your people's question-room. Why should any of us care what happens to you in Corvale?"

"We shouldn't," Seraphine said. "That's rather the point of justice, Mistress Petra. It isn't supposed to make the injured party feel better. It's supposed to make the record true. Your sister and Mother Quill are in the record now. Their names will be in my testimony. Spelled correctly. I checked the spelling twice." She saw the knife-hand waver, just slightly, and did not press. Mercy was not hers to ask for anymore; she had learned that much on the mountain. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm returning property and delivering notice. When the tribunal finishes with me—if anything of me is left to finish with—they'll know the covenant stands reforged, and they may send someone worse than me to test it. Watch your roads. Trust the clans. And keep the child away from the lowlands until this is settled, one way or another."

"Wren stays in the mountains," Oscar said. It was not a request, and Seraphine nodded, because it was the first sensible thing anyone had said all day.

A silence settled, the long kind, full of things neither woman knew how to carry across the distance between them. Then Oscar did something Seraphine had not expected. She stepped closer—close enough that the twins tensed—and extended her hand. Ink-stained, scarred, steady.

"On the Weeping Stair," Oscar said, "you had a blade at my throat and an order in your pocket, and you hesitated. Three seconds. I've thought about those three seconds for weeks. I thought it was doubt, and doubt fails, so I hated you for it." Her mouth curved, dry. "It wasn't doubt. It was you deciding, ahead of schedule, what you're about to spend the rest of your life paying for. So. For the three seconds. For the fragment. For spelling Ilsa's name correctly." She took Seraphine's hand and shook it, once, firmly, like a merchant closing a fair bargain. "We are not friends, Inquisitor. But we are something. Equals, maybe. The world doesn't have many of those left."

"Locke," Seraphine corrected quietly. "If I'm to be destroyed, I'd like it done to my plain name. Seraphine Locke. No title."

"Seraphine Locke, then." Oscar released her hand. "Come back in autumn. If Corvale hasn't eaten you. We'll have a roof on the chapel by then, and Cassian makes a wine that tastes like penance, which you'll appreciate."

"It's a terrible wine," Cassian confirmed.

"I'll consider it," Seraphine said, and found, to her private horror, that she was smiling. Just slightly. Just at the edges. The last time she had smiled without checking who was watching had been before her ordination, and she had not survived the checking.

She turned and walked out of Duskhollow without looking back, because looking back was how you turned a departure into a scene, and she had used up her allowance of scenes for one lifetime. Behind her she heard the hammering resume, and a child shriek with laughter, and Cassian Vale say something that made the mapmaker laugh again—that same unguarded laugh—and she catalogued the sounds the way she had once catalogued evidence, and carried them with her down the valley road.

* * *

Corvale at dusk was exactly as she remembered it, which was the cruelest thing about it.

The white walls caught the last light and threw it back gold, the way they had when she was nineteen and arriving with a trunk of books and a head full of certainty. The bells rang the hour of vespers in the old pattern, seven notes descending, *the gods are bound, the world is safe, sleep soundly.* She had loved those bells. She had wept at her ordination hearing them, believing every note.

Now she stood outside the gate she had entered a hundred times and understood that the bells were a lullaby the singers themselves half-believed, and that the peace they promised had been purchased with a theft so large the thieves had forgotten it was a theft.

The gate guards knew her. Of course they did; she had trained two of them. They saluted. She saluted back, and the familiarity of it nearly undid her—how easy it would be to walk in, report to her quarters, bathe, sleep, and in the morning file some sanitized version of events that preserved everyone including herself. The temptation arrived fully formed, articulate, reasonable, wearing her own voice. *Partial testimony. Name no names. Let the reforms come slowly, from inside.*

She had watched "slowly, from inside" operate for ten years. It operated beautifully. It operated like a millstone.

"Is the Grand Inquisitor in residence?" she asked the gate sergeant.

"Aye, Commander. He's asked after you twice this week. Said you'd report the moment you came down from the mountain." The sergeant frowned faintly. "There was... an incident message. Something about the third shrine. And casualties."

"There was," Seraphine said. "I'll report on all of it tonight. To the Tribunal. Not to him."

The sergeant's frown deepened, and she watched him decide—visibly, in real time—not to think about what she'd just said, because thinking about it would cost him, and the walls of Corvale ran on the currency of not-thinking. She had been rich in that coin herself. She was bankrupt now, and it was astonishing how light she felt.

The Tribunal chamber occupied the oldest hall in the Seminary, beneath the reliquary, a round room of black stone where the Nine sat in a crescent and petitioners stood alone on a worn flagstone in the center. She had stood on that stone twice in her career, presenting evidence against heretics. She knew exactly how the acoustics worked: every word spoken on the stone reached all nine seats with perfect clarity, and nothing the Nine said to each other could be heard below. The architecture of judgment. You stood in the light of ninefold attention and waited to learn if you still existed.

She bathed. She dressed in her formal grays, stripped of insignia—she removed the rank-pins herself, one by one, and lined them up on the washstand like little silver corpses. She ate a full meal, because fainting on the stone would serve no one. And then, in the hour before the tribunal convened, she sat in the empty scriptorium with her testimony and read it through one final time, and added a line she had not planned:

*I ask the Tribunal to understand that I do not offer this testimony in hope of mercy. I offer it because the record must be true. Judge me by everything in it, including the fires I lit. Especially those.*

The bells rang. She rose, squared her shoulders, and walked down through the reliquary dark toward the round black room, the pages steady in her hands, her heart going like a pilgrim's drum.

On the threshold she paused. Somewhere above, the evening bells began their seven descending notes—*the gods are bound, the world is safe, sleep soundly*—and for the first time in her life Seraphine Locke heard them as what they were: a confession, played nightly, by men who hoped no one was listening.

She stepped onto the stone. The Nine turned toward her, nine faces in candlelight, and Anselm Voss among them, his scholar's hands folded, his expression mild and welcoming and utterly prepared.

"Commander Locke," said the Eldest. "You requested emergency audience. State your business."

Seraphine drew breath, and began: "My business is treason. Mine, and the Grand Inquisitor's. I have brought the evidence for both."

Above her, faint through ancient stone, the last bell-note died—and somewhere far to the north, in a valley rebuilding itself board by board, a woman with ink on her hands was lighting lamps against the coming night, and the ember on the horizon glowed steady, and waited.
