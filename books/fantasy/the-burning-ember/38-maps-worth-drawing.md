## 38. Maps Worth Drawing

The thaw came to Duskhollow the way forgiveness came to Cassian Vale—slowly, unevenly, and with a great deal of dripping.

He stood in the doorway of what had been Mother Quill's counting house, now half a home and half a workshop, and watched the snowmelt run silver down the gutted street. Three months ago this had been ash and silence. The Maw's passage through the lowlands, the Church's fires, the collapse of the eastern warehouses—Duskhollow had been a wound in the world. Now it was scaffolding and sawdust, the ring of hammers, Brannoc's one-eyed cursing carrying from the harbor where he was teaching three orphaned dockhands to read tide-tables. Smoke rose from chimneys that hadn't smoked since autumn. Someone—Petra, probably—had hung wind-chimes made of melted candle-stubs from the eaves of the rebuilt tavern, and they clinked in the morning air like small, patient bells.

Cassian had not prayed in four years. He found, these days, that he did something stranger instead: he noticed things. The chimes. The meltwater. The way light came through new glass and laid pale gold across the floorboards where Oscar sat cross-legged with her father's journal open beside her and a fresh sheet of vellum pinned under her hands.

She drew the way other people breathed.

He'd known cartographers before—the seminary had kept a map-room, all vellum and incense and careful distances measured by men who never walked the ground they charted. This was nothing like that. When Oscar drew, she didn't copy the world; she *listened* to it. Her pen moved in long confident strokes for the coastline of things she trusted, then went hesitant, almost tender, at the edges of places she hadn't been. And always, always, there were the blank spaces. She left them deliberately, like doors left open on purpose.

"You're staring," she said without looking up.

"I'm observing. It's different. It has theological weight."

That earned him a glance—one eyebrow arched over eyes the color of wet slate, ink smudged along her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back with dirty fingers. "Everything has theological weight with you."

"Only because I spent ten years learning to weigh nothing." He crossed the room and set down the two cups he carried. Tea, not wine. He still drank wine, but mornings belonged to tea now, bitter and black, the way she liked it. Small surrenders. He'd learned that love was mostly small surrenders, arranged end to end until they looked like a life. "Brannoc wants to know if you're drawing the harbor before or after we rebuild it, because apparently your map is already wrong twice."

"The map isn't wrong. The harbor is." She blew a strand of hair out of her face. "Tell him if he moves the breakwater again I'll draw it where it *should* be and let his pride suffer."

"I'll deliver it verbatim. He'll weep."

"He'll threaten you with the tide-tables."

"He will," Cassian agreed, and sat beside her on the floor, shoulder against hers, close enough to feel her warmth through the wool of his shirt. Four months ago he had taken a blade meant for her on the Weeping Stair, and the scar beneath his ribs still pulled when he laughed too hard. He thought of it sometimes as a signature—as though the world had written its name on him in the only language it knew, and the name meant *hers*.

Oscar set down her pen and flexed her fingers. In the window-light he could see the faint marks on her hands where the ember-glow had lived during the reforging—pale now, nearly gone, like scars from a fever dreamed rather than suffered. She had given away a godhood. He had watched her do it: had stood in that burning chamber beneath Thornfell while she poured ten generations of stolen divinity into the altar like water into thirsty ground, and the mountain had sighed, and the Maw had come apart into rain, and Wren had cried without knowing why.

And afterward, walking out into the grey dawn with soot in their lungs and Ilsa's name unspoken between them, Oscar had turned to him and said, "I don't know who I am when I'm not an heir."

"Neither do I," he'd said. "We'll find out together. Slowly. It's the only honest way."

They were finding out. It was slower than either of them expected and stranger than either of them feared.

"What are you working on?" he asked now, nodding at the vellum.

"Duskhollow. Properly, this time. Every smuggler route, every cellar, every hidden stair." Her mouth twisted wryly. "Ten years I kept those roads secret because secrets were armor. Now—" she tapped the blank margin, where she'd written in her small precise hand: *for the town archive, when there is one* —"now it feels like the secret was really just a gift nobody was allowed to open."

Cassian looked at the map and felt something turn over in his chest, quiet as a key in a lock.

* * *

Afternoons, they worked on the chapel.

It had been the first building the Church burned, back when Locke's Inquisitors swept the coast—a shrine, really, old and undenominational, dedicated to no god in particular but to the practice of kneeling. The villagers had wanted to tear down the charred frame entirely. Wren had argued against it, standing on a crate with all the ferocity of twelve years old and recently appointed First Witness of the new covenant, and somehow the child had won. So they rebuilt it instead, smaller, with wide windows, and no altar at all—just a hearth.

"A church with a fireplace," Brannoc had scoffed. "What do you burn in it?"

"Nothing," Wren said. "That's the point."

Cassian understood it better than anyone. He had been a priest of the Church of the Sundered Veil, once—robes, rites, the whole architecture of certainty—and he had learned in the worst possible classroom that the divine does not live in doctrine. It lived in the space between people. In a hand gripped in the dark. In a girl praying for rain and getting sunlight instead, and being grateful anyway.

So he swung a hammer alongside the dockhands and the twins' surviving sister-forger and the fishermen who'd drifted back to Duskhollow like gulls after a storm, and when his shoulders ached he sat on the chapel steps next to Oscar and let the spring sun work on his neck.

Today she came down from the counting house with the journal under her arm and sat closer than the steps required.

"I finished the Duskhollow map," she said.

"I know. I watched you finish it. You held the pen like it might bolt."

"It might have." She was quiet a moment. Below them, the harbor glittered, Brannoc's half-rebuilt ship riding at anchor—he'd gotten her off the rocks after all, or most of her, and named her *Quill's Revenge*, which everyone pretended not to find sentimental. "Cassian. Can I ask you something strange?"

"I collect strange questions. It's a hobby."

"When you were a priest. Did you ever imagine a life? Not heaven. A life. Ordinary. With someone."

He considered lying, out of habit, and discarded it, out of love. "Every day. That was the trouble with faith for me—it kept promising me eternity and I kept wanting Tuesday. A market day. Someone arguing with me about which bread to buy." He glanced at her. "Why?"

"Because I imagined one too. For ten years, in the margins of maps I never showed anyone. Little houses. A garden. I used to draw them in the corners and then scrape them off, because a Dravenmoor doesn't get corners." She laughed, short and unsteady. "Except I'm not a Dravenmoor anymore. Not in the way that mattered. I gave it up. So the corners are—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't actually know how to want things, Cassian. Duty taught me how to need. Wanting is new territory. It's unmapped."

Something in his chest went very still and very warm.

"Then we survey it properly," he said. "Field notes. Redundant measurements. All my professional rigor applied to the problem."

"You were a priest, not a surveyor."

"I was a priest who ran away with smugglers. My rigor is flexible."

But she wasn't laughing. She was looking at her hands, at the fading ember-marks, and he realized with a lurch that she was afraid—not of the question she'd asked, but of the answer she wanted. Ten years of hiding, a season of war, a godhood surrendered, and this woman who had faced the Maw itself could not quite face a Tuesday.

So Cassian Vale, former priest, current carpenter's apprentice, man with a scar shaped like devotion under his ribs, reached into his coat pocket and took out what he had been carrying there for two weeks, waiting for courage or spring, whichever arrived first.

It was a map. Small, hand-drawn, badly lettered—his work, every line of it. It showed the valley around Duskhollow: the harbor, the chapel, the counting house, the road east. And in the corner, where Oscar put her little houses, he had drawn a house. Two rooms. A garden with absurd, lopsided rows labeled *herbs (hers)* and *things that refuse to grow (mine)*. And a path leading out of the garden, winding up toward the mountains, drawn in dotted line, the cartographer's mark for *unverified*.

At the top, in his terrible handwriting, he had titled it: *Proposed Expedition.*

Oscar stared at it for a long time. Long enough that he began, traitorously, to sweat.

"It's crooked," she said finally, voice thick.

"Yes."

"The scale is wrong. That hill is enormous."

"It felt enormous when I climbed it to see if the view was any good. Artistic license."

"And this path—" her finger traced the dotted line, up and up, toward the high country where the ember glowed steady on the horizon, where the new covenant hummed in the stone like a banked fire, "—this path goes nowhere."

"No," Cassian said. "It goes onward. There's a difference. I've learned it recently." He turned to face her fully. "I'm not asking you to stop mapping, Oscar. Gods, no—I've seen what happens when you try; you get twitchy and reorganize the toolshed. I'm asking you to stop mapping *alone*. Take me with you. Every blank space, every edge of the vellum, every place that needs a name. I want to be the fool holding the measuring chain while you argue with the horizon. I want Tuesdays and market days and expeditions with terrible provisions. I want—" his voice caught, and he let it, because hiding things was the old life, "—I want the ordinary miracle. With you. If you'll have me."

The wind-chimes clinked. Down in the harbor, Brannoc shouted something profane about rope. Spring light lay across the steps like a blessing from a god who no longer demanded anything.

Oscar looked at the crooked map, and then at him, and her eyes were bright and terrified and certain, all at once, the way they'd been in the burning heart of the mountain.

"Cassian Vale," she said, "your cartography is a disgrace."

"I know."

"We'll fix it. Together." She took the map from his hands with the reverence she usually reserved for her father's journal, and folded it with impossible care, and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart. Then she kissed him—ink-smudged and tea-bitter and laughing against his mouth—and the scar under his ribs pulled, and he didn't mind at all.

When they broke apart, breathless, she rested her forehead against his.

"There's one condition," she murmured.

"Name it."

"The blank spaces stay. On every map, forever. One region, unlabeled." She smiled, slow and real. "*For whatever comes next.*"

Cassian laughed—deep, unguarded, the laugh of a man whose prayers had finally been answered in a language he understood. "Deal," he said. "Though I warn you, I intend to fill some of it in."

"Not that part," she said. "Never that part."

Below them the harbor shone, the ship rocked, the chimes sang, and somewhere in the high passes the snow was melting off roads that no map had ever held—roads that would, come summer, hold two sets of footprints instead of one.

* * *

They told the crew at supper, which was to say Wren figured it out in eleven seconds and announced it to the entire tavern before Oscar had finished sitting down.

"Finally," the girl said, with the crushing authority of the newly ordained. "I've been *waiting*. Petra owes me a copper."

"You bet on us?" Oscar said.

"Everyone bet on you. Brannoc had money on midwinter. He's insufferable now."

"I am insufferable," Brannoc confirmed from the bar, raising his mug. "Took you both long enough. I've watched priests move faster, and they're trained to be miserable."

The tavern laughed, and the twins' memory was raised in the old way—three knocks on the table, one for each sister, silence for the one who wouldn't knock back—and then Mother Quill's chair, empty and honored at the head of the room, got its own knock from Wren, small and solemn, before the girl grinned and ruined it by demanding Cassian explain, in detail, whether he intended to marry Oscar or merely cohabitate sinfully, because as First Witness she had opinions about both.

"Wren," Cassian said gravely, "the covenant is three months old. Let's not overload it."

"The covenant can handle it. I asked."

Later, much later, when the fire had burned to embers that glowed steady and unhungry, and the tavern had emptied except for the two of them and the wind-chimes talking softly to the night, Oscar spread a fresh sheet of vellum on the bar and began to draw. Not Thornfell. Not the trade routes. Something new—coastlines she'd never surveyed, rivers guessed at from travelers' tales, a continent's rough dream taking shape under her pen.

Cassian watched her, chin on his hand, and felt the old hunger—for absolution, for certainty, for the silence of answered prayer—sit quiet in him at last, replaced by something sturdier. Not faith exactly. Company.

"What are you drawing?" he asked.

She didn't look up. But she smiled, and turned the vellum slightly so he could see the corner, where a tiny figure with a crooked staff stood beside a tiny figure with ink-stained hands, both of them facing a vast white space at the map's edge.

"That," she said, "depends entirely on who comes with me."

Outside, spring rain began to fall—ordinary rain, sweet and silent, asking nothing, naming no one—and it fell on Duskhollow, on the chapel with its hearth, on the ship called *Quill's Revenge*, on the mountain where the ember burned steady, and on all the country yet unmapped, waiting.
