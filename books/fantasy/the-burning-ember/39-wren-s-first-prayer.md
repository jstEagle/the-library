## 39. Wren's First Prayer

Wren had not prayed in a hundred and six days.

She knew the number the way she knew the scar on her palm—by counting, by touching, by refusing to let it blur. Every morning since the mountain's heart had opened and closed again, she woke in the loft above Mother Quill's rebuilt kitchen and lay still beneath the eaves, listening to the sounds of Duskhollow waking below her: Brannoc arguing with his new ship's timbers down at the waterline, Petra hammering the sign back onto the forge, the smell of bread that meant Ilsa was up before everyone, as always, because grief had not slowed her, only made her quieter.

A hundred and six days without prayer. And this morning, for no reason she could name—or every reason, all of them at once—Wren slid out from under the wool blanket, padded barefoot across the cold boards, knelt at the little window where the frost had melted into a lace of water, and folded her hands.

She did not close her eyes. She had learned that much.

*Thank you,* she thought, and then said it aloud, because the old gods—and the new arrangement, whatever Oscar called it—had never cared much for silence. "Thank you."

That was all. No rain requested. No names asked for. No bargains struck in the desperate arithmetic of a child who had once believed that if she only wanted hard enough, the sky would bend. Just the two words, small and plain as river stones, offered up to a heaven she had helped remake and still did not entirely understand.

The sky answered with sunlight.

Ordinary sunlight—the kind that came every clear morning over Thornfell, the kind that gilded the smoke rising from forty chimneys and caught the frost on the fence rails and made the whole grey valley look briefly, generously beautiful. It came through the window and lay across Wren's hands like something warm and weightless set there deliberately, and it whispered nothing, named nothing, demanded nothing.

Wren laughed.

It startled her, the sound—rusty, too loud in the empty loft, half sob at the edges. She pressed both hands over her mouth and laughed again anyway, because it was *ordinary*, do you see, it was only the sun doing what the sun had always done, and after black rain that spoke in dead men's voices, after a brand that burned like a coal sewn under her skin, after gods wearing the faces of eaten miners and a hunger in the dark beneath the world—after all of that, the miracle was that nothing miraculous happened at all.

"Only you," she told the sunlight, wiping her eyes with her sleeve, "could make me cry by being boring."

Below, Ilsa's voice floated up through the floorboards. "Wren! Bread's out and if you don't come down, Petra gets your share, and you know she will, she has no honor—"

"I have *tremendous* honor," Petra's voice said, closer than it should have been, which meant she was already standing at the foot of the stairs.

Wren pulled on her boots and went down to breakfast, and the sunlight followed her the whole way like a patient dog.

* * *

The kitchen of Mother Quill's house had been rebuilt twice now—once after the fire that took the haven's eastern row, and once, more carefully, after everything, when the whole crew had come back to Duskhollow with ash in their hair and holes in their number and decided, without anyone saying it aloud, that they would rebuild the place exactly as it had been, because some things deserved to survive unchanged.

So the table was still scarred with knife-marks and candle-burns. The shelf above the hearth still held Quill's chipped blue jug, which Brannoc refused to throw away even though it leaked. And the fourth chair on the left side—the one that had been Ilsa's, that Ilsa had died defending on the Weeping Stair—still sat empty, though no one ever moved it to another spot. It simply stayed, and everyone stepped around its presence the way you step around a grave.

Wren took her usual seat beside Cassian, who was already there, as he always was, with his collarless priest's coat brushed clean and his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he would forget to drink until it went cold. He had stopped drinking anything stronger in the hundred days since the mountain. Not dramatically, not with speeches—he had simply stopped, the way a man stops flinching from an old wound once it finally closes. Some mornings Wren watched him reach for the bottle out of pure habit and then watch himself do it, and smile at his own hand as if it were a stubborn child, and pour tea instead.

"You're up early," he said.

"I had something to do."

His eyebrows rose, patient, asking without demanding. That was Cassian's whole art, Wren had come to understand. He could ask entire questions with his face alone.

"I prayed," she said.

The kitchen did not go silent—Petra kept needling Ilsa about the bread, Brannoc kept complaining that the new keel wood was cursed—but it went quiet in the way that mattered, the way a room holds its breath around a moment it knows is larger than it looks. Ilsa turned from the hearth. Even Brannoc's grumbling trailed off mid-syllable.

"And?" Cassian asked softly.

"The sun came up." Wren shrugged, and found her throat tight, and shrugged again to hide it. "Just the sun. Nothing whispered. Nothing burned. It was—" She searched for the word and found it sitting right there, obvious as bread. "*Nice.*"

Isla laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, the kind that had grief still living inside it—and came around the table and put her flour-dusted hand on top of Wren's head like a blessing, which maybe it was. "Nice," she repeated. "Three centuries of Sundering, a war between heaven and earth, and the girl ends it all and calls the result *nice*."

"It's the highest praise I've got."

Cassian was smiling, but behind the smile Wren saw the thing she always saw in him now: attention, real and total, the attention of a man who had spent years believing his prayers fell into a well with no bottom and had lately learned to listen again. "Do you know what you did just now?" he asked.

"Thanked the sky?"

"You prayed *without wanting anything*." He set down his cold tea. "Do you understand how rare that is? How rare it's always been? Most people pray the way drowning people grab at ropes. Fear, greed, desperation—that's the engine of nearly every prayer ever spoken. But gratitude—" He shook his head slowly. "Gratitude is what prayer looks like when it isn't afraid. When it trusts. That's the shape the new covenant wants, Wren. That's the shape Oscar bled to give it."

At the name, the room shifted—not darkly, but the way a room shifts when someone beloved enters the conversation. Oscar Dravenmoor. Who had walked into the heart of Thornfell carrying a godhood she'd been born to, and poured it out like water into thirsty ground, and come back up the smuggler roads three days later mortal to the bone, freckled, exhausted, and grinning like a woman who had finally set down a pack she'd carried since birth.

"She'll want to hear this," Ilsa said.

"She'll be here at midday," Wren said. "She promised to show me the survey maps of the lower valley." And then, because the words arrived before she could examine them, before fear could catch them and stuff them back down: "And I'm going to ask her to train me. Properly. As a witness."

The table went truly silent this time.

Brannoc set down his mug. His one eye fixed on her, fierce and appraising, the eye of a man who had lost a ship to save a child and never once regretted it and never once let her forget it either. "You're sure of that, little fish? After everything that's hunted you? You'd walk *toward* the veil-roads instead of away?"

Wren looked down at her palm. The brand was gone—had faded the moment the Maw dissolved into rain, had left nothing but a faint pale mark shaped like nothing at all, like a scar from a dream. But she could still feel where it had been, the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled.

"When the rain whispered names," she said quietly, "it whispered mine first. Before the village, before the Inquisitors, before any of you. Whatever's on the other side of the thin places knows my voice. It's known me since the beginning." She lifted her chin. "Oscar says the covenant holds now because it was made by choice instead of blood. Well—this is my choice. Somebody has to stand at the door between worlds and listen. Somebody has to be the witness, so the next time the gods stir, we hear it *before* towns start disappearing. I'd rather it be someone who's already been marked once and lived."

Nobody spoke. Then Petra, who rarely wasted words on sentiment, reached across the table and squeezed Wren's wrist once, hard, and let go.

"Well," Brannoc said gruffly, standing to fetch more bread he didn't need. "Someone'll have to teach you to read current-marks on the veil-tides, and that someone is me, because Cassian's sense of direction is a religious tragedy."

"It is not—"

"You got lost going to the privy in Winterhold. In a *straight corridor*."

And the argument rolled on, warm and loud and ordinary, and Wren sat in the middle of it eating bread in the sunlight, and thought: *this is what I was saved for. Not from—for.*

* * *

Oscar arrived at midday as promised, walking up from the south road with her map-case slung across her back and dust on her boots and that particular lightness in her step that Wren had learned to read as happiness. A decade of hiding had taught Oscar Dravenmoor to move like a shadow; the last hundred days had slowly, imperfectly, taught her to move like a person again. She still caught herself flattening against walls sometimes, still scanned rooftops out of habit. But she laughed easily now. That was the truest measure.

She found Wren waiting at the edge of the village green, where the survey stakes marked out the plots for the new houses, and where the old shrine to Vess had been rebuilt—smaller, humbler, just a stone basin and a wooden post, because the drowned goddess had asked for less and Oscar had insisted on giving her exactly that.

"You're glowing," Oscar said, dropping her case in the grass. "Either you're sick or something good happened."

"I prayed this morning."

Oscar went very still. Wren had seen her face do many things in the months since the Weeping Stair—grief at Ilsa's cairn, fury at Locke's testimony, wonder at the reforged altar—but this stillness was new. It was the stillness of a cartographer seeing a coastline match a map she'd drawn from rumor alone.

"And?"

"Just thanks. And the sun came up. Ordinary as anything." Wren grinned. "Cassian cried about it. Don't tell him I said so."

"He cries at everything now, it's very undignified." But Oscar's voice was thick, and she sat down heavily in the grass beside Wren, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. The green stretched out before them, dotted with foundation stones and lumber and children—actual children, two families had come down from the clans since midwinter—chasing each other around the half-built well.

"Can I ask you something?" Wren said finally.

"Always."

"What does it feel like? Being mortal. Now. After—" She gestured vaguely upward, toward the peaks, toward everything. "After holding all that."

Oscar considered it with the seriousness she gave to everything, turning a stick of charcoal over in her fingers. "Lighter," she said at last. "And heavier. Both at once. When I held the inheritance, I could feel every thread of the world—every prayer, every crack in the veil, every hungry thing in the dark. It was like being a map of everything and knowing nothing. Now I just know the things in front of me. This grass. Your face. Whether Cassian remembered to eat." She smiled crookedly. "Turns out the things in front of me are enough. More than enough. Turns out they were the point all along, and I was too busy being an heir to notice."

"That's why I want to train," Wren said, and her heart hammered, but she made herself say it plainly, the way Oscar said things. "As a witness. For the covenant. I know I'm young, and I know what's hunted me, and I know you'll want to say no and wrap me in wool and hide me in Duskhollow forever. But the veil is thinner where I've been touched—I can feel it, some mornings, like a draft under a door. And you can't stand watch over every thin place yourself. You rebuilt the covenant. Let me help keep it."

Oscar looked at her for a long time. Wren made herself hold the gaze, though every instinct honed by witch-hunts and torchlight screamed at her to look down, apologize, shrink.

Then Oscar laughed—not mocking, but bright and startled, and she scrubbed a hand through her short hair. "You know what my father's journal said? The last page, the one that started all of this? It said the covenant would fail because the bloodline thinned. One family, guarding heaven's door alone, generation after generation, until there wasn't enough left of them to hold it shut." She tapped Wren's knee with the charcoal. "You're offering to fix the actual mistake. Not blood. Choice. Witnesses who choose the work." Her eyes were shining. "Yes, Wren. Yes. Gods, yes. We start tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?" Wren yelped. "Why not—"

"Because today," Oscar said, pulling the map-case open with the flourish of a woman unveiling treasure, "you're going to help me survey the lower valley, and I'm going to teach you the first rule of cartography, which is also the first rule of witnessing: *write down what is actually there, not what you expect to find.* Everything else follows from that."

They worked through the afternoon, the two of them pacing the valley with measuring cords and charcoal and a slate, Wren learning to read the land the way she'd once learned to read weather for rain-signs. Twice, as the shadows lengthened, Wren felt it—that faint cool draft at the edge of perception, the whisper-thin place where the world's skin was still tender from the surgery done upon it. And twice, when she paused and listened, the thinness did not whisper or hunger or call her name.

It just breathed. Slow and vast and calm, like something enormous sleeping peacefully at last.

"It knows you're there," Oscar said quietly, watching her listen. "The other side. It remembers you."

"Is that bad?"

"No." Oscar's smile was soft and certain. "I think it's glad."

* * *

Evening came down purple and gold over Duskhollow. Lamplight bloomed window by window. Down at the shore, Brannoc's new ship—the *Second Ember*, because he claimed sentimentality was for people with two eyes—rode at anchor, and somewhere in Mother Quill's kitchen Ilsa was burning a second batch of bread and blaming the oven.

Wren climbed to her loft one more time before bed. At the window, she knelt, folded her hands, and offered up the day's second prayer—the last one she would say that night, and the first of thousands yet to come.

"Thank you," she said. "For the sun. For the bread. For all of them."

And far off beyond Thornfell's shoulder, low on the horizon where the world met the sky, the ember-light of the reforged altar glowed steady and warm—not hungry, not flickering, not calling—like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

Wren watched it for a long moment. Then she frowned slightly, tilted her head, and leaned closer to the glass.

Because—just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of sight—the ember had pulsed. Once. Gently. Almost like a heartbeat answering hers.

Almost like a knock at a door.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold pane, and waited to see if it would knock again.
