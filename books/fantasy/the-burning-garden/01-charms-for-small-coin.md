## 1. Charms for Small Coin

The trouble with selling charms in a city where magic was outlawed was that you had to sell them to people who wanted magic, and the people who wanted magic were, as a rule, the sort of people who got other people burned.

Ines Pemberton had learned this the hard way, which was the only way she ever learned anything, according to her late grandmother — a woman who had once tried to cure a wart with a spoken word and spent the rest of her life explaining the resulting scar to suspicious neighbors.

So on a bright, cold morning in Ashfall, Ines arranged her stall in Copperlane Market with the practiced care of a woman who had nothing to hide and everything to protect. Dried lavender bundles. River stones polished smooth and drilled for cords. Little clay bells that made no sound at all, which she sold as *silence charms* to new mothers of colicky infants, who bought them out of desperation and reported, weeks later, that they worked wonders. They worked wonders because the mothers believed they did, and because belief, unlike swordspeech, was perfectly legal.

"Morning, Mistress Pemberton," called Old Sable from the fish stall two down, already elbow-deep in herring. "You've got a face like a wet Tuesday."

"It's my market face," Ines said. "It's very popular."

"You could smile now and then. Might sell more."

"I sell more when I look mysterious. It's a whole strategy. You wouldn't understand; your product smells."

Sable laughed, a great foghorn of a laugh that scattered pigeons off the awning above, and Ines let herself have one small, private smile while nobody important was looking. This was the trick of it, she had learned over seven years: you could be warm, you could be funny, you could be beloved by half of Copperlane Market — so long as you were never, not for one single breath, *interesting*.

Interesting got you noticed. Noticed got you questions. Questions got you a visit from the king's witch-hunters, and witch-hunters did not believe in silence charms or colicky infants or harmless old women with river stones. Witch-hunters believed in pyres.

She tied back her hair — brown, unremarkable, chosen for its unremarkableness — and set out her morning stock. The lavender went front and center. The stones went in their shallow tray. The clay bells she hung on strings where they could catch the light without catching attention. And beneath the counter, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false-bottomed box that had once held buttons, she kept the only thing in her stall that was real: a short sword, plain-hilted, unadorned, that she had not drawn in seven years.

She didn't think about the sword. She thought about the sword constantly, the way a man with a stone in his boot thinks about anything else.

The first customer came before the church bells finished ringing terce: a boy of perhaps ten, all elbows and freckles, clutching three coppers in a fist gone white with determination.

"For my gran," he announced. "She can't sleep. Da says it's her heart but Gran says it's the ghost of her second husband, who was a miserable man alive and is worse dead."

Ines nodded gravely. "Miserable men do make difficult ghosts. What you want is a dream-stone." She selected a gray pebble worth roughly nothing and held it up to the light as though inspecting a jewel. "This one's been soaked in moonwater for nine nights. See how it holds the cold? The ghost will find it very comfortable and move in there instead of your gran's room."

"Move *in* it?"

"Ghosts are like cats. Give them somewhere better to sit and they'll take it."

The boy considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. "Gran has a cat. Will they fight?"

"Not if you introduce them properly. Put the stone under her pillow and tell the cat it's been promoted."

He paid his three coppers with the solemnity of a man closing a business deal and ran off toward home, and Ines felt the small warm ache she always felt after these transactions — the ache of being useful at something that wasn't what she was actually for.

Because here was the truth she carried under her ribs like a swallowed coal: Ines Pemberton could speak a word into a blade and make it sing through armor, through stone, through the lies men told themselves about their own courage. She had been trained for it since she was six years old. She had sworn an oath to it at seventeen. She had watched nearly everyone who shared that oath die for it at twenty-three, on a night she still refused to remember in full, and she had buried her voice so deep afterward that some mornings she woke with her throat aching from holding it shut in her sleep.

Seven years. Seven years of lavender and river stones and clay bells that didn't ring. Seven years of being *nobody*, diligently, every single day.

It was working. That was the worst part. It was working beautifully, and she was so tired.

* * *

Midmorning brought the regulars. Widow Marsh, who bought a luck-charm every month and had survived eleven years of spectacular misfortune anyway, and who stayed to gossip long enough that Ines learned everything happening within four streets. A tanner's apprentice wanting a charm against burns, which Ines supplied along with actual practical advice about leather gloves, because she was not a monster. Two young women who lingered over the love-charms — dried rosemary tied with red thread, guaranteed to do absolutely nothing — until one of them finally bought one and the other turned pink to the ears, and Ines pretended very hard not to notice which way the charm was pointed.

It was, in short, an ordinary day. Ines had learned to treasure ordinary days the way sailors treasure calm water: knowing full well what was underneath, and choosing, for now, to enjoy the light on the surface.

She was wrapping rosemary for a nervous baker when the market changed.

It didn't change all at once. Markets never do. It changed the way weather changes — a stillness first, a wrongness in the noise, the particular quality of hush that falls when a hundred people notice the same thing at slightly different speeds. The baker stopped mid-sentence. Down the row, Sable's laugh cut off. Somewhere a cart wheel squeaked on and on, absurdly cheerful, the only sound left moving.

Then Ines heard the boots.

Not running. Marching. Evenly spaced, deliberately audible, the boots of people who wanted to be heard coming. Six pairs, maybe eight. And threaded through the boot-sound, thin and bright as wire, a bell — the small handbell of the Office of Purity, rung three times, the signal every child in Ashfall knew the way they knew their own names.

*Raid.*

Ines's hands did not shake. Her hands never shook; that had been trained into her too, though by different teachers than the ones who'd taught her to speak steel. She finished tying the baker's parcel with steady fingers, smiled, said, "There you are — keep the gloves on when the ovens are hot," and took his coin, and only when he had walked away did she allow herself one slow breath and one rapid inventory.

Stall: clean. Lavender, stones, bells, rosemary, luck-knots. Nothing that any inspector could call unlawful, because none of it was. False-bottomed box: locked, key on the cord around her neck, tucked under her shirt where it always lived. Sword: in the box, invisible, a fact she would be hanged for if anyone found it — not because owning a sword was illegal, but because of what she was, and what the sword would say about her, and what her voice would confirm.

Her voice. She pressed her lips together briefly, an old habit, a door checked twice.

Down the lane, they came around the corner of the chandler's shop, and the market seemed to lean away from them like grass before wind.

Witch-hunters. Four of them in the ash-gray livery of the Office of Purity, faces hidden behind the featureless half-masks that had come into fashion three years ago — smooth porcelain things with no eyeholes anyone could see, which everyone agreed must be enchanted, and which the Office neither confirmed nor denied, because fear did half their work for free. Behind them walked the clerk with his ledger and his inkhorn, and behind the clerk, unhurried, hands folded at his back like a man strolling in a garden, came Captain Vess.

Ines had seen Vess twice before, from a distance, both times at burnings. He was not what the ballads promised. No black cloak, no cruel sneer. He was a tidy, middle-aged man with a scholar's stoop and kind, tired eyes, and this was precisely what made him terrifying: Captain Vess looked like a schoolmaster. He looked like someone who would help you up if you fell. He had, by reputation, personally examined over two hundred suspected practitioners, and he had wept at more than one execution, and he had never once found a suspect innocent. Kindness, in Vess, was just thoroughness wearing a softer coat.

He stopped at the mouth of Copperlane Lane and rang the bell himself, three times, almost gently.

"Citizens of Ashfall," he said, and his voice carried the way quiet voices carry when everyone is straining to hear them. "By order of the Office of Purity, this market will be searched for unlawful craft. Your cooperation is noted and appreciated. Those with nothing to hide have nothing to fear."

*Those with nothing to hide have nothing to fear.* Ines had heard the sentence so many times in seven years that it had worn smooth as a river stone, and like a river stone it had no grip left in it at all. Because she had plenty to hide, and she feared plenty, and the sentence was designed — she understood this now, had always understood it — to make the hiding itself feel like guilt. To make concealment feel like confession waiting to happen.

Around her, the market performed its familiar choreography. Stallholders straightened their goods. Mothers gathered children close. Old Sable wiped his hands on his apron and stood very still beside his herring, wearing the expression of a man who hoped fervently that fish were beyond suspicion. Everyone became, simultaneously and without discussion, the most innocent version of themselves.

Ines joined them. She lifted her chin, arranged her features into polite, faintly bored cooperation — the face of a woman whose biggest worry today was whether the lavender would sell before it dried out — and began, methodically, to put her smaller valuables into the lockbox under the counter. Not hiding anything. Tidying. There was a difference, and the difference was survival.

The hunters split into pairs and moved down the rows. She watched them work with the detached expertise of long practice. One of each pair carried the ledger-clerk's lists; the other carried a rod of pale wood, longer than a forearm, capped with dull iron. Everyone in Ashfall knew what the rods were for, though officially they were "verification instruments." They hummed near active craft. They hummed near residual craft. They hummed, occasionally, near nothing at all, which was how innocent people ended up in ledgers.

They hummed, Ines knew from a source she did not permit herself to think about, near oath-bound voices. Faintly. Like a fly in another room.

Which meant that today, as on every day for seven years, her life depended on a piece of wood noticing nothing.

The first pair reached Sable. The rod passed over herring, over barrels, over Sable's own broad and blameless self. Nothing. Sable exhaled with a sound like a bellows and immediately pretended he hadn't. The clerk wrote something anyway — they always wrote something — and moved on.

The second pair worked toward her. Ines counted customers. Three stalls between them and hers. Two. She picked up her cloth and began folding it, unhurried, a woman closing up early because the morning had turned tedious. One.

"Mistress Pemberton."

She looked up with mild inquiry at the hunter standing before her stall. The mask gave nothing back. Its blankness was the point, she supposed — you couldn't read it, so you filled it with your own terror, and then you betrayed yourself.

"Good morning," she said. Her voice came out exactly right: level, pleasant, carrying the faint roughness of a shopkeeper's ordinary throat. Seven years of practice lived in that voice. Every word she spoke in public was a performance, and she had gotten so good she sometimes forgot she was performing, which was either mastery or a kind of dying, depending on the hour.

"Charm-seller," the hunter said. It wasn't a question. The clerk flipped his ledger. "Licensed?"

"Licensed and renewed, mistress. March last." Ines produced the paper from its oilcloth sleeve, holding it out flat, corners square. The license cost her a month's earnings every year and was, in her private opinion, a tax on fear. She paid it gladly. Fear-taxes were cheap compared to the alternative.

The hunter took the paper, held it upside down for a moment — a test, or a habit, or simple cruelty — then righted it and read. The clerk copied the number. The rod came up.

Here it was, then. The moment that happened every inspection and never got easier: the moment Ines Pemberton stood perfectly still and let a stick decide whether she deserved to live.

The rod passed over the lavender. Over the stones. Over the little clay bells, which caught the light and did not ring. It passed over her hands, and Ines thought, as she always thought, of the word for *cut*, and of the word for *hold*, and of the word for *come home*, and she kept all of them behind her teeth where they had lived for seven years, a hoard of fire swallowed whole.

The rod hummed.

Faintly. So faintly that only she heard it — or told herself only she heard it, which amounted to the same thing. A fly in another room. The hunter paused. Tilted the rod. Passed it again over the bells.

Nothing. Clay and string and air.

"Your wares are inert," the hunter said.

"That's rather the point of them," Ines said, and smiled, warm and rueful, the smile of a woman who had heard this joke about her own trade ten thousand times. "If they did anything, I'd be paying a great deal more for this license. Or hanging, I suppose. The Office does keep prices interesting."

Something that might have been amusement shifted behind the blank mask — or didn't. The hunter returned her license. "Keep your papers current."

"Always, mistress."

They moved on. Ines watched them go with her heartbeat loud in her ears and her face doing absolutely nothing at all, and she thought, not for the first time, that the exhausting part was not the danger. The exhausting part was that the danger passed, and then you had to stand back up and sell lavender to the next person, smiling, as though the world were a place where sticks decided things and you agreed with their decisions.

Across the lane, Captain Vess had stopped at Old Sable's stall. He was saying something, and Sable was laughing — actually laughing, the foghorn laugh, because Vess had that effect, because Vess was *nice*, gods rot him — and then Vess's gaze drifted, idly, the idle drift of a man looking at nothing, across the lane, and settled on Ines.

For three seconds — she counted them the way she counted everything, in the old battle-cadence, one-and-two-and-three — Captain Vess looked at her the way a scholar looks at a page he intends to reread.

Then he smiled, tipped his head, a small courteous acknowledgment between neighbors, and walked on.

Ines let out her breath slowly. Around her the market resumed its noise in stages, like a held chord releasing note by note. Sable called something to her about surviving another inspection. A child shrieked happily at pigeons. The baker's apprentice came back for a second charm, having forgotten which pocket he'd put his coin in.

Ordinary resumed. Ordinary was a garment she wore, and it fit, and it was warm, and under it her skin remembered a different country.

She should have felt relief. Mostly she did. But under the relief, patient as groundwater, sat the thing she had been refusing to name all morning, the thing that had made her grandmother's face come to her at dawn: the sense — irrational, unprofessional, dangerous — that the stillness in the market today had not been entirely the hunters' doing. That the city itself had gone quiet around some approaching weight, the way animals go quiet before weather.

She was still telling herself she was imagining it when the screaming started.

It came from the far end of Copperlane, past the chandler's, past the well — a man's voice, ragged with pain and running out of strength, shouting words that scattered the market like sparks in dry straw:

"Witch-hunters! No — please — I need a speaker — *someone get me a speaker!*"

Every head in Copperlane turned. Every face went white. Because there was only one reason a man in Ashfall would shout for a speaker, only one thing a speaker could do that a priest or a surgeon or the law could not, and asking for it aloud, in public, in daylight, was not desperation. It was suicide.

And the man staggering around the corner of the chandler's shop, clutching his side where something dark spread between his fingers, was looking directly — desperately, unmistakably — at her.

At her stall. At her lavender and her river stones and her little silent bells. As if he knew. As if, somehow, impossibly, he knew exactly what she was.

Behind him, at the mouth of the lane, Captain Vess turned at the sound, and his kind, tired eyes began, with terrible patience, to search the crowd for whoever the dying man was calling to.
