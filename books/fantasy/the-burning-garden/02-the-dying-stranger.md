## 2. The Dying Stranger

The trouble with running a shop in Ashfall was that it required a door, and doors had a way of letting people in.

Ines Pemberton had learned this the hard way over seven years of selling charms for small coin — charms that were, she wanted to be very clear about this, entirely harmless. Copper wire twisted into knots against headaches. River stones painted with sigils that were purely decorative, thank you, and if they happened to warm a cold pair of hands on a winter morning, well, that was just good paint. She had built an entire business out of things that did nothing at all, or nearly nothing, and she guarded that distinction the way other people guarded their children.

So when the man came through her door at half past nine on a grey morning, she was already annoyed before she saw the blood.

"Closed," she said automatically, then amended: "Open, but barely. If you're here about the headache-knots, I'm out of copper until Thursday."

The man did not answer. He took three steps into the shop, left a smear of red down the doorframe like a signature he'd never be able to deny, and then his legs simply stopped being interested in holding him up.

Ines caught him under the arms more by reflex than decision. He was heavier than he looked, the way dying men always are, as though the whole weight of them arrives all at once to make its point. She lowered him onto the floor between her workbench and the shelf of decorative river stones, which clinked gently above his head like a small, useless applause.

"Right," she said, kneeling beside him. "Let's have a look at you."

He was perhaps fifty, grey-bearded, dressed in the plain wool of a traveling clerk, except that clerks did not usually have three crossbow bolts fletched into their backs. Two in the shoulder, one lower, near the spine. The blood had soaked through everything and pooled beneath him in a spreading dark shape that Ines's eyes kept trying to read as a map, because her mind, traitorous thing, was already looking for exits.

"Hunters," the man said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a word either; it came out crushed flat, like something stepped on.

"I can see that." Ines pressed her palm flat against the wound nearest her and felt the heat of it, the terrible industry of a body working hard at losing. "Whoever shot you has excellent aim and no manners. Hold still."

She should not help him. That was the first thought, arriving with the punctuality of a tax collector. Helping a bleeding stranger in Ashfall was how respectable charm-sellers ended up answering questions from men in grey coats, and questions were the one thing Ines Pemberton could not afford. Seven years of careful nothing — seven years of a voice kept small and a name kept smaller — and all of it could unravel over one act of ordinary decency.

Her hands were already tearing open his shirt anyway.

"You're a stubborn old fool," she told him, mostly to fill the silence, because silence in a room with a dying man had a way of getting ideas. "Coming through my door. Of all the doors in Ashfall. Do you know what I sell? Knots. Painted rocks. A tin of salve that is ninety percent goose fat and ten percent optimism. There is nothing in this shop worth bleeding on."

He laughed. It cost him; she felt the laugh move through his chest under her hands like a bird struggling in a sack.

"Goose fat," he managed. "That's — hah — that's the famous Pemberton wit, is it?"

Every hair on Ines's arms stood up and saluted.

"I don't know you," she said carefully.

"No." His eyes found hers. They were brown and clear and terribly calm, the calm of a man who had done his arithmetic and gotten a number he didn't like. "No, you don't. But I know your shop. Knew it would be you. Had to be someone who knows how to keep quiet, and there's no one quieter than you, mistress. No one."

"That's slander," said Ines. "I chat constantly. Ask anyone."

Boots sounded in the street outside — several pairs, moving fast, unhurried in the particular way of people who know exactly where they're going. Ines went cold from the scalp down. She blew out the lamp on her workbench, crossed to the window in four steps, and eased the shutter closed until only a finger's width of grey light remained.

Through the gap she saw them: six figures in the king's grey-and-iron, moving down Candlemaker's Row with the loose-legged stride of men who had been walking since dawn. Witch-hunters. Not the street patrols who shook down unlicensed sellers and moved on — these wore the long coats and the white cords at the wrist, and they carried their crossbows slung but close, the way a man carries a tool he expects to use before lunch.

At their head walked a woman with iron-grey hair bound back so tight it looked painted on. Ines knew her by sight the way everyone in Ashfall knew her by sight: Captain Vess, whose convictions were rumored to be load-bearing.

They stopped three shops down, in front of Old Marta's candle stall, and began asking questions with the pleasant menace of men reading from a list.

"They're checking the Row," Ines breathed. "Door to door. We have minutes, maybe."

"Then listen." The man's hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that startled her, given what the rest of him was doing. "Listen, because I haven't got time to say it twice and you haven't got time to pretend you don't want to hear it."

"I genuinely don't—"

"Wren Calloway." He said the name like a key turning in a lock. "My daughter. Her soul was sold. Beneath the mountain, to the court of the Pale King, and she is fading, do you understand me? Fading like a lamp with no oil. And I am the last person who knows where the ledger-vault stands, and I am—" he looked down at himself, at the bolts, with mild academic interest, "—evidently not going to make it to the mountain."

Ines's mouth had gone dry. "You've mistaken me for someone who helps people."

"I've mistaken you for nothing." His grip tightened. "I chose you. Out of every door on this street, I chose the one with the woman behind it who flinches at the word *magic* like it's a thrown stone. Only two kinds of people flinch like that. The ones who hate it, and the ones who are hiding it. You don't have the face of a hater. Haters enjoy themselves."

Outside, the hunters had finished with Marta's stall. One of them laughed at something. The sound carried through the shutter gap with horrible clarity, warm and human, the laugh of a man having a fine morning.

* * *

There is a particular quality to the moments before a life changes, Ines thought later, and the quality is this: they feel exactly like all the other moments. The dust motes turned in the finger of light from the shutter. A kettle ticked as it cooled on the stove. Somewhere below, the river smell of Ashfall crept up through the floorboards, tar and fish and wet stone, the city's honest stink. Nothing announced itself. No bell rang. The world simply went on being ordinary right up until it wasn't, and then it was too late to object.

The man had let go of her wrist and was working at his own coat with fingers that no longer fully obeyed him. Ines watched him fight the buttons and finally leaned in to help, because apparently her hands had defected to his cause without consulting her.

From inside the coat lining he drew a folded square of oilcloth, tied with waxed string, stained dark along one edge where blood had soaked through. He pressed it into her palms and folded her fingers over it, both of his hands around hers, so that she held it the way you hold a bird — gently, and with the growing certainty that it was going to get away from you.

"The map," he said. "To the vault. Under the mountain, past the toll-bridge, down where the court keeps what it buys. Everything sold is written there. Everything taken is kept there." His breath whistled. "Wren Calloway. Say it back to me."

"This is insane," Ines whispered. "I'm a shopkeeper. I sell goose fat."

"Say it."

"...Wren Calloway."

"Good girl." Something in his face loosened, and for a moment he looked less like a dying clerk and more like a father, which was infinitely worse. "She's nineteen. She used to sing while she swept. Did you know that? You can't know a person from a name, but — she sang. And now she sits by the window and watches the road and doesn't sleep, because whatever's left of her knows something's missing and can't remember what. My wife—" he stopped, and started again, and stopped again, and what he finally said was: "It was her mother. Who sold her. But that's not yours to carry today. Today you carry the map."

"I can't carry anything," Ines said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, but you don't understand what you're asking. If they find that on me — if anyone even suspects — there's a stake on the green outside the walls with my—"

"With everyone's name on it eventually." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he was smaller somehow, as though the cough had taken a layer of him with it. "I ran for eleven days, mistress. Eleven days on a bolt-hole leg through three towns, and do you know what I learned? There is nowhere to run *to*. There's only running toward. And I ran out of toward."

The boots outside had reached the next shop. Through the shutter gap Ines could see the grey coats now, close enough to count the buttons. Captain Vess stood in front of the tanner's doorway, saying something to the tanner's wife, and the wife's face was doing the thing faces did around Vess — trying to be blank and failing upward into terror.

"Please," the man said.

And there it was. The whole war of Ines Pemberton's life, conducted daily in miniature, fought again in a single syllable. *Please.* Seven years ago she had made a promise over the bodies of everyone she had loved, a promise written in ash and sealed in silence: *never again, never speak, never be known.* And promises like that don't bend. They hold, or they shatter, and either way they take your hands off.

But he was dying in her shop, and he had chosen her door, and some treacherous part of her — the part that had once been young and armored and certain — whispered that doors are chosen for reasons.

"I won't promise you anything," she said. "Do you hear me? I'll take it. I won't promise."

"Nobody ever does," he said, and smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it forgave her in advance. "That's why I picked the one who might."

* * *

He died eleven minutes later. She counted, because counting was something to do with her mind while her hands did nothing useful at all, which was the whole story of her life in one sentence.

For those eleven minutes she did everything a person could do without magic. She packed the wounds with clean linen. She propped him against the workbench. She got water into him, most of which came back out. She talked to him, nonsense mostly — about the weather, about the price of copper, about how her salve really was mostly goose fat and he shouldn't believe the label — and he drifted in and out and smiled at the parts about the goose fat, and somewhere in the middle of it he said, clearly, as though reporting it to a clerk of record:

"She'll know. Wren will know someone came for her. That matters more than saving. Tell her that. Being sought after — that's the thing. Nobody should be unsought."

Then the boots arrived at her door, and the knocking began, and the man opened his eyes one last time with perfect clarity and said, "Back room. Now. I'll manage the conversation," which was the single bravest piece of nonsense Ines had ever heard, and she went, because there was no time to argue and because some small cowardly hopeful animal in her chest believed him.

She heard it all through the wall. The door opening. The pause. Vess's voice, level as a ruled line: *"A man was seen entering this premises. Bleeding."* And then the dying man — gods, the sheer gall of him — calling out in a cracked, carrying voice: *"Help! Help me, please, someone—"*

*"He came in wounded," Vess said, closer now, inside the shop. "Where did he go?"*

*"Gone," the man gasped, magnificently. "Out the back. Went for the aqueduct gate. Hurry, mistress, hurry, he's killing people—"*

Footsteps. The back door banging. Boots receding down the alley toward the aqueducts, six sets of them, chasing a ghost through a door that led only to Ines's coal cellar and a wall she'd bricked up herself years ago.

When she came out, he was still propped against the bench, and he was still smiling, and he was gone. Whatever animating stubbornness had held him together through eleven days of running had waited precisely long enough to spend itself on one last lie, and then, its accounts settled, had quietly closed the books.

Ines sat down on the floor next to him.

"Well," she said to the empty shop. Her voice came out rough and small. "Well, that was the stupidest brave thing I've ever seen, and I once knew a man who duelled a bear over a hat."

She sat for a long time. The kettle cooled. The dust turned. Outside, Ashfall went about its morning, buying bread, gossiping, none of it knowing that a man had died twelve feet from the street with a secret in his coat.

Eventually she unfolded the oilcloth.

Inside was a map, drawn in ink gone brown with age and dark red with newer history, on paper so thin she could see her thumb through it. It showed the mountain — not the little sugarloaf the city maps gave it, but the whole deep root of it, stairs and galleries descending page after folded page into the dark. Tunnels marked in a clerk's precise hand. A bridge marked with a warning in the same hand: *it takes a memory — decide beforehand what you can live without.* And at the bottom, circled twice, a chamber labeled in letters that had been traced over and over until the ink stood up from the page like scar tissue:

*THE VAULT OF SOLD VOICES.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost shy, as if the writer had hesitated: *Wren.*

Ines looked at it for a long moment. Then she stood, walked to her stove, and held the map over the coals.

The paper trembled in the heat. The ink began to shine. And Ines stood there with her arm outstretched and her jaw set and did absolutely nothing, for the better part of a minute, until her own reflection in the dark stove-front — a woman holding a burning thing and not burning it — made her step back with a curse.

"Oh, you *fool*," she told herself. "You absolute, sentimental, dead-woman-walking fool."

But her hands, traitors to the last, were already folding the map along its old creases, tucking it away inside her shirt, against her skin, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

She looked down at the dead man, and sighed, and fetched a sheet to cover him.

"Wren Calloway," she said quietly, testing the name, finding how it fit in a mouth trained for seven years to hold nothing at all. It fit like a splinter. It fit like a seed.

Somewhere across the city, presumably, a girl was sitting by a window watching a road.

Ines pulled on her coat, blew out the lamp she'd already blown out, and went to find out what a person was supposed to do with a soul-shaped debt she'd never agreed to owe.

The knock came at her door before her hand reached the latch.

Three slow raps. Patient. Unhurried. The knock of someone who had been watching the shop for some time, and who knew perfectly well that the hunters had just chased a ghost into the aqueducts.

Ines froze, one hand on the bolt, the map a hot brand against her ribs.

"Yes?" she called, in her smallest, safest voice.

A young man's voice answered through the wood — rough with travel and something harder underneath it.

"I know what he gave you," it said. "And I've come about my sister."
