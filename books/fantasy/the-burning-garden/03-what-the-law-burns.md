## 3. What the Law Burns

The bells began at dawn.

Ines had lived in Ashfall long enough to know the difference between the bells. There were the market bells, cheerful and greedy, rung at seven and noon to open and close trade. There were the alarm bells, ragged and urgent, for fires and floods and the occasional escaped bear from the menagerie. And then there were the law bells — slow, spaced, deliberate, each toll falling like a stone dropped down a well.

Three of those meant a burning.

She lay in her narrow bed above the shop and listened, counting. One. Two. Three. The third toll hung in the cold air of her room like a held breath, and then the city exhaled around her: shutters opening, carts creaking, the ordinary machinery of Ashfall grinding back into motion as though nothing were about to happen in Copperlane Square.

But something was always about to happen in Copperlane Square on a burning day. That was rather the point of it.

Ines dressed in the dark, fingers finding each garment by habit — the plain grey dress, the apron with its deep pockets, the plain brown scarf she wound twice around her throat. Not because her throat showed anything. Seven years of discipline had made sure of that. She wound it because it reminded her to be careful, the way a soldier might wear a token of a battle he'd survived. *You are a charm-seller,* the scarf said. *You are nobody. You sell luck knots and love charms that do nothing but smell nice.*

She went downstairs and did not look at the drawer beneath the counter where the map lay folded inside a false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden under a nest of dried lavender she'd moved there at two in the morning when sleep refused her.

She looked at it anyway. Of course she looked at it. You could tell yourself not to look at a thing all you liked; your eyes went there the way water goes downhill.

*Later,* she told herself. *After the burning. After I've seen what they do, I'll burn the wretched thing myself and be done with it.*

It was a good plan. It was even, she suspected, a correct plan. She only wished plans didn't feel so different in the stomach than they did in the head.

* * *

Copperlane Square was already full by the time Ines arrived, and full was the wrong word for it. Packed. Pressed. The whole square gone soft and murmurous with bodies, breath steaming in the early cold, children hoisted onto shoulders, vendors — with the unkillable pragmatism of vendors everywhere — selling roasted chestnuts and hot cider to an audience assembled to watch a person die.

Ines bought a bag of chestnuts. She hated herself a little for it, and ate them anyway, because her hands needed something to do and fear made her hungry. This was, she had long ago decided, one of the body's cruelest jokes: that terror and appetite should live so close together in the same house.

The pyre stood in the center of the square, a squat black lattice of timber with a stake in its heart, and around it stood the witch-hunters in their grey-and-white livery, spears upright, faces set in expressions of professional boredom. Captain Vess herself was there — Ines knew her by sight now, though she'd never spoken to her and prayed daily she never would. Vess stood apart from her hunters the way a hawk stands apart from sparrows, still and watchful, her grey cloak pinned with the king's badge, her eyes doing slow circuits of the crowd.

Ines caught herself studying those eyes and immediately stopped. That was the trouble with watching watchers: they noticed being noticed. She turned her attention instead to the chestnut seller, who was telling anyone who'd listen that his wife's cousin had once seen the condemned woman in the flesh and she'd seemed *perfectly normal*, which in Ashfall was either reassurance or gossip depending on how you took it.

The condemned woman was brought out at mid-morning.

Her name, the crier announced, was Maren Ostwick. She was forty-one years old. Her crime was the sale of enchanted goods — specifically, a lodestone ring said to find lost things, three love philtres, and a "speaking mirror" that had answered questions put to it.

Ines's chestnut went cold in her hand.

A speaking mirror. She had sold trinkets beside a woman who kept a speaking mirror. Had they ever shared a stall? Had Maren Ostwick ever bought lavender from her? Ashfall was a big city but the charm-trade was a small world, everyone knowing everyone, prices and suppliers and little tricks of the craft passed along like recipes. Ines searched her memory with the frantic thoroughness of a woman checking her pockets after hearing a thief is loose, and found — nothing certain. A round-faced woman, maybe. A stall two rows over at the autumn fair, maybe. Maybe. Maybe was enough to kill you here.

Maren Ostwick was made to climb the steps of the pyre. She climbed them slowly, and Ines watched the crowd's face change as she did, because up close, in the flesh, without the distance of rumor, the condemned stopped being a story and became a person again. Maren was small. Her hair was going grey at the temples. Her hands shook, and someone in the crowd — a woman near the front, voice cracking — called out, "It was only a ring! It was only a ring!"

A hunter moved toward the woman and thought better of it. The crowd was large today. Larger than usual. There was something in the air besides smoke.

Then Maren Ostwick did a thing Ines would think about for the rest of her life.

She turned, at the top of the steps, and faced the crowd. And she smiled. Not bravely, not defiantly — or not only. It was the smile of a woman greeting old friends across a market stall, tired and warm and faintly amused, as if to say, *Well. Here we all are. Terrible weather for it.*

And then she spoke.

"I never cheated anyone," she said, and her voice carried, thin and clear. "Everything I sold worked exactly as well as I said it would."

A ripple went through the crowd — a laugh, half horror, half something else. Because it was true, wasn't it? Everyone knew it was true. Half the charms in Ashfall were dried flowers and wishful thinking, and everyone buying them knew it too, and loved them anyway, because people need small magics even when they're only the shape of magic. The law didn't burn Maren Ostwick for fraud. The law burned her for the possibility. For the maybe. For the shadow of a thing that might have been real.

Ines felt the truth of it land in her own body like a dropped stone. *They don't know what I am,* she thought. *They're burning her for what I actually am.*

The torch went to the kindling. The crowd made its sound — that low collective noise, not quite a gasp, not quite a groan, the sound of several hundred people trying not to make a sound. Smoke rose white first, then grey, then black. Maren Ostwick did not scream. Ines would carry that fact around with her forever, turning it over: she did not scream. Whether that was courage or shock or some mercy of the body, Ines couldn't say. She only knew that she watched until her eyes streamed and she could no longer distinguish the smoke from her own tears, and that she did not look away, because looking away felt like a luxury purchased with someone else's blood.

*This is what waits for me.* Not if she spoke. Not if she fought. If she was merely *found*. If a hunter pulled open the wrong drawer and found oilcloth and old paper and asked one question she couldn't answer safely.

The bells rang the closing toll. The crowd dispersed with the terrible ordinariness of crowds, resuming conversations, retrieving children, buying more chestnuts. Within an hour the square would be swept and by evening there'd be a market in it again, because Ashfall digested its burnings the way a river digests stones.

Ines walked home slowly through streets that smelled of woodsmoke everywhere, which was the cruelest part — every hearth-fire in the city suddenly indistinguishable from the pyre.

* * *

Back in the shop, she locked the door. Then she locked it again out of pure nerves, which accomplished nothing, and stood in the middle of her own floor feeling like a stranger in it.

"Right," she said aloud, to nobody, because talking to yourself was one of the few freedoms of a concealed life. "Right."

She knelt at the counter, slid open the drawer, lifted out the lavender, pried up the false bottom, and took out the map.

Unwrapped, it was worse in daylight. The bloodstain had darkened to the color of old rust. The dead man's careful lines ran from Ashfall's outskirts down into the mountain country — mine roads, aqueduct channels, a route drawn by someone who knew the way and wanted it remembered. And at the end, circled twice, a mark she'd stared at until dawn: a small gate symbol, and beneath it, in a cramped hand, the words *where they keep them.*

Where they keep them. The souls. Wren Calloway's soul among them, hanging in whatever vault the Pale King's court used for such things, dimming year by year while a girl somewhere in the city slept badly and felt less of herself with every season.

Ines carried the map to the hearth, knelt before the cold ashes, and laid it among the char.

Then she sat there for a long time.

The flint was in her pocket. It was always in her pocket. She took it out. She struck it once against the steel — a bright, harmless shower of sparks, nothing catching — and stopped.

*Burn it,* said the sensible voice, the survivor's voice, the voice that had kept her alive for seven years. *Burn it and go back to selling luck knots. You owe these people nothing. You never met the girl. The man chose you at random because you were the nearest door. Random isn't duty.*

"It isn't," Ines agreed, out loud, to the empty shop. "Random isn't duty."

She struck the flint again. Sparks. Nothing caught. The map lay in the ashes looking, frankly, comfortable, like a cat that had decided the hearth was its spot now.

The problem — and she saw it clearly, finally, with the awful clarity that comes after a burning — was Maren Ostwick's smile. *Everything I sold worked exactly as well as I said it would.* The problem was that Ines had spent seven years selling nothing, harmless nothing, pretty nothing, and calling it safety, and this morning she had watched a woman die for the crime of maybe, and the map in her hands was not a maybe. The map was a *yes*. Somewhere under the mountain a girl named Wren Calloway was being slowly erased, and Ines Pemberton was possibly — probably, if the dying man hadn't been mad — the only person in Ashfall holding the directions.

And underneath that, deeper, in the place she never let herself visit: the oath. *We speak so the silenced may be heard.* Seven years of silence had not dissolved the words. They had only buried them, and buried things grow, quietly, in the dark, like roots under a pavement cracking the stone.

"If I burn it," she said to the hearth, conversationally, "I'm not burning a map. I'm burning her." She paused. "That's dramatic. But it's also true, and I've decided I'm allowed to be dramatic in my own shop."

The map did not answer. Maps rarely do.

She tried a third time. Honest effort, flint and steel, sparks raining into the char — and her hand, entirely without consulting her, closed around the map and drew it back out of the ashes, and Ines sat back on her heels and laughed, a short, cracked, unhappy sound.

"Coward," she told herself.

"No," she answered, after a moment. "Worse. I'm *convinced*."

Because that was the thing fear had done to her over seven years, she realized — it hadn't made her timid. Timid would have been simpler. It had made her *certain*. Certain that hiding was right, certain that silence was virtue, certain that survival was the whole of wisdom. And certainty, she thought, watching the cold hearth, was just another kind of chain. Maren Ostwick had died free of it. Ines was alive inside it, and this morning she had finally felt the weight of the bars.

She refolded the map along its old creases, careful with the bloodstained corner, and wrapped it in the oilcloth, and put it back beneath the lavender — and then, on impulse, moved it. Not the drawer. Too obvious. She sewed it instead into the hem of her winter cloak, working quickly with a candle-end and a bent needle, her stitches neat as prayer, thinking: *if they take me, let it be on my body. Let it be something I chose to carry.*

When she finished, her hands were steady. That surprised her. All morning she had expected to come apart, and instead she felt the strange hard calm that had visited her only a handful of times in her life — before her oath, before the massacre, at the moments when a door closes behind you and there is nothing left to do but walk forward.

"I'll find another way to destroy it," she informed the shop at large. "Tomorrow. When I'm calmer. When I've thought it through properly."

Every word of it a lie, and she knew it, and the shop knew it, and the lavender knew it.

* * *

She opened the shop at noon, because life insists on itself, and sold a luck knot to a nervous bride, and a jar of dream-salve to a man with a cough he swore was nothing, and let an old widow tell her the whole story of the burning twice, adding details both times, none of them accurate.

It was nearly dusk, the light going blue at the windows, when the bell over the door rang and a young man came in off the street — tall, hollow-eyed, travel-stained, with a jaw like his sister's must have been and a look in his face that Ines recognized instantly, because she wore it herself most mornings.

He scanned the shop, found her behind the counter, and said, without preamble:

"You're the charm-seller. The one my father came to."

Ines's hands went very still on the counter, on either side of the drawer that no longer held anything at all.

"And you'd be?" she asked, mild as milk, while her heart attempted to climb out through her ears.

"Tam Calloway," he said. "And I want my sister's map."
