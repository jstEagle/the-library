## 4. Wren

The trouble with having no soul, Wren had decided somewhere around her fourteenth winter, was not the darkness. Everyone assumed it would be the darkness — the hollow feeling, the cold spot behind the ribs where something used to live. And yes, that was there. That was there all the time, like a toothache you stopped noticing until you bit down wrong.

No, the real trouble was the small things.

It was forgetting what bread tasted like. Not in the way of a bad memory — she could recite the taste of her mother's honey loaf if asked, could describe the crackle of the crust and the way the butter went glossy and sank in — but describing a thing, she had learned, was not the same as knowing it. It was like being handed a letter from someone you'd once loved very much. All the words were right. None of them were warm.

She sat at the kitchen table in the grey hour before dawn, because sleep had become a country she could see from a distance but never quite reach, and she watched her mother knead dough for the morning's loaves.

"You should be in bed," Mama said, without turning around. Mothers always knew.

"I slept."

"You didn't."

"I *rested*," Wren said, which was true in the way that a stone resting in a field is rested. "Mama, do you remember what lavender smells like?"

Her mother's hands stilled in the dough. Just for a moment. Then they moved again, folding and pressing, folding and pressing, the way they did when Mama was thinking hard about something she did not want to say.

"Of course I do, love."

"What does it smell like?"

Mama turned then. She had flour on her cheek and shadows under her eyes, and she looked at Wren the way she always looked at her now — like someone reading a letter that kept getting shorter. "Sweet," she said. "A little sharp. Like summer holding its breath." She smiled, and the smile almost reached her eyes. "Why?"

"No reason," said Wren, who had asked because she wanted to know whether the answer would make her feel anything, and it hadn't, and there was no kind way to say so.

* * *

Here is what the neighbors knew: the Calloways had fallen on hard times after Papa's lungs gave out, and Mrs. Calloway had worked miracles to keep the house, and the girl Wren had taken sick the same winter — a wasting sickness, a tiredness of the blood, poor thing, she sleeps all day and lies awake all night and eats like a bird, if birds ate out of politeness rather than hunger.

Here is what was true: on a night in late autumn, when the debt collectors had come twice and the third visit would have ended with the family in the street or worse, Mama had gone up the mountain. She had told the children she was going to pray at the shrine. Perhaps, in a way, she had been.

Wren knew this the way she knew most things now — distantly, like a story about another girl. She had been eleven. She remembered fragments: her mother's coat missing from its peg when she woke. The smell of the mountain wind coming through the shutters. And then, three days later, waking up in her own bed feeling as though a window had been left open inside her, and no amount of blankets or fire or her mother's arms could close it.

She had cried for a week. She remembered crying. What she couldn't quite remember was why it hurt, only that it had, enormously, the way you remember that a burn hurts without remembering the flame.

"Was I always like this?" she asked Tam once, when they were younger and the question still seemed safe to ask. Her brother had been mending a fishing net by the fire, his fingers quick and sure, his mouth set in the stubborn line it got whenever anyone mentioned their mother.

"Like what?"

"Hollow."

His hands had stopped. He'd looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him look away first, which Tam never did, Tam who would argue with a fence post.

"You were the loudest person in Ashfall," he said finally. "You sang off-key on purpose to annoy the neighbors. You once convinced half the street that the tanner's dog could talk." A pause. His voice had gone strange and quiet. "Yeah. You were loud, Wren."

And that was the other thing about having no soul. You became a memory that other people carried. They remembered you louder than you were. They grieved you while you sat across the breakfast table eating porridge you couldn't taste, and you had to watch them do it, and you couldn't even feel properly sad about it, because sadness was one of the things stored in the part of you that was gone.

* * *

Some nights, when the sleeplessness got particularly creative, Wren went through the house cataloguing herself.

Hands: present, functional, good at braiding hair and shelling peas. Warm? She genuinely no longer knew. She held them over candles sometimes to check, which Mama had caught her doing exactly once and never spoken of again, though afterward Mama had started holding her hand at every opportunity, walking to market, sitting at church, as if trying to warm her from the outside in.

Laughter: technically available. She could produce it. It came out sounding correct. But it was like the bread — a description, not an experience. When Tam told a joke, some distant part of her noted *this is funny* the way a clerk notes a delivery. The clerk filed it. Nothing else happened.

Grief: this one surprised her. She had expected grief to be among the missing. But sometimes, watching her mother laugh at something one of the neighbors said — really laugh, head back, eyes shut — Wren felt a faint pressure behind the hollow place, like wind against a boarded-up window. Whatever lived in her soul had known how to ache. It had left instructions.

Love: she tested this one most often, on Tam, because Tam was easy to love even secondhand. He was sixteen and furious at the world in general and their mother in particular, and he brought Wren things — river stones with stripes in them, a stolen orange at midwinter, once an entire book of maps he'd traded three days' wages for, because Wren had said, years ago, that she liked the idea of places she'd never been. He did these things gruffly, tossing them onto her bed without comment, and every time, the clerk in Wren's chest wrote *this is your brother loving you* in a neat hand, and signed it, and Wren read the note and wished — wished with the whole of what remained of her, which wasn't much but was hers — that she could feel it all the way down.

* * *

The fading, everyone agreed, was gradual. Dr. Merrick called it the wasting sickness and prescribed tonics that tasted of pennies and despair. Mama called it a lingering chill and fed her bone broth until Wren dreamed of swimming in it. Only Wren noticed the truth, because only Wren was keeping the catalogue.

Her reflection had begun, very slightly, to disagree with her.

Not dramatically. Nothing out of fairy tales — no missing face, no backward eyes. Just: the girl in the mirror blinked a half-moment after she did. The girl in the mirror smiled a shade too widely, like a portrait artist who'd been told the subject was cheerful and had overcorrected. Some mornings Wren stood at the basin and raised her hand slowly, watching, and the mirror-girl raised hers in perfect time, and yet Wren had the unshakable sense of a dance partner counting a different rhythm.

*You're going mad,* she told herself. *Or the mirror is. One of you.*

But she had started sleeping — lying — with her back to any reflective surface, just in case.

* * *

In her sixteenth winter, the dreams started.

That was the strangest part, because Wren did not dream. Sleep took her like a thief takes a house — quickly, completely, leaving nothing disturbed — and returned her to the same grey morning with nothing in between. For five years, nothing in between.

Then: a door.

It stood in a corridor of black stone, and it was not locked, and light came through the gap beneath it like spilled honey. Wren understood, in the way of dreams, that the door was hers. That everything the door contained was hers — the lavender, the taste of bread, the loud off-key singing, the laughing until her ribs ached, all of it, filed and shelved and waiting.

She also understood, in the same dream-way, that she was not supposed to be able to find the door. That something had gone wrong in the accounting. Somewhere far above, in a court beneath a mountain, a ledger had developed an error, and the error was shaped like a girl standing barefoot in a black corridor, reaching for a handle.

She woke before she touched it. She woke every time, gasping, her heart doing something so violent and so *present* that she lay there marveling at it. Fear, real fear, hot and animal. The first new feeling in five years.

"Mama," she said at breakfast, carefully, "what exactly did you trade at the shrine?"

Her mother dropped a plate.

It shattered, and Mama stood over the pieces with her hands pressed to her mouth, and for one long moment neither of them moved. Then Tam, who had gone very still in his chair, said quietly:

"Wren. What did you just say?"

"I have dreams," Wren said. "About a door. Under a mountain. And I think—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and some cowardly remainder of her wanted to keep it safely hypothetical. But the fear was still burning in her chest, and fear, she was discovering, was honest in a way she'd forgotten anything could be. "I think my soul is behind it. And I think it knows I'm looking."

Her mother sat down slowly, right there on the floor amid the broken plate, and put her face in her flour-dusted hands, and wept — not quietly, either, but in great ugly gulps, the way people cry when they've been carrying something for years and have just felt it shift.

Tam looked from his mother to his sister and back, and his jaw set in that stubborn line, and Wren thought, with the clerk's detached precision: *he is going to do something reckless.*

She was, as it turned out, entirely correct. But that was later. First there were the dreams, every night now, the door closer each time, the honey-light brighter, and once — only once — a voice on the other side of the door, low and cracked and human, humming a tune off-key on purpose.

Wren woke with tears on her face and no memory of crying, and lay in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the hollow place, and whispered into it, the way you speak through a closed door to someone you're not sure can hear:

"Hold on. I'm coming to get you. Whoever you are. Whoever *I* am."

Far away, in a shop in Ashfall that smelled of candle wax and secrets, a dying man had just stumbled through a door of his own.

And beneath the mountain, in a vault full of hanging lights, one dim lantern flickered — and turned, very slightly, toward the sound of a name it had been waiting five years to hear spoken.
