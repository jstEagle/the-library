## 5. The Brother Who Follows

The knock came at half past midnight, and it was wrong in three ways.

First, nobody knocked on Ines Pemberton's door at half past midnight unless they wanted something or were about to die. Second, the knock itself was too polite — three measured raps, like a man who had rehearsed them on the walk over — and politeness at that hour was its own kind of threat. Third, and worst: whoever stood outside had waited until the lamp in her window went dark before approaching. She'd watched him do it. He thought he was clever. He was clever enough to be dangerous and not clever enough to be safe, which described most of the trouble she'd ever been in.

Ines sat very still in the back room of her shop, among the shelves of harmless charms — luck-knots that did nothing but look cheerful, river-stones polished into shapes that suggested patience, little clay houses for people who missed home. Seven years of selling nothing, and selling it well. Her hand found the poker by the cold hearth without her deciding to reach for it.

"Shopkeeper," said the voice through the door. Young. Trying to sound older. "I know you're awake. Your chimney's smoking."

She looked at the banked stove, which was indeed leaking a thin gray thread past the flue. She made a mental note to fix the flue. Then she made a second mental note, filed under *survival*, which read: *never let them know what you've noticed.*

"Market's closed," she called back, keeping her voice flat and shopworn, the voice of a tired woman with a bad hip and no secrets. "Come back at dawn."

"I'll wait," the voice said. And then, after a silence long enough to be honest: "Please. I've walked four days. I don't have anywhere else."

That, too, could be a lie. But lies usually came dressed better than that. His voice had frayed at the edges, the way voices did when they'd been talking themselves out of turning back for miles.

Ines weighed the poker in her hand, then set it down where it would be easy to pick up again, and opened the door.

He was perhaps nineteen, tall in the way of someone who hadn't finished growing into his own shoulders, with mud to the knees and a week of stubble doing its best against a face that was all sharp angles and stubbornness. He had his mother's eyes — she would learn that later, and understand it too late to be useful — but right then all she saw was a boy holding himself upright by will alone, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe as if the street might tilt otherwise.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. "On Candle Lane. The one Merrick Hale talked to before he died."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped coin. She kept her face still. Seven years of practice, and it held.

"Don't know any Merrick Hale."

"He sold charms two streets over from you. Bad ones, actually, mostly. My sister bought a love-charm off him once that just made her sneeze." A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died there. "He came to our house three nights ago with blood on his shirt and a story about a map. Then he ran. Then men in gray coats came asking after him, and now everyone who ever spoke to him is either dead, missing, or being followed." He lifted his chin. "I'm being followed. I checked. You're good at it, by the way. I lost you twice between the market and here, and I only found you again because you stopped to buy apples. Nobody buys apples at dusk unless they're checking for tails. Apples bruise. It gives you an excuse to stop and look around."

Ines stared at him.

"You," she said, "have done this before."

"No," he said. "But my mother taught me to count exits. She said it was for fire safety." Something moved behind his eyes when he said *mother* — grief and fury sharing a room without speaking to each other. "Can I come in? There's a gray coat across the square and I'd rather not stand in the light arguing with you."

There was, in fact, a gray coat across the square. Ines had seen him an hour ago, pretending to sleep in a doorway with his boots pointed the wrong way. Amateurs.

She stepped aside. "Fast. Quiet. Touch nothing."

* * *

His name was Tam Calloway, and he told her so before he'd even taken off his wet boots, which told her he was either very honest or very stupid, and she was beginning to suspect he was both, generously.

He sat on the stool by the stove like a man who didn't trust furniture, perched on the edge, hands wrapped around the cup of tea she'd given him more to occupy his fingers than out of kindness. Steam curled up past his face. Up close he looked worse — hollowed, sleepless, a healing cut above his eyebrow that he clearly hadn't cleaned properly.

"Merrick Hale came to us," he said. "To our house, in Lowmarsh. Half-dead. Said he'd been carrying something for a friend and the friend was gone and the hunters were close, and he needed to pass it on to someone the mountain couldn't buy." Tam's jaw worked. "My mother took him in. Fed him. And he gave her the map, and told her what it opened, and told her—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Told her Wren's soul was down there. Under the mountain. Sold."

Ines kept her hands busy sorting dried lavender that did not need sorting. "Sold how? Souls aren't traded in Ashfall. That's mountain business. Old law. Nobody sells anymore."

"Somebody sold," Tam said flatly. "Eight years ago. My mother signed something. I was eleven; I thought it was a debt paper. Maybe it was. Maybe it was both." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly young. "Wren started fading that winter. We thought it was illness. She slept and slept and woke up tired, and things stopped mattering to her — food, music, the festival, everything. Like someone was turning her down a little at a time. And Mama just watched it happen and never said a word, and last month Wren couldn't get out of bed, and Mama finally told me. Told me she'd signed. Told me what she'd gotten for it."

"What did she get?"

"The debt forgiven." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, a laugh with nothing in it. "We owed the wool factors two hundred crowns. Two hundred. That's what my sister's soul cost. Not even a fortune. Just enough."

The lavender crumbled under Ines's fingers. She thought of the dying man's blood on her floorboards, the map's wax seal, the single whispered name: *Wren.* She thought of a girl she'd never met, sleeping herself into nothing while her mother counted coins.

"And now?" Ines asked carefully. "Your mother sent you?"

"My mother's dead." He said it the way you'd report the weather, which is how some people say the worst things they know. "Fever, three weeks back. Before Hale ever came. So whatever she meant to do about it, she ran out of time." He set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way angry people handle fragile things. "Which leaves me. And the map. Which Hale gave to her, and which she hid, and which I can't find, because she hid it well, because she hid everything well, her whole life."

Ines said nothing. The map was upstairs, sewn into the lining of a coat she no longer wore, and it was going to stay there.

"So I followed the story backward," Tam went on. "Hale mentioned a charm-seller on Candle Lane. The last person he trusted. And here you are." He turned on the stool to face her fully, and there it was — the thing underneath the exhaustion, the engine driving the whole ragged journey. "Give it to me. The map. It's mine. It's my family's. Whatever price the mountain wants, I'll pay it. I'll bargain with the Pale King himself if I have to."

"There is no bargaining with the Pale King," Ines said. "That's rather the point of him."

"Everything bargains. Everything has a price; that's what the court runs on. Souls, debts, memories — I've heard the stories. If it has a price, it can be paid." He leaned forward. "You don't know what it's like, watching someone disappear while they're still breathing. Every morning she's a little less there. Last time I visited, she asked me to describe the sea to her. We grew up a day's walk from the coast. She used to swim every summer. She's seventeen." His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it, shoving the crack back down like a loose board. "So yes. I'll pay anything. That's not bravery. That's just arithmetic."

And there it was, Ines thought. The same arithmetic that had bought Wren's soul in the first place. A mother trading a daughter for two hundred crowns; a brother offering himself for a sister. The mountain didn't need to hunt souls. It only had to wait for people to do the math.

"It's not yours to give away," she said quietly. "Or to spend. You're not a coin, Tam Calloway."

"I'm the only coin we have left."

"No," Ines said. "You're a boy who's walked four days on no sleep, chasing a story that ends under a mountain full of things that eat hope for breakfast. Give it a year. Grieve your mother. Sit with your sister. There are other—"

"There are no other ways!" He was on his feet now, stool scraping, and the gray coat across the square might as well have been a hundred miles off for all the caution left in him. "You think I haven't looked? Priests shrug. Doctors bleed her and charge me for it. The ward-magister told me to make her comfortable." He laughed again, that empty laugh. "Comfortable. She's dying of a hole where she should be, and they want me to make her *comfortable*."

Silence settled over the little room. The stove ticked. Outside, far off, a night-watchman's bell rang the third hour, and somewhere a dog answered it with total conviction.

Tam sank back onto the stool, deflated, and put his head in his hands. When he spoke again, the anger had burned down to coals.

"She used to sing," he said, muffled, into his palms. "Badly. Off-key, always, and loud. Now she doesn't hum. You don't notice a thing like that until it's gone, and then you can't stop noticing." He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "I'm not leaving Ashfall without that map. I'll search this city street by street. I'll follow you. I'm apparently decent at following. So you can give it to me now, or you can give it to me eventually, but you're going to give it to me, because you're the only thread I have and I am not letting go of threads anymore."

It should have been funny. Some part of Ines noted distantly that it was almost funny — this soaked, starving boy declaring a siege on a woman who had survived witch-hunters, a massacre, and seven years of deliberate mediocrity. But mostly it wasn't funny at all, because he was right about the arithmetic, and because she recognized the look in his eyes. She'd worn it once herself, standing in the ashes of her order's hall, promising a dead woman's sword that she would never let the world forget what was owed.

Promises like that didn't keep. They just waited.

"Here's what happens now," Ines said, rising, in her best shopkeeper's voice, which was also her best lie. "You're going to drink your tea. Then you're going to sleep in the back room, because you're swaying on that stool and you'll be no use to anyone unconscious in a gutter. Tomorrow morning you'll leave, and I will consider what you've said, because I'm a fair trader and fair traders consider offers."

"That's not a yes."

"That's not a no. Take it. It's better than what most people get from me."

He glared at her with impressive stamina for someone about to fall over. Then, slowly, the exhaustion won, and his shoulders dropped, and he nodded once — a soldier's nod, grudging, provisional.

"One condition," he said, hauling himself toward the back room. "If hunters come here — and they will, they came to Lowmarsh after Hale, they'll trace him here — you wake me. I don't care how bad it looks. I don't run first anymore. I did that at the house, when the coats came, and my sister's been alone with a fading soul and an empty chair ever since." He paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the dark. "Never again. Wake me."

"Wake you for what?" Ines asked. "What exactly would you do against a witch-hunter?"

"Something stupid," Tam said, with the first real smile she'd seen on him, small and crooked and entirely his mother's stubbornness wearing his father's jaw. "I'm told I'm good at it."

He disappeared into the back room. Within minutes, absurdly, heartbreakingly, he was asleep — she could hear it, the sudden deep quiet of a body surrendering after days of war. Ines stood alone in the lamplight among her harmless charms, listening to a stranger breathe in her house, and felt the shape of her life shifting under her like ice on a spring river.

Because here was the trouble, and she knew it already, had known it since the dying man's blood soaked into her floorboards: she could not give him the map. The map was a death warrant with drawings on it, and handing it to this boy would be murder with extra steps. But she could not refuse him either, because refusing him meant walking back into her shop tomorrow and selling luck-knots to strangers while a girl under a mountain faded one song at a time, and Ines had spent seven years learning exactly what that kind of silence cost, and she had sworn — privately, bitterly, in the dark — never to pay it again.

Two people who needed each other, and trusted each other not at all. That was a bargain the old stories loved. The old stories usually ended badly.

She banked the stove, fixed the flue, and climbed the stairs to check on the map sewn into the coat lining. It was there, of course. Wax seal unbroken, ink browned with old blood, the mountain drawn at its center like a closed eye.

As she smoothed the fabric flat, her fingertip caught the edge of the seal — and the candle beside her guttered, though there was no draft, and for half a heartbeat she smelled woodsmoke and snow and something older, something patient, the way the mountain air must smell at the gates of a court that trades in souls.

From the back room below, faintly, Tam murmured in his sleep. A name. Not Wren's.

*Mama.*

Ines blew out the candle and sat in the dark, holding a dead man's map, listening to a living boy grieve, and understood that whatever she decided by morning, the decision had already begun to make itself.

Down in the square, unnoticed by either of them, the gray coat finally rose from his doorway, stretched, and began — unhurried, certain — to walk toward Candle Lane.
