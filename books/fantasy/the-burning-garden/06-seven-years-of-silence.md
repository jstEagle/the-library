## 6. Seven Years of Silence

The map would not burn.

Ines had tried three times now — once in the shop's iron stove, once over a candle stub she kept for sealing parcels, and once, in a moment of genuine desperation, by holding it out the back window into the rain, as if the weather might be persuaded to do what her own hands could not.

The rain had simply beaded on it and rolled away, leaving the ink darker than before, the mountain road sharper, the little drawn vault at its heart gleaming like a wet stone.

"Stubborn thing," she muttered, and then caught herself, because talking to maps was exactly the sort of habit that got charm-sellers burned, and she pressed her lips together and put the map in the flour tin instead, under the flour, which was not a hiding place anyone would ever suspect because Ines Pemberton had not baked a loaf of bread in seven years and everyone on Candlemaker's Row knew it.

She sat down at her workbench among the false charms — the copper luck-knots that were only copper, the "warding bells" that rang prettily and warded nothing, the love tokens guaranteed to work exactly as well as any other gift a person bothered to choose with care — and she let her hands do their small honest-dishonest work, and she thought about oaths.

Because that was the trouble with the map. It wasn't the witch-hunters, though they were trouble enough. It was that the map was an invitation written in a language she had sworn never to speak again, and every hour it sat in her flour tin, it seemed to sit also behind her breastbone, humming.

* * *

Seven years ago, on the last morning of the world as she had known it, Ines had been arguing about breakfast.

"You cannot train a swordspeaker on porridge," Master Ellery was saying to the cook, in the tone of a man who had been saying this for twenty years and losing for twenty years. "The voice is a muscle like any other. It wants meat."

"The voice," said Cook, who was named Brann and feared no one alive, "is a mouth, and mouths want porridge, and if you fill them with meat at dawn they get lazy."

"It is a well-established principle—"

"It is established that you are a thin man, Master Ellery, and I am not, and I rest my case entirely upon that fact."

Ines had been seventeen, sitting on the long bench in the hall of the Order of the Speaking Sword with her boots muddy from morning drills, laughing into her sleeve, and she remembered — she would always remember, that was the cruelty of memory, it kept the breakfast argument sharp as glass while it let whole years go soft — she remembered thinking that this was what safety felt like. Not grand safety. Small safety. Porridge safety. The safety of being one voice among forty, all of them trained to speak steel awake, none of them important enough alone to matter to anyone dangerous.

Master Ellery used to say that was the whole point of the Order. Not the swords. "A sword is just a word with an edge," he'd say, tapping his walking stick against the practice-yard fence. "Forty of us, girl. Forty voices. No king can buy forty loyalties, and no king can burn forty names off a page without the page noticing." He'd said it cheerfully, the way he said everything, even the things that turned out to be prophecies.

The crown had come for them in autumn, when the leaves were doing their best work.

It hadn't been soldiers, not at first. It had been a letter, beautifully written, offering the Order a place in the royal household: swordspeakers bound to the king's service, oath-sworn to him alone, their magic turned from protection of the realm to protection of the throne. And beneath the beautiful writing, the part that was not an offer at all: *refusal will be noted*.

They had refused. All forty of them, in the hall where Ines had laughed about porridge, standing together while Master Ellery read the refusal aloud in his mild voice, as if declining an invitation to a wedding.

"They'll come," Brann had said afterward, in the kitchen, kneading bread with the calm of a woman who had survived two wars and one marriage. "And when they come, you run, girl. You don't fight, you don't make speeches, you run. Promise me."

"I promise," Ines had said, meaning it the way you mean things at seventeen, which is to say completely and with no idea whatsoever what you're promising.

They came eleven days later. Not soldiers first this time either — witch-hunters, in grey coats, with iron chains and cold-iron gags and a writ signed in a hand so fine it looked like frost. The Order had time to raise perhaps half its wards. Ines remembered the sound the wards made when they broke, a sound like a bell dropped down a well, and she remembered Master Ellery putting his stick in her hands — his plain, unmagical walking stick, the one he'd leaned on for thirty years — and shoving her toward the cellar door.

"A sword is just a word with an edge," he said. "So keep your words, girl. Keep them buried deep, and keep them *yours*."

Then he turned around and walked back up the stairs to meet the hunters, speaking as he went — speaking his true name, loud, ringing off the stones, waking every blade in the armory at once — and Ines ran, as promised, down into the dark, with the sound of forty voices and forty swords and one kitchen full of fire coming down through the floorboards after her like weather.

She did not cry until she was three miles away, in a turnip field, at dawn, and then she cried the way you cry at seventeen, completely, and when it was done she knelt in the dirt and swore her oath.

Not the Order's oath. Her own. There was no one left to administer the old one, so she made it herself, alone, to the turnips and the gray sky, and it was simple, the way true things are simple:

*I will not speak it. Not my name, not my craft, not one word of the speech that wakes steel. I will be no one, and no one is safe.*

She was seventeen, and she buried her voice the way you bury a body: deep, and quickly, and without looking at it too long.

* * *

"Ines? Ines, love, you've stitched the same knot four times."

Marta Ostrow was leaning on the counter of the shop, holding two of Ines's luck-knots and looking at her with the frank concern of a woman who sold fish and missed nothing. Marta was seventy, built like a mooring post, and had decided sometime during Ines's second year on Candlemaker's Row that the quiet trinket-seller needed feeding, protecting, and occasionally scolding, in roughly that order.

"Sorry," Ines said, and unstitched the knot. "Long night."

"Mm." Marta set the knots down. "You know what my mother used to say? A face is a purse. Whatever you're carrying, everyone can see the shape of it."

"Your mother sounds exhausting."

"She was. Wonderful woman. Dead thirty years, still exhausting." Marta patted the counter twice, a gesture that meant *I'm going now but I'm not done with you*. "Whatever it is, girl — eat something. Sleep. And if it's trouble—" she lowered her voice, though the shop was empty, "—there's a boat leaves from the herring quay Thursdays. Old Toma owes me. He doesn't ask questions. He barely asks *hello*."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"No," Marta agreed, sighing. "That's what worries me."

Ines watched her go, broad and unhurried down the Row, and felt the familiar ache of loving people you were lying to. Because that was the arithmetic of concealment nobody warned you about: it didn't cost you your life all at once. It cost it in installments. Every friendship carried a hidden ledger entry. Every kindness given under a false name was a debt quietly accruing interest. Seven years of Marta's soup, of old Toma nodding to her on the quay, of children buying luck-knots with coins clutched in fists — seven years of all of it, and not one soul knew her name was true.

*Ines Pemberton.* She hadn't heard it aloud since the turnip field. Sometimes, in the very worst hours of the night, she said it silently, just to check it was still there, the way you touch a scar to confirm the wound happened.

* * *

The flashbacks came more often lately. She'd noticed that. Grief, she'd learned, was less like a wound and more like a tenant — quiet for months, then suddenly rearranging all the furniture.

She was chopping onions for a stew she'd actually intended to eat when the memory arrived, uninvited, of her third year in the Order, age fifteen, the day she'd first spoken a blade awake.

Master Ellery had put a practice sword in her hands — dull, blunted, more iron than steel — and told her to hold it and say nothing until she felt it. "Feel what?" she'd asked. "You'll know," he'd said, infuriatingly. She'd stood in the practice yard for the better part of an hour feeling nothing but foolish while the other students snickered, and then, somewhere between frustration and boredom, she had stopped trying — and the sword had *hummed*. Faintly. Like a struck tuning fork remembering music.

"Now," Ellery had said softly, "say hello."

She'd said hello. Just that — a greeting, in the old speech, the language that wasn't a language so much as a permission — and the dull practice blade had gone bright along its edge and sung in her grip like something glad to be noticed, and Ines had burst into tears in front of the entire class, and Ellery had pretended not to see, and later he'd bought her a honey bun and told her the truth that governed everything:

"It's not power, girl. Everyone thinks it's power. It's *attention*. Steel spends its whole life being ignored. When you speak to it, you're telling it: I see what you're for. That's all swordspeech is. Seeing truly, out loud." He'd licked honey off his thumb. "Which is why they fear it, of course. A king can abide a strong arm. What he cannot abide is being seen."

Ines stood at her cutting board with onion tears on her cheeks and thought: *he was right, and it killed him, and I have spent seven years refusing to see anything truly, including myself.*

The knife slipped. She swore — a common oath, a safe oath, the kind any fishwife might use — and bandaged her thumb, and ate the stew standing up, and did not taste it.

* * *

There was one more memory, the one she kept deepest, the one she visited least. She took it out now, sitting in the dark shop with the lamp turned low, because the map in the flour tin had started to feel less like paper and more like a question, and questions deserved honest answers even when the answers hurt.

The night of the massacre, running, she had passed through the village of Harrow's Bend at dawn. The hunters had been there ahead of her — or beside her, or behind her; it hardly mattered. What mattered was the well in the village square, and the woman chained beside it, and the gag of cold iron in the woman's mouth, and the crowd, and the way the crowd looked *away*, all of them, in perfect unison, the way people look away from things they intend to live with.

The woman saw Ines. Saw the mud on her, the Order's grey travel-cloak, the telltale calluses of a sword-hand. And her eyes above the iron gag were not pleading.

They were furious. Alive. *Go*, they said. *Run. Don't you dare stop.*

Ines had run. She'd hated herself for it, then and for years after, until slowly she understood: the woman hadn't wanted rescue. Rescue was impossible; they both knew it. She'd wanted a witness. Someone to carry the sight of it forward, so that it had happened *to someone*, not merely *somewhere*.

So Ines carried it. Along with the porridge argument, and the honey bun, and the humming blade, and Master Ellery's stick, which she still owned, wrapped in oilcloth under her bed, the single most dangerous object in her possession, because it was proof.

Seven years. She had gotten good at the small life. Good at knots and bells and Marta's soup and Toma's nods. Good at being no one. And she had told herself, all this while, that the silence was penance — that staying silent honored the dead.

But tonight, with a dead man's map under the flour and a dead girl's name in her ears — *Wren Calloway, whose soul was sold* — Ines finally admitted the truth she'd been circling for seven years:

Silence hadn't been penance. It had been hiding. And the dead had never asked her to hide. They'd asked her to *see*, and to carry what she saw, and there was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

She got up, took the map from the flour tin, and spread it flat on the workbench. The mountain road gleamed. The little vault at its heart waited.

"All right," she whispered — whispering, always whispering, even alone, even now, seven years of habit worn smooth as river stone. "All right. But I'm not speaking. Whatever happens. I'm not—"

Three sharp knocks landed on the shop door.

Not Marta's knock — Marta knocked like a woman announcing herself to a whole street. This was precise. Measured. The knock of someone counting.

Ines blew out the lamp in one motion and stood in the dark with her heart slamming and her thumb throbbing and the map glowing faintly on the bench before her like a lit window, and outside, a voice she did not yet know but would come to dread said, pleasantly:

"Shopkeeper. We know you're awake."
