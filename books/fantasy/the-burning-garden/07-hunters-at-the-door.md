## 7. Hunters at the Door

The knock came at dawn, which was rude, and then it came again, harder, which was worse.

Ines had been awake since before light — she always was; seven years of hiding had trained her body to wake at every sound like a cat in a house full of rocking chairs — but she had been lying still, enjoying the last warm hour of pretending to be an ordinary woman who would sleep until a reasonable time and then complain about her knees. Now she lay in the narrow bed above her shop and listened.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more. Boots on the cobbles outside — not one pair of boots, not two. Six at least, moving with the particular coordinated heaviness of people who had been told they were important.

"Witch-hunters," she said to the ceiling. "Of course. I haven't even had tea."

She dressed quickly and quietly, pulling on the gray dress that made her look like every other tired shopkeeper in Ashfall, and went down the back stairs into the shop proper. The trinkets sat in their cases where they always sat: luck-charms that were only polished river stones, love-tokens that were only pretty glass, protection sigils that were only cleverly carved wood. Harmless. She checked each case out of habit, though nothing had moved. Nothing ever moved. That was the whole point of her life now — nothing moved, nothing sparked, nothing sang.

Except, apparently, Tam Calloway, who was asleep behind her counter.

She had forgotten him. Not entirely — she had put him there herself, two nights ago, after he'd shown up bleeding from a scuffle with a cutpurse and refused to leave until she agreed to hear his plan for bargaining with the court beneath the mountain. She had heard the plan. It was terrible. She had told him so, at length, and he had fallen asleep somewhere around the part where she explained why you cannot simply *haggle* with beings who eat memory.

Now he was sprawled behind the counter like a dropped coat, one arm hanging into the aisle, snoring with the untroubled rhythm of a man who did not yet know that six pairs of boots were about to come through the door.

"Tam," she whispered, kicking his boot. "Tam. Wake up."

He mumbled something. It sounded suspiciously like *mother*.

"*Wake up.*"

His eyes opened, unfocused, found her face, and focused with the slow horror of a man assembling the situation piece by piece. "Is it morning?"

"It's a raid."

That woke him properly. He sat up so fast he cracked his head on the counter's underside, and the thump was loud enough that Ines felt it in her teeth. "Ow. What—"

"Boots," she hissed. "Six pairs. Coordinated. Front door." She looked around the shop with the cold clarity that fear gave her, the old clarity, the swordspeaker's clarity, and hated how familiar it felt. "Back room. Under the floor. Go. Now."

"The floor comes *up*?"

"Obviously, or I wouldn't have said under it."

He scrambled. She hauled up the loose boards — three years of pretending to repair a water stain while actually maintaining an escape hatch — and shoved him down into the dark of the old cellar access, a crawl-space that ran toward the aqueducts. Then she dropped the boards back into place, dragged the dye-stained tarp over them, and arranged two crates of unsold love-charms on top, because nothing said *nothing interesting here* quite like a crate of love-charms nobody wanted.

The knock came again. "Open in the name of the Crown," said a voice through the door, and it was a voice Ines knew without knowing how she knew it — clear, cold, patient as frost forming. "By order of Captain Vess."

She took one breath. Put on the face. The shopkeeper's face: pleasant, slightly dim, mildly inconvenienced. Seven years of practice. She unlocked the door.

Captain Vess stood in the gray morning light like a blade someone had decided to dress in a uniform. She was not tall, but she stood as if height were a technicality. Her witch-hunters fanned out behind her in their ash-gray coats, and one of them carried the long iron pole they used for testing charms — the seeker-rod, which hummed near true magic the way a dog hummed near meat.

"Good morning," Ines said, with the exact warmth of a woman interrupted during breakfast. "If this is about the tax stamp, I paid it Tuesday. I have the receipt."

"This is not about your tax stamp." Vess stepped inside without being invited, which was legal, which was the problem with laws written by frightened kings. Her eyes moved across the shop the way a hand moves across goods, weighing everything. "Maren Pemberton. Charms and small goods."

"Ines," Ines said smoothly. "Maren was my mother."

"Mm." Vess picked up a luck-charm, turned it over, set it down. "You've been in Ashfall seven years."

"Eight in the spring."

"And in eight years, no complaints, no violations, no incidents." Vess turned to face her fully, and her eyes were pale and steady and terrible. "Do you know what I find remarkable about that, Mistress Pemberton? Everyone in this city has *something*. A fine unpaid. A charm sold too strong. A neighbor with a grudge. You have nothing. Eight years of perfect nothing."

"I'm very boring," Ines said. "It's a gift."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement. It might have been the ghost of a noose. "Yesterday," Vess said, "a man died four streets from here. Stabbed. Before he died, witnesses say he was asking after charm-sellers. Asking specifically for a woman who sells *quiet* magic." She let that word sit in the air between them. "Quiet magic. As though magic could be quiet. As though it were some shy animal you could keep in a drawer."

"Some people like their drawers shy," Ines said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Vess's gaze sharpened by a degree, and beneath the floorboards, Ines was almost certain, Tam had just shifted his weight onto a creaky board.

The silence stretched. One of the hunters glanced down at the floor. Ines smiled her blandest smile and gestured at the ceiling. "Roof leaks," she said. "Old buildings settle. Everything in Ashfall creaks except the young."

Vess studied her for a long moment. Then, without turning her head: "Search the shop."

* * *

They searched the way witch-hunters searched — thoroughly, joylessly, and with a great deal of breaking. The seeker-rod passed over the trinket cases and stayed silent, because river stones and glass and carved wood had nothing to say to it. It passed over the counter. Over the stairs. Over the crates of unwanted love-charms, where it paused — one heartbeat, two — and Ines kept her face fixed in mild boredom while her heart performed maneuvers no heart should attempt.

Then the rod moved on, and she learned later that Tam, bless his terrified practical soul, had spent those two heartbeats holding his breath and pressing both hands flat against the underside of the boards, as though willpower alone could hold the rod silent. Perhaps it had. She chose to believe it had.

"Clean," reported the hunter with the rod, sounding disappointed.

"Everything here is clean," Vess said. She was standing at the window now, looking out at the market street, where early vendors were setting up stalls and pretending very hard not to look at the witch-hunters' wagon. "That is what troubles me, Mistress Pemberton. A dying man crawls through Ashfall asking for a seller of quiet magic, and the nearest such seller has eight spotless years and a roof that creaks." She turned. "Where were you yesterday evening?"

"Asleep. By sundown, most nights. I'm forty-one and I sell trinkets; my evenings are not a carnival."

"Anyone who can confirm that?"

"My neighbors can confirm I'm dull. Whether that counts as testimony, Captain, I leave to you."

Again that flicker at the corner of the mouth. Vess walked slowly toward the door, boots clicking, and paused on the threshold. "We burned a charm-seller in Founder's Square last week," she said, conversationally, the way one mentions weather. "A woman. Older than you. She wept and swore her charms were harmless, and perhaps they were. But she had a ledger with names in it, and names are enough. The fire doesn't require much, in the end. Just a reason and a crowd." She adjusted her collar. "Keep your shop clean, Mistress Pemberton. Keep being boring. Boring women live long lives in Ashfall."

She left. The hunters left. The wagon rattled away down the cobbles, and the street exhaled, and Ines stood in her wrecked shop among the broken shutters and scattered stock and did not move for a full minute, because her knees had informed her they were done.

Then the floorboard creaked.

"Tam," she said hoarsely. "Come up before I die of fright twice in one morning."

* * *

He came up gray-faced and shaking, and to her surprise, the first thing he did was start picking up her scattered stock, setting each little carved bird and glass bead back in its place with clumsy care.

"She'll be back," he said. "That's what that speech was. The bit about the fire. She wasn't threatening you for today. She was telling you she's already decided."

"Yes," Ines said. "I got that."

"You're very calm."

"I'm extremely calm. It's the calmest I've ever been. If you drop that bird I will scream in a way that gets us both arrested." She sank onto her stool and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Through the shutter slats she could see the square, the ordinary morning, the bread-seller calling her wares. Somewhere in that ordinary morning, Captain Vess was writing her name in a ledger. *Names are enough.*

"We leave tonight," she said.

Tam's head came up. "Tonight? You said—"

"I said a great many things while explaining why your plan was idiotic, and I stand by all of them, and we're leaving tonight anyway, because my choices are now the mountain or the pyre, and at least the mountain hasn't already lit the torches." She stood, and her legs held, mostly. "Get the map. We'll need food, rope, and lamps. And Tam—" he stopped, halfway to the back room, and looked at her — "when she searches again, and she will, there won't be a floor for her to search. Do you understand? There's no coming back from tonight. Not to this shop. Not to this street. Not to the person I've been for seven years."

He was quiet a moment. He was, she was learning, capable of quiet when it mattered, which she had not expected from the man who'd tried to haggle with the Pale King's toll-collectors in theory.

"My sister's been fading for three years," he said. "And I've been doing nothing. So no. I don't understand staying. But I understand going." He almost smiled. "Besides, you're the one with the secret hatch under the floor. I figure you've done this before."

"Once," Ines said. "Seven years ago. With less warning and better shoes."

* * *

They went out through the aqueducts at dusk, when the city's noise was thick enough to swallow the sound of their footsteps. The old stone channels ran beneath Ashfall like dry veins, built by some earlier, prouder generation and abandoned to rats and smugglers and, apparently, charm-sellers with escape hatches. Ines led with a shaded lamp, walking the route she had walked once before, in the dark, with soldiers' voices above her and a dead order behind her.

Tam followed close, carrying the packs, and for a long while neither spoke. The water-stains on the walls glowed faintly green where old enchantments clung — dead magic, harmless, the residue of a city that had once thought itself too grand to fall.

"That captain," Tam said finally. "Vess. She knew. Didn't she? About you."

"She suspects. Suspicion is her profession. Proof is what she needs, and proof is what she doesn't have." Ines ducked under a low arch. "Yet."

"You could've lied better."

"I could have lied *differently*. Better is a matter of opinion. She wanted me angry or scared, and I gave her boring instead. Boring is the best armor there is." She glanced back at him. "Though I notice you nearly undid it by breathing."

"I was *holding my breath*."

"For two minutes?"

"It felt heroic from underneath the floor."

Despite everything — despite the wrecked shop and the ledger with her name in it and the road ahead that led down into the mountain's dark — Ines laughed. It came out rusty, half-swallowed, the laugh of a woman out of practice, but it was real, and Tam grinned at the sound of it like he'd won something.

They climbed the maintenance stair at the third junction, shouldered open the rusted grate, and came up into the gully beyond the city wall, where the mine roads began and the map in Ines's pocket pointed down into the world like a finger.

Behind them, Ashfall glittered in the dusk, ten thousand windows, none of them hers anymore. Ahead, the mountain stood black against the last red light, and somewhere inside it, a girl named Wren was fading by candlelight, waiting for strangers she'd never met to do something impossible.

"Walk fast," Ines said, and pulled her cloak tight, and started down the old mine road with Tam Calloway at her shoulder — the first ally she'd allowed herself in seven years, fragile as new ice, and bearing her weight anyway.
