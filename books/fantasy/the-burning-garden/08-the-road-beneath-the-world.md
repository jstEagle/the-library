## 8. The Road Beneath the World

**Ines**

The map did not begin at the mountain. It began at a well.

That was the first thing Ines had learned about it, three days out of Ashfall, when Tam Calloway had stood at the edge of a dry stone circle in the middle of nowhere and announced, with great confidence, that they were lost.

"We're not lost," she said. "We're at the beginning."

"It's a hole in the ground, Pemberton."

"It's a marked hole in the ground." She crouched and traced her finger along the map's edge, where the ink had faded to the color of weak tea. There — the small ringed circle, and beside it, in a hand so cramped it might have been written by a spider falling downstairs: *where the water remembers.* "Your father drew this. Or your mother. Someone who knew these roads when they still had names."

Tam said nothing, but he shifted his weight the way he always did when she mentioned his parents — like a man avoiding a loose floorboard in his own house. He was twenty, maybe twenty-one, all elbows and stubbornness, with his sister's dark hair and none of her stillness. He carried the map's other half of their supplies and carried his guilt invisibly, which Ines suspected weighed more.

"The water doesn't remember anything," he said finally. "It's a well."

"Then why does the map say so?"

"Because whoever wrote it was being poetic."

Ines smiled despite herself. She had spent seven years selling charms to people who believed poetry could keep moths out of flour sacks, and she had learned this much: people wrote down only what they were afraid of forgetting. *Where the water remembers* was not poetry. It was an instruction.

She knelt at the well's lip and listened. Below, far down, there was a sound — not dripping, exactly. More like breathing. A slow, tidal hush, as though something vast turned over in its sleep.

"Tam."

"I hear it."

"That's not groundwater."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't."

They followed the sound.

* * *

The mine roads beneath the Ashfall hills had been carved two hundred years ago, back when the mountain was merely a mountain and men dug into it for iron instead of souls. The tunnels ran straight and tall for miles, propped on timbers gone black with age, and every few hundred paces a niche had been cut into the wall where a lamp once sat. The niches were empty now, but the soot behind them remained — little halos of black, marking where light used to live.

Ines lit them anyway. Not with fire. With a word.

Just one word, breathed more than spoken, soft enough that Tam walking ahead never heard it: a small syllable of the swordspeech, the kindling-word, the one novices learned first because it could do no harm. It made lamplight of nothing. It woke old wicks. It cost her a faint warmth in her throat, like swallowing tea too quickly, and nothing more.

Seven years of silence, and here she was, spending her magic a copper at a time.

"You're doing that thing again," Tam said, without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The humming thing. The air goes funny and then there's light and you look like you're about to be sick."

"I don't look sick."

"You look like my aunt Maren after she eats shellfish."

"Charming." She caught up to him, ducking under a low beam. "It's a cantrip. A kitchen-magic. Children in the Order learned it before they learned letters."

"And if a hunter heard it?"

"Hunters don't patrol dead mines, Tam."

"Hunters burned your shop and put your face on a warrant. I think they'd patrol a dead *well* if they thought you'd crawl into it."

He wasn't wrong, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them for the next mile like a third traveler neither would acknowledge. Captain Vess did not give up. Vess was the sort of woman who treated pursuit as a form of prayer — patient, repetitive, absolutely certain of being answered. Somewhere behind them, likely, Vess was reading the same map they had stolen knowledge of, or interrogating the same innkeepers, or simply following the burn scars of Ines's passage south.

So they walked in the dark, and Ines lit lamps that had been dark since before either of them was born, and tried not to count how many words she had left in her before the mountain took the rest.

* * *

They argued about souls on the second night, in a chamber where the ceiling opened to a shaft of stars.

It started, as most of their arguments did, with Tam being reasonable in the way of someone determined to reach a conclusion he had already paid for.

"There has to be a price," he said. He was turning a strip of dried meat over the tiny fire he insisted on building despite Ines's objections. "That's what everything I've read says. The court doesn't *give*, it *trades*. So we trade. I have things worth having. I'm young. I'm healthy. I—"

"You're offering yourself."

"I'm offering a bargain."

"You're offering yourself," Ines repeated, "dressed up in arithmetic." She took the meat from him before he burned it, mostly to have something to do with her hands. "I've met men who bargained themselves away. In the Order we called it martyr's math. You add up everyone you love on one side, yourself on the other, and decide the sum is fair. It never is. The court knows that. It's the first thing they teach *you* — no, wait." She stopped. Corrected herself, quietly. "It's the first thing they taught us. The courts. They let you believe the trade is noble so you won't notice it's just another sale."

"Then what's your plan? Walk in, smile nicely, ask for Wren back?"

"My plan is to find out what Wren's soul is *bound with*. Contracts are knots, Tam. Every knot has a flaw. Your mother tied one to save your family — a desperate knot, tied fast, probably ugly. Desperate knots slip."

"And if it doesn't slip?"

"Then we cut it."

"With what?"

Ines looked at the fire and did not answer, and Tam, to his credit, understood that silence for what it was: not evasion but inventory. She was counting words again. The ones she could spend. The ones that would spend *her*.

"You could tell me, you know," he said, gentler than she expected. "What it costs. Whatever you're not saying about your voice and your oath and why you flinch every time I say the word 'magic' like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me money. It owes me everything else."

"Then let it pay some of it back."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she handed him back his ruined strip of meat and said, "Eat your dinner, Calloway."

But later, when he slept — curled against his pack with the map spread under his cheek like a child sleeping on a letter from home — she sat up and watched the star-shaft and let herself say it, silently, in the privacy of her own skull:

*I don't know if a soul can be bought back. I know they can be freed. Those aren't the same thing, and only one of them leaves the seller whole.*

And underneath that, quieter, in the place where her oath lived:

*And I don't know yet what freeing hers will cost mine.*

* * *

**Tam**

Tam dreamed of the bridge before they reached it.

Not the toll-keeper's bridge — he didn't know about that yet — but the footbridge behind his childhood house, the rotten one over the millrace, where Wren used to dare him to walk the middle plank barefoot. In the dream she stood on the far bank, and she was fading the way chalk fades on a slate left out in rain, and she kept saying *hurry, hurry, hurry* in a voice getting thinner each time, until the last time she said it there was no sound at all, just the shape of the word, and then not even the shape.

He woke with his heart slamming and the fire down to embers and Ines already awake, watching him with those careful gray eyes that missed nothing and offered nothing cheap.

"Bad dream?" she asked.

"Wren." He sat up, rubbed his face. His hands smelled of smoke and old timber. "She's worse. I can feel it. I don't even know how to explain that without you thinking I'm—"

"Superstitious?"

"I was going to say 'mad.'"

"You're neither." Ines fed a stick to the embers. "Souls that are held stretch. Like rope between two hands pulling apart. When the holding end pulls harder, the far end feels it." She paused. "My order guarded a soul-broker's ledger once, before the war. I've seen what held souls look like at both ends."

"You never talk about the Order."

"You never talk about your mother."

The words landed harder than she'd perhaps intended; he saw her realize it a half-second too late, saw her set her jaw against taking it back. Fair enough. He'd earned it, asking about the Order the way he did — sideways, every few miles, like picking at a scab to see if it bled truth.

"She sold Wren," Tam said. Flat. Testing the sentence again, the way he tested it every morning, to see if it had gotten any easier to carry. It hadn't. "Our farm was mortgaged to a man named Corvane, who sold debts to the mountain court the way other men sell wool. When the debt came due, she went under the mountain and came back with money and without—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Without the part of Wren that makes her Wren. And she looked at me afterward and said, *now we're safe,* and I was eleven, and I believed her, because she was my mother and mothers don't lie about safety."

"Do you hate her?"

"Yes." Quick. Too quick. Then, staring into the fire: "No. I don't know. She's got a face I can't remember anymore. Isn't that obscene? She did the worst thing a mother can do and I'm losing *her* too, day by day, like she's being punished twice and I'm the one paying both debts."

Ines was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had a rasp he hadn't heard before, like a blade drawn slowly.

"When my order refused the crown, they gave us a choice. Kneel, or burn. My teacher — Serah Ondrey, she was called — chose to burn standing up. I was her last student. I survived because I ran." She turned her hands palm-up, as though showing him something written there. "For seven years I told myself I hid to keep the oath alive. That a buried sword still counts as a sword. But some nights I knew the truth. I hid because living was easier than burning, and I dressed the fear up in oath-cloth so it would look like virtue." She closed her hands. "Hating someone and loving them can share a bed, Tam. It's a narrow bed, but they manage."

He wanted to argue. He found, examining himself honestly, that he couldn't — that somewhere in the last week the woman selling charms in a market stall had become the person whose judgment he trusted more than his own, and he resented it slightly, the way you resent a splint because it works.

"So we're agreed," he said instead. "Nobody sells anybody. We go in, we find the flaw in the knot, and we cut it."

"We're agreed on the cutting." Ines lay back, arms folded beneath her head. "The flaw we'll have to meet first."

* * *

**Ines**

On the fourth day the mine road began to descend in earnest, spiraling down through the mountain's shoulder like the groove of a screw, and the air changed. It grew sweet. That was the wrongness of it — everyone expected the deep places to smell of rot and sulfur, but the court's approaches smelled of honey and cold stone, of candles and something floral that had no business existing underground. Lure-scent, the old texts called it. The smell of a mouth deciding whether to swallow.

The lamps Ines lit lasted less time here. She noticed it first as a faint extra warmth in her throat, then as a flicker in the flames themselves — they leaned, all of them, toward the downward spiral, like grass bending toward wind. Toward the mountain. Even fire, it seemed, was curious about the Pale King.

"Tell me again what happens if the map's wrong," Tam said. His voice echoed strangely, arriving at her ears a half-beat after his lips moved, as though the tunnel were considering whether to pass it along.

"Then we're lost under a mountain with a fugitive's face and a bag of charms that don't work."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a charm-seller. Cheer costs extra."

But the map was not wrong. At the bottom of the spiral, where the mine road ended in a wall of worked stone older than any kingdom above, the map's final instruction waited, scratched into the rock itself in the same cramped spider-hand: *knock as a guest, not a beggar.* And below it, a door.

Not a grand door. That was the horror of it, Ines thought — the court beneath the world had chosen a door the size of a cottage's, plain oak bound in dull iron, as though the trade in souls were conducted by modest people with sensible overheads. Set into the wood at eye height was a brass plate, green with age, bearing a single line:

*All who enter are already entered in the ledger.*

Tam raised his fist to knock. Ines caught his wrist.

"Wait." Her heart was going hard, and she was surprised to find that under the dread there was something else — something she hadn't felt in seven years and had mourned like a limb. Her blood had recognized the threshold. Magic pooled near doors like water near drains; she could feel the court's attention through the oak, vast and unhurried, the attention of something that had all the time ever minted.

"Whatever happens in there," she said, "we are merchants. We sell charms. We've come to appraise goods, nothing more. You bargain loudly and badly, because that's what merchants' sons do, and I stand quiet and look expensive. Can you do that?"

"Loudly and badly," Tam repeated. He grinned, and it was Wren's grin, and it hurt to look at. "I've trained my whole life."

"One more thing." She held his eyes. "If I say a word you don't understand — a true word, a heavy one — you run. Don't grab me, don't argue, don't be noble. You run, and you find your sister, and you get her out. Promise me."

"Why would you—"

"*Promise me,* Tam."

Something moved behind his face — the same arithmetic she'd scolded him for, martyr's math, done in reverse — and then he nodded once, sharp, and she released his wrist.

He knocked. Three times, firm, the knock of a man with goods to sell and prices to name.

The door swung inward on darkness that smelled of honey and candle smoke, and from somewhere deep within the mountain came a sound like a thousand small bells all learning to whisper —

and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, threading up through the stone like a root finding water, Ines heard a voice she had never heard before and knew instantly, bone-deep, the way you know your own name in a stranger's mouth.

A girl's voice, thin and stretched and fraying at the edges, whispering the same word over and over into the dark of the vault below:

*hurry. hurry. hurry.*
