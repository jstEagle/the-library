## 9. The Toll-Keeper's Bridge

The map said the bridge would be guarded. It did not say by what.

Tam Calloway had spent four days on the road beneath the world learning exactly how much the map didn't say, and he had developed a theory about it: whoever drew this thing had been in a hurry, or had been cruel, or — most likely, in his experience — had been both. The ink was smeared where someone's thumb had dragged through it. The margins were crowded with notes in at least three different hands, one of which simply read *don't* next to a sketch of something with too many joints.

He was beginning to sympathize with that note.

"Tell me again," he said, "why we couldn't have gone around."

Ines Pemberton walked ahead of him along the mine road, her pack riding high on her shoulders, her hood up against the drip of the tunnel ceiling. She didn't turn around. "Because the map doesn't go around."

"The map also has a drawing of a thing with too many joints."

"That's why we're going over the bridge instead of under it."

"That is not reassuring in the way you seem to think it is."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were allowed on the road beneath the world. Four days of travel had taught Tam that Ines rationed her laughs like a miser rationing coin — you'd get one when you least expected it, and then nothing for miles, and you'd find yourself doing stupid things just to earn another one, which he resented deeply and continued to do anyway.

The tunnel opened ahead of them without warning, the way tunnels do — one moment stone walls close enough to touch both sides of, the next a cavern so vast their lantern light gave up trying to fill it and simply pooled around their feet like spilled honey. And across the cavern, spanning a gorge that breathed cold air up from somewhere very far below, stood the bridge.

It was old. That was the first thing. Old the way mountains are old, old the way grief is old — its stones worn smooth and dark, its railings carved with figures whose faces had been rubbed away by centuries of hands gripping them in fear. It crossed perhaps two hundred feet of empty air to a doorway cut into the far wall, and above the doorway hung a lamp that burned with no visible flame, casting light the color of old parchment.

And in the middle of the bridge sat the toll-keeper.

Tam stopped walking. Ines did not, which he decided was either bravery or a character flaw.

The creature was roughly the size of an ox and shaped like nothing Tam had a comparison for. It had a body like a heap of river stones that had decided, at some point in the deep past, to become a person and hadn't quite finished the job. Its skin was slate-grey and veined with lines of faint gold, like ore seams. It had too many eyes — six, arranged in two rows of three — and all twelve eyelids blinked in slow sequence, one after another after another, so that the effect was less like blinking and more like a wave rolling across its face. Its mouth was wide and patient and full of teeth that looked, absurdly, almost kind.

"Well," Tam said. "Hello."

"You may call me Keeper." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, low and resonant, like a word spoken into a well. "Few do. Most prefer to scream."

"We're not most people," Tam said, because his mouth had always run ahead of his sense of self-preservation. "We're barely people at all, some days."

One of the creature's eyes — the middle-left one — swiveled to fix on him. The others stayed politely elsewhere. "You are Tam Calloway. You are going to the court beneath the mountain to buy back your sister's soul with money you do not have, bargaining skill you do not possess, and a plan you have not finished inventing."

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Ines, who had stopped at the near end of the bridge and was watching him with an expression of profound, unhurried satisfaction.

"I told you," she said.

"She told me nothing," Tam said. "She told me *nothing* the entire way here. She communicated exclusively in sighs."

"Sighs are efficient," Ines said.

"They are *not*—"

"Travelers." The Keeper's voice settled over them like snowfall. "The bridge is old, and I am older, and neither of us carries anyone for free. The toll is a memory. One memory, freely given, of your own choosing. You will not miss it — that is the mercy of the toll. You will simply wake tomorrow and there will be a smooth place in your mind where something used to be, and you will not know what filled it, only that once, something did."

Silence, except for the breathing of the gorge below — a long, slow inhale and exhale, as though the mountain itself slept down there and dreamed in cold air.

"And if we refuse?" Tam asked.

"Then you may sit on this side of the bridge for as long as you like. People do. Some sit for years. There is a man two miles back who has been sitting since before your grandmother was born. He waves sometimes. It's very sad."

Ines stepped forward onto the first stone of the bridge. "I'll pay first."

"No," Tam said, and surprised himself with how fast it came out.

She turned. Her hood shadowed her face but not her eyes, which had gone sharp. "You don't get to decide—"

"You've got more memories worth keeping than I do." He heard his own voice go flat and stubborn, the voice he used at card tables when he was losing. "Whatever you buried to survive seven years in Ashfall — whatever your oath cost you — I'm not letting a rock with too many eyes take a piece of that. I'll pay. I'm the one who needs the bridge anyway. This whole trip is my fault. My mother sold Wren's soul; my family's debt put us on this road. If anyone's paying a toll, it's me."

For a long moment Ines said nothing. Then she did something he hadn't seen her do before: she nodded, slowly, the way you nod at a stranger who has just turned out to be something other than what you assumed.

"One memory," she said to the Keeper. "His choice. Not yours."

"It is always their choice," the Keeper said, and there was something in its voice — gentleness, maybe, or the memory of gentleness, worn smooth like the bridge stones. "That is the law of the toll, and laws are the only things I love."

Tam sat down on the middle of the bridge, because apparently that was how it worked — you paid sitting up, facing the thing, like a man signing a contract he'd already lost the argument about. Ines stood at the bridge's near end, arms folded, watching. She'd offered to look away. He'd told her not to bother. Someone should witness it, he figured. Someone should remember that he'd chosen this, even if he wouldn't.

The Keeper loomed over him, all six eyes open at once now, gold seams glowing faintly in the lamplight.

"Choose," it said.

And here was the thing Tam had not expected: the choosing was easy.

He had spent the last four days cataloguing his memories without meaning to, the way a man with a toothache keeps touching it. His childhood in the Calloway house with its crooked chimney. The winter they ate nothing but turnips and his father cried in the privy where he thought no one could hear. Wren at seven, teaching herself to whistle through a blade of grass until the whole street's dogs went mad. Wren at fourteen, hollow-eyed, saying *I'm fine, Tam, I'm fine* in a voice that had forgotten what fine felt like. His mother at the kitchen table, counting coins that weren't enough, her face—

His mother's face.

He reached for it and found it already slipping, the way faces do — he could see her hands, quick and clever; he could hear her voice, sharp as a whip-crack and twice as likely to leave a mark; he could smell the bread she burned every single Sunday without fail, twenty years of burned bread, a monument to stubbornness. But her face — the actual face, the particular arrangement of her — had gone soft in his memory years ago, blurred by anger, worn down by the story he'd told himself ever since: *she sold my sister, she chose the debt over Wren, she is the villain of this.*

He had hated her face for six years. He had hated it so thoroughly and so carefully that he had, without noticing, let it dissolve.

And underneath the hatred, in the place where the face used to be, there was something else. Something he'd been stepping around for years the way you step around a loose floorboard.

"Wait," Tam said.

The Keeper waited. It was very good at waiting. It had clearly had practice.

"There's a memory," Tam said slowly. "My mother's face. What she looked like. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "I haven't been able to see it properly in years. I thought I'd already lost it. But it's still in there. Under everything. And if I give you that one—"

"Then you will lose even the blur," the Keeper said quietly. "You will know you had a mother. You will know you loved her, or hated her, or both — the feeling remains, traveler, feelings are not the toll. Only the shape of her. The color of her eyes. The line of her mouth. All of it goes, and the love stays behind with nothing left to hold."

"That sounds worse than losing it clean."

"Yes," said the Keeper. "It usually does. That is why it is such a good toll."

Ines had come partway onto the bridge. "Tam. Pick something else. A bad memory. Everyone has bad ones, give it one of those—"

"It takes what I choose," Tam said. "Freely given. Those were the rules." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Besides, what would I give it? My worst day? I need my worst days. They're the only reason I'm doing any of this. No. It should be her. It should be her, because—" 

Because he was tired. That was the truth of it, the plain ugly truth he'd been carrying up the mountain alongside his pack. He was tired of hating her. Hating her was a full-time occupation and it paid nothing and it kept him from sleeping and it meant that every time he looked at Wren — fading, flickering, half-gone — he saw his mother's crime instead of his sister's face, and he could not afford that anymore. Wren needed all of him. Every room in him. And one room in him had been sealed shut for six years with his mother inside it, and he was done keeping her there.

"If I can't carry her face and my sister both," he said, "then I choose my sister."

He looked up at the Keeper. "Take it. Her face. All of it. Even the blur. Especially the blur."

The Keeper's six eyes closed, all together, all at once — the first time they had moved in unison — and when they opened again they were very near, and very soft, and Tam had the strange impression that the creature was sorry.

"This will hurt for exactly as long as it needs to," it said. "No longer. Hold still."

It did not touch him. It only leaned close, and breathed out — and its breath smelled of candle smoke and rain on hot stone and, absurdly, of burned bread —

and Tam Calloway remembered his mother's face completely, perfectly, for one entire shining second: the grey in her hair she never admitted to, the scar through her left eyebrow from a fight she'd won against a tax collector's hired man, the way her whole face changed when she laughed, which was rare, which was weather, which was an event the whole household rearranged itself around —

the way her face had looked, that last morning, when she'd kissed the top of his head and said *whatever happens, you protect her* —

and then it was gone.

Not torn away. Not stolen. Just — gone, the way a dream goes when you stand up too fast, leaving the shape of itself behind. He knew he had a mother. He knew he had loved her. The love was still there, vast and embarrassing and enormous, filling a room that now had nothing in it, and he understood, sitting on a bridge in the dark under the world, that the hatred had never been the biggest thing in him after all. It had just been the loudest. Underneath it, all along, quiet as groundwater, he had loved her so much it was humiliating.

"Oh," he said. His voice came out small. "Oh, that's — that's what it was. That's what I—"

"Tam." Ines was beside him. When had she crossed the bridge? Her hand was on his shoulder, awkward and firm, the hand of a woman who had not comforted anyone in seven years and was doing it anyway, badly, on purpose. "Hey. Look at me. You're still you."

"I know." He wiped his face with his sleeve, roughly, like the crying was an inconvenience rather than a grief. "I know. It's just — she kissed my head. The last morning. I'd forgotten she — I forgot she ever—" He laughed wetly. "I paid a toll and got robbed by my own feelings. Don't tell anyone."

"Who would I tell?" Ines said. "I communicate exclusively in sighs."

Despite everything, despite the hole in his head shaped like a woman, Tam laughed — really laughed, the kind that hurt — and heard, distantly, the Keeper make a sound like stones settling in a riverbed, which he suspected was the toll-keeper's version of the same thing.

They crossed the rest of the bridge together. At the far side, before the door with its flameless parchment-colored lamp, the Keeper spoke one last time.

"Traveler Calloway. A gift, unasked-for, against all custom." Its voice was lower now, almost private. "The memory is gone, but I will tell you what I saw in it, for I see all memories that pass my bridge. Your mother did not sell your sister lightly. She bargained for nine days. She offered the court everything else she had, twice. And when she finally signed, she wept so hard that the ink ran, and the clerk made her sign a second time." A pause. The gold seams in its skin dimmed, gently, like banked coals. "I tell you this not to ease you. I tell you because you will need it, where you are going. Hate is heavy luggage on that road. Travel lighter."

Then it turned back toward the middle of the bridge, folded its many-jointed limbs beneath it, and became, once more, merely a heap of stones that had opinions.

The door opened onto a descending stair, and warm air rose to meet them, smelling of honey and candle smoke, and somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat, Tam thought he heard bells.

They had descended perhaps a hundred steps when the sound caught up with them — footsteps above, urgent, echoing down the stairwell — and a voice, ragged with running:

"Wait! Wait — you two — please—"

Tam turned, hand going to the knife he mostly used for cheese. Behind them, silhouetted against the parchment glow of the doorway, a figure stumbled down into the mountain wearing the dust of the surface roads — and carrying news, though they didn't know it yet, that would burn away the last road home.
