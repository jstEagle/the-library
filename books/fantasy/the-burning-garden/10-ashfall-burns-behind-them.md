## 10. Ashfall Burns Behind Them

The inn at Grayhollow smelled of wet wool and boiled turnips, which was to say it smelled like every inn on the mine roads between Ashfall and nowhere, and Ines had stopped minding somewhere around the second night of sleeping in ditches.

She sat by the fire with a cup of something that called itself tea and had probably never met a tea leaf in its life, watching Tam Calloway lose an argument with a map.

"It's upside down," he said.

"It's not upside down."

"The mountain's on the left."

"Because you're holding it sideways." She reached across the table and turned it a quarter-turn. The faded ink lines resolved into the familiar spine of the Serpent's Back, the dotted trail her dying stranger had bled onto the paper, the little circled mark beneath the mountain where — if half of what they'd learned was true — Wren Calloway's soul hung in a vault like a lamp waiting for someone to light it.

Tam stared at it the way he'd been staring at it for three days: hungrily, suspiciously, as though it might confess to lying. He was twenty, maybe twenty-one, all elbows and stubbornness, with his sister's dark hair and none of her stillness. Not that Ines had met Wren yet. Everything she knew about the girl came from a dying man's last breath and from Tam, who spoke about her the way other people spoke about saints or weather — constantly, and with no control over it.

"Two more days," Tam said. "Maybe three, if the pass is washed out."

"Then we sleep in beds while beds exist." Ines sipped the turnip-adjacent tea. "You'll thank me when you're not eating road mud for breakfast."

"I've eaten worse."

"You've eaten *my* cooking, which I admit sets a low bar."

That got the ghost of a grin out of him, there and gone, like a fish surfacing. Three days ago he'd been holding a knife on her in an aqueduct tunnel; now he grudgingly let her pour his tea. That was how trust worked, she was learning. Not a door swinging open. A crack widening a hair at a time, both people pretending not to notice.

The inn door banged open and the cold came in with it, along with a carter in a canvas coat crusted with road salt. He stamped to the bar, slapped down coins, and said the thing that stopped Ines's heart mid-beat:

"You hear about Ashfall? Whole quarter's talking about it. Witch-hunters burned a shop on Chandler's Row two nights back. Big one. Said the owner was an oath-breaker."

The cup was very warm in Ines's hands. She kept her eyes on it. Kept her breathing even. Seven years of practice, and it came back to her now like a language she'd thought she'd forgotten: *be furniture, be wallpaper, be nobody.*

"Anyone hurt?" asked the innkeeper, wiping the bar with the eternal rag of his profession.

"Dunno. They took the shopkeeper in chains first, then torched the place. Captain Vess herself did the lighting, they say. Made a speech." The carter accepted his ale and drank half of it in one pull. "Name of Pemberton, the papers said. Ines Pemberton."

*There it is,* Ines thought, with a strange, floating calm. *My name, in a stranger's mouth, in a room full of strangers, forty miles from home.*

Beside her, Tam had gone rigid. She felt it more than saw it — the sudden stillness of him, the way his hand crept toward the knife at his belt. Under the table, she put her boot on his foot and pressed down until he subsided.

"Fugitive," the carter went on, cheerfully, because ruin was always entertainment when it happened to someone else. "They're offering fifty crowns for her. Dead or alive, but I heard Vess would rather have her breathing. Something about wanting a public burning." He smacked his lips. "Big draw, an oath-breaker. Last one drew half the city."

Ines looked into her tea and saw, very clearly, her own shop going up in flames.

Not the shop, exactly. The *life*. The little bell over the door that she'd polished every morning because the sound pleased her. The shelf of harmless charms — luck-knots and sweetwater beads and little tin birds that whistled when the wind caught them — each one made by hand, each one legal, each one a small daily lie that magic could be this gentle. The stool behind the counter with the wobbly leg she'd never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting she planned to stay. The upstairs room with its narrow bed and its single window looking out on Chandler's Row, where she'd lain awake so many nights listening for boots.

Seven years. She'd built it one careful brick at a time, a fortress disguised as a storefront, and it had taken Captain Vess one night and a torch to unbuild it.

She realized distantly that the carter was still talking, that Tam was saying something low and urgent through his teeth, that her tea had gone cold in her hands.

"—Ines. *Ines.*"

"Hm?"

"I said we have to move. If they're offering fifty crowns, every cutpurse between here and the mountain will be looking for you."

"Yes." She set the cup down. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. "Yes, you're right."

But she didn't stand up. She sat there a moment longer, in the warmth of the bad fire, in the smell of wet wool, and let herself grieve — quickly, privately, the way you'd grieve for a stranger whose funeral you were passing on the road. Because that was what the old life was now. A stranger. A woman named Ines Pemberton, charm-seller of Chandler's Row, who rose at dawn and polished a bell and told herself every night that hiding was the same as living.

The hunters had burned her shop. But standing over the ashes, Ines suspected, was the truth: they hadn't burned anything she hadn't already been slowly losing, one vigilant day at a time. The shop had just been the body. The life inside it had died years ago, quietly, of concealment.

It didn't make the ashes hurt less.

* * *

They took the back road out of Grayhollow before noon, up through scrub pine where the wind talked to itself and nobody talked to anybody.

Tam walked ahead. He'd barely spoken since the inn, and when he finally did, it came out rough: "I'm sorry. About your shop."

"Don't be. You didn't light it."

"I led them to you. Vess followed me, Ines. I know she did. I was asking questions in Ashfall for a month before I ever found you — loud questions, stupid ones. I'm the reason she had your name."

Ines considered this. It was mostly true, and he needed her to say so, and lying to him would cost more than honesty. "Probably," she said. "Though I expect she'd have found me eventually. I was a loose thread. Vess strikes me as a woman who hates loose threads."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you feel *accurate*. Better comes later, if at all." She climbed over a fallen log, her knees complaining. "Besides, if you want to assign blame properly, start with the man who bled out on my floor and handed me a map. Or with whoever wrote the law that makes souls currency. There's blame enough to go around. We can all have some."

That startled a short laugh out of him, sharp and unwilling. "You do that."

"Do what?"

"Make jokes at the exact moment a normal person would panic."

"I panicked years ago," Ines said. "I'm all paniced out. What's left is jokes and walking." She paused, then, because fairness demanded it: "For what it's worth — I don't regret it. Taking the map. Even now."

He glanced back at her, and for once there was no suspicion in his face, only a raw, tired hope that hurt to look at. "Even now? With your name on a warrant and your life in ashes?"

"*Especially* now. My life was in ashes long before Vess brought the torch." She heard her own voice come out quieter than she intended. "I spent seven years being very careful, Tam. Careful isn't the same as safe. It's just slow grief with better posture."

He didn't answer that. But he slowed his pace until they were walking side by side instead of him dragging ahead, and that was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a granite shelf, a small fire screened by stones, hard bread toasted soft on sticks. The stars over the Serpent's Back were brutal and clear, the kind of sky that made you feel like a coin someone had dropped.

Ines couldn't sleep. She lay wrapped in her cloak watching the fire settle and found her mind returning, again and again, to the shop. To small things. The way morning light came through the front window and turned the dust to gold. The regulars — old Marta who bought a luck-knot every month whether she needed one or not, the cooper's boy who saved his coppers for the whistling birds. Had Marta watched it burn? Had the cooper's boy? Were they being questioned right now, asked what they knew of Ines Pemberton, and answering honestly: *nothing, nothing, she was just a charm-seller, she was quiet, she was kind.*

Kind. She clung to that word like a handhold. Whatever else the fire had taken, it couldn't reach backward and unmake seven years of kindness. The luck-knots were still knotted. The birds still whistled, wherever they'd survived. Somewhere in Ashfall, people who'd bought her small harmless magics were carrying them still, and every one of them was a tiny rebellion Vess could never find.

"You're doing the thing again," Tam said from across the fire.

"What thing?"

"The face. Like you're chewing on a thought too big to swallow."

Ines huffed. "My face has opinions. I apologize for them." She fed a stick to the fire. "I was thinking about my bell."

"Your bell?"

"The bell over my shop door. I polished it every morning. Silly habit. It had a particular sound — bright, you know? Most shop bells are dull. Mine sang." She smiled at the fire, and it ached. "I used to think, someday, when it was safe — whatever that means — I'd take it down and keep it. Hang it in a house instead of a shop. Ring it just because."

Tam was quiet a moment. Then, gruffly: "Vess didn't burn the whole row. Just your building, the carter said. Bell might've survived. Brass doesn't melt easy."

"She'd have looted it. Hunters loot. It's half their pay."

"Then buy another bell."

"It wouldn't sing the same."

"No," Tam agreed. "Nothing does." And Ines understood that he wasn't talking about bells anymore — that he was thinking of his sister, hollowed out and fading somewhere in Ashfall, the girl-shaped absence of her, and how nothing sung the same without her. Grief sat down between them at the fire, familiar to them both, and for a while none of them spoke, and it was almost companionable.

Then Tam sat bolt upright.

"Ines. The carter. At the inn."

"What about him?"

"He said Vess wants you *breathing*. For a public burning." His eyes were wide and white in the firelight. "Why? Why would a witch-hunter care about ceremony? She burned a charm-seller on Chandler's Row without blinking. Why save the spectacle for you?"

Ines felt the cold arrive slowly, the way night cold comes up through the ground. "Because a charm-seller's only worth a pyre," she said. "An oath-bound swordspeaker's worth a trial. Confession. Names." Her mouth had gone dry. "Vess doesn't just want me dead, Tam. She wants me to give her the Order. Every name I ever knew. Every sympathizer. She wants me to burn *usefully*."

"And to get that—"

"She has to take me alive." Ines stared into the coals. "Which means she won't risk killing me. Which means—" She stopped. Started again. "Which means she'll follow us. All the way. She knows where the map leads. She's known since the man died in my shop — maybe before. Maybe *he* was running from her when he got here."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, a night bird called once and thought better of it.

"So we're not running toward the mountain," Tam said slowly. "We're running toward it *and* she's running behind us, and we all end up in the same place."

"The court beneath the mountain." Ines pulled her cloak tighter. "Where souls are bought and sold under false law, and where, apparently, everyone I owe and everyone who hunts me is converging like guests to a wedding nobody wants."

"Charming."

"I do try."

But the humor rang hollow even to her own ears, and Tam saw it. He poked the fire apart into brighter flame, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its edge and gained something steadier underneath.

"Then we get there first," he said. "We get Wren, and we get out, and Vess can burn her speeches to an empty courtroom." He looked at her across the fire, and for the first time since the aqueduct, Tam Calloway sounded like someone who believed what he was saying. "Right?"

Ines looked past him, north, to where the Serpent's Back rose black against the stars — the mountain with its buried court, its vault of sold voices, its Pale King who traded in souls. Two days away. Maybe three.

And behind them, forty miles of dark road, a captain with a torch and a warrant and all the time in the world.

"Right," Ines said.

She didn't sleep that night. And near dawn, on the wind coming down off the pass, she heard — or dreamed she heard — a sound she hadn't heard in seven years, thin and far away and unmistakable.

A bell. Bright as morning. Singing.
