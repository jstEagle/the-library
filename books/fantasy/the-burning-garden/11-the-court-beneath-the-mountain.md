## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain announced itself long before we saw it.

We had been walking for two days through country that grew stranger with every mile — fields where the wheat grew in perfect spirals, a river that ran uphill for a hundred paces before thinking better of it, an orchard of trees that had shed their leaves in midsummer and stood bare and embarrassed in the sun. Tam called it "the neighborhood going downhill." I called it the borderland, the soft gray hem of the world where the mountain's court bled outward into ordinary soil.

Then, on the morning of the third day, we crested a ridge of black stone and there it was.

I had imagined a mountain. What stood before us was more like a tooth — a single vast fang of pale rock rising out of the earth, its sides too smooth to be natural, its peak lost in a permanent ring of cloud. And carved into its face, descending from somewhere near the clouds all the way down to a plain of white gravel, was a road. Not cut into the stone. Carved — every step of it shaped like a spine, vertebra after vertebra, so that the whole mountain looked like something buried alive, waiting.

"Well," said Tam, who had been uncharacteristically quiet since dawn. "That's not ominous at all."

"No," I agreed. "It's very welcoming. Look, it's practically smiling."

"It's a mountain, Ines."

"A smiling one."

He snorted, and I was glad of it. Gladness had been in short supply. Since Ashfall burned — since Vess put my shop and everything in it to the torch and nailed my name to every post between there and here — we had walked mostly in silence, two fugitives sharing bread and blame. He still hadn't forgiven me for refusing to hand over the map back in the aqueducts, though he'd stopped demanding it, which I counted as progress. I still hadn't forgiven him for being right about his mother, which I counted as my own failing.

But the mountain shut us both up properly, and we descended toward it together.

* * *

The gates were not what I expected, and I don't know what I expected. Doors, perhaps. Portcullises. Something you could knock on.

Instead, the road ended at a plaza of white gravel so fine it looked like ground bone, and at the far end of the plaza stood a wall of pale stone with no gate in it at all — only a doorway-shaped absence, a rectangle of deeper dark, like a hole punched in the world. Standing before it were figures. A dozen of them, maybe more, arranged in a loose line: merchants, pilgrims, a woman leading a mule loaded with cages, two men in the gray livery of some northern guild. All of them waiting. None of them speaking above a murmur.

And moving among them, unhurried as moths, were the court's servants.

They were tall — taller than any folk I'd seen — and thin as winter reeds, wrapped head to foot in cloth the color of old candle smoke. Their faces were veiled. Their hands were bare, and their hands were wrong: too many joints, fingers that moved like they were listening to music no one else could hear. One of them drifted past us without seeming to walk, its feet making no sound on the gravel, and paused beside the woman with the caged mule. It leaned down. It whispered something. The woman went white, handed over a small velvet pouch, and hurried inside the dark doorway as if late for an appointment with her own funeral.

"Charm-sellers' entrance is on the left," Tam murmured, reading a sign I couldn't see. "Wait. There's a *left*? There's no left. It's a hole."

"There's always a left," I said. "Hells are bureaucratic. Everyone knows that."

I had spent the last mile coaching him, and I coached him again now, low and quick, while we stood at the edge of the plaza pretending to study the mountain. The plan was simple, which meant it would probably fail, but simple plans were the only kind I could carry in my head alongside the constant drumbeat of *don't speak, don't speak, don't speak.*

I was a merchant of charms. Small charms, honest-seeming ones — luck-knots, fever-strings, little braided tokens against nightmares. Trinkets, the kind I'd sold in Ashfall for seven years, harmless enough that even a witch-hunter might buy one for his sweetheart. Merchants were allowed in the court; the court *loved* merchants, because merchants brought coin, and coin could be weighed against souls, and souls were the only currency the Pale King truly respected. Tam was my hired porter. Big, quiet, dull. A man whose entire personality fit in a sack.

"You can do that," I told him. "Dull. You've done dull your whole life."

"I have never done dull in my life," Tam said, offended. "I did loud. Loud got things done."

"Loud got your sister's soul sold," I said, and immediately regretted it, because it wasn't fair — it had been their mother, and grief made me crueler than I meant to be — but the words were out, and Tam flinched like I'd struck him, and then set his jaw in that way he had, the stubborn way, the Calloway way, and said:

"Fine. Dull. But if anyone asks me a direct question, I'm answering honestly."

"That's the opposite of what I need."

"That's what you're getting." He shouldered the pack — our packs, merged into one, because two travelers invited twice the questions — and added, with terrible cheer, "Besides, honesty's disarming. Nobody suspects an honest fool."

I wanted to argue. I didn't have time. One of the veiled servants had turned its head toward us, and though I could see nothing of its face, I felt its attention land on me like a cold coin dropped down my collar.

*Don't speak,* sang the old discipline in my blood. *Smile. Bow. Be small.*

We crossed the plaza.

* * *

The queue moved the way queues move everywhere — slowly, pointlessly, with sudden inexplicable bursts of speed designed to humiliate whoever had just relaxed. I used the time to look, because looking was free and looking might keep us alive.

The court traded openly. That was the thing that curdled me most. In Ashfall, magic hid in cellars and behind false walls; here it was displayed like fish on ice. Along one side of the plaza, under a canopy of gray silk, stalls had been set up, and at them the veiled servants conducted business with the line of visitors. I watched a fat merchant in green trade a leather ledger — thick, much-thumbed, worth real money in any city — for a stoppered glass vial in which something small and bright swam in slow circles, like a goldfish made of lamplight. I watched a young soldier hand over a folded letter, kissing it first, and receive in exchange a tiny silver bell that rang with no wind. I did not know what any of it meant. That was the horror of it. Every transaction was legible and meaningless at once, a market where I couldn't read the prices.

"They're buying memories," Tam said quietly beside me. "Or selling them. Or both. Look — the ledger man. He gave up his accounts. His whole life's work. For a light in a bottle."

"How do you know it isn't worth it?"

"How do you know it is?" He grinned at me, sharp-edged. "See, this is why honesty works. I'm just saying what everyone's thinking."

"Everyone is thinking 'get me through that door alive,'" I said. "Say that instead."

The servant reached us before he could answer.

It stopped a full arm's length away — they kept distance, always, as if closeness cost them something — and inclined its veiled head, and asked, in a voice like a wet finger on glass, "*What do you carry?*"

I had prepared for this question the way I prepared for everything: with rehearsed lies, three deep. "Charms," I said. "Small ones. Luck-knots, fever-strings. Nothing the court would want. I come to sell, not to buy."

The servant's hidden face tilted. Its fingers flexed, all of them, slowly, like a spider tasting air. Then it said, "*Merchants of small things are welcome. The court honors small things.*" And there was something in the phrasing — something almost gentle, almost sad — that lodged in me like a splinter. *The court honors small things.* Of course it did. Small things were easy to take.

It waved us toward the doorway, and I felt, absurdly, relieved — until I remembered Tam.

Tam, standing beside me, had gone rigid with the effort of dullness, and was doing badly at it. His eyes kept sliding sideways to the stalls. His mouth kept twitching toward commentary. And when the servant passed him by without a glance — porters were beneath notice, which was the plan working perfectly — Tam took it as an insult to be corrected.

"*Excuse me*," he said, loudly, to the departing servant. "I'm with her. I'm her porter. I carry things. Very heavy things. You should register me."

The servant turned back around.

I closed my eyes. I breathed. I thought about drowning him in the nearest fountain, and decided the paperwork would be worse.

"What is your name?" the servant asked Tam.

"Calloway," said Tam, honestly, because of course he did. "Tam Calloway. Porter. Strong back. Weak mind. That's what she tells people anyway—"

"*Calloway.*" The servant repeated the name the way a jeweler turns a stone to the light, and something in its voice changed — sharpened, or softened, I couldn't tell which, but it *changed*, and my stomach dropped through the gravel. Because Calloway was Wren's name. Wren, whose soul hung in a vault beneath our feet, dim and flickering, sold by her own mother. If names meant anything here — and in this court, names were the whole economy — then Tam had just spoken his sister's name into the mountain's hearing, and the mountain had heard.

The servant asked, softly, "*Do you have kin within the court, Tam Calloway?*"

And Tam — bless him, curse him, I would never decide which — went still, and thought, actually thought, for the first time in my memory of him, and said: "I don't know. Maybe. My family's scattered. I'm looking for someone."

"*Everyone here is looking for someone,*" the servant said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow crueler, and it gestured us both toward the dark doorway with a hand like a bundle of grey twigs, and let us pass.

* * *

Inside, the mountain swallowed sound.

The passage beyond the doorway sloped gently down, wide as a barn, floored with the same white gravel, lit by nothing I could identify — the walls themselves seemed to give off a faint, sourceless glow, like moonlight that had gotten lost and decided to stay. Our footsteps made no echo. Our voices came back to us flattened, deadened, as if the air itself were padded. I had been in crypts that felt warmer.

And lining the passage, set into niches in the walls at intervals, were lanterns.

Hundreds of them. Glass, each one, round-bellied and stoppered, and inside each one burned a light that was not fire. Some blazed steady and warm as hearth-coals. Some guttered. Some were nearly out — thin blue threads of brightness, shivering, on the edge of dark. They hung at eye level, patient, numbered on little plates of bone beneath them, and as we walked past, I found myself unable not to read the numbers, unable not to wonder, and the wondering sat in my chest like a stone.

Tam stopped at one. A dim one. He didn't touch it — touching was surely death, or worse — but he leaned close, the way you lean close to a sickbed, and cupped his hand near the glass without touching, and stared.

"This could be her," he said. "One of these could be Wren."

"I know."

"She'd be dim. She's been fading for years. Dim like—" He turned the lantern's number plate toward himself, squinting. "There's no names. Just numbers. How would we ever find her?"

"The map," I said. "The vault has a shape. The map will tell us which shelf, which row. Names or numbers, paper doesn't lie."

Tam nodded, and then did something that undid me a little: he bowed to the lantern. Actually bent at the waist, formally, the way you'd bow to an elder. "Just in case," he said, straightening. "If it's you in there, Wren — hang on. We're coming. Don't go out. Whatever you do, don't go out."

The lantern did not answer. Lanterns never do. But I watched it as we walked on, and I would swear — I would swear on my oath, on my order, on every grave I never got to mark — that its thin blue thread burned a shade steadier after he spoke. Call it imagination. Call it hope. Seven years underground had taught me the difference between the two, and this was neither. This was something older, and it lived in voices, and it knew its own.

* * *

The passage opened, all at once, into the court itself.

I am a novelist by trade — or I was, before novels became a liability and trinkets became a living — and I have spent my life trying to describe impossible things in plain words. I failed completely, standing there. I'll fail now. Bear with me.

The cavern was vast beyond sense, a hollowed heart of the mountain, and its ceiling was hung with ten thousand lanterns at every height, so that looking up was like looking into a night sky that had been organized by someone with strong opinions. Terraces of pale stone descended in rings toward a central floor, and on the terraces: the court. Veiled servants gliding in processions. Merchants at long tables, weighing coins against glass lanterns on scales of black iron. Pilgrims kneeling. A musician playing an instrument like a cross between a harp and a rib cage, producing notes that arrived slightly before he plucked them. Everything pale, everything quiet, everything *patient*. At the far end, high above all the terraces, a throne of white stone stood empty.

Empty. I noted that. I held onto it. An empty throne is either good news or very bad news, and I wouldn't know which until I knew where the king was.

"Dreadful," Tam whispered, delighted. "It's dreadful. It's wonderful. Ines, it's the worst place I've ever seen and I grew up next to a tannery."

"Hush."

"There's a *market*. There's a whole market. Do you see the scales? They weigh *coins* against *souls*, that's — that's obscene, that's magnificent, I need a closer look—"

"TAM."

A passing servant's head rotated toward us, slow as a weather vane. I grabbed Tam's sleeve, hauled him behind a pillar shaped like a stalactite that had given up halfway, and performed the fastest, fiercest whisper of my life.

"You are a porter. Porters do not comment. Porters do not admire. Porters stand next to sacks and think about grain prices. Can you think about grain prices?"

"I can think about how much grain costs, yes."

"Wonderful. Think it *silently*."

"But look at the scales—"

"I *saw* the scales." My voice cracked on it, and I steadied myself against the pillar, and for a moment the dread and the wonder got tangled up together exactly as I'd feared they would, because he was right, it was magnificent, it was the most beautiful ugly thing I had ever seen, a cathedral built out of other people's stolen light, and part of me — the novelist part, the part that had survived seven years of hiding by staying curious — *wanted* to look. Wanted to see everything. Wanted to write it all down someday, if there was a someday, in a book no witch-hunter would ever burn.

And underneath the wanting, cold and constant as groundwater: *somewhere in this mountain is a girl named Wren, and somewhere in this mountain is a vault, and somewhere in this mountain is a law written in my own true name, and if I say that name aloud, every soul sold under false contracts goes free — and so, probably, do I, in the other direction, as ash.*

Not yet. Not yet. First: the vault. First: the girl. First: survive the market.

I straightened, fixed my merchant's smile — seven years of practice, it snapped on like a lamp — and steered us toward the long tables, where a servant with a ledger was beckoning to new arrivals.

"Charm-seller," I announced to the room at large, warm and clear and utterly false. "Small goods. Honest weights. Who buys?"

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble reliably does with Tam, within four minutes.

To be fair to him — and I try, I genuinely try, the way you try with a dog that keeps eating the furniture — he was not trying to cause trouble. He was trying to *help*. We had established ourselves at a corner table, me laying out my wares with the practiced patter of a woman who'd done this in a hundred markets: luck-knots, fever-strings, a tray of little brass bells I'd bought in a border town specifically for authenticity. Business was, unbelievably, functioning. A veiled servant examined my luck-knots with its too-many-jointed fingers and pronounced them "*charmingly small*" — the court's highest praise, apparently — and actually purchased three, paying in coins that were warm to the touch and slightly heavier than they should be. I was beginning to believe we might do this. I was beginning to feel, dangerously, almost comfortable.

And then a merchant at the next table — a rat-faced man dealing in bottled dreams, by the smell of his stock — mentioned, loudly, to no one, that the court had recently come into a rare acquisition. A soul of unusual quality. Young. Sold whole, not in pieces. Kept special.

Tam's chair scraped.

I saw it happen the way you see a cart crash: in horrible, complete detail, too fast to stop. Tam turning. Tam leaning across the aisle. Tam saying, in a voice pitched to carry across the entire cavern, "Whole? You mean unspent? There's a soul here that hasn't been spent? Whose? *Whose is it?*"

Every veiled head in earshot turned.

The rat-faced merchant smiled with all his teeth. "Eager, friend? Souls of quality draw quality buyers. Though I warn you—" he tapped his nose, savoring it, "—the price is steep. The court doesn't sell those cheap. Equal weight, freely given. Or—" and here his voice dropped to a purr that carried anyway, because the court's acoustics were built for exactly this, "—or the true name of an oath-bound speaker, spoken aloud. But nobody's seen one of *those* in years. They're all dead. Or hiding."

Somewhere above us, in the ten thousand lanterns, a thousand small lights flickered at once — or I imagined they did.

And Tam, poor doomed honest Tam, opened his mouth to say something else, something louder, something worse — and I did the only thing left. I stood up, seized his collar with both hands, and kissed him full on the mouth, the way a wife kisses a husband who has just embarrassed her at market, to general applause and several knowing chuckles from the dream-merchant's table.

"*Forgive him,*" I announced brightly, to the room, my heart hammering against my ribs like something caged. "New money. No manners. Darling — *sit down.*"

Tam sat down. Tam, to his credit, sat down very quickly, very red, and said nothing at all, which may have been the first silence of his entire life.

Under the table, my hand found his wrist and gripped it hard enough to leave marks, and I leaned close, smiling for the room, whispering for him alone:

"Equal weight, freely given. You heard him. That's the price for Wren — a soul for a soul, or a speaker's name. So listen to me, Tam Calloway, and listen well. If you offer yourself in there, I will drag you out by your ears. And if anyone in this court ever learns what *I* am—"

Across the cavern, high on its terrace, the empty throne was no longer empty.

A figure sat in it now. I had not seen it arrive. No one had. It simply *was* there, pale as the stone, veiled like the servants but robed in white, and though the distance was impossible, though the cavern was vast, I felt its attention move across the market below — stall by stall, face by face — the way a lighthouse beam moves across water.

It reached our table.

It stopped.

And somewhere in the vaults beneath my feet, I would have sworn on anything you asked me to swear on, a small dim lantern flickered — and whispered a word I had not heard spoken aloud in seven years.

My name.
