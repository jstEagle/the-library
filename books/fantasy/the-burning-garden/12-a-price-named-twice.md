## 12. A Price Named Twice

The Pale King did not speak.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when people asked, and they would ask, over and over, the way people ask about a fire or a shipwreck — I would have to explain that the ruler of the court beneath the mountain never once opened his mouth. He simply *looked* at you, and the looking was the conversation. You answered questions you hadn't heard. You agreed to terms you couldn't remember being offered.

It was, I decided somewhere around the third minute of sitting under that attention, an extremely efficient way to run a government, and I hated it completely.

Beside me, Ines had gone very still in the way she went still when things were bad. I'd catalogued her stillnesses by then. There was merchant-still, which was just her haggling face gone quiet. There was hunter's-passing-still, which came with a particular tightening at the jaw and usually preceded her grabbing my sleeve and steering us into an alley. And there was this — a stillness like held breath, like a door braced shut from the inside.

I'd learned not to touch her when she went still like this. But I sat close enough that our shoulders almost touched, which was its own kind of message: *I'm here. I'm loud. If anyone needs distracting, I'm your man.*

The throne terrace overlooked the whole cavern market, and from up here the court beneath the mountain looked almost beautiful. Lanterns everywhere — hanging from wires strung between stalactites, clustered on stalls, bobbing in the hands of veiled servants — each one burning with a soft light that didn't flicker so much as *breathe*. I knew what they were now. Wren had told me about them years ago, back when I thought she was being a strange, sad child with too many nightmares. Souls. Thousands of them. Sold, traded, gambled away, hung up like inventory.

Somewhere down there was hers.

"Merchant Pemberton." The voice came from beside the throne, and I nearly jumped out of my skin, because I hadn't seen anyone approach. A servant — or something shaped like one — stood at the edge of the terrace, veil stirring though there was no wind down here. "The King bids you welcome."

"He does?" Ines said. Her voice was smooth as poured oil. Seven years of selling trinkets to strangers will teach you that. "I hadn't heard him say so."

"The King does not say." The servant's tone suggested this was a lesson taught to children. "The King *is*. He has been watching your stall. Your charms are crude but honestly made. This is rare, here."

"I'll have that carved on my sign," Ines said.

"And you." The veil turned toward me. "Tam Calloway. You have been asking after a redemption."

Every clever thing I'd planned to say evaporated. What came out instead was: "You know my name."

"We know all names here," the servant said gently. "Names are the smallest coin we trade."

*There it is again,* I thought. *Coin.* Everything in this place was money. Not gold — I'd figured out quickly that gold meant nothing beneath the mountain — but the other kind. Years off a life. A memory. A talent for whistling. The color blue, if the stories were true, though I'd stopped believing that story around the time I watched a woman pay for a pastry with the memory of her wedding day and walk away smiling like she'd gotten a bargain.

"My sister," I said. "Wren Calloway. Her soul was sold here eleven years ago. I want it back."

The servant tilted its head, and I felt the Pale King's attention sharpen on me like a blade laid flat against the skin. Not cutting yet. Just letting me feel the edge.

"You want it *back*," the servant repeated. "Not bought. Not bartered. Back. As though it were a coat left at a friend's house."

"It wasn't sold fairly."

"Nothing here is sold unfairly." For the first time, something like warmth entered the veiled voice, and it was worse than the coldness. It was the warmth of a priest explaining doctrine he genuinely loves. "That is the great lie told about us, merchant's son. Above, they say the court cheats. We do not cheat. Every contract beneath this mountain is *exact*. The price named is the price paid. Your mother came to us with a debt that would have enslaved her whole family — and I do not mean worked to death, boy, I mean *owned*, body and ledger both, by creditors who would have taken her children into the bargain. She offered us the only thing of sufficient value she possessed. One soul, unspent, young. In exchange: the debt dissolved, the family freed, no mark upon any of you above ground. She paid exactly what was asked. No more." A pause. "She wept beautifully, if it matters. They always do."

My hands had curled into fists without consulting me. Beside me, Ines's shoulder pressed against mine — deliberately, this time. *Steady.*

"She didn't understand what she was giving," I said.

"On the contrary. She understood perfectly. That is why it was worth so much." The servant folded its hands. "But you did not come all this way to litigate an old contract. You came to redeem. So let us speak plainly, as merchants do, and I will name the terms of redemption, which have not changed since before your city had walls."

The lanterns below seemed to dim slightly, all together, like the market itself leaning in to listen.

"A soul may be redeemed in two ways. The first: by a soul of equal weight, freely given. Not coerced. Not tricked. *Freely* — the giver must stand where you stand, hear what you have heard, and give anyway. The court weighs such gifts carefully. A grudging soul buys nothing. A desperate one buys less. Only a soul given in full knowledge and full love carries the weight required." The veil shifted. "Your sister's soul was young and unblemished when it came to us. Eleven years in the vaults have not diminished it — souls keep well; it is their owners who spoil. To match it, you would need to give a soul equally clean. Yours has debts, Tam Calloway. Resentments. Old angers nursed past their usefulness. These weigh a soul down like wet wool."

"I could still—" I started.

"You could still *try*," the servant said, with terrible kindness. "And the scales would tip, and you would belong to the vaults, and your sister would remain exactly where she is. We would keep both of you. Two lanterns where there was one. The King finds this an acceptable outcome; he is patient, and inventory is inventory."

Cold walked down my spine. Because the awful thing — the truly awful thing — was that some part of me had already done the arithmetic. Had been doing it since the bridge, since I'd handed over my mother's face to the toll-keeper and felt the hole where it used to be and understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, how much I had loved her underneath all the anger I'd built on top.

A soul of equal weight, freely given. Mine had resentments, sure. Wet wool. But I'd spent eleven years telling myself I'd trade anything for Wren. Here was the counter offering exact change.

*And the scales would tip.*

"And the second way?" Ines asked quietly.

The servant turned to her, and I saw — or imagined I saw — the veil pull tight, as though whatever wore it had drawn a breath.

"The second way is older than the first, and rarer than either. A soul bound by contract may be unbound by the true name of an oath-bound speaker, spoken aloud, freely, in this court, before the King." A pause long enough to fall into. "There were once orders of speakers whose words had weight enough to unmake what law had made. The crown burned most of them. The rest went silent. If such a speaker stood before the throne and gave their name freely — knowing the cost, as with all free gifts — every contract sealed under false law would crack like river ice in spring."

"And the cost?" Ines asked. Her voice was still smooth. But I was close enough to see her hand, resting on the table, and her knuckles had gone white as the marble.

"The cost," the servant said, "is that a name spoken freely belongs to whoever hears it. All of it. Forever. The King would hold the speaker's name as he holds every lantern in the vaults. And the crown's hunters, who have spent seven years listening for exactly such a voice, would hear it echo through every stone of this mountain and carry word to Ashfall before the echoes died." The veil inclined toward her, courteous as a headsman. "But of course, you are a charm-seller, merchant Pemberton. Such matters cannot concern you."

"No," Ines said. "They can't."

Liar, I thought. And then: *oh.* And then I very carefully thought nothing at all, because the Pale King's attention was still resting on us like a hand on the back of the neck, and I had a sudden animal certainty that this place could read thoughts the way I read a bill of sale.

* * *

They let us go after that — back down to the market floor, back to our rented corner near the stair, back to the pretense that we were two traders with nothing stranger than inventory on our minds. The veiled servants brought us food we didn't touch: pale bread, pale wine, fruit that tasted faintly of candle smoke.

Ines ate anyway. Methodically, the way soldiers eat. "Eat," she told me. "Whatever happens next, you don't make decisions hungry."

"That's what my mother used to say."

"I know." She said it softly, without looking up, and I remembered — with the strange secondhand tenderness you feel for a memory someone else lost — that she'd known my mother. Known her before. Before the debt, before the sale, before everything curdled. Ines had bought charms from Ma's stall once, years ago, back when Ma still smiled at strangers.

I ate the bread. It tasted like nothing, which somehow made it easier.

"So," I said, when I'd chewed through enough silence. "Equal weight, freely given."

"Don't," Ines said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"You were going to offer yourself. I could see you doing the sums while the veil talked. You've got the same look Wren probably gets when she's counting coins she doesn't have." She set down the bread and finally looked at me, and her eyes in the lantern-light were fierce and tired and older than the rest of her face. "Tam. Listen to me, because I've buried people who made noble trades, and the graves don't get less heavy just because the trade was brave. The servant told you the truth. Your soul is *lighter* than hers — not lesser, lighter — because you've spent eleven years carrying anger like ballast. You'd give yourself, and the scales wouldn't move, and Wren would spend eternity in a vault knowing her brother rotted beside her for nothing. Is that the rescue? Is that the gift?"

"It might work," I said stubbornly. "You don't know it wouldn't."

"No. I don't." Which was the worst thing she could have said, because hope is a worse poison than despair; despair at least kills clean. "But here's what I do know. The second way exists. A true name, spoken aloud. And there is exactly one person in this cavern who might carry such a name, and she is sitting across from you eating terrible bread, and she has not said one word about it, and you are going to respect that silence until she chooses to break it herself."

"I wasn't going to push her."

"You were going to throw yourself on the scales instead, which is pushing her by another road. Because if you die for nothing, she frees you of nothing and loses you besides, and I lose the only fool stubborn enough to follow me down here." She picked the bread back up. "So eat. And think about what 'freely given' actually means. It means nobody standing behind you with a knife. Including yourself."

I ate. And I thought about it, the way she'd told me to, really thought about it for the first time — not the shining version, the version where I stride up to the vaults and say *take me instead* and the music swells. The real version. The scales. The wet wool of my own soul, heavy with every year I'd spent hating Ma for the sale and loving her anyway and hating myself for the loving. The version where it doesn't work. Where Wren hangs in the dark forever next to a second lantern with my face on it, and the last thing I ever gave my sister was another grief to carry.

Nobility, I began to suspect, was mostly a costume that self-destruction wore to church.

"There's a third option nobody's mentioned," I said at last.

"Mm?"

"Nobody's mentioned it because it's insane. But the servant said contracts sealed under *false law* crack. False law. Whose law? The crown outlawed magic. The crown burned the speakers. The crown, as far as I can tell, wrote the rules that let a mother sell her daughter's soul to escape debt in the first place." I leaned forward. "What if the problem isn't the price? What if the problem is the law that set the price?"

Ines stopped chewing.

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I watched something move behind her eyes — fear, yes, a whole ocean of it, but underneath the fear something else, something that looked almost like relief, like a woman who has been holding a door shut for seven years hearing someone on the other side say *you can put it down now.*

"Tam," she said carefully, "if you keep talking like that in this place, someone is going to hear you."

"Good," I said. "Let them listen. Somebody down here should hear the truth for once, even if it's just the furniture—"

The lantern nearest our table guttered.

Not breathed. Not dimmed the way the others had. *Guttered* — flared wild, threw shadows up the wall like grasping fingers, and every veiled head in the market turned toward the great stair at the cavern's edge, where torchlight was descending that was not lantern-light at all. Orange. Harsh. The light of the world above, of pitch and pyres, of Ashfall.

And walking down the stair under a white parley-flag, armor gleaming, face lifted like a woman entering her own cathedral, came Captain Vess.

Beside me, Ines went still — the deepest stillness I knew, the door-braced-shut kind — and whispered, so quietly I almost missed it:

"She's not here for the girl."
