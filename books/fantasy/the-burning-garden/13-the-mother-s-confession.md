## 13. The Mother's Confession

Wren had learned to measure herself in small things.

The weight of a spoon in her hand. The warmth of a cup of tea against her palms — or rather, the memory of warmth, because the tea itself felt like nothing now, like holding a handful of river stones. The sound of her own laugh, which came out thin and strange, like someone else's laugh heard through a wall.

She kept a list. It lived on a scrap of paper tucked under her mattress, and every morning she added to it whatever she'd managed to feel the day before. *Bread, Tuesday. The smell of rain, Wednesday.* Some days the list held three things. Most days it held one. Lately it had been days between entries, and the paper was starting to look like a diary of a girl who was slowly being erased.

Her mother didn't know about the list. Her mother didn't know about a great many things, Wren suspected, though she couldn't have said how she knew. It was one of the few advantages of having no soul: you noticed what other people hid, because you had so little of your own to hide behind.

Tonight her mother sat by the fire mending a shirt that belonged to no one in the house. Tam's shirts were all accounted for. Wren's were too big for her these days; she swam in them like a heron in a coat. The shirt was a man's, and worn at the collar, and her mother stitched it with the particular fierce concentration she reserved for things that made her sad.

"Whose is that?" Wren asked.

Her mother's needle stopped.

"Just mending," she said. "It's good cloth. Waste not."

"It's Father's," Wren said. "From before."

Her mother said nothing, which was answer enough, and went back to sewing, and Wren stood in the doorway watching her and felt — nothing. That was the thing people didn't understand about hollowness. It wasn't sadness. Sadness would have been a gift. It was this: standing in a warm kitchen, looking at your grieving mother, and feeling like a visitor reading about it in a letter from a stranger.

"I'm going out," Wren said.

"It's late."

"I know."

"Wren—"

"I'll be back." And then, because some stubborn splinter of the girl she used to be still lived somewhere in her chest, she added, "Don't wait up."

The night was cold enough to bite. Wren walked without a coat because coats weighed too much lately, and cold was just another thing that happened to her from a distance, like weather in a story. She walked past the tannery, past the well, up the hill road toward the old shrine where she used to play as a child, before everything, before the debt and the winter and the year the family nearly drowned in what they owed.

She didn't know why she was going there. She only knew that something in her — not a feeling, feelings were gone, but something *structural*, something load-bearing — had begun to crack. She could feel it the way you feel a bridge groan underfoot. Three days ago she had looked at her own hands and not recognized them. Yesterday she had caught herself calling Tam by their father's name. This morning she had woken and for four full seconds not known who Wren was supposed to be.

The fading was speeding up. Whatever her mother had done — and Wren had known, hadn't she? She had always known, the way you know a scar is there even through clothing — whatever had been taken was taking the rest of her along with it, like a knot pulled out of knitting.

At the shrine she sat on the cold stone step and waited, because she had decided, in the wordless way of the hollow, that tonight was the night she would ask.

And sure enough — her mother came. Wren had counted on it. Mothers always knew where the breaking point was, even when they pretended otherwise. Footsteps on the hill road, quick and anxious, and then her mother's shape against the stars, breath steaming, wringing her hands the way she did when the ledger came due.

"You shouldn't be out here," her mother said. "You'll catch your death."

Wren almost laughed. Almost. It rose in her and died somewhere on the way, another passenger lost at the border.

"Mama," she said. "What did you sell?"

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for an owl to call twice and give up.

They were sitting on the shrine steps now, side by side, the way they used to sit when Wren was small and afraid of thunderstorms. Her mother's hands were folded in her lap like two birds pretending to sleep.

"What makes you think I sold anything?" her mother asked, and her voice was steady, which was how Wren knew she'd been practicing.

"The debt collectors stopped coming," Wren said. "Three winters ago. Overnight. Papa's debts were more than ten farms. And then one day a man in gray came to the door, and you spoke with him alone, and after that we ate meat twice a week." She turned to look at her mother, and it cost her something to turn her head, as if her neck had rusted. "I did the sums, Mama. I was eleven. I've been doing them ever since."

"You were a clever girl."

"I was a *hollow* girl." The words came out flat, but underneath them something moved, something deep and slow, like a whale turning over under ice. "I remember being clever. I remember liking it. Now I do sums the way a machine does sums, and I eat because Tam reminds me, and I sleep four hours a night because if I sleep longer I can't find my way back to waking. Tell me what you sold."

Her mother looked at the ground. Then at the sky. Then, finally, at Wren, and her eyes were wet, and Wren registered the tears the way you register a painting of tears — accurately, and from very far away.

"Your father borrowed from the wrong men," her mother said. "You know that part. What you don't know is what happens when you can't pay men like that. They don't take the house, Wren. Houses are cheap. They take *people*. Your father would have gone down the mines until his lungs gave out. Tam would have gone into their service, and their service eats boys. And you—" Her voice caught. "You were the youngest. You were worth the most."

"Because I was empty already?"

"Because children are," her mother said, simply, terribly. "Children are worth the most because they haven't spent themselves yet. A whole soul, never bartered, never sworn away. There are buyers beneath the mountain who pay a farm's ransom for a child's soul, whole and unmarked." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "The man in gray made me an offer. He said it wouldn't hurt. He said I could keep a portion. He said — he said you wouldn't even miss it."

"And you believed him."

"I wanted to believe him." The words tore coming out, and Wren watched her mother come apart with the same distant attention she gave everything, and yet — and yet — somewhere far below the hollow, something ached. "God forgive me, Wren, I wanted it to be true so badly that I made it true. I told myself it was like selling hair. Hair grows back. I told myself you'd never know. I signed the paper, and the man in gray put his hand on your sleeping head, and you sighed in your sleep, and I thought — I thought that was all it would be. A sigh."

Wren sat very still.

Below the ice, the whale turned again.

"How much," Wren said.

"Enough. Enough to clear everything and buy the mill lease and keep us fed through the famine year. Enough that we never went hungry again while everyone around us starved." Her mother laughed, a broken sound. "Do you know what the neighbors said? They said the Calloways were blessed. *Blessed.* Mrs. Hedley brought us a pie and told me God had smiled on our house." Her hands twisted in her lap. "I let her believe it. I stood in my kitchen full of bought bread and let a starving woman tell me God loved me."

The owl called again. Wren found she was gripping the stone step, her fingers white, and she examined the grip with curiosity, because she hadn't gripped anything in months. Anger, apparently, lived deeper than the rest of it. Anger had its own cellar, and the sale hadn't reached the cellar.

"You should have sold the mill," Wren said.

"There was no mill yet."

"Then you should have sold yourself. Your soul. You're the one who signed. You're the one who wanted to believe. Why is it *mine* that's gone?" Her voice cracked on the last word, and the crack surprised her — surprised both of them — and in the gap of it, for half a heartbeat, she felt fury, actual fury, hot and specific and *hers*, and it was the most alive she had been in three years, and she hated that, and she clung to it anyway, because it was hers.

Her mother took it. That was the terrible, admirable thing — she took the fury full in the face and didn't flinch and didn't defend herself.

"Yes," she said. "It should have been mine. I offered. The man in gray laughed at me. He said a mother's soul, half-spent on worry and guilt, wasn't worth a tithe of a child's. He said—" she swallowed— "he said the market knows quality."

Wren looked at her mother's hands. The hands that had braided her hair. The hands that had signed.

"I hate you," Wren said, testing the words like a tongue against a broken tooth. "I want you to know that. I want you to hear it plainly, because I can't feel it properly, and it isn't fair to either of us that I can't. Somewhere in me there's a girl who hates you with her whole heart, and she's right, and I can't reach her. All I get is the arithmetic. You traded me for bread. The sum is correct. The sum is unforgivable. And I can't even hate you *well*."

Her mother made a sound like something tearing.

"But," Wren said.

Her mother looked up.

"But you're telling me the truth," Wren said. "Finally. On a cold step, with no one watching, with nothing in it for you. You could have lied forever. I'd have faded quietly and you'd have buried a daughter who died of a wasting sickness, and everyone would have pitied you, and you'd have had the pie and the blessing and the lie." She turned her head, slow as a drawbridge. "So here's my arithmetic, Mama. I hate what you did. I cannot hate you. Both sums are correct. I don't know how to carry them in the same head, and I'm going to stop trying, and you're going to have to live with the remainder. So will I."

Her mother reached for her hand. Wren let her take it, and felt the touch arrive from a long way off, like light from a dead star, and her mother held on as if the hand were the last rope on a cliff.

"I'll get it back," her mother whispered. "However it costs. I'll go to the mountain myself. I'll—"

"No," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"*No.*" And the whale under the ice surged, and for a moment her voice didn't sound hollow at all; it sounded like a bell struck in an empty church. "You've paid enough in souls to that mountain. If anyone goes, it won't be you. Besides—" and here the strangest thing happened, the structural crack widening, letting in a draft of something that felt almost like hope— "someone's already going. Someone's already on the road."

Her mother stared at her. "Who? How do you know that?"

Wren opened her mouth to say *I don't know*, because she didn't, not in any way that would survive daylight —

and then she felt it.

Faint, flickering, impossibly far away, like a candle glimpsed through a keyhole at the bottom of a well: a warmth in the direction of the mountain. A voice, almost. Not words. Just — attention. As if something in the dark vaults beneath the world had lifted its head and turned toward her, the way a plant turns toward a window.

Someone was looking for her soul. Someone with a map, and a name, and a voice that made the vault itself lean closer to listen.

"Mama," Wren said slowly, standing, pulling her hand free, staring east toward the black shoulder of the mountain where the stars ended, "how long does it take to walk to Ashfall?"

"Ashfall? Weeks, if the roads are bad. Why—"

"Because," Wren said, and felt — actually felt, faint as a coin at the bottom of a fountain, but *felt* — the corner of her mouth move upward, "I think help just left home without telling anyone. And I intend to meet it halfway."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the shrine's old votive lantern — unlit for years, its oil long dried — flickered once, softly, and stayed lit.
