## 14. Vess Descends

The trouble with pretending to be a merchant of charms, Ines decided on her third morning beneath the mountain, was that eventually someone asked her to demonstrate one.

She had set up her little table of wares in the gallery outside the Court of Ledgers — a strip of velvet, a dozen trinkets she'd bought honestly in Ashfall years ago and never sold, and the confident patter of a woman who had absolutely nothing to hide. The court's servants drifted past in their grey livery, faces like unlit candles, and occasionally one paused to finger a luck-knot or a painted river-stone before drifting on. None of them bought anything. Ines suspected none of them could remember what buying was for.

"They're staring at you again," Tam murmured beside her. He had appointed himself her assistant, mostly so he could stand close enough to mutter complaints without being overheard. "The tall one. Third time he's circled."

"He's admiring the merchandise."

"Nobody here admires anything. I watched a man walk past a banquet table of roast pheasant yesterday and sigh like it was arithmetic."

"Then he's admiring my prices."

Tam snorted. Under the mountain's strange amber light his face looked younger than it had in Ashfall, the suspicion worn down to something almost like ease. He still flinched at loud sounds. He still checked the map's hiding place — now inside his boot, because Ines had refused to carry it after the toll-bridge — four times an hour. But he had also, two nights ago, fallen asleep mid-sentence while arguing about the ethics of soul-bartering, and when Ines draped her cloak over him he hadn't woken, which was as close to trust as either of them had come.

The map itself had proven maddeningly literal. *Follow the dry vein to where the water learned to sing,* it said, and then, helpfully, nothing else. They had walked three days through abandoned mine roads following a sound neither of them could describe afterward — not singing exactly, more like the memory of singing — until the tunnel opened without warning into the vast ribbed dark of the mountain's under-halls, and there were the gates, and there was the court, and there was no turning around, because behind them the tunnels had quietly filled with mist that Tam swore whispered names.

Ines had been in the court for three days and had learned three things.

First: the Pale King never appeared. His throne stood empty at the head of the great hall, a chair of black glass tall as a chapel, and yet everyone spoke as though he were sitting in it, watching, which was somehow worse.

Second: the court did not trade in coin. It traded in weight — memories, years, names, the small warm things people carried without knowing they carried them. A servant had paid for a loaf of bread with the taste of her grandmother's cooking, and had eaten the bread looking faintly puzzled, as though she couldn't recall why she'd wanted it.

Third: Wren Calloway was alive. A clerk with ink-stained fingers and the only genuinely kind face Ines had seen since the gates had confirmed it for a painted river-stone and a smile. *The girl is kept in the upper galleries,* the clerk had said, glancing over his shoulder. *Her light is small now. The King keeps small lights closest.* And then he had pocketed the stone and walked away, and Ines had gripped the table's edge until her knuckles went white, and Tam had said, very quietly, "We're close," in the voice of a man holding himself together with both hands.

So: three days of selling nothing, learning everything, and waiting for the plan to fail.

It failed on the fourth morning, at half past nine, with trumpets.

* * *

Not trumpets, precisely. Ines would learn later that the sound came from instruments grown rather than made, long pale horns that the gate-wardens carried on their shoulders like yokes. Whatever they were, the note they produced went through the stone floor and up through the soles of Ines's boots and settled somewhere behind her sternum, and every servant in the gallery stopped moving at once, the way a household stops when the master's carriage turns into the drive.

"A parley," said the ink-stained clerk, appearing at Ines's elbow so suddenly she nearly knocked over her display. He was wringing his hands. "A flag of parley has come to the gates. Do you know how long it has been since anyone came under a flag?"

"No," said Ines. "How long?"

"Neither do I," said the clerk, with the air of a man who found this deeply satisfying, and hurried off toward the great hall.

Tam materialized out of the crowd. "Parley means—"

"It means someone from the surface wants to talk instead of fight." Ines was already packing her tray with hands that wanted very much to shake and were being firmly forbidden to. "It means soldiers. It means—"

She stopped. Because there was really only one person it could mean. Vess had burned her shop. Vess had put her name on the fugitive rolls from Ashfall to the border stones. Vess had spent seven years building a career out of finding people who hid, and Ines had spent three days walking straight down the one road that led to her.

"She followed us," Tam said. He had gone the color of tallow. "Through the aqueducts, through the mines, she—"

"Or she let us lead her." Ines slung the tray's strap over her shoulder. "Either way, standing here won't change it. Come on."

"Where are we going?"

"To watch. If Vess is here to bargain, we need to hear what she bargains with. And if she's here for us—" Ines touched his elbow, briefly. "Then we need to know that too. Knowledge first. Running second. That's the order."

"You have a lot of orders for someone whose entire strategy so far has been 'walk toward the monster.'"

"It's worked so far."

"We're not dead," Tam conceded. "I'll grant you that. Though I've started to suspect we're only not dead on a technicality."

They joined the current of servants flowing toward the great hall, and Ines felt the old familiar tightening in her throat — the place where her magic lived, coiled and silent, pressing against the walls she'd built around it. Seven years of silence, and lately, down here in the dark, the silence felt less like safety and more like holding your breath underwater. She pushed the feeling down. Not now. Not yet.

* * *

The great hall beneath the mountain was built to make visitors feel like coins dropped into a well. Its ceiling vanished upward into gloom shot through with veins of dim gold; its pillars were carved, Ines realized on her first horrified day, with thousands upon thousands of names, each one smaller than the last, spiraling down into the stone like sediment. The Pale King's throne sat at the far end, black glass, magnificently empty.

Today it was not entirely empty. Today there was a figure standing before it — small against all that architecture, upright as a pike, wearing the dove-grey coat of the Ashfall witch-hunters with the silver brand of the Office gleaming at her collar.

Captain Maren Vess had not changed in the weeks since Ines had last seen her across a market street full of smoke. She was still narrow and neat, still wore her greying hair bound back so tightly it looked painful, still had the particular stillness of a woman who believed — completely, sincerely, with her whole heart — that she was the most reasonable person in any room she entered. That was the thing about Vess that Ines had never managed to hate properly. Zealots who ranted were easy to dismiss. Vess never ranted. Vess *reasoned*, patiently, at length, and burned you anyway, and looked sad about it.

And beside her, under a rag of white cloth tied to a spear, stood six hunters in mail, and between two of them—

Ines's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

Between two of the hunters, wrists bound, face bruised, chin up with the specific stubbornness of a boy determined not to give anyone the satisfaction of seeing him afraid, stood a girl of perhaps seventeen. Dark-haired. Thin. Familiar from a shrine lantern's glow and a brother's desperate descriptions.

"That's not—" Tam's whisper cracked. "That's not Wren. That can't be Wren, she's supposed to be *here*, she's supposed to be safe in the galleries—"

"Then who is it?"

"I don't—" Tam gripped the pillar they'd taken shelter behind. "Mama wouldn't let her leave the house. Mama barely lets her *stand by windows*. How—"

But even as he said it, Ines saw it: the way the girl moved. Or didn't. The guards steered her with a hand on each shoulder, and she went where she was pointed, feet placing themselves with careful blank precision, eyes fixed on the middle distance. Not drugged. Not broken. Just — absent. A house with nobody home, walking on legs that had been taught to work without instruction.

Wren Calloway, brought down the mountain in chains, hollow as a struck bell.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, no. No, no — Mama must have gone to the Office. Mama must have told them everything, traded Wren to them to get her away from the mountain, and Vess just — picked her up and carried her *down*—"

"Quiet." Ines's voice came out flat and cold, and she was distantly surprised by it. "Listen."

Vess was speaking. Her voice carried through the hall effortlessly, pitched for large rooms and larger judgments, and the acoustics of the place delivered it to every ear at once, intimate as a confession.

"—under flag of parley, by right of the Concord of Stone and Crown, which your court honored when my grandfather's grandfather held this office, and which I trust honors me still." A pause. Somewhere above, in the gold-shot dark, something shifted — a rustle like pages turning, like scales settling. "I come to offer a trade. One soul, freely offered, for one soul currently held."

The hall's silence deepened. Even the servants had stopped their drifting. Ines became aware that she and Tam were pressed against a pillar within thirty feet of the throne, hidden among a knot of grey-livered clerks, and that moving now would be death, and staying might be worse.

"The soul I seek," Vess continued, "belongs to a girl called Wren Calloway, whose mother sold her lawfully and in good faith, and whom the crown has a duty to protect. In exchange—" and here Vess turned, slowly, surveying the hall as though the person she sought might simply step forward if asked nicely, "—I offer the soul of Ines Pemberton. Last oath-breaker of the Order of the Speaking Sword. Murderer of hunters. Fugitive of Ashfall. Whose name I have carried in a warrant for seven weeks, and whose trail led me, at considerable expense, directly to your door."

The words landed in Ines like stones dropped into still water, each ring spreading outward, touching everything.

*Freely offered.* Vess had said *freely offered*, and meant it — meant that she had the right to offer Ines's soul, the way a magistrate has the right to offer a prisoner's labor. As though a soul were an asset on a ledger. As though Ines were a debt to be collected and transferred.

Beside her, Tam had gone rigid. His hand found Ines's sleeve and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "She can't," he breathed. "She doesn't *have* you. You're not hers to—"

"Hush."

"Ines, she's selling you. She tracked you across half the world to sell you like a—"

"*Hush.*" Because Vess was still speaking, and Ines needed every word.

"I am authorized," Vess said, drawing a folded document from her coat, "by the Crown's own seal, to certify this exchange. The girl returns whole to her family. The oath-breaker remains below, in payment of crimes the surface cannot punish adequately. Justice," she said, and her voice softened into something almost gentle, almost sorrowful, the voice of a woman laying flowers on a grave she herself had dug, "is not cruelty. It is balance. I take no pleasure in it. But the law requires a weighing, and I have brought the weights."

Above the empty throne, the darkness stirred again. And this time Ines heard it answer — not in words, not in any language, but in a pressure, a slow attention, like the moment before lightning chooses its ground. The Pale King, unseen, considering.

And Ines understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what Vess had actually done. It wasn't a trap for Ines. Well — it was, but it was more than that. Vess had come down the mountain carrying the one bargaining chip the court could not refuse: a soul of true weight, freely certified, delivered to the door. She had wrapped betrayal in the language of justice so carefully that even the betrayal probably couldn't see itself. Somewhere in that neat grey head, Ines thought, Maren Vess genuinely believed she was saving Wren Calloway. Genuinely believed that trading one condemned woman's soul for an innocent girl's was the cleanest arithmetic in the world.

That was the horror of it. Not malice. *Bookkeeping.*

"She'll say yes," Tam whispered. His face had crumpled. "The King will say yes. Why wouldn't he? It's everything he wants, gift-wrapped—"

"Because," Ines said, and heard her own voice steady in a way she didn't feel, "nothing down here is ever gift-wrapped. Watch."

The pressure above the throne gathered. When the Pale King finally spoke, his voice did not come from the throne at all. It came from everywhere — from the pillars, from the carved names, from the floor itself, a voice like a vault door opening in a very quiet room.

*"The Pemberton woman."* A pause, savoring. *"We know that name. We knew it before your warrant was written, captain. Names of that shape arrive here ahead of their owners."*

"Then you accept the trade," said Vess. Not a question. A statement with a bow attached.

*"We accept the soul,"* said the Pale King. *"Souls are our trade. But souls are not taken at the door like parcels, captain. There are forms. There are weighings. The Calloway girl's contract is old, and old contracts have old clauses."* The attention in the hall swung, slow as a searchlight, and settled — Ines felt it settle, a physical weight between her shoulder blades — somewhere in the crowd of clerks. Somewhere near a pillar. Near her. *"Bring the woman forward. Both women. Let them be weighed together, in the sight of the court. Tomorrow, at the turning of the deep bell."*

Two hunters detached from Vess's escort and began moving into the crowd, and Ines knew — with the sick certainty of a card player watching the deck turn against her — that Vess had described her to them down to the color of her cloak.

"Back way," she murmured. "Now."

"There's no back way, we checked—"

"Then a *side* way. Move."

They slipped backward through the knot of clerks, and Ines pulled the strap of her tray over her head and abandoned seven days of careful cover story on a table she'd never see again. Behind them, Vess's voice rang out once more, crisp and righteous and horribly sincere:

"Ines Pemberton! By the Concord and the Crown, I call you to yield yourself. Come forward, and the girl goes free tonight. Hide, and I will find you — I have always found you — and the girl stays in chains regardless. You have until the deep bell. Think on which choice leaves less blood on the stone."

The hall swallowed the echo. Somewhere far above, faint as a memory, a bell began to turn.

Tam caught her arm at the mouth of a side corridor, breathing hard. "You're not actually considering it. Going out there. Trading yourself."

Ines looked back, once, at the small grey figure by the throne gathering up her documents with tidy satisfaction, and at the hollow-eyed girl chained beside her, and at the black glass emptiness above it all, patient as a debt.

"I'm considering," Ines said, "how much I hate being someone else's arithmetic."

And down the corridor, in the direction of the upper galleries where a small dim light was kept close, a servant in grey livery was waiting for them — the ink-stained clerk, wringing his hands, jerking his head urgently toward a stair that did not appear on any map.

"You'll want to see the vault," he whispered, "before the bell turns. Before she does. Come quickly — and whatever you hear in there, don't answer it."
