## 15. The Vault of Sold Voices

The servant's name was Mott, and he had the face of a man who had spent a very long time being overlooked on purpose.

He was small and gray as a moth's wing, with hands folded before him like a man perpetually in prayer, or apology. He had appeared at Ines's elbow in the third corridor of the guest wing — she never saw him arrive; one moment there was empty air and a sconce guttering blue, and the next there was Mott, bowing.

"You'll want to eat something," he said, "before you do anything foolish."

"I'm not planning anything foolish."

"No one ever is," said Mott. "That's what makes it foolish." He set down a tray of bread and dark honey and a cup of something that steamed though the mountain air was cold enough to fog breath. "Eat. Then come with me, if you're brave. Or don't eat, if you'd rather be brave on an empty stomach. Some prefer it."

Ines ate. It seemed rude not to, and besides, the bread was good — better than anything she'd baked in Ashfall, which stung a little, professionally speaking.

Tam was locked somewhere in the lower cells, bargaining loudly through a door that did not care, and Ines had spent two hours pacing the guest chamber counting exits that all turned out to be walls when she tested them. So when Mott bowed again and said, "Now, if you please," she went.

* * *

They walked for a long time.

The court beneath the mountain was built like a thought someone couldn't stop having — corridors that doubled back into themselves, staircases that descended only to descend further, doors carved with faces whose expressions changed depending on whether you looked at them directly. Ines kept her eyes forward. She had learned, over seven years of hiding, that the safest way through a dangerous place was to look like you belonged to it: unhurried, unimpressed, mildly bored.

Mott walked ahead with his candle, humming.

"What are you?" she asked finally, because the silence was worse than the question.

"A servant," said Mott. "I was a man once, I think. It's been long enough that I've stopped checking." He glanced back at her, and his eyes were ordinary brown eyes, tired and kind, and somehow that was more unsettling than if they'd been flames. "You're the charm-seller. The one who came in with the loud boy."

"He's not my— we're traveling together. Briefly."

"Mm." Mott turned a corner into a corridor so narrow they had to walk single file. "He bargained for your life this afternoon. Did you know? Offered the King a memory. A good one, by the smell of it — paid memories have a smell, like rain on hot stone. Bought you an hour of freedom tomorrow, before your trial." He said it lightly, the way a man reports weather. "Strange thing, an hour. Down here it's worth more than up there. Time moves differently around souls."

Ines stopped walking. "He sold a *memory*? For me?"

"For Wren, probably, if you asked him. He'd say it was for Wren." Mott resumed walking. "Brothers are like that. They'd rather die than admit they love you, but they'll pay any price as long as nobody's watching them do it."

She followed him down, and down, and her chest felt strange and tight, and she decided it was the altitude.

* * *

The vault door was not locked.

That was the first wrongness. Ines had expected iron, chains, wards written in blood — everything the stories promised about the Pale King's hoard. Instead there was a plain archway of gray stone, and beyond it a cold draft smelling of snow and old candles, and Mott pausing at the threshold with his candle cupped, looking almost shy.

"No guards?" Ines whispered.

"Guards guard against thieves." Mott's voice had gone soft. "Who would steal these?" And he stepped aside and let her look.

The vault was a cavern the size of a cathedral, hollowed from the mountain's black heart, and it was full of light.

Souls hung from the ceiling on threads of shadow — hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small burning shape like a lantern blown from glass, swaying gently in a wind Ines could not feel. Some burned white and steady. Some were gold, some green, one far off a deep violet that made her teeth ache to look at. Their combined glow filled the cavern with a light like late afternoon in summer, golden and forgiving, and that was the second wrongness, because nothing in this place should have been forgiving.

"They don't suffer," Mott said quietly, watching her face. "I want you to know that. Whatever else happens here, whatever the King has done — they dream, in there. They wait. It isn't torment. It's only..." He searched for the word. "*Storage.* Like grain in a barn. That's almost worse, isn't it?"

"Yes," Ines said. Her voice came out rough. "It's worse."

She stepped under the lanterns, and the nearest ones brightened as she passed.

She noticed it at first as a trick of the eye — a flicker at the edge of her vision, a warmth blooming along her arm. But then the next soul brightened too, leaning toward her on its thread like a flower toward sun, and the next, and within ten steps the whole near end of the vault was rippling, a tide of small lights straining toward her, whispering.

Not whispering to each other.

To her.

"*Swordspeaker,*" said a voice like a struck bell, from a soul the color of dawn.

"*Speaker,*" breathed another, and another, a chorus rising soft as snowfall, "*speaker, speaker, speaker*" — and Ines stood frozen in the middle of the cavern with her heart slamming against her ribs, because no one had called her that in seven years, no one living, and here were the dead-light souls of the stolen bending toward her like pilgrims toward a shrine.

"Hush," she whispered, uselessly. "Hush, please—"

"They know," Mott murmured behind her. He had gone very still. "We forget, down here, working for the court. We pretend a soul is just... fuel. Coin. But a soul remembers what it was. And a soul knows a swordspeaker the way a bell knows a hand." His candle had begun to tremble. "I haven't seen them wake like this in three hundred years. Not since the last of your order came down here, and—" He stopped himself.

"And what?"

"Nothing," said Mott. "Old grief. Find what you came for. I'll watch the door, and I'll remember none of this, and neither will you, ideally."

* * *

Finding Wren's soul was not like finding a needle. It was like hearing your own name across a crowded market — impossible to miss once it started, unbearable until you reached it.

Ines walked the rows with her head tipped back, reading the lights. Most burned steady. A few guttered, thin and anxious. And then, in a far corner where the shadow-threads grew tangled and the light pooled low, she saw it: a small gray-blue flame, barely larger than a candle's, flickering like a lamp about to drown in its own oil.

"Wren," Ines breathed.

The little soul flared.

It leaned toward her so hard its thread went taut, and the whisper that came off it wasn't a word at all at first — just a sound, high and thin, the sound a girl makes crying in her sleep. Then it shaped itself, struggling, into language:

"*Cold. It's been so cold. Is somebody—*"

"I'm here." Ines reached up. Her fingers closed around air, around warmth, around something that felt like holding a bird that trusted you not to squeeze. The soul pressed into her palm, desperate, flickering hard. "*You're warm,*" it whispered. "*Nobody's been warm. Mama said it wouldn't hurt. Mama said—*" The whisper cracked. "*Did she sell me? Was it really her? I keep dreaming it and waking up and it's still true.*"

Ines's throat closed. She thought of Tam on the road, jaw set, insisting their mother had done it to save them, hating her and defending her in the same sentence. She thought of chapter thirteen of any family's story — the confession that never resolves cleanly, fury and forgiveness colliding and both walking away bruised.

"It was her," Ines said softly. "And she did it to keep your brother fed and your house standing, and both things are true at once, and I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

The soul trembled. Then, very faintly, it laughed — a girl's laugh, rusty with disuse, and Ines felt it like sunlight through a window.

"You sound like my brother when he lies badly," the soul whispered. "'Both things are true.' He says that. He learned it from a priest who owed him money." The flicker steadied, just slightly, feeding on conversation the way a fire feeds on air. "Are you going to get me out?"

"I'm going to try."

"The King won't let you. There's a price. There's always a price, he says it like it's poetry." The little flame dimmed with fear. "Unless..."

"Unless what?"

The soul went quiet a long moment. When it spoke again, its whisper had changed — deeper now, wondering, the way you might speak upon discovering something enormous in a small room.

"You're hiding your name," it said. "I can feel it. It's wrapped around you like bandages. Seven layers deep. You buried it so far down even *you* can't hear it anymore." The flame pulsed, gentle, insistent. "But I can hear it. We can all hear it. Every soul in this room has been listening to it since you walked in, the way the deaf lean toward music."

Ines's mouth went dry. "Don't—"

"*Ines.*"

The name came out of the little soul like a bell note, soft and clear, and the whole vault answered. Hundreds of lanterns flared at once, a wave of light rolling outward from her through the cavern, and the chorus rose — "*Ines, Ines Pemberton, speaker, last speaker, Ines*" — her true name spoken aloud by the stolen dead, ringing off the black stone, and Ines stood in the middle of it shaking, seven years of silence cracking open like river ice.

"Stop," she whispered. "Please. If anyone hears—"

"Everyone hears," said the soul of Wren Calloway, wonderingly. "Down here, everyone hears everything. That's how I knew you'd come. We've been waiting for a swordspeaker for years. We talk about it, in the dark between dreams. *One day one will come down, and speak, and the contracts will break like old rope.*" The flame leaned close, tender as a child. "Your name is the key, Ines. It was always the key. Why do you think it hurts so much to carry?"

Footsteps echoed in the corridor above.

Mott's voice, pitched low and urgent: "Someone's coming. Torchlight on the stairs — the Captain's colors. *Girl, hide.*"

Ines looked up one last time at the small gray-blue flame — at Wren, flickering, fading, waiting — and the choice that had been gathering in her chest for seven years settled into place like a sword coming home to its scabbard.

"Tomorrow," she whispered to the soul. "When they bring me before the throne. Hold on until tomorrow."

"We've held on this long," said Wren's soul. And then, as Ines turned to run for the shadows behind the far pillars, the little flame added, almost playfully, almost kindly:

"He's going to offer you a bargain, you know. The King. He always offers the swordspeakers a bargain first. Ask him what happened to the last one who refused."

The torchlight rounded the corner. Captain Vess's voice rang down into the vault, cold and certain as a verdict:

"She's down there. I can *smell* the magic on her. Bring her to me."
