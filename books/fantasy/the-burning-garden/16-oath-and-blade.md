## 16. Oath and Blade

The trap closed at the third bell of the court's long, lightless morning.

Ines should have seen it. Later—much later, in a valley that smelled of turned earth instead of ash—she would take herself apart piece by piece looking for the moment she missed it. But in truth there was no missed moment. Vess had simply waited until Ines stopped watching for her, which was itself the trap: the captain had counted on hope making her careless.

They were in the Hall of Ledgers, a vaulted gallery where the Pale King's clerks scratched out contracts on vellum made from something that was not sheepskin. Tam had gone ahead to argue with a clerk about Wren's contract—the boy bargained loudly and badly even now, seven days into the mountain—and Ines stood by a pillar pretending to be a charm-merchant counting her stock, which consisted of three copper bells and a lie.

She heard the boots first. Too many boots, moving in step, and witch-hunters never moved in step unless they wanted you to hear them coming. Fear was part of their trade.

"Ines Pemberton," said Captain Vess, from the far end of the hall, her voice carrying the way a blade carries light. "By writ of the Crown of Ashfall, I arrest you for the crime of concealed magic."

The clerks did not look up. That was the worst of it, Ines thought distantly. The court beneath the mountain did not care who burned above; they only cared whether the ink dried.

Tam spun around, and she saw him do the arithmetic—Vess, six hunters, one exit—and do it wrong, because he was nineteen and brave in the way that gets people killed. He grabbed the ledger off the clerk's desk. Wren's page. He actually tore the page out, holding it up like a torch.

"You want this? Then let her go!"

"Tam," Ines said quietly. "Put it down."

"He'll trade," Tam said, wild-eyed, to Vess, to the room, to no one. "Your king trades in everything. Souls, names, memories. Give me my sister and you can have me. That's a fair price. That's—" His voice cracked. "That's what she would have done for me."

*And there it is,* Ines thought. *The same offer he made me on the road. The same offer he'd make anyone.* It was his whole heart, laid out: *take me instead.*

Vess smiled. It was not a cruel smile, which somehow made it worse. It was the smile of a woman who had been handed exactly what she came for, gift-wrapped in a boy's desperation.

"Take him," she said.

Two hunters crossed the hall in four strides. Tam swung the torn page like a sword, which is what it was not, and then he was face-down on the ledger-strewn floor with a knee between his shoulder blades, and the page fluttered down somewhere near Ines's feet.

"Run," Tam said into the floor. It came out muffled and furious. "Ines, *run*, you idiot, don't—"

The second hunter backhanded him across the mouth. The sound went through the hall like a dropped plate.

Something happened to Ines then. She would spend years trying to name it, and the closest she ever came was this: it was as if a door she had nailed shut from the inside opened anyway, not because she pushed it but because the house itself decided it was done being dark.

Seven years ago, in the smoking ruin of the Order's chapterhouse, she had knelt among her dead and sworn an oath of silence. Not the order's oath—that one bound her voice to service. This one was hers alone, private, desperate: *I will never speak again. What I am has killed everyone I loved. Let it die with them.*

She had kept it through seven years of selling charms that did nothing, through seven years of biting her tongue until it scarred, through seven years of waking at night with words rising in her throat like floodwater against a door.

Now Tam Calloway lay bleeding on the floor of a soul-market, and the door broke.

"Captain Vess." Her voice came out low and strange to her own ears—not the shopkeeper's murmur she'd worn like a coat, but something older, resonant, a voice built for rooms larger than this one. Every head in the hall turned. Even the clerks looked up. "You are standing on ground that does not belong to your king."

Vess's smile faltered, recalculated. "There she is," she breathed. "The last swordspeaker. Do you know what your head is worth? Do you know what the king would pay for a voice like yours, alive and *bound*?"

"I know what you paid," Ines said. "I read the parley flag. I know you offered my soul for the girl's." She began to walk forward, slowly, hands open at her sides. "You didn't come here for justice, Captain. You came shopping."

"Hold her," Vess said. "Alive. The throat especially—"

"No," Ines said.

And she spoke the first word.

It was *shatter*—not the common word, the deep one, the one the Order had carved into stone doors so they could be opened by voice alone. She aimed it at the great iron chandelier overhead, and the chandelier burst into a rain of burning metal and light. Hunters scattered. A clerk screamed. The hall filled with smoke and the smell of scorched vellum.

The word burned going out of her. That was the thing no one had told her, or perhaps she had refused to remember: swordspeech was not casting. It was *spending*. Each true word drew heat up from somewhere below her sternum and tore it out through her throat, and her throat paid for every syllable in scorch and blood. She tasted copper. She felt the skin of her neck flush red as sunburn, then redder.

And underneath the pain—God forgive her, underneath the pain—was joy.

It was like a fist unclenching after seven years. Like a held breath finally released. Her voice filled the hall and the hall *answered*, stone humming, ledgers rattling in their shelves, and some buried animal part of her sang *yes, yes, this, this is what your voice is for.*

A hunter lunged at her with a weighted net. She spoke *turn* and the net whirled mid-air and wrapped its own thrower. She spoke *fall* and the man beside him sat down hard enough to crack flagstone. Each word cost her. She could feel blisters forming along her vocal cords like frost forming in reverse, feel her voice roughening toward ruin with every syllable, and she spent them anyway, greedily, like a miser suddenly allowed to give.

"Bind her!" Vess shouted, retreating behind two hunters with tower shields. "Silence-wire! Get the wire on her throat before—"

Ines spoke *break* at the shields, and they split like green wood.

Then she made her mistake, or rather she made the mistake she had been making for seven years: she hesitated, because the men in front of her were not monsters, only men, and the Order had taught her that a speaker's blade fell last, after every other door had closed.

Vess did not hesitate. Something struck Ines across the temple—a baton, a pommel, she never learned which—and the world went white and sideways. She hit the floor among the scattered ledgers. Through the ringing in her skull she heard Vess's boots approach, unhurried.

"Wire," Vess said. "Now."

Cold silver touched her throat. Silence-wire, the hunters' tool, woven with prayers to a god who had never answered Ines either. Once it cinched, she would never speak another true word—not today, not ever, unless someone cut it off her corpse.

Her fingers found the floor. Found the torn page lying there. Wren Calloway's contract, ink still wet with the mountain's patience.

*Sold under false law,* the servant had whispered to her in the vault, when he showed her the lanterns of hanging souls. *Every contract in this court is void if spoken against by a true-named oath. But no one speaks here. No one dares.*

No one dared. Ines Pemberton had been no one for seven years, and it had kept her alive, and it had kept her small, and it had gotten everyone she loved hurt anyway—her order, her city, and now a boy bleeding on the stones because he tried to buy his sister back with himself.

The wire tightened.

Ines spoke *sever*.

The silence-wire parted with a sound like a snapped harp string, and the backlash threw both hunters backward. The pain of the word was beyond anything—she felt something tear, actual tissue, actual blood, felt her voice drop to a ruined rasp even as she used it—but the wire's two halves lay smoking on the floor, and Vess was staring at her with something that had finally replaced the smile.

Fear.

"There she is," Ines rasped, and got one knee under herself, and stood. Blood ran warm from her throat down her collarbone. Her head throbbed where the baton had landed. She had perhaps a dozen words left in her before her voice gave out entirely, and she knew it the way a gambler knows his last coins.

"Kill her," Vess said, and her voice shook only a little. "The bounty stands for the body. Kill—"

*Stop.*

Not a shout. A whisper, almost. But it was swordspeech, aimed true, and it caught Vess mid-word like a hand around her jaw. The captain froze, eyes wide, muscles locked. Ines walked to her slowly, each step a negotiation with her own swaying balance, and took the writ of arrest from Vess's belt.

"I could hold you here until the mountain takes you," Ines rasped. "I've thought about it. You burned my shop. You burned Merla the charm-seller, who sold love-charms to lonely people and never hurt a soul." She leaned close. "But my order taught me the blade falls last. So run, Captain. Run and tell your king the last swordspeaker is coming to his throne, and she is done being quiet."

She released the word. Vess staggered back, gasping, and—for one heartbeat—Ines believed she would flee.

Instead Vess looked past her, at the far end of the hall, and smiled again, blood at the corner of her mouth, and said:

"Your Majesty hears everything in his mountain."

The temperature of the hall dropped. The torchlight bent, all of it, bending toward the great doors at the end of the gallery as though the flames themselves were turning to look. And through those doors came a cold that Ines felt in her teeth, in the scars of her throat, in the oath-scar over her heart.

Tam, still pinned under a hunter's knee, lifted his bloody face and looked at her with an expression she'd seen once before, on the road, when he'd asked her if souls could really be bought back.

It was the expression of a man watching the bill arrive.

"Ines," he whispered. "What did you just call?"

The doors began to open.

* * *

They came through the smoke of the broken chandelier like a tide coming through a seawall—hunters first, then the Pale King's own guard, tall figures in lacquered bone-white armor whose faces Ines could not make herself study twice. And behind them, filling the doorway without quite touching its frame, came the presence she had felt since the vault: the weight of the mountain's master, arriving not as a body but as a pressure, a hush, a cold attention settling over the Hall of Ledgers like snowfall.

The clerks bowed so low their foreheads touched their desks. The hunters knelt. Vess sank to one knee with her head bared, and Ines saw, with a strange distant clarity, that the captain was trembling—not with fear of death, but with the fear of a woman standing before the thing she had sold her conscience to and hoping the receipt still held.

Ines did not kneel. Mostly because her knees had informed her they were on strike, but partly—she would insist later, hoarsely, to anyone who'd listen—on principle.

"So," said the mountain.

It was not a voice. It was the absence of one, a silence shaped like speech, arriving directly behind the eyes. Ines's ruined throat ached in sympathy.

"The last speaker walks into my hall and breaks my wire and burns my chandelier." The cold attention drifted, unhurried, and settled on Tam. "And brings me a boy who offered himself freely. Twice now. Freely given is the rarest coin, little merchant. I was beginning to enjoy the negotiation."

"Touch him and I'll spend every word I have left," Ines rasped. It came out as barely more than breath, blood-flecked, and it cost her a bright flash of pain to say even that much. "And then I'll walk to your throne on my hands if I have to."

"Will you?" The silence seemed amused. "On what will you walk, speaker? Your voice is a guttering candle. I can hear it from here—flickering, tearing, half-spent. You have perhaps ten words left. Perhaps fewer. And I have had seven hundred years to learn what a swordspeaker's words can and cannot do." The pressure shifted, gathering, and the torches dimmed. "Here is what they cannot do. They cannot unmake what is freely contracted. They cannot reach into my vault and pluck out a soul that was *sold*—"

"Sold under false law," Ines said.

The hall went very quiet.

Even the mountain seemed to pause, the way a chess player pauses when an opponent touches an unexpected piece.

"Say that again," said the Pale King.

Ines's legs were shaking. Her vision kept graying at the edges. Somewhere behind her, Tam was shouting her name, muffled, desperate, and a hunter was dragging him upright by the collar. None of it mattered. There was only this: the vault, the hanging lanterns, the servant's whisper, the old law older than the mountain itself.

"A soul sold by fraud," she rasped, each word a coal dragged up her throat, "is not owned. It is *held hostage*. And hostage-law is older than you, Pale King. Older than your court. Older than your contracts. Ask your own clerks. They wrote it down. They write *everything* down."

One of the clerks, at the far end of the hall, very slowly raised his head from his desk. He looked at the Pale King. He looked at the shelf behind him, where seven hundred years of contracts slept in their rows. And Ines watched a lifetime of careful neutrality flicker across his face as he realized, with visible horror, that the mad bleeding woman in the middle of the hall might be right.

"She lies," said Vess, still kneeling. "Majesty, she lies to buy time—"

"Bring the Calloway contract," said the mountain.

The clerk fetched it with hands that were not quite steady. The great doors of the hall boomed shut. And Ines stood in the closing dark, blood drying on her collarbone, ten words left in a voice that was already more scar than sound, and understood that she had just done the one thing she had sworn for seven years never to do.

She had made herself interesting.

The mountain's attention settled on her like a hand on the back of her neck, patient and vast and terribly curious, and far away in its vault a dim, flickering lantern that had once been a girl named Wren stirred on its hook and whispered, into the dark, the first syllable of a name.

* * *

They put Tam in a cell of black glass and Ines in a cell of salt, because the mountain knew old ways too, and salt remembers oaths. They were, at least, adjacent cells, separated by a wall that conducted whispers the way water conducts cold.

"They're going to check the contract," Tam whispered through the wall. His voice was thick; she could hear the swelling where the hunter had hit him. "Ines. If you're wrong—if the fraud clause doesn't hold—"

"Then I've bought us a day," she rasped, "and we spend the day escaping."

"And if you're right?"

Ines leaned her forehead against the salt-crusted wall. Her throat felt like she'd swallowed a kettle of coals. Every word cost her now; even whispering left a taste of iron.

"Then the mountain has a problem," she said, "and mountains solve problems by burying them. Which means tomorrow, one way or the other, we're either free or we're a footnote in a ledger."

A long silence from the other cell. Then, smaller: "Ines. When they took me past the throne room. I saw the vault doors. They were humming. All the lanterns were humming, like—they were like bees before a storm. Was that you?"

"That was one word," Ines said, "that I'm going to regret."

"It sounded," Tam said, and she heard him smiling through a split lip, "like my sister laughing."

Ines closed her eyes. In the dark behind them, the salt walls drank the sound of her breathing, and somewhere above, in a hall full of ledgers, a clerk was reading a seven-year-old contract line by line by line, hunting for the word *fraud*.

And in the vault below, a dim lantern flickered faster, and faster, like a heart remembering how.
