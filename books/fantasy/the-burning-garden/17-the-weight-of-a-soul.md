## 17. The Weight of a Soul

The cell they gave Tam was, by the standards of prisons he had known — which were now, admittedly, limited to this one — almost courteous.

It had a cot. The cot had a blanket, thin and gray as old dishwater, but a blanket nonetheless. There was a stool. There was even a small window cut into the black stone of the mountain, though what it looked out on was not sky but the slow glow of the deep veins, that amber light that seeped up through the rock like honey held too close to a fire. It never changed. He had been staring at it long enough to know it never would.

"Prisoners of standing get windows," the servant had said when she'd locked him in. She was the one who had guided Ines through the halls — Sefa, she was called, with a face like folded paper and eyes that had seen too many centuries to bother being impressed by anything. "The Pale King finds despair inefficient. Despairing prisoners make poor bargains."

"And well-rested ones make good ones?"

"They make *honest* ones," she said, and shut the door.

Tam sat on the cot and took inventory of himself, because it was either that or think about Ines, and he wasn't ready to think about Ines yet.

Inventory: one brother, twenty-six years old, captured. One coat, taken from him at the door and replaced with a robe of undyed wool that smelled faintly of cold stone. One purse, confiscated, containing four coppers and a button he'd been carrying since Ashfall for reasons he could no longer remember. And one memory, spent at the Toll-Keeper's Bridge like a drunk spends his last coin — his mother's face, gone, traded away so that he and Ines could cross into the mountain's shadow.

He kept reaching for it. That was the cruelest part. Not the absence itself, but the reflex of reaching — the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore. He knew his mother had a face. He knew he had loved her, once, before the debts and the selling and the long gray years after. He knew she used to hum while she worked, because Wren had told him so, weeping, and because some part of him ached at the words like a bruise pressed by a thumb. But the face itself — the particular set of her mouth, the color of her eyes, whether her hair went gray early or late —

Gone. Paid. Spent on a bridge by a boy who had told himself he was paying out of spite.

He laughed, alone in the amber-lit dark, and it came out wrong.

"Spite," he said to the window. "Right."

Because here was the truth he had been carrying since the bridge, heavy as a stone in his chest: he hadn't paid that memory to cross. He'd had other things to offer. The toll-keeper — a thing like a heron made of shadow and patience — had listed them. A year of his life. His skill at bargaining, which was, he'd argued, considerable. Even his name, temporarily. All refused. The creature had looked at him with those patient shadow-eyes and said, *No. The bridge wants weight, not dross,* and Tam had understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly which memory weighed the most.

And he had given it up anyway. For Wren. For the sister who was fading like a lamp running dry, whose soul hung somewhere in this mountain in a vault full of sold voices.

For love, then. Which was worse than spite, because you couldn't be angry at yourself for love. You could only sit on a cot under a mountain and feel it, enormous and unpayable, and understand that your mother — who had sold her daughter's soul to keep the rest of them fed and free — had probably felt exactly this, exactly here, or somewhere very like it, when she signed whatever terrible paper she'd signed.

*Oh, Mam,* he thought, and reached again for her face, and found nothing, and lay down on the cot, and did not sleep.

* * *

They came for him on the second day. Or what he assumed was the second day; time down here moved like syrup, marked only by meals that arrived through a slot in the door — bread that tasted of nothing, broth that tasted of mushrooms and regret.

Sefa opened the door without knocking, because of course she didn't knock, and stood there with her paper-folded face arranged into something that might have been sympathy in a younger court.

"You should eat," she said.

"I've eaten."

"You've moved food around the bowl. It's not the same thing." She stepped inside — servants apparently didn't wait for invitations in the court beneath the mountain — and perched on the stool with the ease of someone who had perched on ten thousand stools across ten thousand years. "I've come to tell you something you won't want to hear."

Tam sat up. Every instinct he owned — and he owned a great many instincts, most of them sharpened in back-alley negotiations over his family's debts — went taut. "About my sister?"

"No. About the swordspeaker."

"Ines," he said, and hated how fast the name came out.

"The witch-hunter," Sefa said. "Vess. She has been in audience with the Pale King twice today. She comes with a flag of parley and a smile like a knife laid flat on a table, and she is offering him a trade." The servant's eyes settled on him, ancient and unhurried. "She wants your friend bound. Permanently. Her soul, her voice, her oath — all of it, sealed into the King's ledger, where no fire will ever find it and no word will ever free it. In exchange, the King releases the Calloway girl's soul to her."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the deep veins, counting seconds nobody wanted counted.

"She can't do that," Tam said. "Souls aren't hers to trade."

"Nothing is anyone's to trade, down here. That has never stopped anyone." Sefa folded her hands. "The King is considering it. He considers everything; it is his nature. But I will tell you what I have observed in my long and tedious service: the King likes trades that cost the trader nothing. Your witch-hunter offers him a prize she caught in a net. The King finds nets vulgar."

"So he'll refuse."

"He'll consider it. And while he considers it, the swordspeaker sits in her guest-chamber believing she has come to an honest bargain, and the Calloway girl fades another shade paler, and you—" Sesa tilted her head, birdlike, "—sit here moving bread around a bowl."

"What am I supposed to do? I'm in a cell."

"Yes," said Sefa. "What are you supposed to do?"

She left. Tam stared at the door for a long time.

Then he got up, walked to it, and pounded on it with the flat of his hand until the corridor rang.

* * *

The Pale King received him in a smaller chamber than Tam expected, which he understood immediately was a trick. Everything about the mountain court was a trick of scale — vast halls that made you feel like an insect, and then rooms like this one, low-ceilinged and warm-lit, almost domestic, designed to make you feel like a guest. Like the whole horror of the place was a misunderstanding you could talk your way out of.

The King sat behind a table set with tea. He looked like a man the way a statue looks like a man: correct in every proportion, and utterly without anything moving underneath. His eyes were the amber of the deep veins.

"Tam Calloway," the King said. His voice was pleasant. That was somehow the worst thing about him. "You wish to bargain."

"I'm told that's the local custom."

"It is. Most who come to me bargain badly. They offer what they do not value and ask for what they cannot afford." The King poured two cups of tea and pushed one across the table. Steam rose from it, smelling of honey and something older. "Which are you?"

"The first kind, probably. But I'm learning." Tam didn't touch the cup. He'd watched enough deals go bad in Ashfall taverns to know you never drank first. "I want an hour."

"An hour."

"One hour of freedom for Ines Pemberton. Unbound, unmolested, her own woman, with access to wherever she needs to go in this mountain. Including the vault. Including the throne room." He swallowed. "Including you."

The King regarded him with those still amber eyes, and Tam had the distinct sensation of being appraised by something that had weighed kings and found them wanting.

"And why," the King said, "would I grant such a thing?"

"Because Vess's offer is poison and you know it. She doesn't want Wren freed. She wants Ines delivered — bound, silent, displayed. The moment you hand over Wren's soul, Vess has no more reason to honor any term, and you'll have spent a soul from your own vault on a promise from a woman whose whole profession is breaking promises." He leaned forward. "You said it yourself. You like trades that cost the trader something. What does Vess lose if you take her deal? Nothing. She loses nothing. That's how you know it's worthless."

Something shifted in the King's face. It might have been amusement. On that still face, it was hard to say — like watching a lake decide to ripple.

"You argue well," the King said. "Your mother taught you?"

The question landed like a hook behind his ribs. *My mother sold my sister,* he almost said. *My mother's face is sitting in some toll-keeper's collection like a moth in a case.* Instead he said, "She taught me that everything has a price. I'm just finally learning to pay mine honestly."

"And what," said the Pale King, softly, "are you offering?"

There it was. The whole conversation had been walking toward this door, and now it stood open, and beyond it was the thing he had been carrying since the bridge.

"The memory," Tam said. "The one I paid at the bridge. My mother's face."

The King went very still. Stillness, Tam realized, was this creature's version of leaning forward.

"That memory is spent," the King said. "Paid to the toll-keeper. It does not belong to you."

"Then buy it back. From the toll-keeper. Whatever it costs you, whatever the toll — you're the court beneath the mountain. You have arrangements." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I'm not offering you something I have. I'm offering you something I already gave away. I want to give it *again*. To you, this time, permanently, for one hour of her freedom."

"Why?" asked the King, and the question had teeth. "Why spend twice what others spend once? What is this swordspeaker to you?"

And Tam, who had lied to debt collectors and tax men and his own sister about the state of their accounts, found that he couldn't lie at all.

"Nothing," he said. "She's nothing to me. Eight days ago I tried to rob her. I followed her halfway across the country planning to steal that map out of her pack while she slept." He laughed, wetly. "And then she shared her supper with me. And then she listened to me rant about my mother like she actually cared. And then when the hunters came she grabbed my wrist and pulled me into the dark and said — she said, *nobody gets left behind, that's the whole point of the order I failed.*" He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, furious at himself. "She thinks she failed her order. She's the only person I've ever met who keeps her promises even when keeping them is killing her. And Vess is going to bind her forever, and everyone in this rotten beautiful mountain is going to let it happen because it's *efficient*, and I—" 

He stopped. Breathed.

"I don't have a soul to sell. I checked. Believe me, I checked — I offered myself to your court three days ago through Sefa and you wouldn't take it, because I'm not worth enough, because I'm nobody, because the only valuable thing I ever owned was my mother's face and I already spent it on a bridge trying to fix what she broke." He spread his hands. "So I'm spending it again. Twice. Because that's what it weighs. That's what she's worth. One hour. Take it or leave it."

Silence. The tea steamed between them, untouched, going bitter.

Then the Pale King did something Tam had never seen him do, in all his brief and terrible acquaintance with the court: he smiled. It was a small smile, and strange, and it did not reach eyes that had probably forgotten how.

"A memory given freely is worth more than a memory taken," the King said. "This is the oldest law I know, older than my court, older than the mountain. The toll-keeper took yours. You are choosing to give it. Those are different weights entirely." He rose, and the room seemed to rise with him. "One hour. When the bell of the deep vein rings its next turn, the hour begins. During it, Ines Pemberton is unbound, unmolested, and answerable to no contract in my ledger. At its end..." The amber eyes fixed on him. "At its end, she stands before my throne, and whatever happens there happens. I will not shield her. I will not warn her. Do you understand the terms?"

"Do you understand mine?" Tam said. "If Vess touches her during that hour — if any of your people lift a finger — the deal's ash, and I will spend whatever's left of me making this court regret it. I don't know how yet. I'll think of something. I'm told I bargain well."

"There it is," said the Pale King, and the smile flickered again, brief as a fish rising. "Your mother's stubbornness. I have seen it before, in a ledger, years ago. It is a heavy inheritance, Tam Calloway. Heavier than a face."

Tam's throat closed. "You knew her. You knew what she — you saw the sale."

"I see every sale." For one moment — one strange, flickering moment — the still face looked almost tired. "Go to your cell. Rest. When the bell rings, Sefa will loose the swordspeaker, and the hour will be yours to spend as she chooses."

He turned to go, and paused at the door, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in his chest since the bridge, and if he didn't say it now it would rot there.

"When you buy the memory back from the toll-keeper," Tam said, without turning around, "don't destroy it. Keep it. Put it in your vault with the lanterns, or whatever you do. Because someday — someday when this is over, if there's anything left of any of us — I'm going to come back and earn it. Properly. And I'd like to know it's waiting."

Behind him, silence. Then, quietly, in that pleasant voice:

"The vault of sold voices holds many lanterns, Tam Calloway. Some of them are waiting for exactly that." A pause. "Your mother's face, I am told, hums while it works."

Tam walked back to his cell with his eyes burning and his heart hammering and the strangest feeling in his chest — grief and hope tangled together so tightly he couldn't tell which end to pull.

* * *

The hours passed. He ate the bread. He even tasted it, this time.

He thought about Ines — about the scarred knuckles and the careful smile and the way she went still and listening when she thought no one was watching, like a woman hearing a song only she remembered. About how she'd sworn seven years of silence to honor dead friends, and how he was about to bet everything he had left that she'd know what to do with one hour of freedom.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. He couldn't save Wren. He couldn't fight Vess. He couldn't speak whatever terrible true words Ines carried locked behind her teeth. All he could do — all anybody could ever do, maybe — was buy a little time and trust someone braver to spend it well.

One hour. He'd bought her one hour with the last thing he loved.

Somewhere above him — or below, or sideways, directions meant nothing here — a bell began to ring in the deep vein, a sound like a struck tooth, rolling through the mountain's bones.

Tam stood up.

Far down the corridor, he heard a door unlock, and light footsteps, quickening, and Sefa's dry voice saying something he couldn't catch — and then, unmistakable, growing closer, Ines's voice, sharp with alarm and fury and something that sounded almost like hope:

"*Tam? Tam, what did you DO?*"
