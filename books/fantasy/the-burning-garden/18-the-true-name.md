## 18. The True Name

The throne room of the Pale King was not what Ines had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected instead. Something grander, perhaps. Something with more gold in it. But the court beneath the mountain had been built by creatures who measured wealth in other currencies, and so the great hall was hewn from raw stone that drank the light rather than reflecting it, and the only ornament was the vault itself — visible through a wall of clear crystal at the far end of the chamber, where hundreds of small lights hung in the dark like lanterns in a flooded street.

Souls. Each one a sold soul, hanging in its glass case, dim and patient and waiting.

Ines stood before the throne with her hands empty and her throat tight, and she thought: *I have been silent for seven years, and it has all come down to this room.*

The Pale King sat on a chair of black basalt that might once have been a tree. He was not tall. He was not terrible. He looked, in fact, like a tired clerk who had been doing accounts for a very long time — long fingers, pale skin the color of cave mushrooms, eyes like chips of quartz that caught no light at all. When he spoke, his voice was dry paper.

"The oath-bound speaker," he said. "You are smaller than your reputation."

"I don't have a reputation," Ines said. "I sell charms for small coin. Lucky knots. Love tokens. Nothing that works."

"Everything you have ever sold worked," said the Pale King. "That is the trouble with oaths. They leak."

At his right hand stood Captain Vess.

She had changed out of her witch-hunter's blacks into something finer — a coat of grey silk with silver buttons, the uniform of a guest of the court rather than its enemy. Her hands were folded neatly. Her face wore the expression of a woman attending a wedding she had personally arranged. There was a bruise on her jaw where Tam had hit her in the aqueducts, and she wore it like a decoration.

"Ines Pemberton," Vess said, and the name came off her tongue like something she'd been keeping warm. "Last of the Order of the Speaking Sword. I've carried that name for three years, did you know? Since the archives burned. I read every surviving page of your order's registry. I know your masters' names. I know the words they used to die." She smiled thinly. "I've been waiting a long time to hear yours."

"You'll wait longer," Ines said.

"Will I?" Vess turned to the throne. "Your Majesty. The terms were stated plainly. A soul may be redeemed by one of equal weight freely given — or by the true name of an oath-bound speaker, spoken aloud, which unbinds every contract made under false law. Every contract." She let the words sit. "Including, I imagine, some of Your Majesty's oldest holdings."

The Pale King's quartz eyes moved to Ines. "It is true," he said. "The old contracts were made when the law above was young and cruel, and cruelty makes poor ink. If she speaks her name, my vault empties. Every soul. Gone home to bodies living and dead alike." He paused. "And the mountain comes down around us, because the vault is not merely storage. It is the keystone. Sold souls hold weight. Freed ones do not."

"So speak, then," Vess said to Ines, softly, reasonably, the way one speaks to a frightened animal. "Speak and bring the whole rotten thing down. Or don't, and watch me buy the girl's soul with mine — oh, forgive me. With *yours*." She unfolded her hands. "That was the trade I offered. My service, my records, my testimony against every charm-seller in Ashfall, in exchange for Wren Calloway's soul bound into a vessel of my choosing. The King has accepted. All that remains is for you to stand there and be quiet, Pemberton. Which is, after all, the one thing you're good at."

The hall was silent except for the faint, endless dripping of water somewhere deep in the stone.

Ines looked at the crystal wall. Among the lantern-lights she could just make out one that flickered wrong — too fast, too thin, a candle in a draft. Wren. Somewhere above them, in a guest cell of the court, the girl herself lay fading, hollow as a dropped glove, while her brother sat chained in another cell because he had tried to trade his own soul for hers and been refused — refused, and captured for his trouble, because Captain Vess had been waiting behind the bargain like a spider behind a web.

One hour. Tam had bought her one hour with the last thing he owned — the memory of his mother's face, paid twice now, first to the toll-keeper and then to the King. One hour of freedom, and she had spent forty minutes of it finding the vault, and now here she stood, spending the rest standing in front of a throne.

She thought about her oath.

She had sworn it in blood and ash, kneeling among the dead of her order, with Master Aldric's sword across her knees and the smoke still rising off the orchard where the crown's soldiers had burned everything green. *I will not speak. I will not teach. I will carry the words down into silence so that no king may ever use them.* Seven years she had kept it. Seven years of biting back the syllables when hunters dragged children through the streets. Seven years of watching burnings from shop windows with her hand pressed flat over her mouth like a widow at a funeral.

Her oath had been made to protect the words from being *used*. It had never occurred to her, until this moment, that silence could be a kind of use too. That staying quiet was itself an act, with consequences, with victims. That her silence had a body count she would never be permitted to count.

"You're thinking," said the Pale King. "Good. Most of them scream or beg. Thinking is rarer."

"What happens to the souls if the mountain falls?" Ines asked him. "The freed ones."

"They go where souls go," the King said. "Home, if home remains. Elsewhere, if it doesn't." He tilted his head. "You are asking because you want to know if the girl survives. She does. Probably. Souls are sturdier than bodies. It is bodies that worry me, and the ceiling, and the several hundred tons of mountain between us and the sky."

"And him?" She nodded toward the cells beyond the hall, where Tam was held. "If I do nothing. If I let Vess make her trade."

"Then the boy rots here, the girl fades in a week, and you go home to Ashfall and keep selling lucky knots until the captain finds a reason to burn you anyway. She will. They always do." The Pale King said it without malice, the way a clerk reads a ledger. "I am not your enemy, swordspeaker. I am a businessman. You are the one holding the knife."

*The knife.* Her voice. The words her order had died rather than surrender — not spells, exactly, but true speech, the language that named things so completely the things had no choice but to listen. One word could open a lock. Three could stop a heart. And a full name, spoken whole and true by the mouth it belonged to —

A full name could unmake a lie. And every contract in this vault was a lie wearing legal dress.

Vess stepped closer. Up close, Ines could see that the captain's certainty had hairline cracks in it, the way porcelain does before it fails. "Don't," Vess said quietly. "Whatever speech you're composing in there — don't. I burned six years of my life getting here, Pemberton. I gave up a sister to the fever, a marriage to the job, a face—" she touched the bruise on her jaw, almost absently, "—a great deal. And for what? For a city that sells charms out of back windows and calls it freedom. For people who hide. You *hide*, Pemberton. That's the difference between us. I stand in the light and say what I am."

"Do you?" Ines said. "What are you?"

"A witch-hunter."

"That's a job. What are you?"

For half a heartbeat, something moved behind Vess's eyes — something young and unspeakably tired — and then it was gone, shuttered, and the captain's face went smooth as river stone. "Say your name or don't," she said. "But know that if you don't, I walk out of here with a soul in a bottle and you walk out with nothing, and in a year I find you again, because I always find you. Silence isn't safety, swordspeaker. It's just a slower fire."

Somewhere in the walls, water dripped. Ines closed her eyes.

She found herself, absurdly, in her shop. Not the real one — the one in her head, the one she visited on sleepless nights — with its shelves of harmless trinkets and its crooked door and the smell of beeswax and dust. She saw the morning light through the window. She heard the market calling outside. She felt, with sudden unbearable clarity, how much she had loved that small life. How hard she had built it. Brick by careful brick, lie by careful lie, a fortress with no windows.

And she thought of Wren Calloway, nineteen years old, who had done nothing except be loved badly by a desperate mother, lying hollow in a cell upstairs, fading like breath off glass. Who had never sworn any oath at all, and whose whole self was hanging in a glass case fifty feet away because the adults in her life kept choosing survival over truth.

*Silence is just a slower fire.*

Master Aldric had told her once, in the orchard, pruning apples: *"An oath is a wall, little one. But you must always ask — what is the wall for? Walls that keep nothing out and everything in are just tombs with better masonry."*

She had thought he meant the order's secrets. She had been wrong. He had meant her.

Ines opened her eyes.

The Pale King watched her from his black throne with his quartz eyes, and for the first time she saw something almost like hope in them — the hope of a creature who had grown rich on other people's silence and was tired of the interest payments.

"Your hour is nearly spent," he said. "Choose."

Ines rolled her shoulders. Flexed her hands. And did the thing she had not done in seven years: she reached down inside herself, past the seal she had laid over her own voice, past the scar tissue of silence, and touched the words.

They were still there. Of course they were still there. Words like hers didn't fade. They waited. They had been waiting seven years, coiled under her tongue like a drawn blade, and now she let them rise, and her throat burned as though she had swallowed a coal, and her eyes filled with tears that had nothing to do with grief.

Vess took a step back. Her hand went to a belt that no longer held her weapons. "Pemberton—"

"My name," Ines said, and her voice came out rough and low and shaking, and it was *hers*, wholly hers, the first true thing she had said aloud since the orchard burned, "is Ines Pemberton. Daughter of Marisol the mender. Last of the Order of the Speaking Sword. Sworn in blood beneath a burning apple tree, and I tell you now —"

The lanterns in the vault began to tremble.

"— the law that sold these souls was false when it was written, false when it was sealed, and false tonight. I speak my name, and I unbind every voice held under false law. Let them go *home*."

The word *home* left her mouth like a struck bell.

Every light in the crystal wall flared at once.

* * *

What happened next, Ines would spend the rest of her life trying to describe, and never quite manage.

The sound was not loud. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it — it was not loud at all. It was a single pure tone, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and it passed through the stone of the mountain and through the flesh of everyone standing in the hall, and where it passed, lies came apart.

The contracts did not burn. They *unraveled*. Ines could see them going — threads of pale script lifting off the air itself, the fine print of seven hundred years of bargains dissolving like sugar in rain. The crystal wall of the vault crazed, spiderwebbed, and burst outward in a bloom of glittering shards, and the lights rose.

All of them. Hundreds of them, thousands, souls pouring up through the shattered vault like a reversal of snowfall, streaming toward the distant ceiling and the world above, and the sound they made — Ines pressed her hands over her ears and it got in anyway — the sound they made was laughter, or weeping, or both, in a language older than either.

"No!" Vess lunged for the falling shards, snatching at the light as if she could gather souls into her fists. "No — the trade — the trade was *struck* —"

"The trade was made under false law," said the Pale King. He had risen from his throne, and he was looking up at the escaping lights with an expression Ines could not read — envy, maybe, or relief. "All trades were. Didn't you listen, captain? She said so. And she had the authority to say it. That is what a true name *is*."

The mountain groaned. Dust sifted from the ceiling in grey ropes. Somewhere far above, tons of stone shifted, settling, deciding.

Vess turned on Ines with her face finally, fully broken open, and what was underneath was not hatred. It was hunger. Terrible, bottomless hunger, the kind that eats its owner first. "Give it to me," she whispered. "The words. Give me the words and I'll — I'll give you anything —"

"You couldn't carry them," Ines said, and her scarred voice was gentle. "They'd burn you hollow. Look what wanting has already done."

Vess screamed — a wordless sound, furious and grieving — and threw herself forward, and the collapsing magic of the dying court met her halfway. Pale light poured up out of the cracking floor like water finding a drain, and it wrapped around the captain, and Ines saw her silhouette go bright, then brighter, then translucent, and the last expression on Captain Vess's face was not fear.

It was recognition. As if, at the very end, she had finally seen clearly the thing she had been chasing all along.

Then she was gone, and the light was gone, and the mountain groaned again, deeper, and the Pale King said, "You should run now, swordspeaker. The keystone is out. I would offer you hospitality, but I fear my house is coming down."

"Tam," Ines said. "The cells —"

"East corridor. Go left at the falling water. And swordspeaker—" the tired clerk-king inclined his head, and there was, unmistakably, respect in it, "—it has been a long time since anyone told the truth in this hall. It sounded better than I remembered."

Ines ran.

The corridors were filling with dust and the deep grinding roar of stone settling into new shapes. She went left at the falling water — a cascade bursting through a cracked wall, cold as snowmelt — and found the cells, and found Tam on his feet at the bars with his hands wrapped around iron that had gone soft as wax the moment her name rang through the stone.

"Took you long enough," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it.

"Shut up and run."

They ran together, up through the shuddering dark, past doors standing open, past court servants standing calm and unhurried in alcoves watching their ancient workplace come down around them with the serenity of people who had seen empires end before. The falling water grew louder. Ahead, impossibly, there was daylight — a ragged wound in the mountainside where the rock had split, spilling evening gold into the tunnels.

Tam grabbed her arm. "Wren — is she — did it work, is she —"

Above them, through the splitting stone, Ines heard it: a sound like every bell in the world ringing at once, and underneath it, rising, a girl's voice — hoarse, astonished, alive — crying out a name.

Tam's name.

The mountain shrugged its shoulders. The daylight widened. And Ines Pemberton, half-deafened, throat scorched, lungs full of stone dust, pulled Tam Calloway through the crack in the world and out into the evening air — where, on a slope of broken scree below the smoking peak, a girl was sitting up in the grass, whole, blinking, touching her own face with both hands as if checking that it belonged to her.

"Wren!" Tam shouted, and stumbled down the slope toward her.

Ines stood at the mouth of the mountain, swaying, and watched them collide — brother and sister, laughing and sobbing and holding on — and she felt the silence inside her chest, seven years wide, finally, finally empty.

Not silent anymore. Just quiet. The way a garden is quiet.

Behind her, the mountain settled with a long sigh, letting go of what it had been holding. Above her, the first stars were coming out. And somewhere down the slope, Wren Calloway lifted her head, found Ines standing in the rubble-light, and smiled — a real smile, a whole person's smile — and called out, in a voice rough from seven years of disuse:

"Hey. You're the one who talked me home."

Ines opened her mouth to answer — and discovered, with a tired, wondering laugh, that for the first time in her life, she had absolutely no idea what to say.
