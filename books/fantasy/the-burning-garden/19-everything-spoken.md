## 19. Everything Spoken

The throne room of the Pale King was not a room at all. It was a wound in the mountain, vast and ribbed with stone, lit by the souls that hung from its ceiling like lanterns in a festival no one had survived to enjoy.

Ines stood before the dais with her throat burning and her hands empty, because Tam had bought her one hour with the last thing he owned — the memory of his mother's face — and she would not waste a second of it being careful.

The Pale King sat above her on a chair carved from a single vein of white quartz, and he was beautiful the way frost is beautiful: precise, ornamental, and utterly without warmth. His court stood in ranks around him, courtiers with too many joints and smiles filed to points, and among them, bound in iron that smoked faintly, knelt Tam.

"An hour," the Pale King said. His voice was the sound of coins settling in a strongbox. "The boy purchased you an hour of freedom, freely given, at great cost. I confess I admire the transaction. It is the first honest trade this court has seen in three hundred years." He tilted his head, and the light of a thousand sold souls slid across his face. "What will you do with it, little oath-breaker? Run? The mountain is deep."

"I'm not going to run," Ines said.

Her voice came out rough. Speaking at all hurt now; the swordspeech she'd used against Vess's hunters in the vault corridor had scorched something in her throat that ordinary words had to scrape past. But she could still speak. That was the miracle of it. Seven years of silence, seven years of swallowing every true thing she knew, and her voice was still hers.

"Then what," said the Pale King, "will you do?"

*Tell him,* whispered the soul called Wren, hanging dim and flickering near the ceiling, close enough now that Ines could see the girl inside the light — small, stubborn, half-gone but not gone. *You already know what to do. You've known since the market street.*

And Ines had. That was the terrible joke of it. She had spent seven years believing her silence was a wall she maintained, when all along it had been a door she was afraid to open.

She looked at Tam. He knelt in the iron with his chin up and his eyes wet and furious, and even now — even now — he managed to mouth at her, *don't do anything stupid*, which was the most brotherly thing anyone had ever said to her.

She looked at Captain Vess, standing at the edge of the dais in her white-and-ash uniform, waiting for the Pale King to hand her a soul that did not belong to him. Vess caught her looking and smiled the smile of a woman who believed, sincerely and completely, that she was the hero of this story.

"Last chance, Pemberton," Vess said quietly. "Kneel. Give them your name willingly and maybe they let the boy keep his memories. Maybe they let the Calloway girl fade gently instead of—"

"Captain," said the Pale King, with the mild patience of a man shooing a dog off fine upholstery, "you are interrupting."

Vess closed her mouth. Her jaw worked.

Ines breathed in.

The air of the court tasted of cold iron and old bargains, and beneath it — faintly, impossibly — of rain. Of Ashfall in spring, of the market street where she'd sold charms for small coin, of seven years of a small safe life that had never once been either small or safe, only quiet. She thought about how much she had loved that quiet. She thought about how it had been killing her by inches, the way a slow leak kills a boat, so gently the drowning feels like rest.

She thought about her order. Forty-one voices, silenced in a single night for refusing to speak their names into the crown's service. She had been the forty-second, the one who ran, who buried her magic under an oath of silence and called the burial peace.

*An oath is a promise made aloud,* her teacher Maren had told her, the night before the massacre, sharpening a sword that would never be swung again. *Remember that, little one. Everything spoken can be unspoken. Nothing silent can ever be kept.*

Ines opened her mouth.

The whole court leaned in. Even the hanging souls turned, hundreds of small lights swiveling toward her like flowers finding a sun they'd forgotten existed.

"My name," Ines said, and her ruined voice cracked across the first word like ice on a pond, "is Ines Pemberton."

The words fell into the silence and nothing happened, for exactly one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat she felt the old terror rise up and offer itself to her one final time — *take it back, swallow it, hide, hide, hide* —

"Daughter of the Order of the Speaking Sword," she said, louder, and the terror broke apart like smoke in wind. "Oath-bound. Last of my line. And I say to this court, to its king, and to every contract written beneath this mountain: hear me, and know that I have spoken."

Her name left her body.

It did not sound like sound. It sounded like dawn.

The name rang through the mountain the way a bell rings through water, and Ines felt it leave her — felt it go out of her chest and down through the floor and up through the ribs of the world, and everywhere it touched, things that had been false began, very politely, to come apart.

The first thing to break was the nearest contract. It hung in the air beside the dais as a ribbon of grey script — she could see them now, see all of them, the whole architecture of the court revealed by her speaking, thousands of contracts stitched through the dark like a spiderweb made of fine print — and the ribbon simply unraveled, thread by thread, sighing as it went.

Then the next. Then ten. Then a hundred.

"No," said the Pale King.

He rose from his quartz throne, and for the first time his beauty failed him. Frost cracks when it melts, and he was melting, edges softening, precision dissolving, because a king of false contracts is only as real as the contracts are, and Ines Pemberton had just spoken truth into the middle of them with the authority of the last living oath.

"You cannot—" His voice splintered mid-word. "The names were *sold*. Freely traded. Witnessed and sealed. The law—"

"The law was written by liars," Ines said, and each word cost her fire, and she paid it gladly. "I am an oath, and oaths outrank paper. Every soul held under false law is *free*."

The mountain rang.

Not the court — the mountain itself, the whole black weight of it, and somewhere far above, Ines knew, rock was shifting, old tunnels groaning awake, the aqueducts of Ashfall running suddenly with light instead of water. The contracts tore loose in their hundreds, and the souls they had held came free like birds released from a cage whose door had rusted shut for centuries.

They poured upward.

There was no other word for it. They rose through the dark in streams and rivers, hundreds of lights, thousands, and the sound they made was not screaming and not singing but something between — the sound of people remembering themselves. Ines saw a soldier's soul spiral past, straightening as it went, shoulders squaring. A child's soul shot by so fast it left a streak of gold. An old woman's light paused beside Ines for a moment — just a moment — and Ines would have sworn, later and to her dying day, that it inclined itself toward her in something very like a bow.

"Thank you, daughter of the sword," it said, in a voice like a page turning, and then it was gone, up and out, home to whatever waits for the returned.

The court was coming apart around them. The courtiers with their too-many joints were unraveling, because they had been made of bargains too, and the bargains were void. The walls wept quartz dust. The great wound in the mountain began, slowly, to heal — stone flowing inward like water finding its level — and the Pale King stood in the middle of it all, shrinking, flickering, shouting orders to servants who no longer existed.

"The contracts are MINE," he screamed, and his voice was smaller now, tinny, a coin dropped down a well. "I PAID for them. I always pay. I am the only one who ever PAYS—"

"That," said Ines, "was always the problem."

The throne cracked down the middle with a sound like a struck bell, and the Pale King looked at it, and then at her, and the last thing in his face — before the frost took it entirely — was not rage. It was confusion. Genuinely, heartbreakingly confused, the way a miser looks at a burning ledger. He had never understood. Not in three hundred years. You cannot own what is freely given, and everything else was always going to slip through your fingers.

He came apart like snow in sunlight, and the quartz throne came apart after him, and the light of a thousand freed souls filled the chamber so completely that for a moment there was no dark left anywhere at all.

* * *

Ines found Vess at the edge of the collapsing dais, and what happened to Captain Vess of the witch-hunters was nobody's doing but her own.

She had not run. Ines had watched her, in those first ringing seconds, watch the souls pour free — and Ines had seen the exact moment the captain stopped being a hunter and became a thief. Because Vess had spent her life around magic's wreckage, cataloguing it, burning it, and some starving corner of her had always wanted to hold it, and here it was, loose in the air, unowned for the first time in three hundred years.

She lunged for it. Ines saw her snatch at a trailing ribbon of contract-script — a scrap of the old law, still writhing, still hungry — and press it against her own chest, and speak three words of binding into it, words she must have stolen from a pyre years ago, words meant to claim what burns.

The magic went into her like water into a cracked cup.

"Oh," said Vess. Just that. Just *oh*, in a small voice, the voice of a woman realizing, far too late, that she had never once understood the thing she'd spent her life hunting.

The stolen script wrapped her, and the freed souls' passing light touched it, and the binding — built on false law, on law that no longer existed — turned inside out. It did not burn her the way her pyres burned people. It was worse than that, and kinder. It showed her. Every soul she'd sent up in smoke, every charm-seller and hedge-witch and frightened child, all of them at once, and the truth of each one, and the truth of herself, all in a single unendurable instant of seeing.

Vess screamed once. Then the light took her — or she walked into it, Ines could never afterward decide which — and the white-and-ash uniform flared like paper and was gone, and the scrap of contract-script drifted down to the floor and crumbled into ordinary dust.

Ines stood in the roar of the freeing and felt nothing about it that she had expected to feel. No triumph. No satisfaction. Just an enormous, aching pity, and underneath the pity, a vow she made on the spot, silently, in the privacy of her own scarred throat: *no more pyres. Not for anyone. Not ever again.*

Some victories you win by making sure no one else ever has to lose that way.

* * *

Getting Tam out of the iron was easier than getting him into it had been, mostly because the iron was dissolving along with everything else. He staggered free rubbing his wrists, coughing in the golden haze, blinking up at the river of souls streaming overhead.

"You," he said hoarsely, "are the most terrifying person I have ever met."

"You should meet my teacher."

"Maren. Right. The one from the stories." He squinted at her. "The stories undersold it."

"They usually do." Ines grabbed his arm and hauled him toward the corridor, because the mountain was still healing itself and healing mountains are not gentle about anything in their way. "Can you run?"

"I gave up my mother's face for you. I can absolutely run."

They ran. Behind them the throne room folded closed like a book, sealing the old dark away forever, and ahead of them the tunnels of the mountain were lit — actually lit, warm and gold — by souls on their way home, drifting through the stone as if it weren't there, lighting the road for two exhausted humans stumbling along beneath them.

One of them fell into step beside Ines. Small. Flickering. But brighter than she'd been in the vault, brighter than she'd been in years, and wearing, unmistakably, the shape of a girl.

"Wren?" Tam choked.

*I'm going ahead,* the light said, and her voice was young and dry and, even now, even freed, faintly exasperated in the way only a sister's voice can be. *Don't get lost. Don't argue with the mountain. And Tam — I felt it. What you paid. When I get my body back I'm going to hug you so hard you'll regret every mean thing you've ever thought about me.*

"I've thought a lot of mean things," Tam managed.

*I know. I heard some of them. The bridge ate the memory, not me.* The light bobbed ahead, impatient, alive. *Come on. I want to be hungry again. I want to be COLD. I want to complain about both.*

And she streamed away up the tunnel, laughing — actually laughing, a sound Ines suspected hadn't come out of Wren Calloway in seven years — and Ines and Tam ran after her through the golden dark, out of the mountain, toward whatever was left of the world.

Behind them, with a long sigh like a giant finally setting down a burden, the court beneath the mountain closed its doors for good.

Ahead — Ines could feel it in her burned throat, in her aching bones, in the strange new lightness of a woman carrying no secrets for the first time in her adult life — ahead was morning.

She only hoped her shop's insurance covered acts of truth.
