## 20. What Remains of the Mountain

The first thing Wren felt was cold.

Not the hollow kind of cold she had lived with for six years — the cold of an empty room, a room with the furniture taken out — but real cold, sharp and specific, biting at her bare feet where they dangled off the edge of a slab of broken stone. Cold with edges. Cold that *meant* something.

She opened her eyes.

Dust hung in the air like a held breath. It sifted down through great cracks in the vaulted ceiling above her, catching what little light remained, drifting in slow golden spirals. Somewhere far off, stone groaned and settled, the mountain rearranging itself after whatever had torn through it. The Pale King's court — the marble halls, the lantern-rooms, the long tables where merchants had haggled over years of a child's laughter — lay in ruins around her, half-buried, its false contracts crumbled to ash on the floor.

And Wren was hungry.

She laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and rusty, like a door not opened in years, because it was. She was *hungry*. Her stomach cramped and growled, loud and rude and alive, and she pressed both hands against it and laughed harder until the laugh turned into something else, something wet and gasping, and she sat there on her broken slab in the settling dust and cried for the first time in six years with her whole self present to do it.

She could feel everything. That was the miracle of it, the terrible ordinary miracle: she could feel the grit of dust between her fingers, the ache in her stiff neck, the sting where she'd bitten her own lip, the cold air moving in and out of lungs that were hers, entirely hers, no longer shared with some dim flickering thing hanging in a vault. Her body fit her again. For years she had lived in it the way one lives in a borrowed coat — aware of every wrong seam, every place it didn't reach. Now it reached all the way down.

"Hungry," she said aloud, just to hear it. Her voice sounded different. Thicker. More hers. "I'm hungry. I'm so hungry I could eat Tam's terrible stew."

Her voice echoed off the ruined hall, and no one answered, and for one black moment she remembered: Tam had come down here. Tam had bargained loudly and badly and then he had been *taken*, and Ines —

Ines had spoken.

Wren hadn't seen it. She'd been carried to the court as part of Vess's bargain, laid out on the marble like goods awaiting pickup, and the last thing she remembered clearly was a woman with a scarred throat standing before a throne of cracked glass, drawing a breath that seemed to pull the whole mountain inward.

Then the world had come apart. Then the world had come *back together*, and she was in it.

"Tam?" she called. Her voice bounced and died. She slid off the slab, staggered, caught herself on a fallen pillar, and stood swaying in the ruins of the court beneath the mountain, wearing a thin shift and six years of absence, grinning like a madwoman because her knees hurt and her head throbbed and *she could feel her knees hurt*.

She started walking toward the light.

* * *

Getting out of the mountain took most of the day, though Wren couldn't have said how long anything took. Time moved strangely when you were newly returned to yourself; every sensation demanded to be examined. The roughness of the tunnel walls under her palms. The way her shadow behaved properly now, following her instead of lagging behind like a shy animal. The smell — actual smell! — of wet stone and old iron and, faintly, impossibly, *bread*.

Bread. Down here?

She followed the smell through a collapsed gallery, over a shattered bridge where something with too many eyes had once demanded memories as toll (the creature was gone now, its bridge broken, its toll-booth a heap of rubble — she wondered, with a pang, what Tam had paid it), and out through a fissure in the mountainside into grey evening light and cold clean rain.

She stood in the rain and let it fall on her upturned face until she was soaked and shivering and laughing again, because the rain was *cold* and she could feel it, every drop, each one landing on her skin like a small bright fact.

That was when she saw the fire.

It was a small fire, tucked under an overhang near the mountain's foot, and beside it sat two figures. One was lanky and hunched, wrapped in a blanket, gesturing wildly at a pot suspended over the flames. The other sat very still, wrapped in bandages from shoulder to wrist, her hands resting palm-up on her knees as if they belonged to someone else.

"*Tam!*"

The word tore out of her before she knew she was going to say it, raw and huge, and Tam Calloway shot to his feet so fast he knocked the pot sideways, and then he was running, slipping in the mud, arms out, and they collided hard enough to knock the breath out of her — which was wonderful, because she had breath to knock out.

"You're — " He pulled back, gripped her shoulders, stared at her face like a man checking whether a door was really a door. "You're *here*. You're all here? Your eyes are — you're looking *at* me. You haven't looked at me in years, Wren, you used to look *through* me—"

"I'm here." She was crying again, or still; it hardly mattered anymore. "I'm hungry. I'm freezing. My feet hurt. Tam, my feet *hurt*, isn't it wonderful?"

He made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and crushed her to him again. Over his shoulder she saw the other figure rise slowly, unsteadily, one hand pressed to her bandaged ear.

Ines Pemberton looked terrible. Half her hair was singed to the scalp on one side; her left ear was wrapped thickly in linen; angry burn-marks climbed her throat like ivy, the signature of words spoken too large for any mortal mouth. She walked toward them the way a sailor walks on land after a long voyage, listing slightly, testing each step.

But her eyes — her eyes were clear and whole and looking directly at Wren, and when Wren met them, Ines smiled a small, crooked, exhausted smile.

"So," Wren said, wiping her face with a muddy sleeve. "You're the one who talked my soul loose."

Ines touched her bandaged throat and her voice came out rough as gravel, half-swallowed, oddly musical in its ruin. "More like shouted it loose. There wasn't much finesse left by the end." She paused, and her smile deepened. "You heard me, then. In the vault. Before."

Wren blinked. "I don't remember the vault. I remember — warmth, maybe. A voice saying a name over and over, like it was learning to hold it."

"That was probably me," Ines said. "Your soul said mine back. Did you know that? Hanging in the dark for six years, sold and dimmed, and it still recognized a swordspeaker on sight." She shook her head slowly. "Souls are braver than we are, I think."

Tam had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet when he was feeling too much and wanted everyone to pretend otherwise. He busied himself righting the pot, stirring its contents with excessive concentration.

"It's stew," he announced to no one. "It's terrible stew. Someone has to eat it."

"I do," Wren said immediately. "I want all of it. I want bread too. Is that bread I smelled?"

"It was bread. It's charcoal now, thanks to your dramatic entrance." But he was already digging in his pack, pulling out the sad black lump with the tenderness of a man presenting a crown. "Here. Chew hard."

Wren chewed. It was awful. It was the best thing she had ever tasted. She ate the whole thing while tears ran freely down her face, and neither Tam nor Ines remarked on it, which was its own kindness.

* * *

Later, when the rain had softened to mist and the stew was gone and Tam had actually dozed off mid-sentence for the first time since Wren had known him to be awake, she and Ines sat by the fire together.

Up close, the damage was worse than it had first appeared. The burns on Ines's hands had blistered and wept; the bandage over her ear was spotted red-brown. When Wren spoke softly on her left side, Ines turned her head carefully, like a bird, to bring her good ear around.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked.

"Everything hurts," Ines said, and then, wryly: "But it's honest pain. Pain I chose. There's a difference." She flexed her bandaged fingers, winced. "For seven years I kept my name locked behind my teeth. Do you know what that's like? Carrying a whole self around and never letting it out of its box?"

Wren thought about six years of watching the world through glass. "Yes," she said. "I think I might."

"Yes." Ines looked into the fire. "I told myself it was wisdom. Caution. Keeping the oath. And some of it was. But some of it was just fear wearing wisdom's coat." She turned to look at Wren, and the firelight moved across her scarred face. "You got your self back today. I got mine. We should probably both be insufferable about it for a while."

"I plan to complain about cold and hunger constantly," Wren said solemnly. "Things other people take for granted. 'Oh, my toe hurts.' 'Ooh, this blanket is scratchy.' Every small feeling, announced at volume."

"Good," Ines said. "Do that. It drives the quiet ones mad, and it's good for you." A pause. The fire popped. "What will you do now? Both of you. Ashfall won't have you — won't have *any* of us, after this. The king's hunters will be picking through that mountain for months, trying to understand what broke."

Wren considered the question. It was strange, having a future again — like being handed a purse full of coins after years of empty pockets. Heavy with possibility.

"Tam talks about the valley past the pass," she said. "Where magic isn't outlawed yet. Where people plant things." She hesitated, then asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the fire. "Ines. My mother. She's still in Ashfall. She sold me, and she — " Her voice caught, steadied. "I don't know what I feel about her. Some days I think I'll never forgive her. Some days I remember her singing to me before the debt came, and I miss her so badly I can't breathe."

Ines was quiet for a moment. "Both can be true," she said finally. "Forgiveness isn't a door you walk through once. Some days it's open, some days it's shut, and you're allowed to stand in the doorway either way." She smiled faintly. "I burned my own life down yesterday. Ask me again in a month whether I regret it. I'll probably give you a different answer each time."

Behind them, Tam snored, twitched, and muttered something about tolls.

Wren laughed — easily, warmly, with her whole chest — and Ines looked at her as if the sound itself were proof that everything they'd lost down in the dark had been worth finding again.

Then Ines went still.

Her head tilted, good ear toward the pass, and Wren watched the ease drain out of her face by degrees.

"What is it?" Wren whispered.

"Incoming," Ines murmured. "One rider. Moving fast." Her hand found the broken sword lying beside her — not gripping it, just touching it, an old habit waking up. "And unless I've forgotten the sound of hooves entirely — and fair warning, I may have — "

Through the mist at the mouth of the pass, a horse emerged, and on its back, a figure in the grey-and-silver of the king's hunt.

Captain Vess's colors.

But Vess was dead. They had all seen the magic take her.

The rider drew closer, and Wren felt Tam wake behind her, felt him go rigid, heard him breathe one disbelieving word:

"*Mother?*"
