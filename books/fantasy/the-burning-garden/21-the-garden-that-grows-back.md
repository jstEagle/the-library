## 21. The Garden That Grows Back

The valley had no name that anyone could agree on.

The shepherds who grazed their flocks along its southern rim called it the Hollow of Birds, because in spring the cliffs echoed with so much song it sounded like argument. The old woman who kept the mill at its mouth insisted it was called Merrow's Rest, after a drowned queen nobody else had ever heard of. The mapmakers had given up and written *vale, unremarkable* in small disappointed letters, which Ines thought was the most honest thing any official document had ever said about anything.

She liked it here precisely because nobody could decide what to call it. A place without a fixed name was a place where a woman could arrive without one either.

It had been six weeks since the mountain. Six weeks since her voice had torn itself open on the true name of the Order of the Speaking Sword, since the vault of sold voices had poured out into the dark like a river deciding, all at once, to run uphill. Six weeks since Captain Vess had been unmade by the very magic she'd tried to claim — not burned, exactly, though Ines's dreams sometimes disagreed, but *unwritten*, erased so thoroughly that even the memory of her face came back to Ines smudged, like a thumbprint on wet ink.

Six weeks since Wren Calloway had woken up hungry for the first time in four years, and cried about it, and then eaten an entire loaf of bread while crying, which Tam still described to anyone who would listen as the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

Ines knelt in the turned earth at the center of the valley and pressed a seedling into the soil with two fingers. Her hands were steady. They had been steady for weeks now, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake forever. But it turned out that terror, like grief, did not last at full strength. It settled. It became something you carried rather than something that carried you.

"Row's done," she said.

Her voice came out rough. It always came out rough now, like gravel rolled in honey — there was still music under it, but you had to listen past the scrape. Half-deafened in one ear, half-burned down her throat, the healers in the valley had told her she was lucky. She had thanked them politely and then gone outside and been quietly sick behind the mill, because *lucky* was such a small word for what had happened, and because she hadn't yet learned how to hear it without hearing everything it left out.

But the voice was hers. That was the thing. For seven years she had kept a voice like a knife wrapped in cloth, afraid of its own edge. Now the edge was blunted and scarred and permanently hoarse, and it was *hers*, every rasp of it, and when she spoke, nothing burned unless she wanted it to.

She stood, brushed dirt from her knees, and surveyed her work: three rows of seedlings, a trellis waiting for beans, a fence made of salvaged palings that leaned like tired soldiers. It didn't look like much yet. Gardens never did, in the beginning. That was the whole secret of them — they were just faith, buried on schedule.

"You're planting them too close again," said Wren, from the fence.

Ines didn't startle. She'd stopped startling at Wren months ago, back when Wren was still a hollow girl drifting through Ashfall like smoke wearing a dress, and now that Wren was solid — laughably, gloriously solid, with color in her cheeks and dirt already under her fingernails despite having arrived five minutes ago — startling seemed beside the point.

"They're not too close," Ines said. "They're companionable."

"They're going to fight over the same water."

"Then they'll learn to share. It builds character."

Wren laughed — a real laugh, loud and unguarded, the kind of sound that Ines suspected Wren was still collecting greedily, the way a starving person collects recipes. She came through the gate carrying a basket of bread ends from the mill and sat down right in the dirt beside the newest row, which was the sort of thing a girl did when she had spent four years unable to feel the difference between sitting in dirt and sitting anywhere else.

"I brought crusts," Wren said. "And gossip."

"Gossip first. Crusts as payment."

"The miller's son asked Tam if we're married."

Ines took a crust and considered it. "What did he say?"

"He said, and I quote, 'Gods, no, she's my sister, but the swordspeaker might be available if you enjoy being lectured about soil drainage.'"

"I have never lectured anyone about drainage."

"You lectured me for forty minutes yesterday. There was a diagram. You drew it in the dirt with a stick."

"That was horticultural guidance, not a lecture."

"It had *arrows*, Ines."

Ines bit the crust to keep from smiling and failed immediately. This was the other thing she was still collecting, greedily: moments like this, ordinary and warm and entirely unbloodied. She had spent seven years believing that a life could be safe or true but never both, and here was the proof otherwise, sitting in the dirt eating bread ends in a valley whose name couldn't be pinned down. She intended to hoard every scrap of it.

* * *

Tam arrived at midday, which meant he arrived loudly, which meant he arrived the way Tam did everything.

They heard him before they saw him — the rattle of the cart, the cheerful profanity directed at the cart's left wheel, which had developed opinions, and then Tam himself cresting the rise with sawdust in his hair and a plank balanced on his shoulder like a ceremonial yoke.

"House update!" he announced to the valley at large. "The roof is on. Mostly. The part over the bedroom is aspirational, but the kitchen is watertight, which I argue is the correct priority, because a dry bed is a luxury but dry bread is a necessity."

"You could put the bed in the kitchen," Wren called.

"I could, and I've considered it, but the miller's son already thinks I'm strange enough." He dropped the plank, surveyed Ines's garden rows with the frank assessment of a man who had grown up above a shop and knew commerce when he saw it, and said, "Too close together."

"*Thank* you," said Wren.

"Traitor," said Ines, but she was laughing.

Tam Calloway had changed in ways that were easier to see than to describe. He was still quick-tongued, still prone to bargaining with the universe as though the universe were a slightly dim shopkeeper who could be talked down on price. But the desperation had gone out of it. He had paid a bridge-keeper his memory of his mother's face to buy Ines an hour of freedom, and he had never gotten it back — the toll-keepers did not give refunds, and the mountain's collapse had scattered whatever remained of its ledgers. Some days Ines caught him looking at nothing, reaching for something that wasn't there, and she understood that he was trying to remember whether he had loved their mother or hated her, and finding only the fact of the love with the face worn off it.

He had told her once, on the road out of the mountains, while Wren slept: "I keep thinking I should feel lighter. Everyone says you feel lighter when you let go of resentment. I don't feel lighter. I feel like a man who gave away his coat and can't remember if it was winter."

"What do you do about it?" Ines had asked.

"Ask Wren what she looked like," he said. "She remembers her better than I do now. Isn't that strange? The daughter she sold keeps the mother alive, and the son who stayed gets to visit." He had shrugged, and his eyes had been wet and furious and calm all at once. "Anyway. It was worth it. I'd pay it again. Don't tell her I said that, she'll get insufferable."

Ines hadn't told her. But Wren had found out anyway, because Wren was her brother's sister, and now there was a new bench by the house site that Tam had built without being asked, and neither of them ever mentioned why it faced the mountains.

He helped her finish the fence, complaining the entire time in a manner that Ines had come to understand was simply how Tam expressed contentment. By late afternoon the palings stood straighter, the seedlings had been argued into better spacing, and the three of them sat on the fence rail sharing the last of the bread and watching the light go gold across the valley.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked suddenly. "Ashfall."

Ines thought about it honestly, because Wren deserved honesty; she had bought it at a price no one should have paid.

"I miss the shop," she said. "The smell of the wood polish. Mrs. Halloway next door, who pretended not to know what I was for seven years and left soup on my step every solstice anyway. I miss being nobody." She turned the crust in her fingers. "But I was nobody the way a held breath is nobody. And I don't miss holding it."

"The city burned your shop," Tam said. "Your whole life. Doesn't that make you angry?"

It was a fair question, and she let it sit for a moment before answering.

"When I was young," she said, "my sword-mistress told me that a garden isn't planted on good ground. It's planted on ground that's been burned, or flooded, or broken, because that's the only ground that's ever truly ready to grow something new. Old ground holds on to what it was. Burned ground has no choice but to begin again." She looked out at her three crooked rows. "So yes. I'm angry. And I'm also grateful, and I've stopped pretending those can't live in the same person."

Wren reached over and squeezed her hand. Tam, incapable of leaving sincerity unattended, said, "That's beautiful. Also your fence is crooked on the north side."

"*Get off my fence,*" said Ines, and shoved him, and he went, laughing, into the dirt, and for a while nobody in the valley said anything wise at all.

* * *

Evenings, when the light failed, Ines practiced.

Not swordspeech — not yet, not fully. Her throat wasn't ready for the true words, and perhaps some part of her wasn't either. But she sat on her doorstep after Wren and Tam had gone down the lane to their half-roofed house, and she spoke aloud, deliberately, things she had spent seven years refusing to say.

*I am Ines Pemberton.* Easy now. Almost pleasant.

*I was sworn to the Order of the Speaking Sword, and the order is dead, and I am not.*

*I chose truth over safety, and it cost me my shop, my hearing on the left side, and the smoothness of my voice, and I would choose it again.*

Each sentence came out rasping, imperfect, alive. Somewhere in the dark a nightjar answered her, and she chose to believe it was applause.

It was on one of these evenings — the third week of the garden, the air smelling of turned earth and woodsmoke from Tam's chimney, the first bean flowers opening like small green secrets — that she saw the traveler.

She noticed the lantern first, way down where the valley narrowed toward the pass. Then the figure under it, walking with the particular weariness of someone who had come a long way on a rumor. Not a hunter's walk. Hunters moved like cats pretending to be men. This was a scholar's walk, or a pilgrim's, shoulders stooped under a pack, stopping every few hundred paces to look at the stars as though checking them against a description.

Ines rose slowly from the doorstep.

The traveler stopped at her gate. Up close she could see it was a young woman, maybe twenty, with wind-chapped cheeks and ink stains on her fingers and an expression of someone rehearsing a speech and hoping desperately not to need it.

"Beg pardon," the traveler said. "I'm looking for — well. They say there's a swordspeaker living in this valley. Last of an order that was massacred for refusing the crown. They say she freed a vault of souls beneath the Pale King's mountain." The young woman swallowed. "They say her name is Ines Pemberton."

The old reflex rose instantly, smooth and practiced: *never heard of her, wrong valley, wrong story, good evening.* Seven years of survival folded into a single polite lie. Ines felt it in her mouth like an old coin, familiar and worn.

She looked past the traveler, down the lane, to where lamplight showed in the window of the half-built house, and heard Wren laughing at something inside, and smelled Tam burning dinner on purpose because burnt dinner was funnier.

Seven years she had hidden. One afternoon in a throne room, she had spoken.

"I'm going to ask you a question," Ines said, and her scarred voice carried clearly across the quiet garden, "and I want you to answer honestly, because I'll know if you don't. Why are you looking for her?"

The traveler set down her pack. Drew a breath. And began to speak.
