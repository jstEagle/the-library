## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Noor Halloran reached the bottom of the third ledger, and the wax had pooled into a shape that looked, if one squinted with sufficient imagination, like a kneeling figure. Noor did not squint. Noor was an auditor, and auditors did not squint at candlewax looking for omens; they noted the candle's cost against the Guild's operating expenses and moved on.

Still. They looked at it a moment longer than strictly necessary.

The counting room of the Hollow Guild occupied the lowest floor of a building that officially did not exist, on a street whose name changed depending on who was asking and why. It was a long room, low-ceilinged, its walls shelved from stone floor to smoke-darkened beams with ledgers—hundreds of them, thousands, each spine labeled in the small, precise hand of some dead or retired clerk. *Borrowings, 1147–1151. Sales, Misc., Vol. IX. Debts Uncollectible (Deceased). Debts Uncollectible (Worse Than Deceased).* The air smelled of tallow, old vellum, and the faint copper tang that clung to anything that had once been someone's life.

Noor sat at the central table with three ledgers open before them and a fourth balanced on their knee, and worked the way they always worked: methodically, silently, alone.

This was the quarterly audit of Impossible Accounts—the strangest shelf in the Guild's collection, the entries that made new clerks laugh nervously and old clerks go quiet. Noor had been an old clerk for eleven years now, which meant they no longer laughed at all.

They dipped their pen and began.

*Item: One jar, sealed glass, containing laughter borrowed from the children of Tanner's Row, 1149. Borrower: the Lady Maren Voss, to fill her house after the deaths of her sons. Terms: returnable upon her death, with interest.* Noor checked the jar itself where it sat on the verification shelf—a squat thing, green glass, and even through the seal you could hear it faintly if you held it to your ear: children laughing, high and bright and endless, ten years of laughter with nowhere to spend itself. The interest clause was properly notarized. The lady was still alive, still childless, still paying her annual tithe. Noor entered: *Account current. Note: jar hums when the moon is full. Recommend continued storage away from the grief inventory.*

That last part wasn't procedure. But the ledger beside it—the widow's account—had begun, in recent quarters, to resonate. Grief and laughter, stored too close together, developed opinions about one another. Noor had learned this the hard way, during the incident with the weeping jar and the memory of a wedding, and had written a memo about it that the Guildmaster had approved without reading, as he approved most things that kept the shelves quiet.

*Item: One grief, purchased whole,* Noor wrote next, turning pages. *Seller: Widow Ansel, of Candlemaker Street. Buyer: unnamed (sealed by mutual consent). Description: grief for a husband drowned, duration thirty years, intensity: complete. Price paid: the widow's remaining years, converted at standard rate.*

Noor paused over this one, as they always did. Somewhere in Vell, a woman walked around with lightness in her step because she had sold the weight of loving a dead man, and somewhere else—Noor never learned where—an anonymous buyer carried that love like a stone in the chest, weeping at odd hours for a man they had never met. The Guild called this commerce. Noor had stopped calling it anything, most days, because the names you gave things in the counting room had a way of becoming true.

Their pen hovered. Then, in the margin, in the smallest hand they could manage: *She visits his grave every week anyway. She thinks she doesn't know why. I have not told her.*

It was not a proper audit note. Noor wrote perhaps six such notes a year, and knew exactly where each one lived in each ledger, and had never once shown them to anyone. They were, Noor supposed, the closest thing they had to prayers.

Which was, admittedly, a low bar these days.

Noor set down the pen and flexed their fingers, and in the lamplight the oath-mark on the back of their right hand caught the flame—a thin line of silver script, circling twice around the wrist bone and ending in a small closed eye at the base of the middle finger. Every sworn servant of the Hollow Guild bore a mark. Most bore the mark of the Guild itself: a key, a door, a coin. Noor's was older, and stranger, and had come to them unasked at the age of nineteen, burning itself into their skin in a shrine behind a fishmonger's stall while Vessarine's voice filled their skull like water filling a cup.

*You will keep the accounts,* the god had said. *All debts must be witnessed, little scribe. Even mine. Especially mine.*

And Noor had said yes—of course they had said yes; you did not refuse a god—and for nine years afterward, Vessarine had spoken to them most days. Small things, mostly. Commentary on the weather. Wry observations about the greed of the living. Once, memorably, a forty-minute disquisition on the theological implications of a cheese merchant who had tried to sell the same Wednesday twice. Gods, Noor had learned, were much like anyone else when you got to know them: mostly lonely, occasionally tedious, capable of astonishing tenderness at unpredictable hours.

Then, ten years ago, on the morning of the winter solstice, the voice had stopped.

Not faded. Not gone distant. Stopped—mid-sentence, in fact, which was somehow worse. Noor had been carrying two cups of tea up from the kitchen, one for themselves and one set out, as always, on the counting-room windowsill for the god, and halfway up the stairs the silence had arrived like a door closing. The tea had gone cold on the sill. It went cold on the sill every night now, out of habit, out of something Noor refused to call hope in front of witnesses.

The other oath-bearers of Vessarine had scattered or died or simply stopped marking the solstice. Noor alone still kept the accounts, because the oath-mark had never faded, and an oath-mark that does not fade is a contract still in force, and Noor Halloran honored their contracts. It was the first rule of the trade.

*He is not dead,* Noor told themselves, the way they told themselves every night, in the same flat tone in which they entered figures in columns. *Dead gods do not leave standing oaths. He is silent. Silence is a condition, not a conclusion.*

It was poor theology and worse comfort, but it balanced, and Noor was a creature who needed things to balance.

* * *

The bell above the counting-room door rang at half past midnight, which meant the night runner had come up from the drop-boxes with the day's intake. Tam was sixteen, all elbows and nervous energy, and he stood in the doorway holding a leather satchel like it might bite him.

"Quarter-day entries, Master Halloran." He set the satchel down and hovered. "There's—there's one in there I couldn't make head nor tail of. Didn't want to guess at the classification."

"You never guess," Noor said. "Guessing is how clerks end up owing things."

"Yes, Master Halloran."

"No one has ever ended up owing things through excessive precision. Go home, Tam. Sleep. If you dream of numbers, ignore the ones that talk."

Tam left, relieved, and Noor opened the satchel and dealt the entries out across the table like a card player: a dozen routine transactions, a soul-pawn redeemed at last by a grandmother who had saved for nine years in copper coins, a complaint from a client whose borrowed courage had curdled into borrowed arrogance and required adjustment. All ordinary. All balanceable.

And then, near the bottom of the stack, an entry form that made Noor go very still.

It was the standard form—Guild stock, cream vellum, the watermark of the hollow door visible when tilted to the lamp—but the hand that had filled it out was not any hand Noor knew, and Noor knew every clerk's hand in the building the way a musician knows instruments. The letters were old-fashioned, formal, with the long descenders favored a generation ago. The ink was good ink. And the entry read:

*Received into inventory: one king's shadow. Condition: excellent. Provenance: [blank]. Buyer: [blank].*

Noor read it three times.

Kings' shadows were not unheard of in the impossible trades—shadows were portable, stealable, sellable, and royalty was only people with better fences—but they were rare enough that every historical instance was catalogued, cross-referenced, and taught to apprentices as cautionary lore. There had been the Tarquin affair. The shadow of the Doge of Serel, sold back to him four times before he noticed. A whole grim chapter on the Shadow Wars of the eastern kingdoms, which had started, according to the textbooks, with precisely this kind of entry.

But every entry in history had a buyer. That was the entire architecture of the trade: nothing came into the Hollow Guild's inventory without someone wanting it out again, or wanting it kept, or wanting it used. Desire was the engine. A blank in the buyer's field was not merely irregular. It was like finding a heartbeat written down in a ledger of stones.

Noor turned the form over. Nothing on the reverse but the watermark. They held it to the candle; the watermark showed the hollow door, standard issue, no hidden script. They checked the intake date: today. The drop-box: the one outside the Chapel of Quiet Hours, of all places—which served the poorest parish in Vell and took in maybe three entries a year, usually pawned wedding rings and once, memorably, a grandmother's recipe for forgiveness.

No buyer. No provenance. No deposit fee recorded, which meant whoever had filed it had paid nothing, which meant either the Guild's security had failed for the first time in living memory, or the entry had not come through the box at all but had simply *appeared* in it, and Noor had been in this trade long enough to know that objects which appeared rather than arrived were objects with intentions.

And beneath the description, in that same old-fashioned hand, one more line, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Terms: collectible upon completion of the count.*

Noor sat back in the chair. The candle stub guttered, steadied. Somewhere overhead, the Guild slept on—clerks and runners and the night watchman, all of them trusting the ledgers the way sailors trust the sea, which is to say completely and fatally.

*Completion of the count.* Count of what? The Guild counted many things—debts, years, names, sins—but no entry in any ledger Noor had ever audited referred to *the* count, definite article, capitalized in the writer's intent if not the letter. It was the phrasing of prophecy, or of threat, and both made Noor's skin prickle along the silver line of the oath-mark.

Because here was the other thing. Here was the thing Noor had noticed and immediately, professionally suppressed: the year of the last recorded king's-shadow transaction in the archives—the Binding entries, the sealed folio even the Guildmaster needed three keys to open—was the same year Vessarine had gone silent. Ten years ago. Winter solstice. Noor had checked that coincidence a hundred times over the decade, the way one worries a loose tooth, and had never found a way to make it mean anything.

Now a second shadow-entry lay on their table, buyerless, terms unreadable, dropped into a poor parish's box like a coin tossed to a beggar—or a message slipped under a door.

Noor did what they always did with wrong notes: they reached for a ledger to put it in its place. They pulled the intake register toward them, uncapped the pen, and wrote, in their own small precise hand:

*Entry 4471: one king's shadow, provenance unknown, buyer unknown. Classification: pending. Note:*

The pen stopped.

Note what? That the form felt warm? That when Noor had first picked it up, the oath-mark on their wrist had tingled like a limb waking from sleep—that for one absurd, unforgivable half-second, ten years of silence notwithstanding, Noor would have sworn on every ledger in the building that the silence had *shifted*, the way a sleeping man shifts in a bed, present, attentive, listening?

Noor wrote: *Note: verify against sealed archive, Binding folio, at earliest opportunity.*

Then, after a moment, in the margin, in the smallest hand of all, where no auditor would ever be required to see it:

*I think he felt me reading it.*

Outside, over the rooftops of Vell, dawn was still hours away, and the city slept, and somewhere in the dark a king wore a crown, and a shadow waited in inventory to be claimed, and a god who was not dead kept whatever counsel gods keep when they cannot speak.

Noor trimmed the candle, drew the next ledger close, and worked on until morning, because the accounts, at least, still answered.
