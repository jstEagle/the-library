## 3. The Inquisitor's Summons

The cell smelled of tallow and terror.

Sefa Vane had stood in a hundred of these rooms—stone-squared, windowless, lit by a single lamp that made everything look like a confession waiting to happen. She knew the architecture of interrogation the way other women knew their kitchens: where the shadows pooled thickest, where a man's eyes would go when he lied, where the damp crept up through the mortar so that even the walls seemed to sweat alongside the accused. She had been the Royal Inquisitor's blade for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned that most men broke before she ever raised her voice. The room did half her work. The lamp did the rest.

Father Anselm Rook was not breaking. He was singing.

It was not a hymn, or if it had begun as one, it had long since shed its structure, becoming something looser and more terrible—a thread of melody that wandered off into silence, stopped, began again at a different pitch, stopped again. His voice was wrecked. He had been in the custody of the Temple Guard for three days before they surrendered him to the Crown, and in those three days he had apparently sung without ceasing, because when he tried to speak now, words came out frayed and bleeding.

"He hasn't slept," said Sergeant Corvel, who had escorted her down. A thick man with a kind face ruined by professional boredom, Corvel had served under Sefa long enough to know when to be uncomfortable. "The healers gave him something. It didn't take. He just—" He gestured vaguely at the priest, who sat cross-legged on the cot with his hands open on his knees, palms up, in the posture of a man receiving rain. "He keeps saying *they answered*. That's all. They answered, they answered."

"Who answered?"

"That's the thing, Inquisitor. He won't say who. Just *they*."

Sefa set down her case—the flat leather one with the instruments arranged in velvet grooves, though she rarely needed more than the lamp and her own patience—and studied the priest. He was perhaps fifty, grey-bearded, built soft from years of good living in a prosperous parish. St. Ovel's, the writ said. One of the wealthy river-parishes, incense and gilt, a congregation of merchants' wives. Not the sort of man who cracked easily, and not the sort of man who lied well either. His kind confessed to pride, to embezzled alms, occasionally to sins of the flesh committed with someone else's spouse. They did not confess to madness.

And yet here he was, palms open, singing to no one.

"Leave us," Sefa said.

Corvel hesitated. "He bit the last man who got close."

"Then I'll stay out of biting range." She waited until the door had shut behind the sergeant, then dragged the single stool to the center of the cell, directly into the lamplight, and sat down where Father Rook could see her clearly. She did not speak for a while. This was technique, but it was also courtesy; she liked to give people time to understand what she was before she asked anything of them.

After four or five minutes of her silence layered over his song, the song faltered.

"You're not going to shout at me," the priest said. His voice was a ruin, but there was lucidity in his eyes now, sudden and awful, like light through a crack in a wall. "They told me you'd shout."

"They were wrong." Sefa folded her hands. "I'm Sefa Vane. Royal Inquisitor, Third Chair. I've been sent to determine whether you're ill, deceived, or dangerous. You may help me decide, or you may sing. But I'd rather hear it plainly, whatever *it* is."

Father Rook looked at his own open hands as though they belonged to someone else. "You'll think I'm mad."

"Probably. Madness is often the correct answer. But I've found that madmen and prophets fail the same tests, so we'll run them anyway, and see which failure you are." She kept her tone level, almost gentle. It was not kindness, exactly. It was precision dressed in kindness's clothing, and after eleven years she no longer troubled herself about the difference. "Tell me what happened at St. Ovel's."

The priest's throat worked. When he spoke, the words came slowly, each one handled like something hot.

"It was Tuesday. Evening service. We have maybe two hundred souls on a good night, mostly the fish-market families from the lower rows—they come because the candles are pretty and the benches are warm. An old man came in off the street. Beggar. He was feverish—I could see it from the altar, the way he swayed, the sweat shining on him. He knelt at the rail during the intercession, and he prayed. Out loud. He didn't know how to pray quietly, poor soul, he'd never been taught. He just said it, right there in front of everyone. Said, *please, please, I don't want to die in the cold, my daughter's coming on the barge Thursday.*" Rook's hands closed slowly, fingers digging into his palms. "And the candles went white."

"Went white."

"All of them. Every flame in the church, straight up, colorless, like—like the light itself had been *corrected*. And the fever left him. I watched it go. It came off him like steam off a kettle, and he stood up, and he was well. Whole. He walked out laughing." The priest's voice climbed toward the register it had held when she entered, the wandering note, the edge of the abyss. "Two hundred people saw it. Two hundred people, Inquisitor, and do you know what they did? They didn't rejoice. They *knelt*. All of them, all at once, like wheat under wind. And then they started praying. All of them. Everything they'd ever wanted, everything they'd been afraid to ask, poured out at once—and the candles stayed white, and the prayers—" He stopped. His whole body shuddered once, like a horse refusing a fence. "The prayers were answered. All of them. Small things. A lame boy's ankle. A widow's rent money appearing in her shoe. A woman who hadn't conceived in nine years—weeping in the aisle an hour later, already knowing. Small things, small mercies, dozens of them, falling like hail."

"And this distressed you."

Rook looked up, and what was in his face was worse than fear. It was recognition.

"I have served the Quiet Accord my whole life," he whispered. "I was ordained under the doctrine of Silence. *The gods are gone; the gods are sleeping; the gods, if they ever were, have withdrawn beyond the veil, and our prayers rise like smoke and stop at the ceiling.* That is what I taught. That is what I believed with my whole heart, because the alternative—" His breath caught. "Inquisitor, do you know what happens to a man's faith when it turns out the ceiling was never there? When every prayer he ever mumbled into his collar actually *went somewhere*, and was *heard*, and was simply—not answered? For thirty years? Do you know what that means?"

Sefa did not answer. Her spine had gone very straight without her permission.

"It means they were choosing not to," Rook said. "All those years. Choosing silence. And then something changed, and they stopped choosing it, and now—" He laughed, a dry, splintering sound. "Now I lie awake and I can feel them listening. All of them. Like standing in a dark house full of people who have finally turned toward you. I can't stop singing because if I sing, I choose what fills the silence, instead of them filling it. Do you understand? It's the only prayer I have left. Noise. Just noise."

For a long moment the only sound in the cell was the lamp's small combustion.

"The phrase," Sefa said at last, because it was her job, because the writ required it, because if she did not put procedure between herself and the cold spreading through her chest she was going to sit down in this cell beside him and start singing too. "The Temple Guard's report mentions you repeated a phrase. Written. Where did you see it written?"

"I didn't see it anywhere." Rook's hands uncurled again, palms rising, offering. "That's the horror of it. It arrived. During the service, when the prayers started landing, it arrived in me the way a hymn arrives—whole, complete, as if I'd always known it. And it wouldn't leave. It wanted out. It wanted—" He swallowed. "It wanted skin, Inquisitor. Words that want skin."

"Show me your arms."

He extended them without resistance, turning them over like a man showing a physician a rash. The left forearm was bare of ink except for the ordination sigil at the wrist—but below the elbow, cut into the flesh in a hand far steadier than any madman's, in letters deep enough to have scarred:

**THE CROWN REMEMBERS**

Sefa leaned close. The cuts were clean, deliberate, cauterized by some heat or treated with something that had sealed them black. They were not self-harm in any style she had catalogued across eleven years of cataloguing wounds. They were *inscription*. Someone—or something—had used this man as a page.

"When?" she asked quietly.

"The second night. I woke and it was done. No pain until after. There's more." He hesitated, then, moving with the slow obedience of the utterly broken, pulled his collar aside. On the side of his neck, smaller, finer, half-hidden beneath the jaw:

**IT COUNTS**

She sat back. Her hands, resting on her knees, were perfectly steady, and she was grateful to them for the professionalism, because the rest of her had gone somewhere cold and high and quiet.

"Father Rook," she said. "Has anyone else seen these?"

"The Temple Guard physician. He vomited."

"Anyone else?"

"No one. They wrapped my arms and brought me here."

"Good." She heard herself say it and noted, distantly, how quickly the word had come, how much it sounded like relief. "I need you to listen carefully. What you've described—mass hysteria, ergot contamination in the parish bread, a traveling charlatan with sleight-of-hand and a sympathetic crowd—these are all documented phenomena. The Crown has protocols. What you will *not* do is repeat the answered-prayers account to anyone further. Not to magistrates, not to fellow clergy, not to your own mother. As far as any record will show, Father Anselm Rook suffered a febrile break, was treated, and is being remanded to the Temple infirmary at St. Verity's for rest and observation. The inscriptions will be bandaged and logged as self-inflicted during delirium. Do you understand me?"

The priest stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes—not defiance. Pity.

"You've received orders already," he said. "Haven't you. Before you even spoke to me. You knew what you were supposed to find before you opened the door."

Sefa rose, collected her case, and did not answer, which was itself an answer, and they both knew it.

* * *

The order had come by courier at dawn, sealed with the Lord Chancellor's personal signet, and Sefa had read it three times at her desk with the particular attention she gave documents that felt wrong in the hands. *Re: detainee Rook, Anselm, priest of St. Ovel's. Findings to reflect nervous disorder arising from contaminated provisions. All testimony regarding anomalous events to be struck and suppressed. Inscriptions to be recorded as self-inflicted. No copies. No correspondence. Acknowledge by return.*

No explanation. Lord Chancellor Ives did not deal in explanations; he dealt in outcomes, and his outcomes were always tidy. Eleven years ago, when she was a raw recruit from the inquisitorial academy with top marks and a head full of justice, she would have burned such an order in the lamp and written her refusal in ash. She had imagined doing exactly that, once, in vivid detail, rehearsing the speech she would give.

But she was forty-one now, and she had buried a partner, survived two purges, and learned that institutions did not fall to speeches. They fell to paper trails, and paper trails required survival. So she had acknowledged the order by return courier, as required. *Understood. Will comply.*

Then she had gone down to the cell and done her work, and now she sat alone in her office above the Hall of Inquiry with the lamp trimmed low and the order lying on her blotter like a dead thing, and she understood—with the same terrible clarity Father Rook had shown—that she had known what she would find before she opened the door. Not the details. But the shape. The shape of an order that arrives too fast, too sealed, too silent. Orders like that are not written to protect people. People get warnings, hearings, mercy. Orders like that are written to protect *things*—secrets, arrangements, machinery that must not be seen turning.

*The crown remembers. It counts.*

She thought about the king. Everyone thought about the king lately, in the way one thinks about weather—Aldric the Peacemaker, twenty-two years on the throne, the war ended in his first year, the city fattened on the peace that followed. He was beloved the way landmarks are beloved: constantly present, rarely examined. He had grown strange in the last decade, the courtiers said. Quieter. More precise. He paused sometimes mid-audience, they said, staring at nothing, and then resumed as if the pause had been scheduled.

Ten years, Sefa thought suddenly, and her pen stopped above the page. Ten years since the old shrines had gone dark for good. Ten years since the last recorded miracle in Vell—she had looked it up once, early in her career, some minor healing at a riverside chapel, dated the same season the Court had issued the Edict of Continuity, the one that formally declared the age of divine intervention closed. Coincidence, the academy had taught. The gods' withdrawal was gradual, natural, a tide going out.

But tides did not stop on schedule. And kings did not pause mid-sentence unless something inside them was being consulted.

She looked down at her blotter. The official report lay half-written in her careful inquisitorial hand: *detainee presents symptoms consistent with febrile psychosis; inscriptions self-administered during delirium; recommends transfer to St. Verity's.* All of it false. All of it tidy. All of it exactly what Lord Chancellor Ives required, and she had written hundreds of such sentences in eleven years—softened this, omitted that, bent the record two degrees left so it would fit the channel dug for it—and she had always told herself the bending was small, survivable, the grease that kept the machine from grinding up the innocent.

But this was not two degrees. This was a priest driven out of his mind by the return of the gods, carved with a warning he hadn't written, ordered erased before sundown. *The crown remembers. It counts.* Counts what? Counts whom? And why did the answer frighten the Lord Chancellor more than the question?

Sefa took a fresh sheet of paper. For a long moment she simply held the pen above it, feeling the weight of the choice settle onto her like a coat of mail—cold at first, then merely heavy, then part of her.

Then she wrote, in a small, compressed cipher of her own devising, the account Father Rook had given her: the white candles, the beggar cured, the hail of small mercies, the congregation kneeling like wheat. She wrote the inscription verbatim, twice, letter-perfect. She wrote the date, the hour, the cell number, the name of the escorting sergeant. She wrote her own name and rank at the bottom and signed it, because a document unsigned was a document she could disavow, and she was done disavowing.

She folded the copy small and slid it into the hidden seam of her boot, where it pressed against her ankle like a second pulse. Then she finished the official report, false and tidy, blotted it, and rang for Corvel to take it upstairs.

The lamp guttered. Outside her narrow window, Vell glittered under a fat moon, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke, and somewhere out in those streets, Sefa was certain, more candles were burning white. More prayers were landing. More skin was being written on.

Her duty said suppress. Her oath said serve the Crown. But oaths, she was beginning to suspect, were only as honest as the things they were sworn to—and the thing wearing Aldric's crown had just given her, in a madman's scarred flesh, the first honest sentence she'd read in years.

*It counts.*

She meant to find out what.
