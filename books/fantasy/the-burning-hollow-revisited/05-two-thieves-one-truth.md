## 5. Two Thieves, One Truth

The Hollow Guild kept its ledgers in a vault that did not officially exist, behind a door that answered to three knocks, a whispered word, and the blood of someone who had sworn never to give it. Sefa had all three now—knocks and word bought from a frightened clerk, and blood from her own thumb, because she had once signed a Guild contract herself, years ago, for a name she no longer used.

She told herself it was an inspection. She had a warrant with the royal seal on it, wax still warm from the Chancellor's own ring, and the seal said *seize and suppress*, which was the Inquisition's favorite pairing of words. But warrants were just prayers written by men who didn't believe in gods, and Sefa had stopped believing in her own orders somewhere around the third priest she'd found carved from wrist to collarbone with THE CROWN REMEMBERS.

So she came at night, alone, without telling Ives. Without telling anyone. That was the thing about doubt—it made you a thief before it ever made you brave.

The vault door opened to her knock like something that had been waiting. Inside, the air tasted of dust and candle smoke and old transactions, and the shelves rose higher than lamplight could reach, row after row of them, each ledger spine lettered in a hand too neat to be human patience. *Borrowed Years, 1140–1151. Names, Unmade. Griefs, Purchased and Resold.* The Guild traded in impossible things, and here they all were, itemized, priced, and filed as if impossibility were merely a matter of good bookkeeping.

She found the shadow-ledger on the fourth shelf, exactly where the clerk's trembling directions had promised: a slim volume bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its spine stamped only with a small crown, no title, no dates. Ledgers that wanted to be found were labeled. This one wasn't.

Sefa took it down and opened it on her knees, right there in the aisle, because some part of her already knew she would not be leaving with it quietly.

The first entry was older than she was. *One king's shadow, delivered whole. Buyer: withheld by request of the Crown. Price: deferred.*

Deferred. Not paid. Sixty years of entries followed, each one a variation on the same wound—*interest accrued; payment postponed; buyer requests extension*—and every few pages, in fresher ink, a note in a different hand: *collection imminent. Do not alarm the asset.*

The asset. She turned back to the first page and read the opening line twice, because the first time her eyes had slid off it like water off glass:

*This account concerns the Binding of Aldric, being the price owed for the ending of the War of the Two Rivers, and shall remain open until collected in full.*

Aldric. The king. Her king, whose signature sat at the bottom of her commission, whose face was stamped on the coins in her purse. Someone had put him on the throne the way you'd set a keystone into an arch—and the Guild had held the receipt ever since, and the receipt had never been settled, and the priests were carving words into their own flesh about crowns that remembered, and Sefa stood in a dead man's library holding the arithmetic of everything.

She copied fast. She had brought paper for exactly this—the inquisitor's habit of transcription, drilled into her until it lived below thought. *Price deferred. Interest in lives. Collection imminent.* Her pen scratched in the dark like a small animal.

Which is why she heard nothing else, and why the voice, when it came, arrived from nowhere at all.

"You're copying it wrong."

* * *

Noor Halloran had felt the intrusion the way a housekeeper feels a draft—an absence where warmth should be, a wrongness in the vault's long silence. They had come down for the shadow-ledger themselves, because the wrongness in the accounts had been growing all week like mold, and Noor trusted their own unease more than the Guildmaster's reassurances. They had not expected company.

They stood at the end of the aisle with a lamp raised, watching a woman kneel in the dark with stolen papers fanned around her like a gambler's losing hand, and Noor's first instinct—the trained one, the oath-deep one—was to call out, to raise the vault's alarms, to let the Guild do what the Guild did to people who touched its books.

But the woman hadn't run. That was the strange part. Thieves ran. This one had looked up, taken Noor's measure in a single flat glance, and said, "You're copying it wrong," as though the crime scene were a scriptorium and the thief a tutor.

"Wrong how?" Noor asked, before they could stop themselves.

"The interest." The woman tapped the page with a finger gloved in ink. "You've got it as coin. It isn't. Look at the marginalia—*interest accrued in persons*. Every deferral, someone dies. That's why the entries stop making sense if you read them as money."

Noor came closer despite every instinct screaming otherwise, and knelt, and read. And felt something cold walk up their spine, because the woman was right. Noor had audited this ledger a dozen times and always read the interest column as currency, because that was what the column header claimed, because Noor had spent ten years trusting the Guild's headers the way other people trusted scripture. *Interest in persons.* Forty-one names in sixty years, each death dated within a fortnight of a deferral.

"Who are you?" Noor asked.

"Sefa Vane. Royal Inquisitor." She said it without pride, which was itself remarkable; most inquisitors wore the title like armor. "And you're the Halloran. The Guild's auditor. The one who found the widow's grief misfiled under 'joy, inherited.'"

"That was clerical error," Noor said automatically, then heard how it sounded. Clerical error. As if anything in this place were ever merely clerical.

Sefa almost smiled. It was a thin thing, quickly folded away, but Noor caught it. "Do you know what my orders are? Regarding this ledger?"

"Seize and suppress."

"Seize and suppress," she agreed. "I have a warrant. I'm supposed to take this to the Chancellor, who will lock it in a drawer, and in a month the priests will keep carving, and in a year there will be more priests, and I will keep writing reports that go into the same drawer." She looked at the ledger on her knees the way people look at a dying animal they can't help. "I'm tired of drawers."

Noor should have called the guards. Should have drawn the knife at their belt, should have remembered that this woman served the crown, and the crown wore Aldric, and Aldric wore whatever wore Aldric. Instead Noor heard themselves say, "There's wine above the vault. Stolen, technically. Everything here is."

* * *

They drank in the counting room, at the great scarred table where the Guild tallied its impossible inventory, because it was the only room with chairs and neither of them suggested anywhere else. The wine was Vellish red, confiscated years ago from a smuggler who had paid his fine in vintages, and it tasted of cherries and something faintly wrong underneath, like all things the Guild touched.

Sefa sat with her back to the wall, out of habit, and Noor noted it and sat opposite rather than beside her, also out of habit, and for a while the only sound was rain starting against the high windows and two people drinking carefully, each waiting for the other to make the mistake of honesty first.

It was Sefa who broke. Or nearly.

"I interrogated a priest last month," she said, turning her cup slowly on the wood. "Brother Callow. He'd been found preaching in the Fountain Square at dawn, shouting about debts, and when we took him in he wouldn't stop smiling. You know what he said to me? He said, *Inquisitor, you audit souls for a king who is himself an unpaid invoice.*" She laughed, short and joyless. "I had him flogged for insolence. Standard procedure. And then I examined him properly, and found the words cut into his skin—old cuts, healed white, done over years. THE CROWN REMEMBERS. He'd been carving it into himself since before the prayers started answering. Since before any of this."

"And your orders were?"

"To find who put him up to it. Sedition, incitement, foreign agents—the usual menu." She drank. "There was no one. There was just a man who'd known something for twenty years and finally couldn't hold it alone. And I wrote a report saying so, and the report went into a drawer, and a week later Callow was transferred to an asylum in the marshes where no one visits." Her eyes came up, level and hard. "Your turn."

Noor turned their cup the way she'd turned hers. It felt absurdly like a ritual, like two strangers discovering they'd been raised in the same temple by different priests.

"I keep the ledgers," Noor said. "All of them. Borrowed laughter, purchased griefs, unmade debts. Ten years I've balanced these books and believed—I *chose* to believe—that the Guild was neutral. A bank, not a blade. We don't make the bargains, we only keep the accounts. That was the catechism." They paused. "Then an entry came through last week. One king's shadow. No buyer listed. And tonight I went down to pull the original file, and found the file already open on the floor with a royal warrant's worth of thief kneeling over it."

"Clerical error," Sefa offered.

"Something like that." Noor looked into the wine. "My god went silent ten years ago. Vessarine. The god of oaths and endings—you wouldn't know it, the cult was always small, we kept no idols, just marks. An oath-mark, on the inside of the wrist, that burns when a vow is broken nearby." They pushed back their sleeve without deciding to. The mark showed pale in the lamplight, a thin closed circle with a single line through it. "Ten years, no burning, no whispering, nothing. I told myself the god was sleeping. Dead, maybe. Gods die, or so we're told. And then last week the shrines across Vell started answering prayers, and mine stayed silent—but silent differently. Silent like a held breath." They looked up. "Tonight I pulled the Binding file, and the Binding happened eleven years ago. My god went quiet the year after something was put on the throne with the Guild's help, and the price was left open, and I have been keeping books for the thing that silenced my god."

The rain filled the pause, patient and gray.

"You understand what I'm saying," Noor continued, quieter. "I am the auditor of my own betrayal. Every clean column I ever balanced, I balanced for *that*."

Sefa set down her cup. When she spoke, her voice had lost its interrogator's edge and become something plainer, wearier, almost kind. "I took my commission from a king I watched pause mid-sentence last winter. Three seconds. Eyes gone flat and gold, like a lamp behind horn. Then he finished the sentence perfectly, better than perfectly, and everyone applauded, and I decided I'd imagined it." She met Noor's eyes. "I've spent a year deciding I imagined things. You've spent ten. Between us we've got quite a collection."

Noor laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, brief and rusty. It surprised them both. Sefa's mouth twitched toward a smile again and this time it stayed a moment longer.

"We serve broken institutions," Sefa said. "Yours sells impossibilities. Mine buries truths. And both of us keep showing up to work."

"Somebody has to keep the accounts," Noor said. "Even of the fall."

"Then let's compare notes properly." She pulled her copied pages from her coat and spread them on the table between the cups, smoothing each sheet flat with both hands, precise as a surgeon. "Forty-one deaths tied to deferrals. Here—" she tapped a cluster of dates, "—and here, and here. All within days of the Guild recording an extension. Your masters knew. Every time the crown asked for more time, someone died for it, and the Guild filed the death under *costs*."

Noor stared at the dates and felt the last load-bearing belief in their chest crack and settle. "I signed off on those extensions," they said. "My hand is on half these pages."

"I know. I checked the countersignatures." She said it without accusation, which was worse and better at once. "That's why I'm telling you instead of arresting you. Because either you're the best liar in Vell, or you genuinely didn't know what you were signing. And liars don't look like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a person doing arithmetic on their own grave."

The rain thickened against the windows. Somewhere above them, the Guildhouse creaked and settled, full of sleeping clerks who would wake tomorrow and file the world neatly again, and Noor felt suddenly how far away that morning already was.

"There's a name in the ledger margins," Noor said. "Fresh ink, different hand, appearing every few years. *Vessarine.* Just the name, no context, like someone reminding themselves. The same decade my god went quiet. Whoever writes those notes knows what happened to the old gods—and they're still watching."

Sefa leaned forward. "Or whoever writes them is what happened."

"No." Noor shook their head slowly. "The notes are afraid. You can see it in the pressure of the pen—each one shakier than the last. Fear isn't what power writes. Power writes *deferred*. Power writes *do not alarm the asset*. Whatever hand adds Vessarine's name is a prisoner's hand, or a witness's."

"A witness who's been alive a very long time," Sefa said, "and keeps visiting a locked book in a secret vault."

"Or is kept near it." Noor hesitated, then said the thing they'd been circling all night. "The Guildmaster has a private ledger. Older than the shadow-file. Nobody audits it—not me, not anyone. It never leaves his rooms. And twice this year I've seen fresh ink on his hands at hours when the counting room was empty."

Sefa's gaze sharpened. "You think your Guildmaster is the witness."

"I think my Guildmaster is *something*. I've worked under him ten years and I could not tell you the color of his eyes. Could you describe your Chancellor's hands?" Noor asked. "What does Ives do with them when he thinks no one's looking?"

"He counts," Sefa said instantly, and then went still, hearing herself. "He counts. On the desk edge, during audiences. Thumb to fingers, always. I thought it was nerves." She sat back. "Counting. The priest shouted about debts. The ledger says *do not let it finish counting*—no, wait, that's not in what I copied. Is that somewhere?"

"It's in the oldest file," Noor said. "A warning, unsigned: *the debt is not coin. Do not let it finish counting.*" They looked at each other across the ruined wine and the spread of stolen truth, and Noor saw the exact moment Sefa understood—that the thing wearing the king was tallying toward some total, and every deferred payment, every harvested life, every silent god was a figure in its sum.

"So we steal the rest of it," Sefa said simply. "Your master's private ledger. My Chancellor's correspondence. Between your vault and my warrant, we might actually assemble the whole account before—"

She stopped. Both of them stopped. Because the oath-mark on Noor's wrist had begun, very faintly, for the first time in ten years, to burn.

Not the old burn—not the sting of a broken vow nearby. Something slower. Rhythmic. Like a pulse. Like counting.

Noor pressed their thumb over it and looked up, and found Sefa already watching their face, reading it the way she read witnesses.

"What?" she asked. "What is it?"

"It's counting," Noor whispered. "My god's mark. It's *counting*."

Above them, through the floors of the sleeping Guildhouse, faint and patient as rain, came a sound neither of them could name and both of them would remember: something, somewhere, ticking toward a number.
