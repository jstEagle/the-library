## 10. The Counting Begins

The first body came to Sefa's attention the way all the worst things did: as a footnote.

She was still working from the copied pages of her private file—THE CROWN REMEMBERS, transcribed in her own careful hand, hidden inside a treatise on grain tariffs where no inquisitor would ever think to look—when a runner brought word of a death in the Tanner's Ward. Ordinary business. Vell produced corpses the way the river produced silt, steadily and without much comment. She might have signed it off and moved on if the runner hadn't mentioned, almost apologetically, that the dead man had been found kneeling.

"People don't kneel when they die," Sefa said.

"This one did, m'lady. Face to the floor. Like he was mid-prayer."

Like he was mid-prayer. She had heard that phrase twice before this month, both times from priests who had stopped being able to finish their own sentences. She took the file, took her coat, and went out into the grey hour before dawn, because something in her had learned to distrust anything that happened after sunrise.

* * *

The dead man's name was Corvin Hale, and by every record the crown kept, he was nobody. A dyer's factor with a modest shop, a modest wife, two daughters. He had never been arrested, never petitioned the court, never appeared on any list Sefa had ever seen. His house was clean and warm and smelled of mordant and lye, and his widow sat at the kitchen table with her hands wrapped around an untouched cup of tea, staring at nothing.

"He was fine yesterday," she said, before Sefa could ask anything. "He was fine yesterday and he laughed at supper."

Sefa knelt beside the body where it lay in the back room, still in the position they'd described—kneeling, forehead nearly touching the boards, hands open at his sides like a man setting down something heavy. She had seen a hundred deaths in her years with the Inquisition. Knife wounds, poisons, falls, the quiet failures of overworked hearts. This was none of those. There was no wound, no discoloration, no smell of anything but soap and dye-stuff. Corvin Hale looked less like a man who had died than like a man who had been *emptied*, the way a jug looks after the last of the water is poured out.

She turned his hands over. On each palm, in lines so fine they might have been etched with a needle, she found the same mark: a circle of small strokes, like tally-marks arranged in a ring. She counted them without meaning to.

Fourteen.

"Did your husband ever—" She paused, choosing the words the way you choose footing on ice. "Did he ever make an arrangement? With the palace, I mean. A favor. A debt."

The widow's eyes came into focus for the first time. Something flickered there—not surprise. Recognition, and beneath it, shame.

"He got us through the famine year," she said quietly. "Everyone did what they had to. Everyone signed."

*Signed.* Sefa wrote the word down later, though she didn't yet know why it mattered more than the others.

* * *

By the third morning, there were four bodies, and by the fifth there were eleven, and Sefa stopped sleeping properly.

They came at dawn, always at dawn, always kneeling, always marked with the ring of fourteen tallies. They were bakers and clerks and one minor customs officer and a woman who ran a bathhouse in the Salt Quarter. What they shared was not trade or district or family. It took Sefa two days of cross-referencing—the old way, by lamplight, because she no longer trusted the chancery's clerks not to report what she was looking for—to find the thread.

Every one of them had, at some point in the last twenty years, received a writ of relief from the crown. Debt forgiveness. Pardons. Tax remissions. Grain during the famine. Surgeons' fees for a sick child. Small mercies, individually. But written into each grant, buried in the boilerplate clauses that no one read, was a line she had seen a thousand times and never once thought about:

*And the petitioner acknowledges a consideration owed, to be rendered at the crown's pleasure.*

At the crown's pleasure. Everyone assumed it meant taxes, or service, or nothing at all—a legal fiction, a formality, ink doing what ink does. Sefa had signed similar language herself on documents she'd executed for the Inquisition. She had probably *written* such clauses.

Now the crown's pleasure had arrived, and it was collecting.

She spread a map of Vell across her office floor, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, and began placing markers. Red wax for the dead. She worked through the night, and when the first grey light came through the shutters she stood over the map with cold fingers and felt the dread settle into her bones like groundwater—slow, silent, rising.

The deaths were not scattered. They were *arranged*.

The first body, Corvin Hale, had died in the Tanner's Ward, near the river gate. The second, three days later—or rather, the second she'd learned of; the sequence wasn't the order of discovery, she realized, it was the order of the marks—had died in Chandler's Row, one district inward. The third moved again, another step toward the palace hill. Each death was farther along the same spiral, each one closer to the center, and when she counted the intervals between them—not days, exactly, but something like days, the spacing tightening as the spiral closed—she understood what she was looking at, and had to put a hand against the wall.

It was a countdown.

Fourteen marks on fourteen palms. Fourteen positions on the map, spiraling inward toward the palace like a noose drawing shut. Eleven were already red wax. Three remained—three districts, three dawns—and then the spiral reached its terminus, and whatever the counting was *for*, it would be finished.

She didn't know what happened at zero. That was the worst of it. She had a shape of the thing and no substance, a deadline with no stated consequence, and every instinct she had honed in fifteen years of interrogation told her that the absence of a stated consequence meant only that the consequence was too large to name.

* * *

"You look terrible," said Under-Inquisitor Brannic, which from Brannic was a form of greeting, concern, and compliment all at once.

"I've been mapping murders," Sefa said.

"Murders require murderers." He dropped a stack of reports on her desk. "Chancery wants the dawn-deaths classified as plague. Contagious fever. They're posting quarantine notices in the affected wards tomorrow."

"There's no fever. You've read the physicians' findings. These people weren't sick. They were *harvested*."

Brannic shifted his weight. He was a good man in the way of most men in the Inquisition—decent within arm's reach, willfully blind beyond it—and she watched him do the arithmetic he always did: what was asked of him, what it cost to refuse, what it cost to comply. Fifteen years ago she would have done the same arithmetic without noticing she was doing it.

"The Chancery," he said carefully, "has expressed interest in closing this matter quickly."

"The Chancery has expressed interest in *burying* this matter quickly. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "They're not." And then, quieter, leaning in: "There's talk upstairs. About you. About the priest you interviewed—the mad one. Lord Ives asked after your notes personally. Not through channels. Personally."

Sefa kept her face still. Inside, something turned over. Ives asking after her notes personally was either protection or surveillance, and she had spent enough nights with THE CROWN REMEMBERS burning in her memory to know which way the odds fell.

"Thank you, Brannic."

"Don't thank me. Just—" He hesitated at the door. "Whatever you're not telling me, be careful with it. The walls here have gotten newer ears lately."

After he left she sat for a long time with her hands flat on the desk, listening to the building breathe around her. Then she pulled up a floorboard she'd loosened weeks ago and added the map—rolled tight, tied with cord—to the growing archive of things she could no longer say aloud.

* * *

She went to see the twelfth death happen. Not because she could stop it—she had tried, stationing watchers in the district the spiral predicted next, a weaver's lane off Cordwainer Street—but because she needed to *see*. There was a difference between knowing a thing and witnessing it, and she had learned in the priest's cell that some knowledge doesn't become real until it passes through the eyes.

The watcher woke her an hour before dawn. She arrived while the sky was still black, and she stood across the lane from the weaver's house, in the shadow of a cooper's awning, and waited.

The weaver's name, she had learned, was Anselm Todd. He had received a pardon eleven years ago for a crime his brother committed—a theft, small and desperate, the kind of mercy that ruins families as often as it saves them. He had signed the clause. Consideration owed, at the crown's pleasure.

As the sky went from black to iron to the color of a healing bruise, Sefa watched the windows of the weaver's house. Nothing moved. The street stayed empty. A cat crossed the lane with the proprietary boredom of cats everywhere.

Then the light came.

Not sunlight—it came from *inside* the house, a glow through the shutters, gold and wrong, the exact color she had seen in the king's eyes during that audience she could never stop replaying. It lasted perhaps ten heartbeats. From within, very faintly, she heard a sound she would spend the rest of her life trying not to hear again: not a scream, not a cry, but a long exhale, the sound of a breath leaving a body that would never draw another, drawn out and drawn out as if someone were winding thread off a spool.

When she broke down the door—for she was still, on paper, an inquisitor with authority to do such things—she found Anselm Todd kneeling, forehead to the floorboards, hands open at his sides. Fourteen tallies on each palm. The room was warm. The warmth had no source. On the table, his breakfast sat untouched, and beside it a half-finished letter to his brother, the last line reading: *I have been thinking lately about what we owe, and whether anyone truly keeps count.*

Someone keeps count, Sefa thought. Someone has always kept count. We just never asked who held the ledger.

She stood in the golden-warm room and felt the abstract thing she had been chasing since the priest's carved skin finally grow teeth. This was no longer a mystery. It was a schedule. Somewhere above her, in the palace on the hill, something was counting down to a moment it had planned for longer than any of the dead had been alive, and it was using their lives as the ticks of the clock.

Three marks left. Two dawns, maybe, if the pattern tightened the way spirals tighten.

* * *

She spent the evening in the archive of the Inquisition, pulling records under a pretext so thin she barely bothered to make it convincing. What she wanted was older than the famine grants, older than the current reign's paperwork. She wanted the original instruments—the founding charters, the war-era writs, anything bearing the clause about considerations owed. If the crown had been writing debts into its mercies for decades, then somewhere there was a master account. A ledger of everything collected and everything promised.

What she found instead, in a charter box sealed with the chancellor's own signet, was a single document dated thirty-one years past—the year the war ended, the year Aldric was crowned. It was a receipt. One line, in a hand she recognized from a hundred state documents as Lord Chancellor Ives's own:

*Received: one king's shadow. Paid in full.*

Paid in full. Except, she thought, staring at the yellowed page, it clearly hadn't been. Something had been deferred. Something had been accruing. And now the interest had come due, and it was being collected one kneeling corpse at a time, in a spiral closing on the palace like a fist.

She copied the line into her notebook, added it beneath THE CROWN REMEMBERS, and sat in the archive's dust-smelling dark feeling the deadline press against her chest like a hand.

Two dawns. Perhaps fewer. She had a map, a pattern, a dead chancellor's receipt, and no ally she could trust inside the law—because the law, she understood now with perfect clarity, was not merely failing to stop this. The law was the instrument of it. Every clause she had ever drafted, every writ she had ever executed, might have been a stitch in the same garment.

Which left the people outside the law. The smugglers, the erased, the guilds that traded in impossible things. She had raided their world often enough to know its shape. And there was one guild in particular—one ledger-keeper whose path she had already crossed once, over stolen wine and traded secrets, in a night that had ended with neither of them arresting the other, for reasons she had not let herself examine too closely.

Noor Halloran. Keeper of accounts at the Hollow Guild. The person who had shown her, without meaning to, that the ledger of the impossible ran both directions—that a debt written in ash could perhaps be read, traced, maybe even unwritten.

Sefa blew out her lamp, tucked the charter copy inside her coat, and let herself out into the night. Above the rooftops, the stars were coming out over a city that had two dawns left to live through, and she walked fast, toward the river, toward the Warrens, toward the one person in Vell who might know how to read a countdown written in other people's lives.

Behind her, in the dark of the archive, the seal on Chancellor Ives's box seemed, if one were fanciful, to gleam. Sefa did not look back. She had stopped believing in coincidence the same week she'd started believing in answered prayers, and she knew—with the certainty of a woman standing on a clock's final numbers—that whatever came at zero, she and Noor Halloran were going to be standing at the center of it.
