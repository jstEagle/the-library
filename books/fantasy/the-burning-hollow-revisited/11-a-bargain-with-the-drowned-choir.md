## 11. A Bargain with the Drowned Choir

The river-gate of Vell did not appear on any map that had been printed in the last hundred years, and the maps that were older than that had a way of burning when you looked at them too long. Noor knew this because they had burned three of them. The fourth—the one sewn into the lining of their coat, inked on skin scraped from a hanged man's back because paper forgot and flesh remembered—had survived every fire, every flood, every year of their god's silence.

It showed the Serpentine where it left the city walls: a stretch of black water running beneath an arch of drowned stone, older than the palace above it, older than the crown that sat inside the palace like a spider in a web made of years. The river-gate. The only way down to the Burning Hollow that didn't pass through forty feet of royal guard and Lord Chancellor Ives's smiling teeth.

Nine days. That was what the deaths had bought them—a number, chalked on the inside of Noor's eyelids so they saw it every time they blinked. Nine days until the counting finished, until whatever wore Aldric's crown harvested enough lives to finish whatever it was counting toward. Fourteen days had become nine in five nights of dawn-killings, each body found cold and dry despite the river mist, each mouth open around a word no witness could repeat.

Noor stood at the top of the mud-slick stairs that led to the water and tried to make peace with what they were about to do.

"You're stalling," Sefa said behind them.

"I'm *preparing*."

"Preparing." Sefa came down the last steps, her boots finding the stones with the precise economy of a woman who had spent fifteen years walking into rooms where people wanted her dead. She'd traded her inquisitor's grey for smuggler's brown, and it suited her badly; she wore illegality the way she wore everything, like a uniform she resented having to put on. "You've been preparing for twenty minutes. I've watched you interrogate a Guildmaster with more warmth than you're showing these stairs."

"The stairs deserve it." Noor crouched and touched the water. It was colder than it should have been in high summer, cold the way a grave is cold—not absent of heat but *full* of something else. "You know what's down there."

"I know what the old records say. The Drowned Choir. Petitioners who prayed to the old gods for rescue and were answered with drowning. They guard the river-gate because—" she paused, choosing precision over comfort, as she always did, "—because the gods asked them to. Before the binding. Before everything went quiet."

"And now the gods are waking up." Noor straightened. Their oath-mark, the pale scar coiled at the base of their throat like a sleeping serpent, ached with a low constant burn that had not stopped since the chapel. Since the prayer that had been answered with warmth instead of silence. "Waking up angry. Waking up *hungry*. And the Choir is between us and the door."

"So we go around."

"There is no around. There's through the palace, which means Ives, which means the hunters, which means we die in a corridor with our names spelled wrong on our death warrants. Or there's the river-gate." Noor looked at her. In the grey pre-dawn light, with the black water breathing below them, Sefa's face was all angles and stubbornness, and Noor felt the familiar treacherous warmth in their chest that had nothing to do with gods or oaths and everything to do with the fact that she was here. That after the ash market, after the burned chapel, after Noor had said *I think I love you* into the smoke while hunters beat at the doors—she had stayed. "Or there's the river-gate," they said again, softer.

Sefa heard the softness. She always did. It was the most dangerous thing about her, that hearing. "What do they take?" she asked quietly. "The Choir."

"A voice." Noor touched their own throat, below the oath-mark. "That's the toll. Every petitioner who ever came here begging the gods for something gave up their voice to get an audience. The Choir collects. It's how they sing—they're made of everyone's surrendered words."

"And if you pay it?"

"Then I spend the rest of my life writing notes on little cards like a consumptive duke." Noor smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere near their eyes. "But I'm not going to pay it."

"Noor."

"I'm going to offer it something else."

The look Sefa gave them could have stripped paint. "The last time you gambled 'everything' on faith, you bet a god's name against a river full of dead people. What could possibly be left?"

"That's just it." Noor pulled the coat open, drew out the skin-map, unrolled it across a flat stone. The ink-lines glowed faintly in the dark, the way things did lately—all over Vell, old marks were waking, shrine-stones warming, carved blessings sweating light. The world remembering itself. "There isn't anything left. That's the point. Everything I have—the ledger, the guild, the protection, the oath—I've already spent or burned. All I've got is what I know." They tapped the map, at the place where the arch met the water, where the ink had smudged into something almost like letters. "And what I know is a name."

"Vessarine."

"My god's name. The name it left me in the shrine before it went silent. The name the thing in the crown has spent ten years trying to erase, because a bound god can be forgotten but a *named* god—" Noor's hand closed slowly into a fist. "A named god can be called. Can be found. Can be *freed*. The crown doesn't want anyone saying that word. And I'm going to walk down to the oldest gate in Vell and say it out loud to a chorus of the drowned, and pray to every silent power left in this city that they remember whose name it is, and what they owe it."

The water lapped. Somewhere far upstream, a bell rang the hour, and the sound came across the surface strangely, arriving late and warped, as though the river were chewing it.

"If you're wrong," Sefa said, "you lose your voice, and I lose—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Sefa did not do sentiment in daylight; she did it in ledgers, in lists, in the careful inventory of things she could not afford. "I lose my guide to the Hollow. That's all."

"That's all," Noor agreed gravely. "Your guide."

"Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You said it with your whole face."

Noor laughed—short, surprised out of them—and the sound skittered across the black water and came back changed. Lower. Multiplied. As if something below had caught it and passed it along a long line of throats.

Both of them went still.

"It knows we're here," Noor said.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the stairs should have gone. Forty steps by Noor's count became sixty, then ninety, the air thickening with river-smell and older smells beneath it—incense, rot, candlewax, the mineral breath of deep stone. The walls of the stairwell were carved, though the carvings had been defaced centuries ago, faces chiseled smooth, leaving only gestures: hands raised in petition, hands clasped in gratitude, hands empty. Hundreds of hands. Thousands. The first gods had been worshipped here once, before the Binding, before the crown, before the long quiet.

At the bottom, the stair opened onto a shelf of wet rock above the river, and the river-gate rose out of the water like the ribcage of something that had died praying.

It was an arch, but wrong—an arch built inward, its keystone sunk below the waterline, so that the gate existed mostly underwater, a doorway into the dark beneath the current. And in that dark, in the arch's drowned hollows, they hung.

The Drowned Choir.

Dozens of them. Petitioners in the dress of a dozen generations—farmers' wool, merchants' silk, soldiers' mail gone to lacework rust, a bride's rotted veil drifting like a jellyfish. They did not decay. They did not drift. They hung upright in the flooded arch, hair moving in the slow current, eyes open and white as riverstone, and they sang.

Not aloud. That was the horror of it, the thing no record had managed to convey: the Choir sang *inside*, a harmony that bypassed the ear entirely and resonated in the bones, in the teeth, in the scar at Noor's throat. Words without language. Grief without end. Layer upon layer of voice, each one a person who had come to this water begging for a miracle and been given one, in the way the old gods understood miracles—which is to say literally, and forever.

Noor knelt at the water's edge. Beside them, Sefa did the same, and Noor noticed—with some distant, absurd part of their mind—that even kneeling, even facing a chorus of the dead, Sefa kept her spine straight. Some people were simply built at attention.

"We ask passage," Noor said clearly, to the water, to the arch, to the white-eyed congregation within. "Through the river-gate, down to the Hollow. We'll pay the toll."

The singing shifted. Not louder—*closer*, as if the harmony had leaned in. The nearest petitioner, a man in a ferryman's coat, turned his head with the slow grace of a weed bending, and his white eyes fixed on Noor, and his mouth opened, and the voice that came out was his own and also everyone's, layered, ancient, patient:

**A VOICE.**

The word arrived in the marrow. Noor's jaw ached with it.

"Yes. That's the toll. But I'm offering something else instead." Noor's heart was slamming against their ribs, and they let it, let the fear be real, let it sharpen rather than scatter them. This was the Guild's oldest lesson, the one written on the first page of every ledger: *a bargain made from fear is a bargain half-lost.* "I offer a name."

The Choir went silent.

All of it, at once, everywhere—the bone-harmony cut off like a severed string, and the sudden absence rang worse than the song. The water stopped moving. The mist stopped drifting. Even the river-smell seemed to hold itself.

**NAMES ARE NOT OURS TO TAKE,** the Choir said, and now the voices were distinct, dozens of them speaking in ragged unison, ferryman and bride and soldier, **NAMES BELONG TO THE NAMED. WE CANNOT SPEND WHAT IS NOT GIVEN.**

"It *is* given." Noor stood. Their legs shook and they let them; honesty was currency here, and they would not counterfeit a single coin. "That's the whole of my offer. I give freely what the crown has spent ten years trying to bury. Listen."

They breathed in. Below the oath-mark, the scar burned like a coal.

"Vessarine."

The name left their mouth and the world *moved*.

Not visibly—the water lay flat, the dead hung still—but something vast turned over beneath the river, the way a whale turns under a boat, displacing everything. The carvings on the stairwell wall began to sweat light, the defaced faces glowing through the chisel-marks that had erased them, hundreds of smooth blanks flickering back into suggestion: eyes, mouths, crowns of thorn and wheat. The Choir's white eyes flared gold, every one of them, and the bride's veil lifted in a current that no longer obeyed the river.

**THAT NAME,** said the ferryman, alone now, his voice thin and wondering, **WE KNEW THAT VOICE. IT CAME HERE BEFORE THE BINDING. IT STOOD WHERE YOU STAND AND IT PAID OUR TOLL AND IT ASKED US TO REMEMBER.**

Noor's knees nearly went. "Remember what?"

**IT SAID:** the bride spoke now, her voice young and cracked with riverwater, **ONE DAY SOMEONE WILL COME DOWN THESE STAIRS SPEAKING MY NAME. LET THEM PASS. CHARGE THEM NOTHING. FOR THEY WILL BE CARRYING MY DEBT, AND MY DEBT IS HEAVY ENOUGH.**

The singing resumed—but transformed. What had been grief was now something else, something that made Noor's chest ache in a different register entirely: recognition. Welcome. The Choir was singing *to* them, dozens of drowned throats harmonizing around a name ten years buried, and the sound poured through the arch and down into the dark below, a herald, an announcement, a door being unlocked from both ends at once.

The water in the archway parted.

Not dramatically—it simply withdrew, folding back from the gate's interior like a curtain drawn by a careful hand, leaving a corridor of damp air descending into stone, lit by veins of the same slow fire Noor had seen in the decade-old maps: orange-gold, pulsing gently, like embers that had learned patience.

Sefa exhaled beside them, long and controlled, the sound of a woman recalculating every assumption she owned. "It paid your toll," she said. "Before you were born. It *knew*."

"It hoped." Noor's voice came out rough. They were crying, they realized distantly, and made no effort to stop; the drowned deserved honest tears. "Ten years of silence, and the whole time it was down there in the dark, paying debts forward. Betting on me. On *someone*." They wiped their face with the heel of their hand and laughed, wet and disbelieving. "I came here ready to gamble everything on faith, and faith had already paid the fare."

"Then let's not keep it waiting." Sefa rose, offered her hand—offered it, Noor noted, when a week ago she would simply have started walking—and together they stepped into the parted water, into the arch, toward the fire-lit dark.

Behind them, the Choir sang them down. And as the stairs swallowed the last grey light of the surface, the harmony changed one final time—softened, focused, resolved into a single clear line that Noor felt rather than heard, rising up through the stone from somewhere very far below:

A voice. Thin. Frayed. Half-consumed and whispering counter-prayers against the dark.

But unmistakably, impossibly, *listening*.

Noor's scar blazed like a brand, and the voice—Vessarine's voice, their god's voice, silent ten years and now murmuring up through the veins of fire like blood returning to a numbed limb—spoke its first word to them in a decade.

"*Hurry,*" it whispered. "*It has begun to count the last of them. And child—one of the counters is coming down to confess.*"

Above them, far away in the palace, a door closed on a conversation between Lord Chancellor Ives and the inquisitor he thought he still owned.

Below them, the fire-veins pulsed faster, like a heart remembering its rhythm.

Noor took Sefa's hand and ran into the Burning Hollow.
