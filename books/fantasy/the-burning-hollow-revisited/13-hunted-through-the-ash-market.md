## 13. Hunted Through the Ash Market

The knock came at the Undercroft door an hour before dawn, and Noor knew before Sefa opened it that the world had changed shape overnight.

It was Tam the ledger-clerk who brought the news, breathless and gray-faced, still in his nightshirt under a borrowed coat. "They took the Guildmaster," he said. "Crown guards. Before first bell. And Ives has posted writs — your faces are on every gate in Vell. Not arrest writs." He swallowed. "Collection writs."

Sefa took the paper from him and read it twice. Noor watched her jaw tighten around the word *collection*, because they both knew what it meant. Not prisoners. Assets.

"Nine days," Noor said quietly.

"Eight," Sefa corrected. "Dawn's coming."

* * *

The ash market woke with the city, which was to say it never truly slept. It filled the long trough between the tannery district and the river wall where nothing else would grow, a sprawl of canvas stalls and braziers burning the city's leavings — bone-meal, char, the fine gray silt that settled over everything like patient snow. Merchants sold fire-salvaged goods here, secondhand griefs, wedding rings pried from melted hands. The air tasted of cinders and old smoke, and by midmorning the light through the ash-haze turned the color of weak tea.

Noor moved through it with their hood up and the ledger strapped against their ribs beneath a patched coat, and told themselves the prickling between their shoulder blades was only the cold.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said beside them, not looking over. She wore a fishmonger's apron over her armor, which fooled no one but was better than nothing. "Where you go quiet and start cataloguing exits."

"I'm always cataloguing exits."

"Yes, but usually you pretend otherwise." Her eyes swept the crowd in the same rhythm as Noor's — she'd stopped hiding it days ago. "Stall on the left is selling prayer-candles. Real ones. Look how many people are buying."

Noor looked. A queue of ordinary people — laundresses, carters, a child clutching a copper — waited to buy candles they would light for gods who, ten days ago, had been stone and silence. One woman held her candle up to the brazier and whispered something into the flame, and the flame bent toward her mouth like a listening ear.

"They're not even praying for anything big," Noor said. "Look at them. Fevers. Rain for crops. Forgiveness."

"People don't ask gods for much when they've learned not to expect answers."

Noor thought of ten years of silence. Of prayers spoken into a well with no bottom. They were still deciding whether to say so when the crowd changed.

It was subtle at first — a current running against the flow of bodies, a knot of men in matched gray cloaks moving without browsing, without haggling, without any of the small dishonesties of market life. Then Noor saw the glint beneath one cloak: crown livery, dulled with soot to pass unnoticed, and the pale scar of a binding-sigil inked on the man's wrist.

"Hunters," Sefa breathed. "Three o'clock. Two more behind the candle stall. They're sweeping toward us."

"How—"

"The Guildmaster." Sefa's voice went flat and hard. "She gave us up. Or Ives found another way to read your oath-mark. Doesn't matter now. Walk. Don't run. Runners get chased."

They walked. The market pressed close around them — barrels of rendered fat, cages of ash-pigeons, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking against steel — and Noor counted hunters the way they counted debts. Four visible. Probably six. Moving in a slow converging arc, patient as interest accruing.

"There," Sefa said. "The tallow-row. If we cut through the rendering sheds we come out at the river stairs."

"The river stairs are watched. Everything's watched."

"Then we improvise." She said it lightly, but her hand had drifted to the short blade at her hip, and Noor saw the knuckles whiten around the strap of her basket.

The first hunter made his move at the mouth of tallow-row, stepping out of the crowd with the unhurried certainty of a man collecting a debt he knew was owed. "Halloran," he said. "Inquisitor. Lord Chancellor requests the pleasure—" and then Sefa's elbow found his throat and the sentence ended forever.

The market detonated.

Not all at once — in pieces, in screams and scattering pigeons and stalls going over like felled trees. Noor ran, and the ash rose around their boots in choking clouds, and behind them Sefa fought the way she did everything: precisely, viciously, with no wasted motion. Noor heard steel ring twice, heard a man scream, heard Sefa shout "*Go, the sheds, I'm behind you*" — and believed her, because believing her had become as natural as breathing somewhere in the last nine days without either of them naming it.

The rendering sheds were long low buildings reeking of boiled fat, hung with strips of tallow like pale flags. Noor wove between the vats, vaulted a cooling-rack, burst out the far side into the open lane—

—and stopped, because two more hunters stood there, and these had not dulled their livery at all. Crown gold burned bright on their breasts, and their eyes had gone flat and golden, and Noor understood with a lurch that these were not men sent by Ives. These were worn by something older.

"*Oath-bearer,*" the left one said, in a voice like coins dropped down a well. "*The counting requires you.*"

Noor did the arithmetic of the lane — two hunters, one exit, no Sefa — and chose the vat.

They went over the tallow-vat's rim and into its shallow dregs, boots skidding on cooled fat, and rolled off the far side as the hunters came on with terrible unhurried grace. Something caught Noor's sleeve and tore it; something else scored a line of fire across their shoulder. Then a shadow dropped from the shed roof — Sefa, landing on the nearer hunter's back with both knees, driving him face-first into the ash — and for one bright moment Noor thought they might actually win free.

Then the second hunter's blade found her.

It should have found Noor. That was the geometry of it, plain as a ledger line: the strike meant for Noor's spine, and Sefa turning into it instead, taking the point below her collarbone with a sound like a door closing. She made no cry. She simply folded, and Noor caught her, and the world narrowed to blood spreading black through the fishmonger's apron and the golden-eyed thing raising its sword for a second stroke.

What happened next, Noor would spend the rest of their life failing to describe properly.

The ash market's great central brazier — twenty feet of iron bowl burning the city's sweepings — flared. Not brighter. *Truer*. The fire stood up out of the coals like a summoned witness, and every candle in every stall along the row leaned toward Noor at once, flames bending like grass in wind, and the hunter with the sword hesitated — actually hesitated, golden eyes flickering — and in that hesitation Noor dragged Sefa through the gap between two tallow-sheds and into the labyrinth beyond.

Behind them, the brazier-fire roared once, like a voice clearing its throat, and went out.

* * *

The chapel had burned years ago — Noor remembered the fire, one of the dozens of small holy wars the city fought with itself whenever the old gods' silence chafed too hard. Its shell still stood at the edge of the ash market's worst quarter: walls of scorched brick, a roof open to the gray sky, pews reduced to charcoal stumps. Nobody came here. Even the ash seemed to fall more gently inside it, settling on the altar-stone like a shroud laid with care.

Noor laid Sefa down on that altar-stone because there was nowhere else, and because some animal part of them insisted that holy ground, even dead holy ground, was the right place for this.

"Don't," Sefa said, when Noor peeled back the apron. "Don't make the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing sums and the answer is bad." Her skin had gone the gray of the ash around them. The wound was deep but had missed, by fingers' widths, the things that kill quickly. Fingers' widths. Noor had seen fingers' widths fail before. "Press there. Hard. I'll be — I'm fine. I've had worse."

"Liar."

"Inquisitor," she corrected, and almost smiled, and then coughed, and the smile cost her visibly.

Noor pressed the wound and felt her pulse fluttering under their palm like a bird trapped indoors, and the fear that rose in them was of a wholly new species. Ten years a mercenary, a lifetime of ledgers full of other people's deaths, and Noor Halloran had never once been afraid like this — afraid not of losing but of *the specific loss*, this person, this infuriating, precise, brave woman whose blood was warm between their fingers.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"I need to tell you something and you have to stay awake for it."

"That's a terrible opening line."

"It's true anyway." Noor's voice cracked on the second word. They kept pressing. Around them the burned chapel sat in its silence, and the ash fell, and somewhere outside the hunters were searching street by street. "I spent ten years talking to a god who didn't answer, and I told myself that was devotion. It wasn't. It was habit wearing devotion's clothes. And then you broke into my guild to steal a ledger, and drank my wine, and argued with me about jurisprudence until third bell, and I — I haven't prayed since. Because I was afraid I'd finally be asking for something I couldn't survive losing."

Sefa's eyes found theirs. Unfocused, but finding them. "That," she said faintly, "is the worst confession of love I have ever heard."

"It's a work in progress."

"Good. Because if I die before you finish it, I'm haunting you."

"No you're not. You'd file a formal complaint with whatever passes for a provisional council down there."

Her laugh turned into a grimace, turned into silence, and her hand — the one pressed weakly over Noor's, holding it against the wound — loosened.

And Noor, who had not prayed in nine days, who had sworn off asking, knelt on the scorched floor of a murdered chapel with their hands full of blood and did the only thing left.

Aloud. Voice raw. No ritual, no liturgy, none of the careful forms the Guild taught for speaking to powers. Just:

"Vessarine. I know you can hear me. I know you've been hearing me for ten years, and I know what it cost you to stay silent, and I am *asking* now — not for me. For her. She took a blade that was mine. She's the best thing I've found in a life full of stolen things, and I will trade anything — the ledger, the guild, the oath, all of it, everything I have ever been owed or have owed — just let her keep breathing. Please. *Please.*"

The silence after was enormous.

Then the chapel answered.

It began in the charcoal stumps of the pews — a warmth rising out of them, a red glow kindling in wood eleven years dead, ember-light crawling up the burnt grain like veins filling with blood. The ash on the altar-stone began to shimmer, each gray fleck catching a light that had no source, and the broken bell in the tower overhead — cracked, mute, a decade silent — hummed one low, sweet, impossible note.

Warmth washed over Noor's hands. Over Sefa's wound. It did not heal her — Noor felt that much clearly, felt the god straining against its own chains, pouring what little it could spare through a crack in the world no wider than a prayer. But the bleeding slowed. The gray receded from her lips. Her fingers tightened over Noor's.

"Oh," Sefa breathed. Her eyes were wide now, wide open, fixed on the glowing pews, the singing bell, the ash turning to drifting sparks above them like inverted snow. "Oh, *that's* what it feels like."

"He's half-consumed," Noor whispered. Tears they didn't remember starting cut tracks through the ash on their face. "He's chained in the dark under the palace and he's giving us *this*."

"Then we owe him," Sefa said. She pushed herself up on one elbow, wincing, fierce, alive. "We owe him the way nobody in this city has owed anything in ten years. Nine days, Noor. Eight now. We finish the confession later — you're not done, and I want the whole terrible thing — but first we get down into that Hollow, and we break him loose."

Above them, the cracked bell rang again — soft, certain, a single struck note rolling out over the ash market like a promise.

And in the doorway of the burned chapel, silhouetted against the falling ash, stood a figure in a gray cloak with its hood drawn back, and Noor knew the face: Tam the clerk, breathless, terrified, a sealed letter clutched in his fist.

"She sent me," Tam said. "The Guildmaster. She said — she said tell them she's sorry, and that the original contract is real, and Ives knows where you are."
