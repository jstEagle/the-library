## 14. Nine Days

The Undercroft smelled of tallow and wet stone, of old incense burned down to its bitter resin, of the particular staleness that gathered in places where people lived without ever being certain they'd be allowed to keep living. Noor had been there four days now, long enough to stop flinching at the sounds—the drip of water through the vaulted ceiling, the coughing from the families two cells over, the endless soft shuffle of the legally dead going about their unlived lives.

They sat cross-legged on the floor of their borrowed cell with the shadow-ledger open across their knees, and for the ninth time since dawn, they read the same entry.

*Paid to the Crown of Vell: one binding, willing-borne, sealed in oath's blood. Redeemable only by an oath freely broken by the hand that swore it.*

The words hadn't changed. Noor had checked them against every trick they knew—held the page to candlelight looking for forgery, breathed on the ink to see if it was blood-scribed and would bloom, traced the letters with a fingernail to feel for the ghost of a second text beneath. Nothing. The entry was exactly what it appeared to be, which was worse than any forgery would have been.

*Redeemable only by an oath freely broken.*

Noor closed the ledger and pressed the heels of both hands against their eyes until the darkness behind them bloomed orange.

"You've read it eleven times," Sefa said from the doorway. She had a talent for appearing without announcing herself—a habit of inquisitors, Noor supposed, though she'd lost the office and kept the habits, like a soldier who still slept on the wall-side of the bed years after leaving the barracks. "I counted."

"Eleven times isn't enough." Noor lowered their hands. "Twelve might do it."

"And what will the twelfth reading tell you that the eleventh didn't?"

"That I'm wrong. That I've misread it. That *willing-borne* means something else—something merciful." Noor looked up at her. In the tallow light her face was all angles and exhaustion, the bruise beneath her eye gone green-yellow at the edges where the hunter's blow had landed three days ago. She'd refused the poultice twice and accepted it on the third offer, which Noor had learned was simply how Sefa did everything: she resisted help as a matter of principle and then permitted it as a matter of judgment, and never confused the two. "It means what it says, doesn't it?"

Sefa came into the cell and lowered herself onto the stool, the only furniture either of them had been granted. "You know ledgers better than I do. Does it mean anything else?"

"No." Noor turned back to the page. The paper was old—older than the Guild, older than the war, older than everything except perhaps the Hollow itself—and it had the texture of skin that had been washed too many times. "A binding is a knot. Everyone knows that. And everyone knows knots can't be untied from the outside. You can cut them, but cutting a binding kills what it holds—or releases it wrong, twisted, half-strangled. The old texts are very clear about that. Half-freed gods don't forgive. They *spill*."

"So the crown's binding can't be cut."

"It can't be cut safely. It can't be picked from outside. It can't be dissolved, because it was made with consent—" Noor's voice caught on the word, and they hated that it caught. "That's the whole architecture of it. Whoever bound Aldric—bound the thing wearing Aldric—built the lock so that it could only ever be opened from within. By someone who carried an oath. A real one. Sworn freely, held faithfully, for years. An oath with weight enough to counterweight the binding itself."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was keeping herself level. "And how many people in Vell carry oaths like that?"

"Fewer every year." Noor laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as a dropped cup. "Oath-sworn are rare. Oath-sworn who've *kept* it a decade—that's rarer still. Most gods release their sworn when they die. Most sworn break first. But if you wanted to find the ones who hadn't..." They looked down at their own forearm, where the oath-mark lay beneath the sleeve: a thin line of silvered script that had once moved gently, like water, and had been still for ten years. "If you were something that needed a key, and keys were scarce, you'd start collecting them."

"The harvest." Sefa said it the way she said the names of the dead, each morning, before she began mapping the deaths again. "The people dying at dawn. The bargainers. We thought it was interest collection. Payment on old debts."

"We thought it was greed." Noor shook their head slowly. "It's not. Or—it's not only greed. Look at the pattern again. Who's died? Tell me."

Sefa didn't need to consult her papers; she had them memorized, because of course she did. "Master Corvin, dyer, bargained his wife's illness away fourteen years ago. Old Maren, who sold her sister's name during the famine. The twins at the Ferrygate shrine, who swore service to a river-god that drowned. The lamplighter who traded ten years of his life for his daughter's apprenticeship. The widow Ashen—" She stopped.

"The widow Ashen," Noor said quietly. "Who bought grief from my Guild. Who bought it *willingly*, signed for it, carried it honestly. Every one of them, Sefa. Every single one of them held something—an oath, a bargain, a debt they'd agreed to and honored. Not defaulted debts. *Kept* ones. The crown isn't collecting. It's *testing*. It's reaching into every sworn soul in this city and trying the lock, one by one, to see which key fits."

The tallow flame guttered in a draft from the corridor. Somewhere below them, in the deeper levels of the Undercroft, a child was singing a skipping song about the ash market, off-key and unbothered, and the ordinary sound of it made the horror sit differently in the room—heavier, more specific.

"Fourteen days," Sefa said. "Your count said fourteen when we decoded the death-map. That was five days ago."

"Nine days left." Noor's mouth was dry. "And we're no closer. Because here's the part I keep circling and can't land on." They turned the ledger around so she could see the entry again, and tapped the line beneath the binding-terms, the one written in a different hand, smaller, almost apologetic. "*The bearer shall be sought until found. All other doors shall close.*"

"All other doors shall close," Sefa repeated. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning it's not just searching. It's *narrowing*. Every person it tests and finds wanting—every key that doesn't fit—it removes from consideration. Permanently. And each time it fails, the search gets more desperate, the harvest gets larger, the countdown moves faster. It's a sieve, Sefa. The whole city is a sieve, and the grain it wants is—" 

They stopped. They couldn't say it. Saying it made it true, and it had been true for days already, sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone.

Sefa said it for them, because she was braver than Noor in the ways that mattered, or perhaps just more practiced at saying terrible things aloud. "You. It wants you. Ten years sworn to a god, never broken, never released. You're the last key in Vell that fits the lock."

* * *

They argued about it after that, though argument was too orderly a word for what it became. Noor wanted to run—to leave Vell entirely, walk north past the salt marshes, put a hundred miles between themselves and the crown before the nine days ran out. If the key left the city, surely the lock would starve.

But Sefa had laid the death-maps out on the floor, all of them, the little wax-stamped markers showing where each harvested soul had fallen, and she knelt over them with her hair falling loose and her voice low and certain.

"It's not confined to the city. Look—Corvin died at the ferry crossing, forty miles downstream. Maren's sister was found in the hill country. The reach is longer than we thought. Running doesn't take you out of the sieve; running just means you die tired, alone, and unmourned." She looked up. "And there's this."

She held out a scrap of paper—one of the crown proclamations that had begun appearing on doors and shrine-posts across Vell, printed in that bland official typeface that made atrocity look like bookkeeping. Noor took it and read it, and felt the floor tilt beneath them even though they were sitting down.

*By order of the Crown: all persons bearing divine marks, oath-script, or covenant-signs shall present themselves for registry within six days. Unregistered bearers will be assumed hostile to the realm.*

"A census," Noor said.

"A net." Sefa's jaw was tight. "Every oath-sworn survivor in Vell, ordered to walk into the palace courtyard and write their name on a list. And whoever runs that list will find yours on it eventually, Noor, because the Guild's records weren't the only records. I filed reports on covenant-holders for six years. My own handwriting will lead them to you if nothing else does."

Noor stared at the proclamation until the letters swam. Six days. Nine days. The numbers braided together into a noose. "So we can't run, and we can't hide, and the ledger says the binding opens only from inside, by an oath freely broken—which means the answer has been sitting in my arm for ten years, and I didn't know I was carrying it."

"Would it have changed anything? If you'd known?"

"I don't know." Noor set the proclamation down carefully, as if it might go off. "Maybe. Maybe I'd have broken the oath years ago, out of spite, just to be useless to it. Just to be *safe*." They heard their own voice and despised the smallness in it. "Ten years I mourned that god. Ten years I kept faith with silence because I thought faith was all I had left to give. And the whole time, the keeping itself was the prize. My loyalty wasn't devotion, Sefa. It was ammunition. Someone built a weapon out of my faithfulness and left it lying in my arm for a decade, waiting for the war that needed it."

Sefa crossed the small cell and sat beside them on the floor, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd taken to sitting when the nights got long—not touching, but near enough that the nearness was its own kind of speech. Outside, the Undercroft went on with its muffled living. The child had stopped singing. Water dripped somewhere, patient as a metronome.

"There's another way to read it," Sefa said at last.

"Don't. Don't give me hope shaped like a misreading. I've audited enough books to know what wishful arithmetic looks like."

"I'm not doing arithmetic. I'm doing law." She turned to face them, and in the tallow light her eyes were fierce and red-rimmed and absolutely awake. "The contract says *an oath freely broken by the hand that swore it*. Freely, Noor. That word is load-bearing. A coerced breaking isn't a breaking—it's a seizure. If the crown drags you to the Hollow and forces your renunciation, the lock doesn't open. It can't. The terms forbid it. Which means all its hunting, all its harvesting, all this machinery of terror—it can't actually use you unless you *choose* it."

Noor went very still. "Then why hunt at all?"

"To corner you. To make your choices so narrow that whatever you pick, you pick under its thumb. Fear breaks oaths too, if you bend a soul far enough. It doesn't need to force your hand. It needs only to make you want to break it yourself—make the breaking feel like mercy, like escape, like the only door left open." Sefa's voice dropped. "It's not building a cage around you, Noor. It's building a *reason*."

The candle burned lower. Noor looked at their forearm again, at the sleeve covering the silver script that had been silent for ten years, and understood—with the particular clarity that arrives only when understanding is unbearable—that the trap had never been the hunters in the ash market, or the proclamations, or the countdown ticking toward zero. The trap was the shape of their own heart. Ten years of grief had carved a hollow in them exactly the size of a god's voice, and anything that promised to fill it—

Anything that promised to fill it could ask any price.

"If I break the oath," Noor said slowly, "freely, truly, at the right place and the right time—I unbind myself. And the binding with me. Your god goes free. Aldric dies human. The harvest stops." Sefa nodded. "And I lose the last thing my god ever gave me. Whatever protection remains in that dead mark. Whatever connection. I stand in front of a freed god with empty hands and no claim on its mercy, having just done the one thing I swore I never would."

"Yes."

"And if I don't—if I hold the oath, keep faith, stay loyal to a silence—then in nine days the counting finishes, and it takes the city apart looking for me, and everyone I've ever failed dies at dawn with my name in their mouths."

"Yes," Sefa said again, softer.

Noor laughed—really laughed this time, a cracked and disbelieving sound that startled them both. "Do you know what the Guildmaster would say? He'd say this is the finest contract he's ever seen. Perfectly balanced. No exit that doesn't cost everything. He'd frame it." They pressed their palm flat against the ledger's cover, against the impossible accounting of their own life. "Lock and key. I spent ten years thinking I was the victim of my god's silence. Turns out I'm the mechanism."

Outside their cell, footsteps in the corridor—one of the Undercroft's wardens making rounds, lantern-light sweeping briefly across the doorway and moving on. When it passed, the dark seemed darker for having been interrupted.

Sefa reached over and, for the first time since the burned chapel, took Noor's hand. Her grip was dry and strong and slightly cold, and Noor held on like a drowning man holds a rope, without dignity and without apology.

"Nine days," she said. "Then we stop decoding and start deciding. But tonight—" her thumb moved once across Noor's knuckles, deliberate, "—tonight you sleep. The key doesn't get to arrive at the lock exhausted."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. Then, from the corridor beyond, came the warden's voice, low and urgent, calling for them both—and beneath the words, rising up through the Undercroft's stone throat from the world above, a sound neither of them had heard before and would never afterward forget:

Bells. Every bell in Vell, ringing at midnight, ragged and fast and wrong.

The counting had not waited for morning.
