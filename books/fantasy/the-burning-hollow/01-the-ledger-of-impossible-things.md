## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The autumn market smelled of woodsmoke and bruised apples, and Ansel Lockridge moved through it the way smoke moves through a house—present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

They had perfected the art of it over eleven years: the shoulders slightly stooped, the gaze fixed on middle distance as though perpetually searching for someone just ahead, the face arranged into an expression so unremarkable that eyes slid off it like rain off oiled canvas. A woman selling honeycakes looked directly at Ansel and did not see them. Two guardsmen arguing over dice glanced their way and dismissed them in the same heartbeat. It was not magic, or not only magic. Mostly it was practice—the long, patient discipline of being no one, which Ansel had come to believe was the most demanding craft in the world.

Beneath their coat, against their ribs, the ledger rode warm.

It was a slim book bound in grey calfskin, unremarkable to any eye but its keeper's, and it held the guild's week: every trade made beneath Hollow Bridge since the last moon-turn. Tonight Ansel would record three more entries, close the book, seal it with wax pressed by a thumbprint that belonged to no living person, and carry it down into the vault where the guild kept everything its clients had paid away. Then they would climb back up, walk home through streets that never learned their name, and take from themselves the small tax that kept a roof over their head.

Ansel had stopped finding this arrangement strange years ago. That was the first thing forgetting taught you: how quickly the strange becomes furniture.

* * *

The Hollow Guild kept its offices above a dyer's shop on Candlewick Lane, in rooms that always smelled faintly of mordant and old iron. The trade floor was open tonight, lamplit and busy, because market nights brought the desperate out like moths. Ansel slipped past the receiving desk with a nod to Pell, who nodded back without recognition—he never quite remembered Ansel's face, which was exactly as designed—and took their seat at the corner table where the light was worst and the sightlines were best.

From there they could see the whole room, and the whole room could see nothing worth seeing.

At the far bench sat a young man in a wool merchant's good coat, hands folded around a stoppered bottle the size of a thumb. Inside it, something glowed—not with light exactly, but with the memory of light, the way a shell holds the sound of the sea. The broker across from him, a soft-spoken woman named Odile whose hair was going silver at the temples, spoke to him in the low, unhurried cadence the guild used for the grieving.

"You understand what you're surrendering," she was saying. "Not the girl. Not the love. Only the first moment of it. Everything after remains yours."

"I know." His voice cracked on the second word. "I know. But her father's calling in the debt, and if I don't pay by month's end he takes the shop, and the shop is—" He laughed, wet and helpless. "The shop is how I feed her brothers. She'd want me to keep the shop. She told me once she'd rather be forgotten than hungry."

"Then we'll be gentle with it." Odile touched the bottle. "First kisses are sturdy things. They keep well."

Ansel dipped their pen and wrote, in the small precise hand that had won them the ledger-keeper's post before they turned twenty:

*Bottled: one (1) first kiss. Seller: J. Marrec, chandler's son, age 19. Price: forty crowns, debt discharged. Condition at intake: radiant; salt-sweet; smells of rain on a summer street. Buyer: sealed lot, private collection.*

The words went down clean and cold, and Ansel felt the familiar small shame of it, the way you feel a splinter you've decided not to dig out. Someone would buy this boy's beginning and wear it to a party. Somewhere in the city tonight, a man would taste another man's joy and feel nothing but the pleasure of it, and J. Marrec would go home to his girl tomorrow and look at her mouth and feel—what? Fondness without root. A door with no house behind it.

He would be happier, probably. That was the cruelest arithmetic of the trade, and Ansel knew it better than anyone breathing: people were lighter without their heaviest moments. They laughed easier. They slept. The guild's oldest saying was *we sell relief*, and it was true, and it was monstrous, and both facts lived in Ansel's chest like two birds in one cage, pecking at each other in the dark.

The next client came in while the ink was still drying on the first entry.

This one was older—a soldier, or lately one, though he wore a carter's coat now and couldn't seem to stop his knee from jumping. He didn't bring a bottle. Some memories came out loose, needing the brokers' skill to catch them, and this was plainly one of those. He sat with his fists on his knees and stared at the floorboards and said, "I ran. At Redford Ford. Everyone died and I ran, and I can't—I hear it every time I close my eyes. The screaming. My own feet in the mud. I want it gone. All of it. I'll take whatever you give me."

Odile's apprentice, a nervous boy called Tam, looked to her, and she gave the smallest shake of her head—not *no*, but *carefully*. Cowardice was delicate goods. Take too much and you took the man's spine with it; leave too little and he'd only buy it back within the year, weeping, twice as broken.

"We can lift the memory entire," Odile said. "You'll know the battle happened. You'll know men died. But the running—you won't have carried it. Is that what you want?"

"Yes." No hesitation. "Gods, yes."

Ansel wrote:

*Siphoned: one (1) act of cowardice, Battle of Redford Ford. Seller: D. Hale, former footman, age 34. Price: twenty-five crowns and the guild's discretion. Note: seller wept throughout extraction and thanked us after. Note appended by L.K.: he will not remember why his hands shake in thunderstorms. Recommend follow-up in spring.*

That last line was Ansel's own addition, unofficial, unwelcome. Vess had told them once, flatly, that the ledger recorded trades, not consciences. Ansel had kept writing the notes anyway. It was the only rebellion available to a person built entirely out of not-being-noticed, and they hoarded it like a miser hoards copper.

The soldier left lighter. You could see it happen—watched him straighten in the doorway, watched his knee go still, watched him pause under the lamp outside and frown at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger who had done something terrible once. Then he walked off whistling, badly, and Ansel closed their eyes for one breath and let the ache pass through and out.

*We sell relief,* they thought. *And we are the relief's undertakers.*

* * *

By midnight the trade floor had emptied. Odile counted the strongbox; Tam snuffed lamps; the dyer below banked his fires, and the building settled into the particular silence of a place full of other people's secrets. Ansel finished the final entries, blotted each page, and carried the ledger down.

The stairs to the vault were narrow and worn hollow in the centers, and the deeper Ansel went, the warmer the air grew—which was wrong, by all natural law, for a cellar, but the vault had never obeyed natural law. It was the reason the guild existed. Beneath Hollow Bridge, in a chamber older than the city's charter, stood the shelves: ten thousand bottles and boxes and locket-cases and plain sealed jars, each holding some piece of some person that the person had chosen—or been persuaded—to live without. First kisses. Last words. The smell of a dead mother's kitchen. A betrayal witnessed and never spoken of. A child's face, surrendered by a parent who could not afford to grieve and work at the same time.

The vault hummed. It always hummed, very low, a sound you felt in your teeth more than heard, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. New keepers asked about it. Old keepers said it was the memories talking to each other. Oldest keepers said nothing at all and drank more than was wise.

Tonight, Ansel paused on the last stair, because the hum sounded different.

Not louder. Not softer. *Strained*—the way a rope sounds just before the fibers start to part, a note pulled taut toward somewhere it didn't want to go. Ansel stood listening for the length of ten heartbeats, pen-hand itching toward the ledger, wanting to write *vault hum altered, cause unknown* and hating how little help such a sentence would be. Then the note eased, settled, resumed its ordinary drone, and Ansel told themselves they had imagined it, the way they told themselves many things.

They shelved the day's acquisitions—the kiss between a jar of someone's courage and a lacquered box labeled, in faded script, *the taste of my sister's wedding cake*—and locked the case, and climbed.

* * *

Home was two rooms above a cobbler's on Tallow Street, reached by stairs that groaned like a ship. Ansel lit no lamp—they knew the rooms by touch, by count of steps, by the exact placement of every object, because a person who needed light was a person who left evidence of living—and sat at the narrow table beneath the window where the market's dying fires still stained the sky a low orange.

Here was the part of the night no one saw. Here was the tithe.

Every keeper of the ledger paid rent to the guild in the same coin the guild dealt in: memory. It was Vess's design, old and elegant—no one who worked among stolen things should be trusted to own themselves entirely, and no one who owned themselves entirely should be trusted with the ledger. So each moon, Ansel opened their own skull the way they'd watched brokers open a hundred others, and paid out a sliver. Small ones, always. Carefully chosen. A rainy afternoon at eight years old. The flavor of a particular stew. The name of a village they'd passed through once. Nothing structural. Nothing load-bearing.

Eleven years of small payments, though, add up. Ansel sometimes tried to inventory what was missing and found the task impossible by definition—you cannot list the rooms of a house you no longer know you had. There were gaps they could map by their edges: a whole year of childhood that ended abruptly mid-sentence, like a page torn out. A person whose face surfaced sometimes in dreams, beloved and utterly anonymous, gone by morning. Ansel had learned to hold these edges gently, the way you'd touch a bruise, and then let go.

Tonight, they chose the payment before choosing anything else, because deliberation was how you talked yourself into keeping things.

A market evening, then. Fitting. This very evening, an hour of it—the smell of woodsmoke, the orange sky, the sound of a fiddler tuning near the bridge. Pleasant enough to hurt a little, worthless enough to spare. Ansel set the extraction bowl on the table, silver and shallow, etched with the guild's sigil: a keyhole with no door around it. They pressed their palms to the rim, breathed out slow, and did the thing they were perhaps best at in all the world, better even than invisibility.

They reached into themselves and took.

It came away cleanly, as it always did—a ribbon of color and warmth lifting free, coiling into the bowl like steam made solid. Ansel watched it settle, watched the evening dim inside them by exactly one hour, watched the fiddler's tuning-note vanish from a memory they would not know they'd had. The hole it left was small and smooth-edged. They were practiced at not probing their own wounds.

*Forty-one months of rent remaining on the lease of myself,* they thought, and almost smiled. Almost. Their face rarely committed fully to anything.

They were corking the bowl when the ache arrived—the quiet one, the permanent tenant, the one that lived beneath the competence like water beneath ice. It always came after a payment, in the stillness after, when the rooms were dark and the city murmured below. It had no shape and no story, which was the trouble. Grief with a story could be carried. This was grief with the story removed, sold off in slivers across eleven years, leaving only the weight. Some nights Ansel thought: *I am homesick for a home I cannot name.* Most nights they thought nothing at all, because thinking nothing was the whole of their art.

They sealed the bowl, wrapped it, and set it by the door for morning delivery. And that was when the knock came.

Three raps, spaced evenly, soft as a knuckle on a coffin lid. Nobody knocked on Ansel's door. That was rather the point of Ansel's door.

They opened it to find Guildmaster Vess standing on the landing, which had happened perhaps four times in eleven years, and never once after midnight. Vess was a narrow, iron-grey woman with the eyes of someone who had read too many true confessions to be surprised by humanity, and tonight those eyes were doing something Ansel had never seen them do.

They were uncertain.

"Ledger-keeper," Vess said. Her voice was level, but Ansel had spent a lifetime reading the space between words, and the space between these was wide as a chasm. "You're wanted at the bridge. Now."

"The vault—"

"Humming wrong. Has been since sundown." Vess looked past Ansel's shoulder, out the window, at the sky above the rooftops—at the stars, which were all present and accounted for, and which Vess regarded anyway, for a long moment, as though counting them against some older memory of the sky. "And the border wardstones have gone quiet. All of them. Every stone from here to the Salt Marches, silent as grave-dirt, and the wardens are sending word that they can't say why, only that the stones feel—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if the word itself were contraband.

"Felt how?" Ansel asked quietly.

Vess's gaze came back from the window and fixed on them, and for the first time in eleven years of careful, invisible service, Ansel Lockridge felt thoroughly, uncomfortably *seen*.

"Like a bell," Vess said, "that has just decided to stop ringing. Get your coat, Lockridge. At dawn you'll ride east and tell me what's killing the oldest wards in the world."

Behind her, through the window, high over the autumn market's ember-glow, the stars burned on—every one of them, Ansel would have sworn, a fraction dimmer than the night before.
