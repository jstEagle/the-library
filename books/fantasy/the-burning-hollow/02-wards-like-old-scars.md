## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

The summons came at the hour when the Hollow Guild's corridors were at their quietest—that thin grey time between the last of the night's business and the first kettle of morning tea, when even the vault's restless murmuring seemed to hold its breath. Ansel was already awake, because Ansel was always already awake, seated cross-legged on the cold flagstones of their cell with the day's ledger open across their knees, when Vess's note slid under the door like a pale, insistent tongue.

*The border stones. Today. Bring your quiet.*

No signature. None needed. Only one person in the guild wrote in a hand that slanted leftward as though fleeing from its own words.

Ansel dressed by feel, the way they did everything—boots laced to identical tension, coat brushed of lint that wasn't there, hair pinned back so that no strand could catch light or attention. In the small tarnished mirror bolted beside the door, they checked themselves the way a forger checks a plate: not for beauty but for absence. Nothing memorable. Nothing that would snag in a stranger's mind an hour after they'd passed. A face like a closed ledger. It had taken years to build that face, and Ansel maintained it the way other people maintained devotional shrines—with daily discipline and a private, unexamined fear of what might happen if they ever stopped.

They tucked the ledger into its oilcloth sleeve, slid the sleeve into the inner pocket sewn against their ribs, and went up through the guild's bones toward the light.

Vess was waiting in the map room, which was less a room than a cave the guild had claimed and papered over. Charts of three provinces hung from hooks, layered with annotations in a dozen dead hands: safe roads, toll roads, roads where the toll was paid in things no coin-purse could carry. The Guildmaster stood before the oldest chart—the one showing the old kingdom's borders, stitched in thread gone brown as dried blood—and did not turn when Ansel entered. She never turned. It was one of her hundred small tyrannies, making people announce themselves.

"Lockridge."

"Guildmaster."

"The wardstone at Marrow Ford sang to a tinker two nights past." Vess's voice was gravel poured slow. "Not hummed. *Sang.* Loud enough to crack his cart mugs. Then it stopped. Mid-note." She turned then, and her eyes—pale as river ice, the eyes of a woman who had traded away everything soft about herself decades ago and never once regretted it aloud—fixed on Ansel with the weight of a hand pressing down. "Wardstones don't stop mid-note, Lockridge. Bells do. When they break."

"You want me to look at them."

"I want you to *listen* to them. There's a difference." She crossed to the table and unrolled a smaller map, weighted its corners with candle-stubs. Four marks in red wax dotted the border line. "Marrow Ford. Greyfell Tor. The Twins at Hollowmere. And the Old King's Stone out past the fen road. Four stones within a day's walk of each other, all fed from the same deep current. If one is dying, I want to know if they're all dying. And I want to know *quietly*. No guild colors. No names. You were never sent."

"And if they're all dying?"

Vess looked at the map for a long moment. Outside, the autumn market was waking—carts rattling over cobbles, a fishmonger's bell, children shrieking somewhere near the well—and the ordinary noise of it seemed suddenly precious, like glassware balanced on a ledge.

"Then we have a problem no ledger can solve," she said, "and I will hate every minute of it. Go. Be back inside three days. And Lockridge—" she caught Ansel at the door with one raised finger, "—if anyone asks, you're a surveyor's clerk checking boundary markers for a land dispute. Boring. Be boring."

"Boring is my native language," Ansel said, and had the rare satisfaction of seeing something almost like a smile twitch at the corner of Vess's mouth before it was strangled dead.

* * *

The road north out of Hollow followed the river, and the river followed the old scars.

That was what the wardstones were, really—scars. Ansel had learned that much from the guild's oldest records, back in the years when they'd been new enough to read everything and foolish enough to believe reading was the same as understanding. When the world had been younger and crueler in more honest ways, someone—kings, or priests, or both wearing each other's faces—had driven great standing stones into the earth along the kingdom's borders and taught them to hold a single note. Not music, exactly. More like a held breath. The wards didn't keep out armies; armies found their own ways in and mostly weren't worth the effort. What the wards kept out was older and hungrier: the things that lived in the spaces between belonging, the hollow-born, the unmade, whatever names the hedge-folk gave to the dark that ate names. As long as the stones held their note, those things stayed on the far side of the line, pressed flat like flowers in a book.

Everyone knew this the way everyone knows the sun rises. Nobody thought about it. That was rather the point of wards done well—you forgot they existed, and their existence depended on your forgetting.

Ansel walked fast and made themselves small. They passed a shepherd who nodded without interest; a peddler whose eyes slid off them like rain off waxed canvas; a pair of soldiers playing dice against a milestone who never once looked up. By midday Ansel had become, to any observer, exactly what Vess had ordered: a surveyor's clerk, tired, dull, forgettable before the memory of them finished forming. It should have been comforting. Most days it was. Today, walking alone under a sky the color of unwashed wool, Ansel felt the invisibility press close like a second coat, heavier than usual, and could not have said why.

Marrow Ford announced itself by smell first—woodsmoke, wet stone, the green rot of the shallows—and then by sound: water braiding itself around the ford's slick spine of rock. The village huddled on the far bank, a scatter of roofs and drying nets. And there, on the near bank, set back among birches gone gold as struck coins, stood the wardstone.

It was taller than a man and twice as broad, granite furred with moss, its carved sigils worn to shallow shadows by four centuries of weather. Generations of villagers had leaned against it, sharpened knives on it, tied ribbons to it during the spring festival without knowing why. It was the most taken-for-granted object in the province.

And it was silent.

Ansel felt the silence before they understood it—a wrongness in the ears, the way a room feels wrong when a clock that has ticked all your life has stopped. They crossed the grass slowly and laid a palm flat against the stone.

Cold. Colder than autumn had any right to make it, cold climbing up through the skin like water wicking up cloth. And beneath the cold, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something trembled. Not the deep, steady note the records described—the note you weren't supposed to be able to hear but always half-did, the way you half-hear your own heartbeat. This was a shiver. A stutter. The ghost of a bell that had been struck and was deciding whether to finish ringing or fall apart.

Ansel pressed harder, closed their eyes, and listened the way Vess had ordered—not with ears but with the trained patience of a person who spent their life noticing what others overlooked.

There. Under the stutter: a rhythm. Faint, arrhythmic, like breathing through broken ribs. The stone was trying to hold its note and failing, catching, failing again. And threaded through the failure, so faint Ansel might have invented it, was something else—a sound like a long inhalation from very far away. Like something drawing breath to speak. Or to eat.

Ansel stepped back. Their hand tingled where it had touched the granite, and the tingling did not fade for several minutes.

"Well," they said quietly, to no one, because saying things aloud to no one was a habit of people who had made themselves too invisible to talk to, "that's not good."

They took out the ledger and recorded it—time, temperature, the stutter's interval, the strange drawn breath—all in the compressed shorthand only a guild ledger-keeper could read. Then they shouldered their pack and walked north toward Greyfell Tor, telling themselves the next stone would be fine. Stones held their notes for four hundred years. One bad night at a ford proved nothing.

Greyfell Tor was worse.

Ansel saw it from a mile off, crowning the bare hill like a broken tooth, and heard it before they saw it—a sound that stopped them mid-stride with their heart slamming. The stone was humming. Audibly. A thin, wavering tone that rose and fell like wind through a keyhole, and threaded through the tone, unmistakable now, unmistakably *wrong*, was the other sound. The inhale. Longer this time. Closer. As though the thing drawing breath had moved a room nearer in the night.

A shepherd boy sat at the hill's base, maybe ten years old, hands over his ears, rocking slightly.

"It won't stop," he said when Ansel approached. His voice had the flatness of a child who has been crying and has run out. "Da says it's just wind. Wind doesn't come out of the ground, does it? Wind doesn't come *out* of the ground?"

"No," Ansel said. "It doesn't." They crouched to the boy's level, keeping their face bland, their voice the voice adults used on frightened children and skittish horses. "How long has it been singing?"

"Three days. Ma sent me up with bread for the shrine and it started while I was here and it hasn't—" He swallowed. "Sometimes it stops. For a breath. And when it stops it's worse. When it stops I think it's going to say something."

Ansel looked at the stone. Up close, the sigils carved into its face had changed—or rather, the wear upon them had changed. Moss was dying in lines, retreating from the grooves as though the stone had begun to sweat something the moss couldn't abide. And at the base, where four centuries of offerings had accumulated—ribbons, coins, a child's clay horse, the bleached vertebrae of some festival ox—the offerings nearest the stone were grayed and brittle, like things left too long in frost.

"When it stops," Ansel said carefully, "what do you think it's going to say?"

The boy looked up, and for a moment his eyes were old, old as the stone, old as the hills the stone was named for.

"My name," he whispered. "I think it's going to say my name. And I think if I hear it, I'll go to it."

Ansel gave him a copper for the walk home and watched him go until he was a speck on the downs, and then stood alone under the wavering note for a long time, writing nothing, because there were no words in the ledger's shorthand for *the wards are afraid.*

* * *

Ansel reached Hollowmere at dusk, when the twin villages on the lake's opposite shores were lighting their lamps and the water between them lay still as hammered pewter. The Twins—the paired wardstones, one on each shore, set a quarter-mile apart so that their joined note spanned the lake like a bridge—were visible from the fen road, two dark shapes against the last light.

Ansel heard them from a quarter mile further than that.

Both stones were singing now, and the sound carried across the water with terrible clarity: two voices, once harmonized, sliding slowly out of true. The eastern stone held high and thin. The western had dropped low, guttural, dragging behind like a dancer whose partner had grown heavy. Between them the harmony frayed, tore, knotted. It was the sound of a rope beginning to part strand by strand, and everyone in both villages must have been able to hear it, and the lamps were being lit anyway, and somewhere someone was laughing, and Ansel understood with a cold settling in the stomach that nobody was listening because nobody believed the stones could ever stop.

They took the ferry across—paid the ferryman double to make the crossing at dusk, and the ferryman, a broad woman with forearms like hams, talked the whole way about her son's apprenticeship and never once glanced at Ansel's face, and Ansel noted with distant professional approval that the invisibility was holding even now, even here, even with the sky doing what the sky was doing.

Because the sky was doing something. The stars, when Ansel tipped their head back, seemed dimmer than they should be. Not absent—Autumn's Wain still wheeled above the northern hills, the Shepherd still stood—but muted, as though seen through smoke, or as though they were very far away and getting farther. Ansel told themselves it was cloud. High cirrus. Anything.

The western stone stood in a pasture above the shore, and Ansel climbed to it through wet grass while the dissonance grew from discomfort to ache. By the time they reached it, their teeth had set on edge and their vision pulsed faintly at the edges, and the stone—

The stone was bleeding.

Not blood. Light. Or the memory of light—a pale luminescence seeping from the cracks that had opened along its flank, cracks fine as hair, pooling in the sigil-grooves and running down the granite in slow luminous threads that sank into the earth and vanished. It looked, Ansel thought with a lurch of recognition that felt like falling, like starlight. Like starlight spilled and soaking away into dirt.

And someone was kneeling at the stone's base.

A girl—no, a young woman, slight and sharp-boned, wrapped in a patched traveling cloak gone green with damp. Her hood was back. Her hair was the color of wet sand, cut short and unevenly, hacked with a knife by someone who hadn't cared how it looked. She had her ear pressed against the granite the way a physician listens to a chest, and her eyes were shut, and tears ran freely down her face, and she was singing.

Ansel froze halfway across the pasture.

The girl was singing a song Ansel had never heard and yet recognized instantly, the way one recognizes grief, or home—a rising, looping melody with words in no language Ansel knew, old words, hedge-words, the kind grandmothers kept and churches forbade. And as she sang, impossibly, the stone's guttering note steadied. Just slightly. Just for the length of each phrase. The dissonance eased. The bleeding light slowed.

Then she stopped for breath, and the stone lurched back toward silence, and the girl made a sound like something tearing loose, and pressed her forehead to the granite and wept.

Ansel should have walked away. Every instinct honed over ten years of careful, profitable unremarkability said *walk away, this is not your trouble, report to Vess, let the guild decide.* But their boots had already carried them three steps closer, and the girl's head snapped up—she'd heard them, of course she had, she was the kind of person who heard things—and Ansel saw her face properly for the first time.

She was perhaps nineteen. Freckled everywhere the damp hadn't washed her pale. And her eyes were the strangest Ansel had ever seen: not odd-colored, but *moving*, shifting between grey and green and brown like water over stones, as though they couldn't decide what they were looking at because they were looking at everything at once.

"You're not from the village," the girl said. Her voice was hoarse. "Village folk don't come up here anymore. They say the singing scares the cows." She wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a streak of granite dust across her cheek. "You came to listen. I can tell. You've got listener's feet."

"Listener's feet," Ansel repeated.

"You walk like you're trying not to interrupt anything." She studied Ansel with those shifting eyes, and Ansel felt—deeply uncomfortably—*seen*, catalogued, filed somewhere behind them. "You're guild. Memory-trade. Ledger-keeper, by the ink on your fingers and the way you keep touching your pocket. Don't worry, I'm not going to tell. I'm running from people who'd want me found, and you look like someone who understands not wanting to be found."

Ansel said nothing, which was answer enough, and the girl smiled—a quick, crooked, exhausted smile that transformed her face entirely and was gone again.

"Wren," she said. "My grandmother called me Wren, on account of I never stopped talking, and then she died, and I kept the name because it was hers." She patted the stone beside her, an oddly tender gesture, like soothing a horse. "This one's dying. Did you know that? They're all dying. Marrow Ford's nearly gone. Greyfell's screaming. This one's been holding on because it's got its sister across the water to lean on, but the sister's going too, and when they both go—" She stopped. Swallowed. Looked at Ansel with an expression of such naked, unguarded fear that it hurt to witness, the way open wounds hurt to witness.

"When they both go," Ansel said quietly, "what happens?"

"They stop holding the line." Wren turned back to the stone, laid both palms flat against it, and Ansel saw her whole body flinch. "You've been listening with your ears. I listen with everything else. My gran taught me the hedge-songs—the ones that talk *to* the old things instead of about them. And this stone isn't humming wrong, guildsman. It's not a machine winding down." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "It's screaming. It's been screaming for days. And underneath the screaming, there's something on the other side of the line, and it's *listening back*, and it is so hungry, and it is so patient, and it has just realized—"

She broke off. Her shifting eyes went wide, fixed on something beyond Ansel's shoulder, out over the darkening lake.

"—it has just realized the door is almost open."

Ansel turned. Across the black water, the eastern stone's high thin note wavered, faltered—

—and stopped.

Mid-note. Like a struck bell going silent. Like a throat closing around a word.

In the sudden silence, so complete that Ansel could hear their own blood, the western stone beneath Wren's hands began, very softly, to sing a different song. And Wren, hedge-witch, runaway, granddaughter of a dead wise-woman, threw back her head and answered it in the old tongue, her voice cracking across the pasture like a struck match in a dark room:

"*They're coming home,*" she translated, shaking, "*they're coming home, and the door was never ours to lock—*"

Far to the north, past the fen road, past the hills, in the direction of the capital and the crown and the king nobody thought about—something exhaled.

Every star in the sky flickered, once, like candles in a draft.

Neither of them spoke. Neither of them needed to. The longest night of the world had just taken its first breath, and two strangers stood in a dark pasture beside a dying god of granite, and understood, in the wordless way of people who have just become necessary to one another, that neither of them would ever be invisible again.
