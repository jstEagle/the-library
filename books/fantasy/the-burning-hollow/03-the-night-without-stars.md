## 3. The Night Without Stars

Ansel Lockridge was balancing the ledger when the first star died.

They noticed it the way they noticed everything—sideways, without seeming to look. A candle guttered in its jar on the shelf behind the counter, and beyond it, through the smudged window of the guild's front room, the sky over Hollow's Reach had a hole in it. Not a cloud. Not a wisp of fog. A hole, clean-edged as a burn in paper, where a star had been a heartbeat before.

Ansel set down the pen.

The ink was still wet on the evening's entries. *One bottled first kiss, vintage nineteen years, sold to the widow Marsh for three crowns and a favor to be named later. One soldier's cowardice, extracted at Kettle Bridge, held in reserve.* Ordinary trades. Ordinary night. The guild had been buying and selling the contents of other people's heads since before Ansel could write, and the sky had never once taken notice.

The second star went out. Then a third and fourth in the same breath, like candles pinched by a passing hand.

Ansel stood so quickly the stool scraped back against the floorboards. They pressed their palm flat against the cold glass and looked up through the warped pane at the autumn sky—and watched, in the space of ten heartbeats, the whole of it go dark. Not overcast. *Emptied*. The stars didn't fade; they were taken, all at once, all across the vault of heaven, as if someone had reached into the night and stolen every light that had ever hung there.

The silence afterward was worse than the losing. Ansel hadn't known stars made sound—hadn't known they'd been making it all their life, some vast faint ringing underneath the world, the way a bell keeps singing after you stop hearing it. But now that it was gone, Ansel could feel the absence pressing against their eardrums like deep water.

"No," they said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them real and this one thing they wanted very much not to be real.

Then the wards fell.

Ansel felt it in their teeth first—a high thin note that had lived at the edge of hearing for as long as they'd worked the guild, the hum of the border wardstones thirty miles out, fed by something old and patient. Every memory-thief learned to ignore it the way sailors ignore the creak of a ship. It was simply there, the sound of being safe inside a boundary.

It stopped. Like a string cut mid-note.

And beneath the guildhouse, in the vault that held forty years of stolen lives, something answered.

* * *

Wren heard it from the attic room they'd given her two nights ago, in exchange for sweeping floors and staying out of sight.

She'd been dreaming of her grandmother's kitchen—the smell of bread, the hedge-song hummed low over the stove—when the silence came down on her like a dropped blanket. She woke already sitting up, already reaching for the iron nail she kept under her pillow, because Wren had grown up learning that when the world went quiet in the wrong way, quiet was never empty. Quiet was a mouth, opening.

The stars were gone. All of them. She ran to the little round window and looked up and felt her stomach drop through the floor, because her grandmother had told her stories about this, fireside stories meant for children who wouldn't remember them: *the night the sky forgets itself*. Hedge-witch nonsense, her mother had said. But her grandmother had also taught her the song, the one that named the stars one by one like a roll call, and Wren had sung it to herself every night since she ran away from home, because it was the only prayer she knew.

She opened her mouth to sing it now and found she couldn't hear herself.

Not deafness. Something else. The silence where the stars should sing had spread downward, into the walls, into the floorboards, into the bones of the guildhouse—and below her feet, muffled by three floors of timber and stone, she heard the vault begin to scream.

It wasn't people screaming. It was memories. Wren could hear them the way she could hear wards and rivers and the mood of weather coming—hundreds of them, thousands, packed into lead-lined boxes in the dark below, and every one of them suddenly awake, suddenly straining toward something far away, howling to go home.

She was down the stairs before she decided to move, barefoot, nail clutched in her fist, and she collided in the corridor with Ansel Lockridge, who was running the other direction with a lantern and a face like carved wood.

"The vault," they both said at once.

* * *

The vault door was built to keep thieves out. Nothing about it had ever been designed for what was trying to get *in*—or rather, what was trying to get *out*, in the only direction that mattered to it anymore.

Ansel hit the release lever and the great wheel spun free, and the door swung open onto chaos made visible.

The vault was a long chamber of shelves and lead boxes, each box labeled in the ledger-hand of four decades of keepers. It should have been dim and orderly and dead. Instead it was full of light—soft, shifting, colored light leaking from every seam of every box, and the boxes were rattling on their shelves like crates of live animals. Some had already burst. From the broken ones rose shapes that were not quite smoke and not quite fire: a memory unbound, writhing free, orienting itself toward some distant point with the terrible certainty of a compass needle.

"Vess!" Ansel shouted, and the Guildmaster was already there, pushing past them into the heat of it, her gray braid whipping.

"I can see it," Vess snapped. "Seal the—"

A box on the third shelf detonated.

The blast wasn't force; it was *meaning*. Ansel felt it pass through them like a wave through shallow water, and in its wake came fragments—not theirs, someone else's entire life in shreds: a wedding in a rain-soaked orchard, a debt forgiven, a dog's warm weight against a sleeping leg, a name called across a field. For half a second Ansel *was* those things, wore them like clothes cut for another body, and then the wave moved on and left them gasping against a shelf, their own identity feeling suddenly like a coat they'd have to check the pockets of.

Across the chamber, more boxes were going. The ruptures were finding a rhythm now, a pulse, and Ansel—with the ledger-keeper's curse of seeing pattern in everything—understood the rhythm at once with sick certainty. The memories weren't exploding randomly. They were being *pulled*. All of them, in every box, straining toward the same invisible point on the horizon like iron filings snapping toward a magnet.

Something out there in the dark was calling everything home. Or eating it on the way.

"Get the reserve ledgers," Vess was shouting, and the guild was waking around them—doors banging, footsteps on stairs, Brannic the lock-wright appearing in the vault doorway with a hammer in one hand and no shirt on, Sefa behind him already dressed, which meant she hadn't slept, which meant she'd been forging again. Old Ivo came last, moving slow, his milky eyes wide open, and he stood in the doorway looking at the storm of escaping memories with an expression Ansel had never seen on him before.

Ivo looked *grieved*.

"They're beautiful," the old man said quietly, to no one. "We always forget they're beautiful."

A memory tore loose from a shelf near the ceiling and streaked upward through the stone like light through water, gone. Another followed. Another. The vault was bleeding out from the top, and there was nothing anyone could do but stand in it.

"Wren." Ansel caught the girl's arm as she stared around the chamber, her lips moving, listening to something none of them could hear. "What is it? What are they doing?"

"Going home," Wren said. Her voice was strange, doubled somehow, as if she were speaking for the memories too. "Every one of them knows the way back to its owner. They've always known. We just—" She swallowed. "We just built a box strong enough to make them forget."

* * *

By dawn—or what should have been dawn, except the sun rose pale and wrong behind a sky still scoured of stars—the first clients arrived.

Ansel had prepared for many versions of disaster. Fire. Raid. A rival guild's knives in the dark. They had not prepared for Mrs. Aldery of Cooper's Lane standing in the front room at six in the morning, gray-faced, still in her nightclothes, screaming.

"It came *back*," she was saying, over and over, while Sefa tried to pour tea into her and Vess tried to ask questions. "You took it, you took it fair and square, I signed, I *signed*—and now it's back and I can't—I can't carry it—"

What had come back, it emerged between sobs, was Mrs. Aldery's grief. Sold to the guild eleven years ago, the mourning for a drowned son, traded away so she could go on living. And last night, in the hour the stars died, it had torn across half the city and poured itself back into her where she slept—and eleven years of unfelt grief had landed on her all at once, undiluted, undimmed by time.

"She's lucky," Ivo murmured to Ansel, watching the woman rock and weep in the chair. "Some of them won't be."

He was right. By noon there were nine clients in the front room, and word was spreading down the lanes like spilled ink. The cooper whose cowardice had returned and made him unable to leave his house. The merchant who'd sold his love for his first wife and woke with it flooding back while his second wife shook him and asked what was wrong, what was *wrong*. A man carried in unconscious, his mind nearly crushed under the sudden weight of a memory of a battlefield that the guild had been holding for fifteen years.

Ansel worked the counter because working was the only shape they could give the terror. Check the ledger. Find the entry. Confirm the return. Log the damage. Their handwriting stayed neat. Their voice stayed level. Inside, some part of them that had spent years building walls of careful forgetting watched those walls with new eyes and did not like what it saw: the same hunger loose in the world that had always lived, quiet and professional, in the vault below.

*We just built a box strong enough to make them forget.*

"Ledger-keeper." Vess's hand closed on Ansel's shoulder, drawing them into the back office. The Guildmaster looked twenty years older than she had yesterday. "We're going dark. No new trades, no clients, the sign comes down within the hour. Whatever pulled the vault apart is still out there, and until we know what it is, the guild is a cellar with the door barred. Understood?"

"And the returns?" Ansel asked. "The ones still in the vault? Half the stock is gone, but—"

"Gone where it goes. We don't chase it. We don't touch it." Vess's eyes were flint. "That's an order, Lockridge. You above all people know what happens to curious thieves."

Ansel knew. Curiosity was the first thing most thieves sold.

But that afternoon, while the others nailed boards over windows and burned what records could be burned, Ansel sat alone in the office with the master ledger and turned, slowly, to the oldest section. Forty years of entries in a dozen different hands. And there, near the beginning, in ink gone brown as dried blood, a single page that had been nagging at the edge of their mind since the vault ruptured—an entry they'd catalogued years ago and never thought twice about.

*A purchase. Forty years past. Seller: unnamed. Buyer: the guild, acting for a client who paid in royal gold.*

*Contents: the coronation day of King Aldous III. His memories of it. All of them.*

Bought and sealed away the week after the crown touched his head. And the client's name on the line below, written in that same brown ink, was not a person at all.

It was a seal. The royal seal.

Someone in the palace had paid the Hollow Guild to steal the king's memory of his own crowning—and had then, apparently, bought it and buried it here, in the dark, for forty years.

Outside the shuttered window, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. Below the floorboards, the surviving vault-boxes had gone silent at last—but it was not a peaceful silence. It was the silence of things waiting, all facing the same direction, all listening for a call that would come again.

Ansel copied the entry onto a single loose page, folded it small, and slipped it inside their shirt, against their skin, where the guild's rules and Vess's orders couldn't reach it.

Whatever had eaten the stars had started feeding a long time ago. And the first meal on record had worn a crown.
