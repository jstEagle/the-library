## 4. What Came Home

The client's name was Odell Marsh, and he arrived at the guild's back door an hour past dawn, gray as old tallow, with his hands pressed flat against his temples as though holding his skull together by force.

Ansel knew him from the ledger. *Odell Marsh, cooper, forty-one years of age. Sold: the drowning of his brother, age nine. Price: one winter's rent and a new set of coopering tools.* A clean trade, made six years ago in this same doorway. Ansel had taken the memory themselves—had felt it pass through them like cold water on its way to the vault—and had watched Odell walk away lighter, unburdened, whistling down the alley with the particular gait of a man who has set down something too heavy to carry any longer.

He was not whistling now.

"Lockridge." His voice came out scraped raw. "It came *back*."

Ansel kept their hand on the door, not opening it. The street behind him was wrong in ways they couldn't yet name—the morning market stalls shuttered, a woman standing motionless in the middle of the lane staring at nothing, two children crying without sound. Since the stars had died and the wards had fallen, Hollowbridge had been holding its breath. "Mr. Marsh. If you're here about a refund—"

"It came *back*," he said again, louder, and now Ansel saw what was underneath the gray: terror, bright and total, the kind that lives behind the eyes of a man who has seen something he was never meant to see again. "I was at my bench. Staving hoops. And then I was—" His breath hitched. "I was under the ice. I could feel my brother's hand. I could hear him calling me through the water. Six years, Lockridge. Six years I didn't even know I'd *had* a brother."

Ansel opened the door.

* * *

They got him into the receiving room, into the chair by the cold hearth, and that was when the shaking started—not the small shivering of a frightened man but the full-body convulsion of someone whose flesh remembered dying. Ansel knelt beside him and did the thing they were best at: they became furniture. Unremarkable. Present the way a wall is present. They talked to him in the flat, steady tone the guild taught for handling clients mid-collapse, and all the while they watched the memory work through him like poison.

Because that was what it looked like. Not a memory returning—a memory *attacking*. Odell's eyes rolled white. His fingernails split against the chair arms. He began to speak in a child's voice, high and bubbling, saying *Tam, Tam, I can't reach you, Tam,* and then in his own voice again, gasping, *he drowned, he drowned, I watched him drown, why is it in me, why is it IN ME—*

"The vault ruptured last night," Wren said from the doorway. She'd appeared without sound, as she always did, her hedge-witch senses tuned to whatever frequency the world had begun screaming on since the stars went out. Her face was pale. "Memories going home. All of them."

"They aren't going home," Ansel said quietly. They had spent the night among the vault's wreckage and had seen things they were still refusing to think about directly. "Going home would be gentle. This is being *dragged*."

Odell Marsh screamed then—one long tearing note—and arched out of the chair, and Ansel caught him before his skull could meet the flagstones, and for one terrible moment they felt it through his skin: the memory itself, vast and wet and nine years old, thrashing inside a man who had sold it fair and square, trying to bury itself back into the place it had been cut from, indifferent to whether the vessel survived the reattachment.

Wren was there suddenly, her small hands pressed to either side of Odell's head, her lips moving around words that weren't words—hedge-sounds, grandmother-sounds, the music of a tradition older than the guild, older maybe than the wards. And slowly, slowly, the screaming wound down to sobbing, and the sobbing to shuddering, and the shuddering to the exhausted stillness of a man wrung out and emptied.

"He'll live," Wren whispered. She looked up at Ansel, and her eyes were wet. "But Ansel—it nearly ate him. The memory. It wasn't content just to be back. It wanted to take root and pull everything else in after it. Like it was hungry."

Ansel sat back on their heels.

Hungry. The word landed in them with the weight of a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was there. Last night, in the vault, they had watched stolen memories tear free of their lead-lined drawers—not drifting loose, not scattering, but *streaming*, all of them, in every case, in the same direction. Outward. Away. Toward something. They had told themselves it was the wards collapsing, the great machinery of forgetting failing all at once, a dam breaking. Dams break. It happens. You rebuild.

But dams don't choose a direction.

"Stay with him," Ansel said, rising. "Get broth into him when he can hold it. Don't let him sleep alone tonight."

"Where are you going?"

"To count what's missing."

* * *

The vault beneath the guildhouse had been built by people who understood that some things, once given away, should never find their way back. Three floors of descending stone, each drawer lined in lead and ash-oak and inscribed with the owner's name in ink mixed with grave-dirt. Ansel had walked these corridors a thousand times and felt nothing, because feeling nothing was the trade they'd mastered above all others. Tonight, carrying a single lantern down into the dark, they felt the emptiness the way you feel a missing tooth—with the tongue of the mind returning again and again to the gap.

Two-thirds of the drawers stood open. Empty. Not broken open—*emptied*, the contents gone so cleanly it was almost polite, as if the memories had simply been called home to dinner.

Ansel took the ledger off its chain and began, methodically, to audit the impossible.

*Marsh, Odell. Returned. Nearly fatal.* They wrote standing up, using a drawer's edge as a desk, because their hands needed the work to stay steady. *Corwin, Tessa. Returned: the face of her mother. Client reports weeping for four hours, then silence. Status unknown.* *Bellamy, the ferryman. Returned: twenty years of river-fog. He walked into the Graywater at dawn. They pulled him out alive. He keeps trying to go back.*

Page after page. Hundreds of trades undone in a single night. And threaded through all of it, the pattern Ansel had been refusing to see since Odell collapsed in their receiving room: direction. Every returned memory, every account, every fragment of secondhand report pointed the same way. South and east. Toward the capital. Toward the palace on its hill where the king had not been seen in public for eleven days.

Ansel closed the ledger and stood in the ruined vault and let themselves, finally, think the thought whole.

Something was calling the memories home. Something vast enough to reach across the whole country and hungry enough to want them—not one thief's hoard, not one miser's collection, but *everything ever forgotten*, streaming south and east like iron filings toward a magnet the size of a mountain. The wards hadn't failed because they were old. The wards had failed because whatever fed them had stopped feeding—or been eaten.

And the wards, Vess had said, inspecting the dead border-stones with a face like carved granite, had been fed by the crown. By arrangement. By ancient contract. By the king himself.

Ansel climbed the stairs two at a time.

* * *

Vess received the report in her office, which smelled of pipe-smoke and lamp oil and forty years of decisions no one else had wanted to make. She listened without interrupting, which was worse than interruption. When Ansel finished—Marsh, the vault, the direction, the crown—she was silent for a long moment, turning her heavy signet ring around and around her finger.

"Say the last part again," she said.

"Whatever holds the wards is eating. Or it's been eaten. Either way, it's in the capital, and it's pulling every stolen memory in the country toward itself like a tide going out."

"And Marsh?"

"Alive. Barely. The memories aren't coming home gently, Guildmaster. They're coming home like arrows."

Vess nodded slowly. Then she stood, and Ansel recognized the posture—the straight-backed finality of a woman about to do the thing she'd already decided to do regardless of argument. "Then we go dark. Effective immediately. No new trades, no old clients, no contact with the other chapters. We seal the remaining vault, we scatter the ledgers, and anyone asking questions gets told the Hollow Guild burned down with the wards. Let the tide go out and find nothing waiting for it."

"You want us to hide."

"I want us to *survive*. That thing—if it's what you say—is pulling memories across half a kingdom. What do you imagine it does with a building full of thieves who know how memories are cut, stored, and sold? We're not prey to it, Lockridge. We're *inventory*." She met Ansel's eyes. "Burn your ledger. That's an order."

Ansel carried the ledger back down to the vault, because burning it in front of Vess would have been theater, and theater was Sefa's gift, not theirs. Alone among the empty drawers, they held the book over the ash-bucket where the guild kept coals for sealing wax, and they watched the firelight play across the cover, and they did not let go.

Here was the thing about Ansel Lockridge that almost no one alive knew: they had never once traded away a memory they weren't willing to lose, but they had also never once destroyed a record. The ledger was the guild's conscience. Every name, every price, every grief bought and sold—it was all written down, because Ansel believed, with a ferocity they showed to no one, that forgetting should always leave a receipt. Somewhere in those pages was proof of every kindness the guild had accidentally done and every cruelty it had deliberately committed. Burn it, and the hollow places in a thousand strangers' lives would have no witness.

They opened the book instead, meaning only to choose what to save, and their thumb found the page by accident—or by whatever force moves a hand when the mind claims innocence.

Forty years old. One of the first entries in the guild's books, in faded brown ink, in a hand that wasn't Ansel's. *Received, by private commission: the coronation of King Aldric Vane, second of his name. All recollections thereof, from the royal person entire. Payment: renewal of the border compact, in perpetuity. Witnessed under seal.*

And below the entry, pressed into the paper in wax gone dark as dried blood: the royal seal. The crowned stag of House Vane, rampant, its antlers spread like branches against nothing.

Ansel read it three times. Forty years ago, the guild had taken *everything*—every memory of his own crowning—from a young king, and been paid in wards. In protection. In the compact that had held the borders safe for four decades until three nights ago, when the stars went out and the payment stopped flowing.

Which meant the wards hadn't simply failed. Which meant something had finished eating.

"Stubborn fool," Ansel murmured to themselves, and tore the page free along the spine, careful as a surgeon, and folded it into the inner pocket of their coat, over the heart, where the ledger-keeper's most important entries were traditionally kept—the ones you couldn't afford to lose and couldn't afford to show.

The rest of the book they fed to the coals, page by page, watching forty years of receipts curl and blacken and rise as ash. It felt like closing a grave. It felt like lying.

When they came back up, the guildhouse was already moving—drawers being sealed, false names being distributed, Brannic's voice rumbling somewhere about which walls had hidden passages and which ones merely had rumors of them. Going dark. Disappearing. Becoming, once more, the thing Ansel had spent a lifetime perfecting in miniature: forgettable, unremarkable, gone.

Vess caught them in the corridor. Her eyes went to Ansel's coat pocket—she missed nothing, she never had—and lingered there for exactly one heartbeat too long.

"Burn it all?" she asked.

"Every page," Ansel said.

It was the first lie Ansel had ever told the guild, and it sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. Because the seal was real, and the entry was real, and somewhere south and east a king sat in a palace full of courtiers who couldn't remember his face, while everything he'd ever surrendered was dragged home to something that wore his crown and hungered still.

The guild would hide. Fine. Ansel had never been good at hiding from the truth—they'd only ever been good at hiding everyone else from it.

That night, by candlelight, in a hand smaller and neater than their own, Ansel began a new ledger with a single line:

*What was taken from the king, and where it is being taken to.*

Outside the window, the starless sky pressed down on Hollowbridge like a lid. And far to the southeast, Ansel would have sworn—though they wrote nothing of this, some instincts keeping even from paper—that they could feel it. A pull. Faint as a hook set behind the navel. Patient as hunger.

Something knew a ledger-keeper had kept a page.

Something was starting to turn its attention north.
