## 5. The Girl Who Heard Silence

Wren had not slept in three days, and the silence would not let her.

It lived in the guildhall now the way damp lived in stone—everywhere at once, patient, seeping into the joints of things. The others moved through it as though it were nothing. They lit lamps against it, poured tea over it, argued about ledgers and wards and whose fault the whole catastrophe was, and Wren sat in the corner of the kitchen with her knees drawn up to her chest, listening to the hole where the stars used to be.

Her grandmother had called it the under-song. *Everything that shines sings*, she'd said, turning a hazel wand between her knotted fingers while the fire spat in the hearth of their croft above the fen. *The sun sings loudest—brassy, boastful, all trumpets. The moon keeps time like a drum. But the stars, little wren-bird, the stars are the choir. Thousands upon thousands of small silver voices, each one different, none of them trying to be heard above the rest. That's what singing is supposed to be.*

Wren had been six years old, and she had believed her grandmother the way children believe—the way that is not belief at all but simply the shape of the world.

*And when you learn to listen,* Gran had said, tapping Wren's chest with one finger, right over the heart, *you'll hear them here too. And then you'll never be lonely again, no matter where they put you.*

They had put her plenty of places after that. The workhouse at Marrowfen when Gran's lungs went bad in the wet season. The hedge-witch who took apprentices for the price of their labor and paid in bruises disguised as lessons. The road, after. Always the road. But through all of it, Wren had carried the choir with her, a faint bright shimmer at the edge of every silence, and she had never once been truly alone.

Until four nights ago, when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out.* All of them, everywhere, at once, like a thousand candles pinched between the same invisible thumb and forefinger. And with them went the song—not muffled, not quieted, but *gone*, leaving behind a silence so complete it had texture, weight, an appetite. Wren had been sleeping rough in a ditch outside Hollowmere when it happened, and she had come awake screaming, because the silence had felt like hands closing around her ears from inside her own skull.

She'd thought, in those first mad minutes, that she was dying. Then she'd understood something worse: the stars weren't dead. Dead things stop singing because there's nothing left to sing. This was different. This was a silence that *leaned*. A silence with a shape to it, a hunger, a direction. It pressed inward from everywhere, and somewhere far off—south, always south, toward the capital—it gathered itself like a throat preparing to swallow.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath it, something sharper—ink gone metallic, the scent of memory stored too long in too-small vessels. Wren had been there two days now, brought in off the street by a woman named Vess who ran the place with the brisk mercy of someone triaging a battlefield. Vess had looked at Wren's mud-caked boots and hollow cheeks and asked exactly one question: "You can hear them, can't you? The wards."

"The wards are screaming," Wren had said. "Or they were. Now they're just—" She'd searched for the word and found only the true one. "Empty."

Vess's face had done something complicated and settled on grim. "Then you'll earn your keep telling us what else you hear. There's bread in the kitchen. Don't touch the ledger room."

So Wren ate bread and kept to corners and listened, because listening was the one thing she could not stop doing even if she'd wanted to, which some nights she did.

The guild was nothing like she'd imagined. She'd expected thieves to be loud—swaggering, laughing, quick-fingered people who took what they wanted and burned what they didn't. Instead the Hollow Guild moved like a household of librarians who happened to burgle. They spoke softly. They catalogued. They handled other people's memories in glass bottles and wax-sealed boxes with the reverence of relic-keepers, and Wren, watching them, felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name homesickness.

And among all of them, one person who watched her back.

The ledger-keeper. Ansel. Wren couldn't tell if they were a man or a woman or something that had deliberately sanded both away, and she suspected that was the point—that Ansel had spent years making themselves into a question no one bothered to answer. They had gray eyes that never quite landed on anything, the gaze of someone perpetually looking slightly past the world, and hands that moved with a strange double care, as though everything they touched might be either precious or armed. When the vault ruptured and memories came screaming home to their owners, everyone else had panicked beautifully. Ansel had simply stood in the wreckage of the ledger room, holding one page, reading it over and over as though it were a letter from a dead friend.

Tonight Ansel came into the kitchen past midnight, moving quietly, and stopped when they saw Wren in her corner.

"You're still awake," Ansel said. Not an accusation. An observation, delivered in a voice like a door left carefully ajar.

"The silence won't let me sleep," Wren said.

Something flickered across Ansel's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "You hear it too, then. Not just absence."

"It's not absence." Wren uncurled enough to sit cross-legged, and gestured at the window, where the sky hung black and starless like a lid. "Absence is when something ends. This is when something is being *taken*. Continuously. Right now. You can't hear it?"

"I hear quiet." Ansel pulled out the chair across from her but didn't sit, hovering the way people do when they haven't decided whether they're staying. "I assumed that was all there was to hear."

"No." Wren shook her head, and the words came up out of her before she could weigh them, the way they always did when the under-song was involved. "Quiet is a resting thing. This is a *feeding* thing. My grandmother taught me to listen before she taught me my letters. She said everything that shines sings, and the stars are the choir, and if you're very still you can hear—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "There's a hedge-song. Old. Older than the fens. It's not really a song, more a way of tuning yourself, opening your ears wide enough to hold the sky. I've sung it every night since I was six years old. Two nights ago I sang it and the silence sang *back*."

Ansel sat down. Slowly, the way a man sits down when his legs have decided without consulting him.

"What did it say?" they asked.

"That's the wrong question." Wren leaned forward, and the candle between them guttered as though leaning in too. "Songs don't say. Songs *want*. And this one wants everything. Every warm thing, every remembered thing, every shining thing. It's not empty, Ansel. Emptiness doesn't pull. This is a mouth. A vast, patient mouth, and it has been eating for a long, long time—and we've only just now noticed because it finally swallowed something big enough to feel."

"The stars," Ansel said.

"The stars," Wren agreed. "Or whatever fed them. Because here's the part that's been sitting in my stomach like a swallowed stone: stars don't just go out. Something drank them. And whatever drinks that much light doesn't stop at light."

* * *

They talked until the candle drowned in its own wax.

Wren told Ansel about the fens, about Gran's croft with its bundles of dried mugwort hanging from the rafters, about the night her grandmother died and the last thing the old woman did was press two fingers to Wren's ear and whisper, *listen for what's missing, bird-girl; the missing things scream loudest.* She told Ansel about the hedge-song itself—or tried to. Some of it wasn't words. It was breath-patterns, a rhythm of inhale and hold and release that opened the inner ear to frequencies most people's minds slammed shut against. She hummed a fragment of it now, low, and saw Ansel go rigid in their chair.

"What?" she whispered. "What did you feel?"

"Like someone walked over my grave," Ansel said. "But the grave was mine, and I was in it, and something down there was asking me to come home." Their gray eyes fixed on her with the first real focus Wren had seen in them. "Do that again."

She did. And this time she watched Ansel's face while she sang, and what she saw frightened her more than the silence ever had: recognition. Deep, involuntary, painful recognition, the look of someone hearing a language they'd forgotten they knew.

"You've heard it before," Wren said. "Somewhere. Before the guild."

"I don't remember anything before the guild." Ansel said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the weather. But their hand had drifted to their own sternum, pressing lightly, as though checking whether something was still there. "That's rather the point of me. I'm the ledger-keeper. I keep everyone's memories except my own. I sold them off years ago, piece by piece, rent by rent. Whatever I knew once, I traded."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," Wren said, and then clapped a hand over her mouth, because she hadn't meant to say it aloud, and because Ansel was—of all things—smiling.

"Probably," Ansel agreed. "But it worked. Nothing hurts you if there's nothing left to hurt." The smile faded. "Except lately I keep finding holes where feelings should be. I'll reach for a memory of a place and find the edges of it, the shape of having known it, without the middle. Like a moth-eaten coat. And tonight, when you sang—" They exhaled slowly. "Tonight I heard something in one of the holes. Singing back."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs, a bottle of somebody's first kiss sat in a shattered vault, and its owner was probably weeping or raging or both, and the world outside the window was a black page with nothing written on it, and Wren found herself saying, with terrible certainty:

"It knows you. Whatever's out there doing the eating—it knows you. Or it knows what it took from you. Those holes in your memory aren't damage, Ansel. They're *bites*."

Ansel went very still. Stillness, Wren was learning, was this person's native language—the stillness of prey animals and professional thieves and people who had practiced disappearing so long they could do it sitting in a chair. But underneath the stillness, something had kindled. Wren could see it the way she could see wardlight: faint, stubborn, refusing the dark.

"My grandmother said the missing things scream loudest," Wren went on, softer now. "I think your missing things have been screaming for years. And I think whatever's wearing the silence heard them."

* * *

Dawn did not come gold. It came gray and thin, a grudging concession, and the starless sky paled to the color of old bone. Wren stood at the kitchen window watching it, exhausted past tiredness into a strange clear second wind, when Ansel spoke from the doorway.

"Vess wants to send riders south. To the capital. Quietly. To find out why the royal wards failed the same night the stars did." A pause. "I volunteered."

Wren turned. "You hate leaving the guild. Everyone says so. You're the one who stays, who counts, who keeps the books balanced."

"I know what I am." Ansel's mouth twisted. "That's precisely why I have to go. If the missing things scream loudest, then mine have been screaming longest, and they're pointing south like compass needles." They hesitated, and then did something Wren had never seen them do: they asked for something. "Come with me."

"Why me? You barely know me. I showed up two days ago smelling like a ditch."

"Because you're the only person in this building who can hear the mouth." Ansel held up one hand, counting off fingers with dry precision. "Because you sang a song tonight that made a man with no childhood remember he had one. Because Vess trusts you, which means someone sane does. And because—" the third finger paused mid-air, and Ansel seemed genuinely surprised by their own honesty— "because when you talked about your grandmother teaching you to listen, I felt something in one of my holes move. And I want to know what it is before it kills me."

Wren looked at this forgettable, careful, hollowed-out person offering partnership like a hand across a chasm, and she thought of Gran's fingers on her ear, and the choir she'd lost, and the mouth that had swallowed it.

"If we do this," she said, "we do it my way on the road. We listen before we walk. We sing before we fight. And when I tell you a silence is hungry, you believe me the first time."

"Done," said Ansel.

"And you teach me the ledger," Wren added. "If we're going to hunt something that eats belonging, I want to know how this guild writes belonging down. Maybe the answer's in your pages, Ansel. Maybe it's been in your pages all along."

For a moment Ansel said nothing at all. Then, quietly, in the gray light of a starless dawn: "Page forty-one. There's a royal seal on it, forty years old. I've been staring at it for four nights." Their eyes lifted to hers, and Wren saw the fear in them finally take a shape she could work with. "I think whatever wore the crown that sealed it is still wearing it now. And I think it's getting hungry again."

Wren shouldered her grandmother's satchel, and together they went to read the screaming.
