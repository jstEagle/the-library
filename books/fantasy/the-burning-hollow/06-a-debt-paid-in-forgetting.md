## 6. A Debt Paid in Forgetting

The ferryman's boat smelled of tar and old rain, and his eyes were the color of a river in winter—pale, patient, and cold all the way down.

"Passage to Greywater Landing," Ansel said, "and then the post-road south. Two travelers."

The ferryman looked past them at Wren, who stood a careful distance back with her grandmother's satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, and then he looked at Ansel again, and his mouth did something that was almost a smile.

"I don't take coin," he said. "Coin's for people who still trust tomorrow. I take weight off the soul instead." He tapped his temple with one gnarled finger. "You're guild. I can smell it on you. Ledger-keeper, aren't you? You'll know how this works better than most."

Ansel did know. That was the trouble. They had spent eleven years recording trades exactly like this one—the trembling hand of the seller, the careful cataloguing of what would be taken, the small ritual phrases that made theft sound like commerce. *One memory, freely given, of sound mind and knowing heart.* They had written those words so many times they had worn grooves in their own mind saying them.

They had never once been on the selling side of the ledger.

"How much?" Ansel asked.

"For two, downriver and onto the south road? One memory. Something with weight to it. Nothing thin." The ferryman settled back against his tiller, unhurried, a man who had done business with the desperate since before either of them was born. "I'll know if you try to pass me something hollow. Everyone tries, first time. They hand me what they think is worthless—a Tuesday, a chore, a face they never liked—and I take it, and it weighs nothing, and they're still standing on my bank when we're halfway across the water because the fare wasn't paid." He shrugged. "I put them back on shore. Most of them cry."

Wren made a small sound behind Ansel. When Ansel glanced back, she was watching the ferryman with that tilted-head attention she gave to things that screamed too quietly for other ears. "He's full of them," she said softly. "All the memories he's eaten. They're singing down there in him. Hundreds of them. Thousands." Her eyes came up to Ansel's, wide and troubled. "It doesn't hurt them, exactly. But they're not *home* anymore."

"They're paid fares," the ferryman said mildly. "That's all anyone is, in the end. Something somebody else carried for a while."

Ansel turned away from both of them and looked out at the river.

It ran gray under the starless sky, moving with the slow muscular patience of water that had been going somewhere since before there were names for places. Three days ago the stars had gone out. Two days ago the wards had died screaming. Last night Ansel had sat up with page forty-one and its forty-year-old royal seal, and had understood, finally, that the ache in the world had an address: the capital, the palace, the crown.

And between here and there lay three hundred miles of country where every wardstone was dead and every night came down black as a closed eye.

"We need the crossing," Ansel said quietly. It wasn't a negotiation. It was just the truth, said aloud so it couldn't be taken back.

"I know you do," the ferryman agreed. "Everyone who comes to my bank needs something. That's why I'm rich." He patted the tiller. "Take your time choosing. But choose true. I'll be able to tell."

* * *

Ansel walked up the bank alone, leaving Wren watching the water, and stood among the reeds with their hands in their pockets, and did the thing they were best at in all the world: they took inventory of themselves.

This was the discipline Vess had drilled into them from the beginning, back when Ansel had been a scrap of a child with nothing but a name and a talent. *A thief of memory must know their own vault better than any client's,* the Guildmaster had said. *Otherwise you'll sell your own foundations by accident, one bad winter at a time, until there's no one left inside you to keep the books.*

So Ansel stood in the reeds and opened the shelves.

There was the taste of the guild kitchen's burnt porridge, which they kept because it was the first meal anyone had ever given them without asking for anything back. There was the smell of Vess's office—ink and lamp oil and dried lavender, which the old woman burned against headaches. There was Brannic swearing at a lock in four languages, Sefa laughing with ink on her nose, old Ivo asleep in his chair with a book open on his chest, rising and falling, rising and falling.

There was the market night, last autumn, when the whole guild had crowded onto the roof to watch lanterns rise, and Wren hadn't been with them yet, and Ansel had thought—had actually thought—that this was as good as life got, and had been grateful, and had been afraid of the gratitude, because everything Ansel had ever been grateful for had been taken away once already.

Ansel's hands had gone still in their pockets.

Because there it was. The shelf they never opened. The one at the very back, behind the ledgers and the trade-craft and the careful professional emptiness, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord and labeled, in their own childish handwriting from twenty years ago, with a single word:

*Mother.*

* * *

She had sold them on a Tuesday.

That was the detail that survived everything, the nail the whole memory hung on. Not the hunger, though there had been hunger; not the road, though the road had been long and dust-colored; not even her face, which had gone soft-focus years ago, worn away by Ansel's own careful editing, because some things a child cannot look at directly and survive. But the Tuesday remained. Market day in a town whose name Ansel had also let go, deliberately, the way you let go of a rope over a long drop.

She had held their hand walking in. She had bought them a honey-bun with the last of a coin purse that jingled empty after. And then there had been a man with a guild badge, and a conversation conducted slightly above Ansel's head, in the low tones adults used for transactions they were ashamed of, and Ansel had understood—not the words, but the shape of them, the shape of being *priced*—and had looked up at their mother's face and seen her crying, and had thought, with the terrible clarity of a six-year-old: *she's sad, but she's still doing it.*

The honey-bun had been the payment. Ansel had understood that too, later, when they were old enough to understand what the guild paid for children with useful talents. A sweet thing, eaten in the last minutes of belonging, so the last taste of home would be sugar.

Vess had been kind. That was the joke of it, the wound under the wound. Vess had knelt down in the guild's entry hall, in the smell of ink and lavender, and said, "What's your name, little one?" and Ansel had told her, and she had written it in a ledger, and then she had said, gently, "Would you like to keep it? Or would you rather it stayed here, safe, where no one can use it against you?"

And Ansel—who had just watched their entire world get folded up and priced and handed over—had looked at the ledger, at the neat line where their name now lived as an entry, an asset, a *thing recorded*, and had made the choice that built every wall they ever stood behind.

"Keep it," they had said. "I don't want it."

Not because they didn't love it. Because loving it had gotten them sold. Because a name was a handle, and handles were for people who wanted to carry you somewhere, and Ansel had decided, at six years old, in a hall that smelled of lavender, that they would rather be carried by no one ever again.

They had chosen forgetting as armor. Piece by piece, year by year, they had traded away or buried or filed into darkness everything that could be used as a handle. Their mother's face. Their town. The sound of their own name spoken in a voice that loved it. They had made themselves a person with no loose ends, no debts of the heart, no doors that anyone knew how to knock on.

And then, without noticing it happening—because that was the trick of it, the slow tide of it—they had accumulated a new set of handles anyway. Brannic's gruffness. Sefa's cheating. Ivo's stories. Vess's lavender. And now Wren, standing down on the cold bank with her satchel and her hedge-song, listening to a stranger's stolen memories sing.

Ansel had sworn never to trade the found family. Never. It was the one clause in their private contract, the one page they'd have torn out of any ledger, the one thing they'd burn the guild itself to protect.

But the family wasn't what the ferryman wanted. The ferryman wanted *weight*, and Ansel knew—with the sick clarity of someone who has spent a lifetime weighing other people's souls—exactly which memory had the most of it.

* * *

"You've been up there a long time," Wren said, when Ansel came back down the bank. "I was about to come find you."

"It takes a while to choose."

"What are you going to give him?"

Ansel looked at her. She was younger than them by several years, sharp-faced and quick-eyed, with straw still in her hair from sleeping rough, and she had known the guild for five days and already fit into it like a missing piece clicking home. She trusted them. That was the worst part. Five days, and she trusted them, because Ansel had listened to her screaming wards and taken her seriously and given her soup, and apparently that was all it took to earn the kind of faith Ansel had spent twenty years making sure no one could place in them.

"Don't watch," Ansel said. "Go talk to the ferryman about the river. Ask him about the currents below the weir. He'll like that."

Wren's eyes narrowed. She was not stupid; that was rapidly becoming one of Ansel's problems. "What are you giving him?"

"A memory."

"Which one?"

"One that weighs enough." Ansel heard their own voice go flat and professional, the ledger-keeper's voice, the voice that made theft sound like commerce, and hated it, and used it anyway. "It's fine, Wren. This is what I do. I've just always been on the wrong side of the desk."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." Ansel managed something that was almost a smile. "Ask him about the weir."

She went, slowly, glancing back twice. Ansel waited until she was deep in conversation with the ferryman—he was pointing downstream, gesturing at the white water, and whatever he was saying had pulled her attention clean away—and then Ansel walked to the water's edge and opened the oilcloth at the back of their own vault.

*Mother.*

Not her face. That was gone, edited away long ago, mercifully or cowardly, Ansel had never decided which. What remained was the rest of it, the parts that had survived every pruning because Ansel had never been able to bring themselves to cut them: the feel of her hand, dry and warm and slightly rough, holding theirs on the walk into town. The jingle of the coin purse. The honey-bun, sticky-sweet, the last taste of home. The sound of her crying—not loudly, not dramatically, just the small wet breathing of someone trying not to be heard by a child standing right beside her.

And underneath all of it, the foundation stone, the thing the whole memory was load-bearing for: the knowledge of what it felt like to belong to someone completely, before the world taught you better.

That was the weight. That was what the ferryman would taste and find sufficient. Twenty-three years of carrying it, and Ansel could have told him to the ounce what it weighed.

*Freely given,* Ansel recited silently, the ritual words turning to ash on the way down. *Of sound mind and knowing heart.*

They walked to the boat.

* * *

The ferryman stopped talking to Wren mid-sentence. He looked at Ansel coming down the bank, and his pale eyes did the thing that pale river-eyes do, which is show you nothing while taking everything in.

"Well, then," he said. "You've chosen."

"I've chosen."

"Heavy?"

"Heavy enough."

He extended one hand, palm up, an open question. "Say it, then. Proper words. I don't take what isn't given."

Ansel stepped into the boat. It rocked, steadied. The river moved beneath them, patient and enormous, going where it had always been going. Somewhere far to the south, past the weir and the landing and the three hundred miles of dark country, a king was wearing a crown that ate him alive, and a seal on a forty-year-old page was the only map anyone had.

"One memory," Ansel said. "Freely given. Of sound mind and knowing heart."

"And do you give it knowing what it is? Knowing what it holds?"

Ansel closed their eyes.

"Yes."

The ferryman's fingers touched Ansel's temple, light as a moth landing, and the memory came loose.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscenity of it. Eleven years of recording trades and Ansel had never once understood, truly understood, that it didn't hurt—it *emptied*. The hand, the coin purse, the honey-bun, the small wet breathing, the belonging—all of it slid out through the point of contact like water through open fingers, and where it had been there was not pain but absence, a hole shaped exactly like a mother's hand, and Ansel felt their knees soften and caught themselves on the gunwale and heard, from very far away, Wren shouting their—

Shouting something. Shouting a word that meant nothing. A sound. Someone on the bank calling a sound after them, alarmed, and Ansel turned toward it and felt the hole pull wider, because whoever it was, whatever it was, the name attached to that voice had been part of the same shipment, hadn't it, part of the same weight, the belonging and the being-called-something-by-someone, and the ferryman's hand was already withdrawing, satisfied, and the river was already moving, and the bank with the shouting figure on it was already sliding away into the gray.

"There," the ferryman said comfortably, taking up his oars. "Paid in full. You see? Easy as breathing."

Ansel sat down heavily on the thwart. Their hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against their knees and made them stop, because that was a skill too, one of the earliest ones, and some skills survive even when the reasons for learning them don't.

Something was wrong. They knew something was wrong the way you know a tooth is missing—by the tongue finding the gap, again and again, unable to leave it alone. There was a hole in them with a shape. A hand-shaped hole. A *mother*-shaped hole, except the word arrived without anything attached to it, a label on an empty drawer, and Ansel sat in the moving boat and grieved something they could no longer name, and understood, with the last of the ledger-keeper's cold clarity, that this was what every client had felt, walking out of the guild with their receipt, lighter and richer and robbed.

"Hey." Wren crouched in front of them, gripping the sides of the boat as it swayed. Her face was fierce and frightened. "Look at me. What did you give him? What did you *give him*, Ansel?"

Ansel opened their mouth to say *nothing important*—the professional reflex, the armor answering before the person could—and discovered that the reflex worked fine but the sentence underneath it had fallen through the hole. There was nothing important to protect. Whatever it had been, it was on the ferryman's side of the water now, singing down in him with all the others.

"I don't remember," Ansel said. And then, because Wren deserved better than the flat lie, and because the hole ached worse when they lied around its edges: "That's not a figure of speech. I chose something. I knew what it was when I chose it. And now I know only that I chose, and that it mattered, and that it's gone." Their voice came out level, which was somehow the worst thing about it. "This is what I do to people, Wren. I've watched them walk out that door a hundred times, and I wrote down what they lost in neat columns, and I never once—" The level voice cracked, just barely, a single hairline fracture. "I never once knew what the columns *weighed*."

Wren stared at them. Then she did something Ansel had no defense against, because it required no handle, no name, no memory, nothing that could be sold or stolen: she reached out and took their shaking hands and held them, both of hers around both of theirs, warm and rough and slightly—no. Not slightly rough. Just warm. Just warm and holding on.

"Whatever it was," she said, "you gave it up to go save a king you've never met. That's not nothing. That goes in the ledger."

"The ledger's the problem," Ansel said. "Everything's in the ledger. That's how they find you. That's how they *price* you—"

"Then we'll start a different kind of book." She said it simply, the way she said everything, hedge-witch plain, a girl who had learned early that the oldest magic was just paying attention and refusing to look away. "One that doesn't sell anybody. Now sit still and drink your water, because you've gone gray as the river, and I'm not explaining to Vess why I let you drown yourself for a boat ride."

Ansel drank the water. The boat moved south. Behind them the ferryman rowed back toward his bank, richer by one mother's hand, one honey-bun, one small wet breathing in the dark, and ahead of them the country unrolled, starless and unwarded, three hundred miles of it between here and the crown.

Ansel sat with the hole in them and let the grief move through it like wind through a broken window, and did not look away, and did not file it, and did not write it down.

Some entries, they were beginning to understand, you carried instead.

By late afternoon the weir appeared ahead, white water roaring under a sky the color of dirty wool, and Wren, who had been quiet for an hour, suddenly sat up straight with her head cocked like a bird's.

"Ansel," she said. "Listen."

And beneath the roar of the falls, faint and rising, Ansel heard it too: a sound like a struck bell going silent, stretched across the whole horizon—the same wrong note the border wards had made before they died.

Except this one was coming from the south.

From the capital.
