## 7. The Hollow Court

Ansel had stolen enough faces to know when one didn't fit.

They stood in the shadow of a wine-seller's awning on the Processional Way of Carreth Amal, capital of the Nine Valleys, and watched the king ride by in a litter of white silk and gilded cedar. The crowd roared. Bells rang from the Sept of the Turning Year. Petals fell from balconies in soft pink drifts, thrown by hands that would not remember throwing them.

The Harvest Festival of the Long Light—except the light was wrong this year. It came down thin and gray through a sky scrubbed clean of stars, as if the heavens themselves had been wiped like a slate. Even at midday, the sun seemed to be shining through cloth.

*Look at his face,* Ansel told themself, and looked.

King Aldous the Third, called the Beloved, called the Patient, forty years on the throne. He was not an old man—or he should not have been. He'd been crowned young; Ivo's stories said so, though Ivo's stories were mostly drink and grief. And yet what rode past Ansel now was a face like paper left out in rain: features present, correct, arranged in the proper places, but softened somehow, blurred, as if a hundred painters had each copied him from memory until nothing sharp remained.

He smiled. That was the worst part. He smiled beautifully—a king's smile, practiced across four decades—and it did not reach his eyes. It reached nowhere at all. It sat on his mouth like a mask strapped over emptiness, and behind it something *looked out*, patient and vast and terribly interested in the crowd.

Ansel had spent their life around hunger. They knew its shapes: the desperate hunger of a debtor trading her wedding day for rent money, the cold hunger of Vess pricing a memory before purchase, the quiet hunger they carried themself, always, for a thing they refused to name. But this was none of those. This was appetite without want, desire without direction—the hunger of a thing that ate because eating was all it knew how to do.

The litter passed. The crowd surged after it, laughing, weeping, holding up children who would grow old and never recall their father's king.

"Excuse me," Ansel said to a baker's wife beside them, a round cheerful woman still waving her flour-dusted hand. "What does His Majesty look like? I arrived late—I couldn't see."

She turned to answer, and Ansel watched it happen. Watched her open her mouth. Watched her eyes slide sideways, reaching for the image she'd held not ten heartbeats ago, and find—nothing. A smooth blank place where a face should have been.

"He looks—" she began, and stopped. Her brow creased. She laughed, embarrassed. "Isn't that strange. I was *right there*. I waved at him." She waved again, demonstrating, at the empty road. "He looks like a king," she decided finally, with the flat certainty of someone reciting from a lesson. "He looks exactly like a king ought to look."

"Yes," Ansel said quietly. "Exactly."

They melted back into the awning's shade and found Wren already there, pressed against the wine-seller's shuttered stall, both hands flat against the wood as if steadying herself against a current only she could feel.

"You saw it too," she whispered.

"I saw a man wearing a smile that belonged to someone else."

Wren shook her head slowly. Her hedge-witch senses had been frayed raw since the night the stars went out; she flinched at bells now, at crowds, at anything loud enough to drown the silence underneath. "It's not a man," she said. "Or—it is, but there's hardly any man left in it. Like a coat worn so long the body inside forgets its own shape." Her fingers curled against the stall. "When the litter passed, everything got quieter. All these people, thousands of them, and the world went *quiet*, like the moment before snowfall. Something drank the noise right out of the air."

Ansel glanced at the crowd, still cheering, still joyful, still hollowing. "And no one remembers his face."

"No one remembers his face," Wren agreed. "Because there's nothing left to remember. He's been eaten from the inside out, Ansel. Slowly. For years." She looked up, and her green eyes were wet and furious. "Forty years, maybe. Since the last time anyone wrote down what he actually looks like."

That landed harder than Wren could know. Ansel thought of the ledger page hidden against their ribs—the royal seal, forty years old, the coronation record Vess had ordered burned and Ansel had secretly kept. *Stubborn curiosity,* they'd called it. It felt more like fate now, or a debt coming due.

"We need to see the court itself," Ansel said. "Not the king. The people around him. If he's being devoured, someone has to be feeding it—or at least watching it happen."

"And how do two strangers get into the royal court during a festival?"

Ansel allowed themself the smallest smile. "We're thieves, Wren. Getting in is the honest part of our work."

* * *

Wren hated cities. She had hated them since childhood, since her grandmother first took her to market in Millbrook and the girl had covered her ears and wept, because cities never stopped *singing*—the low drone of ten thousand small memories layered over each other, love and grudge and gossip and grief, humming off every wall like heat off summer stone.

Carreth Amal should have been deafening.

Instead it was the quietest place Wren had ever stood, and that was worse than any scream.

She walked beside Ansel through the festival crowds toward the palace district, and she listened the way her grandmother had taught her—not with ears but with the place behind her breastbone where the hedge-song lived. In Millbrook, walking this street would have been like wading through warm surf. Here—

Here the great houses sang only fragments. A doorman remembered his duties but not his daughter's wedding. A lady in a silk hood hummed a lullaby and could not say whose child it had been for. Whole songs with holes punched through them, notes missing, verses gone, and in every hole the same silence: vast, smooth, *satisfied*.

"It's feeding on the whole city," she murmured. "Not just the king."

"Not just the king," Ansel confirmed. They moved like water through the crowd, unremarkable, instantly forgotten by everyone they brushed past—that old guild trick Wren still found uncanny, watching people's eyes slide off Ansel as if off rain-slick glass. "But the king most of all. Feel it?"

She did. As they climbed the terraced streets toward the palace, the silences deepened. By the time they passed under the Gate of Grateful Years—its arch carved with a thousand names of citizens the crown had honored, half the names now illegible, worn smooth as if licked away—the song of the city had thinned to a thread. And beneath the thread, Wren could hear the other thing. The thing she'd heard the night the stars died.

The hunger. Patient as winter. It wasn't roaring. That was what her grandmother's warnings had never prepared her for. In the hedge-tales, monsters howled and gnashed. This thing didn't need to. It simply *was*, the way a drought is, the way rot is, taking and taking without malice because taking was its whole nature.

And threaded through the hunger, faint as a pulse under ice, something else. A voice. A man's voice, very tired, repeating something over and over like a prayer worn down to three words.

She grabbed Ansel's sleeve. "Stop. Listen."

"I don't hear—"

"*Listen.*" Wren closed her eyes and let the song come through her, let it move her lips. "It's saying its name. Or trying to. *Aldous. Aldous. Aldous.* Over and over. Like—" her voice cracked— "like someone calling for help in a language the monster doesn't understand. He's still in there. Underneath. He's still *himself*, some little piece, and he's been shouting his own name for forty years so he won't forget it."

Ansel was silent a long moment. When Wren opened her eyes, she saw something rare on that forgettable face: naked anger, banked and burning.

"Then we're not just hunting a parasite," Ansel said softly. "We're answering a call. Forty years late, but answering it."

They passed through the outer courts of the palace on festival credentials Sefa would have sneered at—forged servant badges, good enough for the grounds, useless within. And here, among the courtiers gathered on the marble terraces for the Feast of the Long Light, Wren saw the true horror, the thing that made her grandmother's tales seem gentle.

The courtiers moved like sleepwalkers.

They danced, and the dancing was perfect—every step measured, every bow exact—but their faces were vacant as washed slate. A chamberlain offered wine with hands that trembled slightly, as if the body remembered grace while the man inside had gone somewhere far away. Two ladies laughed together at a joke neither could have repeated. A young lord stared into a fountain for so long that Wren followed his gaze and found he was watching his own reflection with an expression of polite puzzlement, as if trying to place where he'd seen that face before.

"They're not empty," she whispered. "That's the trick of it. They're *perforated*. Enough left to function. Enough left to serve. Everything else—" she swallowed— "everything else has been drunk."

A herald's trumpet split the air. On the high terrace above, beneath banners gone gray as ash, the king appeared again—on foot this time, to bless the harvest, to touch the ceremonial sickle to the season's first loaf.

The whole court knelt. Wren knelt too, head bowed, and through the lattice of her own hair she watched.

The blessing was flawless. Every word in its place, the cadence of centuries behind it. And the king's voice—when he spoke, the hungry silence *leaned in*, drinking even the sound of its own prisoner. Between phrases, in the pauses, Wren heard it again, so faint only she could have caught it: *Aldous. Aldous.* The name spoken like a man counting coins in the dark to be sure none had been stolen.

Then the king raised his head to bless the crowd, and for one instant his eyes swept the terrace and caught on Wren—caught and *held*, the way a drowning man's eyes catch on a distant sail.

His smile stayed fixed. His voice continued the blessing. But his eyes were screaming.

*You hear me,* those eyes said. *You hear me. Please. Please, whoever you are—*

The moment broke. The king's gaze slid on, vacant again, and the court rose as one, applauding the harvest, applauding the Long Light, applauding a man none of them could describe.

Wren's hands shook so badly she had to press them flat against the marble.

* * *

Ansel counted exits. It was habit older than memory—doors, windows, guard rotations, the rhythm of patrols—and it kept them sane as they withdrew from the terrace, Wren pale and silent at their side.

Three things they had learned on the palace grounds, and all three were wrong in ways that mattered.

First: the guards were wrong. Not corrupt, not lazy—*blank*. Their armor gleamed; their postures were immaculate; and behind their visors their eyes held nothing whatsoever. Ansel had seen Hollow Guild clients who'd traded away too much, people reduced to pleasant vacancy. These men and women were that, weaponized. Someone had stripped the royal guard down to obedience and dressed the emptiness in steel.

Second: the ledgers. Ansel had slipped into a records alcove while Wren kept watch, and found the palace accounting books for the festival—catering, candlewax, flower orders, all meticulously kept. And on every page, in the margins, in the same careful hand, small tally marks. Rows and rows of them, decades deep, like a miser counting coins. Counting *what*?

Third: nobody asked who they were. Not once. Servants stepped around them. Courtiers smiled through them. Even the captain of the gate had looked directly at Ansel's forged badge and then directly through it. Either the forgery was better than Sefa's usual work—which it was not—or the palace had simply stopped bothering to know its own people. What use was vigilance to a household that couldn't remember whom it served?

They regrouped in a gardener's shed at the edge of the maze gardens, among rakes and clay pots and the smell of cut grass, the first ordinary smells either of them had encountered all day. Wren sat down hard on an overturned bucket.

"He saw me," she said. "During the blessing. Ansel, he *saw* me. He's in there, and he knows I can hear him, and he—" Her breath hitched. "Forty years. Can you imagine? Screaming your own name in the dark for forty years?"

Ansel crouched in front of her, and for once did not bother making their face forgettable. "I can imagine forgetting," they said. "I've built my whole life on it. Sold pieces of myself to pay rent, wore my own name thin so no one could grip it." They paused. "But I chose the size of my losses. He never chose anything. That's the difference, and it's the whole difference."

Wren looked at them—really looked, the way few people ever managed to look at Ansel Lockridge—and nodded slowly.

"So what do we do?" she asked. "We can't fight a guard of hollow men. We can't walk into the throne room. We don't even know what we're fighting. A crown? A creature? A curse?"

"We know three things." Ansel held up fingers, ledger-keeper's habit. "One: it eats belonging—memory, identity, the ties between people. Two: it started with the king, or the king started it, forty years ago at his crowning. Three—" they touched their ribs, where the folded page lay hidden against skin— "there's a record. A coronation ledger, sealed and archived, from the one day this thing might have been weak, or new, or hungry in a way we could understand. Vess wanted it burned. I kept it anyway."

"That's insubordination."

"That's bookkeeping." Ansel almost smiled. "Every entry tells you where the debt lives. We go home, Wren. We read the page. And then we convene the others—Brannic, Sefa, Ivo, all of them—because whatever's wearing that crown, we cannot carry this alone."

Outside the shed, the festival bells began to ring the hour, bright and meaningless, and beneath them Wren heard the endless whisper of a starving king:

*Aldous. Aldous. Aldous.*

Somewhere in the vaults of the Hollow Guild, a page with a royal seal waited like a knife in a drawer. And Ansel Lockridge, who had spent a lifetime becoming no one, was about to discover what it cost to become someone worth remembering.
