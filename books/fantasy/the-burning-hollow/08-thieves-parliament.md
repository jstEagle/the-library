## 8. Thieves' Parliament

The guildhall had never been full in Ansel's lifetime. It had been built for a hundred thieves and their apprentices, back when the Hollow Guild ran the river roads from the border marches to the sea, and now it held perhaps thirty souls rattling around in its bones like pebbles in an empty jar. The great hearth was lit for the first time in years—Vess had ordered it, though she'd said nothing about why—and the firelight climbed the smoke-blackened rafters where old guild-marks were carved: the open hand, the closed ledger, the moth that ate memory and left only silk.

Ansel stood at the head of the long table and felt every eye like a weight placed carefully on a scale.

They had come because Ansel had asked, and that alone should have frightened them. Ansel Lockridge did not ask. Ansel Lockridge appeared at your elbow with exactly what you needed and vanished before you could say thank you, leaving you uncertain whether you'd dreamed them. That was the craft of it. That was the armor. But three nights ago the stars had gone out, and the vault had ruptured, and a client named Aldous Merrin had nearly died on the guildhall floor when forty years of borrowed courage came home to him all at once—and Ansel had stopped being able to pretend the world would let them stay invisible.

"Thank you for coming," Ansel said, and someone snorted.

It was Brannic, of course. Brannic the lock-wright sat with his arms crossed over a chest like a beer barrel, his grey beard singed at the edges from some recent experiment, his hands resting on the table as if they might otherwise get bored and start dismantling something. "You thanked us," he said. "Mark the date, Sefa. Write it down. The ledger-keeper said *thank you*."

"I'll forge it into scripture," Sefa said. She was sprawled sideways across two chairs at the far end, boots up, a half-eaten apple balanced on her knee, her red hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual quill. "Saint Lockridge of the Quiet Feet. Patron of people who owe you money."

"You owe *me* money," Ansel said.

"See? Sainted already."

The laughter that moved through the room was thin and grateful. Ansel understood it for what it was—the sound people make when they've been afraid too long and someone finally gives them permission to be something else for a moment. Let them have it. There would not be much to laugh about after tonight.

At the table's other end, apart from everyone, sat old Ivo. He was the oldest thing in the guildhall except the stones themselves, a small dried-apple of a man wrapped in three coats despite the fire, and he was watching Ansel with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. Ivo remembered too much. It was a condition, the healers said, a sickness of the mind that kept everything, threw nothing away, until his skull must have been as crowded as the vault had been. He remembered the coronation. He'd told Ansel so, twice, in the same words, and both times the telling had made the hair on Ansel's arms stand up.

And at the high seat, where she had not sat since taking the guildmaster's chain, Vess stood with her hands flat on the wood and her face like a door that had been nailed shut.

"You called this parliament," she said. Not a question. Her voice carried the particular flatness of a woman conserving her anger the way other people conserved coin. "Under guild law, any member may convene one. So here we are. Say your piece, Lockridge, and then we'll see whether this hall still answers to me or to sentiment."

* * *

Ansel had rehearsed this speech four times in the dark of their cell above the counting room. They had chosen their words the way they chose everything—carefully, sparingly, each one load-bearing. Standing here now, with the fire cracking and thirty faces turned toward them, they abandoned the rehearsal entirely and simply told the truth, which was the one performance they had never practiced.

"The wards are dead." Ansel laid the wardstone fragment on the table—the one from the border march, the one Wren had wept over. It lay there dull as a river pebble, giving back the firelight without warmth. "Not damaged. Not weakened. Dead, the way a man is dead, which means something killed it. And whatever killed it is the same thing that pulled every stolen memory out of our vault and sent it home. I've been reading the return patterns. Memories don't drift home like birds, they're *pulled*, like iron to a lodestone, and the pull is getting stronger. In another week it won't just be memories. It'll be names. Faces. The reason you get up in the morning."

"You don't know that," said Tam Broadstairs, the fence, from the back.

"I know the king's festival in the capital went on for six days and not one person who attended can describe the king's face." Ansel let that sit. "I know the stars went out the same night our vault broke. I know there's a royal seal on a forty-year-old contract in our own ledgers—a payment, Guildmaster, made by the crown *to this guild*, and then struck from the books by every hand that touched it afterward. Someone paid us to forget something. And whatever we forgot is awake now, and hungry, and it lives in the palace."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the rafters the moth-mark caught the light and seemed, for a heartbeat, to move.

Vess spoke first, as Ansel had known she would. "You want to walk into the capital. Into the seat of a crown that has spent forty years buying silence, with a crew of—" she gestured, and the gesture took in Sefa's boots, Brannic's singed beard, the whole improbable assembly— "*this*. You want me to sanction it."

"I want you to look at the ledger page," Ansel said, and slid it down the table.

Vess did not touch it. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake one has known personally. "I've seen it. I burned my copy twenty years ago, as did the guildmaster before me, as did hers. That page is a debt the guild chose to stop collecting, Lockridge. Some doors you pay to keep shut."

"And now the door's open anyway," Ansel said. "The hinges are off. Whatever we paid to forget is standing in the room with us, wearing a crown, eating a kingdom one memory at a time. We can pretend we don't see it. But pretending is the only trade we've ever sold that doesn't come home."

"It comes home," said a voice from the shadows by the kitchen door, "when the wards fall."

Wren stepped into the firelight, and the room shifted the way rooms do when a stranger enters—not hostility, exactly, but the collective recalculation of a body of people who trusted no one new. She was small and sharp-faced, nineteen at most, with hedge-mud still under her nails and the hollow-eyed look of someone who had heard too much silence lately. She had been with the guild nine days. She had not spoken in the hall before.

"Go on," Ansel said gently.

Wren's hands worked at each other. "My grandmother sang the hedge-songs. The ones that keep the small dark things small. She said the world's protections weren't walls—they were *agreements*. Old ones. The wards didn't keep the hungry things out by force. They kept them out by remembering the terms." She swallowed. "When the border stone died, I heard the agreement end. Not break. *End.* Like a song stopping mid-verse because the singer forgot the words. Something ate the words." Her chin came up, and there was steel under the fear, the kind that gets forged young. "Your ledger-keeper is right. And your guildmaster is right too. It *is* suicide to go to the capital." A breath. "But staying here is dying slower, with better manners."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the ugliest, most honest talk the guildhall had heard in a generation.

Tam Broadstairs argued economics: the guild's clients were fleeing, the vault was a ruin, and a mission to the capital meant spending their last working capital on a ghost story. Marla of the Quay argued fear, more honestly than Tam: she had seen what a returned memory had done to Aldous Merrin, had held his head while he screamed in two voices, and she wanted no part of whatever could do that to a kingdom. Old Pell wanted to know who would run the ferry routes if the specialists all died in a palace. One by one they gave their reasons, and Ansel listened to all of them, and understood all of them, because every reason was a shape of the same thing: *we are small, and we survive by being small.*

Then Brannic stood up, and the room quieted, because Brannic arguing was weather and everyone knew to check the sky.

"I've opened eleven hundred locks in my life," he said. "Bank vaults, tomb gates, a cardinal's chastity belt, don't ask. And I'll tell you what I learned, free of charge, since it's apparently a night for generosity." He planted both fists on the table. "Every lock ever made was built by somebody who assumed the person trying to open it cared about their own skin. Thieves retreat. Thieves cut losses. That's the whole architecture of security—it's built on our cowardice." His eyes found Vess. "So the day we stop retreating, every lock in the world is just a puzzle. I'm sixty-one years old. My knees are shot. I've got no children and no intention of acquiring any. If the choice is die slow in a guildhall full of empty strongboxes or die stupid in a palace doing the one job nobody else can do—" he shrugged, vast and final— "I know which death has better stories."

"Stories are all anyone will have left," Sefa said, and for once there was no joke riding behind her voice. She sat forward, apple forgotten. "Do you know what I do? I make lies so good they become true. Papers that make a smuggler into a merchant. A name that makes a killer into a clerk. And I'm telling you—I can feel it *slipping*. Forgeries I made last year, documents that fooled royal assessors, and last week I look at them and I can't remember which details I invented. My own lies are forgetting themselves." She laughed, but it came out wrong. "If the thing in the capital eats belonging, then forgery is the opposite trade, isn't it? Making things belong that didn't. I want in. Professionally speaking, I find its whole methodology insulting."

One by one, then, the hall began to turn. Not all of it—never all of it—but Tam surprised everyone by saying his brother lived in the capital and he'd had no word from him in nine days; Marla said nothing at all but moved her chair to Ansel's side of the room, which in the grammar of the Hollow Guild was a signed contract; Pell grumbled that someone had to carry the lanterns and it might as well be him. Half the guild, by the time the candles had burned low, stood on the side of the room where Ansel stood. The other half stood where Vess stood, and the space between the two halves of the family was wide enough to drive a funeral cart through.

* * *

Vess waited until the others had gone. She dismissed even Wren, gently, with a look, and then it was only the two of them and the fire burning down to its red bones, and the guildmaster of the Hollow Guild finally sat in the high seat she'd been standing beside all night, as if sitting were the concession.

"My turn," she said. "No parliament. Just an old woman talking to the thief she raised."

Ansel said nothing. Vess had bought Ansel off a slaver barge at eight years old, a child sold for the price of two goats, and had never once mentioned it since—which was, in Vess's language, an act of enormous tenderness.

"You think I forbid this because I'm a coward," Vess said. "I forbid it because I signed the second copy of that contract. Forty years ago. I was your age. The guildmaster sent me to the palace with a sealed box and instructions not to ask what was inside, and I watched men in gold masks carry it down beneath the coronation hall, and I heard—" her jaw tightened— "I heard what was in the box. Singing. Like a kettle. Like something *pleased*. And the young king wept through his whole crowning, and everyone said it was piety, and I knew it wasn't, and I took the crown's gold and I came home and I helped burn the books." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I have spent forty years paying that debt, Lockridge. Every wardstone we maintained. Every hungry thing we turned away from the border. I thought I was keeping the terms of the agreement. Turns out I was feeding the thing I'd helped cage, and now the cage is open, and you want to lead my children into its mouth."

"They're not children."

"No," Vess agreed quietly. "That's the problem. Children obey." She rose, and took the guildmaster's chain from around her neck, and set it on the table between them with a click that sounded terribly final. "Half my guild is going to die in the capital. Maybe more. I can't stop them, because I taught them too well—taught them that debts come due, that locks are puzzles, that no one gets left holding the bag alone." At the door she paused, her back to Ansel, a straight narrow silhouette against the dying fire. "I'm not coming with you. Someone has to keep the hall standing for whoever crawls back. But take the wain in the east bay. Take the good rope. And Lockridge—" 

"Yes?"

"If you find out what we forgot," Vess said, "remember it for me. I don't trust myself to carry it anymore."

* * *

They gathered in the east bay at dawn, such dawn as there was—the sky a bruised grey that never quite committed to light anymore, the sun a pale coin behind gauze. Brannic loaded the wain with tools that looked innocent and were not. Sefa spread her forged papers across a hay bale like a general's maps. Ivo sat in the cart bed wrapped in his coats, having announced his inclusion with five words—"I remember the coronation. I'm coming"—and dared anyone to argue. Nobody had. Wren perched on the rail with her grandmother's songbook tied to her belt, watching the road south.

Ansel stood a moment at the edge of the yard, looking back at the guildhall—smoke rising from the chimney Vess had ordered lit, the moth carved in the rafter, the windows where the half that stayed were already moving about their morning as if the world were ordinary.

*We are small,* Ansel thought. *We have always survived by being small.*

But looking at them—Brannic cursing at an axle, Sefa laughing at him, Ivo asleep sitting up, Wren humming something old and sharp-edged against the dark—Ansel felt the truth of it settle like a keystone dropping into place: small things survive by scattering. Small things that *hold together* become something else entirely.

"Lockridge!" Brannic bellowed. "Are you driving or decorating?"

Ansel smiled, took up the reins, and turned the wain south toward the capital, toward the crown, toward the longest night either of them would ever know.

Behind them, unnoticed by all but old Ivo—who opened one milk-pale eye and said nothing—the last star in the grey sky flickered, guttered, and went out.
