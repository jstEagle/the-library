## 9. The King's Empty Years

Ivo had outlived three guildmasters, two wars, and every promise he'd ever made to die quietly in his bed, and he had learned one thing above all the rest: memory was not a possession. It was a tenant. It lived where it pleased, paid no rent, and came back through the walls when you'd long since boarded up the doors.

So when the stars went out and the vault ruptured and half of Hollow's Reach woke screaming with other people's childhoods in their mouths, Ivo did not panic. He sat by his window with a cup of barley tea gone cold in his hands and let the past come home to him, because he had never once tried to keep it out.

That was his affliction, as Vess liked to say. Old Ivo remembers too much.

He remembered the smell of tallow and wet wool in the coronation square forty years ago. He remembered the taste of a stolen meat pie, bought with a purse that wasn't his. He remembered being twenty-six years old and already a thief of the small, invisible kind—stealing minutes from employers, coins from careless lords, and once, memorably, an entire afternoon from a magistrate who would spend the rest of his life wondering why he felt he'd lost something on the fourth of Harvestmonth.

And he remembered the boy king.

* * *

They had called him Aldric then, before they called him anything else. A prince of nineteen summers, narrow through the shoulders, with his mother's dark eyes and his father's habit of pressing his thumbnail into the pad of his forefinger when he was afraid—a tell so small that only someone trained to watch for tells would ever have caught it. Ivo had been trained to watch for tells since he was seven years old. It was the first thing the guild taught: everyone leaks. Your work is to find the crack and pour yourself through it.

The plan that day had been simple, almost insultingly simple. During the crowning, while ten thousand eyes were fixed on the altar and the archpriest's raised hands, the Hollow Guild would lift the coronation regalia's twin set—the ceremonial duplicates kept in the undercroft beneath the square—and sell them to a Caldran collector who paid in gemstones the size of knucklebones. Brannic's predecessor, a lock-wright named Odo with fingers like tree roots, had already opened three of the four doors between the undercroft and the world. Ivo's job was the smallest one: to stand in the crowd, watch the king, and mark the exact moment the ceremony reached its peak—the moment every guard's attention would be nailed to the throne—so the crew below could move.

So Ivo stood in the press of bodies at the edge of the coronation square, close enough to see the boy's face clearly, and he watched.

He watched the archpriest intone the old words, the ones about burden and bloodline, the ones nobody listened to anymore. He watched the crown lifted on its cushion of white velvet, gold worked into the shape of intertwined branches, older than the dynasty that wore it—older, the scholars said, than any kingdom at all. He watched the boy kneel.

And he saw the moment the light changed.

It was not clouds. Ivo had spent enough years reading skies for escape routes to know cloud-shadow from anything else. This was different: a dimming that came from everywhere and nowhere, as if the whole day had been a lamp and some enormous hand had turned its wick down. The crowd murmured. The banners went grey. And overhead—Ivo looked up like everyone else, like ten thousand faces tilting back at once—the stars were visible. At midday. Faint as breath on glass, but there, scattered across a sky gone the color of pewter.

Then the crown came down onto the boy's head, and everything happened very fast and very quietly.

Aldric wept.

Not the dignified single tear that princes are coached to shed. He wept the way children weep, sudden and helpless, his whole face breaking open, his shoulders shaking under the coronation mantle. The archpriest faltered. The choir stumbled over its note. And the boy king stood there in front of his entire kingdom, crowned and crying, and said something—no one around Ivo heard it clearly, but Ivo read lips the way other men read ledgers, and what the boy said was:

*I don't want to forget her.*

His mother. Dead two winters past. That much even Ivo knew, because grief makes news the way spilled honey makes ants.

The dimming lasted, by Ivo's later count, the length of a sung verse. Then the light returned—not all at once but in a slow tide, as though something vast had finished drinking and lowered its mouth—and the stars faded back into nothing, and the ceremony ground forward to its end, and the crowd, being a crowd, decided within the hour that it had seen nothing unusual at all. Grief, they said. The poor lad missed his mother. Kings are only men.

But Odo came up from the undercroft that evening grey-faced and empty-handed, and the crew's take was nothing, because the duplicate regalia was already gone—had been gone, perhaps, for years, the vault behind the fourth door holding nothing but dust shaped like absence—and Odo said, in the low voice men use for things they don't want the gods to overhear: "There's something down there that eats. Not rats. Something patient."

The guild wrote it off. Guilds always write off the strange things; strangeness is bad for business. But Ivo did not write it off, because Ivo had been standing in the square when the stars came out at noon, and he had watched a boy be crowned and hollowed in the same motion, and he had spent forty years since then noticing how the stories about King Aldric grew thinner with each passing year.

* * *

That was the thing about the king that no one said aloud, the thing that lived in the pauses between court gossip: the stories didn't add up.

Forty years of reign, and what did the people of the capital actually know of him? That he was just. That he was calm. That he had ended the border feuds, stabilized the coin, rebuilt the granaries—achievements, all of them, but achievements without texture, without the grain of anecdote that makes a life. Every other monarch left behind a wake of stories: the time he got drunk and wrestled a bear, the time she disguised herself and danced at a commoner's wedding. Aldric left behind ledger entries. Peace treaties. Tax reforms. A reign like a well-kept account book, and Ivo, who had kept account books his whole crooked life, knew better than anyone what a well-kept account book could hide.

Worse: people couldn't hold his face. Ivo had tested this himself, over the years, in the way of a man who cannot stop picking at a knot. He would ask travelers, merchants, even a defrocked court chaplain he'd once fleeced at dice—describe the king. And they would reach for it and come back with almost nothing. Tall, maybe. Grey-eyed? No—brown. Young still, or old now? There was a portrait in the great hall, wasn't there? Wasn't there?

There was not. Ivo had checked. The gallery in the capital's public hall held kings going back nine generations, and between Aldric's father and the present day hung forty feet of blank wall that everyone walked past without ever quite seeing.

Forty years of a man wearing a crown, and the kingdom had forgotten his face the way you forget a dream: completely, and without ever noticing you'd forgotten.

Ivo had told himself a hundred explanations. Sorcery, certainly—but whose, and for what gain? A glamour to protect the king from assassins—sensible, even admirable. A curse laid by some enemy. Or simply the way of courts, which erode their own inhabitants like water erodes stone, until nothing is left but office.

But explanations are what you make when you don't yet know the answer, and Ivo had learned to hold his explanations loosely, the way you hold a knife you might need to put down fast.

Now, sitting in his cold room above the guildhouse with the stars gone black as a closed eye and the wards fallen like cut strings, he found that all his loose explanations were sliding toward a single knot.

* * *

The girl came to him on the second night after the vanishing.

She'd been sheltering with the guild since the wards first began to scream—the hedge-witch, Wren, all elbows and wary eyes, who heard things in the silence that sane people were spared. She appeared in the doorway of Ivo's room without knocking, which endeared her to him immediately; the young were too polite these days, and politeness was just fear wearing good manners.

"You remember the old songs," she said. It was not a question. Sefa had told her, apparently, that if anyone in the guild knew the deep lore, it was the old man who remembered too much.

"I remember everything," Ivo said. "It's my curse. Sit down, girl, before your legs give out. You look like a heron."

She sat on the edge of his cot like she expected it to bite her. Her eyes went to the window, to the starless dark beyond it, and he watched her flinch at it, the way you'd flinch at a wound.

"Grandmother taught me a song," Wren said. "About the stars. She said they weren't lights. She said they were *kept* things—that every star was somebody's memory, burning in the high places where nothing could eat it. And she said—" the girl's voice caught, steadied— "she said if the stars ever went out, it meant something had gotten into the keeping-place. Something hungry. She called it the Unbelonging." Her jaw tightened around the word like it tasted of iron. "I thought it was a story to scare hedge-children away from the barrow-mounds."

"And now?"

"Now I hear it," Wren whispered. "In the silence where the stars should be. It's not roaring, Ivo. That's the worst part. It's *content*. Like a man leaning back from a full table."

Ivo set down his cold tea. His hands, he noticed distantly, were not steady, and he was too old to pretend they were.

"Girl," he said, "forty years ago, I stood in a square and watched a boy be crowned, and the stars came out at noon like frightened birds, and the boy wept and said he didn't want to forget his mother. And then the light drank itself back down, and everyone decided they'd seen weather." He paused. "Everyone but me."

Wren stared at him. "You think it started then."

"I think nothing starts," Ivo said. "Things are invited. Things are fed. Forty years ago, something was bound to that crown—or bound *in* it, which is worse—and it has been fed ever since, patiently, spoonful by spoonful, on the one thing a king has more of than any man alive." He held up a gnarled finger. "Memory. A king is a reservoir of remembering. Every law he signs, every face he greets, every year of his reign—it all pools in him. The realm remembers *through* him. That's what a crown is, girl. Not gold. A vessel."

"The wards," Wren breathed. "The border wardstones. They're old—older than the kingdom—"

"Older than the kingdom, and renewed by it. Every coronation re-fed them. Every royal rite, every oath sworn on the throne—the wards drink the runoff of royal memory. That's the bargain underneath all the pretty ceremonies: the land keeps the king's belonging alive, and the king's belonging keeps the borders shut against whatever waits outside the world." Ivo's voice dropped. "And if something has been eating the king's memories for forty years—if the vessel has been drained drop by drop until there's nothing left but the shape of a man—then the wards haven't collapsed, child. They've *starved*."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the starless night pressing against the window glass.

"That's why the vault broke," Wren said slowly, working it through, and Ivo watched her mind move with something like pride—he'd known thieves all his life, and this girl had the best kind of thief's mind, the kind that couldn't leave a lock alone. "Stolen memories flying home. If the thing in the crown is starving, if it's eaten through its hoard—"

"Then it's reaching for new food," Ivo finished. "Every stolen memory in the world is unclaimed property to a thing like that. It pulled them loose to see what it could swallow. And when it finishes with the guild's leavings—" he met her eyes— "it will start taking memories that are still *worn*. First the city. Then the country. Names of children in the streets. Faces off portraits. Belonging itself, unwound."

Wren was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lost its wariness, gained a wire of resolve that made her sound suddenly older than her years.

"There's a hedge-song," she said. "For finding water underground. Grandmother used it to locate springs. She said it worked on anything that pooled—water, starlight, memory. If the king's memories weren't destroyed, if they were *kept*—hoarded somewhere beneath the palace like coin in a vault—"

"Then they can be stolen back," Ivo said, and felt the old thrill move through his chest, the one that had never once faded in seventy years of thieving: the click of a lock understanding itself. "Girl, you're talking to a guild of memory-thieves. Taking back what was taken is the only prayer we know how to say."

"It won't be that simple."

"Nothing worth stealing ever is."

* * *

After she left, Ivo sat by his window a long while, doing what old men do: arguing with the past.

Because there was one more thing he remembered from the coronation square, one more detail he had never told a living soul, and it sat in him now like a swallowed stone.

When the boy wept—when the light dimmed and the stars shivered into view—Ivo had felt something brush past him. Not wind. Wind moves cloth; this moved *through* cloth, through skin, through the warm animal house of his body, and for one heartbeat he had felt what it felt.

Hunger. Enormous, patient, and—this was the part that had haunted him for forty years—not cruel. Hunger is never cruel. Cruelty requires a self to be cruel *with*, a self that chooses. What had brushed him in that square was simpler and more terrible than cruelty: it was appetite without a self at all, a hunger that had eaten so long and so completely that nothing remained of it except the eating. It had passed through the crowd like a hand through smoke, tasting ten thousand minds in a single stride, and Ivo had understood, in that one borrowed heartbeat, exactly what it wanted.

Not power. Not worship. Not even memory, really.

It wanted to *have*. To hold. To belong to something, the way a miser belongs to his hoard—possessing everything, contained by nothing, loved by no one. It ate belonging because it had none, and every memory it swallowed was another room in a house it could never live in.

And now, forty years later, it had finished the king. It had drunk the vessel dry. And it had reached out into the dark with that same patient hand and pulled down the stars and broken the vaults and begun, at last and inevitably, to feed on everything.

Ivo rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and took down the old writing box from beneath his bed. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the notes he had made across four decades—the coronation, the missing regalia, the blank stretch of wall in the gallery, the chaplain who couldn't describe his own king, the wardstone renewals recorded in guild ledgers going back generations, each entry marked with the same small notation: *fed by crown rite.*

He had assembled the whole picture years ago, he realized. He had simply never believed anyone would act on it. Who marches on a palace over a theory? Who risks the guild's necks on the recollections of an old man who remembers too much?

But that was before the stars went out. Before the wards fell. Before a hedge-girl heard hunger singing in the silence, and a ledger-keeper who had made himself forgettable started asking questions with a royal seal on the page.

Ivo wrapped the notes back in their oilcloth and carried them down the stairs, slow step by slow step, toward the room where Ansel Lockridge kept the ledgers.

Let the others call it madness, he thought. Let Vess order them all to go dark and dig in like ticks. He had waited forty years for someone foolish enough and brave enough to pull this thread, and he was too old, and had stolen too much, to wait a single night longer.

At the bottom of the stairs, he paused, one hand on the banister, and looked back up through the stairwell's dark at the window where the starless sky pressed in.

"Hold on, boy," he said to a king who could not hear him, to a boy who had wept and begged not to forget. "We're coming to steal you back."

Then he went to find Ansel, and the notes in his arms felt lighter than they had in forty years—lighter, and warmer, like a coal finally handed to someone who would carry it into the dark.
