## 10. Crown of Teeth

The palace archive did not appear on any map of the capital that Ansel had ever stolen, bought, or memorized. That was the first thing they learned about it: it was a building the city itself had agreed to forget.

They found it by walking wrong.

Wren had a word for the technique—*hedge-walking*, she called it, though her grandmother had used a rougher term that made Wren blush to repeat it—and it amounted to this: you stopped trusting your eyes and started trusting the way a place *itched*. Every ward, every concealment, every elegant little spell of unnoticing left a seam, and seams could be felt if you dragged your attention across them slowly enough, like running a fingertip over wallpaper in the dark.

Ansel had spent twenty years making themselves unnoticeable by hand. They knew the craft from the inside out. So when the street bent where no street should bend—when the row of townhouses across from the chandler's shop repeated its third window twice, a stutter in the city's face—they stopped, and turned, and looked at the blank wall between the milliner's and the moneylender's until their eyes watered.

"There," Wren said softly. She stood beside them with her hood back despite the cold, her ears pink, her eyes half-closed as though listening to something very far away. "It's like a held breath. A big one. It's been holding it for years."

"Can you open it?"

"I can knock." Her mouth twitched. "Whether anyone answers is another question."

She pressed her palm flat against the blank stone and hummed three notes—the first three of the hedge-song, the ones she'd told them were older than the wards themselves—and the wall exhaled.

It wasn't dramatic. The stone didn't crumble or slide. It simply stopped insisting on its own blankness, the way a lie stops working when someone finally looks at it directly, and there was an archway where the wall had been, tall and narrow, breathing cold air scented with dust and tallow and something else beneath—something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a sealed room.

Ansel looked up and down the empty street. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"After you," they said.

"You're the thief."

"I'm the ledger-keeper. You're the one who talks to walls."

Wren grinned—that quick, crooked grin that Ansel was beginning to catalog without meaning to, the way they cataloged everything—and went through the archway first.

* * *

Inside, the archive was a cathedral built for no god Ansel recognized.

Shelves rose into darkness overhead, so far up that the lamplight couldn't find their tops, and every shelf was choked with ledgers, folios, scroll-cases, boxes of correspondence tied in ribbon gone gray with age. Millions of pages. Decades of a kingdom writing down everything it wanted to be sure of, all of it gathered here and then—abandoned. Not guarded. Not even dusted. The air had the stillness of a room no living thing had entered in a long time, and yet the sweetness was stronger here, thick enough to taste, and Ansel's professional instincts, honed on vaults full of bottled memory, began to whisper a warning they didn't yet have words for.

"This is wrong," Wren murmured. She'd gone pale. "There should be an archivist. Places like this don't empty out. They *fill*."

"They filled," Ansel said quietly. "And then something ate."

They moved down the central aisle, boots silent on stone, past shelves whose spines told the kingdom's whole bureaucratic soul: *Rolls of the Wool Exchange, Years 31–44.* *Registers of the River Courts.* *Correspondence of the Lord Marshal, Private.* Ansel read spines the way other people read faces, and what they were reading here was a country that had once been meticulous about remembering itself.

Forty years ago, Ivo had said. The coronation. The stars dimming for a single night, and the young king weeping behind his crown.

"Coronation records would be under the crown's own seal," Ansel said. "Separate collection. Royal archive proper." They scanned the aisle markers—brass plates, tarnished, each engraved with a category—and found what they were looking for at the far end of the hall, where the shelves gave way to a low iron door set into the stone. No lock. That was the strangest thing yet. A door protecting the most sensitive records in the kingdom, and not so much as a latch.

It opened at a touch, and beyond it the sweetness was almost unbearable.

* * *

The royal collection was one long room, and it was dying.

That was the only word Ansel's mind would accept. The ledgers here lay open on their shelves as though flung, and their pages—Ansel lifted one, a treasury roll from the king's tenth year, and watched it crumble at the edges like burnt paper that had never known fire—were eaten. Not torn, not rotted. *Eaten*, in great lacy galleries, the words devoured mid-sentence so that a line might read *and the king decreed that all the children of* and then dissolve into holes, into lace, into nothing.

Every document in the room was the same. Forty years of records, and every page that mentioned the king—his decrees, his appearances, his signature—had been chewed hollow around his name. Where some clerk had written *His Majesty* the paper was intact. Where they had written *Aldric* the letters were gone, cut out with a precision no knife could match, leaving a ghost-shaped absence in the ink.

"It's eating him out of the record," Wren whispered. She had both hands pressed over her mouth. "Out of the *paper*. Ansel, how does it eat paper?"

"The same way it eats everything else." Ansel heard their own voice come out flat and cold, the ledger-keeper's voice, the voice they used when the numbers stopped making sense and the only sane response was to keep counting anyway. "Memory isn't just in heads. It's in everything we make. A name written down is a memory wearing ink instead of flesh. If something can eat belonging out of a man—" they turned a ruined page, and it shed fragments like snow "—it can eat him out of his own history."

They worked fast after that, because understanding had arrived, and understanding in a place like this was a kind of countdown.

The coronation ledgers were where protocol said they'd be: a cedar chest, banded in iron, marked with the seal of the Lord Chamberlain. Ansel picked the band's clasp in four seconds—it was a good lock, forty years old, and locks that old had usually outlived whoever remembered their tricks—and lifted the lid.

Three volumes. *The Order of Coronation of His Majesty Aldric, Second of His Name.*

The first two were lace. Ansel turned page after page of devoured text, holes where the king's name should be, holes where the oaths should be, holes where—this was the detail that made their hands go steady and cold—the names of the witnesses should be. Every lord who had stood in that cathedral forty years ago, every bishop, every knight: eaten. Not just the king's memory. The memory *of* him, in everyone who had ever recorded having seen him.

Except.

At the back of the third volume, in a section no clerk would ever have bothered to beautify—a list of expenditures, candles and cloth-of-gold and the fee paid to the bell-ringers—one line survived, tucked between the cost of the anointing oil and the price of the feast, in a cramped accountant's hand:

*Paid to the Crown Wardens, for the binding of that which wears what he cannot bear to lose: sum undisclosed, by order of the Privy Council.*

Ansel read it three times.

"*What he cannot bear to lose,*" Wren repeated. She had come to stand at Ansel's shoulder, close enough that Ansel could feel her trembling—not fear, or not only fear; it was the tremor of someone hearing a scream from very far away and knowing it would get louder. "That's what it eats. Not everything. Just—" 

"Just the things he loves." Ansel closed the volume with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal. "Think about it. Think about what we do. A memory taken cleanly leaves a hole shaped exactly like what was missing. Vess taught us that before we're allowed near the vault. So look at him, Wren. Look at what forty years of clean takings looks like."

They were both silent. Outside, somewhere above the archive's buried dark, the starless sky sat on the city like a lid.

"A man with no memories of anything he loved," Ansel said. "No mother's face. No wedding day, if there was a wedding. No griefs either—grief is love with nowhere to go, and it eats that too, it eats the grief, it eats the *missing*. He wouldn't even know he was missing them. He'd just be—" Ansel searched for the word and hated the word when they found it. "*Comfortable.* Smoothed out. A king with nothing in him worth stealing, worn like a coat by the thing that stole it."

"And the wards."

"The wards are fed on *belonging*." Ansel had understood it in fragments since the border stones, since Wren had put her ear against the humming granite and wept. Now it assembled itself whole, terrible and simple, like a lock opening in reverse. "Old magic. Older than the guild, older than the crown. And what is belonging, at the bottom, but memory? Knowing who you are because of who you've loved. The crown has been feeding the king's belonging into the wards for forty years—drip by drip, year by year, keeping the borders safe and the stars lit while it grew fat on everything that made him a person. And now the hoard is nearly empty. The wards are failing because *he* is failing. There's almost nothing left of him to spend."

Wren sat down abruptly on a stool, as if her legs had resigned without notice. "Then why hasn't it finished? Why leave him a scrap?"

"Because a scrap is all it needs to keep eating." Ansel turned the surviving line toward her, tapped it once. "*What he cannot bear to lose.* Singular. There's one thing left. One memory it's saving for last, the way you save the best bite—or the way you keep a fire's last coal so the fire can be rebuilt. Whatever that one thing is, it's the keystone. It's what's holding the rest of him together, and it's what's holding the crown on his head."

"Do you think he knows?" Wren's voice was very small. "What's been done to him?"

Ansel thought of Ivo's story—the boy who wept and begged not to forget, crowned in tears while the stars guttered—and felt something in their chest turn over with a slow, grinding weight, like a vault door deciding whether to open.

"He knew," Ansel said. "Forty years ago, he knew. Whether there's enough of him left now to know anything—" They shook their head. "That's what we're going to find out."

* * *

They took nothing except knowledge. Ansel copied the surviving line into their own pocket ledger in their small, exact hand, and copied it again, and hid the second copy in the lining of their coat, because a thief who trusted one copy deserved to be robbed. Then they stood a moment longer among the ruined shelves, looking at forty years of a kingdom's devotion to its king, chewed to cobweb, and let the cold clarity settle over them like armor being buckled on.

This was the part of the work no one sang about. The planning was done, the crew assembled, Brannic freed or lost—Ivo still hadn't said which, and Ansel hadn't pushed—but plans were just debts not yet come due, and Ansel had spent a lifetime learning that the moment you truly understood what you owed was the moment the fear started. Fear was fine. Fear was fuel. What mattered was that the fear had a shape now, and shapes could be worked against.

The enemy was hunger. Old, patient, and precise. It did not want the kingdom; it wanted the *king*, the last warm room in a house it had already emptied, and it would defend that room with everything it had learned about making people forget what they were fighting for.

Fine. Ansel had spent twenty years among people the world tried to forget. Runaways and failures and forgers and lock-wrights, hedge-witches and old men who remembered too much. Nobody in the Hollow Guild had ever been permitted the luxury of being forgotten by each other. That was the whole point of them. That was the ledger they kept that never appeared in any book.

"We need to get inside the vigil," they said to Wren, already moving toward the iron door. "Not sneak in—*enter*. Invited. On the books. Because whatever happens in that throne room, it will happen in front of a court that doesn't remember what it sees, and I want us there with names that hold, papers that hold, a reason to stand exactly where we need to stand when the moment comes."

Wren scrambled after them. "You want Sefa."

"I want Sefa." Ansel allowed themselves one thin smile in the dark of the stairwell. "I want the best liar in the Hollow Guild to forge us into the heart of the palace. And then I want to see the thing wearing our king, and learn its true name, and write it down."

Behind them, as the iron door swung shut, the archive breathed its sweet rotting breath into the dark—and somewhere far above, faint as a struck bell going silent, the last wards of the capital shivered, and dimmed, and held.
