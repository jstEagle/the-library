## 11. Sefa's Perfect Lie

Sefa had a rule about forgery: never make the lie more beautiful than the truth it replaced.

The truth of the Vigil of Unremembering was this — once every seven years, on the longest night, the court of Hollowmere opened its doors to pilgrims, penitents, and anyone who could produce a blessing from a bishop of the Amber See. The doors were old oak banded in iron, older than the dynasty, and they opened for paper. Paper and a bowed head. That was all. Sefa had spent three days confirming it through a wine-stained merchant who sold candles to the palace kitchens, and the merchant had confirmed it through a laundress, and the laundress had confirmed it by walking through those doors herself last vigil with nothing but a scrap of vellum and a look of appropriate sorrow.

Paper and a bowed head. Sefa could work with that.

The trouble was not the invitation. Invitations were easy; invitations were practically a love letter to her craft. The trouble was the blessing, because blessings came from bishops, and bishops were *counted*. The Amber See kept rolls. Every consecrated hand in the kingdom appeared in the rolls, and every roll was checked at the door by a clerk with ink-stained fingers and a memory like flypaper.

So the bishop would have to be dead. Dead men's names were struck from the rolls but never quite *erased* — there was always a lag, always a season when a recently deceased bishop still lived in the clerks' habits, still surfaced in their mouths when they recited the dioceses aloud. A bishop dead six weeks was perfect: mourned enough to be remembered, buried enough to be unverified.

Which meant Sefa needed to find one.

She found him in the parish records of Saint Ossian-by-the-Weir, a small cathedral town two days south, where Bishop Aldous Ferran had served thirty-one years without scandal, distinction, or — crucially — any visit to the capital in over a decade. He had died in his sleep in early autumn, gentle as a candle going out, and his successor had not yet been consecrated because the See was in mourning and the roads were bad and no one hurried grief.

Bishop Aldous Ferran. Dead six weeks. Perfect.

Sefa sat in the guildhouse attic with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, an oil lamp guttering at her left hand, and built a man out of ink.

* * *

"You're giving him your handwriting," Brannic said from the doorway, arms crossed, filling most of it. "That's a mistake."

"It's not my handwriting." Sefa didn't look up. Her nib moved in slow, deliberate strokes across the vellum — the blessing text first, because a blessing had to feel *practiced*, worn smooth by repetition, the way a real cleric's hand went lazy and fluent over words he'd written ten thousand times. "It's a dead man's handwriting. I've been studying his letters for two days. Look." She turned the practice sheet toward him: rows of Aldous Ferran's script, copied from correspondence preserved in the parish archive, until her own hand had dissolved into his — the heavy downstrokes, the little hitch where his *r* met its neighbor, the way he crossed his *t*s slightly left of center like a man perpetually running late for matins.

Brannic squinted at it. Squinted harder. "That's uncanny."

"That's the point."

"No, I mean—" he stepped into the room, which he did the way weather did, gradually and then all at once, "—it's uncanny that you can do that. It's uncanny that anyone can do that. You've made yourself into a dead man."

"I've made myself into forty-seven dead men," Sefa said. "Aldous is just the newest. And I'm not making myself into him. I'm making a piece of paper that remembers him better than the world does." She blew across the wet ink and held the blessing up to the lamp, tilting it so the light caught the faint texture of the vellum. "See here? I aged the edges with tea and set it near the hearth for an hour so it smells faintly of smoke. Real documents smell of smoke. Everything in this kingdom smells of smoke. If a clerk holds a document that smells of nothing, some animal part of his brain says *wrong* before his eyes ever finish reading."

Brannic grunted. It was, from Brannic, a standing ovation.

He lowered himself onto the crate she used as a second chair — it groaned under him, everything groaned under Brannic — and watched her work with the particular patience of a man who trusted locks more than people and had, against every instinct of his long suspicious life, come to trust her. "And the identities?"

"Four pilgrims from the Weir country. A widow, her brother, a farmhand, and the farmhand's sister." She tapped four small cards laid out in a row, each bearing a name in different hands, different inks, different degrees of care. "Maren Doss, widowed, coming to pray for her husband's soul. Tobin Doss, her brother, dour as a fence post — that's you, don't take offense, dour reads as honest. Pell Marrow, farmhand, big shoulders, bad knees, comes to the vigil because his mother made him promise. And Wren—"

"Wren isn't a farmhand," Brannic said flatly. "Wren is a hedge-witch with dirt under her nails and a song stuck in her throat. She'll talk. She always talks. She'll talk to the guards, she'll talk to the clerks, she'll talk to the *candles* if they hold still long enough."

"Then Wren will be exactly what a farmhand's sister should be," Sefa said, "which is a country girl overwhelmed by the city, chattering nervously because she's nervous. Nobody suspects a nervous girl. Nervous girls are furniture." She paused, then amended herself with the honesty of a craftsman reviewing her own work: "Nervous girls are *usually* furniture. There's risk. There's always risk. But Wren's risk is smaller than Ansel's."

"And what's Ansel?"

"Invisible." Sefa said it simply, and something in her chest tightened around the word. "Ansel doesn't need a name. Ansel needs the absence of one. I've written them into the party as Maren Doss's paid companion — a servant, a nobody, the kind of person clerks look *through*. No card. Servants don't get cards. They get waved past behind their betters." She looked down at her hands, ink to the second knuckle, and felt the strange tenderness she always felt mid-forgery, the intimacy of inventing lives. "It's the hardest thing I do, actually. Making someone no one. Harder than bishops."

* * *

Wren found them at dusk, when the attic light had gone amber and the lamp was doing most of the work. She came up the ladder the way she did everything — quickly, sideways, half-singing under her breath — and emerged into the room carrying a tray of bread, hard cheese, and stewed apples that Ivo had sent up with instructions to eat it all, delivered in Ivo's voice even though Ivo hadn't climbed the stairs himself.

"Ivo says eat it all," Wren reported faithfully. "He said, and I quote, 'forgers who forget to eat forge badly, and badly forged bishops go to the gallows.'"

"He didn't say gallows."

"He said something about rope. Close enough." Wren set the tray down between them and dropped cross-legged onto the floorboards, chin in her hands, staring at the spread of papers with open delight. "Oh, this is the good part. This is my favorite part. Show me the bishop."

"There's no bishop yet. There's a bishop-shaped hole in the world and I'm pouring ink into it." But Sefa was already reaching for the finished blessing, and she handed it over with the small vanity she never bothered to hide, because hiding it would have been lying to family, and she only lied professionally.

Wren held the vellum in both hands like something hatched. Her eyes moved across the script, and her lips moved too, silently reading, and after a moment she said, softly, "It feels holy."

"It should. I stole the cadence from a funeral rite. Old ones are best — they have that falling rhythm, like footsteps going down into the ground."

"No, I mean—" Wren looked up, and the lamplight caught the green of her eyes, and for a second she wasn't the runaway hedge-witch from the border wards, she was something older and stranger, a girl who heard things in silence. "I mean it feels holy. Like it's been prayed over. Like somewhere, somehow, somebody actually said these words to the sky." She turned the page toward the window, toward the darkening east where the stars should have been and weren't, where the sky lay smooth and starless as the inside of a shell. "Do you think that matters? With everything else gone wrong?"

Sefa considered lying, out of craft-pride. Then she considered telling the truth, out of something bigger than craft.

"I think," she said slowly, "that the crown-thing eats belonging. That's what we figured out, isn't it? It eats the feeling of being part of something. And a blessing is a small machine for making belonging — it takes a stranger and folds them into a congregation, into a history, into a story older than themselves. So maybe a forged blessing is a kind of counter-magic. Maybe it matters very much." She took the vellum back gently. "Or maybe it's just paper and the guards are bored. Both can be true. Most true things travel in pairs."

Wren laughed — that quick bright laugh of hers that Sefa had privately catalogued among the things worth protecting, filed away with Ivo's terrible puns and the sound of Brannic humming off-key while he worked locks. "You should write sermons."

"I write lies. Sermons are just lies with better funding."

"*Sefa.*"

"What! I'm a professional. I know my trade." But she was smiling, and Wren was smiling, and Brannic was pretending not to smile, which from Brannic was the same as weeping with joy.

They ate. The bread was dense and good, the apples sweet-sharp, and the three of them sat around the tray like children around a campfire, except the fire was a lamp and the stories were blueprints. Wren asked questions — how do you age ink, why does the seal need two impressions, what happens if the clerk asks a question — and Sefa answered each one, and somewhere in the answering she realized she was teaching, really teaching, passing the craft along the way Ivo had passed it to her, and the realization settled in her chest warm as the hearth-smell she'd baked into the vellum.

"The seal's the dangerous part," she said, holding up the wax impression of the Amber See's sunburst-and-key. "Real seals are pressed twice — once firm, once feather-light, because the die sits unevenly in a man's tired hand. Forgers press once, perfectly. Perfection is the tell. So I press twice, and I let my wrist wobble on the second pass, and I imagine the bishop is old and his hand aches and it's late and he wants his supper." She glanced up. "You have to love the man you're forging. Did I ever tell you that? You have to give him tired hands and bad eyes and a favorite chair. A forgery without pity is a forgery that gets caught."

"You love all of them," Wren said. It wasn't a question. "All your fake people."

"Somebody has to." Sefa smoothed the edge of the invitation with her thumb. "They don't exist anywhere else."

* * *

Ansel came up near midnight, quiet as a draft under a door, and stood in the shadows of the stairwell for a full minute before anyone noticed — which was, of course, the point of Ansel, the whole architecture of them, the load-bearing absence at the center of the crew. When they finally stepped into the lamplight, Sefa had the full kit assembled on the table: four identity cards, the blessing, the invitation with its double-pressed seal, and a fifth document she'd built without being asked.

"What's this?" Ansel picked it up. A servant's pass, countersigned, the kind issued to attendants of visiting dignitaries.

"Insurance. If the companion fiction fails, you're staff attached to a merchant delegation instead. Different door, different clerk, same invisibility." Sefa shrugged. "I made two ways for you to be no one. Choose whichever fits the night."

Ansel looked at the pass for a long time. Long enough that Sefa felt the silence change weight, the way it did around Ansel sometimes, as though the air itself were being carefully counted.

"Two ways to be no one," Ansel repeated finally. "Most people spend their whole lives trying to be someone, and you just — manufacture it. Wholesale."

"Manufactured anonymity, finest quality, satisfaction guaranteed." But Sefa's voice came out quieter than she intended. Because she knew — they all knew — what waited at the end of this road, what the reservoir beneath the palace would cost, what the ledger entry in Ansel's careful hand had quietly arranged. She knew, and she had decided to know it the way she decided everything: by working. By building the lie that got them through the door, perfect and pitied and loved, every forged soul given tired hands and a favorite chair.

Including, someday soon, perhaps, a lie about who carried the king's memories home. She hadn't forged that one yet. She was afraid she'd recognize Ansel's handwriting in it.

"Thank you," Ansel said, and folded the pass away into whatever pocket of the night they kept such things.

"Thank me when we're inside," Sefa said briskly. "And thank me again when we're out. And if we're not out—" she flipped the lamp's wick down, banking the light to a coal-red glow, "—thank me anyway. Dead forgers' names should be spoken. It's in the rolls somewhere. I'll see to it personally."

Brannic snorted. Wren giggled. Ansel — Ansel smiled, small and real, the rarest document in the whole collection, worth more than any bishop's seal.

* * *

They rehearsed twice. Names, histories, the layout of the vigil route — processional courtyard, the Cloister of Bells, the great west doors where clerks checked blessings by lamplight. Brannic stumbled on his cover name twice ("Tobin. *Tobin.* It's four letters, Brannic") and glowered magnificently enough that Sefa revised her assessment: dour didn't read as honest, dour read as *unforgettable*, and she spent an hour softening his backstory into a man so boring that boredom itself became camouflage. Wren absorbed her new life in minutes and then improved it, adding details Sefa hadn't supplied — a limp from a childhood fall, a fear of pigeons — until Sefa had to stop her.

"You can't add details. Details have to survive questioning."

"But they make her *real*—"

"They make her fragile. A lie is strong because it's simple. One crack and the whole pane goes." Sefa softened it with a hand on Wren's arm. "Save the improvisation for when something breaks. It will. Something always breaks. That's what you're for — not the plan, but the mending."

Wren nodded slowly, filing it away, and Sefa watched her file it and thought: *she learns faster than I did. Faster than Ivo, maybe.* The guild's future, sitting cross-legged on an attic floor eating stolen apples, learning forgery the way other children learned prayers.

By the third hour the lamp had burned low and the rehearsal had dissolved into the loose warm talk of people who trust each other with the small hours. Ivo's voice drifted up once through the floorboards, telling some story about the coronation year, and went quiet again. Somewhere below, Vess's door stayed shut, and everyone pretended not to notice, and everyone noticed.

"One more thing," Sefa said at last, gathering the documents into their oilcloth wallet with the reverence of a priest handling relics. "Tomorrow night, when the clerk looks at our papers — bow your heads. All of you. Not deep. Just enough. Pilgrims bow to the vigil, not to the clerks, and clerks respect the difference." She tied the wallet closed. "And whatever happens in there, whatever you see — the sleepwalkers, the tribute, the thing in the throne room — remember your names. Say them to yourselves. Quietly, often. Our names are the one document they haven't forged."

Silence in the attic. Outside, the starless sky pressed close against the little round window, black and smooth and hungry.

"Tobin Doss," Brannic said suddenly, testing it, and grimaced. "Fine. But if anyone asks, I'm praying for my sister's husband, and I hated him."

"Perfect," Sefa said. "That's the most believable thing you've said all night."

They laughed, all four of them, there in the amber dark — and none of them saw, because none of them were looking, the way the flame of the banked lamp bent slowly sideways, toward the palace, as if something vast on the far side of the city had drawn breath.

The vigil began tomorrow at sundown. The longest night was coming to meet them.
