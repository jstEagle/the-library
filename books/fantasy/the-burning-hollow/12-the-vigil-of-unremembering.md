## 12. The Vigil of Unremembering

The invitations were perfect. That was the first thing that frightened Ansel—not the vigil itself, not the Hollow Guard at the gates with their smooth, empty faces, but the fact that Sefa's forgeries passed inspection without a flicker of hesitation from anyone.

"Of course it's perfect," Sefa had said when Ansel told her so, adjusting the collar of a borrowed mourner's coat. "I didn't forge an invitation. I forged a *reason*. Nobody questions grief. Grief is the one thing in this city they've stopped checking."

Now they stood in the procession winding up the palace hill, hundreds of mourners in grey and ash-blue, each carrying a small unlit candle. The longest night had come two days early—or so it felt; the sun had been a pale coin behind cloud since noon, and by the time the vigil's bells began, the dark was already thick as felt. Above them, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not clouds. Nothing. A sky like the inside of a closed ledger.

Ansel walked between Wren and Brannic, hood up, eyes down, playing the part of Tobin Doss—grieving nephew of a dead bishop whose blessing Sefa had conjured out of ink, wax, and sheer audacity. The bishop had never existed. That was the beauty of it. You couldn't disprove a man no one remembered.

*No one remembers.* Ansel turned the phrase over as they climbed, and found it tasted like home.

"You're doing the thing," Wren murmured without moving her lips.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you count exits. Your eyes go left-right-left like a cat in a sack."

"I'm being careful."

"You're being scared," she said, and her hand found his sleeve, brief and warm. "Both can be true. My gran used to say fear is just care wearing armor. Come on, Tobin Doss. Mourn your uncle."

The palace gates swallowed them.

* * *

Inside, the vigil was beautiful, and that was the worst of it.

Ten thousand candles lined the great processional way, their flames perfectly still, as if the air itself had been instructed not to move. Courtiers knelt along the walls in rows, silver-grey robes pooling around them, heads bowed over little dishes of oil. Musicians played somewhere unseen—a slow, sweet lament on strings and glass chimes—and the sound filled the colonnades the way water fills a cup: completely, leaving no room for anything else, not even thought.

Ansel noticed that last part gradually, the way you notice you've stopped hearing rain. His thoughts kept sliding off the edges of themselves. He would begin to plan—the route to the throne room doors, the timing of the guard changes Sefa had memorized—and halfway through, the plan would simply... soften, like ink in water, and he'd find himself admiring the candles instead. How steady they burned. How peaceful. How good it would be, surely, to kneel among them and stop thinking altogether.

He bit the inside of his cheek hard enough to taste copper. The pain stitched him back together.

*They're not here to mourn,* he realized. *Look closer.*

He looked. Between the bowed shoulders, past the candlelight, he saw the courtiers' hands. Each kneeling figure held a small dish of clear oil before them, and into each dish fell something that was not quite liquid—threads, faint and pale as moonlit spider silk, drifting down from the bowed heads like hair coming loose. The threads touched the oil and dissolved, and the oil glowed a little brighter each time, and the kneeling figure swayed a little more gently, like a plant in a slow current.

"They're giving it away," Wren breathed beside him. Her hedge-witch senses were wide open; Ansel could see the effort it cost her not to cover her ears. "Their memories. They're pouring them out like—like offerings. Ansel, some of those people are giving away their *children*. I can hear it. Little songs going out of them. Names. First steps." Her voice cracked. "And they're *smiling*."

She was right. Every face lifted toward the distant throne wore the same expression: serene, grateful, emptied. A woman near them finished her offering, rose, and drifted back into the crowd with her candle now lit from the glowing dish. Her face was lovely and blank as an egg. As she passed, she said to no one, in a voice soft as falling ash:

"It doesn't hurt. It doesn't hurt at all. That's how you know it's holy."

Brannic made a low sound in his chest, half growl, half prayer. "My sister's husband is looking better all the time," he muttered. "Keep walking."

* * *

They moved through the vigil the way smoke moves through a house—slowly, inevitably, drawn toward the heart of it. Sefa had mapped the processional from servants' gossip and stolen floor plans, and the map held: through the Hall of Bells, past the Gallery of Kings where forty generations of painted monarchs watched with painted eyes, down the cold throat of the Coronation Stair. Twice, Hollow Guards stood at junctions—men and women in black-lacquered armor, faces smooth and attentive as new leather—and twice, Sefa stepped forward with the bishop's blessing held out like a lantern, and twice the guards inclined their heads and let them pass.

It was the third checkpoint that nearly ended everything.

A guard captain blocked the stair's final landing, and this one did not look at the papers. This one looked at *faces*. Its gaze traveled across the four of them—Sefa's composed widow's sorrow, Brannic's scowling piety, Wren's downcast eyes—and stopped on Ansel.

Ansel knew why. It was the same reason he'd spent twenty years making himself forgettable: because he was *looking back*. Everyone else in the palace gazed at the floor or the throne or the middle distance. Only Ansel was reading the room, counting guards, mapping exits—and whatever hollow thing animated the captain's body recognized the difference the way a fisherman recognizes a live line among tangled ones.

"You," the captain said. Its voice was pleasant. That was somehow worse than if it had roared. "You have many memories."

"I keep accounts," Ansel said, and blessed the guild training that let his voice come out flat and bored. "My uncle was the bishop's ledger-keeper. Someone must mind what he owed."

"Minding." The captain tilted its head, and beneath the lacquered helm something shifted with a sound like sand settling. "Yes. We mind things here. All things. Would you like to be minded?"

For one long moment the candle flames along the wall all bent sideways, toward the throne room, toward the thing behind the doors—and Ansel felt a vast attention settle on him like a hand on the back of his neck, patient and enormous and *interested*, and understood with absolute clarity that the crown-thing knew exactly what he was. Not Tobin Doss. Not a mourner. A thief of memories, standing in its larder.

Then Wren coughed—a small, wet, human cough—and began quietly weeping, and the spell broke. The captain's attention slid off Ansel like water off wax.

"Grief is welcome here," it said gently. "Go and be emptied."

They went.

* * *

The throne room doors were older than everything else in the palace.

That was Ansel's first coherent thought as they reached them, slipping into an alcove of carved saints while the last courtiers filed in to kneel. Everything else in the royal seat had been polished, renewed, maintained across centuries—but these doors were dark oak gone nearly black, iron-banded, tall as three men, and they had not been refinished in forty years. No one had dared touch them. They looked less like doors than like a scar someone had decided to keep.

Sefa pressed herself against the wall, breathing carefully. Brannic stood with his arms crossed, radiating the specific fury of a lock-wright confronting a door he hadn't been allowed to study. Wren had both hands pressed flat over her sternum, as though holding something in.

Ansel approached the doors alone.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. The plan required knowing what waited beyond—the layout, the guards, the king. And the doors were the only way to know. But even as he thought it, he felt the truth underneath, the same pull he'd felt at the wardstones, at the vault rupture, at every wrong place the road had shown them: the pull of a keeper toward the thing that needed keeping. Forty years of guild work had taught him to read locks. These doors were a lock. And every lock, Vess used to say, is a question somebody was afraid to answer.

He laid his palm against the iron band.

Cold. Then not cold. Then—

Not roaring. That was the thing he could never have prepared for, the thing he would carry even after everything else was taken from him. He had expected a monster's voice, a hunger howling behind the wood, teeth and thunder. Instead, what came through the door and up his arm and into the marrow of him was a whisper. Thin. Frayed. Endlessly repeating, like a man pacing the same cell for decades, tracing the same words into the wall.

*...Aldous. My name is Aldous. Aldous. Aldous of the line of Merrin, son of Queen Isolde, who taught me to ride on the white hills above—above—*

A pause. The whisper gathered itself, weaker.

*Aldous. My name is Aldous. I had a garden. I had—I had a daughter. Her name was—her name was—*

The whisper broke, and something in it broke too, and Ansel's eyes were burning, and he realized his hand had curled against the iron as if he could hold the fragments together through the wood.

*Her name was—no. No, I wrote it down. Where did I write it down? I am the king. I am the king. Am I the king? My name is—my name is—*

*What was my name.*

The whisper went on, but Ansel pulled his hand back as though the iron had bitten him, and stood shaking in the alcove's shadow, and understood at last, fully, what they had come to do.

The crown-thing wasn't keeping the king prisoner. Prisoners remember their cells. It was *unmaking* him slowly, decade by decade, feeding on the memories that made him a man and leaving only enough to wear—to sit the throne, to smile at festivals, to sign the decrees. Everything it devoured went into the reservoir below, starlight drowned in water. What remained in the king was a whisper pacing a cell, forgetting even the shape of the door.

Forty years. Forty years of a man calling his own name into the dark, hoping someone on the other side would answer.

"Ansel." Wren's hand on his arm. She'd followed him, quiet as her namesake. She looked up at his face and whatever she saw there made her own go still. "You heard him."

"He's still in there." Ansel's voice came out rough. "Barely. But he's in there. Wren—he's asking for his name. He's been asking for forty years."

Wren glanced at the doors, and Ansel watched her listen the way she listened to wards and hedges and the silence where stars should sing. When she spoke, her voice was very soft.

"Then tomorrow night," she said, "we answer him."

Behind them, deep in the throne room, ten thousand candles guttered at once—as if something vast had drawn breath—and beyond the black oak doors, the whisper paused.

Then, for the first time in forty years, it changed.

It said, in a voice worn thin as parchment but unmistakably hopeful:

*Is someone there?*
