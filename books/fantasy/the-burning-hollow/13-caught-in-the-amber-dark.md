## 13. Caught in the Amber Dark

Brannic had built locks his whole life, and he had learned one truth above all others: every door is a promise. A good lock promises that what's inside will still be there in the morning. A bad one promises nothing and lies about it.

The doorway he stood in now was a bad one. Old oak, swollen with damp, its iron fittings pitted with rust from forty years of river fog. He'd already catalogued its failures—the hinge pins soft enough to shear with a hard kick, the frame cracked where someone had once forced it badly, the deadbolt he'd rigged himself an hour ago with wire and prayer because there hadn't been time for anything better. It would hold against a man. Against two men, maybe, if they were stupid.

Against six, it would hold for as long as Brannic could make his body into a second bolt.

He pressed his palm flat against the wood and felt the tremor of running feet through the floorboards beyond—Sefa's quick light steps, Wren's uneven ones, Ivo's shuffle, Ansel's near-silence. Four heartbeats' worth of noise retreating down the cellar corridor toward the drainage grate they'd come in through. Four people who trusted him to be a door.

"Move faster," he muttered at them, though they couldn't hear him. "For once in your lives, move *faster*."

* * *

It had started well, which should have been their first warning. Nothing in Hollow Guild work ever started well unless something worse was waiting to balance the ledger.

They'd come to the tanner's row safehouse to retrieve Ivo's cache—a locked strongbox of documents Ivo had hidden before the stars went out, papers he claimed would prove what had been bound on coronation day. The old man remembered too much; that was his gift and his affliction both, a mind like a warehouse with no doors, everything kept, nothing let go. He'd walked them through streets he hadn't seen in decades, narrating: *That baker sold sawdust bread during the famine year. That window used to hold a girl who sang off-key. That alley is where I buried a man who deserved better.* Brannic had stopped listening after the third block. Not from cruelty—he loved the old man, in the wordless way he loved most things—but because listening to Ivo remember was like standing in rising water. You felt yourself being pulled under into other people's pasts.

The strongbox had been where Ivo said. Brannic had opened it in ninety seconds flat, feeling the old pride warm in his chest, the only clean pleasure he allowed himself. Sefa had begun copying the key documents. Wren had gone to the window to listen to the city.

And then Wren had gone very still, the way she did when she heard something no one else could hear, and said, "Boots. A lot of boots. And underneath the boots—" Her face had done something terrible. "Underneath the boots there's nothing. There are men walking and there's *nothing walking in them*."

Brannic had known then. He'd spent thirty years reading mechanisms, and he knew when a thing was already sprung, already falling, already too late to stop. The question was never whether the trap closed. The question was who it closed on.

"The grate," Ansel had said, calm as ledger-ink. "Brannic—"

"I know my job," Brannic had said, and had gone to stand in the doorway between the front room and the cellar stairs, because that was the job. Someone was always the door. In all his years with the guild, through every job gone sideways, that had been the quiet covenant none of them spoke aloud: *someone stands, so the others run*. It rotated. It was fair. It was, tonight, his turn, and he found—to his own surprise—that he wasn't afraid. He was angry. Anger was lighter to carry.

* * *

They came up the street outside like weather.

He heard them before he saw them: not the ragged rhythm of a watch patrol but a single unified cadence, boots striking cobbles in perfect unison, as though one enormous creature wore many pairs of legs. Through the warped glass of the safehouse window, Brannic watched them arrive under the guttering light of a street-lamp whose flame burned strange and low, amber-colored, barely moving.

Hollow Guard. He'd heard the stories from Ansel, delivered in that flat recitation-voice that meant the facts were worse than the telling. Men taken by the crown-thing and emptied—not killed, never killed, killing wasted material—stripped down to obedience the way you'd strip paint from a door until only bare grain remained. They didn't hate. They didn't fear. They didn't want anything except the next order, and so they could not be bought, bargained with, or broken. Every trick Brannic knew for handling men assumed men wanted something.

These wanted nothing. That made them the worst opponents he'd ever face, and he'd once disarmed a trap guarded by a grieving widow who'd rigged her husband's grave to explode.

Six of them. Amber-eyed in the lamplight—or maybe not amber-eyed, maybe just reflecting the wrong-colored flame, it was hard to tell, and it didn't matter. They moved to the safehouse door without hesitation, without formation even, because formations were for soldiers who might need to adapt, and these needed nothing. One raised a fist and struck the door twice. Not a demand. A notification.

Brannic threw the deadbolt anyway. Ritual mattered. His mother—no. He didn't have a mother. He had a guildmaster who'd found him picking locks at nine years old and instead of beating him had said, *well, do it properly or don't do it*, and that was the closest thing to a childhood anyone in this family had, and he'd be damned if he'd let a pack of empty men walk through a door he'd rigged, even if the rigging was wire and prayer.

The door shuddered. Once. Twice. On the third blow the top hinge pin sheared with a sound like a snapped tooth, and the frame split along the crack he'd noted earlier, and Brannic set his shoulder against the wood and became, for the moment, architecture.

* * *

Fighting empty men was unlike any fighting he'd known.

They didn't feint. They didn't flinch. They came at him with the patient, identical persistence of water working at a foundation, and when he broke the first one's nose—a solid hit, a hit that would have dropped any watchman he'd ever brawled—the man simply continued moving as though his face were someone else's problem. Blood ran freely. The eyes above it stayed flat as coins.

Brannic got the door half-closed again and put his back against it, wedging his boot against the frame, and bought another ten seconds. Then twenty. Somewhere below, he heard the scrape of the drainage grate—too slow, gods, they were going too slow—and he shouted down the stairwell, "Ivo, if you're not through that tunnel in thirty seconds I'm coming down there to carry you, and I'll enjoy it!"

"You're a cruel boy," Ivo's voice floated back, breathless. "Cruel and ugly."

"And you're slow. Go."

The door buckled inward a hand's width. Fingers appeared around the edge—six-fingered grip, three men stacking behind one another with mechanical precision—and Brannic slammed his forearm across the gap and heard bone grind, his or theirs, impossible to say. Pain arrived late and distant, like news from a far country. He'd take stock later. Later was a country he was increasingly unsure he'd visit.

Through the widening gap he looked into the nearest Guard's face, really looked, the way he looked at a mechanism before defeating it. And what he saw there stopped his breath for half a second.

Nothing. Not cruelty, not madness, not the blankness of the drugged or the desperate. Nothing, the way an empty room is nothing. But beneath the nothing, faint as watermark, something flickered when the man's hand touched Brannic's arm—something that might have been a memory trying to surface, a man-shaped shadow pressing against the inside of the emptiness like a face against frosted glass. For one instant the amber eyes *focused*, and the mouth twisted around a sound that wasn't an order-word, wasn't anything the crown-thing would have put there:

"*Run*," the hollow man whispered, in a voice frayed down to thread. "*While you—*"

Then the nothing closed over it like water over a stone, and the hands resumed pushing, and Brannic understood two things at once, both of them unbearable: that the men inside the Guard were still in there somewhere, buried alive in themselves. And that he could not save this one, and could not save any of them, and the only mercy available in the whole amber dark of this night was the mercy of holding this door long enough for four people he loved to crawl through a sewer grate and live.

So he held it.

* * *

Later—if there was a later—he would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory under pressure doesn't record; it survives, in fragments, like cargo fished from a wreck. What came back to him afterward came in pieces:

The doorframe splitting along its length, the whole wall groaning, plaster sifting down over his shoulders like grey snow.

His knuckles connecting with a jaw and the jaw not caring.

Wren's voice from below, high and cracking: *"Brannic, come ON—"*

Him shouting back, "When the grate's shut, count to a hundred before you surface," because even now, even here, he couldn't stop being the one who thought about exits. "Sefa, watch the current past the second junction. Ansel—" and here his voice caught on something he refused to name, "—Ansel, keep them together. Keep them *together*."

"We come back for you," Ansel's voice said, from the dark of the stairwell, and it wasn't a promise, it was a threat, delivered in that flat ledger-keeper's tone that meant it had been entered in the book, that it existed now as debt, and debts to Ansel Lockridge were always collected. "You hear me? This isn't the price. This is a loan."

"Get out," Brannic said, gruffly, because his throat was doing something embarrassing, "before I start charging interest."

The last thing he heard from below was the clang of the grate, and Wren's voice, thin and furious and singing—the hedge-song, the one she'd been humming since the stars went out, the one that held things steady—and then the door gave way entirely and the amber dark poured over him like a tide.

* * *

They took him in the wreckage of the doorway, six sets of hands, impersonal as tide. He fought the way he'd taught the younger guild-thieves to fight: ugly, close, aimed at joints and throats, spending strength like a miser because he'd always known strength was finite. He put two of them down—one wouldn't rise, one rose slowly, cradling an arm—and he broke a third's grip by biting through the meat of a thumb, tasting copper and lamp-oil and nothing else, because even their blood tasted of nothing.

But six is six, and one is one, and the arithmetic of doorways is merciless. He went down under their weight with his cheek against the splintered oak he'd sworn to hold, and the last thing his hands did was grip the frame—not fighting anymore, just holding, the way you'd hold a railing, the way you'd hold a friend's arm—until they pried him loose finger by finger.

They bound his wrists with cord, not irons. Irons were for prisoners. Prisoners had rights, trials, names. These men simply picked him up like furniture and began to walk, and the street-lamp with its wrong amber flame passed overhead, and passed again, and Brannic realized they were circling the block in perfect step, waiting—for orders, perhaps, or for nothing, because waiting required wanting and they had none.

In the silence of being carried, guilt arrived, punctual as rent.

Not guilt for losing. Guilt for the arithmetic. *Someone stands so the others run*—he'd believed it his whole guild-life, had watched Vess rotate the duty with cold fairness, had taken his turns without complaint. But lying in the grip of the empty, watching the gutters run with starless night, Brannic found the flaw in the covenant he'd never let himself see: it only works if the one who stands believes the others will come back. And the crown-thing knew that. Of course it knew that. It ate belonging; it would know exactly how a family worked, exactly where to cut. Take one, and either the rest come—and then it has all of them—or the rest flee—and then it has proven, to itself and to them, that belonging breaks under load.

Either way it fed. Either way, the door he'd held had been a door into a trap shaped like a choice.

"No," Brannic said aloud, to the empty faces above him, to the starless sky, to the thing coiled through a king miles away that he was certain could hear him. His voice came out wrecked and he didn't care. "No. You've got it backwards. You think taking me tests them." He laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway, because Sefa had taught him years ago that laughing at the enemy confused them and laughing at yourself disarmed them, and right now he'd take any weapon. "You've just shown them exactly where to aim."

One of the Guard turned its head. For a moment—half a heartbeat—the amber eyes flickered, and behind the nothing that drowned-in-man surfaced again, mouthed something silent, and was gone.

Brannic stared at where it had been, and his blood went cold in a new way.

Because he'd recognized the shape of that surfacing shadow. The set of the brow. The scar through the left eyebrow, old and white, that he'd last seen across a card table while the man cheated and grinned and swore he wasn't cheating.

The Hollow Guard hadn't been hunting them blind. Its ranks were filled with the guild's own—thieves taken in the night, clients who'd asked too many questions, faces from the vaults. The crown-thing had been building an army out of everyone the guild had ever failed to protect.

And now it had Brannic, and Brannic knew where the dungeons were, because Ivo remembered too much and had made sure they all did, and the last coherent thought he permitted himself before the amber dark closed fully over his mind was a lock-wright's thought, precise and professional and full of love:

*Every cell has a lock. Every lock has a flaw. Find the flaw.*

Somewhere beneath the city, in a room he couldn't see, a ledger-keeper was already turning pages toward him. He had to believe that. He was, after all, a door—and doors, whatever else they promised, always opened from the other side.
