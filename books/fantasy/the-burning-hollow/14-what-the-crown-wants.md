## 14. What the Crown Wants

Sleep did not come easily to Ansel Lockridge. It never had. A memory-thief learned early that the dark behind the eyelids was not empty—it was a warehouse with the doors left open, and anything you'd ever taken from anyone might come walking out of it wearing your own face.

So Ansel had rituals. A pinch of ash from the guild's cold hearth rubbed between the fingers. The ledger locked in its iron box, the key on a cord around their neck, pressed against the sternum like a second heart. And always, always, a candle left burning—not for light, but because Wren had told them once, in her flat hedge-witch way, that *a flame remembers being the sun*, and something about that had lodged in Ansel like a splinter and refused to work itself free.

The safehouse was an abandoned dyeworks three streets from the river, all vats and hanging cloth gone stiff with old pigment. Brannic slept sitting up against the door they'd barred, his lock-wright's tools arranged across his knees like a sleeping dog. Sefa had claimed the loft and filled it with her snoring and her forged papers. Ivo murmured in his corner, dreaming aloud in fragments of forty years ago—*the bells, the bells were wrong, even then the bells were wrong*—and Wren lay nearest the window, one hand trailing off her pallet as if she were still reaching for the silence where the stars used to sing.

Ansel closed their eyes at what should have been midnight.

The night did not agree to end.

* * *

They knew immediately that they were dreaming, which was itself a kind of horror, because Ansel's dreams had always been honest—clumsy, unguarded things full of the childhood they'd sold away in pieces. This dream was furnished.

It was the guildhall. Not the cramped rented rooms where Vess kept the ledgers now, but the guildhall as it had been before the fall of the wards, before the vault ruptured, before everything: firelight in the great hearth, the long table scarred by decades of knives and tankards, the smell of stew and ink and pipe smoke. Someone was laughing upstairs. Someone was arguing over cards below. The sounds overlapped into a warmth so specific that Ansel's chest ached around it like a bruise.

And every chair at the table was occupied.

Brannic sat sharpening a blade he never used. Sefa shuffled a deck with too many queens in it. Ivo stared into the fire with eyes like wet stones. Wren hummed the hedge-song under her breath, except the song came out wrong here, sweetened, its silences sanded smooth. They were all there. They were all exactly right.

That was how Ansel knew none of them were real.

"You've been difficult to reach," said the thing wearing the guildhall.

The voice came from everywhere—from the hearth, from the floorboards, from the mouths of the people Ansel loved. It was not loud. That was the worst of it. It spoke the way a debt collector speaks: patiently, reasonably, with the confidence of someone who already knows how this ends.

"The ferryman dreams loudly," it continued. "The witch dreams in songs. But you—" the firelight guttered, and for half a heartbeat every face at the table went smooth as an egg, featureless, waiting to be written on— "you keep your doors locked. Very professional. I respect a professional."

Ansel stood very still. In dreams, as in the trade, the first rule was the same: *never show them what you're afraid to lose.*

"You have my ledger," Ansel said. Their voice sounded thin in the warm room. "You pulled my name out of the vault along with everyone else's. So skip the flattery."

"Flattery." The word turned over slowly, savored. "No. Honesty. You of all people should appreciate honesty—it's the rarest currency in your profession." The thing in the hearth leaned forward, though the hearth did not move, and the shadows bent toward Ansel like iron filings toward a lodestone. "I want to make you an offer, ledger-keeper. A trade. One of substance."

"I don't trade with thieves."

A laugh moved through the room then, through the walls, through the bodies of the false friends—and it was not cruel, which somehow made it worse. It was amused. Fond, almost. The laugh of something that had been eating a kingdom for forty years and had developed a taste for the seasoning.

"What do you imagine I am?" it asked. "Some mindless hunger? Some beast gnawing at the roots of the world? I am a *curator*. I keep what others cannot bear to keep. Your king wept on his coronation day because he understood what crowns cost, and I simply... took up the accounting. He could not carry forty years of grief, of guilt, of the faces of the men who died building his roads. I carried them for him. Every burden. Every belonging. I have been *generous*."

"And the stars?"

"Collateral," it said, without shame. "Memory is light, little thief. Did your hedge-witch never tell you? Where I store what I take, the sky goes dark to match. When the accounting is finished, the books will balance, and the stars will return." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down beside a plate. "Or they will not. That depends entirely on you."

Ansel's hands had gone cold. In the dream they could see their own breath, though the room was warm. "Say it plainly."

"Gladly. Plainness is a courtesy I extend to professionals." The false Brannic looked up from his blade; the false Wren stopped humming; the false Sefa set down her crooked deck. All of them turned to Ansel with the same patient expression, and the voice spoke through all their mouths at once, gently, like a lullaby sung by a choir of creditors:

"Give me the guild. Not their lives—I have no interest in blood, blood is so *temporary*. Give me what binds them. Every shared memory. Every kindness traded, every secret confided, every night around every fire. The joke Brannic tells badly. The song Wren sings when she thinks no one listens. The way Sefa cheats and lets herself be caught. Forty years of the king's stolen life, returned to him whole, his freedom purchased fair and square—and in exchange, your little family forgets it was ever a family. They will walk out of your life tomorrow morning as strangers. No grief. No wound. They simply will not remember you, or each other, or anything you made together. Clean cuts. I'm very good at clean cuts."

The firelight swelled, tender as a mother's embrace.

"And you," it whispered, "you may keep whatever scraps survive. Or—here is my generosity again—you may keep nothing, and go to your king as a blank page, and he will owe you a life. Think of it, ledger-keeper. The one debt in the world that can never be repaid to you, held forever in the palm of a grateful crown. Isn't that worth more than *belonging*? Belonging is just another word for something that can be taken from you."

* * *

Ansel had spent their whole life learning the architecture of temptation. They had watched clients weep over bottled first kisses and pay a month's rent to forget a dead child's face. They knew every shape wanting could wear—the desperate kind, the shameful kind, the kind dressed up as duty.

So they recognized this offer for what it was, even wrapped in dream-fire and spoken through the mouths of everyone they loved.

It was not a bargain. It was a confession.

"You're afraid," Ansel said.

The guildhall went silent. Even the false fire held its breath.

"Forty years," Ansel said, and their voice steadied as they went, the way it always did when they finally found the true shape of a thing. "Forty years you've been feeding on one man, alone in his own head, and it worked because he was alone. A king is the loneliest creature there is—that's why you chose him, isn't it? Not his strength. His solitude. You eat belonging, and he had none left to lose." They took a step toward the hearth, toward the smooth-faced things wearing their family. "But these people—" another step, and the shadows recoiled from them like water from a hot iron— "these people found each other in gutters and prisons and hedgerows. Nobody gave them to one another. Nobody crowned them. They *chose*, over and over, on purpose, with their eyes open. And you cannot buy a choice. You can only steal it after it's made, and you've just shown me you don't know how."

"We are very old," the thing said, and for the first time the patience in its voice had hairline cracks running through it. "We have unmade empires. We have eaten languages whole."

"And every one of them alone," Ansel said. "Every meal, solitary. You don't understand what happened in that vault when the memories flew home—you think it was a rupture. But I've seen the clients since. Half of them came back to us *laughing*, do you know that? Getting their stolen years back hurt, and they laughed anyway, because for one moment they remembered they'd been robbed and survived it. Together. There were witnesses." Ansel smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and they meant it not to be. "You didn't call me here to bargain. You called me here because for the first time in forty years, the ledger doesn't balance, and you needed to know if I could be bought before the rest of them find out what you are."

The dream shuddered. The guildhall's walls breathed inward. The false faces at the table began, very slowly, to run—like wax, like wet ink, like names dissolving off a page.

"Take the offer," the thing said, and now the gentleness was gone and underneath it was something vast and hollow and *hungry*, a bell-shaped darkness ringing with forty years of swallowed days. "Take it while it stands. Everyone has a price. Everyone. I have never once been wrong."

"Then you've never met a family," Ansel said.

* * *

They woke with the candle burned down to a stub and their hand clenched around the ledger key hard enough to leave its teeth printed in their palm.

The dyeworks was gray with pre-dawn, or with whatever passed for dawn now, under a sky still scoured of stars. For a moment Ansel lay still, taking inventory the way they took inventory of everything: heart beating, name intact, memories accounted for. Wren was awake at the window—of course she was; the girl slept like a cat on watch—and when Ansel sat up, her eyes found them in the dark without any searching at all.

"It talked to you," she said. Not a question. Her grandmother's hedge-song training had ears for this sort of thing, apparently, even through stone walls. "In your sleep."

"How did you—"

"Your breathing changed. Went all... formal." She crossed the room and crouched beside Ansel's pallet, close enough that Ansel could see the ward-sigils inked on her knuckles, freshly renewed. "What did it want?"

Ansel told her. All of it—the guildhall, the offer, the voices through the mouths of their friends. Wren listened the way she did everything, absolutely still, absolutely present, and when Ansel described the thing's fear, its hairline-cracked, starving fear, Wren nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis.

"My gran had a saying about hungry things," she said. "'Never trust a hunger that only eats alone.'"

"It offered me the king's freedom," Ansel said. "For all of you. For everything we—" They stopped. The word *have* snagged in their throat, because the dream had done its work even in failure; Ansel could still feel the weight of that warm guildhall, that impossible completeness, and the knowledge of how precisely the enemy had measured them. "It knew exactly what to price. That's what frightens me. Not the offer. The *accuracy*."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and tapped the ledger key where it hung against Ansel's chest, once, like knocking on a door.

"It showed you a perfect copy of us," she said. "Firelight and stew and everyone in their chairs. Right?"

"Yes."

"That's how you know it's losing." Her voice was soft but certain, the certainty of someone who had grown up listening to things the world pretended weren't there. "Real families aren't perfect copies, Ansel. Real ones argue and cheat at cards and hold doorways open and get taken, and stay anyway. It built you a museum. Museums are where you put things that are *dead*." She stood, and outside the grimy window the starless sky was beginning, grudgingly, to lighten. "It's afraid of the real thing. Which means the real thing must be dangerous."

Ansel got up, stiff and cold, and woke the others. Brannic came alert instantly, hand going to his tools. Sefa surfaced from the loft with her hair full of straw and demanded, as she always did, to know if anyone had died, and whether it could wait until after breakfast. Ivo simply opened his ancient eyes and said, "It came to you, didn't it. It came to me on coronation night, forty years ago. Same voice. Same patience." His gaze was terrible and clear. "It only visits when it's started counting the cost."

By the time gray light filled the dyeworks, the crew was gathered around the cold hearth—Brannic, Sefa, Ivo, Wren—all of them looking at Ansel, all of them alive and imperfect and stubbornly, inconveniently *real*, nothing like the museum the crown had built.

"It offered me a trade," Ansel told them, because they had decided in the dark that this family would not be managed, would not be protected by omission, would not be fed to secrecy the way the king had been fed to solitude. "All of you. Everything we've built. In exchange for the king's freedom."

Silence. Then Sefa said, "What did you counter-offer?"

"Sefa—"

"No, genuinely, I want to know the terms, I'm a professional." But her hands, Ansel noticed, had gone white-knuckled around a scrap of forgery paper, and her eyes were wet and furious. "It tried to buy us. It tried to buy *us*, like we're stock in a vault."

"It's frightened," Ivo said quietly. "Good. Frightened things make mistakes."

Brannic cracked his knuckles, one by one, a sound like small doors closing. "Then let's give it something to be frightened of."

And Wren, at the window, went suddenly rigid, her head cocked toward the floor, toward the deep places beneath the city, toward the reservoir she had found and not yet told them how to reach. Her lips moved around the shape of the hedge-song, and her eyes when she turned back to them were wide and shining and terrified and hopeful all at once.

"Ansel," she said. "There's something under the palace you need to see. I found where it keeps the stars."
