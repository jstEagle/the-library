## 15. The Reservoir Beneath

The hedge-song had no beginning that Wren could remember learning. It had simply always been there, folded into the back of her skull like a seed pressed into cold earth by her grandmother's thumb—*when the stars go quiet, sing them home.* She had thought it a lullaby once, a nonsense string of syllables meant to soothe children frightened of the dark. Then the stars had gone out, all of them at once, like candles pinched by a single wet finger, and the song had woken up inside her and started to *pull.*

Now it pulled her down.

Beneath the capital, beneath even the oldest cellars where the guild's thieves had made their camp among forgotten wine racks and rat-gnawed tapestries, there were stairs that no map admitted to. Ansel had found them by accident—or claimed it was an accident, though Wren had begun to suspect that Ansel Lockridge did very little by accident—and now the two of them descended with a lantern turned low and Wren's bare feet on stone so old it had gone soft as bread at the edges.

"You don't have to come," Wren said, for the third time since they'd left the others sleeping.

"I know," Ansel said, for the third time, in that flat ledger-keeper's voice that meant *I am coming anyway and we both know arguing wastes breath.* "You hear things I can't. If something down here decides to eat you, I'd like to at least witness it. For the record."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. The song was too loud in her head for smiling; it filled her molars, her wristbones, the hollow behind her sternum, a pressure like water behind a dam. Her grandmother had sung it while shelling peas, while burying cats, while dying slow in a bed that smelled of yarrow and smoke—and she had never once explained what it was *for*, only that some songs weren't sung *to* anything. They were sung *as* something. A promise the world had made to itself before people existed to hear it.

*When the stars go quiet, sing them home.*

She hadn't understood until three nights ago, standing at a high window of the guildhouse, looking up at a sky gone utterly black—not night-black, which is never truly empty, but *swept*-black, scoured, vacant—and feeling the silence press against her ears like deep water. The stars didn't just shine, her grandmother had told her once, drunk on elderflower wine and honesty. They *sang*. Constantly. A chord so old and so vast that everything alive was built inside it, the way a pearl is built inside the shell's slow patience. And when the stars went out—

Well. When the stars went out, someone had taken the singers.

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. Wren counted two hundred steps and then stopped counting because the numbers started to feel like a lie. The air changed first—it went cool, then cold, then *old*, a cold that had never known sun, that had been waiting down here since before the palace above had a name. It smelled of wet flint and something sweeter underneath, faint and wrong, like flowers rotting in honey.

"Ansel," she whispered, though whispering felt foolish when the song was this loud. "It's close."

"I feel it too." Ansel's hand found her shoulder in the dark—light, steadying, brief. "Like standing near a struck bell. In my teeth."

That startled her. Ansel wasn't a listener; Ansel was a keeper, a counter, a person who trusted ink over instinct. But the reservoir—if it was real, if the song wasn't lying to her—wouldn't care what kind of person you were. Some things pressed themselves into anyone who came near. That was how you knew they were true.

The stairs ended at a door.

Not a grand one. Not a sealed vault door with wards and warnings, nothing like the guild's own memory-vault with its iron ribs and its locks within locks. This was a door of plain grey wood, unremarkable as a cupboard, set into the rock without frame or hinge-ornament, and it was *open*—ajar by a hand's width, breathing. Air moved through the gap in a slow rhythm, in and out, in and out, and Wren realized with a lurch that the door was breathing with the song. Or the song was breathing with the door. She could no longer tell which was the tide and which was the shore.

Beyond the gap: light.

Not lantern-light. Not torchlight. Nothing burned down here; she would have smelled smoke. This light was silver-white and soft-edged and it moved, rippling slowly against the walls of whatever lay beyond, the way light moves on the underside of a bridge when water passes below.

"Stars," she said, and her voice cracked on the word. "Ansel. Those are *stars*."

Ansel eased the door wider, careful as a thief at a lock, and the light spilled out over them both, and Wren forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The cavern was vast beyond sense—a cathedral hollowed from the mountain's root, ceiling lost in darkness overhead, walls sweating mineral sheen—and across its floor, filling every hollow and channel of the rock like rain filling the seams of a field, lay the water.

Starlight-water.

It pooled in basins nature had never intended, overflowed in slow silver rivulets, gathered in channels carved—no, not carved, *worn*—by centuries of patient flow, and everywhere it moved it sang. That was the thing that undid her. The song in her skull, the hedge-song, the pull that had dragged her down two hundred nameless steps—it wasn't coming from her anymore. It was coming from *them*. From the water. Thousands of small voices braided into one vast chord, each thread distinct if she listened sideways: a high bright note that flickered like laughter, a low warm one that hummed like a mother working at a loom, one that stuttered and wept, one that rang clear as a bell over frozen fields. Every star she had ever wished on was down here, drowned and singing, and the sound of it was so beautiful that it hurt the way grief hurts, the way homesickness hurts, the way love hurts when the beloved is far away and cannot be reached.

"They're imprisoned," Wren whispered. "They're actually—" Her knees gave and she sat down hard on the wet stone, not caring. "Grandmother. You knew. You *knew* this was here."

"What is it?" Ansel crouched beside her, lantern shuttered now, unnecessary. Starlight washed their sharp face silver, softened it, made them look briefly young. "Truly. Not the song version. What am I looking at?"

Wren tried to answer and found her vocabulary failing her. She was a hedge-witch, daughter of hedges, raised on remedies and weather-sense and the small practical magics of small practical people. She knew words for foxglove and fever, for the difference between a haunting and a draft. She did not know words for this.

"The sky," she managed finally. "Someone took the sky and poured it into a cellar." She crawled forward on her hands and knees to the edge of the nearest pool, close enough to see that the light wasn't reflection—it was *in* the water, suspended in it, thousands of points of radiance drifting like seeds in still air, and around each point the water bent slightly, curving toward it, holding it, the way a fist holds a moth. Gentle. Enormous. Absolute. "Forty years ago. The coronation. Ivo said the stars dimmed for one night, and everyone forgot by morning, and that's the trick of it, isn't it? You can steal anything if you also steal the noticing."

Ansel had gone very still—the particular stillness Wren had learned to read as the ledger-keeper's mind moving fast, pages turning somewhere behind those grey eyes. "The wards," Ansel said quietly. "The border wardstones hummed wrong for months before they fell. Vess sent me to inspect them. I wrote in my report that they sounded like a struck bell going silent." A pause. "I thought the bell was cracking. I think—I think the bell was being *unhung*. The wards were fed by royal memory. Everyone says it like a legend, but legends are just bookkeeping nobody wants to do. And if the crown has been feeding the king's memories to something for forty years—"

"—then the memories are the stars," Wren finished. "Or the stars are the memories. Or there's no difference and there never was." She looked down into the pool, into the drifting lights, and one of them drifted nearer, drawn perhaps by her listening, and she saw—she *felt*—a life in it. Small and enormous at once. A child on a wall watching hawks. A first snow. A terror of open doors. A name spoken by a mother whose face was worn smooth as a river stone from use. *The king,* she realized. Pieces of him. Decades of him, harvested year by year, poured down here like wine into a cellar, kept fresh, kept *alive*, singing in the dark because that was what living memory did—it sang, whether anyone listened or not.

And something up in the throne room drank the song.

Her stomach turned. "It doesn't kill them," she said. "The memories. It *keeps* them. Fresh. That's worse. That's so much worse than killing them."

"Why?" Ansel asked, but softly, the question of someone who already suspected the answer and needed to hear it said aloud.

"Because dead things stop wanting." Wren wrapped her arms around her knees. "These want *home*. Can't you hear it? Under the singing? Every one of them is trying to get back to him. Forty years of trying. That's the hunger the crown feeds on—it doesn't eat the memories, it eats the *longing*. It keeps them alive so they keep reaching, and it drinks the reaching forever." Her eyes stung. "My grandmother used to say the cruelest cages are the ones that keep the bird healthy."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The reservoir sang. Somewhere far above, muffled through leagues of stone, the city slept under a sunless sky that stretched on past any honest midnight, and people were forgetting their children's names in the streets, and the Hollow Guard patrolled with their emptied eyes, and the thing wearing the king grew fatter and hungrier and more afraid.

Then Ansel said, in a voice stripped of all its ledger-flatness, raw as a scraped knee: "We can give them back."

Wren looked up. "What?"

"We can carry them back. To him. The king." Ansel was staring into the water, and the starlight reflected in their eyes made twin small skies there. "If the memories return, the wards rebind. The stars go home. The thing in the crown starves. One delivery. The biggest job the guild ever ran, and the client doesn't even know he's the client."

Something in Wren's chest lifted—hope, terrible hope, hope with hooks in it—and then she looked back down at the water and the hooks set themselves.

Because she had touched the edge of the pool. Just her fingertips, just for a moment, when she'd crawled close. Just to see if it was warm or cold.

She looked at her fingers now.

The starlight was still in them.

Faint—three or four tiny points sunk into the skin of her fingertips like splinters of glass made of glow—but unmistakable, and they were *moving*, drifting slowly up her fingers toward her palm, toward her wrist, toward the place where a person keeps the self that makes them a person, and around each point her flesh curved gently, held it, the way the water held them. The way a fist holds a moth.

"Ansel," she said. Very calmly. Calm the way the middle of a fire is calm. "Don't touch the water."

Ansel froze mid-reach. "Wren—"

"It takes you." She watched the lights travel, slow as sap, patient as roots. "It doesn't hurt. That's the trap. It feels like being *wanted*. Like being remembered. Like—" her breath hitched, "—like belonging. It pours itself into you and you pour yourself out to make room, and it's gentle the whole way down, and one day you look in a mirror and there's a stranger wearing your face who knows the king's childhood better than his own name." She pulled her hand back and pressed it flat against her sternum, over the ache, over the song. "Touching the reservoir unmakes the toucher. Grandmother's rule number one: some doors only open inward."

"How many got in?" Ansel's voice had gone tight, controlled, the control of a person gripping a ledge.

"Three. Maybe four. Tiny ones. I can feel them." She closed her eyes and listened sideways, the way she'd taught herself, and yes—there they were, four small voices inside her, four small lives, humming against the inside of her skin. A child on a wall. A first snow. A terror of open doors. A mother's voice saying a name. *His* name. The king's name, worn smooth as a river stone. "But here's the thing, Ansel. Here's the thing I need you to hear."

She opened her eyes and turned to face them, kneeling at the water's edge with starlight caught in her hair and in her blood and in the terrible beautiful chord pouring through the cavern, and she said:

"They're not fighting me. They're not eating me. They're just—*reaching*. Reaching for home. Same as they've done for forty years in this water. And I'm the closest thing to home they've found." Her voice shook and she let it. "Which means they can be *carried*. Which means someone could walk into this water on purpose, fill themselves with all of it, every stolen year of that poor man's life, and walk it back up to him. And it would unmake them completely—there's forty years in there, Ansel, decades of a whole human soul, and no vessel holds that much and stays a vessel. But it could be *done*. The song says so. The song has been saying so since before I was born. *When the stars go quiet, sing them home.* Sing them home yourself. Be the road."

Ansel had gone white in the starlight. "And the someone who does it?"

"Goes with them." Wren smiled, and it was not a happy smile, but it was not an unhappy one either; it was the smile of a hedge-witch who had finally learned what her grandmother's lullaby was for, and wished she hadn't. "All the way home. And doesn't come back."

The silence that followed was total except for the singing—which was not silence at all, which was the opposite of silence, which was ten thousand voices reaching for a man who had forgotten he was missing them.

Ansel knelt beside her at the edge of the water, and did not touch it, and looked into it for a long time. Wren watched their face and saw the ledger-keeper doing arithmetic no ledger should hold: weighing a king against a friend, a kingdom against a person, the whole sky against one pair of hands. She saw them arrive at an answer. She saw them decide not to say it yet.

"We should go back," Ansel said at last, rising, offering her a hand up. "Everyone needs to hear this. Not just us. Nobody carries this alone—that's how the crown wins, it makes everything solitary, it picks people off one at a time—" They stopped. Their jaw worked. "Wren. Your hand."

"I know about my hand."

"Four of them. Inside you. Already."

"Yes."

"And if we don't find a way to send them home soon—"

"Then I become their home instead." Wren flexed her fingers, watching the small lights drift, patient as roots, gentle as drowning. "And honestly? There are worse fates. They're good memories. He was a good man, once—you can tell, even in pieces, you can *tell*—" 

She stopped.

Because the water had changed.

Not the light—the light went on drifting, serene, eternal. The *song* changed. Deep in the reservoir, far out past the shallows where they knelt, something stirred, and the ten thousand voices faltered together, a chord bending, and for one horrible instant Wren heard it clearly: not the reaching, not the longing, but something else underneath, something that had been down here so long it had learned to wait.

A voice, vast and wet and patient, rising through the starlight like a bubble through black water, and it did not speak to her in words so much as in *certainty*, arriving fully formed in her mind the way the hedge-song had arrived, the way truth arrives, unwelcome and complete:

*You found me,* it said. *How clever. Now I don't have to come find you.*

The lights in Wren's fingertips flared white-hot.

Above them, through leagues of stone, through the breathing door, through two hundred nameless steps, the long sunless night outside leaned closer—and somewhere in the palace above, Wren would swear until her dying day, something wearing a crown began, very softly, to laugh.
