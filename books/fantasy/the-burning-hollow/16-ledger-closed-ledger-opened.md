## 16. Ledger Closed, Ledger Opened

The safehouse smelled of tallow and wet stone, of the river that ran beneath the city like a buried vein. Ansel sat at the long table with the ledger open before them, and for a long while they did not write anything at all.

They had gotten good, over the years, at saying hard things in soft ways. It was part of the trade. A memory-thief learns to hold a knife so it looks like a gift. But there was no soft way to say this one. There was no way to fold the truth into something small enough to swallow.

Wren sat across from them, elbows on the table, her hedge-witch's hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea she had let go cold. Sefa was mending a forged seal by candlelight, her needle moving with the patience of someone who had unpicked a hundred lies and sewn them back tighter. Ivo dozed in his chair by the fire, though Ansel knew better than to trust the dozing; old Ivo remembered too much to ever truly sleep. And Brannic—Brannic had come back to them three nights ago, walked out of the Hollow Guard's dungeons with his wrists scarred and his mind strangely whole, as if the thing beneath the palace had looked inside him and found nothing it wanted. He had laughed about it. He was laughing about it now, quietly, sharpening a pick he no longer needed.

Ansel watched them all, and their chest ached in a way they could not ledger.

*Tell them,* said the voice in their head that sounded like Vess, back when Vess still spoke to them. *Tell them what it costs.*

But first Ansel turned the page. First they wrote down everything they knew, because writing made it real, and real things could be fought.

"The reservoir is real," Ansel said at last, when the candles had burned down two fingers' worth. "Wren found it. Under the palace, past the old cisterns, behind a door that isn't a door—it's a wound in the stone that learned to look like one." They glanced at Wren, who nodded once, gravely, the way she nodded at things that deserved gravity. "The king's memories are there. Forty years of them. Every birthday, every grief, every face he ever loved—they're pooled in the dark like starlight in water. Because that's what they are. That's where the stars went."

Sefa's needle stopped.

"Every star that guttered out on the night the wards fell," Ansel went on, "every star that has gone missing since the coronation—the crown-thing drank them. Not from the sky. From him. It ate his remembering until there was nothing left in the man but the shape of a man, and it hung what it took in the dark beneath the throne room, feeding on it slow, the way a spider feeds on a wrapped fly."

"And if we take it back?" Brannic asked. He always asked the practical question first. It was one of the things Ansel loved about him and would never say aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a memory, and memories were currency now, and some things Ansel refused to spend.

"If we take it back, the wards can be rebuilt. The stars come home. The king—" Ansel paused. "The king might wake up. Might. He is worn thin as vellum. There may not be enough man left under all those roots."

"But?" Sefa said. She was watching Ansel's hands, which were flat on the table, very still, the way they went still whenever Ansel was about to lie or about to tell a truth too large for lying beside. "There's a but. There's always a but with you, Lockridge. You get that furrow right here, between your eyes, right before you ruin everyone's evening."

Ansel felt the furrow form and did not fight it.

"But the memories have to be carried," they said. "By a person. Through the water. They won't go home on their own—they've been held too long, bound too tight, and the reservoir unmakes anything that touches it without a vessel to pour into. Someone has to walk into that starlight and let it fill them. All of it. Forty years of a king's life, poured through one human heart." They looked down at the ledger, at the blank space waiting under their hand. "And the carrier doesn't come out the other side."

Ivo stirred in his chair. His eyes opened, milky and clear at once, the way old men's eyes are.

"How much doesn't come out?" he asked.

"All of it," Ansel said. "Everything. You forget your name. You forget your mother's face. You forget how to grieve and how to hope, because both of those need a past to stand on. You come out breathing. You come out walking. You come out—" they searched for the word and hated every candidate— "*empty.*"

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone is doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

It was Wren who broke it, because Wren had never once in her life left an ugly silence standing when she could fill it with something true.

"My grandmother used to say the dead don't leave us," she said, turning her cold tea slowly, watching the leaves spin. "They just stop being able to answer. This is worse than dying, isn't it. Dying keeps you somewhere. This just... takes you out of the story."

"Yes," Ansel said. Because Wren deserved the clean version.

Brannic set down his whetstone. He looked at his scarred wrists, then at each of them in turn, and Ansel saw the decision arrive in his big blunt face the way weather arrives over hills—slowly, then all at once.

"I'll carry it," he said.

"You will not," said Sefa.

"I'm the strongest. If the water pulls—"

"It's not a question of strength, you great oak-hearted idiot," Sefa snapped, and her voice cracked in the middle of the snap, which was how Sefa's voice announced that it was speaking from somewhere below the armor. "It's a question of what gets erased. You think I want to walk out of that reservoir into a world where you don't remember holding that doorway? Where you don't remember any of us? You'd look at me and see a stranger with ink-stained fingers, and I'd have to introduce myself to you every morning for the rest of my life, and every introduction would be a little funeral."

Brannic blinked at her. "You'd introduce yourself?"

"Of course I'd introduce myself. Daily. With forged credentials if necessary." Her jaw trembled. "That's not the point."

"I'll do it," said Ivo.

Everyone turned. The old man had risen from his chair, one hand on its back, standing as straight as seventy years and a bad hip allowed.

"Ivo—" Ansel began.

"Hear me out, boy. Girl. Whatever you are this decade—I stopped tracking." He smiled, and the smile was gentle and terrible. "I am the guild's oldest member. I remember too much—that's my curse, my specialty, the reason Vess kept me even after my hands shook too badly to pick a lock. I remember the coronation. I remember the stars dimming. I remember forty years of jobs and faces and funerals, and I promise you, child, most of what I carry is weight, not treasure. My wife has been dead twenty years. My knees hurt. My best years are ledger entries now." He spread his hands. "What would you actually be taking from me? Pain and pride. I've had my share of both. Let me spend them on something."

Ansel's throat closed.

Because that was the trap of it, wasn't it. That was the cruelty woven into the plan like a silver thread through black cloth: the mission needed a volunteer, and every one of them had a reason, and every reason was love wearing work clothes.

Wren spoke next, quiet as banked coals. "I hear the silence, Ansel. I'm the only one who can hold the song steady in that water. But if I go under carrying him—if I'm the vessel—then nobody holds the song. The memories scatter. So it can't be me, and I hate that, and I want it noted in whatever book you keep that I hate it." She looked up. "But it also can't be Brannic, because Sefa's right, and it can't be Sefa, because someone has to forge our way back out, and it can't be Ivo, because—" she faltered, because how did you say it— "because I only just got a grandfather. I'm not giving him back to the dark."

"So it's me," Brannic said again, stubborn as bedrock.

"So it's me," said Ivo, equally stubborn, older bedrock.

They argued the way families argue—not to win but to be counted. Ansel sat among them and listened, and inside their chest something tore along a seam that had been holding since childhood, and through the tear poured a feeling they had spent thirty years trading away whenever it grew inconvenient:

They could not lose any of them.

Not one. Not Brannic's laugh, which came out of him like a landslide learning joy. Not Sefa's needle-flash fury and the way she cheated at cards and thought no one noticed. Not Ivo's stories, told and retold until they wore grooves in the air. Not Wren's song, which had pulled four broken thieves through a sunless city like a rope through a storm.

And so Ansel did the thing they were best at in all the world.

They lied.

---

Later, when the others had gone to their pallets—Brannic snoring like a contented bear, Sefa asleep over her half-mended seal, Ivo dreaming whatever old men dream, Wren humming the hedge-song low in the dark like a lighthouse made of breath—Ansel stayed at the table.

The candles were stubs now. The fire was embers. The safehouse breathed around them, settling, sighing, keeping its own small memories in its beams.

Ansel opened the ledger to the page where the plan lived. Their handwriting marched across it in columns, neat as fence posts: routes, timings, guard rotations, the door-that-was-a-wound, the depth of the water, the number of minutes a body could burn in starlight before the burning became erasure. Everything measured. Everything accounted.

Except the one column that mattered.

*Carrier:* the entry read, in Ansel's careful clerk-hand. And beneath it, the candidates, listed fair and square, exactly as the parliament of thieves had argued them: *B. Oakfist — viable. I. Marsh — viable, preferred by council. S. Quill — nonviable (essential exfil). W. Thornsong — nonviable (song-anchor).*

Four names. Two struck through. Two remaining.

Ansel dipped the pen.

They thought, for a moment, of Vess. Of the guildmaster who had taught them the trade, who had bought them off a slaver's cart when they were nine years old and all elbows and silence, who had put a ledger in their hands and said, *the world steals from everyone, child; our sin is only choosing what.* Vess had forbidden this mission. Vess had called it suicide dressed as heroism. And maybe Vess was right. Maybe this page was the most dishonest thing Ansel had ever written, and Ansel had once forged a bishop's entire life.

The pen touched the paper.

*I. Marsh,* Ansel wrote, and drew a single line through the old man's name—thin, almost apologetic, the kind of strike-through a forger uses when they want the forgery found but not understood. In the margin, in smaller script, they added: *declined on grounds of health. Hands unfit for the crossing. Council concurred.*

No council had concurred. No such council existed anymore. But Ivo's hands did shake. The lie had a true bone in it, and forgery, done properly, was mostly true bones arranged falsely.

Then the second line. *B. Oakfist,* Ansel wrote, and struck it, and beneath it: *withdrawn. Carrier must be light enough to swim the reservoir channel; subject exceeds safe displacement.*

This lie had no true bone at all. Brannic had swum rivers heavier than himself his whole life. But it sounded plausible. It sounded like the kind of thing a careful planner would note, and planners were trusted precisely because they seemed to eliminate options for boring reasons rather than desperate ones.

Two names remained possible. Ansel needed one.

They turned the page. On the fresh leaf they wrote the summary entry, the one that would be read aloud in the morning, the one that would decide everything:

*Final determination: the carrier must meet three conditions. One: small enough displacement to cross the channel. Two: no essential function in the operation's success beyond the carrying itself. Three: no living dependents whose grief would compromise the guild's continuity.*

Ansel read the third condition back to themselves and almost laughed at the elegance of its cruelty. *No living dependents.* As if belonging were a liability to be disclosed on a form. As if the whole point of the last ten years hadn't been the slow, deliberate construction of dependents—a family assembled from runaways and failures and people the world had misfiled, glued together with ledgers and jokes and the fierce ordinary miracle of showing up.

But the condition was true, in the way that mattered to a ledger. Ansel had no blood kin. Had never married. Had spent decades making themselves unmemorable, unattached, a ghost with excellent penmanship. On paper—in the only language the plan could read—Ansel Lockridge was the cheapest life in the room.

On paper.

Ansel set down the pen and pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes until the darkness bloomed orange.

*You're stealing from them,* whispered the voice that sounded like Vess. *The worst theft of your career. You're stealing their chance to give themselves away.*

*Yes,* Ansel answered silently. *Yes, I know.*

Because that was the shape of it, finally, laid bare on the table among the candle stubs: love expressed as deception. The guild traded memories, and Ansel had always believed memory was the truest coin. But tonight they had learned the denomination above it. Tonight they understood that you could steal a person's sacrifice, and that sometimes it was the only gift left worth giving—to take the wound meant for them and carry it yourself, and let them hate you for it later, if there was a later, if there was a them to do the hating.

If it worked, they would never know what they'd done. That was the mercy of it. Brannic would rage, Sefa would call it the worst forgery of Ansel's career—and she'd be right, she'd be gloriously right—but they would rage at a ledger, at ink, at a dead woman's careful hand, and not at a friend slipping out of the world.

If it failed, none of it would matter anyway.

Ansel blotted the page. Signed it. Dated it in the old guild cipher, the one only four people alive could read, and two of them were asleep upstairs.

Then they did one more thing. A small thing. A thief's thing.

They took out the private page—the one they kept folded against their heart, the one page of the ledger Vess had ordered burned and Ansel had refused to burn. The royal seal, forty years old, pressed in wax gone amber with age. On the back of it, over the years, Ansel had written the things they could not file anywhere else. Names. Dates. The night Wren arrived half-drowned and singing. The day Brannic held the doorway. Sefa's laugh, described in words because words were all Ansel trusted themselves to keep.

At the bottom of that page, in fresh ink, Ansel added a final line:

*If you are reading this, I did not come back. Do not look for reasons. Look for each other. Belonging isn't remembered. It's remade. Build it again without me, the way you built it with me—out of nothing, out of scraps, out of showing up. That's the whole trade. That was always the whole trade.*

They signed it with a name instead of initials. Their full name. Ansel Meridian Lockridge. They had not written it out in full in twenty years; names were dangerous, names were handles, names were how the world got a grip on you. But a grave-marker should bear a whole name, even a marker made of paper, even a grave that was really a reservoir full of drowned starlight.

Ansel folded the page back against their heart.

Outside, the false night pressed against the shutters, deep and starless and hungry, the longest night the city had ever known, still stretching, still refusing the dawn. Somewhere out in that dark, citizens were forgetting their children's names in the streets. Somewhere beneath the palace, the crown-thing coiled through its dying king, drinking, always drinking.

And tomorrow—or what passed for tomorrow, in a world where the sun had lost its nerve—the crew would gather around this table and read the ledger, and discover that the mathematics of sacrifice had been quietly, lovingly, unforgivably rigged.

Ansel blew out the last candle and sat for a moment in the perfect dark, listening to the house breathe, memorizing the sound of it. The snore. The hum. The distant song.

Some things you cannot write down. Some things you carry the only way anything true can be carried: by being there when it happens, one last time.

In the morning, the ledger would open, and the lie would hold, and the march would begin.

Ansel touched the folded page over their heart once, gently, the way you touch a wound you've decided to keep.

Then they climbed the stairs to say goodbye to everyone they loved, without telling them that's what it was.
