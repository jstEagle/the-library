## 17. The Longest Night Marches

The sun did not rise.

It was not that the sky refused it. It was not cloud, or storm, or any weather the old farmers of the river country had names for. The sun simply failed to arrive, the way a friend fails to arrive when something has happened to them on the road—no message, no explanation, only the empty chair and the cold tea and the terrible arithmetic of waiting.

Ansel stood in the guildhouse doorway at what should have been dawn and watched the city understand.

Lamplighters went out on schedule, out of habit, snuffing their flames one by one along Candlemaker's Row—and then stopped, confused, and relit them, because the dark did not lift. Bells rang matins and then rang again an hour later, uncertain, as if the bell-ringers themselves could not agree on what hour it was. Somewhere a rooster crowed three times and gave up entirely, falling into a silence that felt less like sleep than surrender.

"It's stretching," Wren said beside her. She had not slept. None of them had. Her voice was raw from singing to herself through the small hours, the hedge-song held like a blanket around her shoulders. "The night. It's eating the morning. Can't you feel it? It's *hungry*."

Ansel could feel it. That was the worst part. The hunger was no longer distant, no longer a thing behind palace walls or beneath the earth. It was in the air now, the way damp is in the air before rain—a pressure, a leaning-in, a vast attention turning toward the city the way a face turns toward a candle.

Below them, in the street, a woman stood very still in front of her own door, holding a child by the hand. She was looking at him with the particular blankness of someone trying to recall a word in another language. The child tugged her hand. "Mama. Mama, we'll be late for—" And the woman said, gently, kindly, in the voice you use with strangers: "Little one, where is your mother? Let's find her, shall we?"

The child began to cry.

Ansel turned away from the window. There was nothing to be done for the city—not yet, not from here, not with words. There was only the plan, and the plan was a knife with one edge, and Ansel had already sharpened it against everyone they loved without their consent.

"Time to go," they said.

* * *

They went out into the unraveling dark in ones and twos, so as not to draw the eye, though Ansel suspected there was no eye left to draw—that the Hollow Guard had become something else in the long night, something with fewer eyes and more hunger.

Sefa walked ahead as a bishop. It should have been absurd. A dead man's vestments, a forged seal of blessing, a crozier Brannic had welded from a curtain rod and candle-sconce in four hours of profane craftsmanship. But Sefa wore grief like a garment—the real grief of a real dead bishop whose papers she had studied until she could have mourned him at his own grave—and grief opened doors that seals alone could not.

"Hollow God keep you," she intoned to the frightened gate-watch at the Processional Bridge, blessing them with two fingers and a face like carved sorrow, and the watchmen bowed, because even hollowed men remember how to bow.

Brannic grumbled behind her, playing the acolyte, carrying the relic-box that did not contain a relic but contained Wren, folded up among the padding like a knife in a bread loaf. "Blessing *guards*," he muttered. "Forty years a lock-wright and I end my career as scenery in a fraud."

"You love it," Sefa murmured without moving her lips.

"I tolerate it at a volume approaching love," Brannic said, and Sefa's mouth twitched, and Ansel, walking behind them both in the gray robes of a mourning clerk, thought: *remember this, remember this, remember this*—and then remembered that remembering was exactly what they were marching to spend, and made themselves stop.

Ivo brought up the rear, and Ivo was the strangest sight of all, because Ivo was *weeping*. Not loudly. Old Ivo, who remembered too much, who carried forty extra years of other people's memories in his head like stones in a sack—Ivo walked through the starving city and looked at the people in it and cried silently, steadily, wiping his face with a sleeve already soaked.

"The baker on Tallow Street," he whispered to Ansel as they passed. "That man just forgot his own wedding. I can see it going out of him. Forty-one years, gone, and he doesn't even know to be sad." He wiped his eyes. "I used to curse this gift. Carrying too much. Now I'd give my teeth to carry theirs a while."

"That's the whole war," Ansel said quietly. "Right there. That's what we're fighting about."

* * *

The Processional Bridge was where the world came apart.

They were halfway across when the crowd ahead of them stopped moving—not the orderly stop of a checkpoint but the sudden congealed stillness of sheep scenting a wolf. Ahead, torchlight. Armor that reflected no torchlight at all, drinking it in, matte as charcoal. The Hollow Guard had formed a line across the bridge, and they were not checking papers.

They were *collecting*.

A guard moved down the stalled crowd with a lantern that gave off light the color of drowned things, and where the light touched, people swayed. Ansel watched a ferryman open his mouth and pour out his mother's laugh like water from a jug—watched it leave him as a faint silver curl of mist that drifted obediently into the lantern. The man blinked afterward, smiling vaguely, lighter somehow, emptied of something he would never know he'd had.

"They're harvesting in the open," Wren breathed from inside the box, her voice thin with horror. "Ansel. They're not even hiding anymore. The crown isn't rationing. It's *feasting*."

Because the night was long, Ansel understood. Because the wards were dead and the stars were caged and every scrap of belonging in the city was loose game now. The thing wearing the king had stopped pretending to be a king. It was simply a mouth.

"Bishop," Ansel murmured. "Your flock is troubled."

Sefa drew herself up, and Ansel watched her do the bravest ordinary thing Ansel had ever seen: she walked *toward* the line of guards, crozier high, robes snapping in the river wind, and called out in the bishop's rolling pulpit-voice:

"Children! Children of the Hollow God! You bar the bridge against your own shepherd on the longest night? Stand aside. These souls are under my *seal*."

For one heartbeat, one perfect heartbeat, it worked. The guards' blank faces turned toward her, and something in them—some residue of training, some fossil of deference—hesitated.

Then the nearest guard tilted his head, and said in a voice like a drawer sliding open:

"Shepherd. Show me your name."

And Sefa—who could forge anything, who had once counterfeited a pope's dream—opened her mouth and found that the guard was not asking for paper. He was asking for the thing underneath the paper. The memory of being named. The felt weight of a self.

"I—" Sefa said.

The lantern rose.

It was Brannic who saved them, because Brannic had spent the entire walk to the bridge doing what Brannic always did: paying attention to locks. "The parapet chain!" he bellowed, and hurled the relic-box sideways into the guard's knees, and the box burst open and Wren spilled out already singing.

The hedge-song tore across the bridge like a struck match.

It was not loud. It was never loud. It was a small dry thread of a melody, a grandmother's kitchen song, a lullaby for keeping stars—but it hit the harvested crowd and the Hollow Guard alike like cold water, and everywhere it touched, people *shuddered back into themselves*. The ferryman gasped and clutched his chest as if catching a falling thing. The woman from the doorway—wept over somewhere else now—looked down at her crying child and said "*Oh*—oh, sweetheart, Mama's here, Mama's sorry—" and gathered him up and ran.

The guards did not shudder back into anything. There was nothing left in them to return to. But they flinched, all together, like one creature, and in that flinch was a gap, and in the gap was a chance.

"RUN," Brannic roared, and the crew ran.

* * *

They fought the length of the bridge and the streets beyond it, and it was not a battle so much as a flood fighting its way uphill.

Hollow Guard converged from three directions, silent, tireless, wrong—men who did not duck quite humanly, who did not shout, who swung their cudgels with the patience of machines that had been told once, long ago, to clear a path and had never been told to stop. Brannic met the first of them with a pry-bar and the sound was like a churchbell falling downstairs. Sefa threw the fake relics—one after another, each blessed with a sincere obscenity—as distractions, and damned if half of them didn't work, because belief is belief, even forged. Ivo, astonishingly, fought like a man remembering how, which for Ivo meant fighting like six different men at once, borrowing stances from soldiers whose deaths he carried.

And Ansel cut through the middle of it all with a thief's economy, thinking with terrible clarity: *I am spending the last night of my life running through a city I will not remember, toward a death I chose in secret, and the only tragedy I can find in it is that Wren's song will be wasted on me.*

A cudgel caught Brannic across the shoulder and drove him to one knee. Ansel was there before the second blow fell—not brave, never brave, merely *fast*, the way ledger-keepers are fast with numbers—and put a knife-hand into the guard's throat and hauled Brannic up by the collar.

"You're supposed to survive this part," Ansel hissed.

"So are you," Brannic rasped, and there was something in his eyes—something that made Ansel's stomach drop, some flicker of the look a man gives a door he suspects has been locked against him on purpose. "Funny thing. Been meaning to ask. Why does the plan only work if it's *you*?"

"Later," Ansel said. "Reservoir first. Questions later."

"There won't BE a later, you lying little—"

"*MOVE.*"

Wren's song pulled them forward like a rope. Where she sang, the hunger thinned; the guards faltered; forgotten things stirred. She sang walking, bleeding voice, singing past the point where singing should have been possible, and Ansel understood that she knew—had maybe always known, since the night she heard the wards scream—exactly what the song was for. Not a weapon. A *handrail*. Something for the drowning to hold while the water did its work.

They came down off the Processional at last, into the old quarter, into the streets above the buried cisterns, and the city around them had gone quiet in the worst way. No screaming. No panic. Panic requires a self to panic *with*, and the city was losing its selves by the handful. People stood in doorways like suits of clothes with no one home. A man knelt in the middle of an intersection holding a small shoe, turning it over and over, his face serene and utterly vacant, not knowing why he couldn't stop.

"The entrance is here," Wren whispered, hoarse as ash, pointing to a seam in the earth behind a collapsed shrine. "Underneath. I can hear it. The stars are *singing back*, Ansel. They know we're coming."

Behind them, boots. Dozens. The last of the Guard, pouring down the hill like ink finding its level, and above it all—above the rooftops, above the starless sky—Ansel felt the attention of the crown turn toward them like a lighthouse beam, vast and patient and *amused*.

*I know what you're carrying,* the darkness seemed to say, gentle as a hand on the head. *Come. Bring it to me. I have waited forty years for a keeper foolish enough to deliver the vault to the vault.*

Ivo reached the seam first and wrenched the broken shrine-stone aside, revealing steps going down into breathing dark. One by one they dropped through—Sefa, then Brannic, then Ivo, then Wren, who paused at the lip and looked back at Ansel with eyes that held the whole hedge-song and more besides.

"Whatever's waiting down there," she said, "whatever you're not telling us—you should know we already decided we're not letting you do it alone. We voted. While you were brooding. It was unanimous. Brannic cried."

"I did NOT—" came Brannic's voice, echoing up from below.

"See?" Wren said. "Unanimous."

She dropped through. Ansel stood one moment longer at the edge of the underworld, the Hollow Guard thundering closer, the stolen stars singing below, the lie in their pocket burning like coal.

Then Ansel smiled—for the first time in the whole long night, truly smiled—said to the hungry dark, "You're too late. They've already ruined everything,"

—and jumped.
