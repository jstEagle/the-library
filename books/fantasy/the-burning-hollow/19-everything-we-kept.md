## 19. Everything We Kept

The reservoir opened beneath them like a held breath.

Wren felt it before she saw it—the hedge-song in her blood turning from a whisper to a roar, the way it always did when she came near something old and hungry. But this hunger was different. This was not the crown's gnawing, patient appetite. This was grief. Forty years of a man's life, pooled in the dark under the palace like starlight drowned in water.

She went first down the stair that Ivo's maps said should not exist, her bare feet finding stone worn smooth by no one's footsteps, and when she reached the bottom she stopped so suddenly that Brannic nearly walked into her back.

"Oh," she said. It was all she could manage.

The cavern was vast as a cathedral and lit from below. Water stretched to every wall—black water, except where it wasn't, except where light moved through it in slow coils and ribbons, silver-white and blue-edged, drifting like smoke underwater. Stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The stars that had gone out of the sky on the longest night were here, sunk in the king's stolen years, and they turned and drifted and pressed against the surface of the pool like moths against glass.

"They're alive," Sefa breathed behind her. "Wren. They're *alive* down there."

They were. Wren could hear them—not with her ears but with the place behind her sternum where her grandmother's songs lived. Each one was humming its own small note, and together they made a chord so sad and so beautiful that her eyes filled before she knew she was crying.

"It's been singing to itself for forty years," she whispered. "In the dark. Nobody listening."

Brannic set his lamp down with a click that sounded obscenely loud. He had come out of the Hollow Guard's cells three days ago with holes in him where memories used to be, and he still moved like a man walking on a frozen lake, testing every step. But his hands were steady when he unslung the pry-bar from his back.

"Then let's give it an audience," he said.

* * *

Ansel stood at the edge of the water and felt the ledger in their coat like a second heartbeat.

They had forged the page themselves, three nights ago, by candlelight while the others slept: the entry naming the carrier of the king's memory, written in their own careful hand, sealed with Vess's stolen signet. *One candidate. Ansel Lockridge.* A lie built out of love, because love was the only ink they'd ever trusted.

Now, looking at the reservoir, Ansel understood for the first time what they had signed away. The water did not look like death. It looked like *home*. That was the trap of it. Every stolen thing in that pool belonged to someone, and belonging called to belonging, and Ansel—who had spent a lifetime making themselves forgettable, who had traded away whole seasons of their own past to pay rent on a room no one would remember them in—felt the water reaching for them like a mother's arms.

*Come in,* the starlight seemed to say. *Be full. Be someone.*

"No," Ansel said aloud, and Wren glanced over, and Ansel shook their head. "Not yet. Order matters."

Sefa was already working. She had spread her kit on the wet stone—inks ground from ash and salt and crushed pearl, pens cut from goose feather and crow—and around her knees she had arranged forty small vellum tags, each blank, each waiting. Beside her sat Ivo, ancient and straight-backed, his clouded eyes fixed on the water with an expression Ansel had never seen on him before. Not fear. Recognition.

"I know this place," Ivo said quietly. "I've dreamed it since the coronation. Forty years of dreams and I thought they were only dreams." He turned his face toward Sefa. "Names, girl. Give them names. Memory without a name is a river with no banks—it runs everywhere and holds nothing. But if you write the name of the one it belongs to—" his voice caught, "—then it can find its way home."

"That's the problem," Sefa said, her pen hovering. "I don't know his name. The king's name. No one does anymore. It's been eaten."

The silence that followed was the worst kind—the kind with a shape to it, a hole exactly the size of a man.

Then Wren began to sing.

It started low, almost under her breath, the old hedge-song her grandmother had taught her—the one about the silence where the stars should be. But as she stepped closer to the water the song changed, the way living songs do, taking in what it met. She sang the silence, and then she sang into the silence, and the stars in the pool began to rise.

They came up slowly, breaking the surface like fish rising to feed, and each one that breached threw light across the cavern walls, and in that light Ansel saw faces. Not clearly. Impressions—a woman laughing in a garden; a boy on a horse too big for him; an old man's hands folding a paper boat; a young king at a window, watching stars, memorizing them because he already suspected someone meant to take them away.

"There," Ivo said sharply, pointing with a shaking finger. "There. The coronation night. Look."

A single star-memory hung above the water, larger than the rest, dense with light. And inside it, faint as writing under erased ink, was a name.

"*Aldric,*" Ivo read, and wept without shame. "His name is Aldric. He wept when they crowned him, and now I know why. He knew. Some part of him knew what they were putting on his head."

"Aldric," Sefa repeated, and began to write.

She wrote it forty times, once on each tag, and around it she wove names out of thin air and thin truth—the names of the king's dead mother, invented but plausible; the name of his childhood dog, guessed from a star-memory's shape; the name of the nurse who must have sung him to sleep. Forgeries, every one. Lies, every one. And every lie anchored like a hook in deep water, because Sefa's lies had always been true enough to hold weight.

"Anchor lines," she said, tying tags to smooth stones, her hands quick and sure. "You throw them in, they sink, and everything down there grabs hold of its own name and stops drowning." She looked up at Ansel, and her grin was crooked and terrified and brave. "Don't you dare die before I finish these."

"No promises," Ansel said.

* * *

Brannic heard the Hollow Guard coming long before anyone else—the particular silence of men who had been emptied, moving in step because they no longer knew any other way to move.

"Company," he said, and took up his position at the stairhead without being asked, because that was what Brannic did. He held doors. He had held a door against six of them once and lost, and woken in a cell with pieces missing, and here he stood again at another door with the same six coming down the stairs, their lantern-light throwing their shadows up the wall ahead of them like a warning.

"Wren," he said calmly. "Sing louder."

She did. God, she did. Her voice filled the cavern and the starlight rose to meet it, and Brannic planted his feet at the top of the stair and swung the pry-bar in the wide, patient arc of a man who has decided exactly how much of himself he is willing to spend.

He spent it gladly. That was the thing none of them understood yet, standing in the light of a drowned sky—that Brannic had done the arithmetic in the cell, in the dark, and concluded that a life was a fair price for a family, and that he would pay it twice if the ledger required.

But that was later. First there was the door, and the swinging bar, and Wren's song rising over the sound of bodies on stone.

* * *

Ansel walked into the water.

It was warm. That was the cruelty no one had warned them about—it was warm as bathwater, warm as the kitchen of the guildhouse on a winter morning, warm as every good thing Ansel had ever traded away, and it closed over their boots, their knees, their waist, gentle as hands.

The memories rose to meet them.

They came like a tide recognizing its moon. Ansel felt the first of them enter—the boy on the too-big horse, galloping through a summer that had ended forty years ago—and it burned going in, it burned like swallowing a coal, and Ansel gasped and kept walking. Deeper. The water at their chest now. Another memory: a mother's lullaby in a language the court had forbidden. Another: the weight of a crown being lowered, and the cold of it, and the whisper beneath the gold that said *you will not need these anymore.*

*Aldric,* Ansel thought, holding the name like a rope. *Your name is Aldric. Hold on to your name.*

More came. They came faster. Decades of a life pouring into a body never built to hold it, and Ansel burned from the inside out, light showing through their skin like a lantern through paper. Their hands were translucent. Their hair was going white at the temples, then white entirely, then simply going. Somewhere far away Sefa was shouting, throwing her anchor-stones into the water, and each stone that sank sent a shudder through the pool as a memory found its name and stopped thrashing—but there were too many, there were *years* and *years*, and the ones without anchors came for Ansel instead, filling every empty space, and Ansel had spent a lifetime making empty spaces, had cultivated them like a garden, and now the garden was being planted with a kingdom.

*I'm forgetting,* Ansel thought, with strange clarity. *Good. That was always the plan.*

They forgot the ferryman's face, and the price of the crossing. They forgot the smell of the guildhouse kitchen. They forgot—

They forgot Wren's laugh.

No—they didn't. That was the miracle happening at the water's edge. Because Wren had stopped singing the hedge-song and started singing something else, something new, made of every story the crew had ever told about Ansel—the market job, the bottled first kiss, the time Sefa forged a bishop and got the vestments wrong—and the others had taken it up, Brannic bellowing between swings of his bar, Ivo's cracked voice weaving underneath, Sefa laughing and crying and writing names, and they were pouring their memories of Ansel into the water after them, into the burning, into the flood, so that even as the king's forty years scoured Ansel hollow, something else was arriving to fill the shape. Not the old memories. New ones. *Them.*

*We kept each other,* the song said, in Wren's voice, in all their voices. *We kept each other, we kept each other—*

Ansel went under.

And in the dark and the burning, holding a stranger-king's whole life against their dissolving heart, Ansel Lockridge felt the last of their own past gutter out like a star—

—and heard, impossibly, from very far above, the sound of people refusing to let go.

* * *

Above the water, the reservoir blazed like a piece of fallen sky. The stars were rising now, all of them, tearing free of the pool in streams of silver, streaming up the stairwell toward the wounded wards, toward the gutted heavens—and in the center of the light stood the place where Ansel had been, which was becoming a place where Ansel was not.

Wren sang until her throat tore. Sefa threw her last anchor and grabbed Ivo's hand. Brannic stood bleeding at the stairhead, holding the door one final time, watching the light take his friend apart.

And in the throne room overhead, something ancient coiled through a parchment-thin king felt its hoard tearing loose, root by root, year by year, and began—for the first time in forty years—to scream.

The stars were coming home.

And so, somehow, was Ansel.
