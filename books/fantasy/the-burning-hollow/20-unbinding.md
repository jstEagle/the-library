## 20. Unbinding

The memories came in like a tide with no shore.

Ansel had expected a river, perhaps—a current to swim, a thing with direction and banks. What they got was an ocean turned inside out. Forty years of a king's life poured through them all at once: the taste of rain on a palace balcony at seven years old, the weight of a mother's hand on a small shoulder, the smell of ink and tallow in a schoolroom where a boy who would be king learned his letters from a tutor with coffee-stained fingers. It did not flow. It *arrived*, whole and simultaneous, every moment of Aldric Vane's life pressing against the inside of Ansel's skull like light against the skin of a lantern.

They had thought they understood memory. They were, after all, a ledger-keeper of the Hollow Guild, a thief of the intangible, someone who had handled other people's pasts the way a jeweler handles stones—carefully, professionally, without ever letting one cut too deep.

They had understood nothing.

*Hold it,* Wren's voice said, somewhere far above, threaded through the hedge-song that filled the reservoir chamber like sunlight made audible. *Don't fight it and don't drink it. Carry it.*

Carry it. As if forty years of a man's life were a pack that could be shouldered. As if Ansel were anything more than a cup being asked to hold the sea.

The first thing to go was small, and that was how they knew it would work. A name—not their own; something older, something from before the guild, before Vess found them half-starved behind a fish-market in Corrow's Bend. A woman's face, warm and blurred, saying a word that might have been *Ansel* or might have been nothing at all. The memory went soft at its edges, then translucent, then it was simply not there, and where it had been there was a smooth white space, clean as an unwritten page.

Ansel felt the absence and, absurdly, felt relief.

Because they could feel what the memories were doing as they passed through. Each stolen year of the king's life, moving through Ansel toward the reservoir's surface, toward the frail figure waiting above, left Ansel a little thinner and the world a little brighter. The starlight pooled around their ankles rose as the king's remembered nights returned to it—one by one, the imprisoned stars were waking, trembling in the black water like moths remembering flight.

*That's it,* Wren sang, her voice cracking with effort and joy both. *Keep going. Keep going, Ansel, we have you—*

They did not have them. That was the arithmetic of it, the cold ledger-math Ansel had done alone in the dark weeks ago when they forged the reservoir roster and wrote their own name as the only carrier. To move this much memory required a vessel, and vessels emptied. Every year of Aldric Vane's life passing through Ansel displaced a year of Ansel's own. There was no way around it. There had never been a way around it. They had simply hoped, in the coward's corner of their heart, that love would find a loophole.

Love had not found a loophole. Love had found Sefa and Ivo down in the dark, anchoring the flood with forged names so the memories wouldn't scatter; Brannic somewhere above, bruised and half-hollowed but standing guard anyway; Wren singing until her voice bled. Love had found no loophole because none was needed. Love had simply decided to pay the price alongside them, and somehow that made the paying bearable.

Another memory went. This one was theirs—the guild hall at winter, everyone crowded around the long table, Sefa dealing cards she'd marked with invisible ink, Brannic complaining about the cold in a voice like grinding stone, Ivo telling a story no one believed and everyone let him finish. The firelight. The noise. The feeling—Gods, the feeling, the unbearable specific feeling of sitting in a room full of people who knew your worst trade and your best one and wanted you there regardless.

It thinned. It paled. It went.

And Ansel, dissolving, laughed.

* * *

The strange mercy of forgetting was that it happened gently, or seemed to, from the inside. Ansel had imagined erasure as violence—tearing, burning, the scream of a page ripped out. Instead each memory departed the way a guest departs a party late at night: quietly, with a glance back, leaving warmth where it stood.

The road to the capital went first, mile by mile, the ferryman's gray eyes and the hole in Ansel's chest where a childhood had been traded for passage. Then the guild's vault, its shelves of bottled years. Then Vess's face—or rather, the meaning of Vess's face, the particular sternness that had always been, if Ansel read it right, a kind of fierce and awkward love. The name *Vess* remained for a while, attached to nothing, a label on an empty jar. Then it went too.

Through it all, the king's life kept pouring.

Ansel began to know him. That was the terrible gift buried in the transaction: as Ansel emptied, they filled with Aldric Vane, and so they met the man they were dying to save. They stood with him at his coronation, twenty-two years old, hands shaking as the crown came down—and felt the first cold coil of the thing beneath the gold, tasting him, learning the shape of what he loved. They watched him try, over decades, to hold on: repeating his mother's name under his breath in council meetings, writing letters to dead friends he could no longer picture, staring at portraits of his own childhood trying to remember being the boy in them. They felt the hunger take each memory anyway, patient as rot, and felt the king grow quieter, smoother, more polite, less present—a portrait of a king hung where a man used to be.

*I'm sorry,* Ansel tried to say, though they no longer had a mouth, exactly, or a throat, or any certainty about who was doing the apologizing. *I'm sorry we took so long. Forty years. We should have come sooner.*

But the king could not hear them yet. He was above, at the reservoir's edge, held upright between Brannic and Ivo, a man worn thin as parchment, watching strangers carry his life home to him through a girl's song.

The crown-thing noticed them then.

It had been busy—Ansel felt it thrashing against the hedge-song, felt its hunger scrabbling at the walls of the reservoir like a rat in a flooding cellar—but now it turned whatever served it for attention upon the vessel carrying away its hoard, and Ansel felt its regard like frost forming on the inside of their disappearing skull.

*Thief,* it said. Not in words. In the sensation of hands going through your pockets. *Little thief. You of all things should understand ownership. These are mine. He gave them. They were payment. Everything is payment. Everything belongs.*

Ansel had no voice left to answer with. But they had, for a few moments more, a self, and in that self lived everything the guild had ever taught them about ledgers, and so they answered the only way a ledger-keeper answers a fraudulent claim.

They itemized.

*These are not yours.* The thought moved through the flood like a blade through water. *He wept when you took them. I've seen it—I am seeing it—he wept at his coronation, and you called it payment. Nothing given under teeth is given. You hold forty years of stolen goods, and I am the return of property to its rightful owner, and the guild always collects.*

The crown-thing screamed.

Not aloud—or yes, aloud too; later, Wren would say the whole mountain screamed, that the reservoir's black water stood up in spikes, that the stars in it scattered like startled fish. But mostly it screamed in the place where Ansel still existed, a sound like every bell in the world cracked at once, and through the scream Ansel felt it do the only thing left to it.

It pulled.

It hauled on its hoard like a drowning sailor hauling on a rope, trying to drag the king's memories back down into itself, and the flood reversed, and Ansel felt themselves caught between two hungers—the king's need to remember and the parasite's need to devour—with their own vanishing self stretched thin as the line between.

*Wren!* Someone shouted it—Sefa, maybe, or Brannic, or all of them at once. *She's flagging—hold the song—*

*I'm holding it!* Wren's voice, raw and furious and young. *I'm holding—grandmother, help me, help me—*

And then old Ivo did the thing that Ansel, even as they unraveled, would have paid any price to witness properly and instead received only in fragments: he began to recite. Names. Dates. The coronation, the year, the weather, the color of the queen's dress. The treaty of Harrow Ford. The plague winter and who had fed the city. Forty years of history that the guild's oldest thief had hoarded in a head that remembered too much, spoken aloud in a cracked and stubborn voice, anchoring the king's memories to the world the way Sefa's forged names anchored them to their owners.

*You cannot have it,* Ivo said to the darkness, conversationally, as if correcting a junior clerk. *I was there. I remember. And while one of us remembers, it is true.*

The flood steadied. The flood resumed. And Ansel, cup overflowing, vessel failing, carried on.

* * *

Near the end, there was almost nothing left of Ansel to do the carrying.

They had lost the guild. They had lost the road, the market where they'd recorded impossible trades, the wardstones humming wrong, the night without stars. They had lost their own name—they searched for it the way you search for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found only the shape of the searching. They were, by any honest accounting, no one: a function, a channel, a door standing open in a wall.

And yet.

At the very bottom of what remained, they found one thing that would not go. Not a memory—it had no image, no sound, no date. It was more like a direction. A leaning. The way a compass needle leans, not knowing north, only obeying it. Something in them pointed toward laughter they could no longer source, toward a hand on the shoulder they could no longer picture, toward a room with a fire in it that they could no longer find on any map of their erased life.

They clung to the direction. It was not much. It was everything.

The last of the king's memories moved through them: a final fragment, small and human, the oldest one, the first one—a boy of four years old in a garden, looking up at the evening's first star and asking his nurse who lit it, and being told that stars were lamps lit by everyone who had ever been loved, so that no one walking in the dark would ever be entirely alone.

Ansel carried that one home with particular care.

Then they were empty, and the flood was over, and the crown-thing tore loose.

They felt it happen from everywhere and nowhere, because by then Ansel was barely a place at all. The parasite came off the king the way a root comes out of stone—reluctantly, shrieking, in pieces. Starved of its hoard, cut off from the reservoir by the song and the names and the sheer collective refusal of a handful of thieves, it thrashed through the throne room above, scattering courtiers, clawing at the air for memories that no longer belonged to it. For one long moment it gathered itself into a shape—a crown of teeth, a ring of hunger, a circle that wanted to close around something, anything—

And the stars came back.

All at once, the way they had gone out. Through the high windows of the palace, through the mountain's broken veins, through the reservoir's surface where the freed starlight rose like bubbles reaching the top of a held breath—the sky reignited. Thousands of lamps lit by everyone who had ever been loved. The wards along the border woke with a sound like a great inhale, and the hollow-born thing, which had fed for forty years on a world gone dim, found itself suddenly standing in a blaze of belonging it could not touch, could not eat, could not unmake.

It dissolved. Not dramatically. That was the strangest part. It came apart the way fog comes apart at noon—thinning, paling, losing its edges, until the circle of teeth was a suggestion, then a rumor, then nothing at all, and the longest night finally, finally began to end.

In the silence after, a king sat up on the floor of his own throne room, forty years returning to him in a rush, and wept the way he had wept at his coronation—except that this time, no one was feeding on it.

* * *

Ansel drifted at the bottom of themselves, in the smooth white space where a life used to be.

There was no fear in it. That surprised them, insofar as there was still a *them* to be surprised. They had expected terror at the edge of erasure, and instead found something closer to rest—the feeling of a ledger balanced, a debt discharged, a job done well. They were a blank page. Blank pages were not tragedies. Blank pages were beginnings; every book they had ever loved had started as one.

The direction was still there. Fainter now. A compass needle with almost no needle left.

Somewhere above, muffled as if through deep water, voices were descending. A girl's voice, cracked and hoarse from singing, calling a name—*Ansel? Ansel!*—a name that meant nothing and yet landed somewhere, landed in the white space like a seed in snow. Footsteps. More voices, arguing, urgent, one of them low and gravelly and insisting on carrying someone who probably didn't need carrying anymore, since there was hardly anyone left to weigh.

Hands touched them. That was the last thing, the truest thing. Hands—several pairs of them, clumsy and desperate and gentle—gripping the empty vessel that had been a person, as if to say: *we know you're in there, whoever you are now, and we are not finished with you.*

Ansel could not remember why that should matter.

Ansel could not remember anything at all.

But the direction leaned toward those hands like a needle finding north, and in the last flicker of whatever they had been, at the absolute edge of the erasure, they thought—not in words anymore, words were gone, but in the pure grammar of a soul:

*Oh. So this is what I was keeping all along.*

The stars burned overhead. The king breathed. The long night loosened its grip on the world.

And down in the dark, surrounded by strangers who refused to act like strangers, a blank page waited to be written on.
