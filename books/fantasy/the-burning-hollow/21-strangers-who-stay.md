## 21. Strangers Who Stay

Wren had never heard the world sing before.

She'd heard it scream—she'd been hearing it scream since the night the stars went out, a shriek wound through every wardstone and threshold and sleeping hearth in the kingdom, the sound of protections starving. Her grandmother's hedge-song had taught her to listen for what the land felt, and for weeks the land had felt like an animal caught in a snare.

But now, standing on the palace terrace with ash still drifting down like grey snow, Wren heard something else. The wards were waking. She could feel them rising through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her ankles, up into her chest where she kept all the songs that mattered: a low, warm hum, like a struck bell finally allowed to finish ringing. The border stones, forty leagues away, would be singing too. She was sure of it. Everything old and patient in the world was exhaling at once.

The king stood at the terrace rail, and he was smaller than she remembered from the festival—the hollow pageant where his smile hadn't reached his eyes. He was frail now, worn thin as winter paper, his hands trembling against the stone. But his eyes. His eyes were full. That was the only way Wren could think of it. For forty years he had been a house with the furniture carried out of it, and now every room was refilled, crowded, overflowing—and he wept without any dignity at all, great silent gulps of it, remembering his mother's voice, his first snowfall, the name of the nurse who sang to him, forty years of a life coming home all at once.

Beside him stood Sefa, holding the forged decree she'd written three days ago under a dead bishop's seal, which had somehow become, through the alchemy of necessity and nerve, an actual royal proclamation restoring the wards' endowment. Ivo leaned on the rail nearby, muttering coronation dates to himself like a man counting coins, making sure they were all still there.

And below, in the courtyard, the dungeons were opening.

Wren heard the doors before she saw them—iron groaning, then voices, confused and blinking guards stumbling into daylight they didn't remember entering darkness to reach. The Hollow Guard, unmade as the crown-thing dissolved, waking with no orders in their heads and no hunger either. Just men. Just tired men who would spend years wondering where the time went.

Brannic came out last.

He filled the doorway the way he always did, shoulders first, like the world owed him passage. There was blood on his knuckles and a bruise blooming along his jaw, and he stopped on the top step and looked around the courtyard with an expression Wren had never seen on his face before: lost. Brannic, who could open any lock in the kingdom, who held doorways the way other men held opinions, stood in the open air and did not know why his body ached or whose faces he should be looking for.

Then he found them. All of them, at the terrace rail, looking down.

His brow furrowed. Something moved behind his eyes—a fish turning under ice, there and gone. He raised one massive hand, not quite a wave, more a question.

"Brannic!" Sefa called down, her voice cracking in the middle of his name in a way Wren had never heard from her, not once, not even when the Hollow Guard had nearly taken her in the vigil hall.

Brannic squinted up at her. "Do I—" he started, and stopped, and tried again. "You look like someone I ought to know."

"You do," Sefa said. "You absolutely do. Come up here and I'll tell you how much you owe me in card debts."

He climbed the stairs slowly, testing each step like the stones might be lying to him. When he reached the terrace he stopped in front of Sefa, studying her face with the same ferocious concentration he gave to tumblers and wards. "Sefa," he said at last, and the word came out rough, surprised by itself. "Your name is Sefa."

"That's right."

"I don't remember you." His jaw worked. "But I know—I know I'd take a blade for you. It's in my hands. They already know you." He looked at his own enormous hands as if they belonged to a stranger who happened to be very loyal.

Ivo made a sound low in his chest, half laugh, half grief. "That's how it works, lad," the old man said. "The head forgets. The hands keep the ledger."

* * *

They found Ansel in the throne room.

Wren had known, in the abstract, what the reservoir would cost. She had sung the hedge-song while Ansel took the king's memories into themselves; she had felt the starlight pass through her friend like a river through a reed, and she had felt the moment the reed began to come apart. She had kept singing anyway, because stopping was unthinkable, because the song was the only thing holding the flood steady, and because Ansel had looked at her—right at her, in the last moment their eyes still knew her—and smiled.

Not a frightened smile. A finished one. Like a ledger balanced.

Now Ansel sat on the steps below the empty throne, wrapped in a guard's cloak someone had fetched, and looked at all of them with perfect, gentle blankness.

"Hello," Ansel said.

One word. Two syllables. And Wren felt the sound go through the whole crew like an arrow through a shield wall. Sefa sat down hard on the bottom step. Ivo turned away and studied a tapestry as if it required auditing. Even Brannic, who didn't remember why any of this mattered, put a hand on the wall to steady himself, some part of him understanding that the room had just broken.

Ansel blinked at them, polite and faintly puzzled. "Am I supposed to know you? People keep telling me I am."

"Yes," Wren said.

Her voice came out steadier than she expected. She crouched down in front of Ansel, close enough to see that the eyes were still the same color, still quick, still cataloguing everything the way a thief's eyes did—even now, even empty. Whatever had been burned away, whatever the flood had taken, the person underneath the memory was still in there. Still watching. Still *there*.

"I'm Wren," she said. "You pulled me out of a ditch outside Marrowford four months ago. You complained the entire time about the mud. You said—" and here her throat closed, but she pushed through, because this was the work now, this was the whole work, "—you said nobody should have to be invisible *and* wet."

Ansel's head tilted. "That sounds like something I'd say."

"It is. It was." Wren sat down on the step beside them. "It will be again. That's the plan."

"What plan?"

"The one where we tell you everything," Sefa said, wiping her face with her sleeve with the brisk efficiency of a woman forging a document, "until you can't help but remember it."

* * *

They did not leave the throne room that day. Later, when the palace servants crept back to their duties, they found blankets and wine and candle stubs arranged around the dais steps like the remnants of a vigil—which, Wren supposed, is exactly what it had been, though no god was its object.

They told stories.

It began awkwardly. Sefa started, because Sefa always started, because silence offended her personally. She told the story of the Vellum Job, two years back, when she'd forged a merchant's entire identity—childhood, debts, a dead wife, a scar he never had—and the mark had recognized her forgery as more accurate than his own life. "And Ansel," she said, "who never laughs, who I had *never* heard laugh, made a sound like a kettle dying. Fell off a crate. I have witnesses."

"I don't remember laughing," Ansel said carefully.

"No," Sefa said. "But watch." And she told it again, better, adding details, the rain, the crate, the exact pitch of the kettle-sound, and Ansel's mouth twitched—not a laugh, not yet, but the ghost of a groove worn into stone, the path a thing had traveled so many times it could not entirely be erased.

Brannic told the story of the Saltcellar Vault, though he told it strangely, in the third person, about "a lock-wright I apparently know," describing how the vault's mechanism had defeated everyone until Ansel realized the lock wasn't listening to keys at all but to footsteps, and had spent six hours learning to walk in the warden's rhythm. "Whoever this Ansel is," Brannic rumbled, "they're patient in a way I'm not. Six hours of walking. In the dark. On purpose."

"You fell asleep twice," Ivo said. "Standing up. We took turns catching you."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did," Sefa said. "Wren has it in her song."

Everyone looked at Wren. She felt her ears go hot. It was true—she'd stitched the whole guild into hedge-verses over the autumn, the way her grandmother had stitched weather and births and grudges into hers, because songs were how her people kept what paper couldn't hold. "There's a verse," she admitted. "About Brannic sleeping upright. It rhymes."

"I want to hear it," said Ansel.

So Wren sang. Small and low at first, her grandmother's cadences carrying names they'd never been meant to carry: *the lock-wright dreams on his feet like a tree, the forger lies sweeter than truth ought to be, the old man remembers too much and too long, and the quiet one keeps us by writing our song.* Ansel listened with their head slightly bowed, and when she finished, they were quiet a long moment.

"I don't know any of those people," Ansel said. "But the song makes me feel—" They searched for the word, hands opening and closing. "Held. Like being set down somewhere after carrying something heavy."

"That's what family is," Ivo said simply. "In my experience. Which is considerable and mostly regrettable, except for the parts with all of you in it."

* * *

By the second day, the stories had developed rules, though no one announced them.

Rule one: no story ended with *and then Ansel forgot*. Every tale ended forward—with what came next, with the joke landing, with the debt repaid, with the door opened. The past was told as a road still going somewhere.

Rule two: grief was permitted only in small denominations. Sefa cried exactly once per story, timed like a professional. Brannic's mourning came out as gruff corrections—Ivo was misremembering the width of the Marrowford bridge, it was twelve feet, he'd measured it, and Ivo said *you measured it because you were scared witless, boy*, and Brannic said nothing, and that was its own kind of tears.

Rule three, invented by Wren and enforced ruthlessly: every story had to include one kindness Ansel had done and thought no one noticed. This was the richest vein. There were so many. The time Ansel had quietly rewritten the guild ledgers so a widow's debt appeared paid. The time they'd traded their own warm cloak to a ferryman rather than let Wren pay with a memory of her grandmother. The rent Ansel paid in stolen slivers of themselves, year after year, so no one else ever had to.

"You idiot," Sefa said, on the fourth evening, having just learned about the rent. "You absolute, meticulous, self-erasing idiot. You were paying to be forgotten so we'd never owe anyone anything."

"It sounds like me," Ansel said. They said that a lot now, and each time it landed differently—first like a stranger reading someone's letters, then like a student reciting, and lately, lately, like a person recognizing their own handwriting.

Because it was working. That was the miracle none of them dared name aloud. The stories were taking root. Not as memory—Wren understood the difference better than anyone, having grown up on a tradition that insisted memory lived in the blood and the song and the shared telling, not merely the skull. Ansel might never reclaim what the flood took. But they laughed now, sometimes—a real laugh, startled out of them by Sefa's impressions of Guildmaster Vess. They knew Brannic took his tea bitter and Ivo cheated only at dice, never cards, on principle. They knew the sound of Wren's song well enough to hum it, badly, while washing cups.

They were becoming, from the outside in, the person the stories described. And the person the stories described was someone Wren loved completely, which meant the stranger on the dais steps was becoming someone worth loving too, which meant—they had never actually lost Ansel at all. They had only been given the task of saying Ansel's name enough times to make it true again.

On the fifth night, the king came to them.

He walked slowly, leaning on a stick, dressed plainly, having spent five days doing the terrible arithmetic of his reign—forty years of tribute, of courtiers hollowed, of a kingdom fed piece by piece to the thing on his head. He looked older than forty years of kingship should make anyone, and lighter too, the way a drained wound looks when it finally closes.

"I'm told," he said, "that I owe my crown, my mind, and quite possibly my soul to a memory-thief who no longer remembers doing it."

"They weren't a thief," Wren said, before she could stop herself. Then, fairly: "Well. They were. But only the kind that gives things back."

The king smiled—a real one, reaching his eyes, and Wren thought: *so that's what it looks like.* "Then I'll need to repay a thief," he said. "Name the price."

Wren looked at her strange, stubborn, half-remembered family scattered across the dais steps—Sefa already composing the negotiation in her eyes, Brannic pretending not to care, Ivo openly delighted, and Ansel watching the king with that quick, cataloguing gaze, seeing perhaps for the first time the shape of what they had done.

"There's a debt outstanding," Wren said. "A rent, unpaid. Someone spent years buying their own forgetting, and we intend to buy it back—one story at a time, if that's what it costs. So here's the price, Your Majesty: a fresh ledger. Good paper. And a place at your table for six people the histories will insist were never here."

The king considered this with the gravity of a coronation. "Done," he said. "Though I warn you—my archivists are worse gossips than my spies."

When he had gone, Sefa produced, from somewhere in her infinite coat, a small notebook bound in green leather, and tossed it to Ansel, who caught it cleanly, hands remembering what the head did not.

"What's this?" Ansel asked.

"A new page," Sefa said. "First entry's yours. Make it a good one—we'll be checking your work for the rest of your life."

Ansel turned the book over in their hands. Around the fire, the family went quiet, waiting, the way Wren's people waited when a new song was about to be born. Ansel looked at each of them in turn—at Brannic, at Ivo, at Sefa, at Wren—and something flickered behind their eyes, not memory, not yet, but the unmistakable warmth of a hearth in a dark room.

"I don't remember any of you," Ansel said slowly. "But I know I'd take a blade for every one of you. It's in my hands." A pause. The ghost of the kettle-laugh. "Apparently that runs in the family."

They opened the ledger to the first clean page, and wrote.
