## 22. A New Page

The mornings had a shape now.

Ansel learned it the way they'd once learned lock mechanisms—by touch, by repetition, by the patient accumulation of small certainties. The third stair from the bottom of the guildhouse creaked in the middle, not at the edge. The kettle sang a half-tone flat unless you set it just so over the coals. And every morning, without fail, Wren was already awake, sitting cross-legged on the windowsill of the common room with her bare feet against the cold glass, watching the sky as if she expected it to apologize.

It never did. But it kept its stars now, and that seemed to be enough for her.

"Morning," Wren said, not turning around. "You slept wrong again. You've got the crease from your sleeve on your cheek."

"I don't know how I usually sleep," Ansel said. It wasn't self-pity. They were still working out the tone of their own voice, testing each word like a coin bitten to see if it rang true. "I don't have anything to compare it to."

Wren turned then, and her laugh came—bright and unguarded and slightly too loud for the small room, like a bird that hadn't learned about hawks. Ansel felt it land somewhere beneath their ribs, in a place that had no name and needed none. They didn't remember ever hearing that laugh before. But their body knew it. Their body leaned toward it the way a plant leans toward light, without instruction, without memory, without any reason except that it was good.

"That's the spirit," Wren said. "Every day's a fresh scandal."

* * *

The others arrived through the morning the way weather arrives—Brannic first, filling the doorway, his knuckles still scabbed from a door he'd held open for people he couldn't remember holding it open for. He'd walked out of the palace dungeons three weeks ago with an empty head and a full stride, blinking at the sunlight, asking no one in particular: *Well? Did we win?*

They'd told him yes. It was even mostly true.

He crossed the common room now and put his hand on Ansel's shoulder—the same hand, the same weight, the same careful pressure that said *I know you're breakable and I'm choosing not to treat you like it.* Ansel's spine remembered it before their mind could object. Some things went deeper than memory. That was the discovery of these weeks, the one that kept them up at night in a way that wasn't entirely grief: the hands knew. The heart knew. Whatever the reservoir had burned away, it had burned only the record, not the roots.

"You're up early," Brannic rumbled.

"They're always up early," Sefa said from the doorway, shrugging off a coat that had cost someone else far more than she'd paid for it. "I've decided it's a character trait. I'm writing it down." She produced, as if summoned by her own mention of documentation, a deck of cards. "Sit. Lose money to me. It'll feel familiar even if it isn't."

"It won't be losing if I don't remember winning or losing before," Ansel said, sitting.

"See?" Sefa beamed, dealing. "You're a natural optimist. I'm keeping notes."

She cheated. Ansel watched her do it—watched her ring finger flick a card from the bottom of the deck with a fluidity that belonged to twenty years of practice—and something in their chest went warm and certain. They didn't know why they found it endearing. They only knew that they did, that the knowledge lived in them independent of any story about where it came from, the way you know the smell of rain without remembering your first storm.

"You're cheating," Ansel said.

"I am establishing continuity," Sefa said serenely, and took the pot.

* * *

Old Ivo came at midday, as he always did, moving slowly through the guildhouse like a man walking through deep water. Of all of them, Ivo had lost the least—he'd hoarded his memories outside the vault for decades, tucked into odd corners of his skull where no ledger had ever reached—and so he alone remembered everything: the jobs, the jokes, the years. He remembered Ansel entire.

That should have made him the most important person in the building. Instead he'd appointed himself something humbler and stranger: the keeper of the telling.

"Right," he said, lowering himself into the chair across from Ansel with the groan of a man who had opinions about chairs. "Where were we?"

"The ferryman," Ansel said. "Chapter six, you keep saying. You're very insistent on order."

"Order matters." Ivo's eyes were pale and steady. "Memory's a house, girl. You build it wall by wall, and if you build the walls crooked the whole thing comes down in the first hard wind. So. The ferryman. You paid him a memory for passage east. Do you want to know which one?"

Ansel set down their cards. This was the ritual, and they had learned to honor rituals—they could feel, in the hollow places, how much worse the emptiness would be without them.

"Yes."

"It was the taste of your mother's bread." Ivo said it plainly, without softening, because softness would have been a lie between them and they both knew it. "You traded away the taste of your mother's bread to buy a boat ride, because you thought you'd never need it again. And then—" here his voice caught, just barely, on the old grief of it— "and then forty years later you gave away everything else so a king could have his own life back. Which means somewhere in the world there is a man who ferried a child across a river carrying the last warm thing she owned, and he doesn't even know it."

Ansel sat with that. Around them the common room went quiet—Sefa's cards stilled, Brannic's big hands stopped mending the net he was always mending, Wren paused on her way past with an armful of kindling. They had all heard this story. They would hear it again tomorrow, and the day after, because that was the arrangement, unspoken and absolute: *we will tell you who you were until you believe it, and then we will tell you until it grows.*

"I don't miss it," Ansel said carefully. "I can't miss what I don't remember. But I think—I think I'm glad it mattered. Is that strange?"

"No," said four voices at once, and Wren laughed again, and the warmth under Ansel's ribs spread outward like ink through water.

* * *

In the afternoons, when the telling was done, Ansel worked.

Vess had given them back the ledger-keeper's desk—not the old one; that ledger had been sealed and buried with honors in the guild's crypt, its pages eaten blank by the reservoir's hunger—but a new desk, in the same corner by the window, with the same view of the courtyard where the wardstones stood whole and humming their low, contented note. The king had come to the guild himself, once, frail and gray-eyed and free, to thank them in person. He'd shaken Ansel's hand and wept without sound, and afterward Vess had said, in her dry way: *Well. At least one of us got a royal pension out of this.*

The work was different now. The guild still traded in memories—that trade would never die while people carried things too heavy to hold—but the new ledgers recorded consent, provenance, return. Every memory taken was logged with a date of promised restoration. Ansel had designed the format themselves, in handwriting that grew steadier week by week, and Vess had looked it over and said only: *Better than what we had. Don't let it go to your head.*

Today the queue was short. A widow trading her grief for a season's rest, with a clause guaranteeing its return by spring. A young soldier surrendering the memory of a battle he'd survived and his friends hadn't, until such time as he could carry it. Ordinary mercies, weighed and witnessed. Ansel wrote them down in a fair, careful hand, and felt—strangely, steadily—useful.

It was near dusk when they noticed the fresh ledger waiting at the corner of the desk. Blank pages, leather cover, the guild's new seal stamped in silver instead of the old black. No one had ordered it. No one claimed to have left it. It was simply there, the way Wren's laugh was simply there, the way Brannic's hand was simply there—a fact of the world that required no explanation because everyone involved already understood it perfectly.

Ansel turned to the first page.

For a long moment they didn't write anything. Outside, the evening star came up over the wardstones, bright and singular and accounted for. Somewhere below, Sefa was accusing Brannic of counting cards, which was rich, and Brannic was pretending not to understand the accusation, which was richer. Wren was singing something under her breath—an old hedge-song, but changed now, its hungry silence rewritten into something with a melody, something you could dance to.

Ansel dipped the pen.

They thought about writing their name first. It was the conventional opening for a ledger. But their name was a strange thing these days—*Ansel Lockridge*, given back to them by the others like a coat returned from storage, fitting well enough but smelling faintly of someone else's years. They wore it gladly. It simply wasn't the truest thing they knew anymore.

The truest thing they knew was smaller and larger than a name.

They wrote:

*First entry, new book.*

*I don't remember the woman who kept the old ledgers. I'm told she was careful, and lonely, and very good at being forgotten, and that she chose all three the way you choose armor—because the world taught her early that nothing held could be trusted to stay. I believe this the way you believe a map of a country you'll never see. She sounds like someone worth knowing. I wish I'd met her.*

*Here is what I know instead, and I know it in my hands and my chest and the third stair from the bottom, which creaks in the middle:*

*I know the sound of Wren's laugh, and that it means the world has not ended yet.*

*I know the weight of Brannic's hand on my shoulder, and that it is the closest thing to a promise I have ever felt.*

*I know that Sefa cheats at cards, and that letting her is a form of love.*

*I know that Ivo tells our stories in order because he believes order is a kind of shelter, and he is right.*

*I know that Vess says little and means most of it, and that the guild is safer in her silence than it ever was in anyone's speeches.*

*I don't remember belonging. I am told I once believed it was something you could steal, or sell, or lose. The people in this house have spent weeks teaching me otherwise—telling me myself, day after day, until the telling took root—and here is what has grown:*

*Belonging is not something you remember. It is something you remake. Every morning. On purpose. Together.*

*We kept each other.*

*—A.L., ledger-keeper, Hollow Guild, in the year the stars came home*

Ansel read it twice, then blotted the page and closed the cover gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Below, someone had begun to play music—badly, cheerfully, probably Sefa on the fiddle she refused to admit she owned. Ansel rose from the desk. The third stair creaked in the middle, exactly as it should, and the common room door stood open, spilling firelight and noise into the dark, and every face inside turned toward them when they entered, the way faces turn toward the sun.

"There you are," Wren said, scooting over to make room. "We saved you a seat. We always save you a seat."

"I know," Ansel said, and sat down among them, and began—to laughter, to protest, to the scrape of dealt cards—to learn the evening all over again.

Outside, the stars kept their watch. Inside, the family kept theirs. And in the corner by the window, a new ledger waited for tomorrow's first line, patient as dawn, certain as a hand on the shoulder:

*There would be more to write. There always would be. That was the whole point.*
