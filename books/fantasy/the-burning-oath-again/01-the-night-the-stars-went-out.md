## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

I want it on the record that I was going to pass that test. Genuinely, honestly pass it — not the way I passed my third-year warding exam, where Mistress Veyne looked at my work, looked at me, and said, "Coral, this is technically a ward and spiritually a cry for help."

The final apprenticeship test was the Raising. One night, one valley, one apprentice, and the whole of the protective lattice to hold alone from dusk until dawn. Every warden before me had done it. Every warden before me had also had a functioning sky.

I had spent three weeks preparing. I'd memorized the anchor-points, oiled the survey chains, and written out contingency verses in a hand so neat it looked like it was apologizing for existing. At sundown I stood at the valley's heart with my chalk, my chalk-slate, and my entirely undeserved confidence, and I began to speak the valley awake.

And the valley answered.

That part still gives me chills, and not the good kind. Wards are supposed to answer like a struck bell — clean, obedient, gone the moment you stop listening. This answered like something that had been holding its breath for a very long time and finally, gratefully, exhaled.

The lattice came up around the valley rim in silver threads, and then, thread by thread, it came *down*. Not torn. Not attacked. Released, the way a hand releases a bird. I stood in the middle of my own test screaming the retention verses while the wards I was supposed to be holding poured off the mountainsides like water off glass, and above me — above all of us —

The stars went out.

Not clouded. Not veiled. *Out*, all of them at once, like candles pinched by the same wet fingers. The sky went the color of a closed eye. I have seen night my whole life; I had never seen night with the *lights off*.

I did what any sensible apprentice does in a catastrophe. I ran to my mistress's tower, tripped over the survey chains I'd so carefully oiled, and pounded on her door.

"Mistress Veyne! The wards — the wards are *falling* —"

The door wasn't locked. It was never locked. But it swung open before my third knock, and that was the first wrong thing, because Veyne Ondrelle locked her door against apprentices the way other people locked their doors against weather.

The second wrong thing was the smell. Ozone and scorched linen and underneath it something older, like a book left too near a fire.

She was on the floor by her worktable, and she was dying. I knew it the way you know a dropped cup is broken — no diagnosis required. There was a burn across her chest in the shape of a sigil I had never been taught, a knotwork of lines that seemed to move when I looked directly at them, and her hands — her precise, ink-stained, unshakeable hands — were black to the wrist.

"Coral." My name in her mouth sounded like a spell she was spending her last strength on. "Lock the door. Not with chalk. With *yourself*. Stand against it and don't move."

I stood against the door. I am proud to report my legs only shook a moderate amount.

"What's happening?" I asked. "The test — the wards — I didn't do anything wrong, I checked the anchors twice —"

"You did everything right." She laughed, and it turned into a cough that frightened me more than the sky had. "That's the horror of it, child. It was never a test. It was a *handover*. The valley has been waiting forty years for a warden with your blood, and tonight it decided it was done waiting for my permission."

"My blood." I looked at my own hands, ordinary hands, ink on the left, chalk on the right. "Mistress, what did you bind this valley *to*?"

She dragged herself upright against the worktable. She was going to tell me. I could see her deciding, the way she decided everything, with that terrible arithmetic behind her eyes that I'd spent six years mistaking for sternness and was only now recognizing as fear.

"Come here. Quickly. Quickly, Coral."

I knelt beside her, and she pressed something into my palm and closed my fingers over it. Metal, still warm, edges gone soft and bubbled like it had been through a forge. An oath-token — the kind wardens carry to seal the great bindings, the kind I'd only ever seen in the locked case in her study, the kind that were supposed to be *hers* until her death made it official.

It was scorched nearly past reading. But under the burn I could feel the shape of a name, and she said it aloud, each syllable like she was paying coin for it:

"Aldric. King Aldric. Remember the name. Whatever they tell you he is — whatever *he* tells you he is — the crown is not him, and the crown is winning."

"King Aldric." I turned the word over. "Mistress, we don't have a king. The valley's charters —"

"Your valley's charters were written by your grandmother's grandmother, girl, and she wrote them *in blood* because ink wasn't strong enough." Her grip tightened on my wrist, black fingers on brown, and her eyes were the clearest thing left in her. "Your line wrote the binding. Not mine. I was only ever the *caretaker*. The counter-oath lives in your blood, and there are people who will want it, and people who will want *you* dead for having it, and I am so sorry, Coral, I am sorry I let you be a child this long."

The tower shuddered. Somewhere below, the valley bell began to ring the alarm — not the ward-failure pattern, not the fire pattern. A pattern I'd only ever seen in the old manuals, under a heading I'd once giggled at: *Breach from Without.*

"Riders," she said. "Beyond the pass. They'll be through the dead wards by moonrise, and there's no moonrise anymore, so they'll simply come. They're hunting the keeper of the counter-oath." Her mouth twisted. "They think that's me. Let them think it. Go out the scullery stair. Take the token, take nothing else, and go down-valley to the ferry at Thorn's Crossing. There's a man — a warden, or what's left of one. Sef. Disgraced, drunk, dangerous, and the only person I trust to get you out of this valley alive."

"You're coming with me."

"No." And the gentleness in it was the cruelest thing she ever did to me. "I'm going to stand in the middle of the valley and be very interesting until they catch me. I'm good at being interesting. It's the one ward I never taught you."

"Mistress —"

"*Coral.*" She caught my face in her burned hands, and I didn't even flinch, which tells you something about what fear does to a person. "Listen to me. The thing that wears the crown — it was bound in thirteen parts, and the binding turns with the moons, and the thirteenth moon is nearly full. When it completes, the binding becomes permanent, and the king becomes the crown forever, and everything your blood paid to prevent comes true. You have one turn of the moon. One. Now go, before I spend the last of my strength on something sentimental."

I went. Of course I went. I want to say I went bravely. What I actually did was go *angrily*, which is braver's ugly cousin and travels faster.

The scullery stair let out behind the herb garden, where I'd spent six years grinding feverfew and complaining about it. The sky overhead was a black lid. The valley I'd been born in, the only country I knew, lay around me with its wards down like a house with its doors thrown open, and I could hear the riders now — not the sound of them, worse, the *absence* of sound, hoofbeats that made the ground quieter instead of louder.

I ran the river path with the token cutting its shape into my palm and Veyne Ondrelle's name burning in my throat where I'd swallowed the goodbye.

* * *

Thorn's Crossing at night, with no stars, is a rumor of a town. The ferry rope creaked in the dark like something chewing. The ferryman's hut was dark, and the ferry was gone, and I stood on the bank feeling the full comedy of my situation — trained for one night of ward-keeping, armed with a scorched coin and a dead woman's errand — when a voice came out of the reeds.

"You're the apprentice." Not a question. A man unfolded from the reeds like he'd been folded there a long time and hadn't enjoyed it. Big, gray-stubbled, a warden's broken badge hanging off a coat that had been good ten years ago. He looked at me the way you look at a bill you were hoping had gotten lost in the post. "Veyne's girl. Stars out, wards down, and she sends me an apprentice."

"She said you'd help."

"She would." He sighed from somewhere around his boots. "Sef. Don't shake my hand, I've been drinking since noon and it shows in the grip." He squinted up at the dead sky, and something crossed his face that wasn't surprise. Recognition. "So it's finally turning. Thirteenth moon." He looked back at me, and his eyes were bloodshot and clear at the same time, which I would learn was his permanent condition. "You have the token."

I held it up. In the starless dark, the scorched metal gave off the faintest light, like a coal remembering fire.

Sef looked at it a long moment. Then he looked down the valley, toward the tower, toward the woman who was at that moment standing in the open to buy me my head start, and his face did something complicated and settled on grim.

"Riders are twenty minutes behind you and gaining," he said. "We can argue about the state of the world on the water. Can you run?"

I could. I'd been running since sundown.

I just hadn't known yet that I wouldn't stop for a very long time.
