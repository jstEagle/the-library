## 2. A Crown That Eats Its Wearer

The river took us north, and the river was wrong.

I don't mean it flowed badly. I mean that when I looked down into the water, there was nothing looking back. No moon, no stars, not even the gray suggestion of clouds. Just black water carrying black sky, and somewhere in the middle of all that nothing, a small boat with a disgraced warden poling us along like a man late for his own funeral.

"You're staring at the sky," Sef said.

"I'm staring at where the sky used to be."

"Stare at the banks instead. Rocks float in starless country. So do lies." He adjusted the pole. "And stop holding the token like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's the last warm thing in the world. It'll make you slow when you need to drop it."

"I'm not going to drop it."

He didn't argue. That was somehow worse.

We traveled three nights by his counting, because my counting had stopped meaning anything without a sun I could trust or stars to mark the hours. The countryside we slipped through was the valley's ugly cousin — same bones, same hills, but stripped of every ward-light and hearth-glow that should have dotted the dark. Whole villages shuttered. Once, passing a farmstead, I heard a child crying inside and a woman singing to it in a voice pitched deliberately flat, as if loud sounds might attract whatever had eaten the sky.

"They think it's a plague," Sef said quietly. "The church calls it the Dimming. Says the gods have turned their faces away for our sins."

"And you know better."

"I know better than to talk about it on open water." But he glanced at the token in my fist, and his mouth did the complicated thing again.

* * *

*Interlude — Sef*

She sleeps sitting up, which tells me she learned to sleep in a house where sleeping lying down got you punished, or worse, got someone else punished on your behalf. Apprentices. The tower women ran their order like a knuckle-rap made flesh, and this one — Coral, she'd finally told me, after two days of pretending her name was nobody's business — holds herself like a girl waiting for the next correction.

Except she isn't a girl. That's the problem. She's maybe twenty, and she's been an apprentice for what, twelve years? Thirteen? And in all that time, Maren Voss never once told her what she was guarding.

I knew Maren. Everyone who ever wore a warden's oath knew of Maren Voss, even after they threw me out. She was the one who argued against the Second Binding at council, alone, for six hours, until they stopped letting her speak. Then she went home to her valley and her wards and her apprentice, and she spent twenty years building a lock whose key she hid inside a bloodline.

Hers. Or close enough to hers. Which means the girl asleep in the bottom of my boat holding a scorched oath-token is either the most dangerous person in the kingdom or its last spare part, and I genuinely don't know which.

The riders behind us were council men. I recognized the lead one's horse before I recognized the man — Bayard Corren's gray, and Bayard only rides out personally when the council has voted something they don't want written down.

They weren't hunting the king's enemies. They were hunting the keeper. And keepers, in the warden lexicon, are things you find and things you file and things you make sure can't be found again.

I should have left her on the bank. A smart man would have.

I keep telling myself I took her because she's the counter-oath and the counter-oath is the only way any of this ends clean. That's half true. The other half is that Maren stood in her tower door with her arms spread and bought a stranger's escape with her life, and I owe a debt to anyone Maren thought worth buying.

The thirteenth moon turns in nine days. Nine days to get her to the capital, past the council, past the coronation-eve ritual, and in front of a king who may already be more crown than man.

Sleep, girl. It's the last honest thing you'll get to do for a while.

* * *

Coral

The capital announced itself by smell before sight — coal smoke and horse and too many people breathing in too small a space — and then by light, which was the shocking part. After nine days of dark country, the city burned like a wound. Ten thousand lamps, ward-lanterns strung across every street, the palace on its hill crowned in white fire that hurt to look at.

"They're burning the wards fast," Sef muttered beside me in the press of the market crowd. "Using years of stored power in days. Keeping one city lit while the rest goes black."

"That can't last."

"No. But kings don't plan past the next festival." He steered me left with a hand on my shoulder. "Tomorrow. The public audience. You'll see him from the gallery. You need to see him before you decide anything."

"Decide what?"

He didn't answer, which by now I understood was his way of answering.

The throne room was gold and incense and a hundred courtiers pretending the world outside hadn't gone dark. I stood in the gallery between a fat merchant and a nervous priest, and when the herald cried the king's style and titles, I looked down at King Aldric the Third of that name, Protector of the Valley Marches, and my first thought was: *he's young.*

My second thought was that I was wrong.

Because he *was* young — thirty at most, dark-haired, straight-backed — but he moved wrong. He walked to the throne like a man walking through deep water, each step placed with terrible care, as if his body were a borrowed coat he kept forgetting he was wearing. And when he sat, when he lifted his head to receive the petitioners, I saw his eyes.

One eye was a man's eye. Tired, human, afraid.

The other was a hole. Not empty — hollowed. Like a lamp with the flame scooped out, still shaped like an eye, still wet, but giving back no light at all. In the starless country I'd gotten used to darkness. This was different. This was darkness with *appetite.*

The crown sat on his brow like it belonged there more than he did. Gold, old-fashioned, heavy — and around its band, so faint I might have imagined it, a pattern of script turning slowly, the way the token in my pocket sometimes seemed to turn.

A petitioner came forward, some landholder begging relief from the Dimming, and the king answered him. His voice was gentle. Halfway through the sentence, the voice changed — same throat, same pitch, but the cadence shifted, became older, patient, amused — and the hole-eye fixed on the man like a hook in a fish's mouth. The petitioner swayed. Two guards caught his elbows. The court murmured and decided, collectively, to have seen nothing.

Then, for one moment, the king's living eye came up — past the courtiers, past the canopy, up to the gallery — and found mine.

I felt the token go hot against my ribs.

His lips moved. No sound reached me. But I read them, the way you read words written large, and what they said was:

*You're late.*

* * *

*Interlude — Sef*

She saw it. I watched her face in the gallery and I watched the king watch her, and I've spent ten years learning to read rooms full of armed men and I have never seen anything like the look that passed between those two.

The crown knows her. Of course it does. Her people wrote the binding — the Zhou line, the oath-smiths, the ones the histories scrubbed out after the First Binding because a kingdom cannot admit its king was ever a cage. Maren didn't just hide the counter-oath in her apprentice. She handed the crown the one thing it has spent a century trying to destroy.

Which means tomorrow, when I tell her the rest — that her mistress's whole life was a delaying action, that the valley wasn't a sanctuary but a fuse burning slow, that Maren Voss spent twenty years and finally her life buying nine days — tomorrow she either breaks, or she becomes the sharpest weapon this kingdom has left.

Bayard's men are in the city. I counted four tails on our route tonight. The council will move before the thirteenth moon turns, and their plan doesn't require a counter-oath. It requires a knife and a willing regicide and a new binding built on a dead king's bones, and every soul in this city will call it salvation.

Unless the girl speaks first.

Nine days. Gods help me, I'm starting to hope, and hoping is how wardens die.

* * *

Coral

He told me everything that night in a rented room above a dyer's shop, the walls reeking of vinegar, the single candle turned low.

"My family wrote it." My voice sounded far away. "The binding. My blood."

"The First Binding, a hundred years ago. Your ancestors bound a fragment of the thing — the real thing, the one that wears crowns and eats kingdoms — into a piece of gold, and put that gold on a king's head, and called it loyalty. Every generation since, the binding needs renewing. Every generation, your line did the renewing, quietly, until the warden council decided oath-keepers were more dangerous than oaths." He turned the candle. "Maren was the last of the renewing line. When she saw the fragment waking — when she felt the thirteenth moon coming — she couldn't reach the king. So she built the valley wards to slow it, and she trained you, and she waited."

"Waited for what?"

"For you to be ready. For the moon. For—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"She knew the wards would fail. That was the point, Coral. The valley was never a hiding place. It was a clock. She let the fragment break the wards so it would spend its strength doing that instead of finishing the king. She traded her home, her order, and finally herself to buy you nine days of head start." Sef's voice went rough. "She wasn't dying in that tower. She was spending herself. There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

I sat with that. Grief came first, the ordinary kind, the kind that makes the room tilt. But underneath it something else was setting, hardening, like metal cooling into an edge.

She hadn't lost. She'd *planned.* All those years of drills and silence and scolding — she'd been forging a key and calling me a clumsy apprentice so the key would never know its own shape until it was needed.

The token lay in my palm, faintly glowing. Nine days. The thirteenth moon nearly full. A king with one eye left, mouthing *you're late* across a throne room — and if he could still speak through the thing wearing him, even once, even barely, then he was still in there. Still fighting.

The council wanted a dead king and a new cage. My mistress had died for a door.

"He offered me something today," I said. "In the throne room. Through the crown. Power, if I walk away. It'll offer again."

Sef went very still. "How do you know?"

"Because Maren taught me how bindings work." I closed my hand over the token. "And because I intend to refuse it in front of witnesses. Find me the ritual chamber, Sef. Before the moon turns, I'm going to give this kingdom back its king — and then I'm going to have words with the warden council about what they did to my mistress's grave."

Outside, high over the burning city, the thirteenth moon rose — swollen, silver, and one night short of full.
