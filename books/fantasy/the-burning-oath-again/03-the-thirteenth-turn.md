## 3. The Thirteenth Turn

*Coral*

The thirteenth moon rose over the capital like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting — too pale, too close, wrong in a way that made my teeth ache when I looked at it directly.

Sef had told me not to look at it.

I looked at it anyway. I'd been ignoring good advice since the night the stars went out; it seemed a poor time to start developing virtues.

"Stop gawking," Sef muttered beside me, hood up, moving through the coronation-eve crowds like a knife through bread. "The moon isn't going anywhere. That's rather the problem."

Around us, the city celebrated. Lanterns, wine, children with paper crowns that would be soggy by midnight. They thought tomorrow was a coronation. It was, I supposed, in the way a funeral is an appointment.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and tragic and start composing your own eulogy. I can hear it. It's very loud."

"I'm *rehearsing*," I said, with as much dignity as a person can manage while smuggling herself into her own destiny wearing a stolen kitchen cloak. "There's a difference."

"There is," Sef conceded. "One of them ends with you alive."

We came to the ritual hall's service entrance, where two wardens stood guard under lanterns that burned the color of old honey. Sef gave me one last look — the look of a disgraced oath-warden who had bet everything on a dead woman's apprentice and was trying very hard not to count the losses yet.

"The counter-oath needs your voice, your blood, and your name," Sef said. "In that order. Don't let it tempt you. Don't let it *talk* to you. And Coral—" a pause, and something almost soft crossed that scarred face, "—whatever happens in there, you were never just an apprentice. Mistress Vey didn't die for an apprentice."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to make you angry enough to survive." Sef pressed the door latch. "Anger keeps better than grief."

* * *

*Sef*

I had held doors before. It was, if I was honest, most of my talent. Not spellwork — Coral had that covered, gods help us both — not politics, certainly not the quiet patience this plan required. But a door, held against what comes through it: that I could do.

Which is why, when the council's true wardens came down the eastern stair in their grey-and-silver, seven of them with drawn blades and the particular calm of people who have already decided, I put myself between them and the ritual door and did the only thing I have ever been genuinely good at.

"Councillor Vess," I said pleasantly. "Come to wish the king well?"

"Sef." Old Vess didn't even have the grace to look ashamed. She looked *tired*, which was worse. Tired meant she'd been losing sleep over the decision, which meant she'd actually made it. "Step aside. The binding dies tonight either way. The only question is whether Aldric dies with it."

"He's still in there. Half of him."

"Half is too much." Her blade didn't waver. "You've seen what wears his crown. We bind again, or we cut it out. Another binding buys thirteen more moons and costs another bloodline. How many Zhou women does the world owe us, Sef? How many more little apprentices carrying scorched tokens?"

And there it was. The arithmetic of frightened people, done correctly and arriving at murder.

"You could have told her," I said. "You could have told her any of this instead of sending hunters."

"She'd have hesitated. Hesitation feeds it." Vess lifted her chin. "Last chance, warden. Step aside, and I'll say you died defending the door. You might even get a statue."

"I've met statues," I said. "They're terrible conversationalists."

She sighed, and the seven came on, and I set my feet and held the door.

* * *

*Coral*

The ritual chamber smelled of cold iron and burnt rosemary — mistress Vey's herbs, her recipe, her work still holding after all these years. King Aldric knelt at the center of the old binding circle, and up close he was worse than from the balcony. Half of him was a tired man in his forties with kind eyes and ink stains on his fingers. The other half was *smooth*. Not wounded-smooth. Unfinished-smooth, like wax waiting for a seal, and where the crown sat, the metal moved. Slowly. Like breathing.

It noticed me before he did.

*Little keeper,* said a voice that wasn't a voice, arriving behind my eyes like a guest who doesn't knock. *You have her hands. The woman who bound me. Do you know what she felt, at the end? I do. I keep everything.*

"I'm not here to chat," I said, and bit my thumb, and began.

The counter-oath was nine lines. Mistress Vey had drilled them into me for years disguised as a nonsense rhyme about a fisherman and a stubborn tide. I'd recited it a thousand times without knowing what it was. My whole childhood was a lock, and I had been the key learning its own teeth.

Line one. Line two. The circle woke, silver light crawling up from the floor stones, and the crown-thing *screamed* — no, not screamed. *Negotiated.*

*Wait.* The pressure gentled, became reasonable, became warm. *Coral. A fine name. Strong name. Why serve a dead woman's grudge? Look what they've done to you — hunted you, lied to you, sent their wardens to kill the man you came to save. Walk away. Take my offer instead. Power without oath. Freedom without burden. Every keeper before you was conscripted. Be the first who was asked.*

And stars help me, it was a good offer. That was the horror of it. My feet wanted to stop. My mouth wanted to close around line three and swallow it.

Somewhere beyond the door, steel rang, and a body hit stone, and I heard Sef grunt the way Sef grunts when hurt but not finished.

That decided me faster than any philosophy.

"They didn't ask me," I said aloud, blood dripping onto the circle. "My mistress didn't ask me. The council didn't ask me. Nobody in this entire rotten valley of oaths has ever once *asked* me anything." I knelt across from the king, took his unfinished hand in my bleeding one, and looked the crown-thing dead in its breathing metal. "So here's what makes me different from every keeper before me. I'm not walking away, and I'm not inheriting. I'm *choosing*. Line five."

The chamber went white.

* * *

*Sef*

I was on my knees by then, holding my side, watching my own blood decide whether it intended to stay. Vess stood over me, blade lowered, both of us staring through the doorway at a girl wreathed in light speaking words older than the council, older than the crown.

The thirteenth moon reached the top of the window. Held. Began its turn.

And the crown cracked.

Not broke — *cracked*, like ice on a spring river, and out of the crack poured everything the thing had eaten: stolen years, stolen warmth, stolen sky. Through the high windows, impossibly, the first stars came back — one, then ten, then a thousand, falling into place like a sentence completing itself.

Aldric collapsed forward into Coral's arms, whole and weeping and lighter by half.

Vess lowered her sword entirely. "Well," she said, in the tone of a woman revising thirty years of doctrine before breakfast. "That's not in any of the texts."

"No," I agreed, and passed out.

* * *

*Coral*

When I could see again, the crown lay in pieces on the floor, ordinary metal, cooling. The king was breathing. The stars were back, and they looked, frankly, smug about it.

Sef was bleeding badly by the door, and I got there fast, pressing cloth to the wound while Vess shouted for healers with the first honest urgency I'd ever heard from the council.

"Held the door," Sef managed, insufferably pleased.

"You're impossible."

"Told you. Statues. Terrible conversationalists." A wet, rattling laugh. "Coral. Listen. What you did in there — the choosing part — nobody's ever—"

"Save it. You can tell me how remarkable I am when you can hold a cup without spilling."

But Sef's eyes had gone somewhere past me, past the ceiling, toward the returning stars, and though I didn't know it yet — wouldn't let myself know it until dawn — I understood, kneeling there with my hands full of my friend's blood, that some doors, once held, are held all the way.

The moon completed its turn.

And somewhere beneath the palace, in the dark where the crown's fragments cooled, something small and patient began, very quietly, to sing.
