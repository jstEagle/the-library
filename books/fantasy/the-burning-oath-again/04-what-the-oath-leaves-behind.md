## 4. What the Oath Leaves Behind

The crown came apart like a wasp's nest in the rain.

I had expected fire, or screaming, or some grand detonation worthy of a thirteenth moon. Instead it simply sloughed — layer after layer of gold and older, darker metal peeling away from Aldric's brow and crumbling into ash that smelled of cold iron and old promises. The thing inside it made one sound as it went. Not a roar. A sigh. The way a debt sounds when it is finally paid by someone other than the debtor.

Then the stars came back.

All at once, all of them, as if they had only been holding their breath behind a curtain and someone had finally told them the play was over. I sat on the ritual floor with Sef's blood cooling under my knees and looked up through the shattered skylight and did not feel triumphant. I felt like a girl who had just set down something enormously heavy and discovered her arms still remembered its weight.

Aldric made a small noise.

I turned. The king — the actual king, the man who had spent thirteen turns of a false moon wearing his own face like an ill-fitting mask — was sitting where the throne used to be, because the throne had cracked down the middle when the binding broke, and he had apparently decided that sitting on the floor of his own audience hall was preferable to standing. His hands were shaking. His eyes were wet and utterly lost.

"Who am I?" he asked.

It was not the question I'd expected. I'd braced for *what have you done to me*, or *where is my crown*, or the particular royal fury of a man discovering he has been governed by a parasite for years. Instead: *who am I*, asked quietly, the way you'd ask a stranger for directions.

"You're Aldric," I said. "King Aldric. Third of that name."

He frowned, testing the name like a coin bitten for gold. "That's... a title." He looked around the hall — at the banners, the wardens' bodies, the door Sef had died holding. "Titles are things that were *given* to me. I can remember being given them. I can't remember..." He pressed his palm flat against his chest. "There's nothing underneath. It's all given. Who gave me *me*?"

And there it was — the cost no one had warned me about, because no one had known. The crown-thing hadn't just ridden him. It had eaten him slowly, course by course, and what remained after the unbinding wasn't a king restored. It was a hollowed-out man wearing a king's memories like borrowed clothes, unable to tell which ones fit.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, which surprised me.

* * *

They gave him three days.

Three days of physicians and warden-scribes and councilors hovering like carrion birds who couldn't decide whether he was dying or already dead. Three days of Aldric sitting patiently through examinations, answering questions about his own reign with the careful diligence of a student who has crammed for a test on his own life. He could recite treaties. He could name every lord in the western march. He could not, when a chambermaid wept at seeing him and called him by a childhood nickname, remember her at all.

On the third day he summoned me. Not to the throne room — to a small room with a map table and bad lighting, which struck me as far more honest.

"You're the one who broke it," he said. "Coral Zhou."

"Technically I broke the *binding*. The thing in the crown broke itself when—"

"Coral." He said my name gently, the way my mistress used to when I was talking too much. "I've read the reports. I know what you did. I know what the council tried to do instead." His mouth twisted. "I may not remember being king, but I can read a ledger of betrayals well enough."

"The council will answer for—"

"The council," he said, "is currently answering to itself, which means nothing will happen, which is why I'm telling you this before the paperwork makes it official." He slid a document across the table. Heavy paper. Heavy seal. My hands shook reading it — an abdication, signed in a hand that trembled but didn't hesitate.

"You can't," I said stupidly. "You're the king. You're the only thing standing between the realm and—"

"A man who cannot remember why he wanted the crown?" He almost smiled. "I stood in that hall last night and tried to feel something about these borders, these people, this *duty*, and it was like reading someone else's letters. Coral, a king who rules from memory alone is just a well-dressed regent. And I've seen what happens when something else wears the crown while the man inside is absent." He tapped the abdication. "Better an empty chair than a haunted one."

"What will you do?"

"I don't know." He said it plainly, without self-pity, and somehow that was the bravest thing I'd heard in weeks. "Perhaps I'll go find out who I was before. Someone must remember. Some village, some mother, some dog I threw sticks for." He rose, and paused at the door. "The council will want a new oath-keeper. They'll offer you a title. Refuse the title. Take the work instead. There's a difference, and you're one of perhaps three people alive who understands it."

Then he left, and I sat alone with a dead woman's oath-token in my pocket and a dead warden's blood still under my fingernails, and understood that I had just been given advice by a man who no longer existed.

* * *

The council fractured exactly the way rotten wood fractures: loudly, and along lines everyone had pretended weren't there.

Warden-Commander Vess had ordered the regicide — that much surfaced within a day, dragged up by a junior scribe who had kept copies of everything, bless her paranoid little heart. Vess denied it, then qualified the denial, then defended it outright: *"We acted to protect the valley. Would you rather risk another binding? Another thirteen moons of a hollowed king?"* Half the council nodded along. The other half remembered, suddenly and conveniently, that they had expressed doubts at the time. Documents were burned. Accusations were filed. Two wardens resigned in protest; four more were dismissed for knowing too much or too little.

I watched it all from the gallery and felt nothing so much as tired. Sef had been disgraced by these people once already — stripped of rank, cast out, all for the crime of asking questions about the original binding. They had done it again, in a different key, and would have done it a third time if the price were right.

On the last day of the inquiry, Vess found me in the corridor.

"They'll offer you a seat," she said. Her voice was flat, but her eyes were doing arithmetic. "An official appointment. Oath-Keeper to the Crown. You'd be a fool to refuse."

"Probably," I agreed.

Something flickered across her face — contempt, or maybe envy, or maybe the exhausted recognition of someone meeting a person who cannot be bought. "Your mistress bound that thing for thirty years and died for it, and the realm never knew her name. Is that what you want?"

"No," I said. "But I'd rather earn my own name than be handed yours."

She left. I counted it a victory, though victories lately had a way of feeling like debris.

* * *

We buried Sef on a hill outside the capital, on a night so clear it felt like an apology.

There was no rite I knew for disgraced wardens, and no rite I trusted from the council, so I made one up. I wrapped her in her old oath-cloak — the one they'd taken from her when they cast her out, and which I had stolen back from an evidence locker with a hairpin and sheer spite. I put the scorched token from my mistress in her hands, because those two stubborn, law-breaking, duty-haunted women would have liked each other terribly, and because the token had done its work and deserved rest.

I dug the grave myself. My mistress would have approved; she always said magic that doesn't blister your palms isn't really yours.

When I finished, I stood there in the dark with dirt to my elbows, and the stars were all overhead, every one of them back in its place, and I realized I was waiting for something. A voice. A sign. Some cosmic acknowledgment that the debt was settled, the story closed.

Nothing came. Just wind, and grass, and the enormous indifferent beauty of a sky that had never actually gone anywhere.

"That's it, then," I said aloud. "No inheritance. No mantle passed down. Just—" I gestured at the grave, the sky, myself, ridiculous and filthy and free. "Just me, deciding."

My mistress had bound the valley with an oath she never chose — inherited, imposed, a chain dressed up as duty. The council kept their oaths the way misers keep coins: locked away, spent only when profitable. Sef had broken hers to keep a truer one. And Aldric — Aldric had shown me the difference between a title and a self.

So I spoke. Not the counter-oath, not any formula from any book. Just words, mine, said over fresh-turned earth:

"I, Coral Zhou, keeper by choice and not by birth, swear to hold the doors that others won't stand at. To ask the questions that disgrace the asker. To break any bond — including this one — if it ever becomes a cage instead of a promise."

The stars didn't applaud. But somewhere in the grass, a nightbird started singing, and I chose to take it as witness.

* * *

I left the capital two days later, walking east, toward the valley I'd fled and the apprenticeship test I'd never finished.

Word travels strangely now that the wards are down. In every village, people stopped me — not because they knew me, but because they'd heard. *The girl who unbound the king. The girl the stars came back for.* They asked for blessings. They asked for judgments. One farmer asked me to settle a dispute about a fence, which I did, badly, and which taught me more about keeping oaths than any ritual ever had.

I corrected them each time. *I'm not an oracle. I'm not a saint. I'm not even a warden.* I'm a former apprentice with dirt under her nails and a grave to visit on the way home.

But last night, camped under a sky so full of stars it seemed boastful, a traveler shared my fire and asked, casually, who I was.

And I found I had an answer. Not my mistress's name trailing after mine. Not a title granted by a fractured council or a hollowed king. Just:

"I'm Coral Zhou. I keep oaths. Mine are freely given — which means I'm the only one allowed to break them."

The traveler raised an eyebrow, unconvinced, and asked what an oath-keeper actually *does*.

I smiled, poked the fire, and told her: that depends entirely on what comes knocking next.
