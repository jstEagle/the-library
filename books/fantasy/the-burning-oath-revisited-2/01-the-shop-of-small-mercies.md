## 1. The Shop of Small Mercies

The trick to selling magic in a city that has outlawed it, I have found, is to make sure the magic is too small to matter.

Vell's Magistry is very clear on this point. Article Nine of the Concord of Ash: *No person shall craft, sell, or employ enchantments exceeding the threshold of common craft.* Common craft being, in practice, whatever the Magistry says it is that morning. Last spring they confiscated a woman's loom because her thread never tangled, and she couldn't prove it wasn't a spell. They burned the loom in Founder's Square and fined her eleven silver marks, which was roughly everything she had, and the crowd watched with the particular satisfaction of people who are grateful it isn't them.

So my charms are small. Deliberately, painstakingly small. A knot-charm for a fisherman's net, woven from river reed and sealed with plain beeswax — no incantation, no sigil, nothing a Magistry assessor could point to and say *there, that's the working*. Just a knot tied well by someone who ties knots well. If it happens to hold when the net should tear, well. Good knots do that.

A sleep-sachet of lavender and dried valerian, stitched shut with a running stitch any grandmother could manage. If the sleeper dreams gently, that's the lavender. Everyone knows lavender helps you sleep. The fact that I whisper three words into the stuffing before I close it — words so old they've worn smooth as river stones, words that mean roughly *rest, be easy, nothing follows you here* — is between me and the lavender.

I keep a ledger like an honest merchant. I pay my taxes early. I sweep my step every morning before the neighbors wake, because a swept step says *ordinary person, nothing to see*, and in Vell, ordinary is armor.

My shop is called the Shop of Small Mercies, which sounds humble enough that no one asks questions. It sits on Cordwainer's Lane, wedged between a cobbler who smells of tanning oil and a chandler whose candles are, I'm fairly sure, just candles. The sign is painted wood, slightly faded, deliberately so. I faded it myself with tea water two years ago. New signs invite eyes. Old signs are furniture; nobody looks at furniture.

This is the whole architecture of my life: be furniture. Be the swept step, the faded sign, the charm-seller whose wares are barely worth the name. Five years I have built this, plank by plank, habit by habit, until the fortress of my ordinariness has walls thick enough that even I sometimes forget there's anything inside them.

Almost forget.

* * *

The bell over my door has one small enchantment on it that I will deny under oath: it rings differently for different customers. Not magically different — I tuned it, physically, with a file and a great deal of patience — but tuned so that I can tell without looking up whether the person walking in is a buyer, a browser, or trouble.

Trouble rings flat.

At half past nine on a grey Tuesday in the month of Thaw, the bell rang flat.

"Morning," said the man in the doorway. He wore the dove-grey coat of the Magistry's civil office, which is bad enough, but the coat fit him badly at the shoulders, which meant it wasn't his, which meant he'd borrowed it to look official without being official. Junior clerk, probably, sent to walk the lanes and count charm-sellers. "Juniper Halloran?"

"That's me." I kept my hands visible on the counter, sorting dried mugwort into paper twists. Hands that are busy look innocent. "What can I do for the Magistry today?"

"Just a census of licensed vendors." He had a little book, and he wrote in it with the self-importance of a man who has recently been given a little book. "You hold a common-craft license, correct?"

"Third shelf, green folder."

He fetched it himself, which I permitted, because refusing would have been interesting, and interesting is fatal. He turned the pages slowly. I went on weighing mugwort. Mugwort is excellent for looking busy around officials; it requires constant attention and smells faintly of virtue.

"You sell charms," he said.

"I sell *craft goods*," I corrected pleasantly. "Knots, sachets, blessed salt — blessed by me personally, and I'll have you know I'm very secular about it. Nothing in this shop exceeds the threshold."

"Mm." He looked at the shelf of knot-charms. He picked one up, turned it over. My heart did something quiet and disciplined, like a soldier standing at attention while artillery passes overhead. That particular knot-charm was for a laundress on Tanner's Row whose hands cracked and bled in cold water; inside the wax seal was a warmth-glimmer no bigger than a firefly's opinion. Harmless. Invisible. But if he broke the wax—

He didn't break the wax. He set it down, wrote something in his book — I caught only the motion of the nib, not the word — and closed the folder.

"Lovely shop," he said. "Very... modest."

"Modesty keeps the roof on," I said, smiling the smile I keep for officials, which uses exactly enough teeth to seem friendly and none of the warmth that invites conversation.

He left. The bell rang him out, flat again going, which I hadn't known it could do twice. I stood in the empty shop and listened to my own heartbeat come down from the rafters where it had been hiding.

Then I went downstairs and checked the cellar door.

* * *

I should explain about the cellar, or rather explain why I won't explain about the cellar, which is more honest.

Every shop on Cordwainer's Lane has a cellar. Ours were cut into the old foundations, back when Vell was smaller and built its streets on top of whatever came before. Most cellars hold coal and pickled things and the smell of damp stone. Mine holds those too, in the front part, where the steps come down.

At the back of the cellar there is a wall. And in the wall there is a gate.

Not a door. A gate — arched, older than the city above it, carved from a stone that doesn't match any quarry I know, dark and fine-grained and faintly warm to the touch, like the flank of a sleeping animal. It stands perhaps seven feet tall. Around its arch runs a border of lettering in no alphabet used by any living kingdom, and through the middle of the arch, where a door should open, there is only seamless stone — except that it isn't seamless, quite. There's a seam. A hairline crack, vertical, dead center, thin as a spider's leg.

Five years ago I bricked it over. Then I plastered the brick. Then I put a shelf in front of the plaster, and on the shelf I put jars of pickled onions, because nothing says *nothing to see here* like preserved alliums. I did not stop there. I hung a rug on the cellar ceiling above it — don't ask, it made sense at the time — and I developed the habit, refined over years into something like instinct, of simply not looking at the back wall. I can cross the entire cellar, retrieve a jar of onions, and return upstairs with my gaze fixed on the coal scuttle the whole time. It is a skill. I am possibly the only person in Vell who practices not-seeing as a discipline.

Why? Because five years ago I woke up on the floor of that cellar with no memory of how I got there, a scar on my left palm shaped like a small closed eye, and a conviction — bone-deep, unreasoning, absolute — that whatever was behind that gate must never, ever be let through. I don't remember deciding this. I don't remember why. The conviction is simply *there*, the way a scar is there, and I have organized my entire life around it the way a tree organizes itself around a fence post it grew against.

Some mornings I stand at the top of the cellar stairs and think: *you could just look. One look. What harm is one look?*

And some deeper, older part of me answers, in a voice that doesn't sound entirely like mine: *the harm is everything.*

So I don't look. I sell knots and sachets. I sweep my step. I am furniture.

* * *

The rest of Tuesday passed the way Tuesdays should: unremarkably, which is to say perfectly.

Mistress Ansel came for her monthly sachet and complained about her son-in-law for eleven minutes, during which I learned nothing new and restocked nothing, but she left happy and paid in copper and once, years ago, told the Magistry's assessor that I was "a dull, godly sort," which is the finest review a hidden magician can receive. Two boys bought whistling tops that are just tops. A tanner's wife bought a knot-charm for her husband's gloves and asked, in the way people do, whether it really worked, and I gave the answer I always give: "It's a good knot. Knots are honest." Which is true, and also a lie, and also the closest thing to a philosophy I have.

By late afternoon the light through the shop window had gone the color of weak honey, and I was alone with my ledger, doing sums that always come out the same — enough. Enough rent, enough coal, enough food, enough silence. Enough to stay invisible.

Here is the thing I don't think about, on principle: enough is not the same as good.

I eat dinner alone at the counter because the table in the back room feels too large for one. I know the names of everyone on Cordwainer's Lane and none of them know mine beyond *Halloran, the charm-seller, dull sort*. When Sera Quill — the widow two doors down, sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, kind underneath it like a knife wrapped in wool — brings me soup on cold evenings, I accept it and thank her and make sure the conversation stays on weather and prices, because Sera misses nothing, and what Sera sees, Sera wonders about, and wonder is a door I cannot afford to leave open.

Loneliness, I have decided, is a fair price for safety. I decided this five years ago, along with everything else I apparently decided, and I have never once revisited the decision, because revisiting requires memories and mine have a hole in them the exact shape of a person.

That's the strange part. Not that I'm alone — plenty of people are alone. But somewhere in me there is a grief with no object. A mourning for someone I cannot name. Some nights I wake with a name half-formed on my tongue, a shape of a face behind my eyes, and it dissolves before I can catch it, like breath off a mirror. I've stopped trying. Trying hurts, and the hurt leads nowhere, and nowhere is where I live.

Safety. Solitude. Swept steps. This is my kingdom, and it is small, and it holds, and most days that is enough.

Most days.

* * *

I closed the shop at dusk, as the lamps were being lit along the lane. I counted the till, banked the fire, checked the bolt on the front door twice, and then stood for a moment in the quiet, listening to the building settle around me the way it does each night — creaks and sighs, wood and stone talking to themselves.

And beneath the creaks, beneath the sighs, beneath even the distant clatter of the chandler closing up next door, I heard — or thought I heard, or maybe only felt, in my teeth, in the scar on my palm — a sound from the back of the cellar.

Low. Faint. Almost nothing.

A hum.

I stood very still on the stairs, hand on the rail, and told myself it was the pipes. Vell's pipes hum. Old cities hum. Stone settles, wind moves through flues, all manner of harmless things produce low persistent tones, and a sensible person notes them and goes to bed.

But my scarred palm had begun, very faintly, to itch.

I did not go down. I want that recorded. Whatever else happened later, whatever blame lands on me, let it be known that on the night it began, Juniper Halloran stood at the top of the cellar stairs, felt the hairline seam behind five years of brick and plaster and pickled onions shift by some measure too small to see, and chose — firmly, sensibly, in the grand tradition of all my worst decisions — to ignore it.

I went up. I locked my bedroom door, which I never do. I lay awake listening to the house breathe, and somewhere past midnight, through the floorboards, through the joists, through the stone itself, the hum changed.

It stopped sounding like a pipe.

It started sounding like waiting.

* * *

The knock came three nights later, in the middle of the worst storm Vell had seen in a decade, and when I opened the door there was a child on my step — barefoot, soaked through, shivering — and the first sound out of that small mouth was not a cry or a plea or any word I knew.

It was a language. Old and strange and rolling, syllables like stones turning in deep water, and the terrible thing, the thing that made my blood go cold and my scarred palm burn, was this:

I almost understood it.
