## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongue

The storm came up the valley like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, just suddenly there and demanding everything. By the time the first thunder cracked over Vell's rooftops, I had already done my evening ritual: shutters latched, sign taken in, counter wiped down to remove any trace of the day's commerce. The charms I'd sold that morning were all harmless by design. A knot-charm for a fisherman's nets. A sleep-stone for the baker's insomniac daughter, which was mostly river pebble and entirely placebo. A little woven blessing for a new baby, which contained nothing but good intentions and lavender.

Under Vell's anti-magic statutes, all of it was technically contraband. Under Vell's actual practice, nobody cared as long as you didn't do anything *interesting*. Interesting got you a visit from the Magistry. Interesting got your shop sealed and your name entered into a ledger, and I had spent five years cultivating a personality so aggressively uninteresting that even I found myself boring at parties, if I went to parties, which I did not.

I was banking the fire when the knock came.

Three raps, spaced wrong. Not the quick double-knock of a customer caught out in the rain, not the patient official knock of a city inspector. Three slow strikes, like someone who had never knocked on a door before and was working it out from first principles.

I stood very still in the dark of the shop and told myself it was the wind. The wind had been throwing things against the shutters all evening—a branch, probably, or hail, or the general malice of weather. There was no reason for anyone to be on my street at this hour in this storm. My street wasn't on the way to anywhere. That was why I'd chosen it.

The knock came again. Three raps, spaced wrong.

"Closed," I said to the empty shop, because talking to empty shops is what five years of careful invisibility does to a person. "Come back tomorrow."

The third knock was different. Softer. Almost apologetic. And beneath it—or maybe beneath my hearing of it, which is a distinction I've had five years to regret not examining more closely—I felt something shift in the floorboards under my feet. A vibration, low and patient, rising from the cellar like a note held on an instrument too large to see.

The gate.

No. No, I told myself. The gate was old stone. Old stone doesn't hum. Old stone has sat under my shop for five years doing absolutely nothing, and I have repaid its discretion by never looking directly at it, and we have an arrangement, the stone and I, and the arrangement does not include *humming*.

The knock came a fourth time, and this time I heard something else under it: a small sound, high and thin, half-swallowed by the wind. It might have been crying. It might have been singing. With storms, you can convince yourself of almost anything.

I lit a lamp. This was, in retrospect, the moment I decided to open the door; everything after was just my body catching up to my hands.

* * *

The child on my doorstep could not have been more than eight or nine, though there was something in the way they held themselves—perfectly still, chin up, bare feet planted on my wet threshold—that made age feel like the wrong question. They were soaked through. Their clothes were the kind of plain grey homespun that belongs to no particular region, the kind of clothes that look like they were made by someone who had heard about clothing secondhand. No shoes. No coat. In this weather.

And their eyes—well. I'm getting ahead of myself.

"Are you lost?" I asked, holding the lamp out. Standard procedure. Lost children get taken to the constable's office on Cordwainer Street, where a harried woman named Odile feeds them soup and finds their parents, and everyone forgets about it by spring. "Where are your people?"

The child looked at me. Looked at the lamp. Looked at me again with an expression I can only describe as *assessment*, as if I were a lock and they were deciding whether I was worth picking.

Then they spoke.

The words were nothing. That was my first thought—nonsense syllables, wind-sounds, the babble of a fevered mind. But my second thought arrived underneath the first, uninvited, the way a fisherman feels the tug before he sees the line move: I knew some of it. Not the words themselves, exactly, but the *shape* of them. The way the language put its weight on the ends of phrases instead of the beginnings. The way a certain throat-deep vowel recurred like a refrain. Somewhere in me, in some room of myself I had never furnished, a door had opened a crack, and something on the other side was saying *yes, this, you know this.*

"Slowly," I said, and then, God help me, I said something else. Something in reply. Two or three syllables in that same rolling cadence, produced from my own mouth without my permission, without my knowledge of what they meant.

The child's whole face changed. The assessment vanished, and what replaced it was so nakedly relieved that it hurt to look at, the way sunrise hurts after a long night watch. They stepped forward—not into the shop, I noticed, but toward *me*—and gripped my sleeve with both hands, and spoke again, fast now, urgent, a flood of that dead language pouring out as if a dam had broken somewhere behind their teeth.

"I don't—" I started, in Vellish, uselessly. "I don't understand. Mostly."

They stopped. Tilted their head. Studied my face with that unnerving stillness, and then tried again, slower, watching me the whole time like a teacher with a slow pupil. And here is the thing I have turned over every night since: I understood perhaps one word in ten, and yet I understood *more than that*. Fragments rose in me like bubbles through dark water. A word that meant *below*, I was fairly sure. A word that meant *candle*. A word repeated three times that made the back of my neck prickle, because some unfurnished room in me insisted it meant *owed*.

"No," I said firmly, to the child, to myself, to the humming in the floorboards. "Whatever you're owed, you've got the wrong shop."

The child did not understand the words. The child understood the tone perfectly, being, I suspect, well acquainted with adults saying no. Their grip on my sleeve tightened.

Thunder walked across the roof. The rain redoubled, hammering the cobbles like applause for a terrible decision. And the child shivered—finally, belatedly, the ordinary animal shiver of a small body that has been cold far too long—and that broke whatever spell of caution I had been hiding behind.

"Oh, for pity's sake," I said, in the tone Sera the widow uses on stray cats, which I had sworn I would never adopt. "Inside. Before you drown standing up."

* * *

I will spare you the logistics of getting a mute, barefoot, possibly haunted child dry and fed, except to note two things. First: they ate like someone who had learned hunger as a discipline rather than an event—slowly, carefully, wasting nothing, watching me between bites to confirm the food would not be revoked. Second: when I set them by the fire and knelt to look at their feet, expecting cuts or frostbite, I found them *clean*. Not chilled-red and filthy the way any barefoot child's feet should be after a storm. Clean, and unmarked, and warm.

Warm. On a night cold enough to crack puddles.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the child, and the child looked back, and for a long moment neither of us pretended anything.

"What are you?" I asked quietly.

They answered at length, in the dead tongue, and patted the floor beside them—an invitation, or an instruction. Against every instinct I have cultivated like a garden, I sat.

They took my hand. Their fingers were warm too. And slowly, with great patience, they began to draw on my palm—not letters, exactly, or not letters of any alphabet I know, but shapes. A triangle. A line beneath it. Then they pointed down. Through the floor. Toward the cellar.

The hum in the floorboards had not stopped. I realized, sitting there with my hand in a stranger-child's grip, that I had been pretending not to hear it for the better part of an hour, the same way I had spent five years pretending not to see the gate. It was louder now. Not much. Just enough. Like a voice through a wall that has stopped talking to itself and started talking to *you*.

"No," I said again, and pulled my hand back, and stood up, and busied myself with blankets because busy hands are how cowards pray. "There's nothing down there but old stone and bad air and forty years of other people's junk. Whatever you felt on the doorstep, whatever you think is calling you—it's got the wrong address."

The child watched me make this speech with the grave attention of someone witnessing a ritual they don't fully approve of. When I finished, they said one word. Just one. Softly, in the dead tongue, and the unfurnished room in me opened its door wider, and I understood it completely, the way you understand your own name.

It was a word for *home*.

"Not here," I whispered. "This isn't—it can't be—"

The gate hummed. And this time I couldn't call it imagination, because the lamp flames all leaned sideways at once, every flame in the shop bending toward the cellar door as if bowing, and the fire in the grate guttered and steadied, and the child closed their eyes with an expression of terrible, familiar peace—the look of someone hearing a song they'd been searching for their whole life.

I slammed the cellar door's bolt. An absurd gesture; the bolt was iron and the hum was coming through solid stone, but I slammed it anyway, and then I put a chair under the handle for good measure, and then I stood there breathing like a man who has just remembered he's afraid of heights while standing on a balcony.

When I turned around, the child was watching me with those eyes I keep circling and haven't described. Grey, I want to say, except grey isn't right—they were the colour of candlelight seen through amber, warm and deep and *old*, old in a way that had nothing to do with the small face around them. Looking into them was like looking down a well and realizing the water at the bottom is looking back.

They pointed at the cellar door. They pointed at themselves. They said the word for *owed* again, twice, gently, the way you'd remind someone of an appointment.

"We'll talk in the morning," I said, which is what people say when they mean *I need you to stop existing at me until I've had time to lie down*. I spread a blanket by the fire. The child lay down on my good rug without argument, curled small, and was asleep in moments—which itself should have frightened me more than it did, because children who arrive out of storms do not usually sleep like stones in strangers' houses, unless they have traveled a very long way and are finally, finally done.

I banked the fire. I checked the bolt on the cellar door twice more. I told myself the hum was pipes, or settling, or the storm in the chimney, or any of the hundred harmless explanations I had used for five years to keep from thinking about old stone.

Upstairs, in bed, I listened to the rain and the small even breathing below and the faint, patient note rising through my floorboards, and I made myself a promise in the spirit of honest bookkeeping: tomorrow I would take the child to Odile at the constable's office, and Odile would find their people, and this would become one of those stories I tell no one.

Somewhere past midnight, half-dreaming, I heard the child speak in their sleep—one word, clear as a bell through the floor, in that language older than kingdoms.

It was not the word for home.

It was a name. Two syllables, soft and unmistakable, spoken with the weary tenderness of someone greeting an old friend.

My name.

I lay in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, and beneath me, gentle as a tide, the gate began to hum in time with my pulse.
