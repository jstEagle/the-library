## 3. What the Charms Cannot Do

The trouble with a mute child is that they can't tell you what's wrong, which means you have to guess, and the trouble with guessing is that I've built an entire life on never doing it.

I'd spent five years perfecting the art of not knowing things. Not knowing what was behind the cellar door. Not knowing why I flinched at candlelight. Not knowing, most days, why I woke at three in the morning with my heart going like a debtor's fist on a door. Ignorance, I had learned, could be furnished quite comfortably if you committed to it. I had. Wall hangings, cushions, the lot.

Then Ash arrived, and ignorance stopped being furniture and started being a hole in the floor.

* * *

Morning found us in the kitchen above the shop, me playing physician with the confidence of a man who has read one book on the subject and skimmed it. Ash sat on the stool by the stove, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar because everything in my house smelled of cedar, since cedar was cheap and covered the smell of old magic, which is a smell like rain on hot iron.

"Right," I said. "Throat first."

I'd already ruled out the obvious. No swelling, no fever, no injury to the mouth or tongue — I'd checked gently, with Ash's wary permission, given via a solemn nod that made them look about forty years older than their apparent seven. Whatever had taken their voice hadn't taken it by force. It had been *surrendered*. That distinction kept snagging on something in me, like a loose thread I couldn't find the end of.

So: remedies. Mundane ones, because Vell's anti-magic laws were enforced by men in fine coats who enjoyed their work, and because half of me still believed this was an ordinary ailment with an extraordinary coincidence attached.

Honey and warm water. Ash drank it without complaint and looked at me afterward with an expression I can only describe as *politely unconvinced*, as though I were a street performer who had dropped his balls.

Salt gargle. Same result, plus visible distaste.

I steamed a kettle and had them breathe the vapor under a towel, which turned them briefly into a very small ghost and produced, for one heart-stopping moment, a sound — a muffled, startled *hah* through the cloth. I nearly wept. Then the towel came off and the sound went wherever such sounds go, and we were back to silence.

"You're enjoying this," I told them.

Ash blinked once. Slowly. The universal language of the unimpressed.

Fine. Time for the charms.

Now, understand: the charms I sold in the shop downstairs were harmless. That wasn't modesty; it was engineering. A knot of red thread against headaches. A copper ring that kept milk sweet an extra day. Little brass bells that discouraged moths. Magic sanded down so fine it barely qualified as magic anymore — the magical equivalent of a lullaby hummed by someone who's forgotten the words. The Magistry had inspected my stock twice and left both times looking vaguely disappointed, like tax collectors finding honest books.

But upstairs, in a locked drawer beneath the false bottom where I kept nothing, officially, I kept a few things that were more than they appeared. Old habits from before Vell. From before, period, apparently, since there seemed to be less "before" in my head than there should have been.

I took out a throat-charm: a sliver of rowan wood in a net of silver wire, carved with a word I no longer remembered learning. Speak-freely, we'd called them, back when "we" meant someone. You held it to your throat and thought of what you wanted to say, and the charm did the saying, clear as a bell, for as long as you could keep the thought steady.

Ash held it. Thought. The rowan warmed in their small fist.

And said nothing.

Not *failed* — failed would have been simple. Failed would mean broken charm, faulty goods, take it back to the maker. This was different. The charm worked. I could feel it working, the way you feel a door push against your hand from the other side. Something in Ash was pushing back. Or something in Ash had been *sealed*, the way you seal a jar, the way you seal — 

I didn't finish the thought. There was a door in my cellar I'd trained myself not to finish thoughts near.

I tried a dream-charm next, because the nights had been bad. Ash screamed in their sleep — no words, just screaming, which is somehow worse than words, words at least give you somewhere to start. The dream-charm was a flat disc of river-clay baked with lavender and a pinch of grave-soil, illegal in three provinces and standard practice in midwives' bags everywhere before the Magistry got organized. You set it under the pillow and it caught the nightmare like a moth-catch catches moths.

That night I set it beneath Ash's pillow and sat outside their door like a fool, listening.

For two hours, silence. Blissful, ordinary silence. I actually let myself feel relief, which any student of my life could have told me was a tactical error.

Then, past midnight, the disc began to sing.

Not loudly. A thin, sweet note, like a wet finger on glass, rising and falling. I crept in. Ash slept on, brow smooth, breathing easy — the charm was working, catching something, holding something. But the note it sang wasn't the note it was made to sing. Dream-charms don't sing. They sit there and do their grim little work silently, like all good servants.

This one was singing *back*.

I picked it up. The clay was warm. Warmer than a body, warmer than the stove. And under the song, faint as a name called up a well, there was something else — a rhythm, a cadence, a pattern of emphasis that my ear knew and the rest of me refused to know. The same cadence Ash spoke. The dead tongue. The language older than kingdoms, the one I'd understood by halves the night they arrived, the way you understand a song from childhood without knowing a word of its lyrics.

The charm was translating. Catching the nightmare on one side and singing it out the other, and what it sang sounded less like a child's fear and more like — terms. Conditions. A recitation. Somewhere in Ash's sleep, something was reading out a contract, and my cheap clay disc was broadcasting the proceedings like a town crier with a cold.

I did the sensible thing. I threw it in the hearth.

It burned green, which clay does not do, and the flame made a shape I chose not to study, and then it was ash among ashes, and I stood there telling myself that was that.

It was not that. Because the next morning, Ash looked at me across breakfast — porridge, honey, the whole domestic theater of it — and touched their own throat, then pointed at the hearth, then at me. And shook their head.

They'd heard it too. Or felt it. However you perceive a thing like that when you're seven and voiceless and your soul has paperwork attached. They weren't angry. That was the worst part. They looked *sorry*, as though the nightmare had inconvenienced me, as though the burning charm were a cup they'd broken.

I wanted to say, *it's not your fault, none of this is your fault.* I opened my mouth and what came out was a sentence in their own tongue — four words, badly pronounced, that I had no memory of learning. I heard myself say it and felt the floor tilt.

*I'm sorry too,* it meant. Roughly. Probably. My accent was atrocious but Ash's face went soft anyway, and they pressed their hand flat over their chest, twice, which I gathered meant something between *thank you* and *stay*.

I stayed. Obviously. Where else would I go? But I want it noted for the record that I was frightened. Properly frightened, down in the marrow, in the place where a man keeps the fears he doesn't show customers. Every charm I tried failed except the ones that answered, and every answer pointed the same direction: down. Toward the cellar. Toward the gate. Toward whatever five-year-old version of me had done that I'd paid so dearly to forget.

* * *

Sera Quill lived two doors down, in a narrow house the color of weak tea, where she raised pigeons and grew roses that shouldn't have survived the climate and did anyway, lush and red as spilled wine. She was sixty-something, a widow of some twenty years, sharp-tongued, generous, and the closest thing I had to a friend, which is to say she brought me soup when I was ill and gossip when I wasn't, and asked nothing back except that I occasionally lose to her at cards, which I did, competently.

She also noticed things. This was her defining feature and my recurring problem.

She'd noticed Ash within a day — of course she had; Sera noticed a new cat in the district before the cat's previous owners did. She'd brought over a coat, outgrown by a grandnephew, and bread still warm, and stood in my shop doing the thing she did, which was talking while looking at everything else.

"The child doesn't speak," she observed, in the tone other people used for weather.

"They're mute," I said. "An affliction."

"Mm." Her eyes went past me, up the stairs, toward the sound — or rather the absence of sound — of Ash moving overhead. "Mute children usually make noise anyway. Crying, laughing. This one's quiet as a held breath."

"Some afflictions are thorough."

"And they talk in their sleep." She said it mildly, refolding the coat's sleeve, not looking at me. "My window was open last night. Sound carries in these lanes. Odd language. Old. My grandmother used to say words like that, when I was small. Said they came from under the mountain, and you didn't say them indoors unless you wanted company."

My hands, which had been wrapping a moth-bell with professional serenity, went still. Sera watched them go still, and I watched her watch, and neither of us said anything for a moment, and the shop ticked around us with all its small harmless noises.

"There was a fire," I offered. "A charm that misbehaved. Green flame. You may have seen—"

"I saw green light under your door at midnight, yes." Now she looked at me. Sera's eyes were pale gray, the color of the sea before weather, and they did not blink much. "Juniper. I've lived in Vell fifty-one years. I buried a husband. I know what ordinary trouble looks like coming down a lane, and I know what the other kind looks like, and the other kind always arrives barefoot."

"That's very poetic," I said. "Did you rehearse it?"

"Every widow rehearses. It's how we survive." She set the folded coat on the counter, precisely squared. "I'm not asking you to tell me anything. I'm telling you that I see you carrying something heavy, and I'm telling you that I once carried something heavy myself, and that heavy things get heavier when you carry them alone."

"Sera—"

"Bread's on the step tomorrow. Whether you talk or not." She pulled her shawl straight and headed for the door, then paused, hand on the frame, back still to me. "The mountain takes people, Juniper Halloran. It took my sister, forty years ago, and everyone told my mother she'd run off to the coast with a tinker, and my mother believed it because believing it hurt less. It didn't run off anywhere. There are no tinkers on that road. Only tolls."

The bell above the door gave its small dishonest chime as she left — I'd charmed it years ago to sound cheerful regardless of who entered, a bit of theater for the customers. It rang bright and stupid into the silence she left behind.

I stood there among my harmless wares, in my careful life, with my careful lies stacked around me like sandbags, and realized the water was coming through anyway.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Ash, at the window, watching Sera cross the lane below. Watching her the way you watch a door you're not sure is locked.

And beneath our feet, down past the shop, down past the cellar stairs, behind the door I did not look at, something hummed — low, patient, contented, like a creditor who knows he'll be paid.

Three notes. Pause. Three notes again.

Ash's hand found mine on the banister. Small, warm, gripping hard.

I still couldn't have said which of us was comforting the other.
