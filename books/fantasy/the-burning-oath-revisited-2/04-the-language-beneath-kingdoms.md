## 4. The Language Beneath Kingdoms

*Before. Before the shop. Before the woman-with-kind-eyes and the stairs that creak like old men.*

There was a mountain.

That is the first thing, always the first thing, when the dark behind my eyes opens its one eye: a mountain with its head in the clouds and its feet in the ground, and the ground went down much further than the mountain went up. I know this the way you know your own hands. I knew it before I had words for anything else.

The mountain had a door at the bottom of it.

Not a door like doors. A door like a mouth.

* * *

I remember candles.

I remember them because there were so many, and because none of them flickered. That is the wrong thing about them. Fire is supposed to move. Fire is alive, even a little bit alive, even the mean little flame of a tallow stub. These did not move. Thousands of them, down a hall that went on longer than any hall should, standing straight as soldiers, burning without eating anything, and I understood — in the way you understand things before you have the words to be frightened of them properly — that they were not candles.

They were lit anyway.

Somebody had lit them. Somebody kept them lit.

I did not want to think about what burned inside them. I thought about it anyway. That is the trouble with remembering: it does not ask permission.

* * *

Hands.

This is the part that comes back most often, and it comes back the same way every time, which is how I know it is true and not just a bad dream. Bad dreams change. They get sloppy. This one is careful.

Hands signing a paper.

Big hands, dry as autumn leaves, with rings on three fingers. Not gold rings. Older than gold. The kind of rings that make your teeth ache to look at, the way a high note does.

And other hands — smaller, shaking, pressing a mark into wax.

I know those smaller hands.

I have looked at my own hands a hundred times since, turning them over in the light from the shop window, counting fingers, checking the lines of them, trying to see if the shape of what they did is still written somewhere in the skin. Hands don't look guilty. That's the unfair thing about hands. They just hang there at the ends of your arms, innocent as spoons.

But mine signed something. Mine gave something away.

I know this because of the hole.

* * *

Here is what I can explain, and I will explain it badly, because the language I was given — the only language left in my mouth — doesn't have words for *soul*. It has words for breath, for shadow, for the part of you that walks in dreams. It has eleven different words for silence, which tells you something about where I come from. But the thing that was taken, the thing folded up small and carried down past the candle-hall into the deep dark where even the mountain's bones end — there is no word for that. There is only a shape your tongue makes when it tries and fails.

In the old tongue, you say it by stopping.

You say everything up to the edge of it, and then you stop, and whoever listens hears the stop, and that is the word.

So: they took my —

Yes. Like that.

* * *

The hall of candles again. I keep circling back to it. A moth does the same with a lamp; nobody calls the moth stupid, they call it doomed, which is nearly the same thing but politer.

There were jars.

I didn't understand the jars at first. I thought they were lamps, or honey, or the glass stomachs of some enormous patient creature. Amber-colored, each one, glowing soft as a held breath, and arranged in rows going back further than I could see, and each row labeled in a script that moved when I wasn't looking directly at it.

A tall person in gray knelt down to my height. Their face was courteous. That is the word I will use forever for that face: *courteous*. Nothing cruel in it. Nothing kind either. The face of someone doing arithmetic.

*"It doesn't hurt,"* they said, in the old tongue, the way you'd tell a child the doctor's needle barely stings. *"It only changes whose it is."*

Whose it is.

I had not known a self could belong to somebody. I had not known it could be *sold*. And yet the moment they said it, some animal part of me understood completely, the way a lamb understands the smell of the barn where its mother went and didn't come back.

* * *

Between the fragments there are gaps, and the gaps are the worst part.

People think the nightmare is the monster. The nightmare is fine. You can run from a monster; monsters are generous that way, they give you something to do. What I cannot outrun is the white space between one memory and the next. The mountain, then nothing. The candles, then nothing. The hands, the wax, the courteous face — then waking up.

Waking up where?

On a road. Mud. Rain. My feet bare and blue and my mouth full of a language no one around me spoke, shouting it at strangers who crossed the street to avoid me, shouting it because if I stopped speaking it I was afraid the last of me would go out like the candles go out — all at once, politely, without flickering.

Then the shop.

Then her.

* * *

The woman-with-kind-eyes. Juniper. I learned their name from the sign above the door, painted in letters that curl like smoke: THE SHOP OF SMALL MERCIES. I thought the name was a joke at first. Then I saw the charms — the little knots and coins and knotted threads, each one harmless as bread — and I understood it wasn't a joke, it was a disguise. Everything in that shop was pretending to be less than it was.

I felt at home immediately. I am also pretending to be less than I am. I am pretending to be a child instead of a child-shaped hole.

Juniper talks too much when they're nervous. I've counted. They fill silences the way other people fill pantries — frantically, before winter comes. But sometimes they stop talking and just look at me, and their face does something complicated, like a lock turning over inside itself, and I think: *you know me. Not my name. But you know the shape of what happened to me. It's written on you somewhere.*

Their hands shake sometimes. When the humming starts downstairs.

I hear it too.

Three notes. Pause. Three notes. It comes up through the floor like water through stone, and it is saying something in the old tongue, and I hate that I understand it. It says: *still here. Still here. Still here.* Patient as debt.

* * *

Last night I dreamed the whole thing again, front to back, and this time the dream let me stay for the ending, which it never does, and I wish it hadn't, because now I know:

After the hands signed, after the wax cooled with its mark in it like a wound cooling, the courteous one in gray stood up and said, *"Witnessed and sealed. The court thanks you for your custom."*

Thanks you for your custom.

And the small shaking hands — my hands — reached out after the departing gray sleeve, and caught it, and the voice that came out of my own mouth was cracked and desperate and older than I am now, and it said:

*"Take mine instead."*

Take mine instead.

I woke up with Juniper holding me, because I had been screaming, except I hadn't been screaming — screaming is a sound, and what came out of me was the opposite of a sound, the eleven-silences word, the stop-word, the place where my tongue goes to the edge of *soul* and falls off it. Juniper held me anyway. Juniper's heart was going fast enough that I could feel it through two shirts, and I thought: *yours too. Something down there is humming your name as well as mine.*

You cannot say that in any language. But children don't need languages. Children just know.

* * *

Today, while Juniper argued gently with a man about whether a luck-knot was a charm or merely string (it is string; it is also a charm; that is the whole art), I went to the top of the cellar stairs and listened.

The door at the bottom — the one Juniper never looks at, the one their eyes slide past like a stone in a shoe — was warm. I could feel it from six steps up. Warmth is wrong for a cellar. Cellars are supposed to be the cold places, the safe places, the places where milk keeps and secrets keep. This one was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and it hummed its three notes, and beneath the hum, very faintly, something else.

Not words. Almost words. The way a face seen through fog is almost a face.

It was calling a name.

Not Ash. Ash is the name I made myself out of the burnt end of a stick and the grey end of a fire — ash is what's left, ash is honest, ash asks nothing. The name it was calling was longer. It had mountains in it. It had the weight of wax and the shine of amber and the courtesy of the gray-faced ones, and when it touched the inside of my skull it fit perfectly, which is the most frightening thing of all. A wrong key rattles in the lock. This one turned smooth as butter.

My hand was on the stair rail. My foot was on the next step down.

From the shop above, Juniper's voice — bright, too bright, the voice they use to sandbag the flood: "—and that's why the knot wants resting a week before it works, sir, magic's mostly patience—"

Magic's mostly patience.

I took my foot off the step.

Because here is what I understood, standing on those stairs with the warm door breathing below me: whatever is down there has been waiting five years, and it can wait five more, and it does not need me to come to it today. It needs me to come to it *alone*, someday, when everyone who might pull me back is dead or gone or forgetful. That is how the court works. I remember that much. The candles never flicker because they are never in a hurry.

So I went back up. I sat on the stool by the counter. I watched Juniper sell a man a piece of string and call it patience.

But I looked at the floorboards. All the floorboards. Because I have started noticing things: the way Juniper's eyes avoid one board near the corner, the way their boot scuffs around it rather than over it, the way their hand once drifted toward it and stopped mid-air like a bird hitting glass.

There is something under that board.

Something half-burned. I can smell it when the wind is right — old smoke, old paper, old oath. The same smell as the wax. The same smell as the night my hands shook.

Juniper thinks the sealed gate is the only door in this house.

Juniper is wrong. There is a door in the floor, and a door in their head, and both of them are locked from the inside, and I am a child made mostly of holes, and holes are very good at finding other holes.

Tonight, when they sleep, I am going to find out what's under the boards.
