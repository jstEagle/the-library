## 5. Sera Knows

The widow Sera had lived across the lane for as long as I'd had the shop, which meant five years of nodding at each other over fences and never once saying anything that mattered. She was the sort of neighbor who returned your borrowed ladder with the rungs polished. The sort who left turnips on your step in autumn without a note, because a note would make it charity and Sera did not deal in charity. She dealt in turnips.

I trusted her the way you trust weather. Reliably present, occasionally violent, fundamentally indifferent to your plans.

So when she knocked on my door three days after Ash arrived — not the shop bell, the *door*, which nobody used — I felt my stomach perform a small, practiced maneuver it had perfected over years of hiding: the drop-and-settle, the body bracing for exposure while the face arranged itself into pleasant nothing.

"Juniper." She stood on my step with rain beading on her shawl and an expression like a drawn curtain. "Might I come in?"

People in Vell didn't ask to come in. They came in, or they shouted from the street, or they wrote letters with such elaborate courtesy that the letter itself was the entire transaction. Asking was a southern thing, or an old thing, or a dangerous thing.

"The shop's closed," I said. "The child's sleeping."

"I know the shop's closed. It's been closed since Tuesday, and you've hung the *back from holiday* sign, which is a lie, because you haven't left Vell since the day you arrived, and neither have I." She said it without heat. That was the worst part. Accusation I could fight. Inventory I could not. "May I come in, Juniper."

It wasn't a question either time. I stepped aside.

* * *

She sat in my kitchen like she'd sat there before, though I was certain she hadn't. Took the chair nearest the stove. Dried her hands on her skirt with the economy of someone who had spent decades making one fire do the work of three.

"You've got a child upstairs," she said.

"A ward. Temporarily. Relations of—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just final, like a lid going on a jar. "You'll want to lie to me, and I understand why, but we're both old enough to find lying exhausting, so let's skip to the part where I tell you what I actually came to say, and then you can decide whether to lie to me after, when I'm gone and it costs you nothing."

I poured tea because my hands wanted employment. She watched me do it with the patience of a woman watching a child stack blocks it intended to knock down.

"There's a girl in Corren's Row," she said, "who bought a charm off you last spring. A fever charm. Harmless. Good work, actually — better than harmless, if anyone had bothered to look, but no one looks, because you're careful." She accepted the cup. "Her mother talks. Her mother talks the way rivers run. And last week her mother told me something odd: that the charm-seller on Lantern Street has a child now who cries in her sleep in a language the mother's own grandmother used to mutter wards against."

The teapot made a small sound as I set it down. Or maybe I made the sound. Hard to say.

"Old tongue," Sera said. "Pre-kingdom. The kind they speak under mountains."

"They don't speak anything under mountains," I said. "There's nothing under mountains except rock and bad air and miners' regrets."

"Mm." She sipped her tea. Looked at me over the rim with eyes the color of wet slate. "That's exactly what I said, once, to someone I loved very much. And then I said it again at a funeral."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Sera Voss, widow: husband dead eight years, a fall from scaffolding, very sad, very ordinary. No children. Kept hens. Grew leeks with an aggression I'd always privately admired. Here is what I apparently did not know about Sera Voss: whatever lived behind those slate eyes, which had just looked at me the way you look at a scar you recognize because you have one in the same place.

"My sister's name was Maren," she said.

I hadn't asked. I want that on the record. I was standing at my own stove, gripping a towel, saying absolutely nothing, and she simply began, the way water finds a crack.

"She was four years older than me and twice as brave, which is a terrible combination in a sibling. When we were girls — this is fifty years ago, Juniper, before the Magistry, before the laws, back when magic was poor people's medicine and everyone pretended otherwise — there was sickness in our village. Lung-rot, from the tannery. It took our father first. Then it started on Maren."

She turned the cup slowly in her hands.

"Our grandmother knew the old ways. Real ones, not charms — I mean no offense to your charms, I'm sure they're lovely. She knew the roads. And she told us there was a court beneath the mountain that bought things. Souls, mostly. Years. Voices. First memories. Anything a person could carry and couldn't afford to keep. And it paid well, and it always honored its contracts to the letter, and that was the trap, because the letter is never what you mean."

My mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the tea.

"What did your grandmother buy?" I asked. My voice came out steady. I was almost proud of it.

"Nothing." Sera smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "That's the lesson, you see. Grandmother wouldn't bargain. She said the court doesn't take money, it takes meaning, and you never get to choose afterward what something meant. So Maren went to the mountain herself. Sixteen years old, coughing blood, and she walked up the pilgrim road in her good boots and paid — we never learned what — and came home whole. Lungs clear. Strong as an ox. Lived to marry, lived to bury her husband, lived until she was seventy-one."

"That's a happy story," I said carefully.

"It's a story with a long middle," Sera said. "Because whatever she paid, she paid out of herself, and the hole it left grew. She forgot our father's face first. Then she forgot she'd ever been sick. Then she forgot the road, then the boots, then — bit by bit, year by year — she forgot pieces of herself she couldn't name missing. She'd stand in doorways crying and couldn't tell you why. She'd wake reaching for someone who wasn't there and didn't know who. At the end, she held my hand and said, *Sera, I know I loved something once. Tell me what it was.* And I couldn't. Because she'd sold the *telling* too."

The kitchen was very quiet. Rain ticked against the window. Upstairs, faintly, Ash turned over in sleep, and the floorboard sighed.

"So when I hear," Sera continued, "that the charm-witch on Lantern Street has taken in a child who speaks the under-tongue in her sleep — a child whose soul, if I'm any judge of these matters, is currently sitting in a jar somewhere beneath the Grayfell — I pay attention. Because I have seen what that court does with the change, Juniper. It keeps the coin. It gives you back a person shaped exactly like the person you loved, minus the parts that made them *them*. And everyone around them spends the rest of their lives pretending that's fine."

* * *

"You don't know that's what happened here," I said.

"No," she agreed. "I know a sealed gate in your cellar hums loud enough that I heard it from my kitchen two nights ago, through stone and a lane and my own deafness in one ear. I know you've burned salt in every doorway of this house — cheap trick, but well done, I'll grant. I know you flinched just now when I said *jar*, which is not the flinch of an innocent woman, it's the flinch of a woman who has seen a jar." She set down her cup with a click. "And I know that look on your face. I wore it for fifty years. It's the look of someone standing next to a locked door telling herself the room behind it is empty."

"I think you should go home, Sera."

"Probably." She didn't move. "But first I'm going to say the thing I walked through the rain to say, and then I'll go home, and tomorrow I'll bring turnips, and we'll never speak of it again if that's what you choose. Here is the thing." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something older than her voice, something with gravel and riverbed in it. "You cannot get that child's soul back alone. Whatever you're planning — and you are planning, I can smell it on you, it smells like my sister's good boots — you will fail alone, and worse, you'll fail *loudly*, and the Magistry will hang you for unlicensed craft and take the child to an orphanage where the dreams will follow, because the court does not release collateral just because the debtor died."

"I'm not planning anything."

"Juniper." She said my name the way you'd set down a heavy load. "I watched you pour that tea without looking at the pot. I watched the flame bend toward your hand when you thought I wasn't looking. You are the worst-hidden witch in Vell and the only reason no one's noticed is that Vell prefers not to notice things. But the Undercourt notices everything. It's the only thing in the world that reads contracts properly." She rose, took her shawl, settled it on her shoulders. "So. My offer. I know the pilgrim road. I walked the first mile of it with my sister and I have spent fifty years walking the rest of it in my head. I know the tollhouses. I know what they cost. And I am offering — freely, asking nothing, because I owe a debt to a dead girl and debts need somewhere to go — to walk it with you."

"Why?" It came out harsher than I meant. "Why would you help me? You don't know me. You don't know what I—" I stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and I genuinely did not know what lay past the edge. *What I did.* What *had* I done? The thought slid sideways off my mind like water off wax, and for one vertiginous second I stood in my own kitchen unable to remember why I'd moved to Vell, why the cellar door stayed locked, why my left wrist bore a scar like a wax seal pressed into skin—

"—Juniper." Sera's hand was on my arm. When had she crossed the room? "Breathe. There it is. That's the hole, isn't it? I can see it from here. Something's been cut out of you and the edges haven't healed, they've just gone smooth."

"I don't—" My voice cracked. I hated it. "I don't understand what you're talking about."

"No," she said gently. "That's rather the point."

* * *

At the door she paused, rain waiting behind her like an audience.

"Others remember," she said. "That's what you were about to ask, even if you didn't know it. People like me. Scattered, old, mostly women, mostly dismissed — the ones who kept the old knowledge because no one thought us worth silencing. We remember the roads, the tolls, the court's habits. Some of us remember more than we'd like." Her eyes flicked upward, toward the ceiling, toward the sleeping child. "And some of us, Juniper, remember *you*. Or we would, if you'd let us. There are gaps in this city where you're concerned. Gaps with smooth edges. I've spoken to Old Marta at the archive and Tam the lamplighter, and between the three of us we hold perhaps a quarter of your last six years, and none of us knows how we came to hold them, and all of us find that deeply unsettling."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. I gripped the doorframe.

"You've been asking about me."

"I've been *worrying* about you. There's a difference, though I admit the methods overlap." She pulled up her hood. "Tomorrow I'll bring turnips. If you want my help, leave a candle in the front window — white, not the ones you sell, a real one. If you don't, leave it dark, and I'll trouble you no further, and when they come for you — and they will come, the Magistry's already sniffing down Lantern Street, ask Tam — I'll grieve you properly, which is the only kindness left to offer the foolish."

"Sera." She stopped on the step, rain silvering around her. "The child. What the court holds. Can it be gotten back? Truly? Not — not the way your sister—"

"Oh, child." She didn't turn around. "Nothing gets gotten back the way you want. But there are flaws in old contracts. There always are. The court writes them fast and proud, and pride makes sloppy law." She stepped into the rain, then paused once more, half-turned, her voice carrying soft through the wet air. "One more thing, and then I'll let you not-sleep tonight. Your handwriting. I saw it once, years ago, on a receipt for candlewick. Round letters, heavy downstrokes, the *J* crossed like a blessing."

"Yes?"

"It's the same hand that signed Maren's contract. I checked the copy the village priest kept, fifty years ago, before I burned it." The hood shadowed her face, but I heard the smile in it, thin and terrible. "Sleep well, Juniper. Tomorrow, the candle or the dark."

Then she was gone down the lane, and I stood in my doorway with the rain coming in past my shoulders, listening to the cellar hum its low, patient note, and understanding — finally, fully — that the door behind me had never once been locked against the world.

It had been locked against me.
