## 6. The Ledger of Sold Souls

The Vell city archives smelled like a library that had died and been poorly embalmed—dust, tallow, and the faint vinegar tang of old ink eating through old paper. I hadn't been inside in five years. I'd had reasons. Good ones, probably, though at the moment they were locked in the same drawer as everything else I'd misplaced about myself.

I told myself I was there for Ash.

That was true, mostly. It was also a convenient flag to plant over a much uglier country: I was there for me. Because Sera had said one sentence to me on her doorstep two nights ago—"You should look at what's written down, dear, before you decide what you're willing to remember"—and it had lodged under my skin like a splinter of glass. You don't dig splinters out with your fingers. You wait until they fester enough to push themselves up. Mine had taken three days and a sleepless night listening to Ash breathe in the room above my shop, small and quick, like a bird deciding whether to stay.

So: the archives. Wednesday morning, when the clerks were hungover from market-day eve and too bleary to ask questions.

"Name and purpose?" asked the man at the desk. He had the complexion of someone who'd been raised by candlelight and resented the sun personally.

"Halloran," I said. "Charm-seller. Researching folk remedies."

He looked at my hands, which are stained to the wrist with charm-ink no amount of scrubbing fully removes, and then at my face, which is famous in exactly one neighborhood for exactly one thing, and he wrote down *Halloran, folk remedies* without comment. That's the beauty of Vell. Nobody wants to know anything badly enough to make it their problem.

* * *

The archive was organized by a man named Corvus Dill who had died forty years ago and whose system had outlived him the way a bad marriage outlives love. Everything was filed by district, then by decade, then by whatever mood Dill had been in that afternoon. The Undercourt—the court beneath the mountain, the buried dead, the thing under my cellar floor that hummed now whenever Ash came within ten feet of it—was not supposed to be in the archives at all. Officially, the Undercourt did not exist. Unofficially, it existed so thoroughly that an entire wing of shelving had been quietly devoted to pretending otherwise.

I found the wing behind a door marked FLOOD DAMAGE, 1187–1191, which was either a joke or a warning or both. The air changed as soon as I stepped through. Colder. The dust lay differently here—not settled but *arranged*, as if something swept it nightly into patterns only it could read. The shelves went back farther than the building had any right to be deep. Somewhere past the third row, the lamplight gave up and the dark took over the work.

I'd brought my own candle stub, because I am many things but not a fool. I lit it with a match, not magic. There are laws about this sort of thing, and while I break them daily in the privacy of my own shop, breaking them in front of government property felt like showing off.

The ledgers began three rows in. Great fat volumes bound in leather gone the color of tea left standing, spines stamped with years. Souls Bought, some of them said. Others just said *Receipts*, which was somehow worse. A receipt implies a transaction completed, goods delivered, both parties satisfied. Somebody had been satisfied by this. Somebody had signed off on it. Somebody had—

Well. Let me not get ahead of myself. Getting ahead of myself is how I ended up with five missing years in the first place, if Sera was right, and she usually was about things like this.

I pulled the volume for the year I needed—or rather, the year I *didn't need*, the year I couldn't account for, the year with a hole in it shaped exactly like the rest of my life—and carried it to the reading table under the one lamp that still worked. The table had a chain bolted to it, for chaining books to, because apparently even the dead worry about theft. I didn't chain mine. If the archive wanted its ledger back, it could come and explain itself.

* * *

The ledger opened flat, which meant it had been opened often, in the same place, by hands that knew where to go. That detail arrived in my head a few minutes before I understood it, the way thunder arrives before you count out why.

Souls bought by the Court Beneath the Mountain. Each entry in a clerk's neat hand: name, district, date, price paid, and—this was the part that made my stomach perform a slow, dignified revolution—a column headed *Consideration Given*. Not what the soul cost. What the seller received. The court kept honest books, in its way. Every horror itemized. Every grief receipted.

I turned pages. Names I half-knew. The cooper's daughter from Tanner's Row, sold for "relief of father's debts." A ferryman, sold for "passage granted posthumously"—which I took to mean he'd bought himself a better afterlife by selling his current one, a deal so grimly sensible it deserved a toast. Page after page of ordinary desperation dressed up in contractual language. The Buried King, whoever he was, ran a tidy operation. No blood on his ledger. Just ink.

And then I turned a page and stopped turning pages.

There were two entries side by side, dated the same night, five years ago. The ink of the first was brownish, dried slow, blotted once. The second entry sat beneath it like a stone under a leaf.

The first entry read: *One (1) soul, adult, freely surrendered. Name withheld at seller's request.* In the consideration column: *Restoration of health, terminal condition reversed.*

I read that line four times. Terminal condition reversed. Someone had been dying, five years ago, and someone had walked into the mountain—or sent word into it, or stood at a gate like the one rotting under my shop—and traded a soul to un-die them.

Someone had saved a life that way. Someone had paid for it with a soul.

Not their own soul. That was the thing my eyes kept sliding past, the way your tongue keeps finding the chipped tooth. The consideration given was health. The *price* was a different soul entirely. One soul, adult, freely surrendered—but not the seller's. The ledger was very clear about that, in the way ledgers are clear about things people would prefer be murky. The seller had sold someone else.

I looked at the second entry.

*One (1) soul, child, age indeterminate. Name: Ash.*

Ash. Written in a childless clerk's careful letters, and beside it, in the column where the seller's signature went, a name I knew better than my own morning face.

*J. Halloran.*

My handwriting. My slanted, impatient, left-leaning hand, the one that signs charm-wrappers and tax forms and the occasional apology note to Sera when I've let my hedge grow over her fence line. The J with its little hook, like a fishing lure. The double-l I always rush so it looks like a U having ideas. Five years of signing things and there it was, older, shakier, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear slightly at the descender—pressed the way you press when your hand is steady but everything under it isn't.

I recognized it the way you recognize your own voice on a recording: instantly, unwillingly, with a lurch of *that can't be right* followed immediately by the sick knowledge that it is.

* * *

I want to describe what happened next accurately, because I think accuracy matters more than dignity, and I have very little of the latter to spare anyway.

The world tilted. Not dramatically—no thunderclap, no cold wind, nothing cinematic. It tilted the way a floor tilts when you realize it has been tilting all along and you simply hadn't noticed. The lamp seemed to move a foot to the left. The table seemed to drop an inch. My own reflection in the ledger's glossy page—ink does that, holds a ghost of whoever leans over it—looked back at me from an angle I didn't recognize, and for a moment I genuinely could not say whether the person leaning over that book was me or someone wearing me.

Self-betrayal is a strange vertigo. Betrayal by another person has a shape. There's a villain, a wound, a story you can tell at parties or to a magistrate. But betrayal by yourself has no edges. You go looking for the person who did it and every road leads back to where you're standing. You reach for the memory of doing it and your hand closes on empty air—on a gap, a smooth place where a scar should be, five years of floorboards with nothing under them but more floorboards.

I had done this. I had signed away a child's soul, and I could not remember doing it, and the not-remembering was somehow worse than the deed. Because the deed might have been desperate. It might have been loving. It might have been the only card left in a losing hand, played by someone at the end of their rope with a dying partner and a gate that hummed and a king who bargains. All of that might be true. But I couldn't know, because whoever made that choice had apparently decided I shouldn't carry it, and had cut it out of me the way you'd cut out a tumor—or the way you'd cut out a conscience.

The signature wasn't shaky because the hand was unsteady. I understood that now, sitting very still under a bad lamp in a flooded-wing archive pretending to research folk remedies. The signature was shaky because the person writing it was crying, or laughing, or both, and pressing down through it anyway. Signing anyway. That's what desperation looks like in ink. Not wildness. Pressure.

Terminal condition reversed. Someone had been dying. Someone I loved—I knew that much the way you know a room's shape with the lights out; not the contents, just the walls. And I had gone to the court beneath the mountain, or it had come to me, and we had struck terms, and the terms had a child's name in them, and the child was currently asleep above my shop with a charm bracelet on their wrist that I'd woven myself and that was, I now realized, doing precisely nothing, because you cannot charm your way out of a contract. Contracts aren't curses. They're worse. They're *agreed*.

Ash's soul was in a jar somewhere under the mountain, glowing amber, and I had put it there.

Me. With my own hand. My own hooked J.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the candle stub to drown in its own wax. Long enough for the archivist to shuffle past twice and glance at me with the mild concern of a man wondering if the folk-remedy researcher was going to be sick on a priceless ledger. I gripped the table edge and breathed and did not be sick on a priceless ledger, and I counted that among the day's accomplishments.

Here is what I noticed, once the vertigo ebbed enough to let me notice things: the ledger had been opened to these pages often. The binding's spine was cracked at exactly this spread. Someone had come back to this entry again and again—reading it, maybe touching it, maybe pressing a thumb against the signature the way I wanted to and didn't dare. Checking. Making sure it was still real. Still there. Still theirs.

Or—another thought, colder, arriving on schedule like all my worst thoughts do—someone had been sent to check. The court kept honest books. Honest books get audited. Maybe the humming under my cellar wasn't memory at all. Maybe it was *collections*. Maybe the gate under my shop wasn't a relic I'd inherited with the lease. Maybe it was a delivery entrance, and I'd known that once, and arranged my whole life around forgetting it, and the arrangement was coming apart now because I'd let a mute child sleep under my roof, a child whose soul the court already owned, whose true name something on the other side of that gate already whispered.

Sera had lost a sister to the Undercourt. She'd said it plainly, the way you say things that have scarred over. She'd also said: *others remember what you cannot.* Sitting there, I finally understood what she'd meant. She hadn't meant strangers. She'd meant that other people remembered *me*—the me from five years ago, the me who walked into that bargain wide awake. The widow across the lane might know more about my own life than I did. There was a thought to keep a man up at night, and I had a whole list of those already.

I copied the entry. Both entries. My hand shook doing it, which felt appropriate and also annoying, because I pride myself on steady hands—it's the one professional skill I advertise. Name, date, consideration, signature. I wrote *J. Halloran* in the margin next to the printed signature and compared them, letter by letter, hoping to find a difference, a forgery, an out. There wasn't one. If anything, my copy was neater than the original. Grief makes for poor penmanship. So does guilt. I had apparently supplied both in quantity.

Then I closed the ledger, and the closing sounded like a door.

* * *

The walk home took twice as long as usual because I kept stopping. At the corner by the fountain. Outside the chandler's. In the middle of Cooper's Lane, once, standing very still while a cart went around me and the driver swore at me with real creativity. I wasn't seeing the street. I was seeing the night five years ago that I couldn't see, reconstructing it from negative space: here is where the fear must have been, here is where the hope, here is where I must have stood at my own cellar door with a candle and a contract and a name in my mouth that wasn't mine to give.

A child's name. Ash. Except—no. The ledger said Ash, but the court wouldn't have taken a common-tongue name. Courts like that deal in true names, the old language, the language Ash speaks when the nightmares come, the language I half-understand without ever having learned. Somewhere in that amber jar, or in the ledger's deeper pages, or in the sealed part of my own skull, was the sound Ash had been born with. The sound the thing behind my cellar gate had whispered when the child touched the stones.

I had sold that. I had sold a child's *name*, which is to say the handle of their whole self, and the court had picked them up by it and carried them under the mountain, and what walks around my shop now wearing Ash's face is what's left when you take the name out of a person. Mute. Not because the voice is broken. Because the voice belongs to the name, and the name is in a jar.

I made charms for a living. Small mercies, the sign said, and I'd always liked the irony of it—little stitched protections against little harms, sold cheap to people who needed the comfort more than the cure. And underneath my shop, under the floorboards I refused to look at, I had been keeping the largest mercy of all: a child's stolen soul, hidden in plain sight, guarded by a man who didn't remember why he was guarding it. Or perhaps not guarding it. Perhaps merely *warehousing* it, per the terms, until collection.

Five years of careful invisibility. Five years of harmless charms and hedged hedges and never looking at the cellar stairs. I'd called it safety. Standing in Cooper's Lane with carts swearing at me, I finally saw it straight: it hadn't been safety. It had been a hiding place, and I had built it around the evidence of my own crime, and I had moved into it voluntarily, and I had called the arrangement peace.

It was not peace. It was a debt with my name on it, accruing interest in the dark.

* * *

By the time I reached my own door the light was going copper and the lane smelled of someone's dinner, something with onions, aggressively ordinary. I stood on my step a moment with my key in the lock, not turning it, listening. Above me, faintly, Ash was making the small sounds children make when they think no one's listening—half song, half talk, all of it in the old language, rising and falling like water over stones. The gate below answered. I could feel it through the soles of my boots, a hum pitched just under hearing, the way you feel weather in a bad tooth.

Two floors between them. A child and a gate, calling to each other in a language older than kingdoms, and me on the stairs in between, holding a key and a copied ledger page and a signature I couldn't deny.

I went in. I climbed past the cellar door without looking at it, which by now was less a habit than a discipline, like a monk's vow, and about as honest. Upstairs, Ash was sitting on the windowsill watching the lamplighters, and when I came in the child turned and looked at me—really looked, the way children and dogs and the dead look, straight through the costume to whatever's wearing it.

And Ash held out one small hand, palm up, and said a word.

Just one. Two syllables, in the old language, soft as an ember settling. I didn't know it. I knew it. Both at once—it sat in my ear like a key sitting in a lock I'd forgotten my house had. And the gate beneath us hummed louder, one long note, like something enormous clearing its throat after five years of patience.

I know what the word means now. I worked it out lying awake at three in the morning with the charm bracelet cold under my pillow.

It means *soon*.
