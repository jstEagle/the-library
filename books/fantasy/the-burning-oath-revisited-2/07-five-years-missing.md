## 7. Five Years Missing

The ledger was still on my desk when the sun came up, and I had not slept.

I'd tried. I'd made tea twice and let it go cold both times. I'd lain down in my clothes with the lamp burning and stared at the ceiling beams like they might confess something if I looked at them long enough. Beams don't confess. They just hold up the roof and mind their own business, which is more than I can say for myself.

Because here is what I could not stop thinking about: that signature. *Juniper Halloran*, written in a hand I knew better than my own face — because it *was* my own face, or at least the part of me that holds a pen. The slant of the J. The way the r's tail curled under like a hook catching a fish. Nobody forges that. Nobody bothers. And nobody else in Vell wrote like that, because I'd spent five years making sure nobody saw me write anything at all except price tags.

So either someone had studied my handwriting with obsessive care and forged a soul-contract in it, which seemed unlikely, or I had signed away a child's soul to the court beneath the mountain, and then somehow forgotten doing it.

The second option was worse. The second option was also, I was beginning to suspect, true.

* * *

I did what any sensible person does when confronted with an unbearable hypothesis: I made a list.

Not a magic list. A grocer's list, essentially. Things I knew about myself versus things I should have known about myself, laid out side by side like two columns of figures that refuse to balance.

Things I knew: I came to Vell six years ago, give or take. I opened the shop four years after that — no. Wait. Five years ago I opened the shop. That was the number that kept snagging, like a loose thread on a sleeve. Five years ago. The shop, the license, the careful gray life, all of it dated from five years ago, and everything before that was...

Everything before that was fog. Not empty fog — I wasn't going to flatter myself with amnesia-drama, the kind where you wake up in a field with no name and a mysterious locket. I had memories. Childhood, mostly. My mother's kitchen, the smell of scorched honey. Apprenticeship years, learning charmwork from a sour old woman named Pell who charged double for teaching and triple for forgiveness. Those were all intact, bright as painted tiles.

But between Pell's workshop and the shop in Vell there was a stretch of years that should have held — what? A journey? Another city? People? When I tried to look at those years directly, my mind did the thing water does around a stone in a stream. It parted. It flowed neatly on either side of a gap and presented me with a smooth surface, and only if I pressed — only if I sat very still and pushed against the current — could I feel the stone underneath. Rounded. Worn smooth by five years of not-looking.

Five years of practice, apparently. You can get very good at anything in five years, including forgetting.

And here was the detail that turned dread into certainty: the gap wasn't shaped like nothing. Gaps shaped like nothing are ordinary. Everyone has them; whole decades go missing into the gray wash of days that weren't different enough to keep. But this gap had edges. It had a shape. It began abruptly, on a night I could almost see — rain, a door, someone's hand reaching for mine — and ended abruptly, on a morning in Vell with a lease agreement and a landlord named Odo who told me I looked tired.

A gap with edges isn't forgetting. A gap with edges is surgery.

* * *

I went downstairs to the cellar because, apparently, I hated myself.

The gate was still shut. It had been shut since the night Ash arrived and hummed at me, and I had thrown every warding charm I owned at it and then, in a moment of pure superstition, hung my mother's old iron key on a nail beside it, though the key fit no lock I knew. The gate itself was unremarkable — old stone, older iron, a seam down the middle so fine you could lose a hair in it. I had lived above it for five years and told myself it was a foundation wall. Cellars have walls. Walls are allowed to be old.

But now I stood in front of it with a lantern and a list in my head, and I did something I had never once done in all that time.

I looked at it. Properly. Not the sidelong glance of a woman pretending her own cellar doesn't exist, but a long, straight, unflinching look, the kind you give a person you're about to interrogate.

And the gate looked back.

Stone doesn't have eyes. I know this. But there was a quality to the seam, a darkness in it deeper than shadow, and standing close enough to touch it I felt the particular attention of something patient. Not hostile. Patient. There's a difference, and the difference is worse. Hostile things want something from you *now*. Patient things have already got it. They're just waiting for you to notice.

My lantern flame leaned toward the seam. Flames lean toward drafts; that's physics, or near enough. But the cellar had no draft. I checked. I held my hand flat beside the flame and felt nothing, no movement of air at all, while the fire bowed toward the gate like a courtier bowing to a king.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to yourself in cellars is only madness if you're wrong about the company. "What did I do?"

The gate said nothing. Of course it didn't. Gates don't talk; that's doors you're thinking of, and even doors mostly just stick.

But as I stood there, my left hand rose. Not dramatically — no séance theatrics, no ghost-guided Ouija nonsense. It just lifted, slowly, the way your hand rises to touch a scar you've forgotten you're scratching, and pressed itself flat against the cold iron hinge-plate on my side of the gate.

And under my palm, in the metal, I felt it: a shape. Raised edges, worn nearly smooth, but deliberate. A seal. Not a lock's mechanism — I know locks, I've picked half the locks in Vell back when picking them was legal-ish — but a sigil, pressed into the iron the way a signet presses into wax. Circles within circles. A line through the center like a wound stitched shut.

I snatched my hand back. Then, feeling foolish, put it back, because evidence is evidence and fear is no reason to handle it badly.

The seal matched the scar on my wrist.

I'd had the scar for as long as I could remember having wrists, which is exactly the kind of sentence that sounds reasonable until you examine it. A thin white mark circling my left wrist like a bracelet, mostly hidden under my sleeve, which is why I wore sleeves — I'd told myself for years it was a burn from Pell's workshop, a candle accident, one of a hundred small injuries a charmworker collects. Charmworkers do collect scars. It's practically the uniform.

But this one circled. Burns don't circle. Cuts don't circle. Things that circle a wrist are things that were *bound* there — cord, chain, oath-ring — and this scar was the same shape, circle within circle, line through the center, as the seal in the gate's iron.

Someone had marked me. Or I had marked myself. Either way, the mark and the gate were two ends of the same knot, and I was the string.

* * *

You cannot interrogate a gate. You can, however, interrogate a house.

Houses keep records. Not deliberately — houses aren't ledgers — but they keep things the way rivers keep things: whatever sinks, stays. Coins under floorboards. Letters behind plaster. The dust-shape on a wall where a picture hung for twenty years. If I had done something five years ago — if I had signed something, sealed something, burned something — the house would have it, or the house before this one would, and since I'd lived above this cellar since the beginning, this house was the archive.

I started with the obvious place, which is to say the place I never clean: beneath the floorboards of my own bedroom.

There are seven boards in that room that sit differently from the others. I've known this since I moved in. They creak in a different register, a half-tone higher, and I had always assumed it was shoddy carpentry, because assuming the mundane explanation is a full-time occupation with me and I work very hard at it. But shoddy carpentry is random. These seven boards were clustered in one corner, by the window, in a rough rectangle — and rectangles are not random. Rectangles are decisions.

I pried up the first board with a chisel and a prayer to no one in particular.

Underneath: dust, a dead spider of impressive vintage, and nothing. The second board gave up a coin, copper, older than the city walls. The third, nothing. The fourth stuck, and I had to work it loose with real profanity, and when it came up my lantern light fell on something that stopped my breath in my throat.

Paper. Folded small, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked into the joist-space like a letter into a pocket.

I didn't reach for it immediately. I want that on the record. I knelt there over the open floor for a good minute, hands hovering, telling myself all the sensible things: it might be nothing. Deed papers. An old recipe. A love letter from whoever I was before, which would be its own small horror. And underneath the sensible things, the true thing: *if I open this, I cannot unopen it.* Knowledge is a door too, and some doors only swing one way.

Then I thought of Ash, barefoot and mute in the room above me, sleeping badly, dreaming in a language the dead used before kingdoms learned to speak. And the choice made itself, the way choices do when you've already decided who you want to be and just haven't admitted it yet.

I unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside was paper — two pieces of it, actually, and they told the whole story in miniature. The first was whole: a contract, dense with clauses in a legal hand, the kind of document the Undercourt favors, all courtesy and iron. *One soul, freely given, in exchange for one life preserved.* Standard terms. The Buried King runs a tidy shop. And at the bottom, a signature.

My name. My hand. The hooked r's, the slanted J. Dated five years ago, to the day, give or take a week — the date meant nothing to me, which was itself information. Most people know their significant dates. Weddings, deaths, betrayals. This date slid off me like rain off oiled cloth, and that sliding was the smooth surface of the stream again, the water parting around the stone.

The second piece of paper was the reason my hands shook.

It was the same contract. Same clauses, same terms, same date. But it was burned. Half-burned — the bottom third gone to char, the edges still black and crumbling, and across the face of it, scrawled in my handwriting in something that wasn't ink (it was brownish, waxy, and smelled faintly, when I dared sniff it, of tallow and something sharper underneath — myrrh, maybe, or blood dressed up as myrrh), across the face of it:

*BROKEN.*

And beneath that, smaller, hurried, as if written by someone running out of time or courage or both: *the second copy burns first. Let the original stand until I am strong enough.*

I read those words maybe forty times. I want to be precise about what they did to me, because precision matters when you're reconstructing a crime and you are both the detective and, increasingly, the suspect.

They told me three things.

First: there had been two copies of the contract. One for the court, one for the signer — standard practice, even among the buried dead; the King keeps immaculate books. The ledger in the archives held the court's copy. This had been mine.

Second: I had tried to break it. Someone — me, unmistakably me — had taken my copy of a soul-contract and set it on fire and written BROKEN across the ruins, and then, crucially, had *not finished*. Had stopped. Had hidden the half-burned thing under a floorboard instead of destroying it completely, with a note explaining why: *until I am strong enough.*

Third, and this is the one that undid me: whoever wrote that note had believed they would become strong enough. Had planned on it. Had left themselves a message, a task, a promise — *finish this* — and then had apparently... not. Had walked away from their own note. Had built a shop above a sealed gate and sold harmless charms and swept the floorboards and never once, in five years, pried up that corner by the window.

Or had they? Had they come down here on quiet nights, knelt over these boards, hovered, and pulled back? Was the smooth surface of my memory hiding not one act of forgetting but hundreds — hundreds of small nightly surrenders, each one wearing down the resolve that wrote BROKEN until nothing was left but a woman who couldn't say why she flinched at candles?

I sat on the floor of my own bedroom, in the dust of my own concealment, holding the wreckage of my own courage, and I wept.

Not dramatically. I'm not a dramatic weeper; I cry the way rain starts, a few drops, then a steadiness. But I wept, and what I wept for surprised me. Not for Ash, though God knows Ash deserved tears. Not for fear of the court, or the Magistry, or the gate humming in the dark below. I wept for her — for the woman who had written that note. She had been brave. She had been furious. She had held a burning contract in her hands and written defiance across it in her own blood-tallow and sworn she'd finish the job, and then somewhere between that night and this one she had been sanded down into me: cautious, gray, invisible, a keeper of harmless charms who apologized to customers for the weather.

She had a plan. She had fire in her. And I was what was left after the fire went out, and I missed her the way you miss someone whose funeral you weren't permitted to attend.

That's the grief they don't warn you about, losing yourself. You can't mourn properly, because mourning requires memory, and memory is exactly what's been taken. I grieved a woman I couldn't picture. I knew she existed the way you know a room exists behind a locked door — by the shape of the lock. Every flinch, every avoidance, every smooth-worn gap in my past was the impression her absence left, like a footprint in snow filled back in. I knew her by her contours. I loved her by inference.

And inference, I was learning, is how the Undercourt likes its victims. It doesn't need to cage you. It just needs you unable to remember what you were fighting for.

* * *

I put the papers back. Both of them. Under the board, in the oilcloth, in the dark, exactly as found — because I am enough of a professional to know you don't disturb a scene until you understand it, and because some superstitious animal in me believed, still believes, that the half-burned contract was waiting. That it had waited five years under the floor for the right version of me to kneel down and find it. That the note was addressed not to the woman I had been but to the woman I was becoming, the one sitting cross-legged in the dust with ash on her fingers and a plan forming behind her eyes like weather.

Because here's what I had, now. Evidence. A timeline. A shape.

Five years ago: I signed a contract, trading a soul for a life. Whose soul — I knew whose, now, or strongly suspected; the child in my guest room had arrived speaking the language of the party to the contract, and the gate had hummed her name in its sleep. Whose life — that I didn't know. That was the hole at the center of the hole, the stone inside the stone. Someone had been dying, five years ago, and I had bought their survival with a child's soul, and then I had regretted it — immediately, furiously, the same night, judging by the state of the burned copy — and then I had been made to forget the regret along with everything else.

The court hadn't just taken my memory of the bargain. It had taken my memory of *rebelling* against the bargain. Because a rebel who forgets she rebelled is better than a compliant victim. A compliant victim might recover. A forgotten rebel just keeps shop.

Well. I had news for the court, and the news was this: the note survived. The fire survived, half of it, folded in oilcloth like an ember banked against the long winter. Somewhere under that smooth surface of not-looking, the woman who wrote BROKEN was still in there, banked and breathing, and tonight — tonight I had finally gone looking for her, and found her ashes still warm.

I climbed out of the floor, replaced the boards, and sat on the edge of my bed with the lantern turned low. Below me, muffled by joists and silence, the gate hummed its single patient note. Above me, Ash slept, twitching through dreams in a tongue older than grief.

Two floors, two prisoners, one house. And me in the middle, holding a half-burned promise and a question I couldn't yet ask:

*Strong enough for what, exactly? What comes next, after the breaking?*

I didn't know. But I knew someone who might — the widow Sera, who had looked at me across her fence last week with an expression I'd filed, at the time, under "polite neighborly concern," and which I was beginning to suspect belonged in an entirely different filing cabinet.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would knock on her door. Tomorrow I would start asking questions out loud, in daylight, where the Magistry's watchers could hear me — because a woman who hides her questions gets to keep them forever, and I was done keeping things.

The lantern guttered. Downstairs, the gate hummed on, patient as ever.

Patient things, I reminded myself, have already got what they wanted. Which meant it was time to take something back.
