## 8. The Gate Wakes

The humming starts in my teeth.

That is where I keep things now — in my teeth, behind them, where the words I cannot say have learned to live. The humming joins them there, low and round, like a stone being rolled over and over in a very patient mouth. I lie in the narrow bed under the eaves and press my tongue against the back of my teeth and feel the sound answer.

*You know me,* it says, or something like it. The language beneath kingdoms does not bother with *says*. It simply is, the way cold is, the way dark under a door is. *You know this song.*

I do not want to know it. That is the terrible truth of the last few nights: some part of me knows it the way a body knows falling, knows it before the mind has agreed to anything at all.

The shop above the cellar has become a country I am learning to read. Juniper's country. There are laws here, though she pretends they are only habits: charms must be small, magic must be called something else, the floorboard by the stove must never be pried up, and the stairs to the cellar must be gone down quickly, with a lantern held high and eyes on the jars of pickled plums as if plums were the reason anyone keeps a cellar at all.

But I have been a child in houses with secrets before. Children always find the secret. It is the one inheritance we are all given.

* * *

I wait until her breathing goes slow above me — through the floorboards I can hear it, the particular long tide of a person who has finally stopped listening for trouble. Juniper listens for trouble the way other people breathe; when she sleeps, the whole house seems to exhale with relief.

Then I get up.

The stairs are the hard part. Old wood announces you like a town crier. I have mapped them in the dark across many nights — third step groans, fifth step lies about groaning but does anyway, seventh is safe if you walk on its left edge like a cat on a wall. I go down the way water goes down, which is to say without permission and without noise.

The cellar is not dark. That is the first wrong thing.

Moonlight comes through the high slit of a window and lies on the flagstones in a pale stripe, and beyond the stripe everything should be black, but it isn't. It is the color of a room remembered. Shapes stand out the way shapes do in dreams: the plum jars, the sacks of dried beans, the coiled rope that has never once been rope-shaped in any dream I've had here.

And at the far end, where the wall should be —

the gate.

Juniper calls it old stone. She says it the way she says *harmless charm* and *just weather*, two more of her country's little laws. But old stone does not hum. Old stone does not sit in a wall like a coin pressed into bread, rounder than its surroundings, older than its surroundings, waiting.

Tonight it is not humming anymore.

Tonight it is *breathing.*

I can see it now that I am close — the faint swell and settle of the stone's surface, slow as a sleeping flank. Frost ferns crawl out from its edges across the ordinary bricks, silvering them, spelling something in a script made of cold. I know the script. I hate that I know the script. It is the writing from the hall of candles, the mountain-hall that lives behind my eyes where my voice used to be. The letters say nothing so much as they say *here*, over and over, *here, here, we kept your place.*

My feet carry me forward. I would like to report that I was brave, or that I was dragged, but it was neither. It was the feeling of hearing your own name called from another room in a house you thought you lived in alone. You go. You cannot help going. The body decides these things before the heart can file an objection.

Up close, the gate is warm.

That is the second wrong thing. Stone in a cellar in autumn should be cold as a liar's hand. This is warm the way a cheek is warm, the way the space just left by someone sleeping is warm, and my palm rises toward it with the whole stupid confidence of a child reaching for exactly the thing every grown person in the story said not to touch.

I touch it.

* * *

The crack opens the way an eye opens.

Not wide. A seam, a hairline, a thread of gold light drawn down the middle of the stone as if someone on the other side had taken a knife to a loaf. Warm air breathes out through it, and the warm air smells of candle smoke and crushed herbs and — underneath, faint as a memory of a memory — the mountain. Wet granite and deep halls and the particular hush of a place where everyone whispers because shouting would wake what shouldn't be woken.

Through the seam I see light. Not firelight. Candlelight, hundreds of candles, their flames standing perfectly still, none of them guttering, none of them alive in the way flames are supposed to be alive. They burn like painted flames. They burn like flames in a book.

And between the candles, shadows move. Slowly. Courteously. The way people move in a sickroom.

Something on the other side notices me.

I feel it happen — a turning, vast and unhurried, the attention of the whole still room swinging toward the crack like a lighthouse beam. And then the whisper comes, and it does not come through the crack so much as it comes up through the stone, through my hand, through the bones of my arm, settling into my chest where my sold-away voice used to live.

It whispers my true name.

Not *Ash.* Juniper named me that, kindly, the first night, holding a cup of barley tea against my lips like an offering to a small frightened god. *Ash,* she said, *for now, until you can tell me better.* And I had liked it. Ash is what remains after burning; ash is soft; ash asks nothing.

But the name in the whisper is the other one. The long one. The one that was spoken over me in the candle-hall by hands that signed while mouths recited, the name that means *the one who was weighed and found sufficient payment.* When it pours into me I do not hear it so much as remember it, all at once, the way you remember a fall after you've landed.

And with the name comes the rest, in fragments, each one a splinter:

— a table of black wood, and ink that shone like beetle shells —

— my mother's hands, flat on the table, pressing down as if the paper might blow away, though there was no wind underground —

— a voice saying *the child's portion, then, since yours is spent* — and my mother not arguing, my mother *thanking* them, that is the splinter that goes deepest, she thanked them —

— candles bending toward me like grass in wind, all together, all at once, bowing —

I make a sound. I have no voice and I make a sound anyway; it comes out of me as a shape, a pressure, the ghost of a scream, and the gate drinks it in gladly. Of course it does. Sound is currency down there. They bought mine already. Why shouldn't they collect the interest?

The seam widens. A finger's width. Two.

Cold gold light spills across the flagstones and lies there like spilled honey, and in the honey-light I can see more of the hall now: the jars. Rows of them, shelves and shelves, each jar holding a soft amber glow that pulses gently, gently, like a heart that has nowhere to hurry to. Souls, some part of me understands. Kept fresh. Kept *safe.* The court is very proud of how safely it keeps things.

One of the jars, on the lowest shelf, nearest the door, glows a different color. Not amber. Green-gold, like light through spring leaves, like —

like the charm bracelet Juniper tied around my wrist three nights ago, when the voices in my sleep got too warm, too welcoming, and she sat up all night re-knotting the knots and muttering *not this one, not this child, find another.*

The green-gold jar pulses faster when I look at it.

*Come home,* whispers the true name. *We kept your place. We always keep the places. That is the whole of our religion, little vessel: nothing given is ever lost, nothing sold is ever spent, everything is kept, kept, kept —*

And I understand, suddenly, with a clarity that feels like ice water, that they are not lying. That is the worst part. The Undercourt does not lie. It keeps. It will keep me forever, polished and glowing and perfectly preserved, and it will call that love.

I pull my hand back.

The stone does not want to let go. My fingertips drag on the warmth, and for one horrible moment I think the gate means to take the hand as a deposit, a token, a down payment on the rest of me — and then somewhere above us a floorboard creaks, the third step, the honest one, and the gate flinches.

Gates should not flinch. The fact that this one does tells me something no ledger could: whatever waits in the candle-hall, it is afraid of Juniper Halloran. Or of what Juniper used to be. The two may not be the same thing, and I intend, someday, with words, to ask.

Footsteps now, fast and bare-footed on the stairs, and a voice cracked wide open with sleep and terror:

"*Ash — away from it — AWAY —*"

* * *

Here is what happens next, and I set it down carefully because afterward no one will agree on it, least of all Juniper:

She crosses the cellar like weather. She does not run so much as arrive, all at once, between me and the gate, and the lantern she has snatched up is not lit but burns anyway, a small furious flame with no oil to feed on, and I think: *ah. There you are. The person under the shopkeeper.*

Her free hand slams against the stone beside the seam — not on the gate itself, she is careful even in terror, careful the way soldiers are careful — and she speaks.

Not common tongue. Not the merchant cant she uses with customers, not the soft nonsense she uses with me. She speaks the language beneath kingdoms, fluently, furiously, the words coming out of her like sparks off a struck flint, and I understand every one:

"*Closed. Closed and counted. What was paid stays paid; what was sealed stays sealed. By the oath and the burner of the oath — shut.*"

The last word she does not speak so much as press, palm flat against the stone, and the gate obeys.

The seam closes. The gold light snuffs like a pinched wick. The frost ferns stop growing, hang for a moment, and slide off the bricks in a fine silver rain that hisses when it hits the flagstones. The humming stops — no, worse, it doesn't stop, it *finishes*, resolves at last onto its final note, the note it has been reaching for all these weeks, and the note sounds suspiciously like satisfaction.

Then the cellar is just a cellar. Plum jars. Bean sacks. Rope. An old stone gate that is only old stone, breathing nothing, keeping nothing, warm as any other rock.

Juniper stands with her hand still flat against it, shoulders heaving, and in the lantern's unlit flame I watch five years fall off her face and land somewhere on the floor, and what is underneath the five years is young and wrecked and familiar in a way that makes my chest hurt.

Because I have seen that face before. In the candle-hall. On the other side of a table of black wood.

No. That can't be right. That is a splinter that does not fit anywhere, and I put it away in my teeth with the other words I cannot say.

She turns to me. Her mouth opens — I brace myself for anger, for *what did I tell you, for never again* — but what comes out, in rough common tongue, shaking, is:

"Did it say your name? Ash. Look at me. Did it *say* it?"

I nod. Then, because I owe her the truth and have no other coin to pay in, I lift my hand and show her my fingertips, which are gilded — faintly, beautifully, horribly — as if I had dipped them in candlelight and the light had decided to stay.

Juniper makes a sound I have never heard a grown person make. She kneels, takes my hand in both of hers, and holds it the way you hold a bird you are afraid of crushing, and she says, mostly to herself, in the tone of a woman reading her own sentence:

"It touched you. It *touched* you, which means the contract knows you're awake, which means—" She stops. Swallows. Starts over, quieter, the way people talk when they've decided the truth is a load that must be carried in smaller bundles. "Which means we're out of time. All of it. Every day I thought we had."

*We*, she says. I hold on to that word like the railing on the seventh stair.

She looks at the gate a long moment. The gate looks back, insofar as stone can, with the patience of a thing that has already got what it wanted — except that now, for the first time, the patience has a hairline crack in it too, and I saw it flinch, and I don't think either of us is fooled about who won tonight.

"Sleep upstairs," Juniper says at last, hoarse. "In my bed. Take the lantern." The unlit flame gutters obediently, as if it had been waiting to be useful. "I'll stay down here."

I do not want to leave her. But there is a new law in her country, I understand, written tonight in frost and gold: *the keeper watches the gate.* So I climb the stairs — third step groaning, fifth lying, seventh safe — and at the top I look back.

She has not moved. She stands with her palm still against the closed stone, head bowed, and she is speaking again in the dead tongue, very quietly, over and over, and it takes me three repetitions to recognize the shape of it, because it is not a spell at all.

It is a counting. She is counting the years. *Five. Five. Five.* Like a woman checking whether the arithmetic of her exile still comes out, like a prisoner marking a wall.

Below her, faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, the gate hums its finished note once more — and begins, softly, patiently, to hum the next one.

Tomorrow, I think, pulling her blanket up to my chin in her narrow bed, tomorrow she knocks on the widow's door, or I will knock myself, barefoot, mute, gilded at the fingertips, if that's what it takes. Because the past has stopped knocking and started opening doors, and there is only one thing in any world to do about a door like that.

You find out who built it. And then you build a bigger one.
