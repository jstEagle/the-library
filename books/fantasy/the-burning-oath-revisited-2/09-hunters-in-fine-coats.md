## 9. Hunters in Fine Coats

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong on two counts.

First, nobody knocked on my door at half past nine. The charm trade in Vell kept the hours of the guilty: dawn, when the fishwives wanted fair-weather knots before the boats went out, and dusk, when the respectable came slinking in with their collars up and their troubles wrapped in brown paper. Half past nine was the hour of magistrates and debt collectors, both of whom preferred to catch you mid-breakfast, still soft.

Second — and this was the count that mattered — it was a *good* knock. Three raps, evenly spaced, firm but polite. My usual customers knocked like they were apologizing for the existence of the door. This was a knock that had been trained.

I looked at Ash, who was sitting cross-legged on the counter sorting dried lavender by some private system of their own devising, and I put a finger to my lips. Ash, who had never once in three weeks made a sound louder than breathing, nodded with the gravity of a general accepting battle plans. There are times I think the child understands more than any of us, and most of those times are the times I'd rather they didn't.

I opened the door four inches, chain on.

Two people stood in the grey morning drizzle wearing coats that cost more than my yearly rent. Not Magistry grey — that would have been honest, at least. These were fine wool, deep green, cut in the current Vell fashion, the kind of clothes that say *I am not here officially* while every stitch screams otherwise. The man was fiftyish, silver at his temples, with the pleasant, forgettable face of someone paid to be forgotten. The woman was younger, sharp-eyed, holding an umbrella she hadn't opened, as if rain were beneath her notice.

"Master Halloran?" the man said. "Forgive the hour. We're conducting a survey."

"A survey," I said.

"Of licensed practitioners." He produced a document folder, tooled leather, Magistry seal embossed in silver — not wax, silver, because wax could be forged and silver could be *checked*. "Routine. May we come in?"

Everything in me said no. Everything in me also knew that saying no to a survey was how you got a second visit, and second visits came with warrants instead of pleasantries.

"The shop's small," I said, unhooking the chain. "Mind the lavender."

* * *

They moved through my shop the way water moves through a crack in a wall — politely, thoroughly, finding every low place. The man introduced himself as Surveyor Corren; the woman did not introduce herself at all, which told me her name was worth more than mine. He admired my shelves. He complimented my knotwork. He picked up a luck-charm — a harmless thing, thread and riverstone and a word that meant nothing anymore — and turned it over with fingers that were far too careful.

"You hold a license," he said. "Class Four. Charms of comfort and custom. No workings of intent, no bindings, no speech-craft."

"That's right."

"And business is...?"

"Modest." I let a little tiredness into my voice, which wasn't hard. "Vell doesn't love magic, Surveyor. I sell mostly to grandmothers."

"Grandmothers," he agreed pleasantly, "are the backbone of this city." He set the charm down exactly where he'd found it, to the finger-width. That frightened me more than anything else so far. Men who remember where things sit have been trained to notice when things move.

Ash sat very still by the window, lavender forgotten, watching the woman. And the woman — this is what I want written down somewhere, if anyone ever asks — the woman watched Ash back. Not the glance a stranger gives a child. A long, level look, the kind you give a lock you're deciding whether to pick.

"How long have you been in Vell, Master Halloran?"

"Six years."

"And before that?"

"Before that I traveled." I smiled the smile I keep for customs men. "Charm-work pays poorly and wanders widely."

"Mm." He consulted his folder without appearing to read it. "We've had reports, you understand. Nothing about you specifically. Reports of unusual phenomena in this quarter. Voices where there should be none. Lights. A child seen entering premises after curfew." He looked up, mild as milk. "You wouldn't know anything of that."

"I know my neighbors talk," I said. "It's the main industry of this street."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm, and somehow that was worse. "It usually is." Then, folding the folder closed: "One more formality. The survey requires verification of household members. Anyone resident above or below the premises?"

And there it was. The cellar. The gate. Five years of not looking at it, all balanced on the next sentence out of my mouth.

"Just me," I said. "And the child stays with me sometimes. An arrangement with—" I gestured vaguely upward, toward the street, toward a fiction. "Family circumstances. It's all very dull."

"Dull is my favorite circumstance," said Corren. "Then we'll only need the cellar for the record. Standard practice. A look at the foundations, the ward-lines, nothing invasive."

My heart performed a maneuver I'm fairly sure has a medical name.

"Now?" I said, and heard the wrongness in my own voice, one beat too fast, and hated myself for it, because Corren heard it too. His pleasant face didn't change, but something behind it leaned forward, the way a dog leans when it scents a rabbit under snow.

"Is there a difficulty with the cellar, Master Halloran?"

"No difficulty. It's just—"

That was when Sera arrived.

She came through the front door without knocking, which Sera never does, carrying a basket of bread rolls and indignation, already talking. "Juniper, honestly, the baker's boy left the loaves in the rain again, I swear that family would lose their own heads if — oh." She stopped. She took in the fine coats, the silver seal, the umbrella held against nothing. Her face did something remarkable: it became *old*, and worried, and slightly deaf, all at once. "Oh, officials. Is this about the drains? Because I've told the council a dozen times, the drains on this street run backward, and if they're finally doing something—"

"Madam," began Corren, "we're conducting a survey of—"

"Because if it's the drains, you'll want the cellar," Sera said briskly, setting down her basket. "Everyone wants the cellar for the drains. Juniper, love, show them the cellar, and tell them about the water. Black water, comes up through the stones, smells like a tannery in August. I've smelled it from my own kitchen. Terrible for the foundations. Terrible." She said the word twice, and the second time she looked directly at me, and her eyes were not old or worried or deaf at all. They said: *I know what's down there. I'm buying you time. Spend it.*

I spent it.

"The cellar's flooded half the year," I said, catching the thread she threw. "Standing water over the flagstones. You'd ruin those boots."

Corren glanced down at his boots. They were, I noted with a distant, hysterical part of my mind, extremely good boots.

"We can manage," he said.

"It's not the boots," said the woman, speaking for the first time. Her voice was quiet and precise, a scalpel in a drawer. "It's the record. A flooded cellar can't be surveyed properly. We'd note it as *inaccessible due to structural water damage*, schedule a follow-up." She looked at me. "Unless Master Halloran would prefer we note it as *refused access*, which triggers a different procedure entirely. Warrants. Seals. Men with less pleasant manners than ours."

Sera gasped — a masterpiece of theatrical offense. "Structural! Are you suggesting Juniper's building is unsafe? I've known this shop twenty years. The Hallorans built half this quarter with their own hands, and if the council is going around calling buildings unsafe—"

"Nobody is calling anything unsafe, madam."

"Then write *flooded*," Sera said, "and be done with it, and go tell the drain-men to do their jobs."

There was a silence. Rain ticked against the window. Ash, by the wall, had gone so still they might have been another jar of dried flowers.

The woman wrote something. I watched the pen move and could not read it upside down, which I suspect was deliberate. Then Corren closed the folder, and the warmth came back into his face like sun through cloud.

"Flooded," he agreed. "How fortunate. We'll schedule the follow-up for..." He paused, delicately. "Shall we say, within the month? Sooner, if the weather improves."

"Sooner is fine," I said, because refusing to agree would have been its own entry in whatever ledger he carried behind his pleasant face.

At the door, the woman stopped. She didn't turn all the way — just enough to speak over her shoulder, to me, quietly, beneath Sera's ongoing monologue about drains.

"The child," she said. "Where did you get it?"

"*Them*," I said. "A relative. Why do you ask?"

"Because," she said, "children who don't speak don't interest the Magistry. But children who hum in dead languages while they sort herbs — those interest us very much." And then she stepped out into the rain without opening her umbrella, and the two of them walked away down the wet street like a pair of well-dressed crows, unhurried, certain of the road home.

* * *

I locked the door. I put the bar across it, which I haven't done since I moved in, because bars say *afraid* and afraid is a smell the whole street can catch.

Sera stood in the middle of my shop with her basket of ruined bread rolls, looking ten years older than she had five minutes ago.

"Well," she said. "That was closer than I'd like."

"How did you know?" I asked. "To come. To — all of it. The drains."

"Saw them walking up the street from my window. Green coats, no umbrellas, checking house numbers." She set the basket down on a chair with great care, as if it were an offering. "I've seen that walk before, Juniper. Twenty-two years ago, they came to my mother's house asking about my sister, and they wore the same coats, and they checked the house numbers the same way. Like they were memorizing which doors to come back to." Her hands had gone white on the basket handle. "So I put my shawl on and I got here fast."

"Why?" I asked. "Why help me? You barely know me."

She looked at me for a long moment, and what was in her face wasn't kindness exactly. It was something harder and older — the look of a woman standing at a grave she dug herself, deciding whether to dig another.

"Because last month I watched you fail to cure a child's nightmares," she said, "with charms that shouldn't answer back. And because I know what lives under mountains, and I know what it takes from us, and I have spent twenty-two years being the only person on this street who remembers it." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on *remembers*, and I thought: *she knows about the gaps. She knows what it looks like when someone comes back missing pieces.* "I won't watch it happen to anyone else. Not even a fool like you."

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"Don't thank me yet." She straightened, and the hardness settled into something usable. "Listen to me, Juniper Halloran. That surveyor will file his paper tonight. *Flooded cellar, follow-up within the month.* But the woman — I saw her face. She's not surveying. She's hunting, and surveys are just how hunters knock politely. You have weeks. Maybe less, once she starts pulling records." She jabbed a finger at me. "Records. Do you understand? Six years in Vell, a Class Four license, no history before it. They will pull your history, and they will find a hole in it, and holes are the one thing the Magistry cannot leave alone."

I thought of the ledger in the archives. Of my own handwriting beside a sold soul. Of the scar on my palm shaped like a seal, and the half-burned oath hidden under my floorboards, and the five years of my life that were a locked room with me on the outside.

"They'll find the hole," I said quietly.

"Of course they will. Holes are easy to find. It's what's *in* them that's hard." Sera picked up her basket again, businesslike, armor going back on. "Which means you have a choice, and you have it now, not in a month. Run, and spend your life running with a child who belongs to something worse than the Magistry. Or go *there* —" and she tilted her head downward, toward the cellar, toward the mountain beyond the city walls, toward everything I'd spent five years not looking at — "and settle it at the source."

"There's a road," I said. It wasn't quite a question.

"There's a road," she confirmed. "An old one. Pilgrims used it, before the mountain got a reputation. And there's a price for using it, and the price isn't coin, and when you're ready to hear what it is, you come to my kitchen and we'll talk like grown women over tea that's gone cold." She moved to the door, then paused, hand on the latch. "And Juniper? Burn nothing, hide nothing, change nothing in this shop between now and when you leave. If they come back early, let them find exactly what they found today. Hunters remember rooms, love. Give them a room worth forgetting."

Then she was gone, into the drizzle, and I was alone with Ash and the lavender and the sound of my own heartbeat.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat on the cellar stairs with a candle, listening.

The gate didn't hum. It had hummed for Ash, that first storm night, and it had stayed sullenly silent since, as if it too understood the value of playing dumb for inspectors. But I sat there anyway, on the cold stone, watching the dark under the door, and I talked to it. Low. Quiet. The way you talk to a dog you're not sure is friendly.

"I know what I did," I said. Or started to say — because I don't, not really, not yet. "I know *of* what I did. I signed something. I gave something away. And whatever's behind you has a piece of me, and a piece of this child, and I'm coming to take both back."

Silence. Stone silence.

But underneath the silence — fainter than breath, easier to believe I imagined it — something shifted. Not a hum. A settling. The sound a large animal makes when it hears its name spoken and decides, for now, to keep lying down.

I climbed back upstairs and found Ash awake, sitting up in the nest of blankets by the window, watching me with those old, old eyes.

"We're leaving," I told them. "Soon. There's a place we have to go, and it's not a good place, and I can't promise it will be safe. But I promise you this—" I held up my hand, the one with the seal-shaped scar, and turned it in the candlelight so they could see it plain. "Whatever I gave away, whoever I was when I gave it — I'm going to get you back. All of you. Every piece."

Ash looked at my scarred hand for a long moment. Then they reached out and touched it, one small finger tracing the seal's edge, and their mouth moved — no sound, not yet, never sound — but I've learned to read the shape of their lips the way sailors read weather, and I knew what they were shaping.

Not a word. A name. *Their own.*

Yes, I told them silently. That one. We're going to go get that one back.

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as paperwork. Somewhere across the city, a surveyor was filing his report, and a woman with an umbrella she never opened was pulling records, and the clock on my stolen time had begun, at last, audibly, to tick.

Three weeks, maybe. Perhaps less.

I started packing at dawn.
