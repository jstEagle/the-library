## 10. Bargaining with the Widow

*Juniper*

The widow Sera knocked on my door at an hour when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were pretending to be. I knew it was her before I opened it, because Ash had gone still in the corner by the window—the particular stillness of a child listening to something the rest of us can't hear—and then said, in that old tongue that made my teeth ache, a word I was almost certain meant *her*.

Almost certain. My fluency in the language beneath kingdoms extended to perhaps forty words, and half of those were probably swear words, because that's the sort of luck I have.

I opened the door. Sera stood on the step with a lantern in one hand and a bottle of plum brandy in the other, wearing the expression of a woman who had decided to be patient about something and was finding the decision costly.

"May I come in, charm-seller?" she said. "Or shall we conduct our business on the doorstep where the Magistry's night men can enjoy it too?"

"Sera. It's past midnight."

"Yes," she said, and stepped past me into the shop, because of course she did. Widows of a certain vintage do not wait to be invited twice. She set the brandy on my counter, next to the tray of luck-knots I hadn't yet hidden, and looked around the shop with the unhurried attention of someone cataloguing everything she intended to hold against me later.

"You keep the fever-charms under the counter," she observed. "The ones that actually work. The ones on the shelf are decorative."

"They're *subtle*."

"They're *string*, Juniper. I've been buying your string for three years and pretending it helped my knees because you looked like a man who needed the business more than the truth."

I didn't have an answer to that. Ash came out from the corner, barefoot and solemn, and looked up at Sera the way children look at large weather. Sera looked back down at Ash the way weather looks at children.

"This is the one," Sera said. Not a question.

"You said on Sunday you had something to tell me. About my—about the cellar."

"I said I had something to tell you about the mountain." She pulled out a stool, sat, and patted the counter beside the brandy. "Pour two cups. The child can have milk if you have it, though I suspect the child would rather have the brandy, and I suspect the child would be fine."

Ash said something long and liquid and old. Sera's face did something complicated.

"You understood that," I said.

"I understood enough." She took the cup I poured. "My sister understood it better. Sit down, Juniper. This is a long story, and I've brought brandy specifically so you can't escape in the middle of it."

I sat. Outside, the storm that had brought Ash to my door five nights ago had come back around, the way storms do in Vell in the wet months, circling the city like a dog that hasn't decided where to lie down. The lamps guttered. Somewhere below us—I felt it rather than heard it, the way you feel a cart passing two streets over—the gate hummed.

Sera heard it too. Her eyes went to the floor.

"There it is," she said softly. "Five years I've wondered if I imagined it. Every time I bought your string."

"You've heard the gate before?"

"Juniper." She set down her cup with great care, the way you set down something when you're angry and don't want the cup to pay for it. "I'm going to tell you about my sister now, and you're going to listen, and you're not going to interrupt with questions until I'm done, because if I stop I may not start again. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

* * *

*Sera*

He sat there with his cup in both hands like a man holding onto a railing, and I thought: *this is the one who signed it.* I didn't know it yet, not the way I know it now, but I knew it the way you know rain is coming—in the ache in old bones, in the smell of the air.

My sister's name was Wren. I say *was*, though I don't know if she's dead, and that's the cruelty of it. You can mourn someone properly when they're dead. What do you do with a person who was simply *taken*, and the taking was legal?

Wren was four years younger than me and twice as brave, which is the arithmetic of little sisters. When the sickness came through the Salt Quarter—the winter fever, the one they still won't name in the almanacs—she was the one who walked door to door with willow bark and boiled water while the rest of us hid. She was nineteen. She caught it in the house of a dyer on Tanner's Row, and by the time they carried her home she was burning like a lamp with no oil left.

I did what anyone would do. I did what you did, though I didn't know it yet. I went looking for someone who could break the law quietly.

There was a woman then—this was twenty-two years ago, before the Magistry's purges, when the old practitioners still worked in the open in the Salt Quarter—a hedge-witch named Odile who lived above the rope-walk. Odile told me what the old practitioners all knew and none of them said aloud: that some sicknesses aren't sicknesses. That the fever was the Undercourt's collection method. That the mountain under the world has an appetite, and every few generations it sends the fever up like a fishing line.

She said there was a way to buy a soul back. She said it was expensive.

I was twenty-six and I loved my sister more than I loved my own skin, so I said, *name the price.*

She said: *not coin. Memory. The court under the mountain deals in what we are made of. At each threshold on the pilgrim road, you give up a memory, freely given, and the closer you come to the heart of the court, the larger the memory must be. By the time you reach the Buried King, you will have paid so much of yourself that you must hope what remains is stubborn enough to bargain.*

I paid the first toll at the tollhouse at the snowline. A small one. The smell of my mother's bread. I didn't even miss it for years—I just knew that other people spoke of their mothers' kitchens with a warmth I couldn't find in myself, and I thought I was simply cold by nature.

The second toll was larger. I gave up my wedding day. I had been married at twenty to a cooper named Anselm, dead eight years by then, and I gave up his face and the sound of his laugh and the way he said my name when he was teasing me, and I walked the next mile of that road weeping without knowing what I wept for.

I want you to understand something, charm-seller. The court doesn't take memories the way a thief takes a purse. It takes them the way a surgeon takes a limb. You feel the absence. You feel it every day. You just can't see what's missing, because the shape of the hole is the shape of the thing itself, and the thing itself is gone.

I reached the third threshold. The gate at the mountain's knee, the one they call the Candle Door, because the dead who wait outside it hold candles for the souls inside. And there I stopped. Because the toll at the Candle Door was Wren herself—my memory of her, all of her, everything—and I understood, standing there in the snow with the dead holding their little flames, that if I paid it I would walk into the court a stranger who didn't know what she'd come for. I would be a body with a grievance and no name for it. The court is patient. It knows that most people who climb that far will pay anything to be finished, even the reason they came.

I turned around. I climbed down. I lived in Vell for twenty-two years knowing my sister's soul was in a jar under the mountain and that I had chosen my memories of her over her actual self, and I have never once decided whether that was wisdom or cowardice, and I have stopped trying to decide.

And then, five nights ago, in a storm, a child came to your door speaking the tongue of the court, and your cellar started to sing, and I watched you look at that child with an expression I recognized from my own mirror.

So here is what I know, Juniper Halloran, and you may interrupt now, because I'm done.

The road to the mountain is the old pilgrim road, west out of the Salt Gate, the one the maps call the Shepherd's Way because the shepherds still use the low stretches. Three thresholds. Three memories, freely given, each larger than the last. And the court will know what you want before you say it, because the court always knows, and it will offer you a version of the bargain that costs you everything, and it will make the offer sound like mercy.

* * *

*Juniper*

I interrupted, as invited. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going anyway."

"I haven't said—"

"You've been saying it since the night the child arrived. You've been saying it with your face every time the gate hums. I've sold string to you for three years; I know how to read a customer." She refilled her own cup with a hand that did not quite tremble, and I understood that the steadiness cost her. "I'm telling you this because you cannot walk that road thinking it is a road. It is a *price list*. And you cannot walk it alone, because the dead at the snowline will call to you in your own voice, and you will want to answer, and you will need someone with a rope."

"The dead don't call to me," I said. "They call to Ash."

"They'll call to you too. They call to everyone who's sold something and doesn't know what. That hole in you—" she pointed the brandy cup at my chest, rudely and accurately, "—I can see it from across a room. I've been seeing it for three years and telling myself it was grief for a dead wife or a lost war or something respectable. It's not respectable, is it?"

"No," I said. "I don't think it is."

I told her then. Not everything—I don't have everything to tell—but the shape of it. The gap in my past, five years of it, smooth as river stone. The scar on my palm shaped like a seal. The half-burned paper I'd pried out of the floorboards three nights ago and hadn't been able to make myself read past the first line. *I, Juniper Halloran, being of sound mind and desperate heart—*

And the ledger in the archives. Ash's name. My handwriting beside it.

Sera listened the way I imagine surgeons listen: without flinching, cataloguing.

"You signed it," she said, when I finished. "You sold the child's soul."

"I don't remember doing it."

"No," she said. "You paid that memory on the road, or you paid it here, or the court took it as a fee. It doesn't matter. The signature is yours." She was quiet a moment. "Do you know what the worst part is? You probably had a *good reason*. They always do. I did."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to *warn* you. The court doesn't make monsters, charm-seller. It makes bargainers. Monsters you can fight. A bargainer has to fight themselves, and the court knows exactly which version of you to put in front of the mirror."

Ash had fallen asleep against my side somewhere in the middle of all this, which children can do, the merciful ability to sleep through the architecture of their own catastrophes. Sera looked at the child a long time.

"Wren was nineteen," she said. "This one looks seven. Eight."

"Younger. I think."

"Then we go soon." She said *we* without emphasis, without ceremony, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "Don't look at me like that. I've been to the snowline once and turned back, and I have spent twenty-two years being a woman who turned back. I'd like to spend whatever's left being something else."

"Sera, the tolls—"

"I know the tolls. I know them better than you do." She stood, and gathered her lantern, and became abruptly the brisk widow of the Salt Quarter again, the one who bought string and commented on the weather. "First threshold is the tollhouse at the old shepherd's waystation, three days west. The toll there is small—something homely, something you overlook. Don't try to trick it. The court can smell a grudging gift, and it charges interest."

"And the second?"

"Second is the face of someone you loved." She said it flatly, already knowing, I think, that I'd pay it. "Third is the Candle Door. We'll discuss the third when we're on the road, because if I tell you now you'll do something stupid like decide not to go."

She paused at the door, lantern raised, and the storm light made her look twenty years younger and twenty years harder.

"Pack light. Bring the charms that actually work. Bring the child's bracelet, whatever you made for it—it matters more than you know. And Juniper." She looked back at me, and for a moment the widow fell away and it was just a woman with a hole in her chest the exact shape of a sister. "Whatever the court shows you in the mirror-hall—and it will show you something—remember that it shows it to everyone. You are not the first person it has tried to unmake with their own face. You will not be the last. Don't let it work."

Then she was gone into the rain, and I stood in my shop holding a sleeping child and a cup of plum brandy, and I thought about the paper in the floorboards, and the first line, *being of sound mind and desperate heart*, and I thought: *desperate, yes. Sound, we'll see.*

Above the counter, the luck-knots turned slowly in the draft from the closing door, string pretending to be magic.

In three days, I'd have to stop pretending too.

I set down the cup, lifted Ash, and went to pack.
