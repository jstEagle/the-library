## 11. Leaving Vell

Packing a life is an act of ruthless triage.

I learned this the hard way, standing in my shop at midnight with three empty satchels and the dawning realization that five years of careful, invisible living could fit into less space than Ash's small bundle of found treasures — a blue button, a crow feather, a river stone with a hole through it that they refused to explain and I had stopped asking about.

The charms were the first to go on the discard pile. Luck-knots, fever-wards, little braided promises of mild good fortune — all of it string and superstition, legally speaking, which was the entire point. I'd spent five years making sure everything I sold could be defended before a Magistry assessor as *craft*. Now I stood over the counter where I'd arranged them so carefully, all those harmless knots and knots and knots, and felt something between grief and contempt.

*You built a whole life out of pretending,* I told myself. *At least pretend to pack like someone with a spine.*

I kept: the good knife, the one Sera had pressed on me with instructions not to argue; a week of dried food, mostly the kind that doesn't complain about weather; my mother's iron thimble, which has no magic in it at all but which I have carried through every bad decision of my adult life like a saint's fingerbone; and the half-burned oath paper, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against my ribs, where it sat like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out.

Everything else went into the cellar.

Not the gate side of the cellar. The other side, the shelving side, where I stacked boxes of unsold stock and old ledgers and the accumulated sediment of Juniper Halloran's respectable nothing. I worked by lamplight because lighting candles near the gate still felt like an invitation, and I did not look at the gate, and the gate did not hum, and we maintained our five-year arrangement of mutual polite silence.

Ash sat on the stairs watching me, wrapped in a blanket, awake at an hour no child should be awake, saying nothing in the way only Ash could say it — which is to say, entirely, fluently, in that dead language of theirs that lived somewhere behind my understanding like a word on the tip of the world's tongue.

"You should be sleeping," I said.

They signed something. We'd developed a pidgin by then — their hands, my guesses, Sera's occasional corrections. This one took me a moment. *Sleep is where they call loudest.*

I put down the box I was holding.

"Then you sleep where I can hear you," I said, "and if anything calls, you wake me, and I will tell it exactly what I think of it in very rude common tongue."

That got the closest thing to a smile Ash owned. Small, crooked, gone in a second, but real. I've thought since about how much courage it takes to smile when you know something is waiting for you at the bottom of a mountain. Most adults can't manage it. Most adults would have simply lain down in the snow and let the dead have their warmth.

Ash came downstairs instead and helped me pack, which is to say they sat inside an empty crate and supervised.

* * *

Dawn in Vell comes up grey off the harbor, the color of dishwater and second chances. I'd chosen the hour deliberately. The fish-carts start rolling before first light, and a person with a satchel and a child can move through the Lower Terraces without being remarkable. Invisibility, as I've said, is a craft. You don't vanish. You become furniture.

Before I locked the door for what I suspected — though I didn't let myself think the word *last* — I did three things.

First, I left money under the counter for Tam Wicke, who delivers my flour and asks no questions, with a note telling him the shop was closed for family business in the country. *Family.* I wrote the word and stared at it. It wasn't a lie. That was the strange part. Five years ago I'd have written *personal matters* and meant nothing by it.

Second, I gave the key to Widow Prentiss across the lane, who has watched my door for five years with the patient suspicion of a woman who has buried two husbands and therefore trusts nothing that stays shut. She took the key, looked at Ash, looked at the satchels, and said, "Going far?"

"Upcountry."

"Mountain roads are bad this time of year."

"I know."

She turned the key over in her fingers. "My sister went up the mountain once," she said. "Came back different. Quieter." Then she handed me the key back. "Keep it. If you're coming back, you'll want it. If you're not, some other fool will need a shop."

I stood there holding a key to a future I hadn't let myself plan for, and Widow Prentiss went back inside, and that was the whole conversation. Vell people don't waste words. They just make sure each one lands where it hurts or helps.

Third — and I'm not proud of how long I stood there doing it — I looked at the shop. Really looked. The luck-knots turning in the draft. The counter worn pale where my elbows had lived. The little brass bell above the door that I'd never once let ring with actual magic in it, because ringing bells with magic in them gets you a fine or worse.

It was a small life. It was a safe life. It was a life I had built like a wall around a missing room in my own head.

"Come on," I told Ash. "We're done here."

* * *

The watchers found us within four streets, which I'll admit stung. I'd planned the route like a smuggler — down Tanner's Row while the tanneries vented their morning steam, across Coppersmith Bridge in the crowd of mill workers, doubling back through the shrine market where the incense smoke gives even honest faces a guilty blur. And there they were anyway: two men in good coats cut just slightly too well for their covers, one loitering by the fish-stalls with a newspaper he was reading upside down, one following at a distance with the unhurried gait of a man being paid by the hour.

Magistry. Not assessor-grade — assessors wear badges and enjoy themselves. These were the quiet kind, the ones who follow rather than arrest, because an arrest creates paperwork and paperwork creates questions, whereas a shadow creates leverage.

"They're behind us," I murmured to Ash, who didn't need telling. Children feel being followed the way dogs feel storms.

Ash glanced back, then made a quick motion — flat hand, skimming low, like a bird going under a fence.

"Yes," I agreed. "Under. Through. Don't run."

Running confirms. Walking is innocent. So we walked, through the shrine market, past the incense sellers and the woman who sells blessed thread she swears is only thread, officer, and out the far side onto the Pilgrim Stair, where the city starts remembering it was ever a village.

Here's the thing about being followed that nobody tells you: after the first hour of terror, it becomes almost companionable. You learn your shadows' rhythms. Upside-down-newspaper man relieved himself at a public privy on the Stair and had to jog to catch up, dignity in ruins. The hourly-rate man bought a meat pie and ate it walking, and I confess I resented him a little, because it smelled excellent and I hadn't eaten.

"They think we're going somewhere specific," I said to Ash, quietly, as we climbed. "Which means they'll report our direction, and someone clever will work out the destination, and then the mountain road will get interesting."

Ash signed: *Let them come.*

"That's very brave and also we are avoiding them."

Another sign, slower, more insistent. It took me three tries. *You said we stop running from things.*

I had said that. Chapter and verse, three nights ago, sitting on the cellar stairs with a burned oath paper in my hand, having what I'd generously call a resolution and what Ash probably correctly identified as a breakdown with structure.

"There's a difference," I said, "between not running from things and walking toward them with witnesses."

Ash considered this. Then they signed the sign we'd invented together, early on, the one that means *stubborn* but that they use, always, to mean *me*: fist against heart, twice.

Fine. Point taken. The child was braver than me and had been since page one.

* * *

We stopped at noon at the last tea-house before the city gates, partly for food, partly because Sera was already there, occupying a corner table with the air of a woman who had been waiting since dawn and intended to make sure everyone knew it.

"You're late," she said.

"I was being followed."

"I know. They're outside now. There are three." She pushed a cup of tea at me and a bowl of porridge at Ash. "Eat. Both of you. Mountain roads are no place for hunger, and hunger makes people bargain stupidly, and bargaining stupidly is how souls end up in jars."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't ask me again why I'm coming, because the answer hasn't changed since Tuesday and you asking again insults us both." She lowered her voice. "What I *will* tell you is that I spoke to Ferrin at the tollhouse records, and the mountain's thresholds have been restless. Candlelight seen from the snowline. That hasn't happened in thirty years. Whatever's waking up down there, it knows you're coming, Juniper Halloran, and it is *pleased*."

I looked at my tea. Somewhere outside, three men in too-good coats were noting down the fact that I'd met a widow in a tea-house, which would read in their reports as either sinister or domestic, and honestly both were accurate.

"Pleased," I repeated. "Wonderful. I've been invited to a party by a corpse king."

"You've been *summoned*," Sera corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that invitations can be declined."

Through the tea-house window I could see the mountain itself, far off, wearing its cloud like a hood. Five years I'd lived in Vell and trained my eyes never to rest on it longer than a glance. Now I looked at it properly, maybe for the first time since I'd arrived, and felt the scar on my palm — the seal-shaped one, the one I'd had explained to me by no one — itch like a healing wound remembering how it was made.

Ash touched my arm. Signed, slow and clear: *Go now?*

"Now," I agreed.

Sera settled her own pack — a proper pilgrim's pack, bedroll and rope and a hatchet, because of course Sera owned a hatchet — and led us out the back way, through the kitchen, past a scullery girl she paid with a coin and a wink, into the alley where the city's refuse carts began their long trudge to the cliffs.

"The watchers expect you at the east gate," she said. "So we go west, through the tannery flats, and join the pilgrim road at the shepherd's cairn. It adds a day. It loses the coats."

"And if it doesn't lose them?"

"Then they follow us up a haunted mountain," Sera said, "and the mountain does what mountains do." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "I've waited thirty years to introduce the Undercourt to some Magistry men. Consider it a gift."

* * *

The tannery flats smell like regret and lime, and no watcher worth their wage follows anyone there voluntarily, which is why Sera chose them. By late afternoon we stood at the shepherd's cairn, a heap of stones older than the kingdom, where the pilgrim road to the mountain unspooled northward through brown grass and early frost.

Behind us: Vell, smoking gently in the dusk, harbor lights coming up one by one, every window of my shop dark, the key in my pocket because Widow Prentiss had refused it.

Ahead of us: the road, and the tolls, and whatever version of myself was waiting at the bottom of a candlelit hall to explain why they'd done what they'd done.

In between: Ash, holding my hand, their grip fierce as a burr.

I took out the key — the shop key, the useless brass one — and weighed it in my palm. Then I did something I couldn't quite explain. I hung it around my neck on a cord, under my shirt, where it lay against my sternum like a second, smaller heart.

"For the way back," I said, to no one in particular.

Sera heard anyway. "Now that," she said, "is the first sensible magical object you've carried all week."

We walked. The city shrank. The mountain grew. And somewhere behind us, I knew, three men in fine coats were discovering an empty road and beginning, with professional reluctance, to follow.

The frost thickened. Ash began, very softly, to hum — a tune in the dead language, a walking song, the kind mothers sing on long roads. I didn't know the words. My bones did.

Halfway up the first rise, Ash stopped, tugged my sleeve, and pointed back at Vell one last time. Then they signed something new, something I'd never seen, hands moving like they were building a house out of air.

"What's that one?" I asked.

Ash pointed at me. At Sera. At themselves. At the little knot of us standing on the road in the cold.

*Home,* they said, without a sound, in a language older than any kingdom. *We are home.*

And I found that I was not afraid anymore — or rather, that the fear was still there, riding along in my chest where fear lives, but it had changed jobs. It wasn't guarding the door anymore. It was carrying the lantern.

We walked north, into the dark, toward the mountain that remembered everything I'd paid to forget.

And far below the snowline, in a hall of ten thousand candles, I would have sworn — though I had no proof, then — that something ancient opened one amber eye, and smiled, and turned up the light.
