## 12. The Road That Remembers

The pilgrim road out of Vell was older than the city, older than the kingdom that had outlawed the very thing it once led people toward. You could tell by the stones. Kingdom roads were laid in straight lines because kingdoms liked to pretend the world obeyed them. This road wandered. It curved around hills that could easily have been cut through, dipped into valleys that served no purpose but shade, and at one point made a long, patient detour around a single enormous oak that had probably been old when the first pilgrim knelt on this path and asked the mountain for a miracle.

Roads like that don't get built. They get *remembered*.

I felt it by midmorning of the second day—a low hum in my back teeth, like the cellar gate's whisper stretched thin across forty miles of flagstone. Ash walked ahead of me with the tireless, erratic energy of a child who has never once considered that walking might be boring, stopping to examine beetles, to taste a wild berry (I caught their wrist just in time; some things on this road are not food), to press their palm flat against milestone after milestone as if greeting old friends.

They were. I understood that now, in the way I understood the dead language rising out of Ash's throat—sideways, without permission, like water finding cracks in a wall I'd built myself.

"Slow down," I said, for the fourth time in an hour.

Ash turned, walked backward a few steps, and said something in that tongue. Three syllables, lilting upward. Then they pointed at me, then at the road, then made an expression of profound, theatrical patience—the universal face of children everywhere who have been asked to slow down by adults who cannot keep up.

"I don't know what you said."

Ash rolled their eyes. Then they stopped, crouched, and drew in the dust between two flagstones: a little spiral, and beside it, a stick figure with my hair—or what passed for my hair in stick-figure economy—and another spiral coming out of its mouth.

"You're saying I talk too much?"

Ash shook their head, tapped the spiral from my mouth, then touched their own chest, then pointed up the road toward the mountain, hazy and blue-black on the horizon.

"No," I said slowly. "You're saying... the road talks? And I can hear it?"

Ash beamed and nodded so hard their whole body wobbled.

Wonderful. The road talked. The road remembered things, apparently, and it had decided I was a suitable audience. Five years of careful, deliberate, aggressively boring invisibility, and now the landscape itself wanted a word with me.

I kept walking anyway. Where else was I going to go?

* * *

The first flash came at noon, near a shrine that wasn't a shrine anymore—just a niche in a hillside where someone had once set a candle, the stone blackened in a crescent from centuries of flame.

I bent to look at it and the world tilted.

Not the hillside. A kitchen. Copper pots hanging from hooks, steam rising, and someone laughing—laughing with their whole body, head tipped back, flour on their hands, flour *on my hands*, because we'd been baking together, because that was the kind of thing we did on mornings when neither of us had anywhere to be—

I came back to myself kneeling in the dirt with my palms pressed flat against cold stone and my heart going like a trapped bird.

"Juniper." Not spoken. Signed—Ash had crouched in front of me, fingers moving in shapes I somehow read: *you went away.*

"Just for a second," I said. My voice came out wrong. I cleared my throat. "Just remembering."

Ash's eyes narrowed. They signed again, slower: *what remembering?*

And there it was, the question I couldn't answer, the hole in the middle of me shaped exactly like a person whose name I couldn't find. "I don't know," I said, which was the truest sentence I'd spoken in five years.

Ash studied me for a long moment with those too-old eyes. Then they took my hand, pressed it against the blackened stone of the shrine, and said one word in the dead tongue. Low. Deliberate. Like they were handing me something fragile.

I didn't understand it. But my mouth almost shaped it back. Almost. Somewhere under my skin, something stirred and turned over, like a sleeper disturbed.

We walked on. My hand stayed warm for a mile.

* * *

That night we camped in the shell of a waystation—pilgrims' shelter, roof half-collapsed, walls thick enough to hold the wind out if not the memories in. I got a fire going the mundane way, flint and steel, because open magic on the Magistry's roads was still a fine way to end up in a fine coat's ledger, even this far out. Ash sat cross-legged opposite me and began, with great seriousness, to teach me.

They'd started it that morning, actually—pointing at things and naming them in their tongue, waiting until I repeated the sounds. It should have been tedious. Instead it was the strangest comfort I'd known in years, sitting in a ruin with a mute child who spoke only the language of the buried dead, trading words like we were building a bridge plank by plank over something very deep.

"*Senneth,*" Ash said, pointing at the fire.

"Senneth."

"*Halla.*" Pointing at me.

"Halla?" I raised an eyebrow. "That better not mean 'fool.'"

Ash grinned, which told me nothing, which meant it absolutely did.

But here was the thing—the thing I hadn't said aloud, hadn't let myself think past the shape of: the words weren't new to me. When Ash said *senneth*, something behind my eyes answered before my mind could catch up. When they named the stars—*irren, irren-veth, the small ones that wander*—I knew which ones they meant before they finished pointing. I knew the grammar of it the way you know the layout of a house you grew up in, even after the furniture's been sold.

You don't learn a dead language in an afternoon. You remember it. Which meant somewhere in the missing years, someone had taught me—and I had a sick, sliding suspicion about who, and when, and what it had cost them.

"Ash," I said quietly. "When you sleep. Do you dream about the mountain?"

The grin faded. Ash looked at the fire for a while. Then they nodded, once.

"Do you dream about—" I stopped. Started again. "Do you dream about me? About us? Before?"

Another nod. Slower. And then Ash did something that undid me completely: they reached across the fire, took my hand, and traced on my palm, letter by letter, in the common script I'd taught them two days ago on the road, four words:

*I REMEMBER YOU SMILING*

I didn't cry. I want that noted for the record. There was dust from the ruined waystation, and the smoke stung, and that is the official account.

"Then maybe you can tell me," I said, when I could speak normally again. "What happened. What I did. Who I—" The name wouldn't come. It never came. There was just the shape where it should be, aching like a tooth pulled years ago that the tongue still worries. "Who I loved."

Ash's face went carefully blank—that particular blankness children perfect when adults ask questions the answers to which are too heavy to hand over. They shook their head.

"You don't remember?"

Headshake.

"But you knew us. Both of us."

A pause. Then, slowly, Ash signed: *the mountain took it. not from you.* Their fingers hesitated. *from the world.*

I sat with that while the fire burned down. From the world. As if my missing years weren't a gap in me but a wound in everything—as if the person I'd loved had been erased so thoroughly that even the child who'd known them couldn't say their name, couldn't hold onto their face, could only remember that someone had smiled.

The road hummed under the flagstones. The mountain waited on the horizon, patient as debt.

* * *

The second flash came three days later, at a place where the road crossed a river, and it was not gentle.

There was a ford there, shallow and wide, and an old stone marker with worn letters. Ash splashed happily in the shallows while I crouched to read the inscription, and the moment my fingers touched the carved grooves, I was somewhere else entirely.

Rain. Night rain, hammering down. A bedroom lit by candlelight gone guttery and wrong. Someone on the bed—someone I knew the way you know your own heartbeat, someone whose face slid away every time I reached for it like water through fingers—breathing in that terrible wet, rattling way. And me, younger, soaked to the skin, kneeling in a circle of salt with a scrap of parchment burning in my hands, and my voice saying words in the dead tongue, fluently, desperately, promising *anything, anything, take anything, take me, take my—*

The flash broke. I was on my knees in the river shallows, gasping, and Ash had both arms around me, holding on with surprising strength, saying my name—*halla, halla*—over and over in that lilting tongue, and it sounded less like "fool" every time and more like something you'd call someone you were trying to keep anchored to the world.

"It was me," I rasped. Water ran off my chin; I couldn't tell if it was river or rain-memory. "That night. I was the one who called them. I sold—" My stomach heaved. "Ash, I think I sold *your* soul. To save someone. To save—"

The name. The name. It rose in me like a bubble through dark water and burst at the surface empty.

Ash held my face in both small hands and made me look at them. Their expression wasn't fear. It wasn't forgiveness either—it was something more terrible than either. It was certainty.

They signed, deliberately, so I couldn't look away: *you chose. the mountain counts choices, not sins.*

"The mountain can keep its accounting," I said, and stood up, and wrung out my sleeves, and put one foot in front of the other because that was the only spell I had left that still worked.

* * *

We climbed for two more days. The road narrowed. The pilgrim shrines grew more frequent, and each one tugged at me like a hook in cloth—small flashes, mostly: the smell of bread, a voice humming off-key, hands braiding a cord of red thread. Once, terrifyingly, I heard the voice itself, clear as a bell across a valley: *"Juniper, you absolute disaster, hold the ladder straighter,"* and I laughed out loud alone on the mountainside and then had to sit down on a rock for a while, laughing and grieving at the same time for someone whose face I still couldn't see.

Ash watched all of it with growing boldness. The child who had arrived at my door barefoot and silent now chattered constantly in the dead tongue, narrating beetles and clouds and my many failings as a walking companion, and taught me a new word every mile. My accent was atrocious. Ash found this hilarious. Some ancient court of the buried dead had a language older than kingdoms, and its most fluent living speaker used it primarily to mock me.

On the evening of the sixth day, we crested a ridge and saw the tollhouse.

It sat in a col below the snowline, squat and dark against the last red light, with a single window glowing amber. Smoke curled from its chimney. The road ran straight through its archway, and above the arch hung a sign in three scripts—one of them the dead tongue, which Ash read aloud softly, reverently:

*"All who pass give freely, or do not pass at all."*

Ash's hand found mine. Small, cold, gripping hard.

Below us, the amber light flickered, and I would have sworn—would have sworn on whatever was left of my name—that the light pulsed once, twice, like something on the other side of that window had noticed us standing on the ridge.

Like it had been expecting us.

And in the back of my skull, beneath five years of careful forgetting, something very old and very much mine whispered the first line of an oath I didn't remember swearing:

*Toll is taken. Toll is given. Walk on, walker, and pay.*
