## 13. The Tollhouse of Small Memories

The road stopped pretending to be a road somewhere past the snowline's first warning—a cairn of stacked stones that Ash touched reverently, like the hem of a priest's robe—and became instead a suggestion. A path worn into the mountain's flank by centuries of pilgrims who had, presumably, also wondered what exactly they'd gotten themselves into.

I had been wondering for two days. I was getting quite good at it.

"Nearly there," Sera said, which was what she'd been saying since dawn, and before dawn, and once in the middle of the night when I woke to check that Ash was still breathing and found her sitting up by the fire like a woman keeping vigil over a grave she hadn't dug yet.

"You said that at the last bend," I said.

"The last bend was nearly there. This is *more* nearly there."

Ash tugged my sleeve and signed—quick, impatient, the way children sign when adults are being slow—which meant, as near as I could translate from the small vocabulary we'd built between us on the road: *stop talking, walk.*

The child had grown bolder with every mile. In Vell, Ash had been a ghost wearing a body, all flinches and silence. Out here, where the old tongue lived in the stones themselves, something in the child had begun to unfurl. Ash taught me three new words yesterday while we ate hard cheese on a fallen tree: *stone-that-listens*, *cold-with-teeth*, and one word I understood without translation, the way you understand fire is hot or grief is heavy. It meant *home*, but only the kind of home you're taken to, not the kind you go back to.

I did not enjoy learning that word. I learned it anyway. That was becoming the shape of this whole journey.

* * *

The tollhouse appeared the way fog appears—one moment the path was empty, the next there was a building squatting in a hollow where no building had any right to be. It was small, stone, older than the pilgrim road itself if the lichen was anything to judge by, with a roof sagging like a tired shoulder and a single door of black wood that had never known paint.

Above the door, carved deep enough to have outlived every attempt at weathering, were words in the old tongue. Ash read them aloud—or rather, Ash made sounds that my half-remembered understanding assembled into meaning, the way you assemble a face from a description:

*All who pass beneath the mountain pay. The court does not take coin. The court takes what you carry and do not count.*

"What does that mean?" I asked, though I suspected I already knew. Suspecting and knowing are different animals; suspecting lets you sleep at night.

Sera had gone very still. She'd seen a tollhouse before, five years ago, when she came this far looking for her sister and turned back at the second threshold because she couldn't pay what it asked. She'd told me about it on the third night out, in the flat voice she used for things that had scarred over but not healed.

"It means," she said quietly, "that it's time to decide what you're willing to lose."

"I've already lost plenty."

"Not yet." She looked at me with those gray eyes that missed nothing, that had noticed Ash's muteness and my gate and every careful lie I'd told for five years. "You'll know it when they take it. That's the cruelty of it. You don't get to choose what matters until it's already gone."

"That's not cruelty," I said. "That's just Tuesday, most places."

She almost smiled. "Your humor is going to be very useful down there, Juniper Halloran. The dead appreciate a joke. It confuses them."

* * *

We made camp outside the tollhouse rather than inside it, because neither Sera nor I wanted to sleep under its roof, and Ash—sensible child—refused to go within ten feet of the door after touching the frame once and pulling back as though burned. I built a fire from scrub pine that smoked too much and gave little heat, and we sat around it eating the last of the travel bread, and nobody talked, which is its own kind of conversation.

It was Ash who broke the silence. The child crawled into my lap—I'd stopped being surprised by this around day four; children decide these things on their own schedule and you simply find yourself furniture—and pressed one small hand flat against my chest, over the heart, and signed slowly so I couldn't miss it:

*They will take a piece of you.*

"Yes," I said.

*Will it hurt?*

I considered lying. I'd gotten good at lying in Vell—the whole city was built on polite fictions, anti-magic laws and licensed charm-sellers and everyone pretending the mountain wasn't full of the dead. But Ash had heard me lie to Magistry agents through a closed door, and had looked at me afterward with an expression I recognized: the look of a child deciding how much of adult nonsense to tolerate.

"No," I said. "That's the strange part. It doesn't hurt when it happens. It hurts later, when you reach for something and find the space where it used to be."

Ash considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then signed: *I will remember it for you.*

And there it was—that thing children do, offering you their own small strength as though it were nothing, as though a soul sold to the Undercourt had strength to spare. I pulled the child close and felt the humming under Ash's skin, fainter now than it had been in Vell, as though proximity to the court both fed and frightened whatever slept in the child.

"You can't remember things for other people," I said into Ash's hair. "But thank you."

*I can,* Ash signed against my collarbone. *Watch me.*

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and with it, the tollhouse door stood open.

It hadn't been open the night before. I want that on the record. I checked it twice, the way you check a lock you don't trust, and it had been shut—shut the way doors are shut when something behind them is patient. Now it stood wide, and the darkness inside wasn't dark so much as *attentive*, and standing in the doorway was—

Well. I'd expected a monster. Or a wraith, or a skeletal ferryman, some figure from the cautionary tales Sera had finally started telling once she'd committed to this mad errand. What stood in the doorway was a small woman in a plain dress, gray-haired, with flour on her apron and an expression of mild professional courtesy, like an innkeeper greeting guests she'd been expecting for years.

"Juniper Halloran," she said. Her voice was warm. That was the worst part. "And the widow Sera. And the little one." Her eyes found Ash and softened in a way that made my skin crawl, because it was the softness of a shopkeeper appraising goods. "Come in. It's cold on the mountain, and the court keeps a decent kettle."

"We're not staying," Sera said.

"Nobody stays." The woman stepped aside, gesturing. "That's rather the point. Come in, come in. Only the one crossing today?" She looked at me. "Yes. Only you. The widow paid her toll years ago, poor dear, and the child—" her gaze flicked to Ash again, and something moved behind her eyes, something vast and hungry and terribly polite, "—the child's account is already open."

My hand found Ash's shoulder without my deciding to move it. "What does that mean?"

"It means," the woman said pleasantly, "that we should discuss your payment inside, where the tea is. Negotiations conducted on doorsteps always sour. House rule." She smiled. "Also mountain rule. Also court rule. They're all the same rule."

* * *

Inside, the tollhouse was larger than its walls allowed. This is a thing magic does when it stops bothering to hide: rooms unfold, ceilings rise, and you find yourself standing in a kitchen that smells of bread baking and woodsmoke and something underneath it all, something mineral and old, like the air in a cave that has never seen sun.

There was a table. There were chairs. There was, absurdly, a kettle just coming to the boil, and four cups set out, and I thought with a kind of hysterical clarity: *someone set those cups. Someone chose the number four.* The court knew exactly who would walk through that door.

"Sit," said the woman, who had not introduced herself and whom I somehow understood needed no introduction, the way you need no introduction to winter. "Tea?"

"Do I get a choice?" I asked.

"You always get a choice." She poured anyway. "That's what makes the taking bearable. If the court simply seized, there'd be rebellion. But a choice—" she set the cup before me, and the steam smelled like chamomile and, underneath, like nothing at all, "—a choice teaches you what you value. The Buried King finds that beautiful. He collects it, in his way. Understanding, purchased at retail."

Sera sat with her back straight and her hands folded, a woman visiting a place she'd sworn never to see again. Ash sat pressed against my side, small and burning-hot, watching everything.

"So," I said. "Payment."

"Payment." The woman produced, from nowhere, a ledger. Of course a ledger. Everything in this business was ledgers; I'd found that out in the Vell archives, finding my own handwriting beside a child's name. This ledger was different—older, bound in something that might have been leather and might have been skin, and when it opened, the pages turned themselves to a fresh line. "The first threshold asks for something small. Something overlooked. The court begins gently—it wants you to learn the currency before the prices climb."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you turn back, and the child goes to the mountain alone someday, drawn by the humming, and pays far worse than memory." She said it kindly. Kindness, I was learning, was the court's native dialect. "You won't refuse. You've come too far and love too much. The King counts on that too."

I looked at Ash. Ash looked at me and signed, slow and deliberate: *I will remember it for you.*

"All right," I said. "What do you want?"

The woman tilted her head, listening to something I couldn't hear, and then her expression shifted—not cruelly, but with the terrible gentleness of someone delivering a diagnosis. "Oh," she said softly. "*Oh*, that's lovely. Yes. The kitchen."

"The what?"

"Your mother's kitchen. The taste of it." She leaned forward. "You think the great memories matter most—your partner's face, the night of the oath, the big bright wounds. But the court knows better. The court values what overlooks itself. Tell me, Juniper Halloran: when did you last think of your mother's kitchen? Deliberately? Chose to stand in it?"

I opened my mouth and found nothing. Not because the memory was gone—because I hadn't visited it in years. It sat in me the way furniture sits in a room you've stopped seeing: bread dough on her hands, the blue enamel pot, the smell of rosemary and scorched butter, the window over the basin where she'd grow herbs in chipped cups. The taste of her cooking—simple food, peasant food, the food of a woman who had three coins and made them sing. I hadn't thought of it in five years. Maybe ten. It had simply *been there*, load-bearing, part of the floor of me.

"You can't," I said. "That's not fair. That's not even a real—"

"It's the realest thing you carry," the woman said. "The big memories shout. This one hums. And the court, Juniper—" she spread her flour-dusted hands, "—the court is built on humming."

* * *

I won't pretend I deliberated nobly. I sat there with tea going cold in front of me and tried to argue, and the arguments all died in the obvious place: Ash, barefoot in my shop on a storm night, speaking a language older than kingdoms. Ash asleep against my ribs, humming with someone else's stolen song. There was no version of arithmetic where my mother's kitchen outweighed a child's soul. My mother, whoever and wherever she was now, would have done the math herself and slapped me for hesitating.

"How does it work?" I asked.

"You eat," the woman said, and set before me a small plate, and on the plate was a piece of bread.

Just bread. Brown crust, torn unevenly, still warm. Except I knew—knew the way you know in dreams—that it wasn't bread. It was the memory itself, rendered edible, the way the court rendered everything into transaction. Steam curled off it and the steam smelled like rosemary and scorched butter and home, the kind of home you go back to, and my eyes stung before I'd even lifted it.

"One bite," the woman said gently. "Chew slowly. Let it become part of you on its way out. That's the trick of it—you don't lose the memory so much as pass it through, and the court catches what falls."

"And then I won't remember her kitchen."

"You won't remember that you ever had one." She paused. "You'll keep your mother. Faces, voices, love—all that stays. But the kitchen, the taste, the room itself—gone. A hole shaped like warmth where the floor used to be. You'll walk over it every day and never notice, because that's the nature of what we take." She smiled, and for one moment the innkeeper fell away and something immense looked out through her eyes, something that had been counting since before mountains had names. "We are not thieves, Juniper Halloran. We are connoisseurs."

Ash's hand found mine under the table and gripped hard. Small fingers. Sticky with travel bread from the night before. I held on.

"For the child," I said, mostly to myself. "For the child."

"For the child," the woman agreed, and there was, I swear, something almost tender in it. "Now. Before you eat—do you want to say goodbye to it? Most people do. It helps. The King finds the farewells delicious."

So I sat in that impossible kitchen and I said goodbye to a room I'd forgotten to visit. I said goodbye to the blue enamel pot and the herbs in chipped cups and the window over the basin. I said goodbye to the taste of her bread, which I had carried in my mouth for thirty-odd years without once thanking it. I said goodbye to the person I'd been before Vell, before the sealed gate, before the oath I couldn't remember swearing—the person whose worst problem was burnt butter.

Then I picked up the bread, and I ate.

* * *

It tasted like rosemary and scorched butter.

For one perfect moment I was eight years old and the kitchen was gold with afternoon light and my mother was singing off-key and everything was simple and warm and *mine*—

And then it was passing through me, the way she'd said, and I felt it go—felt the court catch it, felt something vast and patient swallow a small warm light—and the bread in my hand was just bread, cooling, ordinary, and I looked at it and felt...

Nothing.

Not grief. Not loss. A *hole*. I reached for the kitchen the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and there was no stair, and worse—there was no memory of there having been a stair. I knew intellectually that I'd had a mother, that mothers have kitchens, that mine must have. But the room itself, the taste, the warmth—it was like trying to recall a dream from someone else's sleep.

"Oh," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Oh, that's—"

"There it is," the woman said softly, and in the ledger, ink wrote itself across the fresh line in a hand that was, I realized with distant horror, my own. *Paid in full: one kitchen, one taste, one warmth overlooked.* She closed the book. "You see? You barely feel it. That's why the court loves the small ones. People pay them without a fight."

Sera put her hand on my shoulder. I let her. Ash climbed into my lap and pressed a palm over my heart again, right where the hole was, and signed against my chest, fierce and deliberate:

*I remember it for you. Rosemary. Burnt butter. Blue pot. Window.*

And I couldn't feel what the child meant—but I could feel *that Ash meant it*, that the words were a gift wrapped in a language older than kingdoms, and I held onto that the way a drowning man holds a rope: not because it holds him up, but because someone is holding the other end.

"The road opens at dawn," the woman said, gathering cups. "Rest tonight. Eat well." At the door, she paused, and her kindness curdled just slightly, just enough. "The next threshold asks for more, Juniper Halloran. Much more. Sleep on what you might be willing to give."

"The next threshold," Sera said quietly, from her chair, in the voice of a woman who had turned back once already. "What does it ask?"

The woman smiled, flour and warmth and abyss.

"A face," she said. "It asks for a face."
