## 14. Voices in the Snowline

The mountain speaks my language.

I did not know it had one. I thought mountains were like Juniper's shop—full of things that hum but never say. But here, where the snow begins and does not stop, the wind comes down in syllables. Old ones. The kind that taste of candle smoke and closed doors.

*Come up, little ember,* says the wind. *Come up, little ember, we have kept your seat warm.*

I do not want a warm seat. I want Juniper to stop walking so fast, because their legs are long and mine are not, and also because I am pretending to look at a rock so they will think I am brave.

The rock is very interesting. It is gray. All the rocks are gray. This is the least interesting rock in the world and I have been studying it for one hundred years.

*Your name,* says the wind, softer now, the way the widow Sera talks when she thinks no one is listening. *We remember your name. Do you?*

That is not fair. That is cheating. I open my mouth to say something rude in common tongue—the only rude word I know is the one Sera said when she stubbed her toe on the shop step, and it is a good word—but nothing comes out, because nothing ever comes out, because my voice was the first thing they took and everything after that was just tidying up.

Juniper looks back at me. They have been looking back at me approximately every eleven steps since the snowline, which I know because counting is something I do instead of sleeping. Their face does the thing where it is trying to be calm and mostly succeeding, the way a lid tries to be a pot.

"Rock still winning?" they ask.

I nod solemnly. It is a very serious competition.

They crouch in the snow next to me, breath coming out in little ghosts. "We'll rest soon. There's a shelf of stone past this rise—Sera said the pilgrims used to camp there." A pause. Their eyes go sideways, toward the wind, toward the syllables I can hear and they cannot. "You hear them again."

It is not a question. Juniper has stopped asking questions they already know the answers to. I think this is what growing up is. I do not recommend it.

I hold up three fingers. Three voices today. Yesterday there were two. The day before, only one, far away, like a knock through a wall.

Juniper's jaw does a thing. "Okay," they say. "Okay. Three." They say it the way you'd say *three knives*, or *three debts*. Then they stand and offer me their hand, and I take it, because the snow is deep and because their hand is warm and because I am allowed to take it. That last part is the important one. Nobody told me I was allowed things before. I am making up for lost time.

*Little ember,* sing the voices as we climb. *Little ember, why do you cling to the cold-handed one? We are your people. We speak the true tongue. We will give you back your voice, your name, your mother's kitchen, your—*

I stop listening. Mostly. It is hard to stop listening when they use the true tongue, because the true tongue does not have a word for *stop listening*. The old language assumes you will always be listening. It assumes everyone is family. That is how they get you.

* * *

The stone shelf is exactly where Sera said, which means Sera has been on this road before, which means there are things Sera has not told us, which means when we get home I am going to look at her very meaningfully until she explains herself. I have learned that from Juniper. It works better than words anyway.

We make a fire small enough to hide. Juniper melts snow for tea that tastes like hot nothing, and I eat the bread that tastes like sad nothing, and above us the stars come out sharp and cold as needles.

Then the voices find the fire.

They always find the fire. Fire is a door, in the true tongue. Every flame is a little door, and doors are how they travel, and I did not know that either until the mountain taught me, and there are many things the mountain has taught me that I would like to return.

*You are burning our gift,* they murmur through the flames. *Every stick you feed it is a page torn from your own book. Come away from the cold-handed one. Come up to the hall of candles, where nothing is ever cold, where no one ever leaves, where you will never again have to count steps because time will stop counting you.*

Time will stop counting you. That is a beautiful sentence. That is the most beautiful sentence anyone has ever said to me, and it is a lie, and knowing it is a lie does not make it less beautiful, which I think might be the whole problem with the Undercourt in one sentence.

My hands have gone still around the tin cup. I notice this the way you notice a stair missing in the dark—that lurch, that wrongness. My feet want to stand. My feet are being very stupid right now.

*Ash.* Not *little ember* this time. My actual name, the buried one, the one that fits me like a key fits a lock I didn't know I had. When they say it, something behind my ribs turns over. Something behind my ribs wants to answer.

I set down the cup. Very carefully. Like it belongs to someone else.

And then I do the thing I promised myself I would do, which is: I go to Juniper.

Juniper is pretending to study a map by firelight, which is a lie, because the map is upside down and has been upside down for an hour and they have not noticed, and I love them so much it feels like a wound sometimes, this ridiculous person who bought my soul with a bargain they cannot even remember and then spent five years selling lucky buttons to pay off a debt they forgot they owed.

I sit down against their side. Hard. Deliberately. The way a cat sits on a book you are reading.

"Oh," says Juniper. "Hello."

I point at the fire. I point at my ears. I make the gesture we invented on the third night of the road—fingers fluttering away like moths—for *they're talking.*

"How many?"

I hold up four. Then, honestly, five. The fifth one is new. It sounds older than the others. It sounds like it knew my grandmother.

Juniper goes quiet in the specific way that means their brain is doing too much. Then they start digging in their pack, muttering, throwing out a spare shirt, a coil of wire, a tin of tea leaves, until they find what they're looking for: a little cloth roll that clinks.

"I've been working on this," they say. "Since the tollhouse. Since—" They stop. They have been stopping mid-sentence a lot since the tollhouse, since they paid away their mother's kitchen and came out the other side with wet eyes and a smile like a cracked cup. "Since I realized the road was going to keep talking to you and I couldn't hear it. Which is unfair. You shouldn't have to be the only translator between worlds. That's a terrible job. I had that job once, apparently, and look how it went."

They unroll the cloth.

Inside are seven small charms on cords—twisted wire, river stones, bits of bottle glass, a button. Ordinary things. Except they are not ordinary; I can feel it from here, a faint warmth like standing near an oven. Juniper magic. The real kind, the kind they hid under the floorboards of their own life for five years, the kind Vell made illegal and Juniper made anyway, quietly, the way you feed a stray cat.

"It's not much," Juniper says, and their ears have gone pink, which happens when they make something and care about it, which is always. "It won't stop them. Nothing stops them, probably. But each stone holds a promise. And promises are—" They fumble. "Promises are louder than invitations. If you wear them, when the voices come, you'll have something to hold that isn't theirs. Something that says who you belong to."

I stare at them.

Because here is the thing nobody understands about being mute: people think silence is empty. People talk over your silence, fill it, apologize for it. But silence is not empty. Silence is a room, and the dead have been shouting into my room my whole life, and I could never shout back, and I got so tired, so bone-tired, of being a doorway that only opens one way.

And this ridiculous person just handed me seven small stones and called them a voice.

I hold out my wrist.

Juniper ties them on one at a time, narrating each one, because they cannot help themselves. "River stone—for the day you arrived and I pretended I wasn't terrified. Bottle glass—for the green sweet you stole from Sera and blamed on a ghost, which, for the record, I believed. Button—for every charm I sold that was fake except the luck I gave away free." Their fingers shake slightly on the last cord. "And this one—" It is a chip of ordinary gray rock. From the mountain. From this morning. From the least interesting rock in the world. "This one is for the rock you studied for a hundred years while being scared out of your mind. Bravest thing I ever saw."

I am not crying. There is ash in my eye. From the fire. There is a lot of ash in both my eyes.

When all seven are tied, the bracelet is heavier than it should be, warm as a held hand. And when the voices come again—*little ember, little ember, come away, come up*—the stones hum back at them. Not words. Just a low, stubborn note, like a struck bell that refuses to finish ringing.

The voices falter.

They are not used to being answered. That is the flaw in them, I realize, watching the firelight bend around Juniper's clumsy beautiful charms. The dead speak a language that assumes no one will ever talk back. They have contracts and candles and centuries, and they have never once been interrupted.

But I have a bracelet full of interruptions now.

* * *

I sleep, that night, actually sleep, curled against Juniper's side with my wrist tucked under my cheek so the stones press warm against my ear like a heartbeat that isn't mine but has agreed to lend itself.

In the morning there is frost on the map (still upside down) and frost on Juniper's eyelashes and frost on everything except the circle of air directly around me, which the charms keep warm, a small stubborn summer the size of a child.

We climb.

The voices do not stop. I don't think they will ever stop; I think they will follow me to my grave and then follow me past it, knocking politely forever. But they sound different now. Farther. Frustrated. *Little ember,* they call, and the stones hum, and the calling slides off me like rain off oiled cloth.

By midday I can see it: a black notch high on the mountain's shoulder, where the snow doesn't fall. The gate. The real one, the mother-gate, the one my cellar gate was only a whisper of. Candles burn in the notch—I can see the light from here, hundreds of them, thousands, amber-colored, and each one is a jar and each jar is a soul and somewhere in that hall is the piece of me they keep in glass.

Juniper sees it too. They go very still, the way they went still in the archives, the way they went still reading their own handwriting in a ledger of sold souls.

"That's it," they say quietly. "One more threshold between us and the court." They flex their hands, and I see them doing the arithmetic they hate: what will it cost, what will it cost, what will it cost. They have already paid a kitchen and a face. They have already mourned someone whose name is gone. I watch them try to imagine what's left to lose, and I watch them fail, and I reach up and touch the bracelet, and the stones hum, and Juniper looks down at me and almost smiles.

"Whatever it asks," they say, "we decide together. Both of us. That's the rule now. New rule."

I nod. Then I point at the notch, at the amber glow, and I make the sign we invented for *the voices*: fingers fluttering like moths.

But this time I add something new. I press my palm flat over the stones, then point at Juniper, then at myself, then draw a circle in the air around us both.

*Ours,* I say, without saying it. *Whatever calls us, we are ours.*

Juniper understands. Juniper always understands, which is either a miracle or a disaster waiting to happen, and knowing my luck, probably both.

Together, we climb toward the candles.
