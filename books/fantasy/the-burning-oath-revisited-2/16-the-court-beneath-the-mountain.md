## 16. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair went down for a long time. Longer than the mountain had any right to be deep, longer than stone should be patient.

I counted the steps at first—two hundred and eleven before I lost my nerve and stopped counting, because counting made it real, and real things could be lost. Ash rode on my back in a sling Sera had sewn from an old pilgrim's cloak, their small weight warm against my spine like a second heartbeat that wasn't mine but had decided to keep time with me anyway. They didn't speak. They never spoke, not in words I could use, but they pressed one hand flat against my shoulder blade in a rhythm—press, press, press-pause—that I had come to understand meant *I am here, are you here too.*

I was here. That was the problem. Here was exactly where I'd sworn five years ago never to go again, and here was where I was carrying a child toward a king who bought souls the way merchants in Vell bought salt: politely, in bulk, with receipts.

The candles started before we were halfway down.

That was the thing nobody warns you about—the light. You expect darkness in a place like this. You brace for it. But the Undercourt doesn't believe in darkness; it believes in *candlelight*, thousands upon thousands of tapers set into niches in the walls, each one burning with a flame that didn't flicker. Not once. Not when our breath moved past them. Not when the air stirred with whatever vast slow respiration the mountain performed. Every flame stood perfectly still, like soldiers, like held breaths, like souls pretending to be fire.

"They're not candles," I said aloud, because saying it aloud made it less true and therefore more bearable. "They can't be candles."

Ash's hand pressed twice against my back. Then paused. Then pressed twice again, which was new, which was either *yes* or *run*, and I genuinely could not tell which.

We descended into the light.

* * *

The hall opened around us the way a cathedral opens—not gradually, not with warning, but all at once, as if the stone had been holding its shape out of politeness and finally gave up.

It was beautiful. That's the part I need you to understand, because everything after depends on it. It was *beautiful*.

Pillars of black granite rose into a ceiling lost in candle-glow, carved with figures that might have been dancers or might have been drowning people—the difference, down there, seemed mostly a matter of perspective. The floor was polished to a mirror sheen, dark as still water, and our reflections walked beneath us, slightly out of step, slightly delayed, as though the floor remembered our footsteps from a moment ago and was replaying them out of courtesy. Niches lined every wall from floor to impossible height, and in each niche sat a jar.

Amber glass, each one, rounded like a lantern or a heart or a skull depending on how the light caught it. And inside each jar burned a soft golden glow—not steady like the wall-candles but *moving*, swirling gently, the way breath fogs glass, the way a moth beats against a window. Thousands of jars. Tens of thousands. The whole hall glowed with them, warm and honeyed and gentle, and if you let your eyes unfocus—if you let yourself see only color and not content—it looked like the inside of a sun. Like warmth itself had been bottled and shelved and lovingly arranged.

I heard myself make a sound. A small one. The kind of sound you make when beauty and horror arrive at the same moment and you can't yet tell which one you're looking at.

Because they weren't lights. I knew what they were before anyone told me. Some knowledge doesn't travel through the eyes; it travels through the teeth, the marrow, the old animal parts of you. Each jar held a soul. Each swirl of gold was someone—someone who had been bargained for, or gambled away, or sold by a person who loved them enough to do the unthinkable, or loved them too little to refuse. The court hadn't imprisoned them. The court had *curated* them. Arranged them by some system I couldn't read, dusted, presumably, by hands I didn't want to imagine.

And somewhere in this hall—maybe on this very shelf, maybe three jars to the left of where I was standing—was Ash.

"Juniper." Ash said it. Not in the dead tongue, not in the humming language of the mountain, but in cracked, rusty, half-remembered common speech, the first word they'd managed since arriving barefoot at my door. They said it like a key turning in a lock they'd found in the dark. "Juniper. My jar."

My heart did something structurally unsound.

"I know," I said. "I know, little ember. We're going to find it."

"We're not taking it," Ash said, and their voice was doing something terrible, something adult, something that had clearly been rehearsed in dreams. "We're taking *me*. The jar is just where they put me while I was walking around being alive without permission."

Five years old, maybe six. Speaking in sentences about ontological permission. I adjusted the sling and kept walking, because if I stopped to feel the full weight of that sentence I would have sat down in the middle of the Buried King's hall and never gotten up.

* * *

He was waiting for us at the far end, though "waiting" implies he'd been idle before we arrived, and I suspect the Buried King has never been idle in his long unlived life. He sat on a throne of fused amber—actual melted soul-light, poured and cooled into a seat—and he was *reading*. A ledger, naturally. Everything down here was ledgers. He closed it when he saw us with the soft care of a man handling something holy, and he rose, and he bowed.

Bowed. To us. To a charm-seller from Vell and a child who owned nothing but the sling they sat in.

"Travelers," he said, and his voice was the most courteous sound I had ever heard underground. Warm as mulled wine. Smooth as a lawyer's handshake. "Welcome to the Court Beneath. Forgive the chill; we keep no fires here except those we are given."

*Those we are given.* As if the souls had arrived as gifts. As if every jar on every shelf had been handed over willingly by someone, somewhere, with a signature and a thank-you note.

"You know why we've come," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only magic I've ever been reliably good at.

"I know why *you've* come," the King said, tilting his head with genuine, horrible kindness. "The child has always known. Children remember what adults pay to forget." He gestured, one pale hand, toward the shelves. "You are here about a jar. You are here about a contract. And you—" and here his gaze settled on me with the weight of a hand on the back of the neck, "—you are here about yourself, though you may not know it yet."

"I know it," I said.

"Do you?" He smiled. It was a lovely smile. It was the smile of a shopkeeper greeting a customer whose money he has already counted. "Then let us dispense with pleasantries, which I confess I adore, and speak of terms, which I adore more."

He began to walk along the shelves, and—this is the detail that will live in my nightmares—he *greeted* them. Touched the jars, one after another, with two fingers, the way you'd greet old friends at a market. "Good evening, mistress Aldeth. Still singing, I see. Good evening, master Corvin. Ah—you've nearly finished your grief. Wonderful progress." And the lights inside swirled toward his fingers like moths, like dogs, like children reaching for the only adult who ever visited.

Every soul in that hall knew him. Every soul in that hall *liked* him. That was the horror of it—not cruelty, not chains, but courtesy so complete it had become its own kind of cage. He wasn't a jailer. He was a *host*, and the Undercourt was a dinner party that never ended, and everyone had been seated according to their contracts and no one was permitted to leave the table.

"You keep them well," I said, and hated myself for the compliment's shape even as it left my mouth.

"I keep them *happily*," he corrected gently. "Happiness is simply a contract honored. Unhappiness is a contract breached. I have never breached a contract, Juniper Halloran. Ask anyone here." He spread his hands at the glowing shelves, and ten thousand souls leaned toward him in their jars like plants toward sun. "Ask anyone."

* * *

He led us—I don't recall agreeing to follow, but follow we did—to a smaller chamber off the main hall, walled entirely in amber jars stacked like wine bottles in a cellar, and here he served tea. Actual tea, in actual cups, on a table of black glass. Steam rose from the cups and did not dissipate; it hung in the candle-still air like a held breath.

"For the child," he said, sliding a cup toward Ash, "a blend of mountain herbs. Perfectly safe. I would not poison a guest; poisoning is a breach of hospitality, and hospitality is a contract, and I told you—I have never—"

"Yes," I said. "You've never breached a contract. You've mentioned."

"Twice," Ash said helpfully, in their rusted little voice, and drank the tea anyway, because they were five and someone had handed them a cup, and I suppose when you've spent your whole life as a line item in someone's ledger, courtesy feels like love.

The King watched the child drink with an expression I can only call *tenderness*, and I understood then that the tenderness was real, and that this was the worst discovery of the entire descent. Monsters who know they're monsters can be fought. But this creature genuinely, sincerely cared for every soul on his shelves the way a collector cares—or a gardener, or a man who keeps birds because he loves their song and sees no contradiction in the cage.

"Now," he said, folding his hands. "Terms. The child's soul sits in niche nine thousand four hundred and twelve, third row, marked in my ledger under the name given at signing. You wish it returned to the body that walks beside you. This is possible. Contracts can be dissolved—but only by the signatory, only in full memory of the signing, and only before the original witness." He tapped the black glass table. "Me."

"And if the signatory doesn't remember the signing?"

His smile widened by a single degree. "Then the signatory must *choose* to remember. There is a toll for that, of course. Memory freely surrendered may be freely reclaimed—but reclaimed at cost. The Court does not give refunds, Juniper Halloran. It offers exchanges."

"What cost?"

"That," he said, rising, bowing again, gathering his ledger with its soft leather whisper, "is discussed in the mirror-hall. With the other party to your contract." At the doorway he paused, silhouetted against ten thousand small golden suns, and delivered the sentence that followed me up out of sleep for weeks afterward:

"He has been waiting for you a very long time. He kept your chair."

* * *

Ash fell asleep against my chest within minutes of the King's departure, exhausted the way only children and soldiers get exhausted, their empty cup still clutched in one fist. I sat in the candle-still chamber and listened to the hall breathe its lightless light, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Vell.

A signatory who doesn't remember. A version of me who signed, who waited, who *kept my chair*. Somewhere behind the next door was the person I had been before I paid to stop being them, and they had spent five years sitting in an amber-lit room rehearsing what they'd say when I finally came home.

I lifted Ash onto my back. The small hand found my shoulder blade. Press, press, press-pause.

*I'm here,* it said. *Are you?*

"Not yet," I whispered, to the child, to the mountain, to whoever I used to be. "But I'm about to find out."

The doors to the mirror-hall opened ahead of us on their own, the way doors do down here—courteously, silently, like everything else in a court built on the principle that you should always feel welcome right up until the moment you cannot leave.

Inside, someone was already standing.
