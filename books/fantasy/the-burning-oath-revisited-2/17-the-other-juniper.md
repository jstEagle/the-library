## 17. The Other Juniper

The mirror-hall announces itself before you see it, the way grief does. One moment you're walking a corridor of black stone lit by candles that burn without heat, and the next the walls on either side of you are glass—not reflective, exactly, not the honest kind of mirror that shows you your own tired face and lets you move along. These are remembering-glass. They show you other versions. Other mornings. A shop with different curtains. A kitchen where someone is humming. A road at dusk with two sets of footprints instead of one.

I tried not to look. I have had five years of practice at not looking.

But the hall is long, and the candles are patient, and somewhere ahead of me there was a door, and so I walked through my own scattered lives like a man wading through shallow water, trying to keep his boots dry. Ash walked beside me, small hand in mine, gripping hard enough to leave marks. The dead had stopped calling to them since the snowline—since the bracelet, since the amber jars we'd passed, each one holding a curled spark of light that might once have been somebody's Tuesday afternoons or somebody's laugh. Ash didn't speak. Ash never spoke, except in that language older than kingdoms, and even that they'd gone quiet about, as if the mountain had finally taught them what their voice was for.

Then the corridor opened into the hall's heart, and there I was.

Not a reflection. Not a trick of candlelight. *Me*—or close enough to make my stomach drop—younger by five years, maybe six, wearing the coat I'd owned back then, the one with the burnt cuff I'd never mended because mending it would have meant admitting how the burn got there. Their hair was longer. Their face was less careful. That was the thing that struck me first, before anything else: how *unguarded* they looked. This was a face that hadn't yet learned to flinch when someone said the word *magic* too loudly in a market square.

"Well," they said, and their voice was mine, pitched slightly higher, slightly rougher. "You took the long way."

"I took the only way," I said.

"That's what I said." They smiled, and it was a terrible smile, warm and crooked and utterly unrepentant, and I hated how much I recognized it. "Five years, though. You gave yourself five years and a hole in your head, and then you spent all five pretending the hole was a feature." They tilted their head at Ash. "And now look. It came knocking anyway."

Ash stared up at my other self with an expression I couldn't read—fear, recognition, something older than both. Their grip on my hand tightened.

"You're not real," I said, which was stupid, because in this place nothing was real in the way I wanted things to be real, and we both knew it.

"No," they agreed pleasantly. "I'm *true*, though. There's a difference. Mirrors show real. I'm what's left over when you subtract everything you paid to forget." They spread their arms, gesturing at the hall, at the remembering-glass, at the whole buried architecture of the court around us. "They keep me here. Or I keep myself here. Honestly, after five years the distinction stops mattering."

"You signed it," I said. My voice came out flat. "You signed Ash's name into that ledger."

Something flickered across their face—there and gone, fast, but I caught it. Guilt has a particular shape no matter how old the guilt is.

"Yes," they said simply. No deflection. No excuse dressed up as explanation. Just the word, laid down between us like a knife on a table.

"Why?"

* * *

They didn't answer right away. Instead they turned and walked, and I followed, because what else was there to do, and Ash came with me because Ash had nowhere else in the world to be. We walked past panes of remembering-glass, and in them I caught glimpses: a hospital cot in a healer's ward that smelled of tallow and fever; a woman's hand, thin and spotted, gripping mine; a night sky over Vell seen from a rooftop I half-remembered climbing.

"Her name was Wren," my other self said quietly.

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. I didn't know it. I knew that I didn't know it, the way you know an amputated limb used to be there—the absence had a shape. But hearing it aloud, in my own voice, did something to the air.

"She was dying," they went on. "Fever-rot, the slow kind, the kind the healers in Vell can't touch because touching it properly would require magic, and magic in Vell requires licenses, and licenses require the Magistry, and the Magistry—" they smiled that crooked smile again, thinner this time, "—the Magistry doesn't grant licenses to charm-sellers who forge their papers. Which I did. Which *you* did. Don't look shocked. You kept the forgeries. They're under the floorboards with everything else."

"The oath paper," I said.

"Among other things." They stopped walking. In the glass beside us, a scene played itself out in silence: a younger version of me kneeling at a bedside, and beyond the window, far off, a mountain with a wound of light at its base. "She had maybe three days. Maybe four. And I had spent my whole life being *careful*, Juniper. Careful with the charms, careful with the laws, careful with the gate in the cellar—never looking at it, never listening to it, keeping my head down and my hands clean. And she was dying anyway, and all my carefulness was worth precisely nothing, and I could not—" their voice cracked, just once, and repaired itself, "—I could not sit there and watch."

"So you went down."

"So I went down." They laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the Buried King said to me? First thing, before any talk of price? He said, *'You came quickly. Most people argue with themselves for years.'*" They shook their head. "He offered me a trade. Her life, restored, whole, decades of it—for a soul. Any soul. Freely given, freely named. And I stood there in that candlelit hall thinking, *anyone, anyone but her, anyone but me,* and then I thought about the children in the poor rows, the ones nobody would miss, the ones whose names weren't even written down anywhere official, and I told myself it would be quick, it would be painless, the King was courteous, he wasn't cruel, the souls just... waited. In jars. Like preserves."

"And then you found Ash."

"And then I found Ash." They closed their eyes. "A child alone in the flood-season streets, speaking a language nobody knew, with nobody looking for them. Nobody *could* look. Do you understand? No family. No records. No one to come asking. The perfect victim. I told myself that too. I was very good at telling myself things." They opened their eyes and looked at me, and their gaze was steady and awful. "I carried them down the pilgrim road on my back. They fell asleep against my shoulder. And at the second threshold I gave Ash's name to the tollkeeper, and I watched it get written into the ledger, and I walked home, and Wren lived."

The glass beside us showed it: a woman sitting up in bed, color in her cheeks, laughing at something out of frame.

"Wren lived," I repeated. The words tasted like ash and like sugar at once.

"For eleven more years? For thirty? I don't know—I don't get updates. But she lived past that fever, and every day she lived was bought with a child's name, and I have sat here in this hall for five years knowing that, and knowing that *you* were up there selling lucky charms and pretending to be a good person, and do you know what the worst part is?" They leaned closer. "The worst part is that I don't regret it. I've had five years to regret it. I've turned it over every way it can be turned. And I still don't. If you put me back on that street with that child and that dying woman, I would do it again. Faster, maybe. With less lying to myself about it."

* * *

I stood there in the candlelight and felt something split open in my chest.

Because I understood them. That was the horror of it—not that they were some twisted reflection, some inverted double, but that they were *right*. Or rather: that I could not prove them wrong. Every argument I raised against them, they had already raised against themselves, in the dark, for five years, and survived it.

"You want to know why they keep me here," they said, reading my silence the way only I could read it. "It's not punishment. The King doesn't punish; he *contracts*. They keep me here because a bargain needs a signer, and the signer can't be allowed to forget. Someone has to remember what you did, Juniper. Since you wouldn't."

"So you're my conscience."

"I'm your *receipt*," they snapped, and for a moment the warmth dropped out of their voice entirely, and underneath it was something raw and furious and exhausted. "I'm the line item. I'm the part of you that stayed in the room after everyone else left. And now you've come all this way, paid two tolls, dragged a child through the snow, to do what? Break the contract? Free the boy?" They gestured at Ash, and their expression softened in a way that undid me worse than the fury had. "Look at them. Look at them, Juniper. They're *whole*. They're safe. Whatever the Undercourt is, whatever these jars are, Ash isn't suffering. Ash is *waiting*. There's a difference, and I clung to that difference for five years like a drowning man clings to driftwood."

"Ash can't speak," I said. "Ash screams in their sleep. Ash flinches from candle flames. That's not waiting. That's a soul in a jar with a hole in the lid."

Their jaw tightened. "And if you break the contract—"

"Then Ash comes whole. All the way whole."

"*If.*" They held up a finger. "If. And here's the part you haven't thought through, because you've been too busy being righteous on a mountainside. The final threshold. You know what it asks. Sera told you, the archives told you, the tollhouse told you with its little polite signs. Everything. All the memories back, all at once—and the oath stays intact. Or stay hollow, and break it." They stepped closer, and their voice dropped low, urgent, the voice I use when I'm frightened and don't want anyone to know. "But that's the *official* version. The version for tourists. I've been in this hall long enough to read the fine print, and the fine print says this: if you take the memories back, you take *me* back. All of me. The one who signed. The one who chose. The one who would choose again. You won't be breaking the oath with clean hands, Juniper. You'll be breaking it with *my* hands. And I am telling you, as someone who knows those hands very well—they will hesitate. Right at the moment that matters most, they will hesitate, because I don't regret it, and you'll be carrying that. Five years of no-regret, sitting in your chest like a stone, while the Buried King watches you fumble the flame."

"Then I'll hesitate," I said. "And then I'll do it anyway."

"You don't know that."

"I know that Ash held my hand down the whole corridor and never once let go. That's more than I know about you."

They flinched. Actually flinched, my bold unrepentant double, my receipt, my signature given flesh—and for a moment they looked not younger than me but older, worn smooth as river stone, and terribly lonely. Of course they were lonely. They had been alone in a hall of mirrors for five years, watching versions of a life they'd traded away.

"You loved her," I said quietly. "Wren."

"More than the world."

"So do I. Whoever she is. I love her in the shape of her absence." I swallowed. "That's why I can't leave Ash in a jar. Because I know exactly what a stolen person looks like. I see one every time I shave."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The candles burned without heat. Somewhere deep beneath us, faintly, the court hummed—the sound the gate in my cellar had made, the sound I'd refused to listen to for five years, and now I understood that I had never been refusing the gate. I'd been refusing this conversation.

"They offered you a choice," my other self said at last, heavily. "The final threshold. Memory and the oath intact, or hollow and broken. But that's not really the choice, Juniper. It never was. The real choice is this: you can walk out of here having freed a child and forgotten why it cost so much. Or you can walk out of here knowing *exactly* what you are." They smiled, and this time the smile had no warmth in it at all, only a kind of terrible tenderness. "Most people choose the first. Most people choose it every single day. It's called being a person."

"I'm not most people," I said. "I'm a charm-seller with a forged license and a hole in my head and a child who calls me by a word I don't understand yet."

"You will," they said softly. "Oh, you will. That word—it's not *protector*. I'll tell you that much for free. It's worse than protector. It's a promise-word. It binds."

"Good," I said. "I'm done with oaths. Promises I can keep."

My other self looked at me for a long time. Then they laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, echoing down the hall of remembering-glass until a hundred versions of me laughed with it, and somewhere in that chorus I heard Wren's laugh too, though I couldn't have said how I knew it.

"You're going to pay the toll," they said. "Aren't you."

"Yes."

"And take me back."

"All of you."

They nodded slowly. Then they reached into the pocket of that old burnt-cuffed coat and drew out something small—a stub of black wax candle, half-burned, its wick shaped wrong, bent like it had been pinched by desperate fingers.

"When you remember," they said, pressing it into my palm, closing my fingers over it, "you'll remember that the oath was sworn by fire. And you'll remember that fire can be sworn by anything that burns—including the thing that swears it. The King thinks contracts bind the soul. He's never once considered that a soul might rewrite itself faster than ink dries." They squeezed my hand, hard, and their eyes were bright and wet and mine. "Don't thank me. I still don't regret it. But I'm tired, Juniper. I'm so tired of being the only one who remembers."

Behind us, at the far end of the hall, doors I hadn't noticed swung open onto darkness, and a voice like courteous velvet rolled through the candlelight:

"*The petitioner grows sentimental. The final threshold is prepared.*"

Ash tugged my hand. When I looked down, the child was staring at the candle stub in my fist, and for the first time since the snowline, Ash spoke—one word, in that language older than kingdoms, soft as ashfall.

Somehow, impossibly, I almost understood it.

Almost.
