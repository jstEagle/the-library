## 18. The Burning Oath

The doors at the end of the hall were older than the rest of the court. That was the first thing I noticed about them, and I want it on the record that I noticed something sensible instead of simply running, because running had occurred to me. It had occurred to me several times over the past three weeks, usually at moments of maximum inconvenience, and every time it had lost the argument to some stubborn, unprofitable instinct that insisted I finish what I'd started.

The doors were black wood gone silver at the edges, carved with a script I now recognized—the language Ash spoke, the language beneath kingdoms—and they stood open onto a darkness that wasn't empty so much as *attentive*.

"The final threshold," I said to the velvet voice. "You'll forgive me if I've stopped finding your thresholds charming. The last one took my mother's kitchen. Before that, a face I apparently loved enough to sell a child's soul for, which, in my professional opinion as a former charm-seller, is a terrible exchange rate."

The Buried King did not answer immediately. He never did. He let silence do his negotiating for him, the way rich men let their lawyers.

Ash's hand tightened in mine. The child had spoken—one word, soft as ashfall—and I still didn't know what it meant. But I knew what it sounded like. It sounded like *don't*. Or possibly like *go*. Ash's language was economical that way; context did the heavy lifting.

"We can leave," I said to Ash, because someone in this candlelit mausoleum ought to say true things out loud. "Right now. We walk back up the mountain, we lose whatever memories are left to lose, and you keep—" I gestured vaguely at all of them, the small warm whole of the child standing next to me. "This. Whatever this is. You keep being you."

Ash looked up at me with those eyes that were too old for the face they lived in, and said nothing, because Ash couldn't say anything, because five years ago a version of me I didn't remember being had sold this child's voice to pay for a miracle I couldn't recall.

That was the arithmetic of it. That was always the arithmetic. Every road out of here ran through the same tollbooth: *them or me.*

"Juniper," said the King, from everywhere, the way he did when he wanted to remind us he was architecture as much as monarch. "The petitioner stands before the final door. Hear the terms."

"I'd love to," I said. "I've been waiting all week for terms. It's the one part of this court I understand."

***

The darkness beyond the doors was not, it turned out, darkness. It was a room, and the room was my own cellar.

Not the real one—I knew the real one, the cramped stone box under the Shop of Small Mercies where I kept pickling jars and refused to look at a certain section of wall. This was the cellar as memory keeps it: too large, too clear, every detail lit by a lamp that burned without flickering. The gate stood in the far wall, sealed, humming faintly, exactly as it had hummed the night everything happened.

And standing in front of it, holding a scrap of parchment that was already beginning to smoke at its edges, was me.

Younger me. Desperate me. The me from the mirror-hall, except here she wasn't a reflection arguing her case—here she was mid-act, caught in amber, doing the thing. Her hair was longer. Her hands shook. She was crying in that ugly, silent way of people who have run out of time to cry properly.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small in the vast remembered cellar. "Oh, that's not fair."

"It is the night," said the King, courteous as ever. "It is preserved entire. Look upon it, Juniper Halloran, and take back what is yours—all of it, every hour, every name, every reason. And the oath stands. Sealed. Burning. The child's soul remains in my keeping until the debt runs its course, and the debt will not run its course, because oaths sworn in blood do not expire. They only complete."

"And the other option?"

"You refuse the memory. Remain as you are—a hollow vessel with a kind heart and no history. And a hollow vessel cannot be bound, for the oath was sworn by a self who would no longer exist." A pause, silk over stone. "Break the oath, and the child goes free. You go free. Empty, but free."

There it was. The whole trap laid out flat, and the terrible elegance of it was that neither option was a trap at all. That was the thing about the Buried King I'd finally understood somewhere around the second threshold: he didn't cheat. He didn't need to. He just set the scales down in front of you and waited to see what you valued, and whatever you chose, he learned something worth more than gold.

A soul whole but enslaved. Or a soul free but gutted.

"Take your time," said the King, who had all of it.

***

Here is what nobody tells you about having holes in your memory: you build furniture around them.

For five years I'd built a life in Vell the way water builds around a stone—flowing neatly past the gap, pretending the gap was a design choice. I told myself I'd come to the city for the markets. I told myself the scar on my palm was from a kitchen accident. I told myself loneliness was safety, safety was virtue, and virtue was enough to live on.

It wasn't. I'd known it wasn't. That was the joke underneath everything, the one I'd never let myself finish: I hadn't erased my memories. Someone had *paid* to erase them, and the currency had been the truth, and I had agreed to the price because the alternative was carrying it.

Now the alternative was carrying it forever, or never carrying it at all.

I looked at young-me, shaking hands and smoking parchment, and I hated her with a completeness that surprised me—not because of what she'd done, but because she'd done it and then arranged for herself to forget, which meant even her guilt was something she'd sold rather than carried. And then, standing there in the lamplight of my own excavated heart, I understood her too, and the understanding was worse than the hate.

Because I could see, now, the shape of the person behind her. Not the face—that had gone through the second toll, paid and mourned—but the *weight* of them. The way young-me kept glancing toward the stairs, toward the sickroom above, toward someone dying by inches while she stood in a cellar learning the grammar of damnation. I knew that posture. I knew what it was to love someone whose breathing had become a clock.

She hadn't sold Ash's soul because she was cruel. She'd sold it because the Undercourt had come to *her*, the way it comes to everyone eventually, sliding a contract across the table at three in the morning when the doctors have run out of Latin and the candles are burning down to the quick, and it had said: *one soul, freely given, any soul, and they live.*

Any soul.

And she—young me, desperate me, me—had thought of the child who'd appeared at the shop door that same storm-week, barefoot, mute already, speaking a language no kingdom claimed. A child with no family, no name anyone could pronounce, no one to bargain for *them*. A child whose soul, the court assured her, would be kept safe. Kept warm. Kept in amber light, unharmed, until—

Until nothing. Until forever. Oaths don't expire.

"She didn't know it was wrong," I said quietly. "That's the worst part. She told herself it wasn't even wrong. Safekeeping. A loan. She'd find another way to free the child later. There was always going to be a later."

"There is always a later," agreed the King, "until there is only the oath."

I turned to Ash. The child stood at the edge of the remembered lamplight, half in shadow, watching the scene of their own sale with an expression I couldn't read—which was its own particular hell, because I'd spent three weeks learning to read this child, and here was the original text of every nightmare, playing on a loop, with me in the villain's role.

"Ash," I said. "Whatever happens in the next few minutes—I need you to know that I'm not choosing between you and the truth. Everyone in this room is going to try to make it look like that's the choice. It isn't. It's a lie with good posture."

Ash tilted their head. And then, deliberately, the child reached into the collar of their shirt and pulled out the charm bracelet—the cheap tin-and-thread thing I'd strung together at the snowline, worth maybe four coppers in Vell, the most powerful object on this mountain. Ash held it up between us.

*Love as lifeline.* Sera's phrase. The widow had a gift for making sentiment sound like tactics.

"Yeah," I said, and my throat hurt. "Yeah, okay."

I turned back to the King's attentive dark, back to young-me and her burning page, and I made the choice I had been walking toward since a storm-night knock on a shop door.

"I'll take the memories," I said. "All of them. Every hour, every name, every reason. Give me the whole rotten, beautiful weight of it."

The velvet voice was quiet for a moment. When it came again, I could have sworn—though I'd swear nothing else in this place—that it sounded almost disappointed. Almost.

"The oath stands," it said. "Sworn and sealed. Do you accept the restoration knowing this?"

"I accept it knowing everything," I said. "That's rather the point."

***

They say drowning people see their lives flash before their eyes. Nobody warns you that sometimes it's the reverse: your life flashes, and *you're* the flood.

It came all at once. Not gently, not in order—a dam breaking sideways, five years of stolen time hitting me with the force of a collapsed roof.

Wren.

Her name was Wren.

I went to my knees on the cold stone of a cellar that existed in two places at once, and I remembered her—remembered her laugh like a hinge that needed oiling, remembered her teaching me to graft charms into songlines, remembered three years of a shared bed above a failing shop in a city that hated magic, remembered the cough that started in autumn and became winter and became a verdict. Remembered the night the fever turned and the doctor packed his bag with that awful gentleness and Wren gripped my wrist with a hand gone bird-light and said, *Don't you dare make a deal, June. Promise me. Promise me you'll let me go.*

And I had promised.

And then I had gone down to the cellar anyway, because promises are what you make to people you expect to survive, and vows are what you make when you can't bear the alternative, and I had knelt before a gate I'd spent years refusing to look at and knocked—knocked!—like a neighbor, and the Undercourt had opened, and the bargaining had begun.

I remembered hating myself even then. I remembered telling myself *any soul, any soul, and the child won't even miss it, souls are abstract, souls are theology, Wren is warm and laughing and HERE* —and I remembered the exact moment that lie curdled, which was the moment the contract materialized and I saw, written in the old tongue I somehow half-read, that the soul to be given must be *named*, truly named, and I had looked at the sleeping child upstairs and known their true name without ever being taught it, because that's what the old language does, it tells you what things actually are.

Ash. Not the name I gave them. The name they came with. *Ember-in-the-dark,* roughly. The name meant something like *the last coal of a fire everyone else abandoned.*

I signed anyway.

God help me, I signed anyway, and the parchment caught fire in my hand, and the burn scar bloomed across my palm in the shape of a seal, and upstairs Wren's breathing eased for the first time in a month, and I sat on the cellar steps until dawn doing the mathematics of what I'd done and arriving, at every sum, at the same total: *unforgivable.*

So I did the only unforgivable thing left. I went back to the gate and I bought forgetting. Paid the court its price in truth. Walked out of my own history and closed the door, and built a little shop above it, and sold harmless charms, and called the emptiness peace.

The memories settled into me like silt after a flood. I knelt there, shaking, whole, and discovered that wholeness felt exactly like grief with better posture.

"Wren died anyway," I said. My voice sounded scraped. "Three years later. The bargain bought her time, and the time ran out, and she died in spring with the window open, and her last words were asking whether I'd kept my promise." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I lied to her. At the end. I said yes."

"You purchased five years," said the Buried King, gentle as a ledger. "Five springs. Five windows opened. The court considers this a satisfactory outcome. The child's soul has been kept in perfect condition, as stipulated."

"In perfect condition." I got to my feet. My legs held, which felt like a personal insult from my own body. "In a jar."

"In amber light," said the King, with something that in a human would have been called pride.

And there it was. Devastating clarity, delivered as promised. I understood young-me completely now—her terror, her love, her cowardice dressed as mercy, her sin of arithmetic, weighing a stranger's eternity against a beloved's afternoon. I understood her the way you understand a wound: intimately, uselessly, from the inside.

And I understood, with the same terrible clarity, why she'd hidden the half-burned oath under the floorboards instead of destroying it. Because some part of her had known. Some part of her had stood in that cellar at dawn, holding the smoking contract, and known that the woman who signed it was not a woman who could be trusted with a soul—and had left the evidence for whoever came after. For me. A message across the erasure, scratched in fire:

*Find this. Finish this. Undo what I was too much in love to undo.*

The self who signed the oath no longer exists. I'd said it myself, days ago, in a mirror-hall, arguing with my own ghost. I'd thought it was a technicality then.

It wasn't a technicality. It was the whole law.

"King," I said, and stood up straight, and held out my scarred palm like a merchant presenting credentials. "I've accepted your restoration. The memories are mine again, whole and entire. Which means the woman who swore your burning oath—the one who signed in fear at three in the morning, who believed souls were abstractions, who thought love was a currency she was entitled to spend—"

I looked down at Ash, at the last coal of an abandoned fire, standing steady in the lamplight with a four-copper bracelet on their wrist.

"—that woman is dead. I remember being her. I am not her. And an oath sworn by a self who no longer exists binds no one but a ghost."

The candles in the endless hall, every one of them, guttered at once.

"*Careful, petitioner,*" said the Buried King, and for the first time in five thresholds, the courtesy cracked down the middle like river ice. "*The oath burns. It burns in my keeping. Touch it and you burn with it.*"

"Then I'll burn," I said, and reached for the fire.
