## 20. The King's Last Bargain

The light landed in the snow like a bird settling on a branch.

Not a burst of fire, not a trumpet blast—nothing so theatrical as the Undercourt had been all night. Just a soft golden glow, knee-high in the drifts, humming the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended. And inside it, taking shape the way a photograph takes shape in a developing bath, was a face I had spent five years unable to remember and one hour unable to forget.

Tam.

Tam Voss, who used to steal my pens and return them with terrible poems written on the backs of receipts. Tam, whose laugh started in the shoulders before it ever reached the mouth. Tam, who had died of a fever that no charm in Vell could touch, and whose dying hand I had held while some younger, more desperate version of myself made arrangements beneath the mountain.

"Juniper," the light said, in Tam's voice, warm as bread. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," I said, because apparently even at the end of the world my mouth ran on its own budget. "He's a king made of candle wax."

Ash's fingers dug into my shoulder. She was staring at the light with an expression I couldn't read—not fear, not joy. Recognition, maybe. The dead had called to her all night in her own tongue; perhaps she knew this particular light the way you know a song you've never heard sung aloud.

Behind us, the mountain shook.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last few minutes happened fast and I want to get them right, the way you want to remember exactly how a bridge looked just before it fell.

We had run from the mirror-hall with the oath still burning in my hands—a scroll of names and prices unraveling into actual flame, feeding on itself, on me, on five years of carefully not-remembering. Behind us the Buried King had risen from his throne of stacked contracts, and for the first time his courtesy had cracked like glaze over bad clay.

"You cannot," he had said. Not *you may not*. *Cannot.* The grammar of a creature who has never once been refused.

"I just did," I'd said, and thrown the burning oath into the great candelabra at the hall's center, where it caught among ten thousand candles like gossip catching in a small town.

The amber jars had shattered in sequence, each one releasing a sound like a held breath finally let go. Souls poured upward through the stone in ribbons of light—the sold, the bargained, the forgotten children of old bargains—and the court began to come apart around them, because it turns out a kingdom built on collateral doesn't survive the debt being forgiven.

The King had watched his court dissolve with an expression I will carry to my grave: not rage. *Accounting.* He was recalculating. Even as the ceiling rained candle-wax and centuries, he was looking for the clause, the loophole, the final offer.

And then, as we fled up the winding stair toward the surface, his voice had followed us—not shouting, never shouting, courteous to the last, which was somehow worse:

"One bargain remains open, Keeper of Small Mercies."

I hadn't stopped running. But I'd heard him. Of course I'd heard him. That was the whole horror of the man; he understood that you always hear him.

* * *

Now the light stood in the snow, wearing Tam's face, and the King's voice came from everywhere—from the shaking ground, from the falling ash-snow, from somewhere behind my own ribs.

"The soul you purchased," he said, "was returned when the contract burned. As were all souls. This is the... inconvenience of mercy." A pause, the sound of a ledger page turning. "But I am not without generosity. Restore the original terms, and I will give you what you bought five years ago. Whole. Alive. Breathing. Walking out of this mountain beside you."

My heart did something complicated and painful.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't fade. It just gets quieter, the way a river gets quieter under ice. And underneath the ice, all this time, it had been saying the same thing it said the night I signed: *anything, anything, anything.*

"Juniper." Sera's hand closed around my arm. She had fought her way up the stair beside us, gray-haired and furious and magnificent, carrying Ash's soul-jar shard in her pocket like a talisman. "Don't listen. It's what they do. They show you the door out of your own grief and lock every other one."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" she asked gently. "Because I've seen that look. My sister's name was Maren. I would have burned this whole mountain in 1661 for half a chance at that look."

The light stepped closer. Tam's face wore the smile I remembered from market mornings, from rainy afternoons in the shop, from the fevered last week when the smile had been the only part of Tam still working properly.

"Ash," the light said, and turned those remembered eyes on the child. "Come here, little one. You're cold. We're all cold down there. But if you come back, if you let the contract stand, you'll be warm forever. Everyone warm together. Isn't that what you wanted? Someone to belong to?"

And Ash—brave, mute, five-years-old-and-ancient Ash—looked at the light for a long moment.

Then she spoke. Not in the dead tongue. In broken, halting, brand-new common speech, words she'd been assembling all night from listening to me and Sera argue our way up a mountain:

"No. Want *this*. Want *them*."

She pointed at me. At Sera. At the road down the mountain, toward Vell, toward a shop with a cellar and a crooked sign and a kettle that only worked if you kicked it first.

The light flickered.

"Oh, well argued," said the Buried King, and there was something almost like admiration in it, which made my skin crawl. "Children are poor negotiators but excellent judges. Very well. Then hear my true final term, Marchetti. Not the child. You."

The ground bucked. Somewhere below us, the court finished collapsing with a sound like a cathedral sighing.

"Your memories," the King said. "All of them. Every year since the bargain. Give them freely, and the soul returns to life regardless of the child. You will not remember why you are standing on a mountain. You will not remember the child, or the widow, or the shop. You will wake in Vell with a partner alive beside you and a hole where your heart's recent history should be. A fair trade. Cleaner than your first."

And there it was. The real trap. Not Ash's soul—mine. Because the King had finally understood what I'd understood in the mirror-hall, facing my younger self: the oath's power had lived in my forgetting. A self who remembers can break anything. A self who remembers nothing can be remade into anything.

"Juniper," Sera said. "Say no. Say it fast."

I looked at the light. At Tam's face, patient in the snowfall.

Here is what I wanted to say: *yes, take everything, I'll learn to love them again, I'll fall in love twice with the same person, people do it all the time.*

Here is what I actually understood, standing there with my heart hammering: the person offering me that trade was the same person who had signed away a child's soul rather than sit with an unfixable grief. Desperation wears many coats, but it's always the same animal underneath. Five years ago I had decided that a living Tam was worth any price someone else paid. If I said yes now, I'd be deciding that a living Tam was worth a price *I* paid—but paid with pieces of myself that belonged, in every way that mattered, to the people standing next to me. My memory of Ash teaching me the word for *home*. My memory of Sera's sister's name. My memory of choosing, tonight, to burn instead of bargain.

You can't give away the choosing and keep the chooser.

"Tam," I said quietly. "If you're in there anywhere—you'd hate this. You'd hate being the argument. You hated being anyone's reason. You told me once—" and the memory came clear and whole, the road-that-remembers giving it back to me like a gift— "you told me the worst thing a person can be is somebody's *price*."

The light wavered. For one moment the smile faltered and something older looked out—something tired, something that had waited five years in amber wanting, maybe, exactly this.

"Then don't pay," the light said, in a voice that was Tam's and also not, and also, at the very bottom, the King's. "Just walk away. Leave the soul on the mountain. It costs you nothing."

"That's the trick, though, isn't it," I said. "It costs *her* everything."

I knelt in the snow, and I held out my hand to the light, the way you hold out a hand to a bird.

"Come home or don't," I said. "But come as yourself, free, or not at all. No jars. No courts. No kings. That's the only offer on my table."

For a long moment nothing moved except the snow.

Then the light sighed—a sound like a jar cracking, like a breath held for five years—and it didn't rise toward heaven and it didn't sink back toward the court. It simply came apart, gently, into sparks that drifted over Ash's head like pollen, and I heard—Sera heard it too, she swore to it later—one word in a voice like warm bread:

*Home.*

And then it was gone, and the mountain was just a mountain, groaning and shedding stone, ordinary in its ruin.

* * *

We ran.

There's no poetic way to put it. The Undercourt's collapse had reached the roots of the mountain, and the pilgrim road was coming apart behind us in slabs and avalanches of ancient candle-smoke, and we ran like three people who had very recently remembered they liked being alive. Sera carried Ash on her back, swearing with a fluency that suggested her sister had been a bad influence in the best way. I ran behind, watching the dark mouth of the tunnel we'd descended through crumble shut forever, sealing the dead court under a thousand tons of honest rock.

Halfway down the snowline, Ash went wrong.

She made a small sound—not a cry, something thinner—and slid off Sera's back, and when I caught her she was translucent. Not metaphorically. I could see the stones through her hand. Her soul, freed from its jar, freed from its contract, was coming loose from her body like a kite string slipping a knot, drifting back toward the deep places that had held it so long it had almost learned to live there.

"Ash. Hey. Look at me." My voice did that thing voices do when they're pretending to be calm. "Stay. Stay *here*."

She looked at me with huge dark eyes and said, in the old tongue, one word I now understood perfectly: *pulling.*

The mountain was pulling her back. Or the habit of the mountain was. Five years in amber leaves a groove in a soul, and hers was sliding into it.

So I did the only thing I know how to do. I sat down in the snow on a collapsing mountainside, took her translucent hands in mine, and built a charm.

Not a licensed charm. Not a harmless charm. A real one—the kind I'd stopped making years ago, the kind that costs the maker something. I fed it everything the night had given me: the weight of the oath burning between my hands, the sound of ten thousand candles going out, the word *home* spoken in a voice like bread. I braided it into a bracelet of red thread and river-stone, the first magic I'd ever learned and the last one I'd ever need, and I tied it around her wrist.

"This isn't a leash," I told her. "It's a handrail. Hold on when it pulls. That's all it does. The rest of the staying—" I pressed her hand flat against my chest, where my heart was doing something embarrassing and sincere— "the rest of the staying, you do yourself. Deal?"

She nodded. The translucency slowed. Didn't stop—she still shimmered faintly, like a figure seen through summer heat—but it slowed, and her grip on my fingers had actual pressure again, and I decided, right there, that pressure was enough. Pressure was everything.

Sera hauled us both upright. "Move," she said. "Grieve later. Walk now. You can have your feelings at the bottom like a civilized person."

* * *

We came out of the foothills at dawn.

Of course we did. Mountains in stories always deliver you at dawn; it's practically contractual, and after the week I'd had I wasn't going to argue with a working contract for once.

Vell lay below in the early light, smoke rising from chimneys, the bell tower counting out seven, utterly unaware that the price of everything had changed overnight. From up here it looked exactly as it always had—gray walls, crooked roofs, the Magistry spire sticking up like a splinter. Home, in the way that ugly familiar things are home.

Ash tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the city, then at herself, then—carefully, testing each word like ice on a pond—said:

"We go... home? Real home?"

"Real home," I said. "Kettle needs kicking. You can do the honors. Consider it your first chore in your new country."

Her face did something I hadn't seen it do in all the weeks since she'd knocked on my door in the storm: it relaxed. All the way. The vigilance she'd worn like a second skin—the look of a child always listening for the mountain—just... set down, like a pack taken off at the end of a long walk.

Sera squeezed my shoulder. "You know the Magistry will have questions. Unlicensed magic, a collapsed mountain, a missing night. There'll be inquiries."

"There'll be tea," I said. "Inquiries can wait their turn."

"And the shop?"

I thought about the cellar. About the gate that had hummed for five years, that had opened a crack for a child's hand, that had led down and down to a court of candles and contracts. With the Undercourt gone, it was just stone now. Old, dumb, honest stone.

"It's got good bones," I said. "Everything else we'll rebuild."

We started down the road, three silhouettes against the sunrise, and behind us the mountain settled into silence with the finality of a book closing. Ash walked between us, holding a hand of each, her new bracelet bright at her wrist, her soul flickering softly like a lamp learning to burn steady.

She was quiet for most of the descent. Then, near the bottom, where the pilgrim road met the cart track and Vell's gates rose ahead of us, she stopped, frowned with the effort, and produced—carefully, deliberately, like setting down something precious—the first full sentence of her new life:

"My name... is Ash. But before... before mountain..." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and fierce and entirely her own. "Before, my name was Wren. I want... I want both. Is allowed?"

I crouched down so we were eye to eye, and I found that my own voice needed a moment to work.

"Wren and Ash," I said. "Both. Always both. Nothing about you gets erased in this family ever again."

She smiled—wide, gap-toothed, incandescent—and grabbed my hand, and pulled me toward the gates, toward the city, toward whatever came next.

And far behind us, so faint I might have imagined it, the mountain hummed once—like a struck bell, like a sealed gate, like something patient waiting to see what we would build on top of the ruins—

—and went quiet.
