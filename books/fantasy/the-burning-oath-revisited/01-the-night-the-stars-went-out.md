## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The hearth in the great hall of Crane House had not been lit in Ada's lifetime, and it had not been properly warm in his grandmother's. He knelt before it anyway, as his father had taught him, as his grandfather had taught his father — a small ritual performed each night on the eve of a binding, when a Crane was to be given to the valley and the valley given back.

He did not know what he was supposed to feel kneeling there. Reverence, perhaps. Or grief. The old words said both: *ash remembers what fire forgets.* But all Ada felt was the cold coming up through the flagstones into his knees, patient as a debt, and the strange hollowness of a room built for two hundred voices that now held only his breathing and the tick of the mantel clock.

Tomorrow he would swear the Oath of Keeping. Every Crane swore it, or had sworn it, back through the generations the ledger could count and further into the years the ledger only guessed at. He would kneel on the binding stone in the square, and Warden Ilse Vane would draw the circle of salt and iron around him, and he would speak the words his mother had spoken at nineteen, and her mother at twenty-two, and so on backward until the names blurred into legend: *to keep the border, to keep the silence, to keep the fire unlit and the dark unbroken.* In exchange the valley would keep him — feed him, shelter him, bury him under Crane soil with his name cut honest into slate.

It should have been a comfort. Everyone told him it was. His father spoke of the ceremony the way other men spoke of harvests, with a settled, seasonal pride. *You were born for this,* Da said, and meant it kindly, and Ada had never found the courage to say what he actually felt, which was that being born for something felt very little like choosing it.

He pressed his palm flat against the hearthstone. Cold. It was always cold. And yet tonight, for the first time, the cold felt less like absence than like *holding its breath.*

* * *

Ada rose and went out through the kitchen, past the racks of drying herbs his mother had hung before she died — thyme and yarrow gone brown as old paper, still hanging because no one had the heart to take them down — and out the low door into the yard.

Crane's Hollow at dusk was a bowl of lamplight. Forty households, give or take, tucked into the fold of the valley like coins in a purse. Beyond the last fence line the fields gave way to meadow, and beyond the meadow stood the border stones: twelve great standing rocks ringed with iron bands, older than any record, mossed and lichened gray-green, each one carved with spirals that no one in the valley could read anymore but everyone could recite the purpose of. *They keep the magic out,* the schoolmaster said. *They keep us safe.* The wards hummed in them day and night, a sound too low to hear and too constant to miss, like the valley's own heartbeat played just beneath the floor of the world.

Ada had grown up inside that sound the way fish grow up inside water. He had never once thought about it until tonight.

Because tonight the hum was wrong.

He stopped at the gate and tilted his head, listening the way you listen to a note held by an instrument you know well — and heard it waver. Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just a thinness in it, a flutter, the way a candle flame leans when a door opens somewhere far off in the house. The hum caught, stumbled, caught again.

Ada stood in the gate a long moment, one hand on the latch, telling himself it was the wind. The wind came down off the rim of the valley every evening right on schedule, smelling of pine and cold stone, and it made the grass hiss and the shutters knock. But wind didn't change the pitch of the wards. Wind had never once changed the pitch of the wards. His whole life, the hum had been the one thing in Crane's Hollow that never varied — more reliable than rain, more faithful than people.

Tonight it stuttered like a man trying to remember a word.

"Ada?" His father's voice from the doorway behind him, rough with pipe smoke. "You'll want your sleep. Long day tomorrow."

"I'm coming." He didn't move. "Da. Do you hear that?"

His father came out onto the step, and Ada watched him listen — watched the exact moment the older man heard it too, saw it land in his face like a bird striking glass. His father's jaw worked. "Wind," he said finally. "Old stones sing sometimes. Nothing to trouble yourself over the night before your binding."

But he said it looking at the border stones, not at Ada, and his hand had gone white on the doorframe, and Ada understood, with the sudden terrible clarity that arrives only on the edge of things going wrong, that his father was afraid.

* * *

Supper was mutton and barley bread and a silence shaped exactly like tomorrow. Ada's aunt Marta fussed with his collar. His cousin Tam cracked jokes about how the binding circle would make his legs cramp, and everyone laughed a beat too quickly, the laughter of people holding a rope together. His father drank one cup more than usual and stared into the middle distance where the ledger lay open on its stand, the family book with its two hundred years of names, waiting for tomorrow to add his.

*Ada Crane. Bound in keeping. Sworn beneath the stones.*

Afterward Ada climbed to his room under the eaves and sat at the window with the shutter open, because sleep would not come near him. The valley lay below in its scatter of lamps, going out one by one as households banked their fires. And above it all — this was the thing he counted on, the thing he had counted on every night of his life — the stars.

There were no stars in Crane's Hollow.

That was not quite true, and Ada knew it was not quite true, and it was one of those truths the valley kept folded away like linen too fine to use. There were stars; you could see them if you climbed to the rim of the valley on a bold night, faint and trembling beyond the ward-line, like lights seen through fogged glass. But inside the Hollow they never showed. The wards ate them. Two hundred years of sealed sky, and the children of the valley grew up knowing the stars the way they knew dragons: as stories. Pretty ones. Distant ones. Things that belonged to the world the border stones had been raised against.

His mother used to tell him otherwise. That was the heresy of his childhood, whispered at bedtime with her hand warm on his hair: that the stars weren't decoration, weren't distant campfires of the outer realm. That they were *counting*. That each one was a life, a burning, and that long ago — before the sealing — the sky over the valley had been so thick with them it looked like snowfall turned upward. She'd died when he was nine, and taken most of her stories with her, and left him only the habit of looking up.

So he looked up.

Midnight came across the valley the way it always did — the church bell tolling slow and bronze, twelve strokes rolling from wall to wall of the Hollow and dying in the pines. On the twelfth stroke, Ada was watching the place where the stars should have been, the black seam of sky between the peaks, and he saw—

Nothing.

That was the horror of it. Not clouds. Clouds had texture, movement, the gray belly-light of the moon behind them. This was nothing — the sky simply *ended*, the way a sentence ends, mid-word. One moment there was the faint starlight that even the wards let leak through, the dim milkiness he had looked at ten thousand nights without gratitude. The next, it was gone, all of it, everywhere, as if a hand had passed over the whole bowl of heaven and pinched it out.

And the hum stopped.

Not faltered. *Stopped.* The silence that fell was so total Ada could hear his own pulse in his ears and the blood moving in his eyes when he blinked. Two hundred years of the valley's heartbeat, ended between one toll of the bell and the next. He gripped the windowsill hard enough to hurt. Somewhere below, dogs began to howl — not the alarmed barking of foxes, but the low, continuous, keening howl of animals that can hear something human ears cannot, dozens of them, all across the valley, all at once, a chorus rising like water coming to a boil.

Then the light came.

It came from the border stones. From his window Ada could see the far ring of them, or rather see *around* them, because they had begun to glow — a dull red heat blooming in the spiral carvings, deepening, brightening, the color of iron pulled from a forge. Twelve stones, miles apart, all kindling at once. And then, faintly, carried up the valley on the dead-still air, a sound that Ada felt in his teeth before he understood it with his mind:

Cracking.

Ice cracks on the pond in late winter — that was the sound, magnified, made monstrous. Deep structural groans, then sharp reports, then long shrieking fissures running through something enormous. The border stones were breaking. Not crumbling the way old things crumble. *Shattering*, the way ice shatters when the thaw comes all at once, from underneath, all along the shore.

Down in the square, doors were opening. Lamplight spilled yellow across the cobbles. Voices lifted, confused, frightened — his father's among them, calling his name, calling for the warden, calling for anyone who knew what the words were, what the procedure was, because there was no procedure. No Crane alive had ever seen the wards fail. The valley had been built on the promise that they never would.

Ada ran. Down the stairs three at a time, past his father in the doorway — *"Ada, get back inside!"* — out into the night, barefoot, half-dressed, drawn toward the square where the whole valley was converging in its nightclothes, forty households' worth of fear packed shoulder to shoulder under a sky with no stars in it.

Warden Ilse Vane was already there. She stood at the center of the square beside the binding stone, and she was the stillest person in the crowd, which frightened Ada more than all the screaming dogs combined. Ilse Vane did not go still when she was calm. She went still when she was calculating. Her gray eyes moved across the glowing border stones, across the empty sky, and then — Ada saw it happen — down, to the ground itself, to the cobblestones of the square, which had begun, very faintly, to tremble.

"Away from the center," she said quietly, and the crowd quieted to hear her, and then she said it again, louder, and began pushing people back with her hands. "Away from the center. *Now.* Move."

Ada did not move fast enough. Nobody did. Because the trembling became a shaking, and the shaking became a roar, and directly overhead — in the dead black seam where the stars had been — the sky opened.

Not broke. *Opened*, the way a door opens, a clean vertical wound in the night, edged in the same forge-red as the border stones, widening with a sound like the world's largest lock turning. And through it, falling, tumbling, trailing fire the way a comet trails its tail, came something enormous.

Ada stood in the center of the square with the whole valley screaming around him and did not run. Later he would try to explain this — to Sef, to himself, to the ledger if it ever asked — and the truest answer he ever found was this: it was falling *toward* him. Not toward the valley. Toward him. He could feel it, the way you feel eyes on your back, a vast attention narrowing through all that burning dark to a single point, and the point was a barefoot boy named Crane standing on the binding stone he was supposed to swear his life away on come morning.

The ember — though he had no word for it yet, no category large enough — came down out of the breach trailing a tail of ash and gold, growing as it fell, filling the sky, filling his vision, and the last thing Ada Crane saw of the only world he had ever known was the faces of his neighbors lit orange and upturned, and the last thing he heard was his father's voice cracking on his name —

and then the fire took him.
