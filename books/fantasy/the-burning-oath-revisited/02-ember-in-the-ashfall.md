## 2. Ember in the Ashfall

The thing that fell from the broken sky did not fall like a stone. It fell like a word — a single syllable of fire, spoken once, and then silence.

Ada had been running since midnight. He had left the cold hearth with its banked coals and the ceremony robes folded on his bed, and he had run through streets that smelled wrong, through air that tasted of iron and endings, toward the border stones at the valley's edge because someone had to see, someone had to know, and no one else in Crane's Hollow would come. The elders had shuttered their windows. His aunt had lit every candle in the house as if wax could hold back what was coming. But Ada had been raised on stories of the wards the way other children were raised on lullabies, and he knew — had always known, in the secret chamber of his heart where he kept the things he could not say aloud — that walls were only ever as honest as the hands that built them.

The border stones stood in their ancient ring above the millpond, each one taller than two men, carved with oaths so old the letters had gone soft as riverbed clay. All his life they had hummed. Not sound, exactly — more a pressure behind the teeth, a feeling like standing near a struck bell that never finished ringing. Tonight the hum was dying. He could feel it going out of them one by one, the way warmth leaves a body: first the feet, then the hands, then the place where the heart used to be.

He pressed his palm against the nearest stone. It was cold. It had never been cold.

"Please," he said, to the stone, to the valley, to whatever kept the wards alive. "Please don't."

The stone answered by splitting. A crack ran up its face with a sound like lake ice breaking in spring, sharp and bright and final, and Ada staggered back as fine dust breathed out of the fracture — not grit but something finer, something that glittered, the powdered light of two hundred years of keeping. Through the crack he could see the sky beyond the valley, and it was wrong. The stars were gone. Not hidden behind cloud — *gone*, plucked out, leaving a black so complete it looked less like night than like a wound.

And then the ember came down.

It came through the breach trailing fire the color of old blood and new gold, and it did not fall so much as *reach* — a long burning arm extending across the valley square, across the millpond, across the frozen gardens, until it found him. Later he would understand that it had been searching, that a dying dragon's last ember knows what it is looking for the way a drowning man knows which way is up. But in that moment there was no understanding. There was only light filling the world, and heat that did not burn so much as *claim*, and a voice that was not a voice saying a word that was not a word, and Ada Crane standing alone at the edge of everything he had ever known with his hand half-raised, as if he had meant to catch it all along.

The ember struck his open palm.

It should have killed him. He knew that afterward, when there was an afterward to know things in. Fire that old, that full of a whole life's burning, does not land gently on mortal flesh. But instead of consuming him it *sank* — sank into his palm and through it, into the meat and bone and further, into some part of him that had no name before that night, and it fused there, a coin of white-gold heat sealed beneath the skin, beating like a second heart.

The wards screamed. There was no other word for it. Every remaining stone in the ring cried out at once, a harmonic shriek that cracked windows in the village and made the millpond shudder into waves, and then — one after another, like a row of candles snuffed by a passing wind — the border stones went dark. Two hundred years of isolation ended in the space of eleven heartbeats, and Ada counted them, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

Then the world pulled.

Not the ember. The *world*. The breach was not a hole; it was a hunger, and hunger that has waited two centuries does not nibble. The air bent toward the opening in the sky, and the ground tilted though it did not move, and Ada felt himself sliding — boots skidding on frost-hard grass, fingers clawing at soil that crumbled like old bread — toward the wound overhead. He grabbed the split border stone with both hands. Its cold bit through his sleeves. Its ancient carved letters pressed against his palms, the oaths of his ancestors, the covenant he was supposed to swear tomorrow, the life chosen for him before his birth.

The stone broke apart in his grip.

He remembered thinking, in the last fraction of a second, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter: *I never swore anything yet. Whatever takes me cannot say I forswore myself.*

Then the dark took him, and the ember in his palm burned brighter than the sun he could no longer see, and Ada Crane fell out of the world.

* * *

He woke to ash falling on his face.

Softly, the way snow falls, except it was grey and warm and tasted of cinders when it touched his lips. He lay still for a long time, listening to himself breathe, cataloguing his body piece by piece the way his mother had taught him to catalogue fear — name it, and it becomes smaller. His skull: intact. His ribs: aching but unbroken. His right hand: *wrong*. He lifted it above his face and the breath went out of him.

The ember sat in his palm like a captive star. It was smaller now, no bigger than a robin's egg, but it pulsed with a slow, deep rhythm — bright, dim, bright, dim — and with each pulse he felt something move in his chest in answer, some second pulse layered under his own. The skin around it was not burned. It was *changed*: smooth and faintly golden, as though the flesh had been fired like clay and come out of the kiln as something slightly other than what went in. When he tried to close his fingers, the ember closed with them. When he tried to open his hand flat, it flattened too. It was not resting on his palm. It was *of* his palm.

"No," he said aloud, to it, to the ash-grey sky, to no one. "No, I didn't swear. I didn't agree to anything."

The ember pulsed twice, quick and bright, and though it made no sound, Ada understood — the way you understand a tone of voice without hearing words — that it disagreed.

He sat up. The world around him was a country made of grief. Ash fields rolled away in every direction, dunes of fine grey powder sculpted by winds into shapes like sleeping animals, and here and there the wind had uncovered things beneath: the vast ribcage of a creature larger than the meetinghouse, arched over a hollow of drifted ash like the frame of a burned cathedral; a slope of ground that resolved, the longer he looked, into a shoulder the size of a hillside, scaled in plates gone dull as old pewter; a forest of what he first took for dead trees until he saw they were spines, a ridge of vertebrae marching over a rise and vanishing into the grey distance. Everything was enormous and everything was still and everything was *dead*, or nearly enough, and the sky above it all was the same starless wound he had seen through the cracking ward — a black so total that it seemed less like absence of light than presence of its opposite.

This was the outer realm. This was the place the wards had been built to keep out. He had been taught its name only in whispers: the Ashlands, where magic ran loose and unbound, where oaths were chains and promises had teeth, where a child who wandered past the border stones would be unmade before supper. He had imagined it his whole life as a kind of teeth. Instead it was this: silence, and ash, and a beauty so desolate it hurt to look at, the way it hurts to look at the face of someone you loved who has died.

Because that was what he was looking at. He understood it suddenly, wholly, the knowledge arriving not through thought but through the ember in his hand, which warmed when he turned toward the great ribcage and cooled when he turned away. These were not ruins. These were *bodies*. The Ashlands were a graveyard, and the graveyard was the size of a world, and every star that had vanished from the sky at midnight had been a soul rising out of a body like this one, leaving the fire-forges below them dark.

His mother's voice came back to him from years ago, telling him the story she told every winter solstice: *Long ago, before the sealing, our family kept faith with the dragon-clans, and the dragons kept faith with us, and the valley's hearths burned with borrowed fire.* She had always stopped there. He had always assumed the rest was lost to time. Now, kneeling in the ash of creatures his family had sworn to serve, Ada began to suspect the story hadn't been lost at all. It had been *cut*.

The ember flared, sudden and urgent, and pulled — not his body this time but his attention, dragging his gaze eastward across the ash fields to where the grey horizon met the black sky. Something was moving there. At first he thought it was wind lifting a veil of ash, but veils of ash do not move with such purpose, such terrible patience, such *direction*. It was coming toward him. Whatever it was, it had seen the ember's light — his light, the beacon fused to his hand that he could not drop, could not smother, could not hide — and it was crossing the dead fields with the unhurried certainty of something that has hunted before and always finished.

Ada got to his feet. His legs shook. The ash gave strangely underfoot, soft as flour, and every step raised small grey ghosts around his ankles. He looked for shelter and found none; he looked for cover and found only ribcages, open archways of bone that would hide nothing from anything. He looked for home and remembered, with a lurch, that home was now on the far side of a wound in the sky that had already closed behind him — or had it? He twisted around, searching the black vault overhead for any seam, any scar, any thread of gold where the breach had swallowed him, and found nothing. The sky was seamless. The sky was a lid.

"I didn't swear," he said again, quieter now, and the ember in his palm pulsed once, slow and almost gentle, and he could have sworn — in the language without words that it spoke — that it said: *You will.*

The shape on the horizon resolved as it came closer, and what he saw made no sense, and he stared at it anyway because staring was all he had. It moved like a rider but rode nothing. It wore a cloak the color of wet slate, and where a face should have been there was a pale oval of light, featureless, tilted downward as though reading the ash for tracks. And it was not alone. Behind it, spread across the eastern dark in a wide crescent, came others — a dozen, two dozen, silent as falling ash themselves, sweeping the graveyard in a line, the way his uncle swept the fields for dropped coins: thoroughly, patiently, certain that what was lost would be found.

They had not seen him yet. The ember chose that moment to pulse — bright, very bright, a flare of gold that lit the ash around him like sunrise — and Ada watched the pale faces turn, all together, all at once, like flowers finding the sun.

Toward him.

He ran. There was nothing else. He ran across the ash of giants with a dying star chained to his hand announcing him to the dark, and the ash dragged at his boots, and his breath tore at his throat, and somewhere behind him the riders began to move faster, gliding rather than walking, closing like a hand. Ahead, the great ribcage rose out of the drifts, and Ada put his head down and aimed himself at its shadow, at the hollow of bones big enough to swallow a village, and he did not let himself think about how much it looked like a trap, or how much it looked like a grave.

He was three steps from the shadow when something moved inside it.

A figure detached itself from the darkness between the ribs — human-shaped, ragged, fast — and a hand clamped over Ada's mouth before he could cry out, and a voice he had never heard said, close against his ear, low and fierce and shaking with something that might have been desperation:

"Don't scream. They track the living, not the light. Breathe when I breathe, move when I move, and whatever that thing in your hand tells you to do—" the figure's eyes found the ember, and widened, and the voice changed entirely, "*—don't do it.*"

Above them, the pale faces of the riders slid past the arching ribs, close enough that their light washed grey across the bone, and Ada Crane, swordspeaker of a fallen house in a realm of ash, held his breath and learned the first lesson of the outer world:

Nothing here was what the stories said. Including, perhaps, the person whose hand was over his mouth — and including, most of all, himself.
