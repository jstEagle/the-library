## 3. The Warden's Road

They traveled by night, because the ash was kinder in the dark.

Ada learned this on the first morning, when the pale sun rose over the bone-arches and the ground began to breathe — a slow, sick exhalation of heat that shimmered up through the grey drifts and made the horizon swim like water. Sef, whose name he had finally wrung out of them somewhere past the third rib-cathedral, simply stopped walking and sat down in the shadow of a fallen vertebra the size of a granary.

"We rest when it breathes," they said. "We walk when it holds its breath."

"That's not how breathing works."

"Nothing here is how anything works." Sef pulled their hood back. In daylight Ada could see them properly for the first time: a face weathered to leather, maybe forty years, maybe sixty — the outer realm did strange arithmetic with people — and eyes the color of old iron, ringed with a faint scarification that followed the jawline like a chain of small linked marks. Oath-chains, Ada realized. Scarred into the skin. He had seen something similar once, in his grandmother's oldest book, in an illustration the valley's teachers had always skipped past quickly.

"You're marked," he said.

"I'm *sentenced*," Sef corrected. "There's a difference. Marked means you kept your oath. Sentenced means your oath kept *you*, and then it broke, and you were left holding the pieces." They flexed their fingers; the scars moved with them, faintly luminous in the ash-light. "Walk. The Warden's hounds run cold trails at dawn, and yours is the coldest thing in this whole realm."

Ada walked. The ember rode fused against his palm, warm as a sleeping bird, and all that day it said nothing — but he had begun, shamefully, to listen for it. To wait for it. On the second night it pulsed once when they passed a dead forge-shrine, and Sef saw him flinch and said nothing, but wrote something down in a little grease-black notebook, and Ada understood that everything he did was being measured against some standard he didn't know existed.

On the fourth day, the riders found their trail anyway.

* * *

Ilse Vane had loved Marion Crane for eleven years, and had buried her love twice: once when Marion married into the covenant, and once, three years later, when the wards took her the way the wards took everyone the valley decided it could not afford to lose.

She did not think about that now. She thought about the trail.

The boy — and he was still a boy, whatever the ember had done to him, whatever the council's frantic divinations claimed — had crossed the breach and gone north-northeast through the ashfields, which meant he was either hopelessly lost or following the ember's pull toward the Last Forge. Both possibilities were bad. A stolen ember in untrained hands was a fuse looking for its powder. And if he reached the Forge before she reached him—

"Track's fresh, Warden." Dagny, her second, crouched over a print in the grey glass where the ash had fused. "Alone. Small stride. He's limping."

"He's been walking four days in boots made for a valley floor." Ilse swung down from her pale-faced courser and knelt beside the print. Around them the ashland stretched silent, ribbed with the bones of dragons two centuries dead, and above it all hung the starless sky — the wound in the world that had opened the night the wards cracked, the night every light in heaven went out at once. She had looked up at that empty black for three nights now and felt something in her chest she refused to name. Grief, perhaps. Or recognition.

The stars had not died naturally. She had known that for thirty years. She had helped make sure no one else knew it.

"Warden?" Dagny said.

"He'll head for the shrine at Karst Hollow." Ilse stood, brushing ash from her grey coat. "The ember will drag him there whether he wants to go or not. It remembers its forges." She remounted. "And we'll be waiting."

Dagny hesitated. "The council's orders were to take him alive if possible."

"The council's orders," Ilse said, "were written by men who have never left the valley. If the boy has sworn anything out here — even accidentally — alive may be the most dangerous thing he can be." She turned her courser north, toward the hollow, toward the place where she had once stood beside Marion Crane under a sky still full of stars, and heard her say: *If it ever comes to it, Ilse, promise me you'll tell my son the truth before you tell him the lie.*

She had promised. She had never decided which one was which.

"Ride," she said.

* * *

Sef caught him at dusk on the fifth day — caught him *again*, though this time Ada hadn't been running from anything except the ache in his feet.

He had wandered twenty paces from their camp to wash his hands in a melt-pool of black water, and when he came back the camp was wrong. Sef was standing very still in the middle of it, facing three figures who had come out of the ash without sound: tall, wrapped in oath-grey, their faces hidden behind masks of hammered tin stamped with a sigil Ada knew — a circle broken by a straight line. The seal of Crane's Hollow. It was carved over his own front door.

"Sef," one of the figures said, and the voice was wrong too — layered, like several people speaking through one throat. "You are recalled. Your sentence is commuted. Bring the ember-bearer to the Warden at Karst Hollow and your chains come off."

Ada watched something happen in Sef's face. Hope, first — naked, terrible hope, the kind that makes a person look younger and older at once. Then calculation. Then, briefly, something that might have been shame.

"And if I don't?"

"Then the sentence stands. And the bearer dies, and we take the ember from his ashes. The Warden is not particular about condition."

Ada's hand closed around the ember without his deciding to. It flared — hot, sudden, almost angry — and all three masked figures took a half-step back, and Sef laughed, a short ugly sound.

"There. You see? It's already chosen him, and it doesn't choose twice." But their eyes, flicking to Ada, said something entirely different: *run.*

He ran.

He made it eleven steps before the ash itself rose up and took him — chains of compacted grey, hardening out of the ground like the world's own oath closing around his ankles, his wrists, his throat. Not tight. Just present. The way, he thought distantly, a promise is present.

The masked figures converged. And it was Sef who knelt beside him in the settling ash, gripped his chin, and turned his palm upward to the fading light — and went absolutely still.

The mark had changed. Where before it had been a simple burn-scar shaped like a spark, it now showed fine lines radiating outward, deliberate as engraving. A sigil. And Ada watched Sef read it the way a priest reads scripture, watched the iron-colored eyes widen, watched the sentence-scars along their jaw begin, very faintly, to glow.

"That's not a bond-mark," Sef whispered. "That's a *covenant mark*. Full rite. That hasn't been—" They stopped. Looked up at the masked figures, who had also gone still. Then back down at Ada, and their voice dropped to almost nothing, meant for him alone:

"Boy. Do you know what your family actually swore?"

"What we've always sworn." His voice came out steadier than he felt, which surprised him. "The Cranes sealed the valley. We keep magic out so it can't hurt anyone. Two hundred years of—"

"Of what?" Sef's grip tightened. "Say the rest of it. Say what the sealing was *for*. Say the words of the covenant, the actual words, the ones they make you memorize for your binding ceremony."

And Ada opened his mouth to recite them — *by stone and silence, by hearth and border, we keep the fire from the innocent* — and found, with a lurch like the ground dropping away, that he couldn't. Not because he'd forgotten. Because now that he stood inside the outer realm with a dragon's ember fused to his hand and a covenant sigil burning on his skin, the words sounded different. Thin. Like a fence built around an empty lot and called a fortress.

"They sealed witnesses in," Sef said softly, while the masked figures waited and the starless sky pressed down and the ember beat against Ada's palm like a second heart. "That's what the original stone says. I've read it. I was sentenced for reading it. The Cranes didn't keep magic out of the valley, Ada Crane. They kept the last witnesses to what your family did locked inside it — and they fed the last dragon-fire a lie for two hundred years, and lies gutter, and now the stars are going out one by one, and your family's great covenant is a wick burning down to nothing."

"That's a lie." His voice cracked on it. "My mother—"

"Your mother," said one of the masked figures, in that layered voice, "is not here to defend herself. Come willingly, ember-bearer. The Warden waits."

And Ada, chained in the ash of a dead world, holding a fire that had chosen him, looked at Sef — at the sentenced oath-keeper who had saved him twice and lied to him never, as far as he could tell — and felt the first true crack run through everything he had ever believed about the name on his own door.

Not the crack of breaking. He would understand that later.

The crack of something opening.

"Karst Hollow, then," he said, and let them lift him to his feet. Above the bone-arches, in the absolute black of the sky, something vast and distant began — very faintly — to glow.
