## 4. What the Covenant Kept

Sef had walked this road a hundred times and never once with company.

The ashlands gave way to scrub and then to something like forest — trees she had no name for, their leaves silvered on the underside so that when the wind moved through them they flashed like fish turning in shallow water. Ada walked half a pace behind her, the way prisoners did, though she had cut his bindings two days ago and he had not run. She kept waiting for him to run. It would have been simpler.

The ember-mark on his palm had not dimmed. That was the thing she could not stop looking at. Embers faded; that was what embers did. They cooled, they grayed, they became the ash everything here eventually became. But the mark on Ada Crane's hand burned steady as a lamp-wick, and every mile it burned steadier, as if it were not dying but *arriving*.

"You keep looking at my hand," Ada said.

"I keep looking at the road."

"You're looking at my hand."

Sef said nothing. The boy was perceptive in the way of people who had never needed to be — who had grown up safe behind wards, where noticing things was a pastime rather than a survival skill. It should have annoyed her. Instead it made her chest ache in a place she had thought long since scarred over.

They crested a ridge at dusk, or what passed for dusk under a sky empty of stars — a thickening of the dark, a bruise deepening — and Sef stopped.

"There," she said.

Below them, sunk into a fold of hills like a coin dropped into soft wax, lay the forge-shrine. Even ruined it was beautiful, and that was the cruelty of it. Its walls had been white once; now they were bone-colored, cracked, furred with pale lichen that glowed faintly when the wind touched it. The great chimney had fallen sideways and lay across the courtyard like a felled tree. And at the center, visible even from the ridge because it caught whatever light remained and refused to let it go, stood the covenant stone.

Ada made a sound low in his throat. Not a word. Something older than words.

"You've seen it before," Sef said. "In your valley. A copy, or a fragment. Your family would have kept one."

"We keep a stone." His voice had gone careful. "It says the covenant protects the valley. *The fire without shall not enter; the peace within shall not be broken.* I've read it since I could read."

"Come read the original," Sef said, and started down the slope, because if she looked at his face any longer she would lose her nerve, and she had promised herself — walking out of the cinder-camps three years ago with her oath-keeper's brand split down the middle and her name struck from the rolls — that she would never again do a hard thing halfway.

* * *

Up close the shrine smelled of cold iron and old smoke, a smell so faint it might have been memory. Sef's boots crunched on slag. Somewhere in the walls, wind found gaps and played them like a sad, enormous flute.

Ada stood in the courtyard and turned slowly, taking it in, and Sef watched him understand — watched it land on him in stages, the way grief always landed, in installments.

"This is a forge," he said. "A real one."

"The first one. The mother-forge. Every dragon-fire in the outer realm was lit from this hearth, and every hearth-fire in your valley too, though your people have forgotten that. Fire remembers where it comes from even when people don't." She crossed to the covenant stone. "Your ancestors came here. They stood where you're standing. They swore."

He approached the stone the way a man approaches a sleeping animal — slowly, palm out, though there was nothing in either palm but the mark. The stone stood twice his height, black as frozen tar, and its surface was carved edge to edge with script that predated the alphabet Sef had been taught. But beneath the old script, added later, in letters she knew: the covenant. The whole of it. Not the excerpt.

"Read it," she said. "All of it. Out loud. It needs a voice. It's been waiting two hundred years for a Crane's voice."

Ada bent close. His lips moved first silently, shaping the familiar opening — *the fire without shall not enter* — and Sef saw the exact moment his eyes reached the line that did not exist in the valley's copy. Saw his shoulders stop. Saw him go still the way prey goes still.

"Read it aloud," she said again, gently. "Lies keep better in silence."

His voice, when it came, was thin.

"*...and the witnesses within shall remain witnesses, until such time as the truth can be borne.*" He straightened. Turned. His face had gone the color of the lichen. "Witnesses. Witnesses to *what?*"

"To the forging." Sef put her hand flat against the stone. It was warm. Two hundred years sealed away from all magic, and the stone was still warm, because oaths do not obey borders; oaths are borders. "The covenant wasn't a wall built to keep dragons out, Ada. It was a wall built to keep *people* in. Your valley isn't a sanctuary. It's a vault. Your family didn't seal magic away from Crane's Hollow — they sealed the last living witnesses to the original oath inside it, generation after generation, so that no one outside could ever hear what was actually sworn."

"That doesn't—" He laughed, one broken note. "That's insane. My grandmother—"

"Told you stories. Yes. So did mine, about the honorable Cranes, the swordspeakers, the shield of the realm." Sef heard her own voice flatten into something bitter and did nothing to soften it. "I trained eleven years to keep oaths, Ada. Do you know what an oath-keeper does when an oath turns out to be rotten at the root? Nothing. There is no procedure for it. You either keep a lie or break yourself. I broke myself. They split my brand and cast me out, and I have spent three years trying to find the bottom of this, and I have finally found it, and it's you."

"Me?"

"Your blood. The Cranes weren't protectors. They were *wardens of a secret*. And the secret is this:" She took a breath, because she had rehearsed this speech in a hundred camps and a hundred ditches and it had never once come out the way she planned. "The original covenant bound the dragon-clans to feed the Last Forge with their own souls. Voluntarily. Freely given — that was the sacred part, that was what made the fire clean. In exchange, the Cranes swore guardianship, honesty, and return: when the dragons' need came due, the Cranes would open the way and give back what was given. The stars going out — the souls burning down — that was always part of it. They were spent, not lost. Held in trust. Waiting for the day the debt came due."

"And the debt came due," Ada said quietly. "And the stars went out."

"And the Cranes sealed the valley instead." The words came out of her like teeth. "Two hundred years ago. Your ancestor — the first Ada, the swordspeaker whose echo still haunts the deep places — looked at the bill that had come due and chose to lock the door. He rewrote the covenant. He fed the copy to his children and buried the original here, where only someone carrying a true ember could ever reach it. And the Last Forge has been guttering ever since, because a forge fed by a freely-given gift cannot run on stolen goods. The fire knows. Fire always knows."

The wind moved through the silver-leaved trees. The lichen pulsed. Ada had not moved. He stood with his marked palm hanging at his side, and Sef watched the ember-light pulse in time with something — his heartbeat, or the stone's, or both, keeping time together like two drums finding each other across a distance.

"My mother," he said at last. His voice was very controlled, which was worse than shouting. "She used to take me to the border stones. She'd press her hand against them and say, *someday you'll understand why we stay.* I thought she meant duty. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "She knew. Didn't she. She knew what the valley was."

"I think she suspected. I think she was trying to find a way to tell you." Sef hesitated. This was the part she had never said aloud to anyone, the part that had cost her everything, and saying it now felt like pulling a splinter out along the grain. "Ilse Vane loved her. Did you know that?"

Ada's head came up sharply.

"Ilse was young then, already Warden-track, already brilliant. Your mother came to her — I've read the fragments, the seized correspondence — and asked her to help open the border. Quietly. One night, one breach, one chance to bring the truth out. And Ilse—" Sef's jaw tightened. "Ilse reported her. Not by name. But she reported the *attempt*, and the attempt was buried deeper, and the wards were strengthened, and your mother spent the rest of her life behind a wall she'd tried to open, raising a son to be proud of the wall." She met Ada's eyes. "Ilse Vane has been protecting the lie for thirty years because protecting it was the only way she could live with what she did to the woman she loved. Remember that, when she comes for you. Because she will come for you."

Ada sat down. Just — sat, right there on the cracked flagstones, in the ruins of the mother-forge, with his elbows on his knees and his marked hand open between them, staring at the small steady flame in his own flesh.

"I practiced my oath-binding speech," he said. "For the ceremony. Three hundred times. *I am Ada Crane, and I speak for the valley.*" He laughed again, and this time it went on too long and ended badly. "Speak for the valley. The valley that's a prison. The name I was supposed to carry forward is a — it's a *lock*, Sef. My whole inheritance is a lock."

"Yes."

"And everyone I ever loved was either a prisoner or a jailer."

"Yes." She crouched beside him, awkwardly, because comfort had never been a skill the oath-keepers taught. "I'm sorry. I've been waiting two days to tell you and I'm sorry anyway."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, something in his voice had changed temperature — the grief was still there, but underneath it, banked and growing, was heat.

"The stone says *until such time as the truth can be borne.*" He looked up at her, and his eyes had ember-light in them now, reflected or borrowed or born. "Borne by whom?"

"By a Crane. With a true bond. Speaking in their own voice." Sef rose and held out her hand. "There's a court of the dragon-clans three days east. Remnants. Survivors. They've spent two centuries debating whether to die with dignity or serve the descendants of liars, and they will test you with every oath your family ever swore, and you will fail all of them, because those oaths are lies."

"Then why go?"

"Because failing their tests honestly is worth more than passing them falsely. And because one of them carries the last coal from the Last Forge — and coal answers to truth, not to blood." She closed her fingers around his wrist, above the mark, and felt the ember leap toward her touch like a dog recognizing a friend. "Come on, swordspeaker. Time to find out what your name is worth when you stop inheriting it."

Far to the east, above the dead hills, the starless dark seemed to lean closer — and somewhere beneath it, Sef knew, something ancient had just felt the ember move, and begun, at long last, to hope.
