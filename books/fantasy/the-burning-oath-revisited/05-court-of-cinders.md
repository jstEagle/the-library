## 5. Court of Cinders

The road to the cinder-court was paved with the bones of promises.

Ada felt it in his feet before he understood it with his mind — the way the ground beneath the ash gave slightly, springy as old thatch, and how Sef walked it without looking down, the way a person walks through a graveyard they've grown used to. Three days out from the forge-shrine, the ashfields had begun to change. The grey had deepened to black, then to something darker than black, shot through with veins of dull red like coals banked under snow. Heat rose through the soles of Ada's boots in slow pulses, steady as a heartbeat, and he realized, with a lurch, that it *was* a heartbeat. The land itself was still alive down there, still burning, however faintly.

"The court sits on the last warm vein," Sef said, not turning around. Their voice was hoarse from days of silence; they spoke the way people drop coins into a well, sparingly, listening for the splash. "The dragons gather where the fire remembers them."

"And if they don't remember me kindly?"

Sef glanced back. In the starless dark their face was all shadow and one pale line of jaw. "They won't remember you at all, swordspeaker. That's worse. You're going to stand in front of creatures older than your valley's stones and be *nothing* to them. A smudge. An afterthought wearing a dead family's mark." They turned forward again. "Try not to take it personally. I didn't."

"You said 'didn't.'"

"I did." A pause, long enough for three heartbeats of the burning ground. "Past tense is doing a lot of work in my life these days."

They crested a ridge of slag, and Ada saw the court.

It was a crater — no, a caldera, vast as the valley he'd been born in, its walls terraced in tiers of black glass where the heat had fused the ash into mirror-smooth slopes. And filling the caldera floor, coiled and hunched and folded like a congregation at the end of the world's longest funeral, were dragons.

Not the dragons of Ada's childhood carvings — those had been fierce and bright, wings spread, jaws open, fire frozen mid-roar in cherry wood and river stone. These were remnant things. Grey-scaled, ember-dim, many of them barely larger than oxen, their wings folded tight against bodies gone gaunt. The largest among them lay like a hill that had given up being a mountain, its chest rising in breaths so slow that minutes passed between them. Here and there a scale still held a ghost of color — a copper flank, a throat of faded gold — the way an old tapestry holds the memory of dye.

And above them all, where a roof or a sky should have been, nothing. The starless dark pressed down like a lid.

"They're debating," Sef murmured. "Listen."

Ada listened. At first he heard only wind moving over scales, a sound like the sea drawn through a thousand throats. Then, beneath it, voices. Not speech as he knew it — the words arrived inside him, resonant, each one carrying weight like a struck bell carries sound.

*—extinction with dignity. We end as ourselves, not as servants.*

*—and what is dignity to an empty sky? I would rather serve a liar than go out like a doused lamp.*

*—there are no more oaths worth keeping. The Cranes taught us that.*

At that name, the whole assembly seemed to shudder. A low sound moved through the caldera, half groan, half growl, and the heat in the ground pulsed faster.

"They know," Ada whispered. "They know about the betrayal."

"Everyone knows except the people who needed to," Sef said. "That's how betrayals work. They're built to face away from the betrayed." They started down the slope, boots ringing on glass. "Come on. Better to be thrown out than to sneak in. Thrown out, at least, they've seen your face."

The descent took an hour. By the time they reached the caldera floor, the nearest dragons had noticed them — heads lifting on long necks, nostrils flaring, eyes opening like furnace doors to reveal embers banked low but not yet dead. A path opened through the congregation, whether from courtesy or contempt Ada couldn't tell, and he walked it with the ember-mark burning in his palm, feeling every eye upon him, feeling precisely as significant as a single flake of ash.

At the center of the court rose a dais of fused glass, and upon it, the great dragon — the hill that had stopped being a mountain. Its scales were the grey of old iron, but along its spine ran a ridge of what might once have been white, now smoke-stained. When it spoke, its voice did not enter Ada's ears so much as settle into his ribs.

"A Crane walks in Crane's Hollow's shape."

"I am Ada Crane," Ada said, and his voice sounded absurdly small, a child's voice in a cathedral. "Son of Maren Crane. Last swordspeaker of the valley."

"Last," the dragon repeated, and something that was almost laughter moved through the assembled clans, dry as sliding gravel. "You hear it, elders? He says *last* as though it were an honor. As though we did not watch his line whittle us down to this." It lifted its head, and its eyes were two dying coals regarding him without hatred — which was somehow far worse. Hatred would have meant he mattered. "Why have you come, little seal? Come to check the lock? Come to count what remains behind your family's door?"

"I came to ask for allies."

The word *allies* fell into the caldera and died there. Silence pooled around it. Then the great dragon drew a breath, and the temperature of the entire court dropped as its lungs pulled heat from the air itself.

"Allies. From the clan whose covenant fed our fire-forges on a lie. Whose wards used our own extinguished kin as mortar." Its head swung toward the darkened sky. "Every star that went out was one of ours, swordspeaker. Every light your valley never saw was a soul guttering because the oath that fed it was rotten at the root. And now one of the root-keepers comes asking for *allies*."

"We can restore the forge. I've seen the covenant stone. I know what my family did—"

"You know *nothing*." The word cracked across the caldera like a whip of ice. "Knowing is not blood. Knowing is not bone. You carry the mark, yes—" the dragon's gaze fixed on his palm, and the ember there flared hot enough to make him gasp, "—but marks can be stolen. Embers can be liars. You will be tested as any supplicant is tested: by the oaths of your own house. Speak them true, and we will hear you. Fail, and leave. Or stay, if you prefer, and become part of the court."

No one explained what *part of the court* meant. No one needed to. Ada looked at the grey congregation, at the hollows where bellies had gone concave with hunger, at the small dragon near the dais whose scales had gone translucent as old horn, fire showing faintly through them like a candle behind paper.

"I'll speak them."

* * *

They brought out the oaths on chains.

Not metaphorically — actual chains, dragged from a vault beneath the dais by two dragons whose wings had long since failed, links of black iron each as long as Ada's forearm. Upon each link, letters were cut so deep they cast shadows even in that lightless place. Sef went rigid beside him when the chains appeared.

"That's oath-iron," they breathed. "It doesn't test words. It tests *truth*. Whatever you say while touching it becomes... weighed. Measured against what is." They grabbed his sleeve. "Ada. Your mother swore these same oaths. If she swore them falsely, the iron will show it, and it will show *you* standing in her shadow."

"Then let it show me," Ada said, and found, to his surprise, that he meant it. Something had hardened in him since the shrine, since the covenant stone, since learning that his whole childhood had been lived inside a locked room. "I'm tired of inheriting shadows."

The first chain was laid across his palms. Cold bit through the ember-mark, and the letters on the iron began to glow a sullen orange.

"*Speak the Oath of Keeping,*" the great dragon intoned. "*As Maren Crane swore it.*"

Ada closed his eyes. He'd known these words since before he could read — carved above every hearth in the Hollow, sung at every binding ceremony. He spoke them:

*"I keep the valley. I keep the border. What lies beyond stays beyond, for the good of all within."*

The iron flared white — then went black, and a sound came from it, a thin note of wrongness, like a bell cracked down the middle. Across the caldera, dragons lowered their heads. Somewhere, something wept in a voice like steam.

"It weighs false," the great dragon said. "Your mother swore those words knowing what lay beyond the border. Knowing *who*. The second chain."

The second oath burned colder. *"Speak the Oath of Protection."*

*"No harm shall cross the ward, nor shall any within seek what lies outside it."*

Black. Wrong-note. This time a young dragon near the front — one of the few with color left, copper-bright along its jaw — made a sound that was unmistakably a snarl.

"The third," the great dragon said, and its voice had gone quiet, which was more terrible than shouting. "*The Oath of Fire. The one that fed our forges. Speak it, swordspeaker. Give us the pleasure of hearing it fail one more time.*"

Ada's hands shook. The ember-mark pulsed against the cold iron, two rhythms arguing. He spoke the oldest words his family owned:

*"By Crane's hand and Crane's word, the fire is fed, the fire is kept, the fire burns as it has always burned."*

The chain did not glow at all. It simply crumbled, link by link, into powder that smoked as it fell, and the powder hissed where it landed, and the hiss sounded, impossibly, like laughter.

Ada stood in the drifting ash of his inheritance, palms empty, cheeks burning, every eye in the caldera fixed on him with the flat pity reserved for things already dead. Humiliation rose in him like floodwater. His mother's voice, his grandmother's voice, two hundred years of Cranes reciting pretty words over cold hearths — all of it crumbling in his hands just as the iron had crumbled.

*Say something,* he told himself. *Anything. Or walk out of here as nothing.*

He looked up at the great dragon. At the dying coals of its eyes. And he thought about the covenant stone in the ruined shrine, about the words carved in his ancestor's hand — *let them witness what we contain* — and about how no Crane, in two hundred years, had ever once said aloud what those words actually meant.

"No," Ada said.

The caldera stilled.

"No, I won't speak another inherited oath. I'll say something else instead. Something no Crane ever dared." His voice cracked, and he let it crack. "My family didn't seal magic out of the valley. They sealed *witnesses in*. They took your kin — living, breathing, trusting kin — and they walled them up behind wards and called it protection, and they fed your fire with an oath they knew was a lie, and they raised their children — raised *me* — singing songs about it. My name is Ada Crane, and my family were jailers, and thieves, and liars, and I am ashamed of them."

The words rang out over the grey congregation and kept ringing, echoing off the glass walls, and with each echo the ember in his palm burned hotter, brighter, until light spilled between his fingers like water.

"I can't give you back the Cranes' honor, because they never had any to give. But I can tell you the truth in my own voice, with my own breath, owing nothing to anyone dead." He lifted his marked hand, and the light threw his shadow huge against the caldera wall. "Judge *that*. Judge me. Not my blood. Me."

Silence. Long enough that Ada heard his own heartbeat, and the heartbeat of the burning ground beneath it, falling slowly into rhythm together.

Then the copper-jawed dragon laughed — a real laugh, rusty and astonished, like a door opening after centuries. "Well," it said. "The little seal broke its own chain."

The great dragon regarded him for a long time, coals banking and flaring, banking and flaring. When it finally spoke, its voice had changed — not warm, nothing so generous as warmth, but *considering*, the way a smith considers ore.

"Truth freely spoken is the only coin we have ever honored, swordspeaker. Your ancestors paid in counterfeit. You have paid, at least, in something real." It shifted, and the movement of its body was like watching a hillside decide to move. "But truth alone will not relight a forge. Words must be hammered on iron, and iron must be fed, and the Last Forge beneath the dead stars is guarded by something even we have feared for two hundred years."

"The first Crane," Sef said quietly, at Ada's shoulder. "His echo still keeps the old oath."

"Yes." The great dragon's eyes narrowed to slits of ember-light. "And you should know, before you go — the Warden Vane has reached the cinder-court's outer roads. She brings wardens. She brings ward-iron." It lowered its immense head until its dying-coal eye was level with Ada's face, close enough that he could feel the faint, fading heat of it on his skin. "You have until she arrives, little truth-speaker. Choose your road quickly. One of them leads to the forge. The other leads to a cell your family built, waiting all this time for someone exactly your size."

Far off, beyond the caldera rim, a horn sounded — thin, cold, and unmistakably human.
