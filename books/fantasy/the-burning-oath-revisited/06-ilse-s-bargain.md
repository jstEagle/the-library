## 6. Ilse's Bargain

Ilse Vane had learned, in thirty years of warden service, that the ashlands told the truth to anyone patient enough to listen. Wind wrote its confessions across the gray flats; rain left its signatures in the crusted mud. So when she found the place where Ada Crane had slept — a hollow pressed into the leeward side of a fallen waystone, still warm with the ghost of a body — she knelt and read it like a letter.

He was traveling east. Toward the cinder-court. Alone, or nearly so; there were two sets of prints now, one of them light and uneven, favoring the left foot. The disgraced oath-keeper, then. Sef of the Broken Anvil, if the old registers could be believed.

Ilse rose, brushed the ash from her knees, and did not let herself think about how small the hollow was. How young he must have been when he first curled up in spaces like that, learning to make himself compact against cold. She had held him once, when he was an infant and his mother had laughed at Ilse's terror of dropping him. *Wardens catch falling stars, not babies,* Maren Crane had said, and the memory arrived now with its usual cruelty, uninvited and precise as a blade between ribs.

She signaled her wardens to hold position and went on alone.

* * *

She caught him at dusk, in the ruins of what had once been a toll-bridge over a river that no longer existed. The ashlands were dotted with such bones of the old world — arches without roads, doors without walls, staircases ascending into nothing. This one had a span of shattered stone hanging over a dry gorge, and Ada stood at its center with his back to her, watching the starless sky as if he expected it to apologize.

The ember-light pulsed faintly through the back of his right hand. Even from forty paces, even in failing light, she could see it: the mark of a swordspeaker's bond, glowing under his skin like a coal wrapped in flesh. He hadn't sworn for it. That was the thing that kept her awake in the brief hours she allowed herself sleep. No one had sworn for it. The ember had *chosen*, which had not happened since the First Forging, and every doctrine she had ever trusted said that should be impossible.

"You walk loudly for someone who hunts quietly," Ada said, without turning.

"I walk exactly as loudly as I intend to." Ilse stepped onto the bridge. Her boots rang on the stone. Behind her, at the gorge's edge, her six wardens fanned out along the broken approaches — not closing, not yet. "You've made good time. Sef taught you the ashroads?"

"Sef taught me that people who say my mother's name like they knew her usually want something."

Ilse stopped ten paces from him. Close enough to see how much he'd changed in three weeks. The boy who had slipped through the breach had been soft in the way valley children were soft, unweathered, all his edges rounded by two hundred years of safety. This one standing before her had ash in his dark hair and a healing cut across his jaw and eyes that had learned to measure distances. He looked like Maren. That was the worst of it. He looked so much like Maren that speaking to him felt like arguing with a grave.

"I knew your mother," Ilse said. "That isn't a lure. It's simply true."

"Everyone out here claims to have known her."

"No. Everyone out here claims to know *about* her. There's a difference, and you're clever enough to know I'm drawing it." She clasped her hands behind her back, the parade rest she'd worn for three decades, armor older than any steel. "I'm going to make you an offer, Ada. I'd ask you to hear all of it before you refuse. You will refuse. But hear it anyway."

Something flickered across his face — not softening, but recognition. He knew the cadence. Valley children grew up hearing it from their elders: the voice of someone delivering a verdict they already considered merciful.

"Say it, then."

"The wards can be restored." She let the words stand alone for a moment, because they deserved it. "Not patched. Not propped the way we propped them for two centuries while the covenant rotted underneath. Restored. The border stones remember their shape. What they lack is fire — dragon-fire, oath-fed, the kind that hasn't burned in the valley since before your grandmother's grandmother. And you are carrying the last living piece of it in your hand."

Ada looked down at his palm. The ember pulsed, slow as a sleeping heart. When he raised his eyes again there was something terrible in them, something that had not been there three weeks ago: the beginning of understanding.

"You want me to give it back to the stones."

"I want you to come home," Ilse said, and heard her own voice crack on the second word, and hated herself for it, and continued anyway because a warden who stopped at the first crack deserved to shatter. "I want you to walk back through a rebuilt breach into the valley where you were born, where every person you've ever known is alive and safe behind stone that will hold for another two hundred years. I want you to keep the life that was waiting for you. Your ceremony. Your binding. Your name intact."

"My name," Ada repeated softly. "Sef showed me the covenant stone, Warden Vane. Have you seen it? The original, I mean. Not the copy in the valley archive — the real one, at the forge-shrine."

Ilse did not answer.

"Because it says something interesting," he went on, and his voice stayed level in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. "It says the Cranes sealed the valley to protect it. That's what we're taught. That's what's carved above the meeting hall door. But the original stone says the valley was sealed to keep witnesses *in*. To keep anyone from ever telling the clans what was done to them. My family didn't build a sanctuary, Warden. They built a prison, and they put themselves in charge of the locks, and they called it honor for two hundred years."

"Yes," Ilse said.

The word fell between them like a dropped stone. Ada blinked. Whatever he had braced himself for — denial, fury, the flat warden's recitation of duty — it had not been simple assent.

"I know," she said. "I've known for twenty-six years."

* * *

The wind moved through the dead bridge, moaning in the empty arches, and Ilse Vane stood very still and let twenty-six years arrive all at once.

She had been young then. Younger than Ada was now, though she hadn't felt young — she had felt forged, finished, certain of every line of her life the way she was certain of the warden's code. And Maren Crane had come to her in the winter of the comet, pale as paper, carrying a ledger she was not supposed to have read, and Ilse had listened to everything her lover said and understood that the ground beneath her whole existence was a painted floor over a pit.

"She found the records," Ilse said. "In the deep archive, under the meeting hall. The correspondence between the first Alder-Crane and the clan-mothers. The terms of the sealing. What was promised and what was actually done." She paused. "She came to me because she didn't know what else to do. Because I was a warden and wardens were supposed to protect the valley, and she thought — she *hoped* — that protection might mean something larger than the lie."

"And you helped her hide it."

"I helped her hide it." Each word cost what it cost. "We altered the index. We misfiled the correspondence where no one would look. And when the council asked questions after her death, I gave answers I had rehearsed for a year. I told them grief had unmade her judgment. I let them believe she had imagined half of it." Ilse's hands, behind her back, had gone white-knuckled. "They gave me her post for it. Warden of the Border. Keeper of the stones. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Ada? They promoted me for helping bury her truth, and I took the promotion, and I wore it for twenty-six years, and every morning I buckled it on and told myself it was for the valley. For the children. For *you*."

"Why?" His voice finally broke its level surface. "If she trusted you — if she loved you — why hide it? Why not open the archive and end it?"

"Because ending it would have killed the valley." Ilse stepped closer, and this time she did not stop at ten paces; she closed the distance until she could see the ember's glow reflected in his eyes, until she was close enough that the old instinct to shield him nearly overcame her. "The covenant feeds the forge. The forge feeds the wards. The wards feed everything — the fields, the springs, the mildness of the winters. Pull the lie out from under it and the whole structure comes down. Maren understood that too, in the end. That's why she never spoke. That's why she spent her last years teaching you to love the valley instead — because she decided the truth was a luxury the living couldn't afford." Her throat worked. "And then the stars went out anyway. And the wards fell anyway. And everything she sacrificed her truth to protect is coming down regardless, which means, Ada — *which means* — the only question left is whether it comes down on our heads or whether we put something back in its place. Give me the ember. Let me rebuild the seal. Let the lie stay buried and the valley stay alive, and I will spend whatever remains of my life making sure no one ever has to learn what it cost."

For a long moment, Ada said nothing. The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below the broken bridge, in the dry gorge, loose stone clattered.

Then he laughed — a short, awful sound with no humor in it at all.

"Do you hear yourself?" he said. "Do you actually hear it? *Let the lie stay buried.* You watched my mother carry this alone until it killed her, and your answer is to hand it to me with the weight still on. You're not offering me passage home, Warden. You're offering me her grave, with better masonry."

"Ada—"

"She died of it." His voice rose, and the ember flared with it, throwing orange light up the ruined arches like a small furious sunrise. "Not of illness. Not of age. Of twenty years of swallowing a truth that was eating her alive, in a valley so safe and so sealed that she couldn't even whisper it to the sky. And you stood next to her the whole time and called it protection." He shook his head slowly. "I used to wonder why she sometimes looked at the border stones the way people look at locked doors. Now I know. Now I know, and you *knew*, and you let me grow up inside the lock."

Ilse felt each sentence land where it was aimed. She had trained herself, over decades, to absorb blows without moving; she absorbed these, and it was the hardest drill of her life.

"If you keep the ember," she said quietly, "the clans will take it from you. Or the forge will burn you out trying to use you. Or the Warden's Council — my successors, my *order* — will declare you a thief and hunt you past the point where mercy is possible. I am offering you the only road I know that ends with you alive."

"It ends with everyone alive and nothing true," Ada said. "I've lived in that valley, Warden Vane. I was born in it. I know exactly what it costs to be safe and lying." He opened his right hand, and the ember sat in his palm, pulsing, and for one vertiginous moment Ilse thought he was going to surrender it — thought the offer had landed after all, thought she would carry it home in an iron box and never forgive herself.

Instead he closed his fist.

"No," he said. "I'm going to the Last Forge. I'm going to speak what my family buried, all of it, in my own voice, and if the valley falls with the lie, then the valley was already falling and just too sealed to notice. My mother deserves a son who tells the truth she couldn't. That's the only inheritance I want."

Behind him, out of the shadow of the far arch, Sef emerged — silent, limping slightly, a coil of oath-chain glinting at their belt — and Ilse understood that the boy had never been alone, that the whole conversation had been witnessed, that she had given her confession to an audience.

"You should arrest me," Ilse said to Sef, without looking away from Ada. "It's what your order would do."

"My order is ash," Sef said. "Like everything else the old oaths built." They looked at Ilse for a long moment, and something almost like pity crossed their scarred face. "But you'll get another chance to choose. You always do. Wardens always do."

Ilse stood on the dead bridge in the gathering dark, her wardens waiting at the edges of the ruin, her orders clear and simple and sufficient: take the ember, take the boy, restore the seal. Thirty years of service fit those orders like a glove fits a hand.

And beneath the glove, for the first time, she felt the shape of her own hand — Maren's name in her mouth, unspoken for twenty-six years, rising now like heat through a cracked stone.

"Go," she said at last, stepping aside. "Before I remember what I'm for."

Ada hesitated only a moment — long enough to search her face for whatever remained of the woman his mother had loved — and then he ran, east toward the cinder-court, the ember blazing in his fist like a stolen dawn.

Ilse turned to her wardens. Six faces, six lamps, six versions of the question she could see forming on their lips.

She drew her sword and pointed it down the eastern road.

"After him," she said. "Slowly."

* * *

They made camp that night at the edge of the cinder-court's approach, where the ground began to warm underfoot and the ash gave way to fields of fused glass. Ilse sat apart from her wardens, sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening, and watched the horizon where the Last Forge's darkness pooled like a hole cut in the world.

Her second-in-command, a broad woman named Ket with frost-scars on her cheeks, crouched beside her.

"You let him go," Ket said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse: it was a question.

"I did."

"The Council will have us both broken for it."

"The Council," Ilse said, "is built on the same floor I stood on for twenty-six years. And I've recently learned what's underneath it." She tested the blade's edge against her thumb, watching the horizon, watching the dark. "When we reach the Forge, Ket, watch what happens. Watch what the old oaths do when they're questioned. And then decide whose side your own voice is on."

Far off, beyond the glass fields, something vast exhaled — a sound like a bell the size of a mountain, rung once, in mourning.

The Last Forge was calling its fire home.

And Ilse Vane, keeper of borders, breaker of her own heart twice over, began — silently, in the old tongue, in words she had not spoken since a winter night with Maren Crane's hand in hers — to practice the truth.
