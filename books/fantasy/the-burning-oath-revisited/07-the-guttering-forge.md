## 7. The Guttering Forge

They ran the last league beneath a sky that had forgotten how to be a sky.

Ada had learned, in the weeks since the ashfall, to read the dead stars the way he'd once read weather over Crane's Hollow — the way a farmer reads clouds, the way his mother had read the hum of the wards. The extinguished stars were not simply gone. They hung in their old constellations like scars in flesh, and around each one the dark was *thicker*, as if the absence had weight. Sef called them forge-marks. Every star that had gone out, she said, was a dragon whose fire had guttered for want of an oath kept.

And they were all drifting toward one point on the horizon, the way iron filings drift toward a lodestone.

"There," Sef said, and her voice did something Ada had never heard it do. It went quiet. Not soft — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone is dying in it. "The Last Forge."

It did not look like a forge. It looked like a wound in the world. The ashlands broke open ahead of them into a vast descending caldera, its rim ringed with the fused bones of mountains, and at its heart — far below, past terraces of slag and channels where rivers of fire had once run and now lay cooled into black glass — a single point of light burned. Orange. Unsteady. Small enough that Ada might have mistaken it for a campfire if he hadn't felt it in his chest, in the ember fused to his palm, answering it like a second heartbeat.

*Come home,* the light said, without words. *Come home before I go out.*

His palm throbbed. He'd stopped trying to hide the mark days ago; it showed through his glove now, a coin of dull red heat that flared whenever a dragon passed within a mile, whenever an oath was spoken too near him, whenever Sef said something true. It flared now so hard his fingers spasmed.

"It's calling," he said.

"Of course it's calling." Sef was already moving down the first terrace, her oath-keeper's chains clinking at her belt — the broken ones, the ones she wore as penance rather than office. "It's been calling for two hundred years. Nobody came." She glanced back at him, and the starless dark made her face all shadow and teeth. "Don't make me regret dragging you here."

"You didn't drag me."

"No," she admitted. "Worse. You followed."

* * *

They descended through the corpse of the forge-city.

Ada had grown up on stories of the outer realm as a place of appetite — magic without law, dragons without mercy, oaths twisted into weapons. His father had described it the way you describe a flood or a fever: something that happens to you, not something anyone *built*. But this had been built. Stairways wide enough for twenty dragons to land abreast. Dwellings carved into the caldera walls, each with a hearth-mouth big enough to swallow a house. A great gate, half-melted, still bearing the relief of two figures clasping hands above an anvil — a Crane and a dragon, by the shape of them, though the faces had run like candlewax.

Nobody lived here anymore. But nobody had left, either. In the doorways sat shapes that might have been statues until Ada looked too long and saw the faint suggestion of wings folded, tails curled, heads bowed. Dragons who had come here to die beside the last fire and had not even managed that. They were turning to cinder from the inside out, patient as geology.

"Don't touch them," Sef said. "And don't speak oaths aloud. Not here. Words have weight in a place like this, and the walls remember everything."

"The walls remember what?"

Sef didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They climbed down through terraces of slag, past channels of glassed lava, and with every step the ember in Ada's palm burned hotter and the single point of light below grew — until it wasn't a point anymore but a cavern mouth, breathing out thin orange light and thin gray smoke, and inside it, visible even from the rim, a shape that made Ada stop walking.

An anvil. Vast as a barn, black as the space between stars, and above it a fire no bigger than a cookstove's, trembling, guttering, burning *downward*, as if it had given up on reaching toward anything.

And standing between them and the anvil, motionless as the cinder-dragons in their doorways, stood a man.

"That's—" Ada's throat closed. He knew the silhouette. He'd seen it every day of his childhood, rendered in stained glass in the valley chapel: broad shoulders, long coat, a sword carried flat across both palms like an offering. "That's Aldric Crane. The First. My ancestor."

"That's what's *left* of him," Sef said grimly. "I told you. Oaths are literal chains here. He swore to enforce the covenant until it was fulfilled or forsaken. It was never either. So he waits." Her jaw tightened. "Two hundred years, Ada. He's been standing there for two hundred years, keeping everyone away from a fire that's dying because of what he helped do."

"He helped—"

"He sealed the witnesses in. He fed the lie. And then he chained himself to guard it." Sef started down the last slope. "He isn't your ancestor anymore. He's the lock."

* * *

The heat hit them at the cavern mouth — not the fierce heat of a living forge but the stale warmth of a room where a fire is going out, the warmth of embers raked over and forgotten. Inside, the air tasted of iron and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of the valley in late summer, and Ada understood with a lurch that this was what the covenant had once been: not a wall, but a hearth. This fire had warmed two realms once. His family had let it starve.

The echo of Aldric Crane turned as they entered.

It had eyes. That was the worst of it. Two hundred years should have hollowed them out, and instead they burned — a pale, banked gold, like coals under ash, and they fixed on Ada with an expression he recognized because he'd seen it on his father's face a thousand times: disappointment arriving ahead of any reason for it.

"A Crane," the echo said. Its voice was dust and bell-metal. "At last. Come to fulfill?"

"I've come to reforge," Ada said.

"Reforge." The echo considered the word the way a judge considers a plea. "The covenant stands. It has always stood. No Crane has forsaken it; therefore it binds; therefore it will be honored; therefore the fire is fed as it has always been fed." Each clause landed like a hammer-tap, and with each one the ember in Ada's palm flared painfully, as if the mark itself were being cross-examined. "You carry the bond unsworn. You are either thief or heir. Which?"

"Neither," Ada said. "Both. I don't know yet."

"Honesty," said the echo, and something almost like grief moved through the banked gold of its eyes. "Your line lost that somewhere along the way. It is good to see it return at the end."

Behind them, boots rang on stone.

Ada turned. Warden Ilse Vane stood at the cavern mouth with six wardens fanned out behind her, their ward-lanterns casting hard white circles on the glassed floor, and her sword was already drawn. Ash sifted from her shoulders. She had crossed the ashlands faster than any human should have, and her face was the face of a woman who had stopped sleeping.

"Surrender the ember, Ada," she said. "Last kindness I'll ever offer you."

The echo of Aldric Crane inclined its head to her, courteous as death. "Warden. You bear the old signs. You swore the secondary compact."

"I did." Ilse's voice didn't waver, but her sword-hand did, just slightly, and Ada — who had spent three weeks learning to read oaths in people's bodies — saw it. "The compact holds. The valley stays sealed. Give me the boy and the ember and the breach closes tonight."

"The compact," said the echo, "is a splint tied round a broken bone. But it holds. Yes."

*Splint,* Ada thought. *Not healing. Splint.* His whole life, the wards had been described to him as armor, as protection, as the family's great gift. Standing here, hearing his own ancestor call it a splint, he felt the last load-bearing lie in his chest crack clean through.

Then Sef stepped forward, out from behind him, into the open floor between the wardens and the anvil, and began unbuckling her chains.

"Sef," Ada said. "What are you doing."

"What does it look like?" Her fingers were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "There are seven of them and one of me, and I'm the only oath-keeper present, which means I'm the only one here whose surrender means anything legally. Formally. However you want to say it." She got the last buckle free and held the broken chains up in both hands, an offering. "A disgraced keeper, voluntarily submitting to wardens' judgment, in the presence of the First Crane's own echo. That's a proceeding. That buys time. Proceedings are slow — it's the only thing about the law that's ever been slow."

"You'd be giving yourself to them." Ada's voice cracked. "Ilse will bind you. You know what happens to keepers who submit. They get *unmade*, Sef. We saw what's left of the last one."

"I know exactly what's left of the last one." For the first time since the ashfall, Sef smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing. "I also know how long an hour is when it's measured in procedure. Go to the anvil, Ada. Put your hand on it. Start speaking. Whatever happens behind you — whatever you hear — *do not turn around.* An oath interrupted is worse than an oath never begun. You taught me that, actually. Or your mother did, in a book I stole from a warden's library." She pushed the chains toward Ilse. "Warden Vane. I yield myself to your judgment, formally, fully, and *at length*."

"Sef—" 

"*Go.*"

Ada went.

He crossed the floor with the wardens' lantern-light crawling over his back and the echo's coal-gold eyes tracking him and Sef's voice rising behind him in the formal cadences of submission — article, clause, precedent, objection — each word a brick in a wall she was building with her body. At the anvil he stopped. Up close it was worse than it had looked from the rim: the surface was scarred with two centuries of nothing, cold except for one small region near the center, warm as a sleeping animal, where the last fire's heat still pooled.

Above it, the flame burned downward, smaller than when they'd arrived. He could see it guttering in real time. He thought of stars going out one by one, and understood that he was watching the next one die.

He pressed his marked palm against the warm metal.

The fire leapt.

Not much. A finger's width taller, a shade brighter — but the whole cavern answered it. The cinder-dragons in their doorways, miles above, must have felt it, because a sound came down through the stone, faint and vast, like a sigh passing through a graveyard. The ember in his hand roared against the anvil's warmth, and words rose in him the way water rises in a well, and he opened his mouth—

"—object to the jurisdiction of this proceeding," Sef's voice rang out behind him, sharp as a struck bell, "on grounds that the secondary compact was executed under concealment of material fact, namely—"

—and Ada spoke.

He spoke the truth of the betrayal. All of it. The sealing of the witnesses, the feeding of the fire on a lie, the Cranes who knew and the Cranes who chose not to know, his mother's name caught in the middle of it like a bird in a shuttered room. He spoke it into the warm metal, into the guttering flame, while behind him Sef's voice climbed higher and Ilse's answers came harder and the echo of Aldric Crane pronounced judgment after judgment after judgment, slow as continental drift.

The flame grew. Slowly. Agonizingly slowly. A hand's width. Two. It stopped burning downward and stood uncertainly upright, like a child learning to stand.

But it was not enough. Ada could feel it — the way you can feel a fire that needs more than kindling, that is waiting for a log, for a whole tree, for a forest. The truth alone would not feed it. The truth was only the clearing of the ground.

The flame bent toward him. Waiting.

And the echo of the first Crane spoke over every other voice in the cavern, dust and bell-metal, final as a headstone:

"The hour turns. Speak the old oath, heir, and be done — or speak nothing, and watch it die."

Behind him, he heard Sef's voice break mid-clause, and the sound of chains closing on wrists.

Ada looked at the fire. The fire looked back.
