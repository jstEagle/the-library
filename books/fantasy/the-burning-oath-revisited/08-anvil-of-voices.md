## 8. Anvil of Voices

The chains on Sef's wrists did not clatter. That was the worst of it. Warden-iron was made to be silent — made so that the bound could not even have the dignity of noise — and Ada heard the absence of sound the way he had once heard the wards hum wrong: as a truth arriving ahead of its explanation.

He did not turn around. If he turned, he would see Ilse Vane's face, and Ilse Vane's face had been the face of his mother's oldest friend, of every winter festival and every story told too many times by the hearth, and he was not yet ready to let that face become a stranger's.

"Ada." Her voice came down the cavern stair with the wardens' boots behind it, measured, almost gentle. "Step away from the anvil."

"The hour turns," said the echo of the first Crane, from everywhere and nowhere, dust and bell-metal. "Speak the old oath, heir."

The forge-fire bent toward him like a listener leaning in.

Ada looked at his hands. The ember sat fused in his right palm still, sunk past skin into something deeper, a coal-colored star that pulsed when the Last Forge breathed. Two hundred years of Cranes had carried oaths in their mouths like coins they never spent. He had come down into this dark expecting to spend one. Instead he found himself holding the only currency that mattered, and everyone in the room — the echo, the warden, the dying fire itself — wanted him to counterfeit it.

"Ilse," he said quietly. "Tell me what the old oath says. All of it. Not the valley version. The version you helped hide."

Behind him: silence, and then the small, terrible sound of Ilse Vane deciding how much truth she could afford.

"Ada—"

"Because I've read it now." He turned at last, and yes, her face was a stranger's face, or rather it was the same face finally telling the truth, which amounted to the same thing. "The covenant stone at the shrine. *The valley is kept, and the keeping is kept.* I always thought it meant protection. It means imprisonment. My family didn't seal magic out of Crane's Hollow. They sealed witnesses in. They sealed *us* in, Ilse. Every farmer, every child, every oath-binding ceremony where we swore gratitude for walls we were never told were cages."

"They would have burned," Ilse said. "In the first years, before the wards, the clans came for the valley's grain and its children both. Your ancestor made a hard choice—"

"My ancestor made a *convenient* choice, and then made it a lie, and then made the lie an inheritance." The words came out of Ada with more heat than he knew he carried, and the forge flared at the sound of them, hungry. "And you helped bury it. For my mother. Say that part too."

Ilse's jaw tightened. Torchlight from the wardens' descent moved across her face like weather. "Your mother asked me to hold it. She was dying, Ada. She asked me to let you grow up whole before you learned you were built on a grave."

"She let me grow up *hollow*." His voice cracked on it, and he hated the crack, and then he stopped hating it, because the fire leapt toward the crack like a tongue finding the split in a log. "There it is," he whispered. "That's the kindling."

"Enough." The first Crane's echo filled the cavern, and this time it was not bell-metal but something older, the sound of a mountain deciding. "Heir. You stand at the anvil your blood made. Speak: *I am Crane, and the valley is kept, and the keeping is kept, world without end.* Speak it, and the fire lives, and your people sleep safe another two hundred years, and none of this need ever have been said."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the last dragon-fire dies tonight, and the stars stay dead, and the outer realm goes dark forever, and your name becomes the name of the man who let the light go out."

Ada laughed. It surprised him, coming out of him, rough and unsteady. "You think that's the threat? I've been the heir of a dead name my whole life. You're threatening me with the thing I already am."

* * *

They brought Sef forward then, chains whisperless, and set her against the far wall where Ada could see her. Her face was gray with exhaustion, split lip crusted black, but her eyes were clear, and they found his across the cavern with the steadiness of someone who had already lost everything except the truth and therefore had nothing left to hedge.

"Don't listen to any of us," Sef said. "Not the ghost. Not the warden. Not me. Listen to the fire. It's the only party here that's never lied."

"You are not helping," Ada said.

"I'm not trying to help. I'm trying to be quiet long enough for you to hear yourself." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "You've had other people's voices in your mouth since the day you were born, swordspeaker. Everyone in this room is terrified of the moment yours shows up."

The wardens shifted. Ilse raised a hand and they stilled, and Ada understood suddenly that she was giving him time — that some part of her, the part that had loved his mother, was standing in the doorway of her own duty hoping he would walk through it and break it.

The forge breathed. Up close, at the anvil, Ada could see what he had refused to see since they arrived: the fire was not merely low. It was shaped. Coiled around a central dark, the way a hand curls around something it refuses to drop. Souls, Sef had called them, back in the ash fields. Dragons whose forges had gone out, waiting in the last warmth for someone to say a word worth burning for.

"I can't give you the old oath," Ada said to the fire. His voice sounded very small under the vaulted dark. "It's a lie. Lies don't feed fires. They smother them — isn't that what got you here?"

The flame shivered. Waited.

"But I don't know what the true oath is either." He spread his hands, ember and all. "I'm twenty-two years old. I've never left a valley. I've never forged anything harder than a hinge. What do you want from me?"

And the answer came not in words but in heat — a pulse through the ember in his palm, up his arm, into the place behind his sternum where dread had lived all his life beneath the comfort of a chosen-for-him future. It wanted nothing from him. That was the revelation that nearly dropped him to his knees. It wanted nothing *given*. It wanted something *spent*.

"Oh," Ada said softly. "Oh, you want the cost."

* * *

He picked up the hammer.

It lay on the anvil where generations of Cranes must have laid it down and never lifted it — a hammer of black iron gone orange at the head from centuries near fire, heavy as a sin. The echo of the first Crane roared somewhere above him, and Ilse was saying his name in a voice fraying at the edges, and the wardens' boots were moving, and Sef was shouting something that might have been his name or might have been a warning, and Ada stood inside all of it the way one stands inside a storm, hearing each drop separately.

"I am Ada Crane," he said, and the echo seized on the words, triumphant, *yes, heir, speak*, and he let it finish believing before he broke it open.

"And I am the last of a line of liars."

The cavern went silent the way a struck bell goes silent — everything ringing at once and then nothing.

"There was no protection," Ada said, and each word he spoke aloud he also fed into the ember, and the ember drank them and flared brighter, and he understood that this was the forging: truth, spoken at cost, hammered into matter. "There was no covenant kept. There was a theft, dressed as a gift, and my family wore it for two hundred years and called it honor. We took the dragons' fire and gave them our silence. We took their sky and gave them our walls. We sealed the witnesses because the crime needed guarding more than the valley did."

"HEIR." The first Crane's voice shook grit from the ceiling. "You unmake your own house—"

"Yes," Ada said, and swung the hammer down onto the ember where it lay on the cold anvil-face, and the blow rang through the mountain like a funeral toll. "That is exactly what I am doing. I am unmaking my own house, because a house built on a grave is just a grave with a roof."

Blow. The word *theft* went into the metal. Blow. The word *silence*. Blow after blow after blow, and with each one the fire rose higher, and with each one something burned off him — he could feel it leaving, the way fever breaks. His family name went first. He felt it go as a loosening, two centuries of inherited weight sliding off his shoulders and into the flames, and what was underneath was terrifyingly light and entirely his own.

Ilse had stopped giving orders. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her hand pressed flat over her mouth, watching the name *Crane* burn out of the air, and her eyes were wet, and she did not look away.

"Ilse," Ada said between blows, not ceasing, not gentle, because gentleness now would be another lie. "You loved my mother. So tell the fire what you buried. Tell it *now*."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then Ilse Vane, Warden of the Marches, keeper of the concealment, stepped into the firelight — out of duty's armor, into whatever had been underneath it all along — and began to speak, and her voice was wrecked and true, and the forge drank her confession the way it drank his, and the coiled souls in the heart of the flame stirred like sleepers turning toward morning.

Sef was laughing, or crying, or both, chained and radiant against the wall. "There it is," she called. "There's your voice."

Ada raised the hammer a final time. In his palm the ember had changed — no longer a splinter of a dying dragon but a seed of a living one, molten, singing. He set it into the anvil's hollow, where a covenant stone had been broken two hundred years ago, and he spoke the last of it, the whole truth, plain as bread:

"My name was Ada Crane. I release the name. I keep the debt. Let the fire judge what I build instead."

He struck.

The sound was not a toll. It was a dawn.

Light went up through the mountain like water rising in a well — through the shrine, through the ashlands, through the cracked border stones of a valley two hundred years sealed — and in the heart of the Last Forge the coiled darkness opened, and the first freed soul rose out of the flame and passed through the cavern roof into the starless sky.

Above them, impossibly, through miles of stone, a single point of light bloomed.

Then a second.

Then, like a field catching fire in slow motion, the whole sky began to burn.
