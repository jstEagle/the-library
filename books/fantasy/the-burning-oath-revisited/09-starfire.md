## 9. Starfire

The new covenant stone came off the anvil glowing like a held breath.

Ada had not known he was screaming until the hammer's last fall and the silence after it, when his own voice rang in his ears like a cracked bell. The truth he had spoken into the forge still hung in the air of the Last Forge, written in fire on the underside of the dark: *the Cranes sealed witnesses, not wards; they fed a lie to a living flame; they let the stars go out rather than be shamed.* Every word of it had cost him something. He could feel the shape of each one, the way you feel a tooth pulled — absence with edges.

The ember in his palm pulsed once, twice, and then poured itself into the stone.

It did not explode. That was the first mercy. Ada had braced for violence, for the forge to reject him as it must have rejected every Crane who came before with a lie between their teeth. Instead the stone drank the fire the way dry ground drinks rain, and light climbed its carved lines — the old covenant-script dissolving even as it burned, letter by letter, until the surface was smooth and blank and waiting.

"Say it again," Sef whispered beside him. Their hands were still bound to his where the echo of the first Crane had chained them to the anvil — chains that were, even now, going slack, link by link, as if the forge itself had decided the debt was paid. "Not the truth. The oath. Your own words."

Ada looked down at the blank stone. His hands were blistered. His family name felt like ash in his mouth.

"I don't know what to say," he admitted.

"You knew what to say when you spoke the truth."

"That was easier. That was just — opening a wound." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "An oath is supposed to hold. How do I know mine will hold?"

Sef turned his wrist over, gently, the way they handled everything now — as if he might come apart at any moment and they had decided, without discussion, that he wouldn't. "You don't," they said. "That's what makes it yours."

So Ada bent over the stone, over the small furnace-heart of it, and spoke in his own voice, the one no ancestor had lent him:

*I am Ada Crane, last of a name I will not pass on unexamined. I swear fire to the dragon-clans, and truth to the fire, and my own two hands to the work of keeping both. No valley of mine will ever again be a cage wearing the shape of protection. Come home, if you wish. Be free, if you wish. But burn — burn somewhere someone can see.*

The stone flared white.

And then the world outside the forge began, very quietly, to catch fire.

* * *

It started with one star.

Ada saw it through the great broken throat of the forge-shaft above them — a single point of gold where there had been nothing but the wound-black sky, a point that trembled like a candle in wind and then steadied, and then *bloomed*, pouring light down the shaft in a column that turned the ash-dust in the air to drifting sparks.

"That's Vessith," Sef breathed. They had gone very still, the way people go still before weeping. "The oldest of the clans. She chose extinction rather than serve the lie. She's been dark for sixty years." Their voice broke on the number. "She's *lighting*."

A second star kindled. A third. Then it was not one at a time but a rain of them, a slow avalanche of ignition spreading across the whole black bowl of the sky, each new star flaring and settling and burning, and Ada understood — not with his mind but with the ember-mark in his palm, which sang like a struck wire — that these were not stars being born.

They were souls coming home.

He could almost see them: vast shapes of light unfolding out of the dark between the stars, wings made of released fire, the extinguished dragons rising out of whatever cold country their deaths had been and climbing back into the sky they had been taken from. The freed dead. The forgiven dead. For that was the thing that undid him, standing in the ruin of the Last Forge with soot in his throat and his family's crime cooling on the anvil behind him — that the fire had not punished anyone. It had simply *let go*. It had taken the truth he fed it and turned it not into a blade but into dawn.

"They're not angry," he said stupidly.

"No," said a voice from the doorway of the forge. Ilse Vane stood there, and she was not the Warden who had hunted him across the ashlands. She was smaller than that person had been, and older, and her ward-badges — the silver rank-marks of the order that had guarded the lie for two hundred years — lay scattered on the ground around her boots like shed scales. She had torn them off. Her hands were shaking. "No, Ada. They never were. That was always the worst part. We told ourselves they'd punish us if they could, because punishment would have been easier to deserve than forgiveness."

Behind her, through the doorway, the sky was doing something impossible. The border-wards — the ones that had sealed Crane's Hollow, the ones whose collapse had begun all of this — were visible from here as a faint scar across the horizon, a seam of failing light. And along that seam, the returning dragon-souls were gathering, circling, pressing against the last thin membrane of the old covenant like moths against glass.

"The wards are coming down," Ilse said. "All of them. Everything my order built, everything I *maintained*—" She stopped. Started again. "I spent forty years holding up a ceiling so it wouldn't fall on the people underneath it. I told myself that was love. Your mother asked me once, near the end, whether the valley was a home or a vault. I never answered her. I'm answering her now."

She stepped over the threshold into the forge. Into the light of the relit sky. She knelt — the Warden of the Marches, kneeling in the ash — and pressed her bare palm flat against the floor.

"Ilse," Ada said carefully. "You don't have to—"

"Yes," she said, "I do. That's the whole point. That's the only thing left that is entirely mine to do." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I helped bury the truth to protect your mother. I won't help bury the sky to protect her memory. Tell me what the new stone needs, swordspeaker. Because whatever comes through those wards when they fall — I intend to be standing on the right side of it."

* * *

They went out together into the ashlands under a sky full of returning fire.

It was Sef who saw it first — the seam of the old wards brightening, straining, the dragon-souls wheeling above it in slow concentric rings like water finding the level. The new covenant stone hummed against Ada's chest where he carried it wrapped in scorched cloth, and with every wingbeat of the circling dead it hummed louder, pulling, asking.

"It wants to be placed," Sef said. "At the breach. Where the old stones cracked."

"I know." Ada did know; the mark in his palm knew. "But if I set it at the breach, the valley opens. All the way. No walls, no seals, nothing between Crane's Hollow and everything out here. My whole life, everyone I love has lived inside a locked room, and I'm about to take the door off its hinges."

"And if you don't?" Sef's voice was gentle. "The stars stay half-lit. The clans stay exiles at their own door. And the valley stays a museum of a lie, with your neighbors sleeping soundly inside it." They paused. "You already spoke the truth, Ada. This is just the truth getting where it's going."

Ilse walked ahead of them toward the seam, her empty badge-scars pale on her collar, and where she passed, the failing ward-light bent away from her like water from oiled cloth. The wards knew her. The wards had loved her, in their blind architectural way, and she was walking through them carrying nothing but her choice.

The three of them reached the breach as the first true dawn of the new sky broke — not a sun yet, but the gathered light of a hundred relit stars pooling along the horizon like a tide deciding to come in. Ada knelt at the center of the cracked border-stones, unwrapped the covenant, and set it down in the wound.

It sank into the earth like a coin into soft wax.

Light ran outward from it through the cracks in the old stones, filling them, healing nothing and hiding nothing — tracing every fracture, every fault line of the two-hundred-year seal, illuminating the whole architecture of the cage so that for one long breath everyone in the valley below, if they looked up, could see exactly what had been built around them and how.

Then the light let go.

The wards did not shatter. They *exhaled* — two centuries of held breath leaving the borders of Crane's Hollow all at once, a sound like the whole world sighing, and the dragon-souls came down through the opening like rain coming home to a river. Ada stood in the wash of them, laughing or crying or both, the ember-mark in his palm finally quiet, finally cool, finally just a mark and not a command.

Above the valley, the stars burned. All of them. Every soul the old lie had snuffed out, back in its place in the sky, and beneath them the fields and roofs and cold hearths of Crane's Hollow lay open to the heavens for the first time in two hundred years.

Sef took his hand. Ilse stood at his shoulder, unbowed, watching the wards' last light gutter and die with an expression Ada could not read and did not need to.

"Well," Sef said at last, hoarse. "Now we go home, swordspeaker. And explain to a valley full of frightened people why the sky is full of dragons."

Ada looked at the road leading down into the open valley — at the tiny figures already emerging from their houses, staring upward, pointing, afraid and awed in equal measure.

"Yes," he said. "Let's go tell them the truth. It seems to be the family business now."
