## 10. The Open Valley

The road home was not a road anymore.

Where the border stones had stood for two hundred years — a ring of gray monoliths humming with the old wards, cold as teeth in the valley's jaw — there was now only a scar of broken earth and a scatter of shattered granite, half-buried in new grass. Ada walked it slowly, because some part of him still expected the hum to rise up and stop him at the threshold, expected the familiar pressure of the wards against his skin like a hand pressing him back into the shape he had been born to.

Nothing stopped him. The air passed over the broken border without resistance, carrying the smell of rain on turned soil and woodsmoke from hearths that had burned all night with their doors open, because no one in Crane's Hollow could quite believe the sky would stay.

He had left the valley a boy under an oath he hadn't chosen. He came back with an ember's ghost still warm in his palm and a covenant stone wrapped in oiled cloth across his back, and behind him, a day's walk down the mountain switchbacks, Sef climbed with the patient, deliberate stride of someone who had decided where they were going and refused to be hurried about it.

The valley opened before them like a held breath finally let out.

Crane's Hollow had always been beautiful in the way of things kept in glass — green fields combed into obedience, whitewashed houses, the great cold hearth in the square that no one had tended because tending implied fire, and fire implied magic, and magic was death. Ada had loved it the way you love a room you've never been allowed to leave: completely, and with a low constant ache he'd mistaken for contentment.

Now the fields ran wild at their edges, wheat leaning into the lanes, and the doors stood open, and above it all — above everything — the stars were out.

They hung thick as spilled salt. The dragon-souls burned in their old constellations, and when the wind moved through the valley, Ada could have sworn the light moved with it, rippling faintly, as if the whole sky were one vast slow breathing thing.

"They're watching," Sef said, coming to stand beside him at the broken border. Their voice was rough with ash-dust and three days of silence. "Your neighbors. They've been standing in their doorways since dawn."

"I know."

"Are you going to stand here until they come to us, or are we walking in?"

Ada almost smiled. Almost. "You could still turn around, you know. Go back to the ashlands. Find whatever's left of the oath-keepers' order and rebuild it. You'd be a hero there now. They sing songs about you already — I heard them at the last waystation."

"The songs are terrible," Sef said. "They rhyme my name with *shame*. And besides." They looked past him, at the open valley, at the stars, and something in their weathered face went young and uncertain. "I've spent my whole life keeping other people's oaths. I thought I'd see what it was like to stay somewhere by choice."

* * *

They did not come to him singing.

That was the first thing Ada understood, walking down into Crane's Hollow with the stone on his back: there would be no triumph. The people gathered in the lane — the miller's family, old Tam Voss who had taught him his letters, the widow Hearn with her hands pressed over her mouth — they looked at him the way you look at a man returned from a funeral. Because that was what this was. He had buried something for them. Two hundred years of certainty. The comfortable dark.

His aunt Maren reached him first. She was his mother's sister, broad-shouldered and unyielding, and she took his face in her two hands and looked at him for a long moment as if checking that all of him had come home.

"You broke it," she said. Not accusing. Wondering.

"I finished it," Ada said. "There's a difference. It took me a long time to learn the difference."

Her hands trembled against his jaw. "Your mother—"

"Knew." He said it gently, though the word still cut him on its way out. "She knew what the covenant was. She spent her life trying to find a way to tell you all and couldn't. That's why Ilse—" He stopped. Even now, three days after the forge, after the sky caught fire, he could not say *after Ilse died* without the ground tilting. "That's why the Warden helped me, in the end. Love wears strange armor, Aunt. You'll want to be angry about all of this. Be angry. But then listen to what I have to say, because the anger is only the first door."

Maren lowered her hands. Behind her, the lane had filled — dozens of them now, silent, the whole valley come out to meet the boy who had carried their lie into the fire and brought back the truth still smoking.

"Say it, then," she said. "Here. Now. Whatever it is."

So Ada set down the covenant stone in the middle of the lane, unwrapped the oilcloth, and told them everything. The sealing that was never protection but imprisonment. The witnesses walled in so no living voice could contradict the Cranes' version of the oath. The dragon-fire fed on a falsehood until it starved, and the stars going out one by one like lamps in an abandoned house. His own voice cracked twice. He did not hide it. That, too, was part of the telling — that a Crane could crack in public and remain standing.

When he finished, no one spoke for a long time. Then old Tam Voss sat down right there in the dirt of the lane, an old man folding up like a dropped coat, and wept — not loudly, but terribly, the way grief sounds when it has been waiting sixty years for permission.

And the valley began to mourn.

It went on for days. There was no ritual for it; they had to invent one, because the old rites were all built around the sealed hearth and the closed sky. So they mourned in the open, in the wheat and the lanes, and it was ugly and necessary and true. Some people raged at Ada. One family packed a cart and left for the lowlands, and he watched them go and did not try to stop them, because a covenant spoken freely has to include the freedom to refuse it. But others came to him quietly, one at a time, and asked questions they had never been allowed to ask — *what is magic, really; what does an oath cost; can I speak one too* — and each question was a small window opening in a long-sealed house.

On the seventh morning, Ada carried the new covenant stone to the place where the border had stood.

It was smaller than the old one. That had surprised even him, when it cooled. The ancestral stone had been a monolith, heavy as a verdict. This one he could have carried in two arms — a slab of forge-black rock veined with ember-light, warm to the touch, humming very softly with a sound nothing like the old ward's pressure. The old wards had said *stay out*. This one said only, in the wordless grammar of oaths: *keep your word, and be welcome.*

He dug the hole himself. Sef helped, though they mostly supervised, offering commentary on his spade-work that was technically accurate and entirely unnecessary. When the stone stood upright in the earth where the first border monolith had cracked, Ada knelt before it, laid his marked palm flat against the warm surface, and spoke.

Not the old words. Never the old words again.

"I am Ada Crane," he said, "last swordspeaker of the sealed valley, and I swear this in my own voice, which is the only voice I have. No one bound within these bounds shall be bound by another's promise. Every oath spoken here shall be chosen or it shall be void. To the dragon-clans: witness. To the sky: witness. To whoever comes to this stone wanting in, or wanting out: the door is real, and it opens both ways."

The stone drank the words. The ember-veins brightened, pulsed once, and settled into a steady glow like a banked hearth — alive, tended, awake.

Behind him, he heard the valley exhale.

* * *

Sef stayed.

Ada had braced himself for the leaving — had rehearsed the goodbye a dozen times on the walk down the mountain, composing and discarding versions of it, the gracious one, the wounded one, the one where he pretended he wouldn't count the days. He should have known better than to script anything where Sef was concerned. On the ninth evening, while Ada was re-laying the hearthstones of the old cold hearth in the square — the first fire it would hold in two centuries — Sef appeared beside him with an armload of split oak and said, "You're building that crooked," and that was all. No announcement. No ceremony. Just an armload of wood and a future assumed.

The first fire in the great hearth caught at dusk, and half the valley came to watch it burn, sitting in a wide silent ring like people at a birth. The flames threw light on faces that had only ever known firelight from candles and fear. A child laughed at the popping of the pitch, startled and delighted, and no one hushed her.

Afterward, Ada and Sef walked out past the new stone to the field where the wheat leaned wild, and the stars were so thick overhead it seemed you could lean back into them.

"I keep waiting to feel like a stranger here," Sef said. "I keep waiting for someone to tell me disgraced oath-keepers aren't welcome in paradise."

"No one's told you?"

"Not yet. The widow Hearn keeps feeding me pie, which I suspect is a trap, but I'm willing to be trapped."

Ada laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising him, loose and easy in a way his voice had never been inside the old wards. He looked over at Sef, silhouetted against the starfire, ash-scars and stubborn jaw and eyes that had seen every kind of broken promise and somehow still bothered to hope, and felt the thing that had been taking root in him since the forge put out another quiet inch of growth. It didn't feel like falling. It felt like planting.

"Sef," he said.

"Don't make it an oath," Sef warned. "I know how literal you people get."

"It's not an oath. It's just my voice." He paused. "Stay. Not because the valley needs an oath-keeper. Because I do."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Above them, one of the dragon-souls drifted slowly across the sky, trailing light like a comet that had forgotten to hurry. "Well," Sef said at last, gruffly, looking hard at the wheat instead of at him. "Someone has to make sure you don't build any more crooked hearths."

* * *

Three years later, on a bright autumn morning, seven children sat in a half-circle on the grass beside the covenant stone, cross-legged and fidgeting, aged between five and eleven. They were the first generation born under the returned stars — the midwife called them the starlings, and the name had stuck the way true names do.

Ada crouched among them, gray threading his temples now, the ember-mark faded on his palm to a pale warm shimmer that woke when he spoke carefully and slept when he lied.

"An oath isn't a spell," he told them. "It's not a rope to tie yourself with. It's a bridge. You build it out of your own voice, plank by plank, and then you have to walk across it. That's the whole secret. Ready?"

Seven small faces nodded, solemn as owls.

"Now, Wren — you wanted to swear something. What is it?"

The smallest girl stood up, took a breath that puffed out her whole body, and planted her feet in the grass the way she'd seen Ada plant his. "I swear," she announced, in a voice that carried clear across the open valley, "to feed the hearth-fire every morning, even in winter, even when I'm sleepy — *especially* in winter — and I choose it, and nobody made me, and it's mine."

The covenant stone glowed. Somewhere far above, faint as a heartbeat, a star brightened — just slightly, just enough that the children gasped and pointed, and Wren stood in the center of their wonder with her chin high and her oath still hanging in the air like smoke that meant to stay.

Ada looked at her — at all of them, at the open sky, at Sef leaning on the fence with pie in hand and an expression that was trying very hard not to be proud and failing completely — and thought of his mother, and of Ilse, and of every voice that had ever been sealed shut, and he smiled.

"Good," he said. "Now the rest of you. One at a time. Speak loud enough for the sky to hear you."

And one by one, under the burning stars, the children of the open valley began to speak.
