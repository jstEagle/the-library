## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats always knew before she did.

Yara Vasquez learned that in her first winter in the Hollow, when the sky went the color of old pewter and the herd refused to leave the fold, standing shoulder to shoulder and staring at the eastern ridge as though something walked there. Nothing did. But three days later the storm came down off the mountains like a judgment, and Yara, who had spent seven years learning to listen to animals because listening to people had never once gone well for her, remembered to be grateful.

So on this morning in late autumn, when the goats would not pass the third fence gate — when Bramble, the oldest and orneriest of them, planted all four hooves and looked at Yara with those horizontal-pupiled eyes full of ancient goatish contempt and something else, something almost like warning — Yara stood in the frost-stiff grass and paid attention.

"Not today, then," she said aloud. Her voice came out rough from disuse. She talked to herself more than she liked to admit; a person alone too long started narrating her own life just to hear it spoken by someone, anyone, even herself.

Bramble bleated once, low and wet, and turned back toward the barn. The rest followed like a congregation after a reluctant priest.

Yara shut the gate and stood a moment with her hand on the post, looking east. The ridge was clear. Blue morning, hard light, no smoke, no riders, nothing. Just the border stones at the valley's mouth, gray and lichen-eaten, half-swallowed by the hillside as if the earth itself were trying to forget they'd ever been set there.

She knew better than most what those stones meant. Everyone in the Hollow knew, the way you know the floor is beneath you — without thinking about it, without gratitude. Magic died at the border stones. That was the whole of it, the whole strange covenant of the place: whatever crossed that line came across diminished. Charms went flat. Curses unraveled. Hedge-witches who fled into the Hollow found their craft dying in their hands within a season, their knotted cords just cord, their brewed infallibles just bitter tea. The valley was a sanctuary built on amputation. You could live here. You could not *be* here, not entirely, not everything you had been.

That was why she'd come. That was the whole ugly truth of it, the truth she kept folded up small and buried deep, the way you'd hide a knife you were ashamed of owning.

She had run. Seven years ago, in the spring, with Maren's grimoire still warm from the hearthstone where the old woman had been reading it, Yara had walked out of her apprenticeship the way you walk out of any life — suddenly, at night, telling herself stories. She'd tell herself even now, some mornings, when the guilt woke before she did: *I wasn't ready. She asked too much. The rite she wanted me to attempt was beyond me.* All true, in the way that convenient things are true. And underneath it, also true: she had been afraid, and prideful, and twenty years old, and Maren had said one word too many in one tone too sharp — *"You are not special, girl. You are merely willing"* — and Yara had taken the grimoire's sister-volume, the field book, the one with the practical charms, and left the rest behind on its chain, and gone.

Gone south. Gone over two ranges and through a river country and finally here, to the Hollow, because she'd heard in a market town that magic couldn't follow you past the stones. She hadn't wanted to escape Maren exactly. She'd wanted to escape being *known* — being seen every day by someone who could measure the exact distance between what Yara was and what Yara might have been. In the Hollow nobody measured anything. In the Hollow she was just the woman with the goats on the west slope, competent, close-mouthed, unremarkable. She had made herself unremarkable the way other people make themselves rich: deliberately, painstakingly, over years.

It had worked. That was the terrible thing. It had worked, and she hated how well it worked, and she could not have undone it now if she tried.

* * *

The cottage sat where the slope gentled toward the stream, stone-walled, turf-roofed, one room and a loft and a lean-to that leaked in exactly the way she'd stopped noticing. There was a garden going to seed for the winter — kale gone blue-black with frost, onions in their braids hanging from the beam, a row of carrots she kept meaning to pull. A spinning wheel she'd bought secondhand and used more for the rhythm of it than for any need of thread. A hearth that drew well. A bed she'd built herself, which creaked in a particular way at the footboard that she had long ago stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

She put the kettle on. She fed the fire a knot of birch bark and watched it catch, and while it caught she did what she did every morning and hated every morning: she checked the little shelf above the lintel, where the field book sat.

Maren's field book. Taken, not given. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as ear, its spine held together with a strap Yara had cut from an old harness. She didn't read it anymore. She hadn't read it in years. But she checked that it was there the way you check a wound has stopped bleeding — touching the edges of a thing to confirm it hasn't grown.

Inside it were charms that did nothing here. Wards that wouldn't ward. A charm against fever that was, in the Hollow, merely a list of herbs and a prayer-shaped arrangement of words. She kept it anyway. Of course she kept it anyway. It was the only thing she owned that proved another life had happened, that a woman named Maren Osgar had once stood in a kitchen smelling of ash and tansy and told Yara Vasquez she had talent, real talent, the kind that comes along twice in a generation — and then, later, told her she was not special, only willing, and Yara had run from the second sentence without waiting to understand that the first one was still true.

Some nights she dreamed of the cottage she'd left. Not the grand visions, not the burning crowns — those came separately, worse, and she'd get to those — but the small dreams. The kitchen. The smell of bread failing to rise because Maren's hands had gotten too shaky for kneading. The sound of the old woman's voice reading aloud in the dark, because her eyes were going and she'd memorized the pages instead, and she read them to the empty room as if the room were a student.

In the dreams Yara always stood in the doorway. Never entered. Never left. Just stood there, and Maren never turned around, and Yara woke with her jaw aching from clenching it.

* * *

Midday, she mended the byre roof where a slate had slipped, working alone with the hammer and the cold biting her knuckles, and it was while she was up on the ladder that the dream came back to her waking — the way it had begun doing lately, bleeding upward out of sleep into the daylight hours like water rising through a floor.

*A crown, iron, ringed in thorns that were not thorns but teeth. It sat on no head. It simply burned, suspended, and around it the air bent inward as though the world itself leaned to look. And beneath it, kneeling figures — hundreds, thousands, ranks of them receding into dark, all facing away from her, all wearing crowns of their own, smaller ones, copper and bone and glass, and each crown caught fire in turn, top to bottom of the endless rows, and none of the kneeling figures moved, none screamed, they simply burned and stayed kneeling, and the heat of it was not heat but attention, a vast and patient attention turning toward her, and a voice that was not a voice said—*

She came back to herself with the hammer mid-swing and drove the nail through the slate and into her own thumb, and swore, and sat down hard on the ridge of the roof with her hand in her mouth.

Third time this week. The dreams had started at harvest's end, gentle enough — embers, a glint of gold — and had been building since, night by night, more detailed, more insistent, like a knock at a door repeated by someone who knows you're home. And now they were following her into the day.

Fever, she told herself. Something in the water. Too much cheese before bed. She ran through the sensible explanations the way she ran through everything — thoroughly, alone, arriving nowhere — and then climbed down and went inside and did not look at the field book, which is to say she looked at nothing else.

She made herself eat. Barley porridge, salt, a spoon of honey she rationed like a miser. While she ate she watched the window, and the window showed her the ridge, and the ridge showed her nothing, and the nothing felt less like peace than like a held breath.

Here was the thing about the Hollow that the people born here never understood, because they'd never lived anywhere else: safety, in the Hollow, was a kind of silence. Not the good silence of a finished day, but the silence of a room where something has been removed. She had come here to be safe from magic, and she had succeeded so completely that she had also become safe from herself — from the part of her that had once known things before they happened, that had read weather in Maren's moods and illness in the taste of well-water. That part had starved quietly, politely, over seven years, until some mornings she genuinely could not remember whether it had ever been real or whether Maren had flattered her, whether the talent had been true or only the loneliness talking.

But lately — these last weeks, since the first ember-dream — she had caught herself knowing things again. Small things, stupid things. She'd known the miller's dog was dead an hour before the miller's boy came crying down the lane. She'd known which ewe would founder, and it had. Little flickers, like a lamp turned low but not out, guttering in a draft.

Magic was supposed to die at the stones. Hers was doing something worse. It was *waiting.*

* * *

Evening came early, as it does in valleys. By the time she'd brought the water up and banked the fire, the ridgelines had gone the purple of a bruise healing, and the first stars stood over the eastern rim sharp as nailheads. She lit the lamp, took up the mending — a sock, always socks, the goats saw to that — and let the quiet settle around her the way it did every night, the familiar quiet, the chosen quiet, the quiet she had spent seven years building stone by stone around herself like a wall.

And then, sitting there with the needle in her hand, she heard it.

Not a sound. A *pressure.* The way the air feels before lightning, except there was no storm coming — the sky through the window was swept clean, cold, enormous. The pressure came from everywhere and nowhere, from under the floor and over the roof, and the lamp flame stood perfectly still, unnaturally still, like a painted flame, like a flame in a memory of a flame.

And the dream rose in her, wide awake, eyes open, the whole of it at once: the iron crown ringed in teeth, the kneeling thousands, the fire moving down the ranks, the vast patient attention turning—

—and this time, the voice that was not a voice spoke words she could almost, almost hear, and the almost-hearing was worse than any hearing, because her mind strained toward it like a dog toward a scent and found only the shape of meaning without the meaning itself. Two syllables. Maybe three. Something about an oath. Something about breaking.

Then it was gone. The lamp flame remembered how to be a flame. The sock sat in her lap with the needle still through it, and outside, far off, a fox began to scream on the ridge — ordinary, animal, mortal — and the sound of it was so mercifully normal that Yara laughed, one short harsh note, at herself, at the room, at the whole trembling arrangement of her life.

"Nothing's coming," she told the empty cottage, in the voice she used for the goats, steady, reasonable, a lie delivered with conviction. "Nothing ever comes. That's the entire point of this place."

The fire ticked. The kettle, forgotten, had gone cold.

She slept badly, and near dawn she dreamed the dream one final time that night — but changed. This time the kneeling figures were not faceless. This time, as the fire came down the ranks toward her, she saw their faces, and the nearest of them, the last one, the one whose crown would burn next, lifted its head and looked at her with eyes she knew.

Maren's eyes.

Yara woke with the grey light in the window and her heart slamming and the word *go* already forming somewhere below thought, below reason, in the place where the goats knew storms before storms came.

Outside, the morning was beautiful. Hard frost, windless, the border stones visible for miles down the valley, gray and patient in the new sun.

And on the road, far off, at the very edge of seeing — a speck. Small. Moving. Coming up the valley toward her door, through the frost, too small to be a rider, too deliberate to be a deer.

Too small, she realized, with a certainty that arrived whole and unbidden and unwelcome, the old knowing rising in her starved and blinking —

too small to be anyone but a child.
