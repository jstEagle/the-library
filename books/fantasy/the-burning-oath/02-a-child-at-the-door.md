## 2. A Child at the Door

The speck did not waver.

Yara watched it from the doorway, arms crossed against the cold, telling herself all the reasonable things. Children wandered. Shepherds' brats got lost in the high pasture and came down the valley crying for their mothers. The Hollow had seen lost children before — there'd been a boy from the Tarnside farms, years back, who'd chased a lamb over the ridge and spent two nights in the rocks before old Hedda found him half-frozen and weeping.

But that boy had come stumbling. That boy had zigzagged like a drunk thing, falling and rising, small legs failing him.

This child walked.

Straight up the valley road, through the frost, with a gait too steady for its size, as though it knew precisely where it was going and had known for some time. As though Yara's crooked little house at the valley's end were not an accident of geography but a destination, marked on some map she'd never been shown.

She made herself go back inside. Made herself feed the fire, split kindling, do the ordinary arithmetic of a morning — because the alternative was standing in her doorway watching something approach that her whole body had begun to ring about like a struck bell.

*It's nothing,* she told the kettle. *A lost lamb-chaser. You'll give it bread and point it home.*

The knowing in her chest said nothing at all. It only waited, patient as the border stones.

* * *

By midday the light had gone strange. The hard blue morning curdled into a bruised gray, and wind came down off the western crags smelling of iron and deep snow. Yara had lived in the Hollow long enough to read its weather the way other people read faces, and this was not Hollow weather. The valley kept its own counsel, mild and closed, protected by whatever old bargain slept beneath the border stones. Storms passed around it like water around a stone in a stream.

This storm was coming *in*.

She brought the goats in early, their udders tight and their tempers foul, and she was still wiping down the milking stool when the first thunder rolled — low and wrong, more felt than heard, a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the mountains.

The old knowing stirred again. *Beneath,* it said, without words. *Not above. Beneath.*

"Stop it," Yara said aloud, to herself, to the starved thing inside her. Her voice sounded thin in the empty house. "You're done. We're done. There's no such thing anymore."

The kettle answered with steam. Outside, the first fat drops hit the roof like flung gravel.

She should have barred the door then. Later, on all the long roads of what came after, she would think of that — how she stood with the bar in her hand, the iron cold under her palm, and could not make herself slide it home. Some part of her, the part that had run away from Maren's cottage seven years ago and never once looked back until looking back became the only thing she did, understood that barring doors was how you ended up alone in them.

The storm broke fully at dusk.

It came down the valley like a living thing, rain and sleet together, hammering the turf roof, finding every gap in the shutters and whistling through them in thin mad voices. Yara lit every candle she owned and hated herself for it — candles cost tallow, and tallow cost the winter's fat, and she was a woman who counted things, who had learned counting as a substitute for caring. But the dark tonight had a texture she didn't trust, and the flames stood up straight and burned with a steadiness that had nothing to do with the draftless room, and she chose not to look at that either.

She was ladling barley soup into her one good bowl when the knocking came.

Three knocks. Small ones. Fists too little to make more than a polite tapping against oak, almost lost under the storm's roar — but Yara heard them the way you hear your own name across a crowded market, the way certain sounds are shaped for your ear alone.

She stood very still, bowl in hand, soup steaming.

Three knocks again. Then silence, and beneath the silence, so faint she might have invented it, a voice saying something in no language she had ever heard — and yet.

And yet she understood it.

That was the horror of it. Not the knocking, not the hour, not even the wrongness of a child alone on the Hollow road in a storm that shouldn't exist. The horror was that when the small voice spoke through the door, words in a dead tongue that no living kingdom had spoken since before the kings were carved kneeling in the mountain halls, Yara's mind turned them over and they came out *meaning*, plain as milk, plain as her own thoughts:

*Cold. Door. Please.*

Yara set down the bowl. Her hands were shaking. She wiped them on her apron twice, three times, uselessly, and went to the door, and lifted the bar, and opened it onto the roaring dark.

* * *

The child stood on her threshold caked head to foot in mud, and the mud was wrong.

Yara saw that immediately, before she saw anything else — before the smallness, before the shivering, before the cropped dark hair plastered flat against a narrow face. The mud was wrong because it wasn't valley mud. The Hollow's soil ran red-brown with old iron; everyone in the valley wore its color on their hems from March to November. This mud was black, riverine, smelling of places downstream that Yara had never seen and would never see, because the Hollow did not let its people leave and did not let strangers in, and yet here was its proof otherwise, dripping off a child no older than six or seven onto Yara's clean-swept stones.

"You're freezing," Yara said, which was stupid, because the child couldn't understand her any better than she should have understood it.

But the child looked up.

Its eyes were dark. Not brown-dark, not shadow-dark — dark the way a well is dark, depth without bottom, and in that darkness two small flames reflected. Yara's candles were behind her, across the room; the fire was banked low in its hearth. There was no light source anywhere near the child's face that could have put fire in those eyes.

But there they were. Two flames, burning steady, unblinking, reflected in eyes that did not reflect anything else — not the lightning that chose that moment to split the sky, not Yara's own lantern-lit silhouette leaning over the child. Only fire. Fire that wasn't there.

Every sensible bone in Yara's body screamed at her to close the door.

She stepped aside instead.

"Come in, then," she said, and the child crossed her threshold with that same unhurried deliberation it had shown on the road, leaving small black footprints on her floor, and Yara closed the door against the storm and slid the bar home and thought: *too late, too late, whatever this is, it is already inside.*

* * *

She fed it first. That was the hedge-witch training in her, Maren's voice in her bones — *feed first, question after; a body in shock can't answer and a soul in shock won't.* The child ate barley soup with a spoon held in a fist, methodically, entirely, watching Yara the whole time with those flame-dark eyes. It didn't shiver anymore. Yara noticed that too. The child had been shaking on the doorstep, genuinely shaking, teeth chattering — but inside, warmed, it had gone perfectly, unnaturally calm, like a lamp flame sheltered from wind.

Yara sat across the table and wrapped her hands around her own untouched bowl and tried to be a sensible woman having a sensible evening.

"What's your name?" she asked. "Where did you come from? Who sent you?"

The child swallowed, set down the spoon, and answered.

The sounds it made were nothing — clicks and breaths and vowels shaped strangely in a small mouth, a language like water talking over stones, like wind through a ruined hall. No kingdom spoke it. No kingdom *could*; the scholars said the tongue had died with the pre-kingdom peoples a thousand years ago, ground out between the rise of Aldric's line and the fall of everything before it. Yara knew that the way everyone in the valley knew it, the way you know the shape of clouds.

And she understood every word.

*"The crown is heavy. I carried it far. You are the one who left."*

Yara's blood went cold and stayed cold. "I don't understand you," she said, in the great tradition of liars everywhere.

The child tilted its head, considering her, and something ancient moved behind the small face — not cruelty, nothing so simple, but a weight, an attention, the way a mountain might regard a woman standing on it. Then it spoke again, and the dead tongue came out slow, deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*"Liar. But that is also a kind of leaving."*

Yara stood up so fast her chair went over backward. She caught the table edge. The candles guttered and righted themselves, all together, all at once, though there was no draft.

"I don't know what you are," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like a girl's. "Whatever you are, whatever you've brought with you — I didn't ask for it. I left all that behind. There's no magic past the border stones. Nothing crosses. Nothing *happens* here. That's the whole point of this place."

The child regarded her overturned chair, her shaking hands, the flames burning in its eyes.

Then it reached across the table — small fingers, mud still dried in the creases of its knuckles — and touched the back of Yara's wrist.

* * *

Later, Yara would try to describe what happened next, and she would fail, because language was built by people who had never stood where she stood.

There was no pain. That was the first impossible thing. There was a voice — not the child's voice, not any voice that had ever used a throat — arriving not through her ears but through the place where her skin met the child's fingers, rising up her arm like water up a wick, filling her chest, and it said:

***The oath is breaking.***

Three words. And in those three words fell everything: the fever-dreams of burning crowns that had haunted her for a year, the thunder from beneath the mountains, the storm that had broken a thousand years of the Hollow's sheltered weather to deliver this one small muddy messenger to her door. She saw — not imagined, *saw* — a crown of black iron wreathed in real flame, and beneath it a man's face, kind once, being slowly worn away like a cliff worn by sea, and around the crown a binding woven of a hundred lives, thread after thread after thread, and threads snapping, one by one by one, each snap a small sound like ice cracking on a spring pond—

The vision ended. Yara was on the floor. She didn't remember falling. The child knelt beside her, close enough that she could see the flames in its eyes guttering now, dimming, exhausted, and up close its face was just a face — a tired, frightened, mud-streaked child's face, and Yara understood suddenly that whatever rode behind those eyes, the child itself was real, and cold, and impossibly far from home.

"The oath," Yara whispered. Her lips were numb. "What oath? Whose oath? Child — *whose?*"

The child opened its mouth.

Nothing came out. It tried again, throat working, and what emerged was not the dead tongue, not any tongue — only a dry rasp, air without shape, and the child's face crumpled with something worse than fear: frustration, the fury of the silenced. It pressed its small hands against its own throat as if it could find the blockage, the theft, and its eyes — flameless now, just dark, just wet — filled and spilled over.

Mute. Whatever had spoken through it on the road, whatever had spoken in the dead tongue at her table, the child itself had no voice at all. Yara had heard a ghost of a language through a locked door and a borrowed meaning through a touch, and the actual child — the flesh-and-blood, six-or-seven-year-old actual child — could say nothing.

Except that when Yara, moving on instinct older than her exile, gathered the small shaking body against her chest, the child's hand found hers and pressed it flat, deliberately, over its own heart. And through the contact, faint as a word spoken in sleep, came:

*"Sefa."*

Her name. Or the name it wanted called. The only word it had left.

Outside, the storm raged on, hammering, insistent, and beneath the rain Yara heard it again — that low, vast, subterranean rolling that was not thunder, that had never been thunder, something enormous turning beneath the mountains, restless, waking.

The child — Sefa — burrowed against her shoulder, and slept, just like that, mid-terror, as though a year of exhaustion had finally been given permission.

Yara sat on the cold floor of her house holding a stranger's child, listening to the mountains grind their teeth, and understood with perfect clarity that her uneasy peace was over. Whatever slept beneath the Hollow's quiet, whatever wore a crown far beyond the border stones, had noticed her. Had *sent* for her.

She looked at the door, barred against the storm. She looked at the road beyond it, invisible now, running down the valley and out through the stones toward a world she had spent seven years refusing to think about.

And she thought of the one person alive who might know what a seal-child was, what a breaking oath meant, what to do with a mute girl full of borrowed fire.

Maren.

Seven years of silence. Seven years of shame sitting between them like a third person at the table. To go to her now, hat in hand, with a miracle in her arms and no explanation that wouldn't begin with *I'm sorry* —

The storm threw itself against the roof. Sefa slept on, breathing small and even, one muddy hand still curled in Yara's collar.

Yara closed her eyes.

"In the morning," she said to no one, to the dark, to the starved old knowing that had finally, horribly, stopped waiting. "In the morning, we'll go to Maren."

The fire in the hearth flared — briefly, gratefully, like a held breath released — and settled.

And far beneath the valley, beneath the border stones, beneath the roots of the mountains themselves, something turned over in its long sleep and began, slowly, to smile.
