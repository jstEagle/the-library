## 3. What Maren Knew

The child slept in Yara's bed, and Yara sat on the floor beside it with her back against the cold hearthstone, watching the door.

She had not lit a candle. The storm outside had spent itself sometime past midnight, leaving behind that wet, listening quiet that followed hard weather in the Hollow — the sound of the valley holding its breath. Water dripped from the eaves in slow counts. The goats shifted in their shed, restless. And the child breathed, small and even, with her hands folded on her chest like a little effigy carved for some saint no church remembered.

Yara had tried to sleep. She had managed perhaps an hour, and even then she had dreamed of burning crowns again, only now there was a face inside the fire, and it was the child's face, and it was also somehow her own, and she had woken with her heart slamming and the taste of ash in her mouth.

*The oath is breaking.*

The words had come out of her own throat when she touched the child. She kept turning that over, the way she might turn a stone in her hand looking for what lived underneath. It had not felt like hearing. It had felt like *remembering* — like the sentence had been sitting in her all along, folded up small in some crease of her she'd never thought to look in, and the child's skin against hers had simply shaken it loose.

Which was impossible. Which was worse than impossible, because magic did not work in the Hollow. That was the whole point of the Hollow. That was why Yara was here at all.

She looked at the border stones every morning from her ridge — three grey teeth rising from the bracken where the valley narrowed to its western mouth, older than any kingdom's maps, older than the road that had once run through them and been abandoned. Her mother had told her, before the fever took her, that nothing crossed them. Not spells, not curses, not the long arm of any lord or guild or crown. Things went *in*. Nothing came *out* changed.

And yet a mute child had walked through a storm to Yara's door speaking a dead tongue, and Yara had understood every word, and when she touched the girl's shoulder she had heard a voice say the oath was breaking, and somewhere far off — she could not say how far, only that it was *down*, down under stone and water and years — something enormous had turned over in its sleep.

The child stirred. Her eyes opened, and in the dark they caught the last grey light from the window and held it wrong — held it the way embers hold light, orange at the center, as though something burned behind them that had nothing to do with the moon.

She looked at Yara. She pressed one small hand flat against the blanket, and her mouth shaped a word without sound: *Maren.*

Yara's whole body went cold.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which was a lie so immediate and so complete that she was almost proud of it. "I don't know anyone by that name."

The child just looked at her. Children were merciless that way; they hadn't learned yet that a lie could be left alone if nobody stared at it.

Yara got up, stiff-kneed, and busied herself at the shelf with the clay crocks, moving them for no reason, straightening jars that were already straight. Maren. Twelve years since she'd said the name aloud, and it still came out of her like a splinter — the whole soft length of it barbed at one end.

Maren Ostreyka, hedge-witch of the western slope, who had taken in a sharp-tongued orphan girl of eleven because, she said, the girl argued with herbs the way scholars argued with scripture, and someone ought to teach her which arguments she'd win. Who had taught her the bitter teas and the bone-setting, the reading of weather and wounds, the hundred small crafts that kept a village alive and called themselves medicine so no one burned for them. Who had wanted, in the last year, to teach her more than that. To teach her the other craft. The craft with a capital the girl could feel coming like thunder before it arrived.

And Yara had run.

Not dramatically. That was the shameful part. No slammed door, no shouted curse. She had simply gone out one grey morning to check the snares and kept walking, west, past the border stones, out of the valley where magic couldn't follow her, and she had built this life here — the goats, the garden, the cottage with its one room and its honest smoke — and she had told herself for twelve years that it wasn't running, it was *choosing*, and she had almost made herself believe it.

Almost. Right up until a mud-caked child said *Maren* without saying anything at all.

"She can't help you," Yara said to the child, and hated how her voice cracked on the middle of it. "Whatever you think she is. Whatever you think I am. She's an old woman on a hill who talks to bees, and I'm a goatherd, and neither of us—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Neither of us is whatever you need."

The child reached up and took two of Yara's fingers in her small hot grip. Not pulling. Just holding. And though no voice came, though her lips never moved, the meaning arrived in Yara's mind the way warmth arrives through a wall — not words, exactly, but unmistakable:

*Please.*

Outside, far away toward the western stones, something howled. Not wolf. Wolves sounded like hunger. This sounded like grief with an edge on it.

Yara stood in her dark kitchen holding a stranger's hand, and understood that the peace she had spent twelve years building was already broken, had maybe been broken the moment the child crossed the stones, and that everything she did next was only deciding what shape the ruin would take.

"All right," she said, to the child, to the dark, to the girl she had been. "All right. Damn it. Get your shoes."

* * *

They went up through the birch wood while the sky was still the color of dirty wool, Yara carrying the child on her hip part of the way because the girl's feet were torn raw from wherever she'd walked to get here. The Hollow in early morning was beautiful in the dumb, indifferent way of places that don't know what's coming — mist pooled in the low fields, a heron stood in the shallows of the mill stream like a monk at prayer, somebody's rooster was making an idiot of himself two farms over. Ordinary. Heartbreakingly ordinary. Yara found herself cataloguing it all, the way you catalogue a room you're about to leave.

Maren's cottage sat on the shoulder of the western hill above the apple orchards, and Yara saw the smoke before she saw the house — except it wasn't smoke. It rose too straight, too white, and it didn't thin. It climbed into the grey sky in a single unbroken thread, like a plumb line dropped from heaven, and it did not bend in the wind that was bending the birches around them.

"That's not right," Yara said. Her mouth had gone dry. "That's not—"

The child slid from her hip and ran.

Yara chased her up the final stretch of path, past the beehives — empty, all six of them, their entrances webbed over with dead grass, abandoned — past the herb beds gone to seed and the well rope rotted through and lying in the yard like a shed skin. The cottage door stood open. Of course it did. Maren had never once locked a door in her life. *Locks are for people with something worth stealing,* she used to say, *and witches have nothing but trouble, and trouble comes in whether you lock the door or not.*

Inside, the smell hit Yara first: juniper smoke and old linen and underneath both something faintly sweet and rotten, like fruit going over. The one room was dim, the shutters half-closed, and in the bed beneath the window lay a shape so small and shrunken that for one horrible heartbeat Yara thought it was a bundle of laundry, a scarecrow, anything but—

"Maren."

The old woman turned her head on the pillow. Her hair, once iron-grey and cropped practical-short, was now white as frost and thin enough to show scalp, and her face had fallen in along the cheeks the way faces do in the last chapter of a long illness. But her eyes — her eyes were the same eyes, quick and dark and amused at the world's expense, and they found Yara in the doorway and crinkled at the corners.

"Well," Maren said. Her voice was a dry leaf of a voice, but the dryness had always been half performance, and even now Yara could hear the wit rustling in it. "Look what the storm washed up. Twelve years, Vasquez. I'd have settled for twelve weeks. Twelve days. A postcard."

"I—" Every speech Yara had rehearsed on the walk up, every apology, every explanation, every defense — all of it fell out of her head at once, and what remained was a girl of eleven standing in a doorway smelling of goat. "You're sick."

"You're observant." Maren coughed, a deep bad sound, and waved off the hand Yara instinctively extended. "Sit down before you fall down, and put the child by the fire, she's shivering. There's broth on the hob if the mice have left any. Go on. You remember where things are." A pause, delicate as a blade. "You used to."

The child had already gone to the hearth and crouched there, staring into Maren with that ember-eyed intensity, and Maren stared back, and Yara watched something pass between them — recognition, confirmation, the silent nod of two people who have independently read the same terrible letter.

"Yara," Maren said quietly, all the amusement gone out of her voice. "Shut the door. Then come here and look at me properly, because I'm only going to say most of this once, and I haven't the strength to say it twice, and you're going to want to argue, and we haven't time for you to be right about anything."

Yara shut the door. The white plumb-line of smoke beyond the window did not flicker.

"Show me your arms," Maren said.

"What?"

"Your forearms. Sleeves up. Now, please."

It was such a teacher's voice, such an examination-room voice, that Yara obeyed before she thought about it — pushed her sleeves to the elbow and held her arms out in the dim room like a penitent, like a patient. And there, on the inside of her left forearm, where no mark had been when she dressed that morning, lay a thin grey line, fine as a hair, running from wrist to elbow in a curve that was almost — almost — a letter. Almost a word. In a script she had never been taught and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face across twenty years.

The same script was inked — tattooed? branded? — in faded blue-black along the child's spine. Yara had seen it last night, bathing the grime off her. She had told herself it was a birthmark.

"No," Yara said. "No, I'm not marked, I've never been marked, magic doesn't cross the stones, that's the entire—"

"The stones keep magic *out*," Maren said. "They were never very good at keeping it *in*. Especially not the kind that's already in someone. Especially not," and here her gaze slid to the child, gentle and grave at once, "the kind that walks in wearing its own body and asking at doors."

She beckoned, and Yara came and knelt by the bed, and Maren took her marked forearm in both hands — hands light as birds' bones, trembling — and turned it to the window-light.

"Wasting curse," Maren said. "Mine, I mean. Mine is mine, earned fair, we'll come back to that. Yours is different. Yours isn't eating you. It's *waking* you. That line is a seam, girl. It's been in you since before you could walk, and something on the other side of it has started knocking."

"The voice," Yara whispered. "Last night. When I touched her. *The oath is breaking.*"

"The oath is breaking," Maren agreed. "And you heard it because you were always going to hear it, because you are what you are, and running to the one valley in the world where you couldn't find that out was always going to buy you time and nothing else." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had made eleven-year-old Yara furious and twelve-year-old Yara laugh and twenty-three-year-old Yara, on the last morning, turn away down the west road rather than see it. "Time's spent, my dear. Sit. Listen. Hands busy if it helps — there's wool in that basket, card it, you always thought better with your hands full."

Yara did not take the wool. But she knelt, and she listened, and the child climbed onto the bed's edge and tucked herself against Maren's side as naturally as a kitten, and Maren put her hand on the small head and began.

"They call her kind seal-children," she said. "That's the polite term. There are ruder ones in older books. They are born, not begotten — or if begotten, then by no father you'd want to meet — and each one carries a binding. A great binding. The oldest binding in the world, older than kingdoms, older than the language she speaks, which is not dead, whatever your books say. It's just been locked away where only certain ears can reach it." Her fingers moved absently on the child's hair. "A thing was bound, long ago. Bound into a crown, and the crown bound to a bloodline, and the bloodline thinned to one man, and the man sits a throne in the east and does not know, most hours, what wears him. The seal that keeps the thing fastened there is not a chain. Chains break. It's a person. It has always been a person. Willing, once — the first one gave herself freely, and every seal-child since has been her echo, born when the binding needs mending, dying when the mending's done."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the empty hives hummed with wind.

"And her?" Yara asked. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "This one?"

"This one is the last echo. The binding is failing — you heard it, the oath is breaking — and when a binding fails, the seal-child feels it first. Feels it like the ground going out from under her. So she runs. They always run. Toward the echo of the first binder." Maren's dark eyes came up and rested on Yara with unbearable gentleness. "Toward you, Yara. Because you carry a piece of the first binder's soul. That seam on your arm isn't new. It's just opened."

The room tilted. Yara put a hand on the bedframe. "That's not possible. I'm a goatherd. My mother was a washerwoman, my father was nobody, I have no—"

"Your mother," Maren said, "was found on the steps of the Amberfall temple of the Veiled Lady with a grey line already drawn on her arm, wrapped in cloth woven in a pattern no weaver in this kingdom makes. I told you once, when you were fourteen and asked why I'd taken you on. You didn't like the answer then either."

"I don't remember any answer."

"No," Maren said softly. "I made sure you wouldn't. That was the second-worst thing I ever did, and I'll answer for it, but not today. Today you get the truth and the book and no time at all to hate me properly, which may be the only mercy in this whole rotten morning."

She turned back the blanket, and Yara saw then the full architecture of the wasting curse — saw how it had been drawn onto Maren's own body in that same grey script, lines converging over her heart, deliberate, paid-for. A witch's price, self-imposed. Twelve years of it, maybe more. Twelve years of buying something with her own life, alone on this hill, while her apprentice ran west and told herself stories.

"What did you buy?" Yara whispered.

"A door that stays shut," Maren said. "Twelve years of it. Long enough for you to grow up. Not long enough, it turns out, for the world to stay sensible." She coughed again, longer, and the child pressed close against her, and when Maren could speak she said, "Under the bed. The box with the iron corners. Bring it up. Careful — it bites fools."

The box was oak and iron and heavier than it had any right to be, and when Yara set it on the blanket Maren's hands shook so badly opening it that the child helped her, small fingers deft with the triple latch. Inside, on a bed of oiled leather, lay a book.

Yara had seen grimoires in engravings, in the peddler's forbidden pamphlets, in the fever-dreams she'd never admitted to anyone. This was not grand. It was a working book — thick as a brick, rebound twice in mismatched leather, its spine stitched with wire, its pages swollen and stained and crowded edge to edge with Maren's narrow slanted hand and, beneath that hand, in older inks, the writing of other women going back and back, the earliest entries in the grey script itself. The first binder's hand. Yara knew it the way she knew her own reflection.

"It's yours," Maren said. "It was always going to be yours. I only ever kept it warm."

"I don't want it." The words came out before Yara could stop them, childish and true. "I didn't want any of this. I wanted goats. I wanted a quiet life. I *ran*—"

"Yes," Maren said. "You did. And here's the lesson, girl, the last one, so attend: running is a direction, not a destination. You ran from me and arrived at yourself. Everything you fled is waiting in that book, and everything you'll need is waiting in that child, and the road out of this valley runs east whether you take it or not, because Veyra's riders are already on the border stones — oh yes, don't make that face, the bees told me a week ago, and the smoke tells me things the bees can't — and they cannot cross, but they can wait, and they can starve a valley, and they can burn a world until the only safe place left is wherever you're standing. The crown remembers what the wearer forgets. Write that on the inside of your skull. King Aldric has forgotten everything — his marriage, his sons, his own face on the coin. But the crown remembers. The Wound remembers. And Veyra serves what remembers, thinking she serves the realm, and she will tear this world open seam by seam to bring the child to the mountain where the first oath was sworn, because the binding must be renewed or broken, and she has chosen breaking."

Maren's breath was shortening. Yara could hear it now, the rattle underneath, and terror rose in her like floodwater — not the child's terror, not the world's, but the small human animal terror of losing, again, the only person who had ever—

"I'm sorry," Yara said, and it came out wrecked. "Maren, I'm sorry, I should have written, I should have come back, I should have—"

"You should have," Maren agreed placidly. "And I should have chased you. We're both cowards of long standing; it's how I knew you'd survive this. Cowards make excellent survivors." Her hand found Yara's and gripped it with shocking strength. "Listen. The rite is in the book. The unbinding. Read it and weep, and then read it again, because you'll need to know its cost before you choose it, and you must choose it freely — that's the whole grammar of the thing, freedom is the only lever that moves it. Don't let them tell you what you owe. Don't let me tell you, either. I'm only the map. You're the road."

"Maren—"

"Hush. Moonset's close." The old woman's eyes had begun to shine, fixed on the shuttered window as though she could see through it to the fading moon. "I bought twelve years with this curse and I'd buy twelve more if the market were open, but it's closed, and honestly, girl, I'm tired. I've been tired since the day you walked west and I let you." Her thumb moved over Yara's knuckles. "You were the best thing I ever grew. Better than the comfrey. Don't tell the comfrey."

A laugh tore out of Yara, wet and awful, and the child looked up at the sound and then laid her head against Maren's ribs, and Maren gathered them both as best her bird-bone arms allowed — her runaway apprentice and the last seal-child, kneeling in the juniper-smelling dark of a cottage at the end of the world.

"The crown remembers," she whispered. "So you remember for the ones who can't. That's the whole craft, in the end. That's all any of it ever was."

The moon set. They could not see it through the shutters, but they felt it — a change in the weight of the air, a letting-go — and Maren Ostreyka exhaled once, long and easy, like a woman setting down a load carried up many stairs, and did not breathe in again.

The white column of smoke above the cottage bent, thinned, and scattered on the wind, and the child began, silently, tears sliding down her ember-dark eyes, to speak in the dead tongue — a low flowing lament, formal as liturgy — and Yara, holding a book that bit fools and a hand going cold in hers, understood every word of it, and understood too, with the terrible clarity of grief, that the words were not for Maren.

They were a warning. *Riders,* the dead tongue said, *riders on the stones.*

* * *

Yara buried her at noon, under the apple trees at the orchard's edge, with the grimoire wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat and the child standing witness at the head of the grave in the manner of someone performing an office older than burial. Yara dug until her palms bled because it was that or scream, and when the last spade of earth went on she stood a long time with her hat in her hands.

Then, from the ridge to the west, thin on the wind, came a sound she had never heard in the Hollow and knew instantly, the way you know a knife: the ring of harness, the tread of many horses, and a horn blown once — a signal, not a call. Patient. Certain.

They could not cross the stones. But they had stopped pretending they weren't there.

Yara took the child's hand. East lay the mountains, the throne, the thing wearing the king. West lay the riders and the waiting. Behind her lay a grave and twelve wasted years, and ahead lay everything Maren had died to buy her time to face.

"Right," she said, to the child, to the dead, to herself. "Then we go through."
