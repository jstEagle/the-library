## 4. Riders on the Border Stones

The goats knew first.

Yara had learned to read them the way other people read weather — the flick of an ear, the sudden stillness of a grazing head, the low collective unease that rolled through the herd like a stone dropped into still water. On the morning the riders came, every goat on the south slope lifted its head at once and stared toward the border stones as if something had called their names.

Yara straightened from the bramble she'd been cutting and followed their gaze.

Mist clung to the valley's rim in the early light, thick as wool, and for a long moment she saw nothing but the gray shapes of the standing stones rising out of it like broken teeth. Then the mist moved — not the way mist moves, drifting and dissolving, but the way a curtain moves when someone pushes through it. Shapes resolved within shapes. Horses. Armored men. A banner hanging limp and rain-dark against a lance.

Her stomach tightened around nothing.

They can't cross, she told herself. Nobody crosses. That was the whole of the Hollow's promise, the one thing her mother had said with something like reverence before everything else between them curdled: *Nothing in, nothing out. The valley forgets, and is forgotten.* Magic died at those stones like a moth against glass. Whatever came down from the high road would look at the valley, find nothing worth looking at, and move on.

But these riders were not moving on.

She counted them as they assembled along the ridge above the stones — twelve, then fourteen, then a fifteenth who rode slightly apart, whose horse was taller and whose armor was not the dull iron of soldiers but the black-lacquered plate of someone who had never once been asked to dig his own latrine trench. The banner unfurled at last in a wind Yara couldn't feel down in the valley, and she saw the sigil clearly.

A crown. And beneath the crown, an open eye.

Something cold walked up her spine on small feet. She didn't know the sigil. She had never seen it on any merchant's seal or tinker's wagon, and yet some animal part of her recognized it the way a rabbit recognizes the shape of a hawk's shadow — instantly, uselessly, too late to matter.

Behind her, in the cottage, Sefa was sleeping. Or pretending to sleep. The child had done little else since Maren's death three nights past, curled on Yara's bed with the grimoire clutched against her chest like a talisman, eyes closed, breath slow, and Yara had almost convinced herself that the child was simply grieving in the way children grieve — silently, strangely, without the machinery of words.

But the goats were staring, and the riders were not leaving, and Yara found herself walking up the slope with her staff in her hand before she had decided to walk anywhere at all.

* * *

The border stones stood in a rough crescent along the valley's southern lip, eleven of them, each taller than a man and older than any kingdom Yara could name. Lichen silvered their flanks. Generations of shepherds had carved initials into them, shallow and doomed — the marks always faded within a season, as if the stones refused to hold even the memory of being touched.

The captain of the riders reined in ten paces from the nearest stone and sat there, looking down into the valley, and Yara understood with a lurch that he was looking directly at her. Not at the valley. Not past it. At her.

That was wrong. Everything about that was wrong. The Hollow was supposed to be unfindable, unremarkable, a fold in the world's cloth that the eye slid off like rain off oilcloth. Her whole life, travelers on the high road had passed within a hundred paces of the border stones without so much as turning their heads. She had watched it happen. She had counted on it happening now.

The captain raised a gauntleted hand, and fifteen horses came to a stop as one.

He was younger than she expected — thirty perhaps, with a close-cropped beard going gray at the jaw and a face built for patience rather than cruelty. His armor bore the crowned eye at breast and shoulder, worked in white gold that caught the morning light. He did not shout across the distance. Instead he dismounted, handed his reins to a soldier, and walked forward alone until he stood an arm's length from the nearest border stone.

Then he reached out and pressed his palm flat against it.

Yara's breath caught. Nothing happened — no flash, no scream, none of the stories' punishments. But she saw the captain's shoulders stiffen, saw him exhale slowly through his nose, and when he withdrew his hand there was a mark on the stone where his glove had touched: a faint scorch, already fading, already being forgotten by the lichen and the moss.

"Interesting," the captain said. His voice carried down the slope easily, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud from a report he had already memorized. "It doesn't burn. It *unmakes*. Do you know how rare that is, mistress? Most wards are walls. Walls can be breached, starved, outlasted." He turned his head, and though he was still far away, Yara felt his gaze land on her like a physical weight. "This is not a wall. This is a door that eats anyone who tries the handle."

"Then you've answered your own question," Yara called back. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Go try another door."

"I intend to." He smiled — a thin, professional thing. "But first, courtesy. I am Ser Halven Ostre, captain of the Ninth Vigil, in service to the Crown Regent Veyra, may the realm endure her stewardship." Each title landed like a coin placed precisely on a scale. "I'm told you have something in your keeping that belongs to the Crown. A child. Small, dark-haired, mute — or near enough. She went missing from her minders some weeks ago, and Her Grace has been beside herself with worry."

Beside herself with worry. Yara thought of Maren dying by inches on her cot, of the word *seal-child* spoken with Maren's last strength, of the fever-dreams of burning crowns that had haunted her for years before any of this began. Beside herself with worry.

"She's not here," Yara said.

"No," Ser Halven agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. The valley is empty. It has been empty for as long as the maps have declined to mention it. And yet." He drew something from his belt — not a weapon, but a purse, heavy enough that it clinked when he tossed it lightly in his palm. "Her Grace is generous with those who help her, and patient to a fault with those who don't. Fifty crowns. Gold, minted in Amberfall before Amberfall forgot whose face belonged on its coins. Enough to buy a new life anywhere you please. Enough to leave this—" he gestured at the valley, the goats, the crooked cottage with its smoke-stained roof, and Yara hated how accurately the gesture captured all of it, the smallness, the shabbiness, the years of lonely competence worn smooth as river stone— "behind."

Fifty crowns. It was more money than Yara had seen in seven years. It was, she suspected, more money than the entire valley had seen in seven years. And that was the point. The sum wasn't a bribe; it was a message. *We know exactly what your poverty is worth, and we can erase it with a gesture.*

"The child isn't here," Yara said again.

"Mm." Ser Halven tucked the purse back onto his belt, unhurried, and rested his hand on the border stone again — not touching it this time, just laying his fingers against the air beside it, the way a man might test a fence. "You know what fascinates me about this place? My orders say I cannot enter it. Not *may not* — *cannot*. The Vigil's scholars wrote three treatises on why, and all three disagree. But here is what my own eyes tell me." He leaned closer to the stone, and though his voice stayed conversational, something underneath it had gone quiet and cold. "The ward doesn't keep people out. It keeps the valley *forgotten*. Anyone inside cannot be remembered from without. Which means, mistress, that whoever hides in here is protected only for as long as no one outside remembers to look."

The cold thing on Yara's spine grew claws.

"And someone," Ser Halven continued, straightening, "has remembered. Someone carried word out of this valley — a name, a description, a direction. Someone talked. So the question I would put to you, if you were the woman standing on that slope, is not whether the Crown will find its lost child." He mounted his horse in one fluid motion, gathering the reins. "It is whether the valley will still be forgetting when we come back. Wards are old things, mistress. Old things tire. And Her Grace has scholars of her own."

He did not wait for an answer. He wheeled his horse, and the fifteen riders formed up behind him with the smooth mechanical grace of men who had drilled a thousand times, and they withdrew along the ridge — but not away. They made camp. Yara watched them do it, unhurried, methodical: pickets set, cookfires lit, the crowned-eye banner staked at the center of the little camp like a flag planted in conquered ground.

A siege. Not of the valley's walls — the valley had none — but of its meaning. They would sit there. They would wait. And every day they waited, they would remember harder.

* * *

Sefa was awake when Yara came down the slope, standing in the doorway with the grimoire hugged to her chest, watching the distant smoke of the soldiers' fires. Her eyes — those eyes that sometimes held reflections of flames that weren't there — were wide and fixed.

"They'll be back," Yara said, needlessly, because the child had heard every word. Sound carried strangely in the Hollow; Yara had never tested whether it carried up the slope to the stones, but the captain had spoken as if he expected to be heard, and the captain did not seem like a man who wasted expectations.

Sefa looked up at her. Then, slowly, she raised one small hand and pointed — not at the camp, not at the stones, but at the grimoire. Then at Yara. Then, with terrible clarity, she pressed her fist against her own chest and mouthed a single word in the dead tongue, the language that lived in Yara's understanding like a splinter she'd never known was there.

*Stay,* the word meant. *Remain. Endure.*

And then she shook her head.

No. Don't stay. Don't remain. The grammar of it turned over in Yara's mind, that impossible bilingual knot, until she understood what the child was actually saying, and her knees went briefly weak.

*If we stay, we end.*

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic no one should ever have to do.

The Hollow had food for one person through a hard winter, barely. For two, if the goats gave well and the root cellar held. For two, indefinitely, if nothing changed — if the riders grew bored, if the regent's scholars failed, if the ward outlasted patience and politics alike. Those were enormous ifs. Maren had taught her, in the one year of apprenticeship Yara had allowed herself before running home to safety, that hope was a currency and should be spent as carefully as coin. Yara had spent seven years hoarding it instead, and now the bill had come due with interest.

Because the ward was old. She had known that the way you know a grandmother is old — abstractly, comfortably. But Ser Halven had said it plainly, and plainness was a knife: *old things tire.* What did a ward eat to stay alive? Memory, maybe. Forgetfulness itself. And the world outside had just been reminded, loudly and officially, by fifteen witnesses in crowned-eye steel. Every day the camp sat on the ridge, the siege would grind against the valley's forgetting like water against stone. Maybe it would take a year. Maybe five. But the riders had time, supplies, and the backing of a regent who commanded armies, and Yara had goats.

And there was the other arithmetic, the one she kept trying not to do. If the ward fell while they huddled inside it, they would be caught in a cage with the door kicked open. At least out there—

Out there was magic. Out there was whatever vast hungry world the Hollow had shut away from her for twenty-seven years, the world Maren had tried to prepare her for and Yara had refused to be prepared. Out there were roads and cities and the regent's reach, and a child who was a living seal binding something ancient to a king's crown, and Yara had no plan beyond *away*, which was not a plan, which had never been a plan, which was the exact shape of the mistake she'd made at seventeen when she'd run from Maren's cottage back to the valley and let seven years of guilt grow over the choice like scar tissue.

Running was how she'd gotten here. Running had cost her Maren's final years — Maren, who had died alone except for a granddaughter-apprentice who arrived too late to do anything but watch and inherit. Yara had told herself for seven years that staying safe was wisdom. Now safety had riders camped on its doorstep, counting the days.

She sat on the cottage step until the light went long and gold, turning Maren's grimoire over in her hands. The leather was cracked, the clasps green with age. Inside were wards and rites and a dead woman's cramped, furious handwriting, and somewhere in those pages — Maren had said so with her last breath — was the reason a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice could understand a language no living kingdom spoke.

Sefa came and sat beside her without asking. That was new. For three days the child had kept a careful arm's length of grief between them. Now she leaned, very slightly, so that her shoulder touched Yara's arm, and together they watched the smoke rise from the soldiers' fires on the ridge.

"We can't stay," Yara said aloud. Saying it made it real, made it weigh. "Not because of them. Because of us. There's no version of this where we hide long enough." She laughed, short and bitter. "Seven years I told myself this valley was a sanctuary. Turns out it was just a slower trap."

Sefa said nothing — Sefa said nothing in any tongue common speech could hold — but she reached over and laid her small hand flat on the grimoire's cover, and her eyes reflected fire that was not in the hearth, not in the sunset, not anywhere in the valley at all.

Yara looked at the border stones, silvered and patient in the last light, the stones that had kept her safe and small and sorry for half her life. Tomorrow, or the day after, she would walk up that slope with a child and a dead woman's book, and she would step past the crescent of standing stones into a world that had been waiting for her the way a snare waits.

She was, she realized, already grieving it — the goats, the step, the smoke-smell of her own hearth, the whole small architecture of her exile. And she was already, underneath the grief, feeling something she hadn't felt since she was seventeen and magic had seemed like a door instead of a debt.

Terror, yes. But also the wild, shameful thrill of a door opening.

Above the ridge, the crowned-eye banner snapped once in a wind that never reached the valley, and Yara began, quietly, to pack.
