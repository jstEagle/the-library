## 5. Crossing Over

The border stones of the Hollow did not look like much. That was the first thing Yara had ever learned about them, back when she was small enough to believe the world was larger than it was, and it was the last thing she thought about now, standing before them with a mute child's hand in hers and a dead woman's grimoire wrapped in oilcloth against her spine.

Seven stones, gray and lichen-crusted, set in a crooked arc across the mouth of the valley like old teeth. Goats had rubbed their flanks against them for generations. Moss grew in the letters carved into their faces — letters no one in the Hollow could read anymore, though everyone agreed they were very old and best left alone. Her whole life, the stones had been a fact of the landscape, as unremarkable as rain. You lived inside them. You did not think about what lived outside.

Now she thought of little else.

Pell stood at her hip, breathing fast through his nose, his pack straps creaking with every shift of his weight. He was a goatherd, a poacher of mushrooms, a boy who had once cried for two days over a lamb lost to a winter storm — and he had come anyway, because she'd asked, because Sefa had looked at him with those flame-lit eyes and he had not been able to say no. He kept glancing at the stones the way a man glances at a cliff edge.

"We don't have to do this today," he said, which was the fourth time he'd said it.

"Yes we do." Yara adjusted the grimoire's strap on her shoulder. "The captain said *a week*. Men like that don't say a week and mean a week. They mean until they've decided the waiting is more expensive than the taking."

"The stones won't let them take her."

"The stones didn't stop Maren from dying." The words came out harder than she intended, and she saw Pell flinch. She softened her voice. "They keep things out, Pell. They don't keep us safe from everything. And they don't keep us safe from ourselves." She looked down at Sefa. The child stood perfectly still, mud still dried in the creases of her neck from the night she'd arrived, watching the border stones with an expression Yara had never seen on a child's face: recognition. Like someone greeting a relative they hadn't seen since childhood — one who had changed, and not for the better.

"Sefa," Yara said — the name she'd given her, a placeholder, a sound to call her by. The girl turned her face up. Her lips moved around words in that dead tongue, soft ones, almost a question.

"I know," Yara said, though she didn't, not really. She understood the child's language the way she understood her own heartbeat — without knowing how, without being able to stop. It had been that way since the storm night. Every word out of the girl's mouth landed in Yara's mind already translated, warm and strange, like bread baked by someone else's hands. *Are we going past?* the girl was asking now. *Past the teeth.*

"Past the teeth," Yara confirmed.

Pell made a small noise. "You're talking about it like it's a door."

"It is a door."

"Doors open both ways."

Yara looked at him then, really looked — at the boy who had shared her fire and her silence for three days while they packed, who had fed her goats and promised to leave them with Widow Anselm, who had not once asked her why she was doing this when the answer was written plainly across her face. *Because I ran once already. Because running cost me eleven years and Maren's last breath.*

"That's rather the point," she said.

* * *

They went at dawn, when the mist lay thick along the valley floor and the riders beyond the stones would be cold, slow, and half-blind. Yara had spent the night not sleeping, but reading — or trying to read — Maren's grimoire by tallow light. Most of it resisted her. The script shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it, and some pages were written in that same dead tongue Sefa spoke, which should have been useless and instead settled into her mind like water finding a channel. One passage she read four times, and each time it made less sense and felt more true:

*A seal is not a wall. A seal is a wound kept closed by a living hand. Remember this, whoever you are, wherever you are reading: the hand will tire. The hand always tires.*

She had closed the book on that and not opened it again.

Now, in the gray morning, the three of them walked up the long slope of the valley toward the stones, and the Hollow did what the Hollow had always done: nothing. No wind rose to see them off. No bird sang farewell. Magic did not live here; it could not even visit. The land was honest the way only dead things are honest — grass, stone, sky, goat-track, done. Yara had loved that honesty once. She had fled to it. There was comfort in a place where power couldn't reach you, where the worst thing that could happen was weather.

But she had learned, these past weeks, that the worst thing that can happen is never weather. The worst thing is a knock at your door in a storm. The worst thing is a mentor dying slowly of something with teeth marks in it, and a captain in black armor offering gold for a child as if children were grain.

The stones grew taller as they approached — no, not taller, Yara corrected herself. Closer. As if the arc of them had been far away all along and was now simply near, the way a decision is far away until the moment it isn't.

Sefa stopped walking three paces out.

Yara turned. The girl stood with her chin lifted, nostrils flaring, tasting the air between herself and the stones. And Yara saw — actually saw, in the flat gray light — that the flames in the child's eyes were moving. Not reflecting anything. Burning. Small and steady, like candles behind amber glass.

"What is it?" Yara knelt to her level. "What do you see?"

The girl spoke, and the translation bloomed in Yara's mind unbidden, in a voice that was somehow both the child's and much older:

*"The teeth remember me. They are deciding whether to bite."*

Pell took an involuntary step backward. "What did she say?"

"She says—" Yara stopped. How to explain? "She says the stones know her."

"That's not comforting, Yara."

"No," Yara agreed. "It isn't." She held out her hand. "But she came *to* me, Pell. Out of a storm, across whatever distance, straight to my door, in a valley where magic dies. If the stones wanted her gone, they've had weeks to push her out. Whatever they're deciding—" she swallowed, "—I think they're deciding whether to let her leave."

"And if they decide wrong?"

"Then we'll know very quickly."

Sefa put her small, mud-cracked hand in Yara's. Her palm was hot. Fever-hot, though the child showed no other sign of illness — she had run warm since the night she arrived, and Yara had stopped worrying about it somewhere around the time she'd started being afraid of it.

Together, the three of them walked the last three paces.

* * *

Crossing the border was, Yara would later reflect, the least dramatic moment of it all. One step she was in the Hollow; the next step her boot came down on grass that was, by every measure her senses could offer, identical grass. The mist hung the same. The cold smelled the same. For three full strides, nothing happened at all, and she allowed herself one wild, foolish flicker of hope that the stories were stories, that the border was superstition, that she could walk back into her old life and—

Then the world *inhaled*.

There was no other word for it. The air itself drew inward, a vast and silent intake of breath, and everything changed. Color deepened so suddenly it hurt — the mist went from gray to silver to a shifting mother-of-pearl, and the grass underfoot blazed green as if lit from within, every blade distinct, every dewdrop a lens. Sound arrived from everywhere: the hum of the ground itself, a low note Yara felt in her molars; the whisper of things growing, actual growing, roots pushing through soil at the edge of hearing; and beneath it all, faint and rhythmic, something like a heartbeat that was not coming from any of their chests.

"Oh," Pell said, in a very small voice. "Oh, that's—"

He sat down. Simply folded onto the grass, pale as milk, gripping a fistful of glowing turf as if the world might otherwise shake him loose. Yara knew how he felt. Her own knees had gone watery. Because she had grown up in the Hollow, yes, but she had also been born outside it — she had crossed in as an infant, Maren had told her once, carried through the stones in a basket, and some part of her had always known there was more. Knowing and feeling were different animals entirely. This was like a woman blind from birth seeing color for the first time: not beauty, at first, but *assault*.

And underneath the assault, threading through everything, was the other thing. The hungry thing.

Magic. Real magic, ambient and ancient and thick as summer honey, and it was not neutral. It was not scenery. From the moment they crossed, Yara felt it turn toward them — toward *her*, briefly, a curious pressure against her skin like a finger testing fruit at market — and then slide away, dismissive, uninterested.

It slid toward Sefa.

Yara felt the change in the air before she understood it. The pressure that had touched her swung past like a searchlight and locked onto the child, and the whole bright morning seemed to lean. Mist curled toward the girl from every direction at once, drawn uphill against the breeze. The low hum of the ground sharpened, climbed in pitch, became almost eager. Dewdrops trembled on their blades. And Sefa stood in the middle of it with her arms slightly out from her body, her head tipped back, her burning eyes wide — not afraid. *Recognizing.* The way a drowning woman recognizes water.

"It knows her," Yara breathed. "Pell. Pell, look at me. It knows her, and it wants her."

Pell hauled himself upright by sheer stubbornness, swaying. "Wants her how?"

"I don't know. That's what frightens me." Yara pulled the grimoire around and fumbled it open one-handed, pages riffling in a wind she couldn't feel on her own skin. The dead-tongue passages leapt out at her now, legible, urgent, as if crossing the border had tuned her to them. Her eyes caught fragments: *...the bound shall draw the loose power as a wound draws flies... the seal must be kept from the deep places... where the binding is thickest, the hunger gathers...*

A wound draws flies.

She looked at the child, at the mist streaming toward her like iron filings to a lodestone, and understood that they had not escaped a siege by leaving the Hollow. They had walked out of a fortress and into the open country, carrying the one thing in the world that every hungry, formless, ancient scrap of power could smell.

"We have to move," she said. "Away from the stones, downhill, south — Maren's maps showed a river road. We put distance between us and this place before whatever is smelling her comes closer."

"Comes closer?" Pell's voice cracked. "Yara, what *comes closer*?"

"I don't know!" It burst out of her louder than she meant, and Sefa flinched, and Yara made herself breathe. Made herself kneel, and gather the girl's hot little hands in hers, and speak gently. "I don't know yet. But I know someone who might have. And she left me a book, and I intend to read every word of it." She squeezed Sefa's fingers. "Can you walk?"

The girl nodded. Then she paused, and her brow furrowed, and she said something in her rolling, ancient speech — and this time the translation did not bloom softly in Yara's mind. It *struck* it, like a bell being rung inside her skull, and Yara gasped aloud, because the voice that came with it was not the child's and not the old one either but something layered beneath both, vast and patient and terribly awake:

*"You hear me now. Truly hear. Do you wish to know what I am called?"*

Every instinct Yara owned screamed at her to say no. Names had weight here — she could feel it in the humming ground, in the leaning mist, in the grimoire trembling against her knee. In the Hollow, names were just sounds. Here, a name was a handle. A hook. A door.

But the child was looking at her with those candle-flame eyes, and there was trust in them, and trust was a debt Yara had already failed to pay once in her life, to a woman dying alone in a cottage full of unread books.

"Yes," Yara said.

The mist went utterly still. The hum of the earth dropped away to silence. Even the distant heartbeat paused, as if the whole listening world had leaned in.

And into that silence, direct into Yara's mind — bypassing the child's mouth entirely, spoken in the layered voice of something older than kingdoms — came the name:

***Sefa.***

Not the placeholder she'd chosen. Not a sound, but a *weight*: the name landed in her with the force of a falling star, and with it came a flood she could not have stopped if she'd tried — an image of a hall of kneeling kings turned to gray stone, a crown of black iron wreathed in slow fire, a wound in the world shaped like a person, and a line of small hot-handed children stretching back through centuries like beads on a wire, each one a hand holding a wound closed, each one tired, each one *waiting* —

It broke off. Snapped shut like a trap. Yara found herself on her hands and knees in the luminous grass, blood trickling from her nose, Pell shouting her name, and Sefa kneeling beside her with tears cutting tracks through the dried mud on her cheeks.

The girl spoke, and her own voice this time, small and grieving, rang clear in Yara's newly-opened mind:

*"Now you know why I ran. Now you know what is coming for me. I am sorry. I am so sorry."*

Far to the north, beyond the hills, a horn sounded — long, low, and answered by another, and another, a chain of them rolling nearer through the bright and terrible morning.

Pell hauled Yara to her feet. "Riders," he said. "That's not the captain's men. That's too many horns."

Yara wiped the blood from her lip, pulled the grimoire tight against her spine, and took Sefa's burning hand in hers.

"South," she said. "Run."
