## 6. The Company of the Broken Road

The road is a river and Sefa is a leaf in it.

She knows this the way she knows the words that live behind her teeth — not learned, but remembered from somewhere older than her body. The road flows downhill through country she has no names for, and Yara walks beside her with one hand always near, never quite touching, the way you hover over a candle flame to feel its warmth without burning.

*Pell* is the man's name. Pell of the long legs and the longer silences. He carries their bundle and complains about his feet in a voice like gravel poured slowly, and when he thinks no one is watching he gives Sefa dried plums from a pouch he pretends is empty. She pretends not to notice. This, too, feels like something she has done before, in some other skin, some other century: letting kindness arrive sideways so it doesn't have to explain itself.

The world outside the valley is too much. That is the truth of it, the truth she cannot say because her mouth only opens on the old tongue, the dead one, the one that makes Yara's eyes go wide and frightened every time. Outside the Hollow, everything *speaks*. The grass speaks in green whispers. The stones underfoot hum old arguments. The sky is a bell that has been ringing for ten thousand years and nobody thought to stop it. In the valley, all of that was muffled, wrapped in wool, drowned. Here it pours into her through the soles of her feet and the crown of her head and the soft place beneath her ribs where something ancient sleeps curled like a cat made of embers.

*Do not wake it*, she tells the ember-cat. *Please. Not yet.*

The ember-cat does not answer. It never answers. It only turns over in its sleep, and somewhere far away a king she has never met groans in his golden chair.

* * *

They walk for three days before the road grows teeth.

Sefa smells them first — or rather, she smells what they are not. The travelers ahead have no smell of home-coal, no smell of hearth-smoke baked into wool. They smell of iron and of roads walked too long, and underneath all of it, faint as a fingerprint on glass, they smell of *fear*.

Yara's hand closes on her shoulder. "Stay behind me," she murmurs, though Sefa notes — with the strange floating attention she brings to everything now, everything seen as if through water — that Yara herself has nothing behind which anyone could stay. A hedge-witch who fled her own magic. A woman armed with a grimoire she hasn't opened and a stubbornness the size of a mountain.

The three figures rise from the ditch where they'd been resting like ghosts deciding to be solid again.

The first is a knight — Sefa knows knights the way she knows the word *oath*, from the inside out, from somewhere deep in the ember-dark — but a knight gone wrong somehow, or gone *loose*. His armor is mismatched, a shoulder here, a breastplate there, polished in places and rusted in others, as if he couldn't decide whether he had quit or been quit. His sword stays sheathed, but his hand rests on the hilt the way a man rests his hand on an old grief. He has a face that was probably handsome once and is now mostly jaw and suspicion, with eyes the grey of a blade kept too long in its scabbard.

The second is a woman sitting atop a rolled carpet of leather and paper, and she does not stand at all. She looks at them the way a hawk looks at a field — cataloguing, measuring, already drawing them. Ink stains climb her fingers to the wrist like dark ivy. Across her knees lies a board, and pinned to the board, a map. Sefa stares at the map and the map seems to stare back, and something in it *itches* against the thing sleeping under her ribs, the way a name almost-remembered itches against the tongue.

The third is simply enormous. He rises out of the ditch the way a hill might decide to take a walk, and Sefa's first thought — arriving whole and unbidden, in the dead language, in the language of the first binding — is *stone that learned tenderness*. He is a deserter; she can see it in how he holds himself, shoulders curved inward as if trying to make less of himself, hands open and empty and turned slightly outward, palms showing. A big man making himself small. It breaks something small and warm loose in her chest.

"Well," says the loose knight. "Three travelers on a road with no army behind them and no wagon ahead of them. Either very brave or very stupid."

"Or very poor," Pell says, "which is its own kind of armor."

The knight's eyes move over them — Pell, Yara, and last, longest, Sefa. When his gaze touches her, the ember-cat under her ribs stirs, just slightly, and stretches one paw toward him, and Sefa understands suddenly and completely that this man has broken something. An oath. A vow. Something sworn in fire and iron that he walked away from, and the walking-away still bleeds in him daily.

*Oath-breaker*, whispers the ember-knowledge. *Which means oath-keeper once. Which means it mattered enough to break.*

"Where are you bound?" the knight asks.

"Away," Yara says.

The ink-stained woman laughs — a short, surprised sound, like a hiccup of genuine delight. "Best answer I've heard all season. Most people lie. 'To my aunt's.' 'To the coast.' 'To fortune.'" She rolls up her map with a snap. "Away. Honest."

"Ivetta," the giant rumbles quietly, a warning.

"I'm being friendly, Brannoc. You remember friendly? We read about it in a book once."

* * *

That night they share a fire because the night is cold and pride burns fewer calories than wood.

Sefa sits at the edge of the light, as she always does, watching the flames do their bright dumb dance. Fire is honest. Fire only ever says *I am burning*. Everything else in the world says seventeen things at once and means none of them.

The knight — Doss, the giant called him, Doss — sits across the flames sharpening a knife that does not need sharpening. Men do that, she has noticed. They need their hands to have an opinion so their mouths can keep quiet. Yara sits beside Pell, pretending to sleep, which is its own kind of sharpening.

"So," Ivetta says, ink-woman, map-woman, sprawled against Brannoc's side like a cat claiming a warm rock. "The child doesn't talk."

"She talks," Yara says, without opening her eyes. "Just not to you."

"To anyone?"

"To me." A pause, the length of a held breath. "In a language I shouldn't understand. But I do."

The knife stops moving. Doss looks up. Even Brannoc shifts, the ground complaining softly under him.

"That's not nothing," Doss says carefully.

"No," Yara agrees. "It's very much something. And it's ours."

Sefa watches them circle each other, these strangers, these wounded animals sniffing at the same carcass of trust, and she thinks — in the old tongue, in the tongue of standing kings and buried halls — *this is how it begins. This is exactly how it began the first time.* Because she remembers now, in fragments, in fever-flashes: the first binder also gathered strays. The first binder also sat at a fire with people who owed her nothing and gave her everything anyway. The pattern repeats. Patterns are how the Wound keeps its shape.

*But patterns can be re-cut*, she thinks, surprising herself. *A seamstress said that once. Or will say. Time is funny out here.*

"You're staring at my map," Ivetta says suddenly, and Sefa flinches, caught.

The map is unrolled again between Ivetta's inked fingers, weighted with two stones, and by firelight Sefa can see it properly now: mountains drawn in confident strokes, a river that bends where rivers don't bend, and at the center — freshly inked, the ink still wet enough to shine — a symbol like an eye wearing a crown.

The ember-cat under her ribs stands up.

Every hair on Sefa's body rises. The fire gutters. Somewhere impossibly far away, a king in a golden chair lifts his head.

"What is that?" Yara breathes, and she is awake after all, she was always awake, hedge-witches sleep like cats with one ear cocked.

"A city," Ivetta says, and her voice has changed — lost its hawkish play, gone quiet and careful, the voice of someone admitting to a sickness. "Except it isn't. Not yet. That's the trouble with my maps." She taps the crowned eye. "I draw places that don't exist. Haven't existed. Sometimes I go looking for them and find a village that will become the city in fifty years, or a ruin that was one three hundred years ago, or—" she hesitates "—or nothing at all, yet. But this one came to me three nights ago. Drew itself, near enough. I woke with ink on my fingers and this on the page and no memory of the doing."

"And the sigil?" Doss asks.

His voice is wrong. Sefa hears it go wrong — flat, stripped, a soldier's voice, a voice from behind a wall he built a long time ago. He sets down the knife. He leans forward into the firelight, and the flames paint his scarred jaw gold, and the ember-cat purrs against her ribs like it recognizes him, like it has *waited* for him.

"The crowned eye," Doss says. "Say where you saw it, Ivetta. Exactly where."

"On a rider's shield. Two days south. A column of them, maybe forty, riding hard for the mountain passes. Black shields, silver sigil. They didn't stop for anything. Didn't camp. Just — flowed past like water finding the low road."

Doss closes his eyes. When he opens them, the wall behind his voice has cracked, and what shows through is old and tired and terribly certain.

"That's Veyra's mark," he says. "The regent's. The crowned eye — the realm watching over the king, they call it. Pretty words." He spits into the fire, and the flames hiss like something offended. "I wore that mark for six years. I was at the gate the night the old marshal's family was arrested. I stood post while they were taken. And the next morning I took off the sigil, left it on the sergeant's table, and walked out of the garrison before dawn, because I understood — finally, too late — that the eye wasn't watching over the king." He looks at Sefa. Only at Sefa. "It was watching *for* something. Waiting for orders. And the orders weren't coming from any living mouth."

The fire pops. Sparks climb toward the indifferent stars.

"Forty riders," Pell says slowly, "riding for the passes. Toward us. Away from Amberfall, away from anywhere worth taking."

"Toward the mountains," Ivetta whispers. Her finger drifts along her impossible map, following the road they themselves have walked, and stops at the crowned-eye city-that-isn't, and then continues, up into the high stone country, into the blank space beyond the map's edge where she has written, in her neat cartographer's hand: *here the old world begins.*

And Sefa knows — with the certainty of the ember-cat, with the certainty of the seal, with the terrible borrowed knowledge coiled in her small mute chest — exactly what lies in that blank space. Standing kings. Kneeling kings. A hall of stone where the first oath was spoken over a wound that has never stopped bleeding.

*They are coming to me*, she thinks. *Or to what I am. Or to what I hold.*

Her hands begin, very softly, to shake.

Yara sees. Of course Yara sees — Yara, who fled magic and got chased down by it anyway; Yara, whose hand finds Sefa's small shaking fist and closes around it, warm and callused and absolutely unwilling to let go.

"They're coming for her," Yara says. Not a question.

"Forty riders don't ride for a child," Doss says.

"This one they would," Yara says.

Silence around the fire. Brannoc has gone very still, the stillness of a man listening to something far off, or remembering something close. Ivetta's fingers hover over her map as if the wet ink might still change its mind. Pell chews a plum stone and watches everyone with the patient arithmetic of a man counting exits.

Then Doss does something Sefa does not expect. He picks up his knife, and instead of sheathing it, he drives it point-first into the dirt between himself and the fire — a boundary marker, a grave for something, a promise.

"I ran from that sigil once," he says. "Left it on a table and walked into the dark and told myself the story was over. That whatever the crowned eye wanted, it wasn't mine to fight." He looks around the fire — at Pell, at Ivetta, at Brannoc, at Yara, and last and longest at Sefa. "It followed me anyway. It always follows. So." He shrugs, a small bitter motion. "I'm done being followed. If those riders want the child, they come through four people with nothing left to lose and one enormous man who's very hard to kill."

"Five," Yara says quietly.

Everyone looks at her.

"Five," she repeats, and her chin comes up, and Sefa sees the moment the lonely woman who tended goats in a valley that forgets decides — visibly, like weather changing — to stop running. "You'll want a witch. Trust me. Before this is over, you'll want a witch very badly."

Brannoc smiles. It transforms his whole vast face, the way sunrise transforms a cliff. "Company of the Broken Road," he rumbles. "That's what we are, isn't it? All of us walked off some road we were supposed to stay on."

"Speak for yourself," says Ivetta. "My road doesn't exist yet. I'm walking ahead of schedule."

But she is smiling too, ink-stained and fierce, and Pell sighs the sigh of a man accepting a fate he saw coming three days ago, and Doss retrieves his knife and begins, finally, to actually sharpen it.

Sefa sits in the firelight among them — the oath-breaker, the map-maker, the gentle mountain, the dry-voiced man, the runaway witch — and the ember-cat under her ribs curls tighter, and for the first time since the valley, since the storm-door, since the beginning of everything, she is not afraid of the dark beyond the fire.

*This is how it begins*, she thinks again. But the thought has changed shape. *This is how it begins — the found thing. The made thing. The family that isn't born but built, plank by plank, out of the shipwrecked.*

She falls asleep to the sound of Doss's whetstone, slow and steady as a heartbeat, and dreams — for the first time in her short long life — not of fire.

She dreams of a door, standing open, with lamplight spilling out of it, and voices inside arguing cheerfully about whose turn it is to cook.

* * *

They break camp at grey dawn, five adults and one silent child, and the road takes them north toward the mountains, toward the blank space on the map, toward the place where the old world begins.

It is Ivetta who spots it first, an hour after sunrise. She stops mid-stride, unrolls her impossible map against Brannoc's broad back, and goes the color of old milk.

"Sefa," she says, and her voice is very small. "Come look at this."

On the page, in ink that was certainly not there yesterday, a new line has drawn itself along the mountain road ahead of them. Not a city. Not a river. A single sentence, in Ivetta's own unmistakable handwriting, though her ink-stained fingers haven't touched a pen since midnight:

*Forty riders. Two days. They know the child's face.*

And beneath it, smaller, pressed so hard the nib nearly tore the page:

*So does the crown.*
