## 7. The Free City of Amberfall

The walls of Amberfall rose out of the morning haze like the ribs of some enormous, patient beast — pale stone veined with amber quartz that caught the light and held it, so that the whole city seemed to glow from within, as if it had swallowed a sunset years ago and was still digesting it. Ivetta had seen it twice before in her life, both times as a girl, both times from a merchant's cart with her father's hand heavy on her shoulder and his voice murmuring the city's history like a litany: *last free city of the western reach, never taken, never bought, never kneeling.*

She had believed it then. Standing at the head of the caravan road now, with forty miles of dust on her boots and a child's cold hand tucked into hers, she found the belief had worn thin as her map paper.

"They're burning the outlying farms," Doss said quietly beside her. He nodded toward the eastern horizon, where three columns of smoke stood up straight against the windless sky, black and orderly, the smoke of deliberate fires rather than accidents. "Scorched-earth doctrine. Veyra wrote it herself, back when she was still just a colonel clearing bandit holds in the Marches. You burn everything within a day's march of your objective so nothing can feed or shelter your enemy." He paused. "Or shelter anyone running from you."

Yara shifted Sefa higher on her hip. The child had not spoken since the map had spoken to them — had not made a sound at all, which was somehow worse than the dead-tongue whispering. She only watched the walls with those eyes that sometimes caught flame where there was none, and pressed her thumb against Yara's collarbone in small, absent rhythms, like a heartbeat checking itself.

"Will they let us in?" Brannoc asked. He had to keep his voice low even to be heard by five people; anything louder seemed to frighten him, a man built like a granary who spoke like an apologetic librarian.

"The gates are open to refugees," Ivetta said. "That's the whole point of Amberfall. It's mustering every soul the free towns can spare." She hesitated, then said the rest of it, because someone had to: "Which means the gates are also open to Veyra's spies."

Doss grunted, which from him was agreement.

They joined the river of the displaced flowing up the caravan road — farmers with their livestock on ropes, a glassblower carrying his kiln door on his back like a turtle's shell, old men pushing barrows heaped with things no one fleeing should have bothered to save: portrait frames, wedding linens, a taxidermied owl with one glass eye. Nobody spoke much. Fear, Ivetta had long observed, did not make people loud. It made them quiet and particular, counting what they carried, touching what they loved.

At the gate, a bored sergeant of the city watch looked at their little company — a hedge-witch with a child, a disgraced knight whose armor had been sold piece by piece down the road until only his sword remained, a giant deserter, and a woman with ink-stained fingers and a satchel full of maps that lied — and waved them through without a second glance. That, more than anything, frightened Ivetta. She had spent three days watching Doss watch crowds, and she knew what he saw: that in all this river of misery, no one had looked at Sefa twice.

*Forty riders,* the map had written. *They know the child's face.*

Someone in this crowd knew exactly what they were looking for, and was being very patient about it.

* * *

Amberfall inside its walls was a city holding its breath and pretending to exhale. The great market square, which Ivetta remembered as a sprawl of silk and spice and shouting, had been converted into a drill yard. Companies of militia — farm boys mostly, in quilted jacks and spears taller than they were — stumbled through formations under the bellowed instruction of mercenaries hired at ruinous rates. Banners hung from every balcony: the amber stag of Amberfall, the crossed hammers of the smith-guilds, the white tower of the Reach. And above them all, from the citadel, the king's own banner — the crowned stag — though everyone knew King Aldric had not been seen outside his palace in two years, and everyone said it differently depending on how much they'd had to drink.

Ivetta found them lodging in the Rookery, a warren of rented rooms above a chandler's shop in the tanners' quarter, because Doss insisted on a building with two exits and a landlord too deaf to answer questions. Then, while Yara fed Sefa bread and honey and Brannoc fell asleep sitting upright like a horse, Ivetta went to do the thing she had come to the city to do.

"I need to see the Council," she told the chamberlain's clerk, a young man with a beautiful doublet and the harried eyes of someone who had said no four hundred times that week.

"Petitions are heard on Thirdday."

"It concerns the regent Veyra's army."

"Petitions are heard on Thirdday."

"It concerns a child the regent's riders are hunting across half the kingdom."

Something flickered behind the clerk's beautiful boredom. He glanced past her shoulder, at the crowded antechamber, at the hundred other petitioners with their hundred other emergencies. "Madam," he said, not unkindly, "everyone in this city believes their matter is the one that matters most. The council has three hours a day and a war. Take your number."

He handed her a wooden token stamped with the number sixty-one.

Ivetta stood there holding it, feeling the absurdity of it in her palm — sixty-one, behind fifty-nine others, behind the widow claiming her late husband's pension and the miller disputing a boundary stone — and understood with sudden clarity why kingdoms fell. Not to armies. To queues.

It was Doss, when she returned and told him, who laughed — a short, bitter sound. "Of course. I sat in on councils for six years under Veyra. You know what decides wars, Ivetta? Not courage. Not prophecy. Agendas. Whoever controls the order of business controls the world." He turned the token over in his scarred fingers. "But there's another way in. The council's master of works is an old comrade of my father's. Halvard. He owes me nothing, but he hated Veyra before hating her was fashionable. If anyone can get us heard, it's him."

"And if he reports us instead?"

Doss smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Then we'll know where we stand. Which is more than we know now."

* * *

Halvard received them that evening in the master of works' office beneath the citadel — a low stone room lined with shelves of building records going back four centuries, lit by lamps that turned everything the color of honey. He was a barrel-chested man with a surveyor's squint and mortar permanently ground into the creases of his knuckles, and when Doss walked in, he looked at him for a long moment and said:

"You have your father's chin and none of his sense. Sit."

He listened. That was the remarkable thing — while the council clerk had given her sixty-one, Halvard gave Ivetta his whole attention, elbows on the table, while she laid out the story: the seal-child, the crown, the regent's riders, the map that wrote itself. She kept nothing back except the rite, which Yara had asked her to keep back, and which she suspected Yara herself did not yet fully understand.

When she finished, Halvard was silent for a while, turning his lamp wick lower with one thick finger.

"A child," he said finally. "Binding a king. Sealing something under a crown." He rubbed his jaw. "Girl, I've spent thirty years keeping this city's walls standing. Do you know what I've learned? Every disaster arrives dressed as something unbelievable, and the wise men always wait until it's believable, and by then it's through the gate." He sighed. "I believe you. Gods help me, I believe you because I've read the old foundation surveys, and there are chambers under this kingdom that no record explains. But believing you and moving the council are two different animals entirely."

"Then move them," Doss said.

"With what? Look out that window." Halvard pointed at the drill yard, visible as a smear of torchlight through the arrow-slit. "Four thousand militia, maybe six by month's end. Half of them have never held a spear. Veyra has twenty thousand veterans and the road network to move them wherever she pleases. When I bring the council a child instead of a wall repair estimate, they will laugh me out of this room, and then they will ask me why I'm wasting their hour on fables while the enemy burns the wheat fields." He spread his huge hands. "Bring me proof that matters to *them*. Bring me something about grain, or roads, or walls. That's the language this council speaks. Everything else is poetry, and poetry doesn't stop pikes."

* * *

It was later that night — the others asleep, the chandler's shop below breathing its faint smell of tallow through the floorboards — that Ivetta unrolled her maps again and found the answer to Halvard's demand, though it was not the answer anyone wanted.

The new ink had come while she slept. It always came while she slept, or while she blinked, or while she looked away; her maps were written by some hand that used hers only when her hands were idle. This time the additions covered the eastern sheet, the one showing the mountain country beyond Amberfall — the Grey Teeth, the locals called the range, though no trade route crossed it and no mine worked it, and Ivetta had always drawn it, per her sources, as blank high country.

It was blank no longer.

A road ran through it now, fine-lined and precise, climbing from the eastern plains in switchbacks up a pass she had never charted because no map in any archive charted it. The road descended on the far side into a valley, and in the valley, drawn in that same handwriting that was hers and not hers, stood a symbol she recognized from the oldest plates in the Amberfall archives — the mark the pre-kingdom peoples had used for a place of oath-taking. A circle. A point within it. Radiating lines like a wound given light.

And around the symbol, in letters pressed deep enough to score the paper:

*Veyra marches here.*

Ivetta sat back. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like a drum struck once and still ringing.

She thought: *grain.* Halvard wanted grain, roads, walls. Very well. She got out her supply ledgers — the ones she'd begun keeping the day they'd fled the Hollow, because a cartographer knows that armies are logistics wearing armor — and she did the arithmetic she should have done days ago. Veyra's host, twenty thousand strong, had left the royal heartlands eleven days ago. Their magazines could carry them fifteen days. After that, they lived off the country — but the country ahead of them was the burned farmland, and beyond it the Grey Teeth, where nothing grew and nothing grazed.

An army does not march into barren mountains unless it is going somewhere specific, and quickly, and with a purpose worth starving for.

She overlaid the sheets. The supply lines, the scorched farms, the impossible road, the symbol of the oath. They fit together with the terrible click of a lock accepting its key. Veyra wasn't marching on Amberfall at all. Amberfall was the diversion — the city that would mass its militia on its walls and wait for an assault that would never come, while the real army slipped east into mountains no map admitted existed, toward the place where, a thousand years ago, something had been bound beneath a crown.

Toward the Oath's original site. Toward whatever waited there that a seal-child was born to hold shut.

"Oh," Ivetta said aloud, to the empty room, and her breath fogged the lamplight. "Oh, you fools. You magnificent, doomed fools. She's not besieging you. She's *bypassing* you."

The floorboard creaked behind her. Yara stood in the doorway wrapped in a blanket, Sefa's small shape curled asleep against her shoulder, and Ivetta realized she must have been standing there long enough to read the maps upside down.

"You've seen it," Yara said. Not a question.

"See for yourself." Ivetta turned the sheets toward her. "Your council of walls and grain — Veyra has already counted them and moved on. Every spear they sharpen in that drill yard is being sharpened for the wrong war."

Yara studied the map for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was hollowed and older than her years, and Sefa stirred against her shoulder without waking, one small hand opening and closing, opening and closing, as if grasping at something in a dream.

"Then we go to the council tomorrow," Yara said. "All of us. And if they won't listen—"

"They won't listen."

"—then we don't need them to." Yara lifted her eyes, and Ivetta saw in them the same thing she had first seen on the road out of the Hollow: not bravery, exactly. Something more stubborn than bravery. "We need them to *hesitate*. One day of hesitation, and we're through the pass ahead of her."

Below them, in the sleeping city, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bells, but the deep, slow tolling of the citadel's night watch, marking the hour. Ivetta counted the strokes out of habit. Eleven.

And on the eastern sheet, even as they watched, fresh ink seeped up between the fibers of the paper like blood through a bandage, drawing itself in that handwriting that was hers and not hers, adding a final line beneath *Veyra marches here*:

*Nine days. Then the mountain closes.*

Ivetta looked at Yara. Yara looked at Ivetta.

"We leave at dawn," they said, together.
