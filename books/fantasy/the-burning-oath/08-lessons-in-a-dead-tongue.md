## 8. Lessons in a Dead Tongue

The safehouse Ivetta had found them was an old tannery at the edge of Amberfall's lower terraces, abandoned long enough that the smell of its former trade had faded into something almost gentle — old wood, cold ash, the ghost of salt. Rain came off the mountains in slow gray sheets, drumming on the slate roof, and for three days no one had come looking for them. For three days, Yara had let herself pretend they were only travelers waiting out weather, and not fugitives carrying the most dangerous child in the world.

It was in that pretense that the lessons began.

They started by accident, the way most true things do. Sefa sat cross-legged on the floor by the cold hearth, sorting through a pile of river stones she'd collected from the yard, and when she held one up to Yara — a flat gray pebble, nothing remarkable — she said a word.

*"Sess."*

Stone. Yara knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, without knowing how she knew it. She blinked at the pebble, then at the girl.

"That's stone," Yara said. "In common speech. *Stone.*"

Sefa's dark eyes went wide. She turned the word over in her mouth — *stone* — and something happened to her face that Yara had not seen before: not the grave stillness the child wore like armor, but a crack of pure delight, quick as a fish rising. She snatched up another pebble. *"Dun."*

"Water."

*"Dun,"* Sefa repeated, pointing at it, then at Yara's mouth, demanding the common word again.

"Water."

By evening they had filled half a slate with words. Fire. Sky. Hand. Bread. Sefa learned them the way dry ground learns rain — greedily, everything soaking in at once, nothing running off. And Yara learned too, though what she learned frightened her more each hour, because every dead-tongue word Sefa gave her arrived wrapped in something else.

* * *

It was the word for fire that showed her first.

*"Vash,"* Sefa said, holding her small hands toward the hearth where Doss had coaxed a blaze an hour before. And Yara saw —

— a fire that burned black at its heart, ringed in white, standing in a hall so vast its ceiling was lost in smoke and shadow. The fire did not flicker. It stood like a pillar, patient as a mountain, and around it knelt figures in robes gone gray with age, their foreheads pressed to cold stone. And above the fire, above the kneeling, hung something that watched. Not eyes. Not a face. A *presence*, crowned in broken light, and Yara felt it notice her across an ocean of years, felt its attention settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck—

She gasped and the vision tore away. The hearth-fire was ordinary orange. Sefa was watching her with an expression far too old for her face.

"You see it," the girl said. Not a question. One of her new common-speech sentences, assembled carefully, like a child stacking stones. "When I give you words. You see."

"What is it?" Yara's voice came out rougher than she intended. "What am I seeing?"

Sefa considered for a long moment. When she answered, she used the dead tongue first, then reached for the common words like someone wading into deep water. "Old. Before kings. It was—" she frowned, dissatisfied, tried again. "It fell from between. From the place where nothing is. And it was hungry, because it had been nothing for so long. So they bound it. The first ones. The word-binders."

"Bound it *where*?"

Sefa touched her own chest. Then her own brow. Then, slowly, she drew a circle in the air above her head — a crown's shape.

Yara felt the floor tilt under her certainties. "A person. They put it in a person."

"Not put." Sefa shook her head hard, frustrated, searching. She took Yara's hand — her grip surprisingly strong — and pressed Yara's palm flat against her own sternum. "Not cage. Not prison-box. *Give.* Someone gave. Willing." Her common speech failed her; she slipped back into the old language, and the word she chose carried its vision whether Yara wanted it or not:

*"Oth-varess."*

Yara saw a woman. That was all, at first — just a woman standing at the edge of that black-hearted fire, thin as a winter reed, hedge-witch poor, hands cracked from labor and herb-staining. But the vision had weight, and the weight was grief, and the grief was *chosen*. The woman knelt of her own will. She opened her own mouth and breathed in the Crowned Wound the way a drowning woman breathes water, and she did it *gladly*, because the alternative was watching it eat the world, and love — love for people whose faces blurred in the vision's depths — was stronger than fear.

The vision broke. Yara was on her knees and hadn't remembered falling. Her heart slammed against her ribs. Sefa knelt with her, small hands hovering near Yara's shoulders, not touching, waiting — the way you wait with a spooked animal.

"The binder," Yara whispered. "The first one. She was — she was nobody. A hedge-witch. Why her? Why would they let it be *her*?"

Sefa said something soft in the dead tongue, and this time the meaning came plain, unaccompanied by vision, as if the language itself were being gentle with her: *Because the small ones love the largest, and the large ones never know it until they are asked.*

"I don't understand."

"Me neither," Sefa admitted, and for the first time since the storm door, since the mud and the burning eyes, she laughed — a small, startled sound, immediately covered by her hand as if she'd broken something. But it was out. It hung in the smoky air of the tannery like a struck bell.

Yara found she was laughing too, wet-eyed and shaky, and didn't fully know why, except that terror and tenderness had gotten tangled somewhere inside her and couldn't be separated anymore.

* * *

The lessons became the shape of their days.

Mornings belonged to common speech. Yara wrote words on the slate and Sefa copied them in a careful, angular hand, muttering each one like a spell she intended to master. Afternoons belonged to the dead tongue, and those afternoons Yara both craved and dreaded, because every fragment Sefa taught her came freighted with the Wound's history, and the visions grew longer, closer, more detailed — as if each word opened a door a little wider, and something on the other side had begun to notice the draft.

*"Hallow-maren,"* Sefa said one afternoon, and Yara stood in a harvest field under a green sky while the Wound — newly fallen, newly hungry, wearing no crown yet, only raw need — walked through the wheat and the wheat died in rows behind it like soldiers cut down. She saw the first binders' faces as they made their plans: not heroes. Farmers, midwives, a smith, a wandering singer. Ordinary people deciding, together, that someone must pay so that everyone else might keep their small lives. She heard them argue about who. She heard the hedge-witch volunteer before the argument could finish, and heard the relief in the room, and hated them all a little for it, and understood them completely.

Another day: *"Sereth-kai,"* the word for oath, and Yara saw the binding itself — not chains, not circles of chalk, but a conversation. The Wound spoke. That was the horror of it; it *spoke*, in a voice like a thousand bells rung underwater, and it bargained, and the hedge-witch bargained back, and the terms of the seal were terms of surrender: a soul given freely, renewed in echoes down the years, each seal-child a verse of the same long poem. The Wound agreed because a willing vessel held tighter than any chain. Chains can break. A gift cannot be stolen back without breaking the giver.

Yara came out of that one with tears freezing on her cheeks in the tannery's chill, and Doss found her there, and didn't ask, just sat beside her on the floor and handed her his own supper bowl because she'd missed hers. Brannoc, saying nothing, draped a blanket over both of them. Ivetta pretended very hard to study her maps. None of them asked, and that not-asking was its own kind of language, and Yara was beginning to understand it fluently.

But at night, alone, she let herself ask.

She lay on her pallet listening to the rain and turned the visions over like stones, looking for what lived underneath. The binding used a person. Willingly given. Renewed in echoes. Every seal-child since has been her echo — Sefa had said it plainly enough, or the language had said it through her. Which meant the line was a line of *people*, daughters of the bargain, each one living and dying as a lock. Each one perhaps dreaming the others' dreams. Each one perhaps, in some buried fold of the soul, *tired*.

And there was the thought she could not put down, the one that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: the binding was failing. Maren had said so. Sefa's arrival said so. And bindings made of willingness fail when the willingness fails.

What happens, Yara wondered at the dark ceiling, when the last echo decides she has given enough?

* * *

On the sixth day, Sefa taught her the word for mother.

*"Emm-ua."*

The vision took her gently, which somehow made it worse. No fire, no falling thing. Just the hedge-witch — the first binder — in a small cottage much like Maren's, teaching a child of her own to speak. Pointing at things. Naming them. *Stone. Water. Fire.* The child laughing. The mother laughing. And underneath the whole warm scene, quiet as groundwater, the knowledge the mother carried every single day: *I am a door, and my daughter will be a door, and someday a granddaughter, and we will hold, we will hold, we will hold—*

Yara surfaced with Sefa's face inches from hers, searching her.

"You cried," Sefa observed.

"A little."

"In the seeing, or in the here?"

Yara huffed out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Both. Mostly here." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "That woman. The first one. She had a family."

"Yes." Sefa looked down at her slate, at the angular rows of common words. "Every echo does. That is why the line wants to end." She said it simply, the way children say enormous things, and then looked up. "But ending is not allowed. So we get tired instead."

"*We*," Yara repeated softly.

Sefa's chin lifted a fraction — stubborn, suddenly every year of her strange age. "You carry a piece of her. I have seen it since the valley. You hear the tongue because her tongue is in you, sleeping. I do not know why you. I only know *you*." She tapped Yara's sternum, once, over the heart. "So when I say *we*, I mean you also. I'm sorry."

Sorry. As if it were Sefa who had done the sentencing.

Yara pulled the girl against her side, and Sefa allowed it — stiff at first, then settling, the way a wild thing settles when it decides the warmth is real. Outside, the rain kept on. Somewhere below the city, Veyra's armies were marching toward something beneath the mountains, Ivetta said. Somewhere a king wore a crown that wore him back. But in the old tannery there was slate-dust and rain-sound and a child's head heavy against her shoulder, and Yara thought: *this is what the first binder fought for. Not the world. This exact weight. This exact ordinary evening.*

"Doss says you'll leave soon," Sefa murmured, drowsy. "To the mountains. Under them."

"Soon," Yara admitted.

"Will it hurt? The unbinding. Ivetta and Doss talk low but I hear low."

Yara hesitated. She had read Maren's grimoire by lamplight these past nights, deciphering Maren's cramped hand, and she knew more than she'd said aloud. The rite needed a witness who shared memory with the bound. She had told no one yet what that requirement meant — that the witness's memories would be the coin paid, that the rite consumed what the witness loved. She had been carrying that alone, telling herself she'd find another way, knowing she wouldn't.

"It might," she said honestly. "But some hurts are doors, little one. You go through them to reach the other side."

Sefa was quiet a long moment. Then, in the dead tongue, soft as an ember settling, she gave Yara one last word for the day.

*"Valess."*

No vision came with it. Just the meaning, arriving clean and bare, the way the most important meanings do:

*The debt that love pays gladly.*

Yara closed her eyes and held the sleeping-weight of the child and thought about Maren's grimoire hidden in her pack, and about how many debts of that kind one heart could pay before there was nothing left of it to pay with.

Above the tannery, the rain thickened toward snow. In three days, Ivetta's maps said, they would march for the mountains — and whatever waited beneath them, in the hall where kings knelt in stone, already knew they were coming.
