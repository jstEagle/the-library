## 9. The Regent's Shadow

Doss had learned to sleep in armor the way other men learned to pray — badly, and only because he had no choice. In Amberfall's undercity, in a rented room above a dyer's shop where the walls wept indigo stains and the floorboards smelled of vinegar and old fish, he slept with his sword unsheathed across his chest like a lover he no longer trusted.

He woke because the dogs stopped barking.

That was the thing about Amberfall that had never changed, even in the nine years since he'd last walked its crooked streets: the undercity kept dogs, and the dogs kept watch, and when strangers came down the stair-streets from the lawful city above, the dogs announced them with a chorus of complaint that could wake the drowned. But tonight the silence was total, thick as tallow, and Doss lay beneath his blanket with his hand already on the crossguard and understood that someone had come who knew how to make dogs quiet.

Not kill them. Anyone could kill a dog. Poisoning took time and left evidence; blades made noise and grief. No — to *quiet* a dog, all of them, across three warrens of tenement and tannery, required something else. It required a powder called hushmeal, ground from pale mushrooms that grew only in one place Doss knew of, mixed into meat and scattered on a schedule, street by street, by teams moving in coordination. He had watched Veyra's quartermasters invent it. He had helped carry the first sacks.

He was moving before his mind finished the thought, boots in hand, sword belt cinched over his nightshirt, because there was only one reason soldiers of the regent's shadow came into an undercity at the hour when even smugglers slept.

The child.

* * *

The company had taken rooms in three separate houses, per Ivetta's insistence, though Yara and Sefa shared the nearest — a garret above the dyer's shop, close enough that Doss could hear Yara's low voice through the wall some nights, teaching the girl words. *Water. Bread. Door.* And Sefa answering in her dead tongue, each syllable landing in Doss's chest like a stone dropped down a well, because he did not know why the language unsettled him, only that it did, the way a battlefield unsettled a man who had buried too many friends upon one.

He crossed the landing in four strides. The stairs were narrow, spiraling, lit by nothing but the memory of light. Below him, through the gaps between steps, he saw it: a thin line of lantern-glow where no lantern should be, sliding along the base of the door to Yara's room.

Smoke. They'd use smoke first — sweet-smelling, fast-working, poured under doors from clay flasks. Then they'd go in with knives and do it quiet, because a dead child was worth more than a living one now. A living child could be rescued, could rally sympathies, could grow up with grievances. Veyra had taught him that too, in another life, in a lecture hall with maps on the walls: *A martyr is a debt the realm pays forever. A corpse is paid once.*

Doss did not take the stairs. He went through the window.

Amberfall's undercity was built in layers, generation stacked atop generation, and a man who had grown up here — as Doss had, before the knighthood, before the fall — knew that every wall was a ladder if you hated it enough. He dropped onto the dyer's awning, rolled, hit the alley mud, and came up running along the front of the shop toward the outer stair. Behind him, inside, he heard the soft *pop* of a flask seal breaking.

He took the stairs three at a time. The door to Yara's room was already breathing smoke through its seams, thin white tendrils curling like fingers testing a lock.

There were two of them at the door. Good ones — he could tell by the stance, weight forward, blades low and reversed, the economical stillness of professionals waiting for the drug to finish its work. They wore no colors. Of course they wore no colors. But their boots were military issue, oiled black, and the shorter one held his knife in a grip Doss had drilled into a hundred recruits himself.

The old shame rose in him like floodwater. He let it carry him instead of drowning him.

"Oi," Doss said pleasantly, from the bottom of the stairs. "You're standing in my doorway."

They turned well — he'd give them that. The tall one came down the stairs to meet him rather than letting him choose the ground, which was correct doctrine, and Doss felt a flicker of professional pride even as he broke the man's wrist with the pommel of his sword and put him face-first into the steps. The short one was faster, better; he got a cut across Doss's forearm before Doss caught his blade-arm in the crook of his elbow and squeezed until something gave with a sound like wet rope snapping. The man made no noise at all. Discipline. Veyra's people never screamed; she'd forbidden it, said pain was information, and information belonged to the realm.

Doss headbutted him into unconsciousness anyway. Some lessons took longer than others.

Then he put his shoulder to the door of Yara's room and the lock burst like a rotten tooth, and the smoke rolled out around him, sweet as candied pear, and he heard Yara coughing, heard the scrape of a shutter being wrenched, and he plunged in.

* * *

Later — after the window was open, after Sefa had been carried gasping into the cold night air, after Yara had thrown up in the gutter and sworn at him in two languages for not warning her, after the two assassins had been trussed with the cords off the dye-shop's drying lines and dragged into the cellar — later, they sat in the cellar among the vats and the reek of mordant, and Doss told them the truth.

It came out of him the way an arrow comes out of a wound: badly, tearing, with a great deal of blood.

"I served her," he said. "Veyra. Before she was regent. Before Aldric ever took the crown." He sat on an overturned dye vat, his arm bound in a strip of Yara's petticoat, which she had torn off with the brisk fury of a woman who resented having to be grateful. Sefa sat pressed against Yara's side, watching him with those eyes that sometimes caught flame that wasn't there. Tonight, in the lantern-light of the cellar, they looked almost ordinary. Almost a child's eyes. That was worse, somehow.

"I was born four streets from here," Doss went on. "Undercity brat. The army was the way out — everyone knows that, everyone says it, and it's even true. I was good. Better than good. By thirty I wore a captain's badge and carried dispatches for the Crown Guard, and the woman who read those dispatches was Veyra Solenne, then just a colonel, then a marshal, then—" he turned his hand over, palm up, the gesture of a man showing an empty cup, "—the regent. The power behind a boy-king who never seemed to age out of his tutors' shadow."

"You deserted," Yara said. Not a question. Her voice was flat, but Doss had spent nine years learning to hear what lived underneath flat voices, and underneath hers was fear. For herself. For the child. Perhaps, though she'd deny it to her grave, for him.

"The night she took power," Doss said. "You want the whole ugly story? Fine. You've earned the whole ugly story, since your throats are the price of me keeping it."

He looked at Sefa. The girl looked back, unblinking, patient as a stone saint.

"There was a village," he said. "Harlow Fen. Out past the eastern marches, marsh country, poor as sin. There'd been trouble there — a tax revolt, nothing much, a burned granary, a dead assessor. Standard misery. Veyra was sent to restore order. I rode with her column. Two hundred men." His hands had gone still in his lap, very still, the stillness of a man holding something down. "We found the village empty. They'd fled into the fens. All of them — grandmothers, babes, the lot. And Veyra stood at the edge of the water and looked at it for a long time, and then she said, 'If they will not be governed, they will be examples,' and she ordered the reeds fired."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, the dyer's shop creaked as its owner shifted in his sleep, nine streets and a world away from knowing what was stored beneath his floorboards.

"Wind was wrong that day," Doss said. "Fire jumped the channel. Burned four square miles of fenland. Killed everything in it. When it was done, Veyra stood in the ash and ash-fall was coming down on her like grey snow, and she turned to me — to *me*, because I was her best captain and she wanted witnesses she trusted — and she smiled. She smiled, and she said, 'Now write the report, Doss. Write it so the realm can sleep.'"

"And you wrote it?" Yara asked quietly.

"No." The word came out of him rough as gravel. "I wrote it. That's the shameful part, girl — I wrote it, exactly as she asked, beautiful and clean and full of lies, and I sealed it, and I handed it to her courier. And then I couldn't sleep. Three nights I couldn't sleep, and on the third night I got drunk in the officers' tent and I heard her talking to herself in the dark — talking to *someone*, low and steady, conversational, like a woman taking counsel — and there was no one in the tent with her. No one. And the next morning I saddled my horse and I rode west and I never stopped riding until the road ran out of kingdom."

Yara had gone very pale. "Talking to whom?"

"I didn't know then." Doss met her eyes. "I know now. Or I know what I believe. She took power that same season — the old king died, the boy was crowned, and Veyra's hand was on the crown when it settled onto his head. And I have spent nine years telling myself I ran from a butcher. That's the comfortable version. But I've been listening to this child breathe for a week, and I've seen what follows her, and I'll tell you what keeps me awake now." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the dripping cellar wall. "I don't think Veyra is the disease. I think she's the symptom. I think something has been whispering in the dark to powerful people for a long, long time, and I think it's wearing the king's crown, and I think tonight's work — hushmeal in the dogs' meat, knives in the dark, a child murdered in her sleep — I think that's not policy. Policy doesn't smell like that. That smells like *hunger*."

Sefa moved. It was small — a shift of her weight, a lifting of her chin — but both of them turned to look at her, because in a week and a half they had learned that the child did nothing without meaning.

She pointed at Doss. Then she touched her own throat. Then she made a shape with her hands — two hands, closing, like a book shutting, or a mouth.

"She's saying you're marked," Yara translated softly. "Or— no. Wait." Her brow furrowed; Doss watched her wrestle with the current that ran between her understanding and the dead tongue, that impossible gift none of them could explain. "Not *you*. Your name. She says... your name is known. Was known. To the thing below." She swallowed. "She says it remembers captains."

The cellar felt suddenly colder than the night outside.

"That's madness," Doss said, and heard how little conviction the words carried.

"It usually is," Yara said. "That's never once stopped it."

* * *

They argued about flight until the small hours. Ivetta, woken and fetched, arrived with her map-case already strapped on and her hair pinned up with whatever had been nearest — a stylus, Doss noticed, jabbed through the knot like a dagger, and decided not to ask. Brannoc filled the cellar doorway entirely and said nothing for a long time, which from Brannoc was a speech.

"We run," Ivetta said, unrolling a chart across a dye-vat lid, weighting the corners with candle stubs. "South road, then the salt flats. Amberfall's council won't shield us — they'll trade us to Veyra the moment she offers them anything, because councils are machines for converting other people's children into peace treaties. We go south, beyond the marches, and we disappear."

"With what?" Yara demanded. "Every rider she owns behind us? And then what — we live in a hole for twenty years while that thing eats the kingdom? While it finds another child?" Her hand closed protectively around Sefa's shoulder, and the girl leaned into it, and Doss thought: *there it is. There's the thing I deserted Harlow Fen rather than become, and there's the thing I should have been.*

Brannoc spoke at last, his voice a landslide heard from far away. "The big one," he rumbled, nodding at the taller of the two trussed assassins, who was beginning to stir. "He wakes soon. Waking men talk or die. Choose before he does."

But Doss was barely listening, because the tall assassin *had* stirred, and had turned his head, and was looking at Doss with the flat professional appraisal of a man cataloguing inventory — and Doss knew that look, had worn that look, and knew what came after it.

Recognition.

The man's gaze went from Doss's face to the scar on his jaw — gotten at the ford at Callowmere, in Veyra's service, in the old life — and then to the way Doss stood, weight balanced, sword-hand loose, and something kindled in the assassin's eyes that was worse than hatred. It was certainty.

"Well," the assassin said. His voice was hoarse but steady. "Captain Drenn. Nine years, and the realm's best deserter crawls out of his hole to die in a dye-cellar." He smiled with cracked lips. "She never struck your name from the rolls, you know. Veyra keeps them. Every name. She said—" and here he pitched his voice lower, mockingly gentle, an impression, and Doss's blood went to ice because the impression was *good*, "—she said, 'Loyalty is a ledger, Captain. Debts keep.'"

The cellar was silent except for the drip of mordant and the sound of Yara's breath going shallow.

"They know my face," Doss said quietly. "Which means by tomorrow they know all of ours. We're not travelers anymore. We're a named company with a price on it, and every garrison from here to the sea will have our descriptions by week's end." He looked around at them — the cartographer with her impossible maps, the giant with his gentle ruined hands, the hedge-witch and the child who dreamed in fire — these strangers who had somehow, without his noticing, without his permission, become the only people whose deaths would actually grieve him.

"So much for disappearing," Ivetta said bitterly.

"So much for running," Doss agreed. He straightened, and his arm throbbed, and he found — to his surprise — that the throb felt almost like relief. Nine years of running, and here at last was the place the road had been taking him: a cellar that smelled of dye and lies, beside people worth standing for, with the regent's shadow stretching toward them across the whole breadth of the kingdom.

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the first bell of dawn began to toll from the temple district — and then stopped mid-swing, choked silent, and did not begin again.

Brannoc tilted his great head. "Bells don't stop," he said.

"No," Doss said, reaching for his sword. "They don't."
