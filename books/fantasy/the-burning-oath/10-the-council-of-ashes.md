## 10. The Council of Ashes

The council chamber of Amberfall had been a granary once, in the years before the walls grew tall enough to need one. Ivetta knew this because she had drawn it — not the chamber, but the granary, from the memory of an old woman who sold honeycakes near the Tanner's Gate and remembered when her grandmother had measured out winter barley on this very floor. The old stones still showed the ghost-lines of the grain bins, worn channels where a thousand harvests had been dragged and poured, and now the city's worthiest sat upon benches built over them, deciding whether to give away a child.

Ivetta sat at the back with her maps rolled tight against her chest like a shield that had never once worked. She had asked to speak. She had been granted three minutes, timed by a clerk with a sandglass and the particular patience of a man who has watched many people waste his time.

"Cartographer Ivetta of the Broken Road Company," the herald announced, and there was something almost apologetic in it, as if her name were an unfortunate smell someone had carried into the room.

She walked the length of the floor anyway. She had learned long ago that institutions respected nothing so much as a person who behaved as though they belonged there. Her boots rang on the old granary stone. Forty faces turned toward her: guildmasters, harbor-lords, two generals whose armor was newer than their courage, and First Speaker Ondrel, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had already made up their mind before she reached the third bench.

"You have three minutes," Ondrel said.

"I'll need less." Ivetta unrolled her map on the council table, weighting the corners with the salt cellar, a candlestick, and a small brass model of the city's lighthouse that some dignitary had left behind years ago. "This is the Veyra Road. This is the pass above the Undermount. And this—" she tapped a place where her own ink showed a valley that no surveyor had ever recorded, a valley ringed by hills that did not exist on any chart but hers, "—is where her army is actually going."

Murmurs. Someone laughed, briefly, and stopped when no one joined them.

"Your maps show places that aren't there," said General Corvane, a broad woman with a voice like a door slamming. "We've discussed this."

"They show places that don't exist *yet*," Ivetta said. "I know how it sounds. I've lived with how it sounds for eleven years. But every map I've ever drawn ahead of its time has come true, General, and this one is coming true faster than most. Look at the roads. Look at the supply lines. Veyra isn't marching on Amberfall. She's bypassing you entirely. All those soldiers, all those siege engines you're counting and losing sleep over — they're headed beneath the mountains, to a place older than any kingdom on my charts. She doesn't want your city. She wants what's under it."

"And what is under it?" Ondrel asked, and his voice was soft, which was worse than if he'd shouted.

"A child," Ivetta said. "A child who is holding shut a door that must not open."

The silence lasted four heartbeats. Ivetta counted them, because counting was what she did instead of screaming.

Then Corvane leaned forward. "A child. You are asking this council — this *city* — to stake its survival on the word of a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice, a knight who deserted his post, a giant who deserted his army, and a mute girl who speaks a language dead before our grandmothers' grandmothers were born. That is your testimony."

"That is my family," Ivetta said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and hated herself for it, because cracks were things you could follow into ruin. "And yes. It is also my testimony."

Ondrel folded his ink-stained hands. "Ivetta of the Broken Road. Do you know what Veyra offered us?"

The room went very quiet. Even the sandglass seemed to hold its breath.

"She sent terms last night," Ondrel continued. "Not an ultimatum. Terms. She will lift her blockade of the harbor. She will recognize Amberfall's charters in perpetuity. She will withdraw her armies beyond the Veyra Road and pay reparations for the fishing fleets she burned at Saltmere. In exchange, she asks for one thing only." He looked at Ivetta with something that might, in a kinder man, have been regret. "The child. Delivered unharmed, with her companions free to go wherever they wish, provided they leave the realm."

"There are no terms with that woman," Ivetta said. "There is only the shape of a trap, and she's very good at making the shape look like mercy."

"The alternative," Corvane said, "is siege. Winter is coming. Our granaries—" and here she gestured, deliberately, at the floor, at the ghost-channels of the grain bins, and Ivetta understood that the general had chosen this chamber for exactly that reason, "—hold perhaps ninety days. Her army holds forever. You ask us to choose a child over ten thousand lives."

"I'm asking you to understand that the child *is* the ten thousand lives. That everything outside these walls — every farm, every harbor, every city you're trying to protect by giving her what she wants — ends the moment that door opens. I've seen it. Not dreamed it. *Seen* it, in lines I drew before I understood what I was drawing. Cities burned from the inside out. Rivers running backward. A crown of fire sitting on a throne of ash."

"Visions," someone muttered.

"Maps," Ivetta corrected. "Everything I draw comes true. Ask me why. Ask me what I gave up for it. Go on — I have time now, apparently, since my three minutes don't matter."

But Ondrel was already rising, and the council was already moving through the forms, and Ivetta stood at the edge of the great table watching forty people do arithmetic with a child's life. She thought of Sefa that morning, sitting on the steps of the safehouse, patiently teaching Yara the word for *rain* — a word that sounded, in the dead tongue, like something being forgiven. She thought of the child's eyes reflecting flames that weren't there, and how lately Ivetta had begun to wonder whether the flames weren't there at all, whether everyone else simply couldn't see them yet.

The vote was not close. It was not even dramatic. That was the worst part — how ordinary it sounded, the rustle of robes, the clearing of throats, hands raised one after another like wheat bending before a storm that hadn't arrived yet but always would.

"By majority," Ondrel said, "the council of Amberfall accepts the Regent's terms. The child will be delivered to the Veyra Road within three days, under escort, unharmed." He paused, and added, as though it mattered, "The council thanks the cartographer for her service."

*Her service.* As if she were a hired wheelwright. As if she had not just watched them sell a living girl the way her grandfather had sold horses — with inspection, with haggling, with a handshake at the end.

Ivetta rolled up her map with steady hands. She had learned that too, in eleven years of drawing futures nobody wanted: the world did not end with thunder. It ended with procedure.

She walked out of the granary without looking back, and went to find her family, and to tell them it was time to run.

* * *

They left by the coal chute.

It had been Brannoc's idea, in the end — the giant deserter crouched in the safehouse kitchen, turning an apple over in hands that could have palmed a loaf whole, saying quietly, "My mother kept pigs. Pigs find the ways people forget." And Doss had stared at him, and then at Ivetta, and Ivetta had already been pulling out her maps, because of course he was right. Every city forgot its own underside. Amberfall had forgotten hers so thoroughly that the coal chutes of the old foundry district opened onto tunnels no guard had walked in sixty years — tunnels that existed on no map but hers, because she had drawn them eight months ago, in a room that smelled of tallow, without knowing why.

Now she knew why. Now the why was sprinting beside her through the dark with a child on her back.

Yara ran ahead with the lantern shuttered to a coin of light, grimoire strapped across her chest like armor. Doss brought up the rear with his sword drawn, though against whom he could not have said — guards, perhaps, or the dark itself. And Sefa rode Ivetta's shoulders with her small hands knotted in Ivetta's hair, silent as always, except that twice Ivetta felt the child's lips move against her scalp, shaping words in the dead tongue, and each time the tunnel air warmed by a degree Ivetta would have sworn to.

"Left at the fork," Ivetta whispered. "Then down. There's a stair cut into the rock, older than the city. It comes out behind the tanneries, past the wall, into the drainage cut."

"You're certain?" Doss breathed.

"I drew it," Ivetta said. "It's true."

Behind them, muffled by stone, the city was waking to its decision. Bells — not alarm bells, not yet, but the summoning bells of the watch-houses, calling guards to assembly. Somewhere a herald was reading the council's decree aloud in the squares: *the child will be delivered; the city will be spared; all citizens will remain indoors.* Ivetta imagined neighbors hearing it, nodding, relieved. She imagined the honeycake woman bolting her shutters. She imagined the whole bright body of Amberfall turning over in its sleep, comfortable, complicit, saved.

"They'll search the safehouse first," Yara murmured. "Then the gates. Then—"

"Then they'll remember the old quarter drains somewhere," Doss finished grimly. "How long do we have?"

"An hour. Perhaps two. Less, if anyone down here remembers their grandparents' stories."

They came to the stair. It was exactly as Ivetta had drawn it: steps cut by hands that predated the kingdom, descending into a blackness that swallowed the lantern light whole. Brannoc went first, testing each step with his weight, and the others followed in his footfalls like ducklings after something far more dangerous than a mother.

Halfway down, the sound began.

Boots. Above them, faint but multiplying — boots on foundry floors, boots in the upper galleries, the disciplined cadence of the Amberfall watch sweeping the district grid. Ivetta pressed against the wall and closed her eyes and saw her own map in her mind, saw the red thread she had inked along this very route eight months ago, and understood with cold clarity that she had drawn this night too. The pursuit. The gate. The choice Brannoc was going to make, though he didn't know it yet, though she couldn't warn him because she had never learned how to tell people the futures she carried for them.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, not knowing yet whom she was apologizing to, and took Sefa's hand, and kept climbing down.

* * *

The drainage cut let them out behind the tanneries, where the stink hid them better than any warding. Beyond lay the marsh fields, then the river road, then — three days hard walking — the foothills, and beneath them, waiting in Ivetta's unfinished maps, the mountain hall where all of this had been heading since a fever-dream first taught Yara Vasquez to fear burning crowns.

They nearly made it.

They were in the reed-banks along the cut, moonless, moving by touch and memory, when the lanterns bloomed behind them — a dozen, two dozen, strung along the wall-top like a festival gone wrong. A voice called out through a speaking-trumpet, young and unhappy about its errand:

"In the name of the council of Amberfall! You are ordered to halt! The child is to be returned! No harm will come to anyone who submits!"

"No harm," Doss repeated softly. He had put himself between the lights and the others, sword low. "That's what they told the Saltmere fishermen, I imagine."

"We can outrun them to the marsh," Yara said. "The reeds go on for a mile. They can't search it all before—"

"They don't have to search it all," Ivetta said. Her mouth had gone dry. She knew this ground. She had drawn this ground. "There's a sluice-gate. Iron, old, half-rusted. It crosses the cut where the marsh narrows. If it's closed, we're wading through shin-deep mud with lanterns on us. If it's open—"

"Then someone opens it," Brannoc said.

Everyone turned to look at him. The giant stood in the reeds with the water to his knees, and in the distant lantern light his face wore the same gentle expression he wore when feeding stray cats behind the safehouse, or carrying Ivetta's pack when her shoulder ached, or listening to Sefa's silent stories with the patience of a man who understood that some words take their time.

"It's an iron gate," he said. "Rusted. It'll need holding while the rest of you get through. And then it'll need..." He considered. "...holding."

"Brannoc." Yara's voice cracked. "No."

"They'll not kill me," he said, and smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ivetta had ever seen, sadder than any burning city on any of her maps. "I'm worth more taken than dead. A deserter of the Ninth, famous size, they'll want me standing trial. Trials take time. Time is what you need." He looked at each of them in turn, memorizing, the way Ivetta memorized coastlines. "Doss. Get them through. That's an order. I was a sergeant once; I never got to give the order that mattered."

The lanterns were closer now. Shouts in the reeds. The splash of men wading.

Brannoc turned and walked toward the sluice-gate, and even his walking was calm, unhurried, a man crossing his own yard at dusk. Ivetta wanted to call out — wanted to tell him she had drawn this, that she had known for eight months, that knowing should count for something, should buy *something* — but the words stuck in her throat like the tannery stench, and all she could do was grab Sefa's hand and run.

The gate groaned as Brannoc's shoulders met it. Behind them, iron shrieked against iron, and then held — held against the weight of the marsh current and the weight of the man and the weight of every pursuing boot that struck the far side and rebounded. Through the reeds Ivetta heard the shouting tangle and break apart, heard the trumpet-voice crack with frustration, heard — once, terribly — Brannoc laugh, that deep gentle laugh, as though the whole thing were a game his mother's pigs had gotten them into.

Then the reeds swallowed the sound, and the marsh swallowed the reeds, and the company of the broken road ran north with one member fewer, and Ivetta ran with Sefa's hand in hers and tears freezing on her face and the map of the mountains burning behind her eyes, every line of it true, every line of it paid for.

* * *

They walked through the hours before dawn in silence, because silence was all any of them could carry.

When the sky grayed, they stopped in a hollow of gorse below the river road, and Yara built no fire, and Doss cleaned a sword that did not need cleaning, and Ivetta unrolled her maps on the wet grass with hands that shook until Sefa, without a word, climbed into her lap and pressed both small palms flat against the paper, holding it still, holding *her* still.

"He's alive," Yara said at last. It wasn't hope, exactly. It was arithmetic, done aloud, the way the desperate do sums. "They need him breathing. Trials. Ransoms. He bought us time, so they'll keep him alive to—"

"Yes," Doss said, in the voice of a man cutting off a wound's description before it sickens everyone. "He's alive. And we will get him back. After."

"After," Ivetta echoed. The word tasted like ash and ink.

Sefa stirred in her lap. The child turned, and looked up at Ivetta with those eyes that held flames no one else could see, and lifted one hand from the map, and touched Ivetta's cheek — gently, the way you touch a thing you're afraid of breaking. Then she spoke. Not the dead tongue. Common speech, rough and new, three words she must have gathered from weeks of listening, spent now like coins saved for something precious:

"Not. Your. Fault."

Ivetta broke then, quietly, the way she did everything — thoroughly, and alone, except that for the first time in eleven years she wasn't alone at all. Yara's arm came around her shoulders. Doss looked away and pretended the horizon required his attention. And Sefa stayed in her lap, warm and solid and heavier than any child should be, heavy as a sealed door, heavy as a future.

Far to the south, behind them, Amberfall's morning bells began to ring — the cheerful bells, the market bells, the bells of a city telling itself it had been saved. Ahead, north and east, the mountains waited under cloud, and beneath the mountains, in halls of kneeling stone, something old was stirring toward an ending Ivetta had already drawn.

She wiped her face, took up her charcoal, and began, with a steady hand, to draw the road they would take to get their giant back.

The first line came true even as she drew it.
