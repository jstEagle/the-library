## 11. The Grimoire's Price

The grimoire did not want to be read.

Yara had suspected as much from the moment she'd lifted it from Maren's deathbed chest — a book bound in grey leather gone soft as old skin, its spine cracked like a riverbed in drought. Maren had warded it the way she'd warded everything: not with grand sigils but with small stubbornnesses, knots of intent tied into the stitching, a charm sewn into the binding thread that smelled faintly of rosemary and iron when Yara pressed her thumb against it. *You'll have to earn me,* the book seemed to say. *Like everything else she ever taught you.*

So Yara earned it. Three nights in the loft above the stable where the company sheltered outside Amberfall's walls, she sat cross-legged with a stub of tallow candle and worked through Maren's cipher — the same cipher Maren had used on Yara's copying lessons a lifetime ago, each rune shifted by the number of letters in whatever word Maren had been angry about that week. It had been a joke once. *A witch's temper is the best lock there is,* she'd said, laughing, and young Yara had laughed too, before she'd understood that every lesson was also a door being quietly locked behind her.

Now the anger-words were all Yara had left of her, and she used them like keys. *Ungrateful.* Nine letters. *Stubborn.* Eight. Once, heartbreakingly, near the middle of the book: *Yara.* Four.

She stopped then. She let the candle gutter and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw stars that weren't stars, and downstairs Doss's low voice rumbled through the floorboards, telling Sefa some story or other, and the child's rare, rusted-hinge laugh came up through the wood like something growing toward the light.

Then Yara wiped her face, trimmed the wick, and went back to work. Because the book had finally told her what it was for.

* * *

The unbinding rite filled eleven pages, and Maren had written it out three times — once in her early hand, neat and confident; once in a shakier script annotated with corrections; and once, at the very end, in letters so small and cramped they looked like a confession rather than a spell. Between the versions, in the margins, Maren had argued with herself across decades. *No. The witness cannot be unbound themselves.* Then, years later, in different ink: *I was wrong. The witness must be.*

Yara read the passage seven times before she let herself understand it.

*The Crowned Wound is not a thing held by chains. Chains can be found and broken. It is held by an oath, and an oath is held by memory — the memory of the one who swore it, and the memory of the one who witnessed it sworn. To unbind, both memories must be brought to the same fire. The seal-child carries the binder's oath. The witness must carry the witness's half: must share memory with the bound, must be of the binding's blood in soul if not in flesh, or the words will pass through them like wind through a broken window and the Wound will keep its crown.*

*There is only one way to be such a witness now. The first binder poured herself into the sealing. What remains of her has passed down, echo to echo, seal-child to seal-child — and sometimes, rarely, sideways. Into others. Into hedge-witches who hear dead tongues without learning them. Into women who touch a strange child in a storm and know her name before anyone speaks it.*

Yara set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Outside, rain ticked against the shutters, and somewhere below Brannoc was snoring with the deep contentment of a man who had decided, provisionally, that the world was survivable.

*Sideways. Into others.*

She thought of the fever-dreams that had haunted her in the Hollow — burning crowns wheeling over the valley like carrion birds. She'd told herself it was guilt. Guilt over Maren, over the apprenticeship she'd run from, over every harsh word she'd never unsaid. She'd built a whole life on that explanation, the way you build a hut on ground you refuse to examine too closely.

But magic could not cross the border stones. That was the whole point of the Hollow, the whole reason she'd chosen it — no power could reach her there, no curse follow her in, no whisper of the wide world's sorrows touch the goats and the garden and the small hard silence she'd made of her days.

And yet the dreams had come. And yet a child had spoken a language dead a thousand years, and Yara had understood her — not learned her, not puzzled her out, but *understood*, the way you understand your own heartbeat. The Hollow hadn't kept it out because it had never been outside her. It had been riding in her all along, quiet as a passenger, patient as stone.

*A fragment of the original binder's soul.*

She said it aloud to the empty loft, and the words sounded less like a revelation than a diagnosis.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Toward the grey hour before dawn she went down into the yard, and found Ivetta already awake at the map-table under the eaves, as she always was, her impossible maps spread around her like a hand of cards she was still deciding how to play.

"You look," Ivetta said without looking up, "like someone who found the answer and wishes she hadn't."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've got Maren's ink on your fingers and a face like a woman reading a bill she can't pay." Ivetta tapped the table. "Sit. Talk. I'm better at listening than I look."

Yara sat. For a while she said nothing, watching the rain bead and run down the eaves, and Ivetta, to her credit, let the silence do its work. That was the thing about the cartographer — she understood that some truths needed to be approached the way you approached an unmapped river: slowly, from high ground, looking for the ford.

"There's a rite," Yara said finally. "In the grimoire. It can free Aldric. Unmake the binding entirely — not patch it, not pass it to another child, but end it."

"That's good news." Ivetta's quill paused. "That's the first good news we've had since Amberfall, and you're saying it like a funeral."

"The rite needs a witness. Someone who shares memory with the original binding. Someone who carries part of the first binder." Yara turned her hands over, looked at the ink stains on her fingers as if they might explain something. "That's why I understand Sefa. That's why the dreams followed me into the Hollow. There's a piece of a thousand-year-old witch living in my head, Ivetta, and it's been there my whole life, and I never knew because it never had a reason to speak until a mute child walked through my door."

Ivetta set down her quill. "And the cost?"

Of course she asked. That was the other thing about Ivetta — she never flinched from the margin notes.

"Memory," Yara said. "The rite burns the witness's memories as fuel. All of them. Every person she loves. Every bond. It takes the whole ledger — that's what makes it strong enough to unmake an oath that old. You can't hold back a corner of it. Maren tried to design a version with a remainder, a keepsake, and the margins say it fails. The Wound keeps whatever you keep. It uses it. A single remembered face is a door left open." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for delivering bad news to herself. "I would walk out of that hall knowing no one. Not Sefa. Not you. Not Doss, not Brannoc. Not Maren. I wouldn't even know what I'd lost. That's the worst of it — there'd be no grief. Just an empty shape where a life used to be, and strangers crying over me and no idea why."

The rain filled the pause. Ivetta studied her for a long moment, and then, instead of arguing — instead of saying *there must be another way* the way kind people do, which is its own kind of cruelty — she pulled a map toward herself and began, quietly, to draw.

"What are you doing?"

"Making a copy." Ivetta's pen moved in quick sure strokes. "Of everything in this book that matters. Routes, names, the rite itself if you'll allow it. Because here's what I know about maps, Yara: a place isn't gone just because the traveler forgets it. It stays drawn. It stays *findable*. Other people can carry you back to it." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce behind their tiredness. "I don't accept your arithmetic. You're counting the cost like it's paid alone. Nothing on this road has been paid alone yet."

Yara wanted to believe her. She looked at the maps instead — the real ones and the impossible ones, cities that didn't exist yet sketched in Ivetta's confident hand, waiting to be true — and she thought: *she draws things into being. Maybe she thinks memory works the same way.*

Maybe it did. But the grimoire's margins were full of Maren's certainty otherwise, and Maren had spent forty years studying exactly this, and Maren had died with the knowledge locked behind nine-letter curses so that only the student she trusted most could ever reach it.

Which meant Maren had known. Had read these same pages, decades ago, and understood what they meant for the girl she was raising — and had written the cipher anyway, had left the key anyway, had died pressing the book into Yara's arms anyway.

*She gave me the choice,* Yara realized. *That was the inheritance. Not the spells. The choice.*

* * *

Sefa found her at midday, up in the loft again, though there was nothing left to decipher — only the same eleven pages, read soft as a rosary.

The child climbed the ladder with the unhurried competence of someone who had decided Yara's loft was partly hers now, and settled beside her, and looked at the open book with those eyes that sometimes caught light wrong, that sometimes held flames that weren't there. They were quiet today. Just dark. Just a child's eyes, tired around the edges in a way no child's should be.

"I've been teaching you words," Sefa said. Her common speech was coming quickly now, quicker than it should, as if the nearer they drew to the mountains the more of her woke up. "And you've been teaching me. But you never ask for the word for *witness*. I know it. You avoid it."

Yara's throat tightened. "Do you know what the rite costs?"

"I have always known what bindings cost." Sefa said it simply, without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "I am the cost. Of the last one. And the one before." She reached out and put her small hand flat on the page, over Maren's cramped final version of the spell. "The first binder had a sister who witnessed for her. Did your book say? She forgot her too. The sister lived thirty more years in the village below the mountain, and every year on the solstice she baked honey-bread, and she never knew why, and she cried every time without knowing why, and the villagers thought her touched by grief for a husband who had never existed." Sefa looked up. "The binder watched from inside the seal. She watched her sister forget her for thirty years. That is what the line remembers most. Not the Wound. The forgetting."

"Then why—" Yara's voice cracked. She started again. "Why would the line want it done at all? If the price is that?"

"Because the alternative is us." Sefa spread her hands, indicating herself, the whole long chain of children made into locks. "Because a seal that lasts forever is just a wound that lasts forever, wearing a child over it like a bandage. The line wants to end, Yara. We are tired. All of us, all the way back. Tired in a way you can't imagine." Her small fingers curled against the page. "But none of us ever got to choose. The first binder chose. Everyone after was *made*. So when I say the rite must happen, understand — I am not asking you to pay. I am telling you what I have waited a thousand years to see: someone standing at the fire *freely*."

Yara looked at her — this mud-caked, storm-born, impossible child who had walked into her valley and dismantled her whole life in a season — and felt the fragment in her stir, that old passenger turning over in its sleep, and for one vertiginous moment she almost remembered something that wasn't hers: a stone hall, a sister's face, honey-bread, fire.

She blinked it away. Her cheeks were wet.

"Sefa," she said. "If I do this. When I do this. Will you tell me? Afterward? Who I was?"

The child smiled, and it was the saddest and kindest thing Yara had ever seen. "I will tell you everything. One word at a time. I am very patient. I have had practice."

Below them, boots on the yard stones — Doss's stride, urgent, doubled by another set of feet he was hurrying along. A fist hit the ladder.

"Yara!" Doss's voice, tight as a bowstring. "Riders on the north road. Amberfall colors — and one of them carrying Veyra's own banner. They're asking for you by name." A beat. "They know about the child. They know about *all* of us. Yara — somebody talked."

Sefa's hand found Yara's in the dark of the loft, small and cold and steady, and the fragment of the first binder stirred again beneath Yara's ribs, whispering in a dead tongue that needed no translation:

*It begins. Choose quickly, sister. The fire is lit.*
