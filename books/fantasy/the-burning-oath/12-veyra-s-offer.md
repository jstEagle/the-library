## 12. Veyra's Offer

The scouts came in at dusk, and they came badly.

Doss heard them before he saw them — the wrong kind of quiet on the eastern slope, the silence that meant men trying not to make noise. He had spent eleven years learning to hear that particular silence, first as a knight of Veyra's own household guard and then as a man running from it. He set down the fish he was cleaning, wiped his hands on his thighs, and said quietly to Brannoc, "Two of them. Coming up the creek bed. They think they're clever."

Brannoc rose from the fire like a hillside deciding to walk. "Alive?"

"For now. We'll want to know what they know."

It was over quickly and almost gently, which was more than Doss would have given them if the choice had been his alone. The scouts were young — boys, really, in mail a size too large, with the crowned-eye sigil stamped on their pauldrons in white enamel that still gleamed because it had never been tested by anything worse than parade ground. They knelt in the mud of the camp with their wrists bound behind them, breathing hard, trying very hard not to look at Sefa, who watched them from beside Ivetta's cart with the unblinking calm of a child who had seen far worse than two frightened soldiers.

Yara crouched in front of the taller one. She had Maren's grimoire slung across her back these days like a second spine, and when she moved, Doss sometimes caught glimpses of her mentor in her — the same economy of motion, the same way of looking at a thing until it confessed.

"Names," she said.

"Corren," said the tall one. His voice cracked on it. "That's — that's Tam. Ser, we're not — we were told you'd be gone from here. We were told—"

"You were told to find us and count us," Doss said. He stepped forward so the boy could see him clearly, could see the old scar that ran from his ear to his collar, the scar every soldier in Veyra's service had been shown once as a cautionary tale about what happened to men who left. "And then you were told to ride back and report. So report to me instead. What does the regent offer?"

The younger one — Tam — made a small sound, like a hinge rusting shut. Corren swallowed. "You're Doss," he whispered. "They said — they said you'd be with them. They said you'd gone soft."

"They say a lot of things." Doss held out his hand, palm up. "The message. All of it, word for word, or I'll let the big one decide how this evening goes."

Brannoc smiled at them. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had once carried a gate off its hinges and through a crowd of city guardsmen, and both scouts looked at him the way lambs look at weather.

Corren recited it. He had clearly memorized it, which told Doss everything: Veyra did not send messages that could be garbled in transit. Her words arrived the way her armies arrived — exactly where intended, exactly as composed.

*"To the woman Yara Vasquez, the knight called Doss, and those who shelter them: the regent Veyra, voice of the crown and protector of the realm, offers peace. Deliver the child into the king's keeping and each of you will receive a full pardon, lands in the river provinces, and a purse of five hundred crowns. Refuse, and the regent will take the child anyway — but first she will burn Amberfall. Then Highmoor. Then the free cities one by one, until there is nowhere left for a traitor to stand. The realm has been patient. The realm is done being patient."*

Silence, except for the fire popping and the goats — someone's goats, bought off a farmer two valleys back — shifting in their pen.

"Word for word," Yara said quietly.

"Yes, ser." Corren's eyes were wet now. "Please. We're just — we're just messengers."

"I know," Yara said, and something in her voice made Doss look at her sideways. There was no cruelty in it. There was something worse: recognition. She knew what it was to be used as an instrument by people who never thought of you as a person at all. "We're not going to hurt you. Eat something. Sleep if you can. In the morning we'll decide what happens to you."

She stood and walked away from the fire, out past the cart, toward the dark edge of the camp where the pines began. Doss gave the prisoners to Brannoc's watchful shadow and followed her.

* * *

She was standing with her arms wrapped around herself, looking east, toward the mountains, toward whatever waited beneath them. The grimoire's leather strap cut a line across her shoulder. Up close, in the last grey light, Doss could see how tired she was — tired in the bone, tired in a way sleep didn't touch anymore.

"You're going to tell me we should run," she said, without turning around.

"I'm going to tell you what I think," Doss said. "And yes. It involves running."

"Of course it does."

"Don't do that." He kept his voice level. Eleven years ago he'd have bristled; he'd have reached for rank or sarcasm or his sword hilt. Now he just stood beside her and let the cold come up off the ground. "Don't make it small because it's ugly. Hear me out, and then hate it if you have to."

She turned her head slightly. That was permission, or near enough.

"Look at the arithmetic," he said. "Veyra has — what? Four thousand veterans in the field, maybe six by spring, plus whatever garrisons she strips from the crown cities. Amberfall's council can't agree on bread prices, let alone war. Highmoor has walls older than its lord's sanity. We have seven people, a cart, some goats, a dying book of spells, and a child who matters more than all of it combined. Every day we stay within her reach, we gamble Sefa's life on our ability to win fights we cannot win. And the moment we lose one fight — one — she takes the girl, and everything everyone has bled for ends in a cellar under her palace."

"So we run."

"So we run." He gestured north, into the black wall of the hills. "There are ports on the far coast. Free ports — no crown, no regent, no crowned-eye anything. Ships leave for the southern isles every month. We buy passage with Ivetta's maps, or Brannoc's shoulders, or my name if it's still worth anything, and we go somewhere the Wound's whisper can't follow. Somewhere the whole rotten kingdom can eat itself without us in its mouth. Sefa grows up. Alive. That's the only victory I care about anymore."

Yara was silent for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was strange — flat and careful, like someone walking on ice they'd already fallen through once.

"When I was seventeen, I ran."

Doss said nothing. He knew pieces of it — the hedge-witch apprenticeship abandoned, the valley, the guilt that lived behind her eyes like a tenant who paid no rent. But she had never told it straight through, and he understood that this was her telling it.

"Maren took me in when I had nothing. Taught me everything — herbs, wards, how to listen to a sick animal, how to listen to a sick person. Ten years. And when the work got hard, when she pushed me past what I thought I could do, I decided she was holding me back. I decided I deserved a bigger life than goat paths and poultices." A breath. "So I ran. Crossed the border stones and never looked back. Told myself she'd understand. Told myself she'd be proud, even."

"And she wasn't."

"She died alone last week with a curse eating her alive, and the last thing she did with her final strength was hand me her life's work and ask me to finish what she couldn't." Yara's jaw tightened. "Running felt like freedom, Doss. It was just a longer leash. Everything I ran from caught up with me wearing different faces. And now you want me to teach Sefa the same lesson. That when the world gets dangerous, the answer is to be somewhere else. That love is a thing you pack lightly."

"That's not fair."

"No," she admitted. "Maybe it isn't. But it's true, and truth doesn't owe us fairness." She turned to face him fully. "If we run, Veyra burns Amberfall. You heard her. Not as a threat — as a schedule. Those people opened their gates to us. Ivetta's friends are on that council. The innkeeper with the bad knee who gave Sefa honey cakes. If we vanish over the sea, we don't escape the burning. We just arrange it so we don't have to watch."

"We can't save everyone, Yara."

"No," she said softly. "But we can decide which losses are ours. That's the only choice anyone ever really gets."

* * *

They brought it to the others at the fire, because that was the rule they'd built on the road without ever saying it aloud: no one decides alone, not anymore. Doss laid out the arithmetic. Yara laid out the refusal. Ivetta unrolled three of her impossible maps on a blanket and weighted the corners with stones, and the firelight made the ink lines seem almost to move.

"The southern ports are real," Ivetta said slowly, tracing a coastline that, by every honest chart, should not exist yet. "I've drawn them. Which means they'll exist. Which means passage is possible." She looked up. "I can get us to a ship. I want that understood — I'm not the obstacle here."

"But?" Doss said.

"But maps show roads, not reasons." She rolled the charts back up with gentle, precise hands. "I've been mapping places that don't exist since I was a girl, and the one thing I've learned is that the future isn't a country you emigrate to. It's a country you build or you don't. Run, and we survive. Stay, and maybe we don't — but maybe the world after this one is worth having been brave in." She glanced at Sefa, asleep against Brannoc's side, small and impossibly light for everything she carried. "I didn't draw a map with a grave on it yet. I'd rather not start."

Brannoc said little, as always. He simply shifted so Sefa's head rested more comfortably against him, and looked at Doss with mild, terrible patience, and that was his whole argument: *she trusts us. Look at her sleeping.*

Doss sat back down heavily. He had known, walking away from the fire with Yara, how the vote would fall. He had argued anyway, because someone had to say the cowardly thing out loud — because a company that couldn't hear its own fear wasn't brave, just deaf.

"One more thing," he said. "Those two boys. Corren and Tam. We can't keep them and we can't turn them loose knowing what they know."

"Send them back," Yara said.

Doss stared at her. "Back? They'll report our position. Veyra will know exactly where—"

"They'll report whatever we tell them to report." A thin, sharp smile crossed her face — Maren's smile, the fox one, the one that meant a trap was already built. "We break camp tonight and move north through the high pass. The scouts go home at dawn and swear blind we're still here, because we'll have paid them to say it, and because terrified boys repeat the story that keeps them safe. Veyra's vanguard spends three days surrounding an empty valley while we're under the mountains." She paused. "And Doss — they saw your face. Both of them. Word will reach her that you're with us, truly with us. Whatever door back into her service you kept pretending was open—"

"It closed years ago," Doss said. "I just liked standing near it."

He went to wake the prisoners himself. Corren flinched when Doss crouched in front of him, and Doss took his time, letting the fear settle into something more useful.

"Dawn tomorrow, you ride south," he said. "You tell the regent's captain exactly what you saw: seven people, this camp, no intention of moving. You tell her the knight Doss argued for surrender and was refused. Make it convincing — your lives depend on the quality of the lie, hers and ours both. Do that, and I give you gold enough to disappear somewhere her writ doesn't run. Fail, and—" He shrugged. "Well. You met Brannoc."

Tam made the hinge sound again. Corren nodded, twice, too fast.

Doss walked back toward the fire and stopped halfway, looking up. The stars over the eastern peaks were hard and cold and indifferent, and beyond them, somewhere in the deep stone, the thing in the crown was waiting for its seal to crack. He thought about the sea he would never see now, the isles with no names yet, the small clean life he had offered and been refused. It stung. Let it sting. Fear that got no vote became rot; fear that got heard became fuel.

Yara joined him at the edge of the light. Neither spoke for a moment.

"You know what she'll do when the lie fails," Doss said finally. "Three days, four, and then she comes for us angry."

"I know."

"And you know what the rite costs. You've read the pages. You know what it asks of whoever stands witness."

Yara was quiet long enough that he turned to look at her. Firelight moved on her face, and behind her eyes something was already grieving — grieving in advance, the way soldiers do, spending the loss early so it can't ambush them later.

"I know," she said again, softer. "Get some sleep, Doss. Tomorrow we go under the mountains."

Above the pass, unseen, the first snow of the season had begun to fall — fine and dry and red-tinged at the edges, though none of them would learn why until much later, and by then it would be too late to turn back.
