## 13. Beneath the Mountains

The map should not have existed.

Ivetta had drawn it three nights past, in the cold hours before dawn, when her hand moved without her and her ink went where it pleased. She had woken with black fingers and a finished chart spread across her bedroll: a range of mountains she knew only as a smudge on other cartographers' work, and beneath them — rendered in careful, confident strokes — a road that no surveyor had ever walked. It descended in long switchbacks through stone marked *hollow*, *deep*, *older than naming*, to a chamber at the bottom drawn as a simple circle with a single word inside it.

*They knelt.*

She had stared at it until the candle guttered. She had checked it against every map in her satchel, against the royal surveys she'd copied in Amberfall's archive, against the half-legendary charts of the First Kingdom that scholars paid gold to glimpse. Nothing matched. Nothing came close. And yet her hand had known the way the way a tongue knows a word learned in childhood — without thought, without permission.

Now she stood at the mouth of that road with the wind pulling at her coat and the company gathered behind her, and she understood, finally, what kind of cartographer she was. Not one who recorded the world. One who drew the world toward itself.

"Tell me again," Doss said quietly beside her. He was watching the treeline more than the mountain, his sword-hand flexing the way it did when he was afraid and would not say so. "Why we're trusting a map you dreamed."

"I didn't dream it." Ivetta touched the parchment where it lay rolled against her heart. "I received it. There's a difference, and I can't explain the difference, but I've stopped pretending there isn't one. Every impossible place I've ever drawn has been real by morning. The bridge at Sorrel Ford. The drowned chapel. The white tree. I drew them first. The world caught up after." She looked at him. "This time I don't think we're waiting for the world to catch up. I think the world has been waiting for us."

Behind them, Brannoc shifted his enormous pack and said nothing, which was how he said most things. Yara stood with Sefa's small hand wrapped in hers, and the girl was looking up at the mountain with an expression Ivetta had never seen on her face before — not fear, not wonder, but something older. Recognition. Like a woman returning to a house she'd been born in and left before she could walk.

"She knows this place," Yara murmured. "Don't you, little one?"

Sefa did not answer. But her grip tightened, and she stepped forward — ahead of Yara, ahead of all of them — toward the cleft in the rock where the road began.

It was not a natural opening. That became clear within the first hundred paces. The walls were dressed stone under centuries of mineral bloom, and though the mountain had shrugged and cracked and half-swallowed the passage, the bones of masonry showed through like ribs beneath skin. Someone had built this road down into the dark, deliberately, and then let the world forget it. Pillars stood at intervals, sheared off at varying heights, their capitals carved with symbols Ivetta's eye kept snagging on: circles broken by lines, crowns with too many points, eyes that were not eyes but wounds shaped like eyes.

She sketched as they walked. It was compulsion as much as craft; her fingers needed the pencil the way Doss's hand needed the sword-hilt. And as she drew, the strangeness settled over her fully: she was not recording this place. She was *confirming* it. Every pillar, every symbol, every turn of the descending road appeared on her impossible map already, inked three nights before she ever saw it. She began checking ahead rather than behind — reading the map like prophecy, calling out hazards before they reached them.

"There's a collapse fifty paces on, left side. We keep right and duck."

"How do you know that?" Doss demanded.

"Because I drew it." She said it simply, because there was no other way to say it. "Doss. At some point on this road you're going to have to decide whether my maps are madness or miracle. I'd suggest deciding soon, because there's a chamber coming where I'd rather you believed me."

He grunted — the sound of a man choosing belief out of sheer exhaustion — and when the collapse came exactly where she'd said, he ducked without argument, and something in the company changed. They stopped questioning her. They started following her. For a woman who had spent her whole life being told her gift was a defect, it felt less like triumph than like coming home to a house she'd never owned.

Hours passed, or what felt like hours; the dark made time soft. They ate cold bread and hard cheese in a widening gallery where water sang somewhere below them, a sound so clean it seemed to wash the road dust from their thoughts. Brannoc hummed while he chewed, tuneless and low, and Sefa leaned against Yara and watched the darkness with those flame-lit eyes that saw, Ivetta suspected, considerably more than any of them.

It was during that rest that Sefa spoke.

Not fragments. Not the dead-tongue words that only Yara could hear the shape of. She stood up in the middle of the gallery, faced deeper into the mountain, and said — clearly, fluently, in the old language that no living kingdom remembered — a sentence that ran on and on like water finding its course.

Yara went very still. "What did she say?"

And here was the thing that turned Ivetta's blood to winter water: for the first time since the storm-night at her door, Yara did not know. The hedge-witch who had understood the child from the first moment, whose ear the old language had poured itself into as if it were her mother tongue — Yara listened, and frowned, and shook her head slowly.

"It's slipping. The words are still there but the meaning is going out of them, like heat leaving a stone." Her voice was carefully level, and Ivetta heard the fear underneath it. "Sefa, sweetling — slower. Tell me again."

But Sefa only looked at her with terrible gentleness, and touched Yara's cheek with one small muddy finger, and said nothing more. As if she knew. As if she understood that whatever thread had connected them was being wound back toward the place they were walking into, and that some things could not be explained, only arrived at.

"We're close," Ivetta said, unrolling her map with hands that were not quite steady. "Very close. One descent left. Then—" she found the circle, the two words inside it, "*they knelt*" — "then the hall."

"The hall of what?" Doss asked.

"I don't know. The map doesn't say. It just says they knelt."

Brannoc rose, brushing crumbs from his beard, and looked down the passage with an expression Ivetta had seen on him only once before — at the gate he'd held alone. Reverence, maybe. Or dread wearing reverence's face.

"Then let's go meet them," he rumbled, and led the way down.

* * *

The last stair was cut from living rock, each step broad enough for three men abreast, worn hollow at their centers by feet that had climbed and descended for generations before any kingdom had a name. Their torchlight slid along walls covered in relief-carvings — processions of figures, harvests, coronations, wars — and Ivetta, who had spent years studying the fragmentary art of the pre-kingdom age, realized with a lurch that she recognized none of the iconography and understood all of it anyway. The images bypassed scholarship and went straight to the blood. She knew the crowned figure at the center of the great frieze was a king. She knew the kneeling ranks around him were swearing something. She knew, without knowing how, that the oath they swore was the reason the world above had kings at all.

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened.

Ivetta had imagined many things in the nights since drawing the circle on her map. A tomb. A treasury. A temple drowned in stalactites. None of it prepared her.

The hall was vast beyond sense — a cavern so large their torches were candles in a cathedral of dark, its far wall a rumor, its ceiling lost in blackness where pale formations hung like frozen rain. And filling that immensity, rank upon rank upon rank, receding into shadow like an army petrified mid-prayer, stood the kings.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Figures of grey stone, life-sized or near it, every one bent forward in the act of kneeling — caught, all of them, between standing and submission, frozen at the exact moment the oath took hold. Some wore crowns. Some wore helms. Some wore nothing but the posture itself, hands open, heads bowed, faces turned toward the hall's hidden center with expressions Ivetta could not bear to study for long. Relief. Terror. Devotion. Grief. All of it preserved in stone with a fidelity no sculptor could achieve, because no sculptor had made these. Stone does not hold grief like that unless the grief was real when the stone took it.

"They're not carvings," Yara whispered. Her voice came out flattened, stripped of everything but fact. "Look at the grain. Look at the strata running through them. These were people."

"They knelt," Ivetta said, and heard her own map speaking through her mouth, three nights old and true. "That's what it says. That's all it says. They knelt."

Doss had gone the color of ash. His hand rested on his sword but without intent, the way a man touches a talisman. "An army of kings. Why would— who kneels to *what?*"

"To the thing the crown remembers," Yara said softly. And then, sharper, urgent: "Where's Sefa?"

The child had walked away from them.

She moved down the central aisle between the kneeling ranks with the unhurried certainty of someone crossing her own courtyard, torchlight sliding over her small shoulders, and the stone kings seemed to lean toward her as she passed — Ivetta told herself it was the flickering light, she told herself firmly, it had to be the light — and at the head of the hall, where the ranks converged on a raised dais of black stone, Sefa stopped and lifted her face to something Ivetta could not see.

And spoke.

The old language poured out of her, fluent and full, rising through the vast dark in a voice that was still a child's voice and yet carried like a bell across water. Ivetta understood nothing of it and was moved anyway, the way one is moved by a lament in a foreign land — the meaning arriving through melody, through cadence, through the ache underneath. Around them, in the deep places of the hall, the sound seemed to wake echoes that were not echoes. Answers, almost. Something in the mountain listening.

Yara had both hands pressed to her mouth. When she lowered them, tears cut bright tracks through the dust on her face.

"She's answering it," Yara said. "Whatever's here — she's answering. And I can't— I can't understand her anymore, Ivetta. The words go in and just fall through me." Her voice cracked. "We're standing in the middle of the story and I've lost the language."

"No." Ivetta took her arm, steadying her, steadying herself. "You haven't lost it. You've given it back. Think — the understanding was never yours, it was borrowed, a fragment carried forward from whoever bound the Wound the first time. We're in the place where that binding lives. The fragment wants to go home." She swallowed. "Maybe that's what the whole journey is. Everything we carry, going home."

Sefa's voice fell silent. The silence afterward was enormous, ringing, absolute — and in it, Ivetta heard what the child must have been speaking to: a breathing. Slow and vast and patient, the respiration of something asleep rather than absent, drawing air through halls of stone that had no business holding breath.

The torches bowed, all together, toward the dais.

On the black stone, faint as memory, a shape of light assembled itself: a crown, hovering, turning slowly, its points too numerous, its circlet seamed like scarred flesh. And threaded through the crown's glow — thin, exhausted, human — a voice.

"*You came.*"

Doss's sword cleared its sheath. Brannoc stepped in front of the others, all instinct, all bulk, a wall of a man making himself a wall. Yara pulled Sefa back against her, and the child went, but her eyes never left the crown, and in their depths the flames burned steady and unafraid.

Ivetta stood her ground, map forgotten at her side, and looked up at the thing that had waited under the mountains since before kingdoms, and found — to her astonishment — that she was not looking at a monster.

She was looking at a prisoner.

"You're Aldric," she said. It was not a guess. It arrived in her the way her maps arrived, whole and certain. "The king. You're alive in there."

The light flickered — and if a crown could laugh, it laughed, bitter and brief and unbearably tired.

"*One of me is,*" said the voice in the dark. "*Come closer, cartographer. Come closer, witch. Come closer, knight, giant, child. I have been kneeling in the dark for longer than your kingdoms have had names, and I have so very much to tell you — and so very little time left in which to tell it.*"

The breathing in the walls quickened.

"*Before it wakes.*"
