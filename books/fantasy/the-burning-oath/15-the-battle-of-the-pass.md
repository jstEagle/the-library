## 15. The Battle of the Pass

Brannoc had carried a lot of things in his life — sacks of grain, fallen comrades, the weight of a deserter's shame — but he had never carried a mountain on his shoulders until now.

The pass above the caverns was not a pass so much as a wound in the mountain's spine: a throat of grey stone barely wide enough for four men abreast, walled on one side by a cliff that went up and up into cloud, and on the other by a drop that went down and down into nothing. Wind screamed through it constantly, a low moaning note like a giant mourning something no one else could see. The old kings' road had once run through here, Ivetta said. Now there was only broken flagstone and ten years of scree and the bones of some caravan animal whose ribs had been picked white by the weather.

Brannoc stood at the mouth of it with an axe in each hand and tried to remember how to breathe.

Behind him, down the switchback trail and through the hidden door Ivetta had found on her impossible maps, lay the stone hall where Yara and Sefa were even now preparing whatever terrible thing the grimoire demanded. He couldn't think about that. If he thought about that he would want to be down there instead of up here, and being down there would kill everyone he loved.

Up here, at least, the work was simple. Simple as swinging wood. Simple as standing.

"They're coming." Doss crouched beside him at the lip of the pass, peering down the long approach where the trail climbed out of the valley below. His armor was mismatched, scavenged from Amberfall's armories during their flight, and the crowned-eye sigil had been chiseled off the breastplate leaving only a raw scar in the steel. "Scouts first. Then the vanguard. We have maybe an hour."

"An hour is good," Brannoc said. "An hour is a long time."

Doss looked up at him. There was something in the knight's face Brannoc had learned to read over the months of the road — the particular set of his jaw when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "How many do you think you can hold against?"

Brannoc considered. He was the biggest man most people had ever seen, seven feet of him before you counted the boots, and he'd spent eight years in Veyra's army learning exactly how soldiers died in narrow places. "More than four," he said. "Fewer than forty."

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't laughing."

* * *

They had thirty-one fighters, all told. A remnant of Amberfall loyalists who had refused their council's surrender — mostly young, mostly frightened, led by a gray-haired sergeant named Osla who had fought in three wars and trusted none of them. Plus the company: Doss, Ivetta, himself, and a handful of Ivetta's map-scholars who had followed her out of the city carrying satchels of drawings of places that did not exist yet.

Ivetta had laid out the ground three times over, walking the pass with charcoal and parchment, muttering about sightlines and chokepoints the way other people prayed. Her plan was simple because it had to be. Hold the mouth of the pass. Fall back in stages if they must. Buy hours, not victory. Every hour was another hour for Yara.

"Every hour," she'd said, tapping the map, "is a prayer we're buying on credit."

Brannoc liked Ivetta's way of putting things. It made the terror feel almost mathematical.

He spent the hour doing what he always did before a fight, which was the opposite of what the drill-sergeants had taught him. They had taught him to hate the enemy early, to sharpen himself on imagined wrongs. Instead Brannoc sat on a flat stone and thought about the men who would be climbing toward him. Farm boys, most likely. Men who'd been promised gold or pardon or simply hadn't been asked. Men who, like him, had once stood in a square and watched Veyra take power and had told themselves it would be fine, it would all be fine.

He thought about the night he'd walked away from it. Eight years ago. Rain, again — it was always rain in his memories of leaving. He'd handed his sword to a corporal he liked and said *I can't*, and the corporal had looked at him with such pity, such terrible understanding, and said nothing, because what was there to say? You didn't get to be gentle and stay. That was the whole bargain of soldiering. Brannoc had chosen gentleness and had been running from the cost of it ever since.

Well. Here was the bill come due at last, climbing up a mountain trail with banners.

"Brannoc." Ivetta touched his elbow. She had ash smudged across her cheekbone from copying maps by lamplight, and her eyes were red-rimmed though her voice was steady. "If it goes badly—"

"It won't go badly."

"If it goes *badly*," she repeated, with the patience of someone talking to a very large, very stubborn child, "you fall back. That's the plan. You fall back through the second chokepoint and we collapse the ledge behind you. Doss rigged it."

"Doss rigged it," Brannoc said, "because Doss knows I won't fall back on my own."

Ivetta's jaw tightened. She looked away, down the pass, at the empty grey distance. "Then don't make me watch," she said quietly. "I've lost enough this year. Don't make me stand there and watch the mountain eat you too."

Brannoc put his hand on her head, carefully, the way you'd steady a bird. "You won't watch," he said. "You'll be busy drawing maps of places that don't exist yet. Draw me one. Somewhere warm. With goats."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which was all he'd wanted. He was good at that — getting the ghost of a smile. It wasn't much of a gift, but it was his.

* * *

The scouts came first, just as Doss promised: six riders picking their way up the switchbacks, unhurried, professional. Osla's archers took two of them before the rest wheeled and fled back down. The sound of the horns started twenty minutes later — three long notes echoing off the cliffs, answered by more horns further down the valley, so that the whole mountain seemed to be calling to itself.

Then the vanguard came, and Brannoc stopped counting.

They came in column, shields locked, armored in the black-lacquered plate of Veyra's regulars, and they came without hesitation, which was the worst part. No war-cries. No taunting. Just the drumbeat rhythm of boots on stone and the glitter of spearpoints in the grey light, rank after rank after rank, filling the switchback trail like water filling a channel.

"Gods below," whispered one of Osla's boys. "There's hundreds."

"There's a narrow place," Brannoc said, and stepped forward to meet them.

The fighting, when it came, was ugly and close and exactly what he'd expected. The pass was too tight for their numbers; only the front rank could reach him at any moment, and Brannoc was a wall that swung axes. He'd learned this style of killing in Veyra's own shield-lines — short, brutal strokes, no flourish, conserve everything, let the enemy tire themselves against you. The first man to reach him took an axe-head under the collarbone and went down gurgling. The second and third tried to hook his legs with spears and he stamped the shafts apart and split a shield in half and drove its owner stumbling back into his fellows.

Behind him, Osla's archers shot over his shoulders in timed volleys, and Osla herself bellowed the rhythm — *loose, loose, blades forward* — and the line held.

It held, and held, and held.

Time went strange, the way it did. There was no sun in the pass, only the wind and the iron smell and the weight of bodies pressing toward him. He killed a boy who couldn't have been older than Sefa and felt something tear inside his chest that had nothing to do with wounds. He killed a sergeant who fought well and honorably and whispered *sorry* as the man fell. He took a spear-graze along his ribs that burned like a brand and didn't slow down, because slowing down was dying, and dying here — well.

Dying here was the point. That was the arithmetic he'd done at dawn while the others slept. Someone had to stand at the mouth of the pass, and he was the only one big enough, stubborn enough, and expendable enough. Doss was marked by Veyra's assassins and needed alive. Ivetta's maps were worth more than a hundred swords. And Brannoc — Brannoc was a deserter who had run from one fight eight years ago and had been looking, ever since, for the fight he couldn't run from.

Here it was. He planted his feet.

* * *

The second wave broke against him at midday, and the third, and somewhere in the fourth his left axe-shaft cracked and he fought one-handed until he tore a spear from a dead man's grip and kept going. Blood ran into his eyes from a cut above his brow. His arms had gone from burning to cold, which he knew was bad. The pile of dead in front of him had become a rampart in itself, and the enemy had to climb their own fallen to reach him, and still they came, because whoever commanded them below had decided this pass would open today.

A horn sounded — different note, sharper. The pressure slackened. Brannoc sagged against the cliff wall, dragging air, and saw why: riders pushing through the ranks on barded horses, and at their head a banner he knew too well. Not the crowned eye. Something worse. The personal standard of Veyra's own household guard, which meant either the regent herself had come, or someone with her authority, and both meant the probing attacks were over.

"Fall back!" Osla's voice, hoarse. "Fall back to the second point! *Now!*"

The loyalists withdrew in good order, gods bless the old woman, leapfrogging by pairs while the archers covered. Brannoc turned to follow —

— and the world tilted.

He caught himself on the rock. Looked down. The spearhead stood out of his thigh, low, deep, exactly where a man who knew anatomy would put it to drop an ox. When had that happened? In the fourth wave? The fifth? He hadn't felt it. He tried to step and the leg folded like wet rope and he went down hard on one knee, and the pain arrived all at once, vast and white, and he heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize as his own voice.

*Get up.* He put his hand flat on the stone and pushed. The leg shook. Would not hold. *Get up, you great useless mountain.*

The household guard advanced at a walk.

Brannoc looked back down the pass, at the trail winding away toward the hidden door and the hall beneath the mountain, where a child who was a seal and a witch who was a witness were doing something that would either save the world or end it. Too far. Too far to drag himself. And if he fell back wounded, they would follow him, these patient professionals, straight to the door, and everything — Maren's grimoire, Sefa's sacrifice, Yara's terrible bargain — would be for nothing.

There was one thing left he could carry. One last weight for his shoulders.

He hauled himself around to face the advancing line, dragged the dead man's spear to him, and wedged his back against the narrowest point of the pass, where the cliff came closest to the drop. He set the cracked axe across his knees and the spear in his fists and made himself as wide as a gate.

"Osla!" he roared, and his voice cracked the wind. "Collapse the ledge! *Now!*"

"No—" That was Ivetta, far back, her voice tearing across the distance. "*No, Brannoc—*"

"Do it!" He didn't look at her. He couldn't. If he looked at her he would see her face and he would start begging, and he was done with begging. "Draw your maps, Ivie! Warm place! Goats! *Go!*"

The household guard broke into a run.

What happened next, Brannoc did with the calm of a man already halfway out of the world. The first rider came on and he took the horse's legs and the rider with them. The second and third dismounted and came at him with swords and he killed one and hamstrung the other. A blade opened his forearm to the bone. Another punched into his shoulder. He stopped feeling any of it. He was a wall. He was the mountain's own anger. He was every gentle thing that had ever been forced to learn violence, standing in the last narrow place, saying *no further, no further, no further.*

And then, impossibly, from behind him — from the direction of the retreat, from the direction he had forbidden — came a battle-cry he knew.

"*BRANNOC!*"

Doss came through the smoke and the wind like something out of the old songs, mismatched armor streaming, sword alight with a fury Brannoc had never seen in the quiet knight, and hit the household guard's flank like a hammer falling. Two guardsmen went down before they understood they were attacked. A third turned to meet him and Doss killed him with a technique Brannoc recognized — Veyra's own academy form, the one they taught the ones they meant to make officers — executed with a hatred of it that made it beautiful.

"You absolute," Doss snarled, parrying, bleeding, backing up against Brannoc's side so their shoulders touched, "stubborn, mule-headed — I told you — I *told* you I'd come back for you—"

"You were supposed to rig the ledge and leave," Brannoc gasped.

"I lied." Doss grinned through the blood on his face, wild and young suddenly, the man he might have been before Veyra hollowed the world. "Come on, old mountain. Lean on me. We're both leaving."

They fought back to back then, the deserter and the marked knight, in the throat of the pass, and for a handful of shining minutes it was enough — it was more than enough, it was almost joy, the pure animal joy of not being alone in the dark. Brannoc's leg was gone, his vision greying at the edges, but Doss's shoulder against his was an anchor and he anchored.

Then the horn sounded again, and the ledge behind them boomed like a struck bell as Osla's charges brought half the trail down, sealing the retreat, sealing the door, sealing them in with the enemy.

Doss laughed — actually laughed, breathless and horrified. "Well," he said. "No way back now."

"No," Brannoc agreed, and gripped his axe, and smiled for the first time in eight years.

Below them, past the ranks of the household guard, up the trail, a palanquin was ascending on the shoulders of eight men, curtained in grey silk, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once been told *no.*

And from behind the curtain, in a voice like cool water over stones, a woman's voice called up through the wind:

"Ser Doss. How good of you to save me the trouble of hunting you."

* * *

Brannoc spat blood onto the stones, shifted his grip, and settled his ruined weight against the rock.

"Hold the line," he rumbled to Doss, quietly, the way you'd speak in a barn at night. "Whatever happens. Hold it."

The curtain of the palanquin stirred, and Regent Veyra stepped out into the wind.
