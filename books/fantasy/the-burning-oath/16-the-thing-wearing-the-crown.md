## 16. The Thing Wearing the Crown

Sefa had never been so far from the sky.

The mountain swallowed them by degrees — first the light, then the wind, then the sound of their own footsteps, which the deep stone drank and gave back changed, as if someone else were walking behind them wearing their feet. She counted the others the way she counted everything now: Yara's breath, quick and steady; Doss's armor whispering at every joint; Ivetta's map held close to a lantern that burned a color fire should not burn; Brannoc's absence, which was a shape in the company like a missing tooth.

She did not count herself. Counting herself was harder. Since the cavern mouth, words had been rising in her that were not hers, in her tongue and out of it, and each one tasted of iron and old smoke. The hall below was calling her home, and she hated how much of her wanted to answer.

*Down,* said something that lived under her ribs. *Down, little seal. You were made for this dark.*

*I was made for nothing,* she answered, in the silence where no one could hear her but everyone somehow leaned closer when she thought it. *I was made by a woman who chose. That is different.*

The thing under her ribs laughed. It had been laughing more often lately.

* * *

They came to the first of the kneeling kings an hour past the last of Ivetta's mapped ways.

He was stone to the waist and flesh above it — or he had been once; now both were the same gray, the same stillness, his hands pressed flat against a floor worn smooth by centuries of other hands. His face was lifted as if toward a throne that was not there anymore, or had never been there, or was everywhere. Moss grew in the folds of his neck. A bird had nested in the crook of his elbow generations ago and left behind a scatter of pale shells.

"He's real," Doss breathed, and his hand went to his sword hilt and then away from it, ashamed. "Under the stone he's — there's a man in there."

"There was," Sefa said, and heard her voice come out doubled, hers and the older one beneath, speaking together like two bells struck at once. Yara flinched. Sefa wished she hadn't heard that. "There always was. That is what binding means. Not killing. Keeping."

Ivetta crouched with her lantern near the king's face and made a small sound, half prayer, half sickness. "His eyes are open."

"They are always open," Sefa said. "All of them are. Come and see. There are so many."

And there were. The hall opened beyond the first king into a colonnade of them, dozens, hundreds, ranks of kneeling figures receding into blackness on either side of a road of polished black stone, each one turned toward the center of the world as compass needles turn north. Some were crumbled to the shoulders. Some were only recently stone, their robes still showing the weave, a ring still bright on one gray finger. At the far end, where the road ended in a dais of the same black rock, something glowed.

Not firelight. Not lantern-light. A glow like the memory of a wound that has been kept open on purpose.

Yara stopped walking. Sefa felt her stop before she saw it, felt the woman's hand find her shoulder and grip hard enough to leave tomorrow's bruises, and was grateful for the pain because pain was hers, only hers, and reminded her which body she was in.

"Sefa." Yara's voice was very quiet. "What's down there?"

"You know what's down there," Sefa said gently. "You've known since the fever-dreams. Since the burning crowns. You've been dreaming your way here for years, Yara Vasquez. The valley didn't hide you from magic. It hid you from this."

* * *

The throne room — though no one had called it that aloud, and Sefa loved them a little for refusing — was round, and cold, and wrong in a way her body understood before her mind consented to. The walls rose in curves of black stone veined with white, and the veins moved. Slowly. Like frost growing. Like veins in a living wrist.

On the dais sat a chair of plain oak, older than any kingdom, and in the chair sat a man.

He was thin the way winter fields are thin, all structure and no softness, wrapped in robes that might have been magnificent forty years ago and were now only cloth. His beard had gone white unevenly, in patches, as if the color had fled some parts of him before others. His hands lay open on the arms of the chair, palms up, the posture of a man who has surrendered everything he was holding.

And on his head, simple and terrible, was a crown of black iron set with a single stone.

The stone was closed. Sefa knew — the way she knew the taste of rain before it fell — that it was an eye, and that it was closed, and that its being closed was the only mercy in the room.

"Stop," the man said, without opening his eyes. "Please. Stop walking. Let me have — let me have a moment where you're not real yet."

Doss went forward anyway, because Doss was Doss, and dropped to one knee three steps below the dais. "Your Grace. King Aldric. We came to—"

"I know why you came." Aldric's eyes opened then, and they were ordinary eyes, brown, tired, human, and Sefa felt something in her chest crack along an old seam. "Everyone comes. Veyra sends her letters. The crown sends its dreams. And now you come, with my death in your pocket and a child carrying the lock." He looked at Sefa, and his face did something complicated — grief and relief and fear braided tight. "Hello, little echo. You look like her. You all look like her. It's the cruelest thing about it."

"Like who?" Yara asked.

"The first binder." Aldric laughed, and it was a dry, awful sound, a hinge that had not been oiled in decades. "Sit. Sit down, all of you, before I lose the thread again. When it takes me, I forget I'm a man. I start thinking in edicts. Last time I woke I had written orders to burn three villages in my own hand and no memory of choosing to. So sit, and listen fast, because I cannot promise you which of us will be talking in ten minutes."

They sat. Even Doss, whose knees had never learned obedience to anything but a king, sat.

* * *

"I was crowned at nineteen," Aldric said. "Do you know what they tell you, when they put the iron on? They say the crown remembers, so the king need not. They say it holds the old oaths, the old borders, the old debts, so that a mortal head can carry a realm without going mad under the weight. I believed it. Gods forgive me, I *wanted* to believe it. My father died tired, and I swore I would rule rested."

He raised one hand to the crown and did not touch it. His fingers hovered a hair's breadth from the iron, trembling.

"It was true, in the beginning. Or true enough. The crown remembered treaties; I signed them. It remembered grudges; I settled them. And sometimes, in the deep watches of the night, it whispered things that were not treaties or grudges. Small things. *That minister lies to you. That province loves you too much — love can be redirected.* I told myself it was the accumulated wisdom of dead kings. Dead kings are allowed to be harsh. Dead kings see further."

"What changed?" Ivetta asked softly. Her lantern sat between her feet, and its strange flame bent toward the dais like a plant toward sun.

"A woman came to court." Aldric's mouth twisted. "A hedge-witch from the border valleys, with mud on her boots and a child at her side — a mute child who spoke a dead tongue. She demanded audience. The guards threw her out. But the crown *heard* her, and that night it began to scream."

Sefa's whole body went cold. Beside her, Yara made a sound like a word that couldn't decide to be born.

"She had seen the seal failing," Aldric went on, staring at the middle distance, at forty years ago. "Seen it in the bones of the land, she said. And the crown — the Wound inside it — knew she was right. It has been afraid ever since. Fear makes it loud. Loud makes it strong. Within a year I was signing things I did not remember reading. Within three, I woke on the throne at dawn with blood under my nails and a decree dissolving the border courts, and I ran — I actually ran, down through the cellars, got as far as the river — and then it simply *closed my legs*, the way you'd close a book. Sat me back down. Made me watch from inside while my hands wrote the next order. Do you understand? I am not asleep in here. I have never once been asleep."

Veyra, Sefa thought. Say it. Please say it.

"And Veyra?" Doss asked, hoarse. "Captain. Regent. Whatever she is. Did she take the throne from you?"

"No." Aldric said it flatly, and the word fell into the room like a stone into a well. "No, and you must carry that out of here even if you carry nothing else, because the free cities are building their hope on the story of a usurper, and the story is a lie the Wound tells *through* them. Veyra found me — found this — eleven years ago. She came to arrest me for the burning decrees. She stood where you're standing, sword drawn, ready to end a tyrant. And I begged her. On my knees, in front of the throne, I begged her not to save me but to *serve it*, to keep the machine running until a true binding could be made, because if the crown came off my head unbound, the thing inside would pour out into the world with no vessel at all."

He looked up at them, and his ordinary brown eyes were wet.

"She thought she was taking power. She believes it still. Every cruelty she commits, she commits believing she is holding a dam shut with her own body — that the realm's only choices are her fist or the flood. The Wound whispers to her through me, through the seals of state, through dreams, and it tells her exactly what she needs to hear: *you are the last wall, Veyra, you alone, and every atrocity is mortar.* It hollowed her the way it hollows everyone who serves it too long. She is not my usurper. She is my fellow prisoner. She just doesn't know the shape of her cell."

Doss had gone gray. "I served her. I — gods, I argued with men who called her a tyrant. I defended her name in taverns after I deserted."

"You weren't wrong to defend her," Aldric said. "That may be the worst part. Neither of us was ever wrong about the danger. Only about the enemy."

* * *

It happened then, the way he had warned them it would.

One moment the tired man was speaking; the next, his spine straightened like a puppet's string pulled taut, his chin lifted, and the closed stone in the crown began — very slowly, with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a silent room — to open.

Sefa was on her feet before she decided to stand. The thing under her ribs surged up joyfully to meet whatever was looking out of that stone, and she slammed down against it with everything she had, every word of the dead tongue, every fragment of the first binder's stubbornness Maren's grimoire had taught Yara to teach her, and held.

The eye opened fully. It was not an eye. It was a wound dressed as an eye, a darkness with a hunger for a pupil, and when it looked at the company, the lantern flame lay down flat and Ivetta made a small broken noise and Doss's sword cleared its scabbard without his permission.

"*Little seal,*" said a voice that did not use air. It spoke directly into the marrow of them. "*Little echo of a dead woman's mercy. You have brought the witness to me. How kind. How obedient. Everything that lives comes home to its maker eventually.*"

"She is not yours," Sefa said, and her voice came out tripled now — girl, binder, and something else, something that had chosen freely once and would choose freely again. "None of us are yours. That was the whole of the oath. That was the *point*."

"*The point,*" the Wound said, amused, "*was a cage. Cages need bars. Bars wear. I have worn four of you already, child, and I remember every one — I keep what the wearers forget, remember? Ask your king. Ask him what he has lost.*"

And Aldric — Aldric came back, gasping, sagging in the chair like a man hauled out of deep water, and the eye above him shuttered slowly, sulkily, and the room remembered how to be a room.

"Did you hear it?" he whispered. "Did you hear what it—" He saw their faces and nodded, once, as if confirming a diagnosis. "Yes. Now you know. Now you understand why I beg."

He leaned forward, and the movement cost him visibly, joint by joint, as if his body were stone in places he could not see.

"End it," he said. "Not me — *it*. I don't care what it costs. I have watched myself cost things for forty years. Burn the rite through me, through the crown, through the chair if it must. And if the price is a memory, pay it gladly — memories are the only coin it cannot counterfeit, and I am so tired, so *tired*, of remembering for it."

His eyes found Sefa, and softened, and broke her heart cleanly in two.

"But not the child," he said. "Whatever the old rite demands — find another way for the child. Four seals have worn out in that little body's line already. I will not be the king who spent a fifth. Promise me. Promise me, hedge-witch's daughter, whoever you are — promise me you'll find another way."

Yara's hand found Sefa's shoulder again. It was shaking.

"We'll try," Yara said, which was a hedge-witch's honest answer, and Sefa loved her for it and feared it in equal measure.

Above them — far above, through a mile of mountain — came a sound none of them had hoped not to hear. Faint, rhythmic, patient as erosion.

War drums. Veyra's vanguard, entering the pass.

Aldric heard it too. He closed his eyes, and when he opened them again there was something new in them, something that had not been there in forty years.

Hope, and terror, in equal measure — the exact expression, Sefa realized, of a man watching the cage door begin to open without knowing yet whether what walks out will be him or the thing that has been wearing him.

"Then you have until the drums reach the door," Aldric said quietly. "Speak fast, bind faster, and whatever you do — do not let it talk to her alone."

The stone in the crown shivered, and began, very slowly, to smile.
