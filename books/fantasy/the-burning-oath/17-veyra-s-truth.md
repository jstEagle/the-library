## 17. Veyra's Truth

Doss heard them coming long before he saw them — the particular cadence of armored boots on old stone, disciplined, unhurried, the walk of soldiers who believed the battle was already won. He had marched that walk himself once. He knew its arrogance and its grief.

He was standing in the antechamber beyond the hall of kneeling kings, a spear taken from a dead man's hands held loosely at his side, when the torchlight at the far end of the passage began to multiply. Yara was below with Sefa and the king. Ivetta had taken the child's place at the mouth of the tunnel, ready to run her out if it came to that. Which left this corridor, this narrow throat of mountain, to him.

Of course it did. It had always been going to be him.

"Company halt." The voice carried down the stone like a blade laid flat against a whetstone. Doss closed his eyes for half a heartbeat. He would have known it blindfolded, deaf, drowned. *Veyra.*

He stepped out of the shadow of the archway into the torchlight, alone, spear grounded, and let them see him.

There were perhaps forty of them filling the passage, ranked shoulder to shoulder, and above them all, on a rise of broken stair, stood the Regent of the Realm in her campaign black, the crowned-eye sigil worked in silver thread over her heart. She looked older than he remembered. Not weaker — Veyra would never be weak — but worn in the way a whetstone is worn, hollowed by use. Her eyes found him across forty feet of ancient stone and did not widen, did not soften, did not do anything at all.

"Doss." His name in her mouth sounded like a verdict being read. "I wondered which direction you'd finally run."

"I didn't run," he said. "That was the problem. You told me once that a soldier who can't choose his ground has already lost it. So I chose mine."

Something flickered behind her face then — not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. The ghost of a woman who had taught a raw young officer to read terrain by moonlight, who had sat with him over cold stew and explained why she served: *because someone has to hold the realm together while the king dreams.* He had believed her then. He had wanted so badly to believe her.

"Stand aside," she said. "The child comes with me. No one else needs to die today. That is not mercy, Doss. It is arithmetic."

"It's neither." He shifted his grip on the spear, not raising it, just holding it like a man who intended to keep holding it. "You should hear what's down there before you spend those forty lives. Before you spend yours."

"The ramblings of a madman and a hedge-witch—"

"Aldric spoke to us." He said it plainly, loudly enough for the soldiers to hear, because they were the ones who mattered now, not her. "The king. Alive. Lucid. He told us what wears his crown."

The passage went very quiet. Torches crackled. Somewhere far above, muffled through fathoms of rock, the war in the pass went on — the distant thunder of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Veyra descended one step. Then stopped herself, as if the motion had been involuntary and she resented it. "You expect me to believe the king sent you messages from inside his own madness."

"He isn't mad." Doss took a breath, and found that his hands were steady, which surprised him. He had rehearsed this speech a hundred times on the road, in the dark, in the space between sleeping and waking, and every version had ended with her ordering his death. Maybe this one would too. But Aldric's voice was still in his ears — *tell her, tell her, she was never meant to know* — and some debts you pay even when the payment kills you. "He said: tell the regent the crown lies to her in her own voice. Tell her the realm she serves ended years ago, and what she's been holding together since is a puppet's limbs."

"Enough." The word cracked off the stone.

"He said you were never the usurper, Veyra. He said—" Doss made himself look at her, really look, past the rank and the armor and the ten years of distance. "He said you were the first victim. That the Wound got into you before it ever got into him. That everything you've done since, you did because something wearing your loyalty like a coat whispered that it was duty."

The silence that followed was the worst kind — the kind where forty soldiers are listening, and their regent's face is the only map of the truth any of them will ever get.

Doss watched her face. And there, for three heartbeats, he saw it happen: the mask didn't fall, but it *thinned*, the way ice thins over dark water, and beneath it something moved that was not command, not certainty, not the Regent of the Realm at all. Something small. Something that had been screaming behind glass for years.

"You deserted," she said quietly. "The night I took power. I signed the order for your arrest myself. Do you remember what you said to me, when I came to your quarters?"

"You never came to my quarters."

"No." A muscle moved in her jaw. "No, I didn't. I sent a lieutenant. I told myself it was beneath my office. I told myself a great many things were beneath my office." She came down another step, and this time she didn't stop herself, and her captains exchanged glances but said nothing, because no one gave orders around Veyra except Veyra. "You want a confession, Doss? Here is the only one I have. Nine years ago I woke in the royal tent after the fever took half the camp, and I could not remember the winter. Not the campaigns. Not the men we buried. Not my own sister's funeral. There was a hole in me shaped like four months, smooth-edged, as if it had been cut with a surgeon's knife. And the crown was there, on the campaign table, though the king was a hundred miles away. And it spoke to me. In my own voice. It said: *you were tired, Veyra. You asked to rest. This is the rest you asked for.*"

Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the silver sigil over her heart.

"And I chose to believe it," she said. "Do you understand? I *chose*. Because the alternative was to stand in that tent and admit that something had been inside my head, wearing me, and I had never known. That every decision I'd ever trusted might have been made by a thing that eats kings. A soldier can survive defeat. A soldier cannot survive not knowing whose hand holds the sword." Her voice dropped, and it was almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "So I decided the hand was mine. I have been deciding that every day since. It is the only oath I have left."

Doss's throat ached. Ten years he had hated this woman. Ten years he had built her into a monument of everything wrong with the realm — ambition dressed as service, cruelty dressed as order. He had not built her into a prisoner. He had not let himself.

"That's what Aldric said," he managed. "Word for word, near enough. *She serves it believing she serves the realm.*"

"Then the king is more merciful than I deserve." She looked past him then, down the corridor toward the deep places, toward the glow that seeped up from the hall of stone kings like light through water. "It's louder here. Has anyone told you that? The closer to the Oath's root, the clearer it speaks. I have been hearing it since we entered the pass. It is telling me right now that you are a traitor and the child is a weapon and if I take both, the realm survives." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Doss had ever seen on a living face. "It has been telling me versions of that sentence for nine years. And every day, some part of me believes it, because the part that doesn't believe is the part that remembers my sister's funeral, and I buried that part a long time ago."

"Then put it down," Doss said. "The sigil. The office. Whatever it is you're carrying. Stand aside. Let them finish it."

"You think it's that simple? Look at them." She turned her head slightly, indicating her soldiers without pointing — an old habit, economical, drilled into everyone who ever served under her. Forty faces in torchlight, hard and young and certain. "They follow the crowned eye because I taught them to. Because I told them order was salvation. If I stand aside, they will not stand aside with me. They have their own holes in their memories, Doss. Half of them have dreamed of burning crowns since the mustering. The Wound doesn't need me anymore. I was the door. They are the house."

"Then talk to them. Tell them the truth you just told me."

"To what end?" For the first time, real anger — or what wore anger's shape — flashed through her voice. "You think a confession unmakes nine years? You think these men lay down arms because a regent weeps? They'll cut me down as a traitor before they cut down you, and they'll be right to, by every law I ever taught them." She stopped. Drew breath. And when she spoke again it was barely above a whisper, and it was not for the soldiers, and it was not entirely for him. "You were the best officer I ever trained, Doss. Do you know why I never came to your quarters that night? Because I was afraid that if I looked at you, I would ask you the question I couldn't afford to ask. Whether you'd felt it too. Whether the dreams had started for you before you ran. Whether I had already begun hollowing the people closest to me, the way it hollows everyone, and simply couldn't see it." Her chin lifted, the old iron reasserting itself, but her eyes stayed wet and furious about being wet. "Well. Ask it answered. You ran, and you came back whole. That is my answer. That is the whole of my life's answer. Everything I kept, it took. Everything that got away, it spared."

* * *

What happened next, Doss would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a bridge collapsing — not as sequence but as sound.

Because Veyra did the unthinkable. She unbuckled her sword belt and let it fall.

The clang of it in the stone passage was enormous. Forty soldiers flinched as one animal.

"Regent—" Her captain, a broad man with a scarred jaw whose name Doss had once known, took a half-step forward. "Lady Veyra, the orders—"

"There are no orders." Her voice rang clear and terrible. "Not from me. Hear me: whatever you have been given in my name since we entered this mountain, I did not give it. Check the seals. Check the words. It writes in my hand now. It has written in my hand for years, and I let it, because the alternative was madness, and I chose obedience instead of truth, and I am done choosing." She looked at Doss, and nodded once, small, like a salute. "Go down. Finish it. I will buy you what time I can."

And that — that was the moment Doss understood the beat of the trap, the last cruel turn of the whole long road. Because the soldiers did not obey her renunciation. They obeyed the thing that had been speaking through her, the thing that had spent nine years teaching them its voice by way of hers, and it did not need her permission anymore.

"Hold the passage," the scarred captain said, and his voice was flat, dead, wrong in a way Doss felt in his teeth — the cadence of a man reciting words he did not know he was reciting. "None pass. Regent's orders."

"I rescind—" Veyra began.

"Regent's *orders*," the captain said again, louder, and drew his sword, and forty blades came free of forty scabbards in a single rippling shiver of steel, and the torchlight ran along them like fire along a fuse.

Veyra stared at her own army with an expression Doss had never imagined seeing on her face: bewilderment. Pure, naked, civilian bewilderment, the face of a woman discovering that the machine she had built no longer recognized its maker. "They aren't hearing me," she said, to Doss, to no one. "They aren't— I'm standing right here—"

"It doesn't need you," Doss said. "Aldric tried to tell us. It wears whoever's nearest. Right now, that's them."

The captain raised his sword, and it was not a command to advance, it was simply the first domino, and Doss brought his spear up at last because there was nothing left in the world between those forty blades and the people below except him and a woman who had just thrown away her sword.

"Veyra." He planted himself beside her, back nearly to hers, absurd, suicidal, exactly where he had chosen his ground. "Pick up your belt."

"What?"

"You spent nine years serving a lie because the truth was unbearable. Fine. Here's a bearable one." The soldiers were coming now, boots striking stone in that disciplined, unhurried cadence, and Doss set his feet and felt, strangely, no fear at all — only a fierce and final clarity, the kind that comes once in a life, at the exact center of it. "You don't get to quit. You get to *choose*. First true choice in nine years. Make it count."

For a moment she only looked at him. Torchlight and steel and the distant drum of the war overhead, and between them ten years of everything unsaid.

Then Veyra bent, and took up her sword.

"First rank," she said, and her voice came back to her like blood returning to a numb limb — ragged, human, *hers*, "will kneel. Second rank, disarm. That is not a request from your regent." She rolled her shoulders, settled her grip, and Doss saw the woman he had followed once, before everything, shining through the ruin of her like a lamp through smoke. "It is a request from the last honest officer in this army. And I am very, very good at making requests stick."

The first rank did not kneel. The first rank charged.

But they charged at two instead of forty, and the passage was narrow, and Veyra fought like a woman with nothing left to lose and one thing left to prove, and somewhere below, Doss knew, a hedge-witch was opening a book that would cost her everything — and the only question left in the world was whether the two of them, traitor and regent, deserter and hollowed, could hold one corridor of old stone long enough for a stranger to save them all.

Steel met steel. Doss laughed — he couldn't help it, wild and terrified and free.

"Still teaching terrain by moonlight?" he shouted over the din.

"Shut up and fight, Doss," said Veyra, and for the first time in nine years, the crown was silent in her head, and her voice was the only one there.
