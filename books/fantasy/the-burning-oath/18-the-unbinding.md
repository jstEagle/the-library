## 18. The Unbinding

The sound of the battle reached the stone hall as a heartbeat made of iron.

Yara felt it through the soles of her boots — the mountain itself drumming with the clash above, each blow in the pass a pulse transmitted down through rock older than kingdoms. Dust sifted from the ceiling of the kneeling kings, settling on their bowed stone heads like snow that would never melt. Somewhere overhead, Brannoc was holding the chokepoint with his body and his shield and whatever prayers a deserter still remembered. Somewhere overhead, Doss was laughing again, that wild terrified laugh she had come to love without ever quite admitting it.

And here, beneath all of it, in the hall where the first oath had been sworn by people whose names the world had eaten, Yara Vasquez knelt beside a dying king and opened Maren's grimoire.

The pages were warm. That was the thing no one had warned her about — not Maren, not the grimoire's own marginalia, not Sefa's careful dead-tongue fragments. The book was warm the way a hand is warm, the way a cheek is warm, and when Yara laid her palm flat against the vellum she could feel something underneath the ink moving like blood under skin.

*You knew this would be the price,* she thought at the book, at Maren, at whoever was listening. *You knew, and you gave it to me anyway.*

*I gave it to you because you were the only one stubborn enough to read it,* said the memory of Maren's voice, which lived now entirely inside Yara's head and always had, if she was honest, even before the wasting curse took the woman herself.

Sefa stood at the center of the hall, in the ring of standing stones that the first binder's people had raised before there were borders or crowns or anything worth lying about. The child had stopped being small. Not in body — she was still thin, still mud-brown, still the girl who had appeared at Yara's door in the storm with fire in her eyes — but in presence. The dead language poured out of her now without pause, without translation, and the hall drank it in. The kneeling kings seemed to lean closer. The dust hung suspended. Even the war above felt very far away, a rumor happening in another world.

Aldric lay at the ring's edge where they had carried him, the king who had been a crown's coat for longer than he could count. He was lucid tonight. He had been lucid for perhaps an hour, and he spent it the way he had spent every lucid hour since they found him: apologizing.

"I signed the orders," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Veyra's orders. My hand. My seal. I watched myself do it from somewhere behind my own eyes, and I — I want you to understand that I saw what I was doing, most of the time. That's the worst of it. Not the dark. The watching."

"I understand," Yara said, and found that she meant it. She had run from one life rather than watch herself fail in it. Aldric had stayed and watched for years. She did not know which of them was braver, and suspected the question was worthless.

"The rite," Aldric said. "The witness. What does it take from you?"

"Everything I love."

He closed his eyes. "Then let me say this while you can still hold it: whatever comes after, you did not fail anyone. I have been failed by everyone, these nine years. I know the shape of it. This isn't it."

Yara's throat closed. She bent over the grimoire instead of answering, because some kindnesses you cannot look at directly, like the sun, like grief, like the face of someone forgiving you for something you haven't done yet.

Sefa turned. Her eyes held flames that weren't there — had always held them, since the first night — but tonight the flames were quiet. Banked. Waiting.

"Yara." The common tongue, in Sefa's mouth, still sounded borrowed, a coat worn carefully. "It will ask you for the memories in order. Not the order you'd choose. Its order. The oldest first, then inward, like rings of a tree. Do you understand what the last ring will be?"

Yara understood. She had understood since the caverns, since the confession of the first binder had risen through her like water through a well. The oldest memory would go first, and the newest would go last, and the newest — the dearest, the one worn smoothest by handling — was a mud-caked child at a door in a storm, saying words in a dead tongue that Yara had somehow, impossibly, understood.

"Say it anyway," Yara said. "I want to hear it out loud once, while hearing it still means something."

Sefa crossed the hall and knelt in front of her, small again, just a child, just for a moment. "The last thing you'll lose is me."

Above them, the mountain shuddered. A crack ran up one wall, fine as a hair, and dust hissed down across the kneeling kings. The battle was coming closer, or losing, or both; Yara could not tell and had decided not to try. Doss and Veyra and the loyalists and the remnant of Amberfall's honor — all of it was a wall between this hall and the world, and walls exist to be spent.

She looked at Sefa. She let herself look, really look, the way you drink before crossing a desert.

"All right," Yara said. "Let's burn."

* * *

The grimoire opened itself to the final page.

Not the last page — the books of hedge-witches do not have last pages, they have pages that wait — but the page that had been blank every other time Yara had opened the book, through all the weeks of deciphering, all the nights of ink-stained frustration. Now it bore writing in a hand she recognized as her own and knew she had never written: the first binder's words, passed down hand to hand, soul-fragment to soul-fragment, waiting in Yara's blood until the hour they were needed.

They were simple. That was the cruelest part. No thunder, no grandeur. Words a woman might say over a hearth. Words a hedge-witch would choose, because hedge-witches know that the biggest magics are the ones that fit in a kitchen.

Yara began to speak.

The first word left her mouth and the valley came back to her.

Not a memory surfacing — a memory *arriving*, whole and sensory and merciless. The Hollow in high summer, the smell of crushed thyme under the goat-paths, the border stones warm as sleeping animals, the particular quality of light in a place where magic goes to die and therefore everything ordinary shines twice as bright. She was eleven and running through the grass with nothing wrong in her at all, and she heard herself speaking the second word, and the valley began to burn.

Not flame. *Unmaking.* The memory didn't fade; it was consumed, deliberately, the way a letter is consumed by someone who never wants it read again. The grass went gray from the edges inward. The goats dissolved into warmth. The border stones stood a moment longer than everything else, faithful even in deletion, and then they too were gone, and Yara knelt in the stone hall with tears running down her face and could not remember why.

She kept speaking.

Maren came next — no, Maren came third, because the second memory was her mother's hands, hands she had not thought of in twenty years, flour-dusted and cracked at the knuckles, teaching her to braid bread dough. She lost her mother's hands and did not know she had lost them, and then Maren arrived, vast and irreplaceable: Maren scolding, Maren laughing into her sleeve, Maren's cottage smelling of feverfew and smoke, Maren dying at moonset with the grimoire pressed into Yara's arms and the words *the crown remembers what the wearer forgets* still hanging in the air like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

Gone. The guilt went with it — the guilt that had been Yara's spine for fifteen years, the guilt that had made her fierce and lonely and self-sufficient, the guilt that had driven her out of the valley and back again. It burned away and left a hollow where her self-hatred used to live, and the hollow was worse than the pain, because the pain had at least been proof of love.

"You're doing it," Sefa whispered. Her hand hovered near Yara's shoulder, not touching. The seal-child knew better than anyone what happened when things touched during a rite. "You're doing it exactly right. Keep going."

"I don't know what I'm keeping going *for*," Yara said, and heard the words come out steady, and hated how steady, and spoke the next phrase.

Doss's laugh.

Oh, it saved the worst for almost-last after all. The laugh arrived complete — the first time she'd heard it, on the Broken Road, when Ivetta's impossible map had led them straight into a river and Doss had laughed until he wheezed, wild and warm, the laugh of a man who had decided to stop being ashamed of himself and hadn't managed it yet but was trying. Then the laugh on the wall at Amberfall. The laugh in the pass, hours ago, steel ringing, *still teaching terrain by moonlight?*

It burned. And with it went the way his voice dropped low when he talked about the night he deserted, and the way he looked at Veyra like a man reading an old scar, and the thousand small loyalties that had assembled themselves around Yara without her permission until they were the family she'd never had —

— gone, gone, gone, and Yara was a stranger kneeling in a strange hall, weeping without knowing for whom, speaking words in a language she somehow still knew because the words lived deeper than memory, in the fragment of soul that the rite could not touch because it *was* the rite.

Ivetta's maps. Gone. Brannoc singing to the goats. Gone. The storm. The door. The mud-caked child —

"No," said Yara, in common speech, breaking the rite for half a breath, half a heartbeat, because some part of her was clawing at the walls of her own skull trying to keep one single thing. "No, wait, please —"

The grimoire turned its own page. Warm as a hand. Patient as a grave.

Sefa took Yara's face in both small hands. Fire reflected in eyes that held no fire at all now, only a terrible tenderness.

"This is why it has to be you," Sefa said. "This is why it was always you. Someone who would fight for the last ring. Someone who would refuse to let go. Hold on as long as you can, Yara. Fight it. It makes the binding stronger — the first binder wrote that in the margin of everything, in every hand, in every generation: *the witness must love enough to resist.* You are doing it perfectly. You are doing it so perfectly."

And Yara — who could not remember the valley, could not remember Maren, could not remember the laugh or the maps or the song — looked at this child and felt the one remaining truth rise up through all the ash: *I know you. Whatever else is gone, I know you.*

She spoke the final words.

* * *

The Crowned Wound came out of King Aldric the way a splinter comes out of a wound that has held it too long — violently, reluctantly, screaming in a language of pressure and hunger that predated speech.

It rose above him as a shape that the eye refused: a crown, yes, but also a wound, a ragged opening in the world wearing gold, an absence dressed as authority. Nine years of a king's dreams poured off it like rain. Every whisper it had ever breathed through Aldric's skull, every order signed by a hollowed hand, every soldier marched and city threatened and child hunted — all of it was visible for one instant, written in the air, undeniable.

The armies above, mid-clash, felt it. Later, survivors on both sides would swear the fighting stopped for the length of a held breath, every blade in the pass stilled by the same cold certainty that something old had just been seen naked.

Yara, empty of everything except the rite, raised her hands.

The Wound turned toward her. Of course it did. She was the binder's echo; she was the door it had been trying to break for a thousand years. It rushed her — not physically, not exactly, but the way night rushes a candle — and Yara stood in its path with nothing left to lose and discovered that *nothing left to lose* is the strongest armor ever forged.

"You want in," she said, in the dead tongue, in the living tongue, in the tongue of kitchens and hedge-craft. "I know. I've known since the valley. But the line ends here, and it ends *chosen*."

"Sefa," she said. "Now. If you choose. Only if you choose."

And Sefa — the seal-child, the latest echo, the girl who had been bound before she could walk and hunted since she could run — stepped forward freely, for the first time in the long history of her line, and opened her arms.

"I choose," she said in the pre-kingdom tongue, and the hall translated it into every language at once, and the kneeling kings finally, after a thousand years, lowered their heads the rest of the way — not in subjugation but in farewell. "*I choose, and I unchoose, and I end it.*"

The Wound hesitated. A thousand years of taking vessels that had no choice met, for the first time, an open door offered gladly — and something in it, some starved animal part older than malice, *wanted* that, wanted the one thing it had never been given. It poured into her like a river accepting the sea.

For three heartbeats, Sefa blazed. Fire filled her eyes, her mouth, the spaces between her fingers; she was a crown of flame walking, the whole ancient hunger wearing a child's body one last time.

Then she smiled — Sefa's own crooked, mud-brown smile — and said, in common speech, in a little girl's voice:

"No."

And she dissolved the binding forever. Not sealed. Not transferred. *Ended* — unwoven thread by thread by a soul that had spent a thousand lifetimes learning exactly how the weaving worked. The Wound screamed down through every register of existence, and then, gently, like a storm exhausting itself over open water, it came apart. What remained drifted down through the hall as light, as ash, as something like gratitude, and the stones drank it, and the mountain sighed, and far above, the battle stopped for good.

King Aldric drew a breath — his own breath, unshared, unwatched — and wept like a man emerging from nine years underwater.

Sefa swayed. Yara caught her. Held her. Knew her name, knew it still, the last ring, the one thing the fire had spared because the witness had loved it hard enough to make it stronger than the burning —

— and then felt even that begin to slip, softly, like a boat leaving shore.

"Sefa," Yara said quickly, desperately, tasting the name like the last sweet of summer. "Your name is Sefa. Remember that I knew. Remember that I — that we —"

Her knees gave. The hall tilted. The last thing she saw was the child's face, blazing and wet and free, and the last thing she thought was not a memory at all but a hope, small and stubborn as a hedge-witch:

*Maybe someone will tell me.*

Then the dark came down, kind and total, and Yara Vasquez forgot herself completely.

* * *

When she woke, she was lying on stone warmed by a thousand years of oaths, and strangers were crying over her.

One was a huge scarred man, holding her hand as if it were made of glass. One was a woman clutching a map roll like a weapon. One was a knight with a broken smile, saying over and over, "You did it. Whatever else — you did it." And one was a child with quiet eyes, no fire in them anymore, who leaned close and whispered words in a dead tongue that Yara somehow, impossibly, still understood:

"Don't be afraid. We brought your stories with us. We're going to give them back — one at a time."

Yara looked up at all of them, at these weeping strangers who held her like treasure, and felt a warmth she could not explain and did not need to.

"Who are you?" she asked.

The knight wiped his eyes and grinned through it. "No one yet," he said. "But we've got time, and we've got stories. Can we start?"
