## 20. A Stranger Among Friends

The first thing Yara knew was warmth.

It came to her before anything else — before light, before sound, before the slow, bewildered surfacing of a self that had no shape yet. Warmth like sun on stone, like bread pulled from an oven, like a hand held over a candle flame at just the safe distance. It was everywhere and it was patient, and it seemed to be waiting for her.

She opened her eyes.

Light came through a window she did not recognize, falling in long amber bars across a floor of old grey planks. Dust motes turned slowly in it, unhurried, as if the whole world had all the time there ever was. She was lying in a bed beneath a quilt stitched from a hundred mismatched scraps — reds and blues and a faded green that might once have been someone's favorite color — and every single patch had been mended at least twice. Someone had loved this quilt. Someone had loved it enough to keep saving it.

She did not know where she was.

She did not know what she was.

That second thought arrived quietly, without panic, the way one notices a missing tooth with the tongue. She reached for her own name and found nothing there — not a blankness exactly, but a smoothness, like the inside of an unfired pot. There should have been something written there. There was only the curve of the vessel, waiting.

*Yara.*

The name came from somewhere outside her, soft as breath, and she turned her head on the pillow toward it.

A child sat beside the bed.

She was small — six years old, perhaps seven — with dark hair cut unevenly, as though someone had trimmed it with a knife and more love than skill. Her eyes were extraordinary: deep brown shot through with flecks that caught the window-light and held it, so that for a moment Yara could have sworn there were embers behind them, banked and gentle, a fire that had learned how to rest.

The child was watching her the way people watch a hearth they've tended through a long winter.

"You said that," Yara whispered. Her voice was rust and honey, a voice unused for days. "That word. *Yara.* Is that — is that me?"

The child nodded. Then, carefully, she pressed her small hand flat against her own chest. "*Sefa,*" she said, and waited, and said it again, slower, the way one offers a gift across a wide table. "*Sefa.*"

Sefa. The name landed in Yara like a stone dropped into a well — she felt it fall and fall and never heard it strike bottom. Something in her chest ached at the sound, a sweet and terrible ache, grief with no memory attached to it, mourning with no grave. Tears were running down her face before she understood she was crying.

"I'm sorry," she said, wiping at her cheeks with fingers that felt borrowed. "I don't know why I'm — I don't know you. Do I? I don't know *anything.*" The panic came then, finally, rising like floodwater. "I don't know my own name until you say it. I don't know this room, this bed, this —" she gestured helplessly at the window, the light, the world — "any of it. What happened to me? What's *wrong* with me?"

The door opened.

* * *

Three people stood in it, and every one of them was weeping.

The first was a big man — enormous, truly, tall and broad as a doorframe, with a beard like storm-clouds and hands the size of dinner plates. He filled the doorway the way a mountain fills a horizon. And he was crying openly, great silent tears rolling down into his beard, making no move to hide them, as though shame had never once been introduced to him.

The second was a woman, lean and sharp-eyed, with ink stains on her fingers and a rolled bundle of papers tucked under one arm like a soldier carries a spear. She was crying too, but furiously, angrily, dashing the tears away as fast as they came, as though they were a personal insult she intended to file a complaint about.

The third man did not cry at all. He simply stood there, and his stillness was worse than weeping. He was weathered and wiry, with grey coming into his dark hair and a sword-callused hand resting on the doorframe, and he looked at Yara the way a man looks at a country he thought burned to the ground, standing green again.

"Oh," said the giant, and his voice was low and rolling, a landslide trying to be gentle. "Oh, she's awake. Brannoc, don't — don't crush her, mind the quilt, mind—" But he was already crossing the room, and the woman grabbed his arm, and between them they achieved a kind of hovering, trembling halt at the foot of the bed, three strangers arranged like mourners who had arrived too early for the funeral and found the deceased sitting up asking for breakfast.

Except none of them looked at her like a stranger.

They looked at her the way you look at home.

"Stop," Yara said, and her voice cracked on it. "Please. Stop looking at me like that. Like you — like you *know* me. Because I look at you and there's nothing. You're kind faces and I'm sure you're kind people, but I don't — I can't—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "There's a hole where everything should be. Who am I? Who are you? Somebody please just *tell me.*"

Silence, except for the woman's ragged breathing and the creak of the floorboards under the giant's weight.

Then the third man stepped forward.

He moved slowly, deliberately, the way a man approaches a frightened animal or a drawn bow. He knelt beside the bed — knelt, this hard-bitten soldier of a man, folding down onto one knee on the cold boards — and he brought himself level with her, and when he spoke his voice was rough and careful, sanded smooth at every edge.

"My name is Doss," he said. "Doss Ferren. I was a knight once, in Veyra's own company, and I deserted her the night she took power, and it took me a long time to learn that leaving a rotten thing isn't the same as being rotten yourself." He paused. "You taught me that. Or — you will have. I'm told you did."

"Doss," the woman hissed, "what are you—"

"Hush, Ivetta." He didn't take his eyes off Yara. "Let me do it properly."

He cleared his throat. And then, absurdly, wonderfully, he bowed — a full, formal, courtly bow from one knee, sweeping his hand across his chest, the whole ridiculous ceremony of it performed beside a sickbed in a small room smelling of herbs and woodsmoke.

"Doss Ferren," he said again. "Knight of nowhere, servant of these good people, terrible singer, worse cook, better friend than I deserve to be. And you are Yara Vasquez, hedge-witch of the Hollow, and the bravest person I have ever known." His voice caught on the last words and he let it catch; he didn't fight it. "I know you don't remember any of that. I know it sounds like a story about somebody else. So I'm not going to ask you to believe it. I'm only going to ask—" and here the composure he'd been holding so carefully finally trembled, and his eyes went bright, and he laughed at himself, wet and broken, "—I'm only going to ask if I can try again."

"Try again?" Yara whispered.

"Meet you." He spread his hands. "All of us. We got to know you once already — the hard way, the road way, mud and blood and fear and all of it. And you knew us. And then you paid the price for saving every living soul in this kingdom, and the price was us. Was all of it." His jaw worked. "So we're going to give it back. Not all at once. We're going to tell you everything, piece by piece, day by day, for as long as it takes. And today, right now, the only thing I want is to shake your hand and introduce myself, like we've just met on a road somewhere. Because in every way that matters—" his voice broke clean through, and he smiled anyway, "—we've just met. And I would very much like to know you again."

Yara looked at him — this stranger kneeling in the dust motes with his heart hanging out of his chest like a banner — and felt that same bottomless ache open in her, the one Sefa's name had made. Grief for something lost that she could not remember losing. Love arriving ahead of its own reasons.

She put out her hand.

His swallowed hers whole, warm and scarred and shaking.

"Hello, Doss Ferren," she said. "I'm — apparently — Yara."

"*Apparently*," snorted the woman — Ivetta — and then she was laughing and sobbing at once, ugly and glorious, and she thrust her ink-stained hand at Yara as if offering evidence. "Ivetta Marrow. Cartographer. My maps show places that don't exist yet, which sounds mad, and is mad, and you believed me about it when nobody else would, so we're stuck with each other forever. That's how it works. Those are the rules."

"And I," rumbled the giant, dropping to his knees by the bed with a crash that shook the frame, taking her free hand in both of his enormous ones with the delicacy of a man holding a sparrow, "am Brannoc. I held a gate once because you asked me to. Best thing I ever did with my life, and I include the time I ate forty eggs on a dare." He beamed through his tears. "You'll hear that story. There's a song now. Ivetta wrote verses. They're inaccurate."

"They're *dramatized*—"

"They're lies with rhythm."

And Sefa climbed up onto the edge of the bed, uninvited and entirely welcome, and leaned her small warm weight against Yara's side, and pointed at herself, and said again, patiently, "*Sefa.*"

"Sefa," Yara repeated, and the ache sang. "I keep — when I hear that name, I feel like I've lost something. But I haven't lost anything. I have it right here." She touched the child's hair, wondering. "Why does it feel like grief?"

Nobody answered right away. Doss and Ivetta exchanged a glance over her head, some whole conversation passing between them in a heartbeat, and Yara saw it and felt the shape of it — things being withheld, kindly, temporarily, the way you carry a gift behind your back.

"Because it's true," Sefa said.

Her voice was clear and small and old, older than her body, older than the room, and the embers in her eyes stirred.

"You gave up your memories so that I could end the binding. So the Wound would never wear anyone again. Everyone here stayed with you while you slept. Everyone here cried. That's why it feels like grief." She laid her head against Yara's shoulder. "Because it is. But it's the good kind. The kind that means somebody loved you enough to lose you, and stayed anyway."

Yara sat with that. Outside the window, birds were arguing about something in a language she almost — *almost* — felt she should understand. The quilt lay warm over legs she didn't recognize. Four strangers crowded around her bed like a wall against the world, and the strangest thing, the thing that made fresh tears come, was that the warmth she'd woken in hadn't left. It had only turned out to have faces.

"I don't remember any of you," she said quietly. "I want to be honest about that. If I spend a year hearing your stories, some part of me will always be listening to a stranger's tale. I can't promise it'll ever feel like mine again."

"That's alright," said Doss.

"It doesn't have to be yours alone," said Ivetta, patting her map-roll like a drum. "That's rather the point. Shared memories are load-bearing. Ask any bridge."

"We'll hold them for you," Brannoc said simply. "Until you're ready to carry your share."

* * *

They fed her soup that afternoon — Doss's, by the sheepish admission of everyone else, and genuinely terrible, thin and oversalted, and Yara ate every drop and asked for more just to watch him glow. Ivetta spread maps across the foot of the bed and began, tentatively, with geography: *this is Amberfall, this is the pass, this is the Hollow, this is where you were born.* Names without roots, but she listened hungrily anyway, planting each one like a seed in the smooth soil of herself, trusting something to grow.

Brannoc dozed in the corner chair that groaned under him. Sefa never strayed farther than arm's reach.

And near dusk, when the light went long and gold across the floorboards, Doss returned and sat on the edge of the chair beside her bed, elbows on his knees, and asked, "Where shall we start?"

"The beginning," Yara said. "However much of it you have."

He smiled — a real one, crooked and tired and lit from inside. "The beginning's a valley," he said. "A valley where magic dies at the border stones, and a woman who kept goats, and a storm, and a knock at her door." He glanced at Sefa, curled now against Yara's side with her ember-eyes half closed. "Shall I start with the night the two of you met?"

"Yes," Yara said — and then stopped, because something had surfaced, sudden and bright as a fish breaking water. Not a memory. Nothing so kind. Just a feeling, a single grain of truth washed down through all that emptied country: the certainty that once, a long time ago, someone had told her that stories kept things alive, and that she had believed it with her whole stubborn heart.

"Start there," she said softly. "Tell it true. I'll be listening for what I lost."

Doss drew a breath, and outside the window the first star came out over mountains she did not know and would learn to love, and he began:

"There was a woman in a valley that forgets..."
