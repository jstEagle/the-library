## 21. One Story at a Time

### I. Doss

Doss had never been a man for words. In Veyra's service he had given orders in clipped syllables, and in disgrace he had gone quieter still, letting silence do the work of apology. But now he sat on a low stool in the firelit hall of the Hollow — the valley that had once been a prison and was becoming something else — and found that words were the only thing he had left to give.

Yara sat across from him in a chair they'd dragged from Maren's old cottage, her hands folded in her lap like a guest at someone else's table. Which, Doss supposed, she was. She knew none of them. She knew nothing of the road, the pass, the stone kings kneeling in the dark beneath the mountains. She knew only what her eyes told her: four strangers who wept when she woke, and who now would not leave her alone.

He'd promised himself he wouldn't cry while telling it. He managed it for nearly an hour.

"You have to understand," he said, "the first time I saw you, I thought you were trouble." He laughed, rough and wet. "Not the child. You. You came out of that valley with a goat-herd's staff and a dead woman's grimoire strapped to your back like a sword, and you looked at me — a knight, or what was left of one — like I was the one who needed protecting."

"I did?" Yara said. Her voice was careful, the voice of someone testing ice.

"You did. And you were right, which was the worst part." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and let the memory come up out of him like water from a deep well. "There was a bridge, three days out of Amberfall. Rotten through in the middle. Brannoc could have waded the river, but the rest of us couldn't, and night was coming, and things lived in that river that Ivetta's maps didn't bother naming because she said naming them gave them ideas."

"Did it?"

"Ivetta says everything gives something ideas." He smiled. "You walked out onto that bridge first. No hesitation. Just — stepped out over black water fifty feet down, testing each plank with your staff, and you turned around and said, *'If I fall, keep walking. Don't make my mistake dramatic.'*"

Yara's brow furrowed. Not recognition — Doss had learned by now not to hope for that — but something adjacent to it. The shape of herself, offered back to her.

"That sounds like something a fool would say," she said.

"It is exactly what a fool would say. It's also what saved our lives, because Sefa heard you say it and decided you were the bravest person alive, and she followed you onto the bridge without a flicker of fear, and after that none of us could be afraid either. Fear's a contagion. So is courage. You caught us all with it."

The fire popped. Outside, the autumn wind moved through the rebuilt border stones, and the sound they made now was different than the sound Doss imagined they'd made before — less like a wall, more like a wind chime. Open gates did that.

"What else?" Yara asked quietly. "Tell me something else."

Doss settled back. He had weeks of this. He had a whole road stored up inside him, every mile of it, and he meant to walk her down every step.

"Well," he said. "There was the night you tried to cook rabbit and set Ivetta's maps on fire..."

### II. Ivetta

Ivetta did not tell stories. She showed them.

She spread the maps across the long table in Maren's cottage — the good ones, drawn on cured hide and vellum, inked in three colors of ink she'd ground herself — and weighted the corners with river stones and a teacup and Brannoc's enormous hand.

"This is where we met," she said, tapping a bend in the Broken Road west of Amberfall. Her finger was precise, cartographer's steady, though her heart was hammering the way it always did now when she showed these to Yara. "You were standing in the middle of the road arguing with a goat. The goat was winning."

"I don't believe that," Yara said, but she was leaning over the map, tracing the lines with her fingertip, and Ivetta watched her do it and felt something crack open gently in her chest.

"The goat's name was Duchess. You'd named her after a duchess you claimed to have met in a fever dream about burning crowns. You said the duchess had terrible taste in hats." Ivetta slid a second map across, this one newer, the ink still sharp. "This is the road as we traveled it. Look here — see how the line wavers? That's where Sefa's presence bent the land. Magic pooled around her like heat around a forge. My compasses spun. My maps drew themselves wrong. This village—" she touched a smudge, "—moved half a mile east overnight. I mapped it twice just to be sure I wasn't mad."

"And was she there? Sefa? In your memories of her?"

Ivetta paused. This was the question that hurt, the one that sat under her ribs like a swallowed stone. Because yes, Sefa was there, silent and flame-eyed and holding Yara's hand on every mile of that road, and now Sefa was — elsewhere. Dissolved into the stones of the valley, into the kindness of the world, present only as a warmth in the air and an echo in the border stones that sometimes sang if you stood close enough.

"She was everywhere," Ivetta said simply. "But this isn't about grief. Well." She corrected herself. "It's about grief, but it's also about geography, and I've always trusted geography more."

She turned to the last map — the impossible one, the one she'd drawn in the caverns beneath the mountains, the one showing passages that hadn't existed until they walked them. Places that didn't exist yet, drawn true. She'd stopped questioning it somewhere around the third miracle.

"Now watch," she said, and took Yara's hand, and placed the woman's finger on the little inked mark of the Hollow itself. "You are here. And here—" the finger moved south, along the road, past Amberfall, into the mountains, "—is where you went. And here is what you did. And I'm going to walk you through every league of it, and when we're done, you'll have traveled the whole road twice: once in the world, and once on paper. And paper, Yara Vasquez, holds its ink better than any head I've ever known."

Yara looked up at her. "Why are you doing this? All of you. Strangers don't do this."

Ivetta smiled, and it cost her something, and she paid it gladly.

"We're not strangers," she said. "We're just early. You'll catch up."

### III. Brannoc

Brannoc sang.

He had never sung for anyone before — his voice was a landslide given melody, too big for rooms, suited only to mountains and empty fields — but the Hollow's evenings were long and the fire was warm and Yara had asked, shyly, whether the scar across his chest still hurt.

"Only when it rains," he said. "And when I remember how I got it. So: mostly when it rains."

"How did you get it?"

And so he told her, but telling wasn't enough, not for this. Some things lived in the body rather than the mind, and the Battle of the Pass lived in his ribs, in the ache of the spear-wound that should have killed him, in the memory of turning around at the chokepoint and seeing Doss's face as they dragged him away.

So he sang it instead. An old deserter's tune his grandmother had taught him, about soldiers and snow, and he bent new words into it, clumsy words, true ones:

*At the throat of the pass where the cold wind cries,*
*I stood with a gate-bar taller than skies,*
*And the riders came on like a flood unbound,*
*And I held, and I held, and I held my ground.*

Yara listened with her hands pressed together in her lap, and when he reached the verse about the moment the bar finally splintered — *and the wood gave way but the will did not* — he saw her mouth move silently along, shaping words she couldn't possibly know yet.

Brannoc stopped singing.

"You know it," he said, wonder cracking his voice wide.

"No," Yara said slowly. "I mean — I don't *know* it. But it feels..." She searched for the word. "Familiar. Like a room I've been in. Like the smell of bread when you wake up hungry."

Brannoc wiped his eyes with the back of a hand the size of a dinner plate, and laughed, and the laugh filled the cottage the way his voice filled valleys.

"That's it," he said. "That's exactly it. That's what we're doing, all of us. We can't put the memories back where they were. They're gone from you, and grieving that won't grow them back. But a song doesn't live in one head, does it? A song lives in *every* head that's ever carried it. So we'll carry yours for a while. And when you hum along — even a note, even half a note — that's you taking some of it back. Not as it was." He tapped his scarred chest. "Better than it was. Shared things weigh less and hold more. My gran said that. She was right about almost nothing else."

He picked the song up again from the top, slower this time, leaving space between verses, and by the third verse Yara was humming, uncertain and off-key and absolutely unmistakable, and Brannoc sang louder to cover his own crying, which fooled no one, least of all him.

### IV. Yara

At night, alone in the small room above Maren's cottage — *her* cottage now, though the word still fit strangely, like a borrowed coat — Yara lit a candle and opened the grimoire.

She couldn't read it. That was the cruelest joke of her new life: the book was hers by inheritance, by blood, by the fragment of the first binder's soul that had lived in her line for generations, and its pages were blank to her. Whatever knowledge had unlocked them had burned away with everything else in the rite.

But she remembered — no, not remembered, *had been told*, which was becoming a different and stranger kind of knowing — that Maren had taught her from this book. That fragments of Maren's teaching had come back to her already, surfacing in dreams, arriving in Doss's stories like fish rising to feed.

She closed her eyes and let the candle burn and waited.

*Maren's voice,* in Doss's retelling from two nights ago: *"Magic isn't power, girl. Power is what magic looks like when it's frightened. Magic is attention. You pay attention to a thing long enough, honestly enough, and it pays you back."*

Yara whispered it aloud to the empty room. "Magic is attention."

And beneath the words, faint as a watermark, something stirred — not a memory exactly, but the groove a memory leaves, the way a path remains after the grass dies. She saw, for half a heartbeat, a kitchen. A kettle. A woman with iron-gray hair scolding her lovingly about chopped herbs. Gone before she could hold it.

But the shape of it stayed. The warmth of it stayed.

She understood then what the others were doing, what they perhaps only half-knew they were doing. They weren't restoring her. You couldn't restore a burned field by replanting it row for row; you planted anew, and what grew was kin to what was lost but not identical — hardier, sometimes, in the places the fire had fed the soil.

They were telling her own life back to her, and every telling grew something.

She took out charcoal and a scrap of vellum — Ivetta kept her generously supplied — and wrote, in her own hand, the words she'd gathered so far, a harvest gleaned from other people's mouths:

*Duchess the goat. The rotten bridge. Don't make my mistake dramatic. Magic is attention. The wood gave way but the will did not. Sefa means "hearth" in the old tongue. Doss laughs like a door coming off its hinges. Ivetta names storms. Brannoc is afraid of ducks.*

She stopped. Laughed. Wrote again:

*I was loved. Evidence: overwhelming.*

She looked at that last line for a long time. It was the only entry she hadn't been told. It was the one she'd deduced, the way Ivetta deduced coastlines from the curve of a single bay — and it was the truest thing on the page.

### V. Doss

On the last night of autumn, the whole company gathered in the great room, and Doss brought out the thing he'd been carrying since Amberfall: a whetstone, worn smooth, that Yara had once used to sharpen her staff into something closer to a spear.

"You dropped this crossing the pass," he said. "I meant to give it back a hundred times. Then you forgot, and then—" he gestured vaguely at everything, the forgetting entire, "—well. Seemed important to keep it safe."

Yara took it. Turned it over. And frowned.

"There's writing on it," she said.

There was. Tiny letters, scratched into the stone's face, worn nearly smooth: a name, and a date, and three words.

Doss leaned in and read it aloud, and his voice failed halfway through, because he'd never noticed the inscription before — or perhaps it hadn't been there before, and the valley, which remembered what people forgot, had grown it the way it grew moss on the border stones.

"*Maren Vasquez. For my Yara. Pay attention.*"

The room went very quiet. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, faint and clear, the border stones were singing — the sound they made now, everyone had noticed, whenever Sefa's echo passed among them, which seemed to be often, and seemed, lately, to be listening.

Yara held the whetstone against her chest with both hands, the way you hold a thing that is small and weighs nothing and contains everything.

"Maren," she repeated. The name of a woman she'd mourned twice: once in life, once in forgetting. "My mentor. The one who died giving me the grimoire."

"Yes," Doss said softly.

"I don't remember her." Yara's voice was steady, but her eyes shone. "But I remember being told about her, and I remember that all of you go gentle and sad when you say her name, and I remember that she wrote *pay attention* on a whetstone, which is the most hedge-witch thing I've ever heard, and somehow—" she laughed, wet and wondering, "—somehow I think I loved her. Am I right? Did I love her?"

"You did," Brannoc rumbled.

"Fiercely," said Ivetta.

"With your whole stubborn heart," said Doss. "And tomorrow, if you want, I'll tell you about the day she died, and what she said to you at moonset. I've been saving it. It deserves its own evening."

Yara nodded. Then she looked around the room — at the knight who'd deserted an empire for a conscience, the mapmaker whose maps dreamed ahead, the giant who sang battles into lullabies — at the family assembled out of exiles and accidents and one impossible child's quiet gravity.

"Then we'll keep going," she said. "One story at a time. Until there's nothing left to tell." She paused, and smiled, and the smile was new and old at once. "And then, I suspect, we'll start making new stories worth telling. Starting tomorrow morning — because someone has to teach me to tend goats I apparently once owned, and I hear Duchess answers to no one but me."

They laughed, all of them, and the fire burned low, and outside the window the border stones sang their open-gated song into the dark.

But it was later, much later, when the others had gone to bed and Doss lingered banked the fire, that Yara spoke once more from the doorway of the stairs — quietly, as if asking permission.

"Doss. The grimoire. Tonight, when I held it near the whetstone... one page wasn't blank anymore."

He turned. "What did it say?"

Her eyes reflected the ember-light, and behind them, faintly, something flamed that might have been memory or might have been beginning.

"It said: *'When she remembers the oath, bring her to the stones. There is one more story, and it is hers to swear.'*"
