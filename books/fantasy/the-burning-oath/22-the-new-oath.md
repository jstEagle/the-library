## 22. The New Oath

The border stones of the Hollow had been broken once by riders and once by grief, and now they were broken a third time — deliberately, carefully, by Yara's own hands and the hands of everyone she loved.

She knelt in the spring mud with a chisel that had belonged to Maren, working at the seam of the great standing stone where the old binding-script still curled like frost across its face. The letters were older than any kingdom. They were the words of the first binder, the hedge-witch whose soul-fragment Yara had carried all her life without knowing it, and they were made to hold things in: to wall magic out, to wall people in, to make of a valley a locked box with a woman inside it who called her loneliness safety.

*Not anymore,* Yara thought, and struck.

The chisel bit. A shard of script flaked away and crumbled between her fingers into ordinary dust. Where the lettering broke, the stone beneath was just stone — grey, patient, warm from the morning sun. It had only ever wanted to be a rock. Someone had made it a prison, and someone else, a long time ago, had made *her* believe a prison could be a home.

"You're going to ruin your back," Doss said behind her. He was hauling the second stone upright with Brannoc's help, the two of them grunting and swearing companionably as the pillar swung into its socket. "And then Ivetta will draw a map of you lying flat for a week, and it will be very accurate, and we will never hear the end of it."

"I heard my name," Ivetta called from the cart, where she sat cross-legged with ink to her elbows and three maps fanned out around her like the tail feathers of an enormous, disreputable bird. "And for your information, I have already drawn it. *The Convalescence of Yara Vasquez, Annotated.* There's a small illustration of you complaining."

"There is not," Yara said.

"There is. You're very recognizable. I gave you a dramatic scar."

Yara touched her cheek, where no scar was, and laughed — really laughed, the kind that came up from somewhere below memory, below even the place where her memories used to live. That was the strange arithmetic of this new life of hers: she remembered everything that mattered, but none of it was hers alone anymore. Every recollection had been handed back to her piece by piece over the long winter, told aloud around fires and over dishes and on long grey roads, until each one bore the fingerprints of the teller. Her memories of Maren were also Doss's memories of hearing about Maren. Her memory of the storm and the mud-caked child at her door belonged now to all five of them, because Sefa had asked to hear it eleven times and Ivetta had cried at the good part every single time.

She had woken in the stone hall beneath the mountains knowing nothing — not her name, not her own hands — and these strangers had wept at the sight of her open eyes. She understood now what they had known then: that she had paid everything, and that they had decided, before she could even speak, that they would pay it back.

It had taken a year. It was not finished. It would perhaps never be finished, and that was the point.

"Again," Brannoc rumbled, setting his shoulder against the third stone. He moved more slowly than he had before the pass; the wound in his side had healed crooked, and the cold made him stiff. But he was alive, which had seemed impossible on the night the company fled Amberfall and left him holding a gate against men who had once been his neighbors. Doss had gone back for him through two days of frozen forest and come out the other side carrying a giant on his back and a story he would tell, for the rest of his life, to anyone who would listen — usually twice.

The third stone rose. The fourth. They worked through the morning, and the valley watched them work.

The Hollow had changed while they were away — or rather, it had changed the moment the binding broke, and the year since had been spent learning what it wanted to be. Magic crept back over the border stones like water finding an old channel: shy at first, then greedy. Wildflowers bloomed out of season in colors that did not quite have names. The goats — Yara had insisted on bringing the goats, over considerable objection — had produced two kids whose bleating occasionally echoed a half-second after their mouths moved. Old Tomas down the valley swore his well water tasted of starlight. Nobody starved. Nobody fled. People were frightened, and then curious, and then, slowly, glad, in the way people are glad of weather that finally breaks.

Yara had expected to feel like a stranger here. In a way she did — the girl who had tended goats in this valley was someone she knew only as a story now, told by others, softened at the edges. But the strangeness sat differently than she'd feared. It sat like a door left open. She could walk through it whenever she liked, in either direction.

* * *

They broke for bread and hard cheese at midday, sitting in a row along the fallen half of the oldest stone, and Sefa came down from the ridge to join them.

She was fourteen now, or thereabouts — no one knew her true age, least of all Sefa — and she walked like someone who had chosen her own body recently and was still pleased with it. The flames that had lived in her eyes in those first terrible months were gone. What remained was something quieter: a depth, a patience, the look of a person who has stood at the center of something vast and come back knowing how small and how enormous a single life can be.

She sat down next to Yara without asking, which was her way, and stole half the cheese, which was also her way.

"How does it feel?" Yara asked. "Being near the stones now?"

Sefa considered this with the seriousness she brought to all questions, chewing. When she spoke, her voice was her own — common speech, learned word by word across a hundred lessons, though sometimes, when she dreamed, other words came through her, older ones, and the stones themselves seemed to lean in to listen.

"Like being listened to kindly," she said. "Before, when I touched them, they were afraid. Everything here was afraid. Now—" She laid her palm flat against the fallen stone, and Yara felt it too, faintly, the way you feel a cat purring through a floorboard: a low contentment rising out of the earth. "Now they are just glad the shouting stopped."

"The shouting," Doss said, "being an ancient entity of hunger and flame bound into the crown of kings."

"The shouting," Sefa agreed serenely, and took more cheese.

Yara watched her and felt the old ache move through her, the one that never fully healed and that she had stopped wanting to heal. Because Sefa was not entirely Sefa — had never been entirely anyone, that was the tragedy of the seal-line, generations of children who were echoes of a hedge-witch dead two thousand years. But the Wound was ended. Dissolved, unmade, unbound forever when Sefa had taken it into herself freely, willingly, and let it go — and in the letting-go, whatever echo of the first binder had lived in the line had finally finished her long work and gone to rest. What remained in Sefa was only ever going to be Sefa: a girl who loved cheese and stories and, Yara suspected, Doss's terrible singing, though she'd die before admitting it.

Sometimes, though — rarely, gently, the way wind moves a curtain — Yara felt the stones notice Sefa. Felt the valley regard her with something like gratitude. As if the land itself remembered being saved, and knew whom to thank.

"She's listening right now, isn't she," Ivetta said softly. "From the stones. Your echo."

Sefa smiled. "She's always welcome," she said. "But she isn't me. I'm me." And she said it the way she said everything now, plainly, like a person setting down a heavy thing and finding her arms still worked.

* * *

The last stone was the hardest, because it was the keystone — the one at the head of the old road, the one Yara had stepped past on the day she crossed over, magic flooding back into the world around her like blood into water. Its face was dense with script, the masterwork of the binding, and Maren's chisel skated off it twice before Yara stopped, wiped her hands on her skirt, and admitted to herself what she already knew.

This one wasn't meant to be chiseled. This one was meant to be answered.

She stood before it in the late afternoon light, and the company gathered behind her — Doss with his arms folded, trying to look casual and failing; Ivetta with ink-stained fingers and her newest map rolled under her arm, the one of the Hollow redrawn with its gate drawn open; Brannoc looming and gentle as a hillside; Sefa barefoot in the grass, watching with eyes that held no fire and all the warmth in the world.

Yara put her hand flat on the stone. The old words hummed under her palm, the first binder's words, the ones she had spoken in the dark beneath the mountains while her memories burned away one by one — Maren, the valley, Doss's laugh, Sefa's name — trading her whole self for the world's freedom, and meaning it, and being glad, even as it went.

She knew them all again now. Not as they were — some of them were different, retold, improved, embroidered by a year of loving tellers — but as they had become. Shared things. Stronger things. A memory kept by one person is a candle in a window; a memory kept by five is a bonfire, and it does not go out when one of you sleeps.

"I don't know the counter-word," she said aloud. "There might not be one. So I'm going to say my own."

Doss made a small sound, like a man swallowing a speech.

Yara closed her eyes. The stone waited. The valley waited. Somewhere in the grain of the rock, kindly, an old echo leaned close to listen.

"I was bound here once," Yara said. "By fear, mostly. Mine and other people's. This valley kept me safe the way a locked room keeps you safe — from everything, including living." Her voice caught, steadied. "I ran away from Maren because running was the only shape my courage knew. I came back too late. I will carry that as long as I carry anything, and I don't want it taken from me, even if I could trade it. It's mine. It taught me."

Behind her, quietly, Brannoc began to hum — the slow road-song he sang at crossings and departures, the one about walking on.

"So this is the new oath," Yara said. "Not a binding. A belonging. I swear it on this stone, and on the grave-marker we raised for Maren on the hill, and on every story these people have given back to me." She opened her eyes. "I swear to keep the gate open. To anyone fleeing anything, the way I was fleeing, the way Sefa was fleeing: come in. Rest. Leave when you're ready or stay forever, and either way you'll be fed. I swear this valley will never again be a wall. It will be a door. And I swear—" her voice broke properly now, and she let it, because these people had seen her at every possible worst and loved her anyway, and there was no longer any version of herself she needed to protect— "I swear I will remember. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. Because you told me. Because you refused to let me be lost. Because love that gets retold is still love, and maybe it's the only kind that lasts."

She pressed her hand harder against the stone, and spoke the final words in the old tongue — the dead language she had somehow always understood, the language of binders and seals and the long war between crowns and wounds. But she bent the words, the way Sefa had taught her, the way living languages bend. She took the grammar of imprisonment and turned it inside out.

*I am not held,* she said in the tongue of the first binder. *I hold.*

The script on the keystone flared — gold, then white, then a soft green like new leaves — and did not shatter. It *changed*. Letter by letter, the old binding unwove itself and rewove, the same craft pointed in the opposite direction, and Yara felt it settle through the stone and down into the roots of the valley like a seed taking hold. Not a wall. A threshold. The stones would still stand. Travelers would still pause at them, the way travelers pause at any door worth knocking on. But nothing would be kept out, and no one would be kept in.

The light faded. The stone was just stone again, warm under her hand, wearing its new words like a name freshly given.

Sefa stepped forward and touched the keystone beside Yara's hand, and the valley sighed — there was no other word for it, a long exhale through grass and water and the roots of hills, and far off, the goats started bleating in a way that suggested the echo was doing its half-second trick again, and Ivetta burst out laughing, and Doss said, "Well," in a voice that wasn't steady at all, and Brannoc's humming swelled into actual singing, which was worse and better than the humming.

* * *

They built the fire that night at the foot of the keystone, because it seemed right to mark the day, and because Ivetta declared that historic occasions required stew, and because nobody had ever once won an argument with Ivetta about food.

Yara sat with her back against the warm stone and looked at them all in the firelight and performed the quiet ritual she did now most evenings, the one no one had taught her and that she suspected she would do for the rest of her life: she named them, silently, and with each name she set a memory against it like a coin on a scale.

*Doss.* The knight who deserted rather than serve a hollowed regent, who went back through frozen hell for Brannoc, who had knelt beside her empty-eyed body in the stone hall and introduced himself as if for the first time and asked, so gently, *may I try again?* She remembered the laugh she'd lost and been given back — his laugh, big and unguarded, the laugh of a man who had decided to stop being ashamed of joy.

*Ivetta.* Whose impossible maps had led them through places that didn't exist yet, and who had drawn the mountain hall afterward and marked it, simply, *a grave*, and wept doing it, and then drawn the Hollow with its gate thrown wide.

*Brannoc.* Who held gates. Who sang badly and meant it completely. Who had chosen, twice, to stand in the worst place so others could pass, and who was here, alive, crooked-healed, second helpings of stew stacked on his bowl like masonry.

*Sefa.* Who had been a vessel and a seal and an echo, and who had chosen, freely, at the end, to take the Wound into herself and end it — and who was now, gloriously, only a girl, arguing with Ivetta about whether the stew needed more salt, in two languages, winning in both.

*Maren.* On the hill, under the cairn they'd built, with the grimoire buried at her side at last — its work done, its warnings all delivered, its author finally allowed to rest. Yara remembered her dying at moonset, remembered the wasting curse and the last fierce grip of her hand, remembered *the crown remembers what the wearer forgets*. She remembered, and it hurt, and she kept it.

That was the whole secret, she thought, watching sparks climb toward the early stars. That was what the first binder had never had, poor ghost, sealing her wound alone in the dark with no one to carry her memory home. Forgetting hadn't been the price. Forgetting alone had been the price. And her family had simply — stubbornly, impossibly, one story at a time — refused to let her pay it by herself.

"Yara." Sefa had appeared at her elbow, silent as ever, holding something cupped in both hands. "Look. It came out of the keystone today, when the words changed."

In her palms lay a splinter of stone no bigger than a finger bone, and threaded through it, glowing faintly gold, ran a single line of the old script. Yara turned it in the firelight and read it, and her breath caught.

It was the new oath. The whole of it, compressed into one living line — the grammar of belonging, self-sustaining, growing. A seed of the door.

"It wants to be planted," Sefa said. "Somewhere else. There are other valleys, Yara. Other walls. Other people locked in rooms that feel like safety." She looked up, and in her eyes, for just a moment, Yara saw not the old flames but something kinder and no less bright: the reflection of the fire, and beyond it, reflected again, a horizon full of roads. "The echo thinks so too. She says the work isn't finished. She says it's never finished. She says—" Sefa grinned suddenly, fourteen years old and ancient as the hills. "She says we should pack tonight."

Yara looked at the splinter glowing in the child's hands. She looked at her family around the fire, already, without discussion, beginning to argue happily about routes and provisions and whose turn it was to lead the goats. She looked east, past the open gate, toward all the locked valleys waiting in the dark for someone to bring them a key made of stories.

"Well," Yara Vasquez said, and stood, and felt every memory she owned rise with her like a tide. "Then we'd better start telling the next one."
