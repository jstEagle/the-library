## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The thistle was best cut before the sun touched it.

Lena Ishikawa had learned that in her first autumn in Saltmere, from Maren's own mouth, delivered in the flat tone the old woman reserved for things she considered obvious. *Cut it before the light finds it, girl. The salt rises overnight and settles in the stems like breath in lungs. Once the sun warms them, they exhale, and you're left holding nothing but weed.* Maren had been dead three years now, and still Lena rose in the blue-black hour to walk the flats with her sickle, because some lessons were carved deeper than grief.

She moved along the eastern margin of the salterns where the ground crusted white and brittle, boots crunching a rhythm she knew by heart. The valley lay quiet around her—not sleeping quiet, but the particular hush of Saltmere, which was less an absence of sound than an absence of *reach*. No owl called here. No fox screamed on the fells. Even the wind came down the mountain passes already tamed, arriving at the valley floor too thin to carry anything but cold.

That was the salt. Everyone in Saltmere said it without thinking, the way farmers elsewhere spoke of rain. The salt kept things out. The salt kept things *quiet*.

Lena knew better than most what else it kept out.

She knelt at the first stand of salt-thistle, silver-grey spines catching the last of the starlight, and set her basket down with care. The cutting was a ritual older than her presence in the valley—older, Maren had claimed, than the kingdom itself. Left hand flat against the earth, feeling for the slow pulse of brine beneath the crust. Right hand low, blade angled against the stem's grain. One clean stroke, no sawing, no waste.

*Take only what the plant has finished with,* Maren had said. *The valley remembers greed.*

Lena cut twelve stems, then thirteen, then stopped at fourteen because fourteen was enough and wanting more was how people got noticed. She wrapped them in damp linen to keep the salt-sap from bleeding away and tucked them into the basket's woven heart, where they wouldn't shift against each other on the walk home. Small things. Careful things. Her whole life had become a collection of small careful things, arranged so precisely that no one looking at her would see anything worth a second glance.

A hedge-witch's apprentice, the village called her now that there was no hedge-witch left to apprentice to. A quiet woman who sold poultices and bitter teas, who mended bones and drew fevers, who asked fair prices and gave nothing free except advice, which nobody wanted anyway. Seven years, and Saltmere had folded her in the way valleys fold in weather—without enthusiasm, without resistance, simply accepting that she was part of what fell upon it.

They didn't know her name meant anything. They didn't know the name itself was a lie worn smooth with use.

*Ishikawa.* A stone-river name from a country she'd invented one sleepless night in her third month of running, pieced together from half-remembered trade-tongues until it sounded plausible. Maren had raised an eyebrow at it and never asked again, which was one of the thousand reasons Lena had loved her.

She straightened, pressed her palm once more against the crusted earth—a habit, a courtesy, a superstition she no longer bothered to examine—and turned toward the cottage on the valley's western shoulder.

* * *

Maren's grave sat behind the cottage, under the rowan that the old woman had planted herself forty years before, back when planting a rowan over your own future grave was either confidence or a joke. Lena had never decided which. The headstone was fieldstone, unmarked; Maren had forbidden words. *Names are handles,* she'd said, near the end, when her hands shook too badly to grind her own valerian and Lena did it for her morning and night. *Anyone who can read a name can pull it. Leave mine blank and let me be heavy.*

So the stone stayed blank, and every morning Lena pulled the weeds around it by hand rather than with charm or tool, because Maren had also said—less often, quieter—that tending a grave with your bare hands was the last honest magic left in the world.

The frost had come in the night. It silvered the grass in a wide ring around the stone, and Lena crouched at its edge and worked with numb fingers, clearing the dead nettle and the creeping sorrel, loosening the soil so the worms could do their slow work. Her breath hung in front of her in small clouds. The rowan's berries had gone the deep red of old blood, heavy clusters bending the branches toward the grave like mourners who couldn't quite commit to kneeling.

"You'd laugh at me," she told the stone. Her voice came out rough with disuse; she spoke aloud perhaps twice a week, and mostly to him. "Fourteen stems again. You always said I counted like a miser counts coins, and I always said a miser dies rich, and you always said—" she paused, pulling a stubborn root free, "—you said a miser dies *safe*, and safe is its own kind of poor. I know. I remember."

The stone said nothing. The rowan creaked in the wind off the passes.

Seven years ago she had come over those same passes with burned feet and a face she'd altered with the last working she ever allowed herself—the one indulgence, the one necessity—and Maren had found her collapsed at the salt-line like something the mountain had spat out. She remembered almost nothing of that first week. Fever, mostly. And Maren's voice, threading through it, dry as the old woman's herb drawers: *You're lucky, girl. Most things that cross this line arrive empty. You arrived merely broken.*

She hadn't understood then what Maren meant by *empty*. She would learn. She had seen the hollowed ones since, once, from a distance, on a caravan road two valleys east—a line of men and women walking in perfect unison, eyes like unswept hearths, carrying loads no living spine should bear. She had looked at them and felt the fever-memory rise in her throat: hands reaching into her chest, not through flesh but through something finer, taking hold of the bright thing at the center of her and *pulling*—

She had run. She had crossed the salt-line, and the salt had closed behind her like water over a drowned coin, and whatever reached after her had found nothing to grip. In Saltmere, magic died. That was the whole of its mercy. The wards Maren maintained were crude, folk-charm things—iron nails at the thresholds, hagstones in the windows, a chalked spiral above every door—but the true ward was the valley itself, the ancient brine soaked through its bones, deadening everything that tried to work here. Nothing could find her in Saltmere. Nothing could *touch* her.

Nothing could touch anyone. That was the price, and the villagers paid it gladly without knowing they paid it. Their prayers went unanswered here. Their charms curdled. Their wise-women were reduced to herbs and bone-setting and common sense. Saltmere was a valley of the magically deaf, and it had been deaf so long that most of its people had forgotten sound was possible.

Lena had never forgotten. Every morning at Maren's grave she remembered, deliberately, the way you press a tongue against an aching tooth—to confirm the hurt is real, and therefore the escape is real, and therefore the fear that drives both is justified.

Contentment built on fear. She had made her peace with the architecture of it.

"Two years since you taught me the fever-bark trick," she said, sitting back on her heels, surveying the clean circle of earth. "I used it eleven times last winter. Nobody died. You'd say that's a low bar. You'd be right."

She laid three of the fourteen thistle stems across the stone—Maren's tithe, the first-cut offering, another ritual whose origin neither of them had ever explained aloud—and gathered her basket, and went inside.

* * *

The cottage was Maren's cottage, down to the iron nail driven into the lintel and the hagstone hanging in the east window, its natural hole framing a slice of whitening sky. Lena lit the stove, hung the kettle, and began the morning's accounting.

This was the other ritual, the private one, performed at the scarred oak table with the ledger open and the lamp trimmed low. Not magic—Maren had been firm about that. Arithmetic. *Counting is the mother-art,* she'd said. *Every charm is just arithmetic wearing a costume. Count honestly and the costume doesn't matter.*

Lena counted honestly.

Salt-thistle: fourteen stems, twelve after the tithe. Enough for six doses of the nerve-ease tea, or four of the lung-wash if she stretched the sap with barley-water, which she would, because winter was coming and stretched medicine beat no medicine.

Dried yarrow: one jar, half full. Comfrey root: two bundles, going soft at the ends—use them first. Beeswax: one cake, hoarded. Honey: three crocks from the summer's barter, one already promised to Tam Voss's wife for her cough, which everyone in the village knew was grief wearing a cough's clothing, though no one said so.

Coin: eleven coppers and a clipped silver bit that might have been a full silver once, in some other valley, in some other life.

And beneath the false bottom of the ledger box—the bottom Maren had built and shown her only in the last month of her life—there were the other stores. The ones that weren't stores at all. A vial of grave-dirt from outside the valley, carried in at terrible risk, for workings that must never be done here. A length of red thread. A knife with a name scratched out of the blade. And papers. Always the papers.

Lena did not open the false bottom. She never opened it. She only rested her hand flat on the ledger box for a moment, the way she rested her hand on the earth at the graveside, acknowledging something buried and best left so.

Eleven coppers. Three crocks of honey. Fourteen stems, minus one.

It was a poor kingdom, hers. It was also, she reminded herself, pouring the kettle's first steam over the day's thistle, a *safe* one. Safe was its own kind of poor. She had chosen poor. She would choose it again tomorrow, and the day after, all the way down the long grey corridor of her life, because the alternative was the thing with hands, the bright thing torn out, the caravan roads lined with the hollow-eyed.

Outside, the light had finally crested the eastern fells, and the salterns caught fire with it, every white crust blazing pink and gold. Beautiful, in its barren way. The valley that forgot. She had made her home in forgetting, and forgetting had been kind to her.

She drank her tea. She washed her cup. She opened the shutters and set out her signboard—the modest painted board reading *POULTICES & PHYSICK, REASONABLE RATES*—and prepared to be invisible for another day.

* * *

The first snow came early that year.

Lena noticed it mid-afternoon, between patients—one of the Corrigan boys with a splinter gone septic in his thumb, old Widow Hale with her knees announcing weather. The light through the east window changed, went strange and thick, and when she glanced up from binding the boy's thumb, the hagstone's hole framed not sky but falling white, fat unhurried flakes descending with the calm of something that intended to stay.

"Early," Widow Hale said, following her gaze. She said it the way villagers said everything about the weather: as accusation. "Snow before the solstice markets. Mark my girl's word, it'll be a hard one."

"Probably," Lena agreed, tying off the bandage. Hard winters were good for business and bad for everything else. She took the widow's payment in eggs and saw them both out, and stood a moment in her doorway watching the snow come down over the salterns, erasing the valley's edges one flake at a time.

There was a feeling she got sometimes, in weather like this—a prickle at the base of her skull, old instinct stirring like a dog lifting its head. Seven years of safety had not entirely atrophied it. She stood very still and let the feeling speak.

It said: *the air is wrong.*

Not dangerous-wrong. Not the electric reek of a working done nearby—she hadn't smelled that in seven years, and Saltmere's salt made it impossible besides. Just... wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved one small object and hasn't yet been caught. The snow fell too evenly. The silence lay too smoothly. Somewhere at the edge of perception, the valley's long deafness seemed to have developed a faint ringing in its ears.

Lena told herself it was the season turning. She banked the stove, brought in the signboard, chalked a fresh spiral above the door—reflex, ritual, respect for a dead woman's rules—and ate her supper of barley and salt-fish standing at the window, watching the white world erase itself.

By full dark the snow had settled into a foot of new silence, and the cold had sharpened, and the lane below the cottage lay unbroken in both directions, a clean page.

She was banking the lamp when she heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. The valley didn't permit much in the way of sound after dark. But beneath the wind's thin whine, beneath the tick of the cooling stove, there came a rhythm—small, regular, unmistakable once she'd identified it.

Footsteps. Bare feet, by their softness, pressing through fresh snow. Coming up the lane. Toward her door.

Lena stood in the dark of her kitchen with her hand flat against the ledger box, and the prickle at the base of her skull became a cold wire drawn tight, and she thought, absurdly, of Maren's voice: *Most things that cross this line arrive empty.*

The footsteps stopped outside.

Then came the knock—three small taps, polite as a bird pecking glass—and beneath the door, through the gap where the lamplight spilled, the shadow of a child's feet, bare and blue in the snow, waiting.
