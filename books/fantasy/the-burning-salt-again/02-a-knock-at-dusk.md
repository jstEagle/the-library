## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The first snow of the year came down like a held breath finally released—slow, fat flakes that seemed to consider their landing before committing to it. Lena watched them from her kitchen window while the kettle muttered on the stove, and she thought, as she always did on first snow, *Now they'll stop looking. Now the valley will close over me like water.*

Seven years had taught her the arithmetic of safety. Saltmere forgot things; that was its nature and its mercy. The salt in the earth ate magic the way rust ate iron, and what the salt ate, the valley's memory could not hold. A woman who arrived with no history and made none would, in time, become furniture—a shape in the landscape, unremarked and unremarkable. She had counted on that. She had built her life out of it, plank by careful plank.

She was counting stores when the knock came.

Not a knock, precisely. Three soft taps, spaced wrong—the rhythm of someone who had heard knocking described but never done it. Lena's hand stopped over the jar of dried salt-thistle, three fingers deep, enough to get through midwinter if she was frugal. Nobody knocked on her door. In seven years, nobody had knocked on her door more than four times, and two of those had been Tam Voss come to tell her the millrace had flooded, and one had been a boy running from a wasp.

The fourth time had been Maren, the winter before she died, standing in the snow with her shawl pinned wrong and an expression Lena had never learned to read before the chance closed forever.

Lena did not move toward the door. That was the first rule, though she'd never written it down: *when something comes to you in the dark, let it wait.* Things that waited were things that could be studied. Things that couldn't wait were things that wanted something from you too badly to be safe.

The taps came again. Same wrong rhythm. Then silence, and beneath the silence, a sound that raised every hair on Lena's arms: humming.

It was a melody she didn't know, and yet it slid into her chest like a key into an old lock. It was built on intervals that human throats shouldn't favor—too many half-steps, a rise where a fall belonged—and it moved the way water moves under ice, patient and certain of its destination. On the shelf by the window, her salt lamps guttered. Both of them. At once. In a sealed room, with no draft, in a house whose every window and sill was lined with salt against exactly this.

The flames bent sideways, toward the door, as if leaning in to listen.

Lena set down the jar. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; seven years ago they would not have been. Fear, she had learned, was like salt-thistle—you could harvest it, dry it, store it, and draw on measured portions when needed. She took a portion now. She took the iron poker from beside the stove, because iron was honest even when nothing else was, and she went to the door.

*Let it wait,* she reminded herself, and then opened it anyway, because the second thing seven years had taught her was that rules kept you alive but curiosity kept you human, and she had spent too long being only alive.

* * *

The child stood on her step like something the snow had decided to grow.

She was small—six years old, perhaps seven, though there was something in her stillness that made age hard to guess. Barefoot. That was the detail that struck first and refused to leave: bare feet in fresh snow, on a night cold enough to crack buckets left outside, and the girl stood with her toes slightly curled in the white as if it were summer grass. No frost on her skin. No shivering. Snow gathered on her dark hair and on the shoulders of a dress that might once have been fine—good linen, embroidered at the collar with a pattern Lena's eyes kept sliding off of—and the snow simply lay there, unmelting, as though the child ran no warmer than the air around her.

Her face was ordinary. Round-cheeked, snub-nosed, chapped at the lips. A valley child's face, or any child's face. Except for the eyes, which were the gray of old dishwater and looked through Lena rather than at her, focused on some point behind her sternum.

And she was humming. The melody came out of her small body without moving her mouth much, without seeming to cost her breath, and this close it was worse than it had been through the door. Lena felt it in her teeth. Behind her, both salt lamps guttered again, and one of them—the little clay one Maren had fired herself, thirty years ago, the one that had never once gone out in Lena's keeping—shrank to a blue bead of flame no bigger than a peppercorn.

"Stop," Lena said. Not unkindly. The way you'd say it to a horse about to bolt. "Whatever you're doing—stop."

The humming stopped. The lamps steadied. The blue bead swelled back to a proper flame, and Lena exhaled a breath she hadn't known she was rationing.

The child looked up at her then, and for a moment the dishwater eyes sharpened into something present, something *there*, and Lena saw in them a fear so old and so worn smooth that it had stopped looking like fear and started looking like weather. This was a child who had been afraid for a very long time, long enough that terror had become the ground she stood on.

Then the girl's gaze dropped to the poker in Lena's hand, and something almost like politeness crossed her face—an apology, nearly—and Lena felt the strangest urge to throw the iron into the yard.

"Come inside," she heard herself say, and hated how easily it came. Seven years of invisibility, undone by one pair of bare feet. But the alternative was closing the door, and closing the door meant lying awake all night listening to the snow, wondering, and wonder was a debt that always came due with interest. Maren had taught her that too, though Maren had phrased it better: *A question you don't answer will answer itself, and it won't ask politely.*

The child stepped over the threshold without touching the frame. Lena noticed that. Later, turning it over in the dark hours, she would notice it again: the girl had passed through her doorway the way smoke passes through a window, avoiding the wood entirely, as if the doorframe were something she'd been taught—or made—to step around.

* * *

Inside, the domesticity of Lena's life reasserted itself with the stubbornness of all domesticity. There was soup on the stove. There was bread wrapped in cloth. There was a chair pulled up to the table, and after a moment's hesitation the child sat in it, feet dangling, hands folded, watching Lena move around the kitchen with the fixed attention of a cat watching birds through glass.

Lena put soup in front of her. The girl ate—thoroughly, methodically, with the mechanical completeness of someone refueling rather than enjoying—and Lena sat across from her and studied her and felt the dread rising in her like groundwater.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been circling since the doorway, refusing to name, naming now in the privacy of her own skull while the child spooned up broth:

Magic did not work in Saltmere.

That was not a boast or a belief. It was geology. The whole valley sat on a spine of salt older than the kingdoms, older than the pre-kingdom tongues, and salt drank power the way sand drank rain. Witches who came through the pass found their charms dead in their pockets within a day. The nearest working hedge-magic lived a week's ride east, and everyone knew it, and that was why Saltmere existed at all: a pocket of quiet in a loud world, a place where a woman could be only a woman. Lena had chosen it the way a drowning swimmer chooses a rock. She had not questioned the rock. You did not question the rock.

And yet. The lamps had guttered. The melody had moved them. And the child sat in her kitchen untouched by cold, untouched by frost, wearing snow that wouldn't melt, and every one of those facts was impossible in the way that a fire burning underwater is impossible—not subtly wrong, not arguable, but *fundamentally* broken, a hole punched in the floor of the world.

Magic had crossed the line. Magic was sitting at her table eating her soup.

"Where did you come from?" Lena asked. "Who sent you?"

The girl looked up. Her mouth worked—Lena watched her try, genuinely try, lips shaping around sounds that collapsed before they became words. What emerged was a soft cluster of syllables, melodic, structured, utterly meaningless. Not the valley dialect. Not lowland trade-speech, which Lena spoke well enough to bargain in. Not the high-tongue of the eastern courts, which Maren had drilled into her until it lived in her back teeth. Not anything. The sounds had grammar—she could hear the grammar, the way phrases rose and fell with intent—but the words themselves belonged to no language Lena had ever heard spoken or read or dreamed.

The child tried again, harder, and this time frustration cracked through her stillness. Her small fists pressed flat against the table. She made a sound halfway between a word and a sob, and the salt lamp nearest the window flickered in sympathy, and Lena said, quickly, "It's all right. It's all right. Don't—" *Don't do whatever that is,* she didn't say. "You don't have to talk."

The girl stilled. Looked at her. And then, deliberately, she reached across the table and took hold of the little dish of coarse salt Lena kept for seasoning, and turned it over.

Salt spilled across the wood in a white heap. The child regarded it for a moment with her dishwater eyes, and then her finger moved—quick, sure, unhesitating, the way a scribe moves, the way someone writes who has written ten thousand times—and drew a spiral.

Not a scribble. Not a child's sun or snail. A spiral drawn in a single unbroken stroke, tightening inward through five exact turns to a point at its center, each curve equidistant from the last, precise as something compass-drawn. And as her finger lifted away, the spiral *moved*. Just barely. Just at the edge of seeing. The innermost curl tightened a fraction further, like a pupil contracting, and then lay still, and the lamp flames leaned toward it, all three of them, gentle as flowers toward sun.

Lena did not breathe for a long moment.

"What is that," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. Questions go up at the end. Hers went down, flat and heavy, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant and finding their name spelled correctly.

The child pointed at the spiral. Then she touched her own chest, just below the collarbone, where the embroidery pattern writhed its unreadable pattern. Then she pointed at the spiral again.

*This is me,* she was saying. Or: *This is what I am.* Or: *This is what was taken.*

Lena stood up so fast her chair skidded. She crossed to the window, to the dark glass, and caught her own reflection ghosted over the falling snow—hollow-eyed, lit yellow by lamplight, a woman shaped like a question—and she made herself stand there until her hands stopped wanting to shake.

There was a box under her bed. Had been under her bed for seven years, carried out of Maren's cottage the night after the burial, along with the ledgers and the good knife and the seed tin. Maren's papers. Maren's buried business, literal and otherwise, which Lena had sorted once, read twice, and then sealed with wax and salt and the firm decision that some inheritances were better left in the ground next to their owners. Maren had taught her everything worth knowing about surviving, and nothing at all about what Maren herself had survived, and Lena had loved her enough not to dig.

But the spiral was still turning on her table, faintly, patiently, and the child was watching her with eyes that had seen the far side of something terrible, and the snow was coming down like a curtain between Saltmere and everywhere else, and Lena understood—with the particular clarity that arrives only when it is least welcome—that the past had not stayed buried after all. It had merely been waiting for a messenger it could trust.

Barefoot. Through the pass. Through the wards Maren had woven into the valley's very soil, wards that had held for two hundred years against everything with teeth.

The girl hadn't gotten lost on her way to Lena's door. Nothing got lost in Saltmere unless the valley permitted it.

Lena turned from the window. The child was still watching her, hands folded, snow melting off her hair at last now that she sat near the stove—melting, Lena noted, only now, only indoors, only since the humming stopped, as if the cold on her had been a garment rather than a temperature.

"I don't know what you are," Lena told her. It felt important to say aloud, to establish the terms honestly, the way you'd state terms to any stranger at your table. "I don't know who sold you or sent you or what wants you. But you're not going back out into that tonight."

Something moved behind the dishwater eyes. Not gratitude—gratitude was too simple, too warm. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing a sentence in a language they'd forgotten they knew.

Lena banked the fire, fetched the quilt from the chest, and laid it pallet-style on the floor beside the stove, where the warmth pooled thickest. The child lay down without being asked, curled small, and was asleep between one breath and the next—too fast, too completely, sleep taking her like a trapdoor opening.

Lena sat at the table a long while, the poker across her knees, watching the girl breathe and the spiral lie still in its spilled salt and the lamps burn steady, steady, steady.

Then, very carefully, she swept the salt with the spiral intact onto a square of clean linen, folded it shut like a letter, and tucked it into her apron pocket. Tomorrow she would open Maren's box. Tomorrow she would dig where she had sworn not to dig.

Tonight she sat watch over a sleeping impossibility in a valley that forgot everything, and understood, with dread settling into her bones like the cold under the door, that some things arrive precisely because you have hidden well—that hiding is a scent, and somewhere, something had followed it home.

In her sleep, the child began to hum again, very softly, and in the ledger of the world—though Lena could not know this yet—a new line was already drying.
