## 4. The Language Before Names

*Cold.*

That is the first word, though it is not a word. It is a temperature that has learned to think.

The girl is small inside herself. She knows this the way you know the shape of a room with your eyes shut: by the way her own edges do not reach the walls of her. There is a hollow where something used to live, and the hollow is shaped exactly like her, and she fits into it too well, the way a hand fits a glove that has been worn by someone else first.

She is walking. She does not remember beginning to walk. Her feet are bare and the ground is white and each step makes a sound like a held breath being let go very slowly — *hssss* — and the sound is wrong, because snow should crunch, and this does not crunch, it *sings*, low and mineral, and some part of her that is still hers says: *salt, this is salt, salt is safe, salt is—*

Safe for whom?

The question arrives from outside. It arrives the way cold arrives under a door.

* * *

There was a hall once. There is a hall now. The two are the same hall; only the light has changed.

It runs longer than any hall should, its ceiling lost in dark like the underside of a frozen lake. The floor is black stone veined with white, and if you look at the veins too long they resolve into writing, and if you read the writing you have already agreed to something, so the girl looks at her feet instead, at her bare blue toes on the black stone, and counts them. One two three four five. One two three four five. Counting is a wall. Counting is what her mother taught her before the before, back when there was a before, back when names were still warm things you could hold.

Her mother's hands. Flour on them. A kitchen with a crooked window. *Say it again, little bird.* And the girl said it, whatever it was, her name, her true name, the one from before kingdoms, the one that sounds less like a word and more like water finding its way downhill —

And then hands. Not her mother's. Many hands, or one hand many-jointed, cool as coins left out overnight, and they had reached into her chest without breaking anything, without even parting the skin, and taken the bright thing.

She remembers the bright thing better than she remembers her mother's face.

It was not large. It was about the size of a plum, or a heart, or a word you love. It was warm the way a stone is warm after a whole day of sun. When they lifted it out — and it did not hurt, that was the horror of it, it did not hurt at all, it felt like setting down a heavy basket after a long road — when they lifted it out, the hall got quieter. Not silent. Quieter. As if one voice among ten thousand had stopped singing, and all the other voices noticed, and leaned toward the silence the way flowers lean toward light.

They put it in a book.

Not beside the book. *In* it. The pages opened like a mouth and the bright thing went in and the pages closed and somewhere far away a pen scratched, scratched, scratched, writing down a number, and the number was her, and the number was smaller than she thought it would be.

* * *

Warmth now. A strange warmth, orange-colored, smelling of smoke and of something green and bitter — herbs, an old woman's cupboard smell — and the girl surfaces toward it like a swimmer toward light.

A room. Low beams. A fire. A woman with dark hair going gray at the temples, kneeling, holding a lamp close, and the lamp is guttering, guttering, because the girl cannot stop humming, the melody pours out of her on its own, the court's melody, the one the ledger sings when it balances itself at night.

*Stop*, she tells her own throat. *Stop, you'll be found.*

But the throat is not hers anymore. Not entirely. Things that are sold keep singing for their buyers. That is what a vessel is. That is what she is: a jar shaped like a girl, waiting to be filled, and until she is filled she echoes whatever comes near.

The woman is saying words. Living words, kingdom words, soft and rough at the edges. The girl understands none of them and all of them, the way you understand rain without knowing its language. The woman's face is doing something complicated — fear moving under kindness like a fish under ice.

The girl wants to say: *I had a mother. I had a name. There was flour on her hands.*

What comes out instead is the melody, lower now, almost a lullaby, and the lamp flame lies down flat as if a great weight has passed over it.

* * *

In the hall beneath the mountain, the ledger breathes.

This is not a thing the clerks admit. But the girl knows it, because part of her is still in the ledger, pressed flat between pages 4,011 and 4,012, and from there you can feel the whole book inhale when a debt comes due. You can feel the other bright things around you, hundreds of them, thousands, each one humming its owner's name in a voice too small to hear — except that here, in the dark between pages, small voices are the only kind there are.

Some of the bright things have been in the book so long they have forgotten they were ever anything else. They hum contentedly. They have become entries.

The girl's bright thing has not forgotten. The girl's bright thing presses against the page that holds it and thinks *flour, crooked window, little bird,* over and over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

And sometimes — this is the secret she will never be able to tell, not in any language, living or dead — sometimes the bright thing is not lonely. Sometimes the hall is beautiful. The cold is clean, like the inside of a bell. The veined writing on the floor glows faintly gold when the sovereign passes, and the sovereign is made of every soul it holds, and when it moves it moves like weather, like a change coming over a landscape, and there is a terrible grandeur in it, and the bright thing feels itself leaning toward that grandeur the way the voices in the hall lean toward silence.

That is the trap no one warns you about. The hollow does not only ache. The hollow also *fits*. Whatever fills it becomes home, because home is just the place that shapes you.

* * *

Snow. The woman's floor. Salt spilled from a fallen dish, and the girl's finger moving through it before she decides to move it, drawing the spiral, the sigil, the mark that was cut into her understanding the moment the bright thing left — the mark that means *property of*, the mark that means *return address*.

The spiral is the court's oldest word. Older than kingdoms. In the language before names, the spiral meant *that which gathers*, the way water gathers, the way debts gather, the way souls gather in a book. Drawn small, it was a prayer. Drawn large, it was a claim.

The girl draws it small. She is trying to make it a prayer.

The woman goes very still above her, stiller than still, and the girl thinks: *she recognizes it. Good. Good. Someone who recognizes it can read it, and someone who can read it can find the hall, and someone who finds the hall—*

Can what? Buy it back? Steal it? The bright thing in the ledger hums hopefully, foolishly, and the girl hates it a little for hoping, and loves it completely for hoping, because it is the only part of her that still remembers how.

* * *

Fragment: a winter kitchen. Flour. Her mother's voice saying a word that is not a word, a rising-falling sound like a bird's wing beating twice. *Little bird. Little bird. Hold my hands and say it back.*

Fragment: men at the door with a contract and a lantern that burned green. Her father's face doing arithmetic. Her father's face losing the arithmetic. Debt is a language too, and it is spoken fluently in villages, and it always ends the same way: *something of value.* A cow. A roof-beam. A daughter.

Fragment: the road north. Snow. Her mother running behind the cart, not shouting, worse than shouting, singing — singing the name over and over so the girl would carry it, so it would be sewn into her somewhere the hands could not reach.

But the hands reached everywhere. The hands were patient. The hands had been buying things since before there were kingdoms to buy them from.

Fragment: the taking. No pain. The quiet afterward, vast as a field under snow. The clerk — a gray person, genderless, kind in the way a scale is kind, perfectly balanced, perfectly indifferent — the clerk saying, in the language before names, in her own mother-tongue, the words for *thank you* and *it is done* and *you may go.*

*You may go.* As if there were anywhere left to go, with the middle of yourself lying flat in a book.

* * *

Now: the woman's hearth. Night pressing at the windows. The girl sits wrapped in a blanket that smells of lavender and old wool, and watches the woman move through the small rituals of a small life — banking the fire, setting water to warm, checking the locks on a door that locks against nothing, because the danger did not come through the door. The danger came through the girl. The danger *is* the girl, or rides in her like a rider in a cart.

The woman speaks to her gently, kingdom-words, and the girl lets them wash over her, and under the washing she keeps her grip on three things:

One: flour on her mother's hands.

Two: the spiral, drawn in salt, which is a claim but can be a prayer.

Three: the melody, which she must stop humming, which she cannot stop humming, which is getting louder tonight, because somewhere north of here the ledger has turned to her page and begun, softly, patiently, to sing it back.

Come home, sings the ledger. Come home, little entry. Winter is coming, and winter is our season, and on the longest night the book opens widest, and what is written must return to be read aloud.

The girl pulls the blanket tighter. She looks at the woman — Lena, the house itself whispers, the salt in the floorboards whispers, *Lena Ishikawa, seven years hidden, seven years safe* — and the girl reaches out with the only limb she has left, the hollow itself, and touches the woman's attention the way you touch a sleeping animal: carefully, so as not to startle it.

*Help me*, says the hollow, in no language at all.

And far beneath the mountain, between pages 4,011 and 4,012, a small bright thing begins — very softly, very stubbornly, in the language before names — to sing back.
