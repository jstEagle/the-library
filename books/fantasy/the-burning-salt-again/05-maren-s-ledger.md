## 5. Maren's Ledger

The child slept in the loft above the hearth, curled in Maren's old quilt like a comma in a sentence that hadn't been finished yet. Lena had watched her breathe for an hour before she let herself do anything else.

She told herself it was caution. It was not entirely caution. There was something in watching a sleeping child that loosened a knot she had carried for seven years—the knot of being alone in a valley that forgot her on purpose—and she resented the loosening even as she leaned into it. Fear was simpler when you were the only thing at stake.

But the girl breathed, and the salt lamps held their small steady flames, and outside the first snow kept falling with the softness of something deciding whether to become a storm. Lena banked the fire, set water to warm for morning, and went to the floorboards beneath her bed.

She had not opened them since the funeral.

Seven years ago she had knelt where she was kneeling now, in this same cold square of lamplight, and pried up the board Maren had tapped twice with her cane every night before bed—a habit Lena had thought was superstition, then arthritis, then grief. Underneath: a cedar box, iron-banded, locked with a key that had arrived by crow three days after Maren died, as if the bird had been waiting all along for permission. Inside the box, papers. She had read none of them. She had carried the box down into the root cellar, set it behind the salt barrels where no magic could reach it, and put the floorboard back, and that had been that. Grief was a country she could not afford to live in. She had a garden to keep. She had wards to renew. She had herself to hide.

Now there was a child under her roof whose soul had been sold like a sack of barley, and the only person who might have explained how such a thing was possible was seven years dead, and Lena was done pretending the dead stayed buried just because you asked them nicely.

The box came up smelling of cedar and earth and, faintly, of Maren—of rosemary oil and woodsmoke and the particular bitterness of willow bark chewed through long winters. Lena sat back on her heels with it in her lap and did not open it for a long moment. The fire ticked. Snow whispered against the shutters. Somewhere upstairs the child turned over in her sleep, and the sound of it—small, human, unguarded—was what finally made Lena's hands move.

The lock took the crow's key without complaint, which felt like a rebuke.

* * *

The top layer was ordinary. That was the first cruelty of it.

Seed accounts. Tithes owed to the valley council. A running tally of eggs traded to Tam Voss's household going back eleven years, each entry in Maren's cramped, upright hand, each figure exact to the half-egg. Recipes for poultices. A list of births and deaths in Saltmere kept more faithfully than the village kept its own. Lena read these with a smile that hurt her face, because it was so completely the woman she had loved: Maren Osgood, who could raise a storm-wind with a word but who recorded that Widow Prue owed two eggs and a knob of butter against midsummer, and who never once forgave the butter.

Lena turned the pages. The handwriting changed.

It changed gradually, the way weather changes—she was three pages deep before she realized the upright hand had begun to lean, to crowd, to press hard enough in places to tear. The entries stopped being about eggs. They were about *holdings*. About instruments. About a column of figures marked only with a symbol Lena didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a line, drawn with a flourish, over and over, like a signature practiced until it became a reflex.

She knew that symbol. She had seen it last night, scratched into spilled salt by a mute child's finger.

Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, the way you note the behavior of someone else's hands. Seven years of hiding taught you to watch your own fear from a little distance, to keep it in a pen like livestock, fed and contained. The hands turned the page.

There was a ledger within the ledger. Sewn into the binding of the seed accounts, its spine hidden under pasteboard, was a slimmer book—maybe forty pages, written in cipher. Not a true cipher; Lena recognized the structure of it immediately, because Maren had taught it to her as a game when she was nineteen and homesick and missing her mother's tongue. *A hedge-witch's letters must be dull to any eye that falls on them uninvited,* Maren had said, laughing. *Boredom is the best ward ever devised.* Substitutions built on the salt-thistle planting rhyme, rotated by season. Lena had used it to pass notes about boys. Maren had used it, apparently, to keep accounts of souls.

Deciphering it took her until well past midnight. The fire burned down. The snow thickened. Twice she heard the child stir overhead and paused, listening, one hand flat on the page as if to hold it still. Both times the girl settled again. Both times Lena noticed her own heartbeat and made it slow down.

The first entries were dated thirty years back. *Received: one name, minor gentry, north road. Consideration: debt forgiveness, 40 marks. Condition: intact.* Received from whom? Sold to whom? The columns gave no names—only the circle-and-line sigil, and numbers, and a second symbol like a closed eye that appeared whenever the destination was written, as though even in cipher Maren would not put certain names on paper.

Lena read entry after entry. Names received. Names delivered. Considerations rendered. Conditions: intact, frayed, *spent*. One entry simply read *condition: gone*, and beside it, in the margin, in ordinary script breaking through the cipher like bone through skin, a single word: *forgive me.*

She did not know for whom that word was meant. She suspected, with a sickening lurch, that it might be meant for her.

Because there—at the bottom of the third gathering of pages, dated the spring of Lena's own arrival in the valley—was her name. *Lena Ishikawa.* Written out whole, not ciphered, as if Maren had wanted it legible to anyone who ever found this book. And beside it, in the consideration column, in Maren's most careful lettering:

*Collateral. Held.*

Not sold. Not spent. *Held.* As though Lena herself were a purse of coin sitting in someone else's strongroom, drawing quiet interest, waiting to be called.

She sat with that for a long time. The lamp guttered and she did not trim it. Collateral. Held. Against what? For whom? She read the entry backward and forward, hunting context, and found none—only the date, which was the week she had stumbled half-dead across the salt-line with Maren's old summons crumpled in her fist, and the notation beneath it in cipher: *payment deferred by holder's discretion. Interest accruing.*

Interest accruing. On what? On her? On the seven years she had believed herself safe?

The wind rose outside and pressed at the shutters like a large patient animal. Upstairs, the child murmured in her sleep—that pre-kingdom language flowing out of her now even unconscious, syllables older than any kingdom, older than any alphabet Lena knew—and the salt lamps bent their flames toward the sound, the way flowers bend toward light, or the way men bend toward a thing they have been told not to want.

Lena climbed the ladder and stood over the girl a while. In sleep her face lost its strangeness and became only a child's face, chapped lips, lashes furred with melting snow that hadn't fully dried. Lena pulled the quilt higher. The girl's hand came out from under it and caught Lena's wrist—not waking, not gripping, just resting there, the way a sleeping person reaches for warmth—and Lena stood very still and let her, and understood that she was already lost. Whatever this cost, whatever the ledger said she was worth, she was not sending this child back into the snow.

She went back down and finished the book.

* * *

The last entries were the worst, because they were impossible.

Maren had died in the autumn, seven years after Lena's arrival—quietly, in her chair, between one cup of tea and the next, with the satisfaction of a woman who had arranged everything. Lena had buried her on the hill above the thistle field. She had sat the death-watch alone. She had burned the rosemary and spoken the words and covered the grave with stones she carried up one at a time because Maren had once said, jokingly, that she'd trust no gravedigger's spade near her bones.

The final entries in the hidden ledger were dated *after* that autumn.

Three of them. Each bearing the circle-and-line sigil. Each recording a transaction in the same format, the same hand—or a hand so like Maren's that Lena laid the pages side by side and compared letterforms until her eyes ached, looking for the forgery in it, needing there to be a forgery in it. The *g*s matched. The crossings matched. Even the little ink-blot habit Maren had, the way her pen always stuttered at the start of a numeral—matched.

The third of the posthumous entries was dated four months ago. Four months ago Maren Osgood had been a year and a half in her grave, and her seal—her sigil, her hand, her *name*—had signed away the soul of a child.

*Received: one vessel, female, approx. six years. Origin: north road, unnamed party. Consideration: [the closed-eye symbol]. Destination: [the closed-eye symbol]. Brokerage fee: waived. Note:* —and here the cipher broke into plain script, hurried, the pen pressing through the paper— *note: seal used under compulsion of prior agreement. I am sorry. I am so sorry. There was no other way to close the account. Tell her—*

And nothing. The sentence ended. The page ended. The book ended, the remaining leaves blank, the binding worn smooth as if a thumb had worried it for years.

Lena read *tell her* perhaps twenty times. Tell her what? Tell *whom*? Her? The child? Some third woman somewhere, another student, another collateral?

The fire had gone to embers. The cold had come up through the floor and into the room like a tide, and Lena sat in it and felt the shape of her seven years rearrange itself around her, the way a landscape rearranges when fog lifts and shows you the cliff you've been walking beside.

She had thought Maren had saved her out of kindness. Out of the old fierce charity of hedge-witches, the creed of the overlooked: *no one is refuse.* She had built her whole heart on that—a contentment with fear inside it, yes, but honest fear, the ordinary fear of being hunted, not this. Not the possibility that her rescue had been a transaction. That *collateral, held* meant Maren had taken her in the way a merchant takes in goods against a debt. That the woman who taught her to ward, to plant, to count, to laugh again—that every lesson had been interest paid on a holding.

And worse—worse than that, because grief makes room for worse the way a wound makes room for infection—if Maren's seal could sign after death, then either someone had learned to forge the finest hand Lena had ever known, or Maren had bound her name into something that outlived her, some prior agreement, some *compulsion*, that let the dead keep brokering. Either way, the woman in the grave on the hill was not done transacting. Either way, Lena had spent seven years living inside a contract she had never read, and the counterparty had just taken delivery of a child.

The lamps guttered. The snow ticked against the glass. And Lena Ishikawa, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of wanting nothing, discovered that wanting nothing had been a luxury purchased with ignorance, and the bill had come due, and it was wearing a child's face.

She closed the ledger. She did not put it back under the floorboards. Some things, once opened, do not go back down.

Upstairs, the girl cried out in her sleep—one sharp note, the universal note, the note children make in nightmares in every age and every tongue—and Lena was moving before she decided to move, taking the ladder in three steps, gathering the small shaking body against her chest.

The girl's eyes opened. They were clear, and present, and terrified, and they fixed on Lena with an expression that needed no translation in any century: *you know now, don't you. You know what I am.*

"Yes," Lena said, though the girl couldn't understand the word. "I know."

The child reached up and touched Lena's cheek, and then, deliberately, traced one finger across it—a spiral, the sigil, drawn in the air an inch from her skin. Then she pointed at Lena's chest. Then at her own. Then she folded Lena's hand around the quilt and held it there, over her own sternum, over the hollow place, and looked at Lena with an urgency that was almost anger.

*Something of yours is in here too.* That was what the gesture said. Or maybe: *we are the same kind of empty.* Lena couldn't tell. Perhaps the child couldn't either.

Below them, in the cold room, the ledger lay open on the table, and the lamp flame stood perfectly straight despite the draft, and outside the snow had stopped falling all at once, the way snow does when the temperature drops past some threshold and the sky simply gives up. Silence lay on the valley like a held breath.

Lena rocked the child until she slept again. Then she dressed for the cold, and took down Maren's traveling cloak from its peg, and set the hidden ledger inside it, against her heart, where the paper could feel her pulse.

If her name was collateral, she would go and see the creditor. If Maren's seal still walked and signed, she would find what wore it. And if the answer was under the mountain—well. She had crossed the salt-line once before, half-dead, running the wrong way. She could cross it again, alive, running the right way.

At the door she paused, one hand on the latch, and looked back at the sleeping loft, at the small mound of quilt, at the salt lamps burning steady as stars.

"Keep her safe," she told the wards, the house, the valley, the dead woman on the hill—to all of them, since she no longer knew which of them deserved the request. "Whatever else is true. Keep her safe."

No one answered. But as she stepped out into the blue-black cold, the first light of false dawn caught the hill above the thistle field, and the stones on Maren's grave stood black against the snow, arranged—for one vertiginous moment, in the half-light—less like a cairn than like a closed eye, watching her go.
