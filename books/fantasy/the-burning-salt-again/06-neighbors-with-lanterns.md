## 6. Neighbors with Lanterns

Tam Voss had kept the valley's lanterns lit for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one thing above the rest: a village is a fire that must be fed, and everything you feed it eventually becomes fuel.

He was trimming wicks in the meetinghouse when Old Sef came pounding on the door with snow in his beard and terror in his voice.

"Tam. The salt-line at Miller's Ford. Come see."

Tam set down his knife. He did not ask what was wrong; in Saltmere, when a man ran instead of walked, the wrongness was already understood. He took his coat from its peg, and then—because he had been doing this long enough to know what mattered—he took the lantern too.

* * *

Miller's Ford was where the stream left the valley, cutting through the salt-ridge in a narrow throat of stone. For as long as anyone living could remember, the water had run out of Saltmere clear and dead, stripped of every whisper of magic by the miles of salt it crossed before it reached the ridge. Dead water was good water. Dead water meant the valley was working.

The water running through the ford this morning was not dead.

It glimmered. That was the only word for it—a faint, oily shimmer beneath the ice, like moonlight caught in a jar and shaken. Where the spray froze on the stones, the frost had grown in spirals, tight and perfect, whorls within whorls, the kind of pattern no frost made and no frost should want to.

Sef stood well back from the bank, arms wrapped around himself. "It started three days ago, I think. Maybe four. I told myself it was nothing." He spat, missed, spat again. "You know what I told my boy when he asked about the shine? I said it was fish eggs. Fish eggs, Tam. In midwinter."

Tam crouched at the edge of the bank and held the lantern low over the water. The light caught the spiral-frost and something in the patterns seemed to catch it back, bending it, drinking it. His hands were steady. They had been steady through the fever year and the rockfall and the night the sky over the ridge turned green, and they were steady now, but his stomach knew better than his hands. He had seen spirals like these once before, thirty-one years ago, drawn in spilled flour on a tavern table by a woman who paid for her supper with a charm and never gave her true name.

The night Lena Ishikawa came down out of the pass half-dead, with winter in her lungs and something hunted in her eyes.

"Get Brannic," Tam said. "And Wenna Hale. And tell the miller to keep his children away from the water until I say otherwise." He paused. "Don't tell them why."

"They'll ask."

"They always ask." Tam straightened, knees cracking like green wood. "Tell them the ford needs mending. A lie small enough to hold is worth more than a truth too heavy to carry."

Sef went. Tam stayed a while longer, looking at the shining water, and found himself thinking—not about wards, not about fraying lines, but about a girl barefoot in the first snow, seen once from a distance, walking beside Lena Ishikawa toward the cottage at the valley's edge. Walking as if she'd never seen ground before. As if she'd never been given any.

* * *

The council met that evening in the meetinghouse, twelve souls around the long table with the stove ticking in the corner and the lanterns hung high, because in Saltmere even arguments were conducted by lamplight. Magic slept here. Firelight kept watch.

Brannic spoke first, because Brannic always spoke first. He was the valley's smith, broad as a barn door, with a voice that filled whatever room contained him. "The ford isn't the only place. Wenna found spiral-frost on her south fence. The Hedley well tastes sweet—sweet, in a valley where nothing sweet survives unless we make it so. And old Marta swears her hens haven't laid since the snow came." He planted both fists on the table. "Something's eating our salt."

"The salt doesn't get eaten," said Wenna Hale, who taught the village letters and numbers and half its common sense. "Salt wears. It thins. It has thinned since before any of us were born—that's why we renew the line every autumn. What's different now is the direction of the wearing. It's thinning from inside." She looked around the table, and her eyes stopped, deliberately, on Tam. "From the middle outward. Like something inside the valley is pulling at it."

"Like what?" asked young Piet Hedley, who was on the council because his father had died and someone had to be.

Wenna didn't answer. She didn't have to. Tam watched the silence move around the table like a draft, touching each face, and he knew exactly which name it settled on without being spoken.

"The stranger-child," said Sef, who had never in his life met a silence he couldn't fill. "Lena Ishikawa's girl. Nobody knows where she came from. Nobody knows her name. She don't talk—can't, maybe—and she was found walking in the snow like she'd dropped out of the sky. And now the wards are failing." He spread his hands. "I'm not saying. I'm just saying what everyone's saying."

"You're saying it louder than everyone, though," Wenna observed.

"I'm saying it in here, where it can be answered. Better than out there, where it can't."

That, Tam thought, was the most dangerous wisdom Sef owned, and he wielded it more often than anyone liked. Because he was right. The talk was already out there—in the bakery queue, at the well-head, in the mutter between neighbors shoveling walks. Saltmere was a small bowl and rumor filled it to the brim faster than water.

"What do we know of the child?" Tam asked, into the quiet. "Facts only."

"She arrived eleven days ago," Wenna said. "During the first snow. Barefoot. No tracks behind her—I've heard this from three separate people who saw the yard the next morning, and none of them will swear to it publicly, but all three saw it."

"No tracks," Brannic repeated flatly. "In snow."

"She hums," said Piet, unexpectedly, and went red when they all looked at him. "My little sister heard her. Through the hedge, near Ishikawa's cottage. Said the lamps guttered while she sang. Said it was pretty, though. Pretty and sad."

"Lamps guttering," Sef muttered. "There's your answer."

"It's not an answer, it's a hen story," Wenna snapped. "A child sings, a lamp flickers, and suddenly we're burning times all over again? My grandmother told me about the burning times. I'd hoped we'd buried them deeper than that."

"We're not burning anything," Tam said, and the table went still, because Tam rarely raised his voice and had just raised it one degree, which in Tam was a shout. He let the quiet sit. Then, more gently: "We're going to do what Saltmere does. We're going to be careful, and slow, and kind, in that order. I'll speak with Lena Ishikawa myself. Alone. If there's something wrong with the child—if she's carrying something that pulls at the wards—then Lena needs to know it before we do, and she'll need help dealing with it, not torches at her door."

"And if she won't deal with it?" Brannic asked. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it—Brannic wasn't a cruel man. He was a man with a forge and a family and a valley he loved, asking the question any sensible man would ask. "If the stranger won't send the girl off, or fix whatever's broke? What then, Tam?"

Tam looked down at his hands on the table. Old hands. Lantern hands. Hands that had trimmed wicks through thirty-one winters so that the dark would stay outside where it belonged.

"Then the council will decide," he said. "As the council decides. Together."

It was the right answer. It was the answer the room needed. He felt it settle over them like a blanket, and he hated how easily it settled, how quickly twelve frightened people became one comfortable decision waiting to be made.

Because Tam remembered something the others didn't. Tam remembered the night Lena Ishikawa arrived—remembered standing in this same doorway, lantern raised against the dark, watching Maren Dole walk a half-dead stranger up from the pass with her arm around the woman's waist like she was carrying something breakable. He remembered the vote afterward, the one nobody talked about now: whether to turn the stranger out. Eleven to one, they'd wanted her gone. Maren had stood in this very room and said, *She's mine to keep, and my roof is my business,* and the valley had yielded to Maren because everyone owed Maren, and because the alternative was arguing with a hedge-witch about the boundaries of her own doorstep.

Maren was seven years in the churchyard now. There was no one left whose word outweighed the fear.

*Tonight,* Tam decided. *I'll go tonight, before the talk grows teeth.*

* * *

The walk to Lena's cottage took the better part of an hour, along the valley's eastern rim where the salt-hedges grew thick and gray and the ground crunched underfoot like old porcelain. Tam carried his lantern high. It was habit and it was signal both—in Saltmere, a man who came visiting after dark carried his light openly, so no one would mistake neighbor for night-thing. The lantern said: *I am only myself. I am only Tam.*

The cottage sat in its hollow below, windows glowing amber, smoke rising straight from the chimney in the windless cold. As he came down the path he heard it—the humming. Thin and strange, threading through the walls, a melody in no scale Tam knew, rising and falling like breath. The hairs on his forearms lifted beneath his sleeves. The flame in his lantern leaned hard sideways, though there was no wind, and steadied only when the humming stopped.

The door opened before he knocked.

Lena Ishikawa stood in the gap with a candle in one hand and a salt-spoon in the other, and she looked, Tam thought, like a woman who had been expecting wolves and was almost disappointed to find only a neighbor. Seven years had worn her from the hollow-cheeked fugitive of that first winter into something quieter and harder to read—a lean, weathered woman with gray coming in early at her temples and eyes that measured everything twice.

"Master Voss." Her gaze went to the lantern, read its message, returned to his face. "Is someone sick?"

"No one's sick."

"Then it's the ford."

Not a question. Tam felt his eyebrows climb despite himself. "News travels."

"Nothing travels in this valley except trouble and gossip, and they take the same road." She stepped back from the door. "Come in before the cold gets ideas. Mind the floor by the hearth—the girl spilled salt there and I haven't swept it. On purpose," she added, before he could ask.

Inside, the cottage was warm and close and smelled of dried thistle and tallow. The child sat on the bench by the window, wrapped in a blanket far too big for her, watching Tam with eyes so dark and so still they seemed less like a child's eyes than like wells. She didn't flinch from him. She didn't smile either. She simply watched, the way deer watch—not afraid yet, but keeping count of the distance.

"That's Nell—for now," Lena said, following his look. "She hasn't told me her real one. Or can't." She set the candle down and folded her arms. "Say what you've come to say, Master Voss. You walked an hour in the dark to say it, so it won't keep till morning."

Tam lowered himself onto the offered stool with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. He looked at the spilled salt on the floorboards—at the spirals traced in it, fine as spiderwork—and then at the girl, and then at Lena, and chose, deliberately, to begin somewhere safer than the truth.

"Do you remember your first winter here?" he asked.

Something flickered across her face. "Some of it."

"Maren brought you up from the pass on the third night of the snow. Half-frozen. I stood in the meetinghouse door with a lantern and watched you come, and I voted the next day to send you away." He said it plainly, because he had decided years ago that if he ever spoke of it, he would speak it plain. "Eleven votes to one. Maren stood up in front of us all and told us your keeping was her business and our opinions could stay in our pockets. And we let it lie, because it was Maren, and because the snow was deep, and because it was easier." He rubbed his thumb along the handle of his lantern. "I've regretted that vote for seven years, Mistress Ishikawa. Not because I was wrong to be afraid. Because I let Maren spend her credit so I wouldn't have to spend my courage."

Lena said nothing. But her arms came unfolded, slowly, and she sat down across from him, and he understood that he had just been granted something rare: her full attention, unarmored.

"Now the valley's afraid again," Tam went on. "The wards are fraying—from the inside, Wenna says, which means whatever's pulling at them is inside the line. Inside the valley. The frost grows in spirals at the ford, the well runs sweet, and every tongue in Saltmere is wagging the same two words: *the stranger.*" He nodded toward the child. "They mean her. Some of them mean you, for keeping her."

"I know what they mean." Lena's voice was level. Too level. "What do *you* mean, Master Voss?"

"I mean I'm an old man who's kept the lanterns lit a long time, and I'm telling you what I see coming, neighbor to neighbor, with no council behind me and no vote in my pocket." He leaned forward. "Send the girl away."

The words fell into the warm room like a stone into a well. The child—Nell—went very still. Lena didn't move at all.

"Send her where?" Lena asked quietly.

"Away. Out of the valley. Anywhere. Back wherever she came from."

"She came from nowhere. She arrived with no tracks behind her, same as I did, if your memory serves. Whatever has her—" Lena stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Tam watched her choose caution over confession. "Sending her out won't end this. Whatever pulled her here will still pull. It'll just pull at a child alone in the snow instead of a child behind my door."

"Then the valley breaks the pull some other way. Rites. Renewal. Burn the hedges back and lay new salt if we must—"

"With what rite? Who in Saltmere remembers rites older than the churchyard?" Lena shook her head. "Your valley has been safe so long it's forgotten *why* it's safe. You think the salt-line is a wall. Walls don't need feeding. But this one does, and something's changed the appetite, and you want to solve it by putting a child outside the wall for the wolf." Her eyes flashed, and for a moment Tam saw the other woman underneath—the one who had come down from the pass half-dead and survived it. "No."

"Mistress Ishikawa—"

"Lena."

"*Lena.*" Tam held up a hand. "I'm not the wolf. I'm the man telling you the village smells blood. There's a difference, and it matters, and I need you to hear the rest before you decide I'm your enemy." He drew a breath. "The council meets again in three days. Brannic wants action. Sef wants action. Young Piet wants whatever keeps his sister from humming the child's songs in her sleep—yes, that's happening too, and no, I don't understand it, and neither does anyone. Wenna will argue for patience, and she'll lose, because patience doesn't warm a house when the well runs sweet and the hens won't lay. In three days, maybe four, this council votes. And when it votes, it will not vote the way it voted for you. Maren's dead. Nobody owes anybody anymore. Fear is the only coin in the purse, and fear spends fast."

The fire ticked. Outside, the cold pressed at the shutters like a listener.

"So I'm giving you what nobody gave me," Tam said. "A warning with the knife showing. Send the girl away—or find a way to mend what's fraying before the vote—or when the council comes up this path, it won't be me holding the lantern, and it won't be a friendly call."

"And if I can't?" Lena's voice was soft now, and worse than angry. "If mending it means something bigger than your council can imagine? If sending her away kills her?"

Tam looked at the child. Nell looked back, unblinking, and in the depths of those dark eyes he saw—or dreamed he saw, and would spend many nights deciding which—a flicker of light moving, like a lamp carried down a long corridor, far away and getting farther.

"Then God help us all," he said, "because I don't have a third answer. I only have the hour." He rose, knees protesting, and lifted his lantern. "Three days, Lena. Spend them well."

He let himself out into the cold. Behind him he heard the door close, gentle as a held breath, and then, faintly, begin again—the humming, rising through the walls, through the snow, through the salt-hedges, a melody from before kingdoms, and Tam Voss stood a long moment on the path with his lantern shaking in his grip before he made himself walk home.

Halfway back, he stopped. Turned. Looked north, past the black shoulder of the ridge, to where the mountain stood against the stars with its crown of cloud.

The clouds over the peak were turning in spirals.

Tam stood in the frozen road, an old man with a lantern and a debt of his own, and understood that three days was a number someone else had chosen—and that the choosing had begun long before the child ever reached the valley's edge.
