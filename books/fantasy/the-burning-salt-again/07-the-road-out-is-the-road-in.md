## 7. The Road Out Is the Road In

Lena packed the way she did everything: quietly, in order, twice.

The cottage had taught her its own liturgy over seven years. Three turns of the key in the door, though there was no lock worth the name. A pinch of salt at each sill. The kettle emptied and hung upside down so no spirit could nest in the water's memory. She performed the rituals now with a strange double vision—half of her hands moving through the motions, half of her watching from somewhere above, already grieving them.

She was leaving. The thought still didn't fit inside her.

She packed bread wrapped in waxed cloth, a jar of rendered tallow, the good knife with the antler handle. She packed Maren's papers—all of them, even the ones she hadn't deciphered, because she no longer trusted what she knew and what she didn't. And she packed the ledger page with her own name on it, *collateral, held*, folded into an oilcloth square that she tucked against her ribs like a second heart.

The child sat on the doorstep, watching the snow come down in fat, unhurried flakes. She had found one of Lena's old shawls and wound it around herself three times, so that only her face showed: dark eyes, chapped lips, the patient stillness of something waiting to be collected.

"We're going north," Lena said.

The girl tilted her head.

"To the mountain." Lena crouched to her level, breath fogging between them. "I don't know if I can fix this. I don't know what they did to you or how to undo it. But I know who brokered the sale, and I know where the debt lives, and I know—" she stopped, because the next part was true and terrible—"I know I can't stay here and let you hollow out while I count my jars."

The child reached into the fold of the shawl and produced something: a spiral drawn in salt on a flat stone, precise as engraving. She held it up. Then she turned it over. On the back, scratched clumsily, was a second mark—a crooked line, like a road.

Lena's throat tightened. "You understand me."

The girl pressed the stone into Lena's palm and closed Lena's fingers over it, both her small hands wrapped around Lena's fist, and nodded once.

* * *

They left before dawn, taking the shepherd's track behind the cottage rather than the village road. Lena had spent seven years learning which paths were watched and which were merely walked, and she chose now as she had always chosen: invisibly. Through the blackthorn stand where the thorns grew thick enough to comb wool. Along the dry streambed where the stones kept no footprints. Past Maren's grave, where she stopped only long enough to press her bare hand flat against the frozen ground.

"I'm sorry," she said. "And I think you knew I'd have to go."

The grave said nothing. Graves in Saltmere never did; that was rather the point of the place.

By midmorning the village was behind them, and by noon the valley had narrowed to a stony throat between two shoulders of hill, and Lena allowed herself—for the first time in seven years—to look back.

Saltmere lay below in the snow like a spilled handful of bone beads. Smoke from a dozen chimneys. The salt-crusted flats gleaming white-pink where the thistle grew. Her whole world, small enough to hold in one eye.

She had told herself for years that she loved it here. Standing above it, she understood at last that she had loved it the way a prisoner loves a cell with a comfortable chair. It had been *enough*. It had been safe. And safety, she saw now, was just fear with good posture.

"Come on," she said hoarsely, and turned north.

* * *

The salt-line was not marked. That was the first thing people got wrong about Saltmere, the few travelers who ever asked. There was no wall, no standing stones, no warning post. The boundary was simply a place where the earth changed its mind—a seam of grey rock veined with white, running across the track like an old scar, easy to step over without knowing.

Maren had made her memorize it anyway. *You'll feel it*, she'd said. *Like walking into cold water. Don't fight it. Let it close over your head.*

Lena stood before the seam with the child's hand in hers and felt, absurdly, like a woman about to dive off a cliff she'd been politely declining to notice for seven years.

Beyond the line, the world would remember magic. Beyond the line, the world would remember *her*—everything she'd buried, everything she'd starved, every trick and talent she'd amputated to become small enough to hide. She had spent seven years being a hedge-witch of no particular gift, and she had told herself so many lies about what that cost that she'd nearly believed them.

The child tugged her hand. Not forward. *Back.* The girl looked up at her with those dark eyes and shook her head slowly, and Lena realized with a chill that the vessel knew what waited beyond the seam better than she did. The mountain was calling her home. Every step north was a step toward the thing that had bought her.

"I know," Lena said. "I'm scared too. We're going anyway."

She stepped over the line.

* * *

It hit her like drowning in reverse.

The world came *on*. Color first—the snow suddenly blue-shadowed, the grey rock suddenly loud with veins of green and gold that no eye alone should see. Then sound: the wind carrying voices in it, old ones, argumentative, the mutter of the land itself remembering its own names. Then weight—she staggered, gasping, one hand on the cold stone, because the air had thickness now, texture, current. Magic moved through the world beyond the salt-line the way blood moves through a body, and she had stepped directly into the stream after seven years of standing on the bank.

And then it found *her*.

It started in her hands. Warmth, then heat, then a low singing ache, as if someone had taken bands of iron off wrists she'd forgotten were bound. Power rose through her like water finding an old channel—fast, greedy, filling shapes that had been carved long ago and left empty. She could feel the salt-thistle growing miles behind her, each root a small pale flame. She could feel the child beside her, a hollow shape ringed in borrowed light, humming. She could feel the seam under her feet, the whole boundary of the valley, vast and patient as a sleeping animal—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a signature. A working laid down generations ago, woven from something older than spellcraft.

Blood. The wards of Saltmere were written in blood, and some deep instinct told her whose.

"Lena." The word arrived in her own voice, and she realized she'd said it aloud, testing whether she was still herself.

The child was staring at her. Not afraid—or no, afraid, but the way you're afraid of a storm you've been praying for. The girl reached up and touched Lena's hand, and where her fingers met skin, sparks crawled, tiny blue-white insects of light, and neither of them flinched.

"Oh," Lena breathed. The vertigo was enormous. The world tilted and righted and tilted again, and underneath the nausea was something worse and better: *exhilaration*. Seven years of famine ending in one swallow. She could feel charms assembling themselves behind her eyes unasked—wards, bindings, little knives of intent. Skills she had not practiced since she was nineteen years old, surfacing whole, like bodies rising in a thawed lake.

Survival skills. Maren had trained her for exactly this, she understood suddenly—not for the quiet life, not for the garden and the grave-tending. For the road. For the hunt. For whatever Maren had known was coming and never lived to face.

"Right," Lena said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, roughened with power like wind-roughened wood. "New rules."

She knelt in the snow and drew a circle with one finger. Where her fingertip passed, the snow melted and refroze into a fine glassy thread—a warding line, clean and bright, the kind of work that would have taken her a full day and a bag of prepared salts back in the valley. Here it took four seconds. She set a second circle around the child, nested inside the first, keyed to the girl's hum so that anything answering that melody would find itself turned around and walking the other way.

The child watched all of this with her head tipped, then carefully stepped outside both circles and took Lena's hand again.

"No?" Lena asked.

The girl pointed north. Then at herself. Then she made the spiral sign on her chest, and shook her head: *the wards won't hold me, don't waste them.*

"They're not for you," Lena said. "They're for what's following us."

Because something was. She could feel it now, the way she'd never been able to feel anything in the valley—a pressure at the edge of perception, patient and cold, keeping pace with them along the ridge. Not the court itself. Something smaller. A collector, maybe. An interest payment, coming due.

* * *

They walked until the light went long and copper, and made camp in the lee of a fallen rowan, and Lena discovered that fire-building was another thing the world remembered how to do. The flames burned tall and steady, and when she fed a thread of will into them they burned blue at the heart, and the child held her hands out to the warmth and smiled—the first smile Lena had seen on her, small and crooked and devastating.

Lena sat watch while the girl slept, and let the power move through her, learning its shape the way you learn a new house in the dark. It was hers. That was the terrifying part. It had always been hers, and she had spent seven years pretending to be a woman with empty hands.

Somewhere past midnight, the pressure at the edge of her senses stopped pretending to be distant.

She woke the child without touching her—just a whisper of will, and the girl's eyes opened, instantly awake, instantly silent. They crouched behind the rowan together as the thing came down the slope: a lean shape wrapped in a cloak of stitched paper, each sheet covered in dense handwriting, faceless under a hood, leaving no prints in the snow. It stopped at the ashes of their fire. It bent. From somewhere within the paper rustle came a sound like a quill scratching.

It was writing them down.

Lena felt the old fear rise—the seven-year fear, the *be small, be nothing, be overlooked*—and she let it rise, and then she did the thing she had sworn never to do again.

She stood up, and she answered it.

Her voice came out doubled, hers and something older underneath, and the words were not words she knew but words she *made*, on the spot, in the grammar Maren had drilled into her at nineteen: a counter-entry. *Not yours. Not owed. Witnessed and refused.* She spoke the child's protection into the air between them like a blade laid on a table, and the paper-cloaked thing went very still, and the scratching stopped.

For a long moment, collector and witch regarded each other across the dead fire.

Then it inclined its hood—an acknowledgment, courteous and chilling, of a debt disputed rather than denied—and unfolded. The papers of its cloak spread wide like wings, blank sides flashing, and it poured itself uphill into the dark, gone between one heartbeat and the next, leaving only a single sheet fluttering down onto the snow.

Lena picked it up with fingers that would not quite stop shaking. The handwriting was Maren's.

One line, in ink gone brown with age:

*If you are reading this, you crossed the line. Good girl. Now hurry—it knows your name, and it has known it far longer than you have.*

The child leaned against her side, humming softly, and away to the north, under the snow and the stone and two hundred years of accumulated interest, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count.
