## 8. Ink and Ashes

The town was called Grayharbor, though there was no harbor and nothing gray about the mud. It sat where the salt plains gave out and the king's road began, a smear of roofs and smoke at the edge of the world Lena had spent seven years pretending ended at Saltmere's last fence post.

She had not expected the noise.

Saltmere made sounds like breathing—wind in thistle, snow settling on thatch, Maren's kettle long ago. Grayharbor made sounds like a wound: cart wheels screaming on frozen ruts, a smith's hammer keeping no time at all, dogs, drunks, a woman three doors down singing something bawdy to a child who laughed too loudly for Lena's nerves. The child herself—she still could not think of her as anything but *the girl*, though she'd caught herself twice almost saying a name she didn't know—pressed close against Lena's hip and put her fingers in her ears.

"I know," Lena said. "I know."

Three days north of the salt-line, and the girl had not spoken. But she watched everything. That was worse, somehow. In the valley she had watched the fire, the salt lamps, the spirals she drew with a patience that made Lena's skin crawl. Here she watched people—their hands, their mouths, the way they moved through crowds—and Lena saw her cataloguing them the way a starving woman catalogs bread behind glass.

Lena pulled the girl's hood lower and walked.

*Don't look at anyone. Don't let anyone look at you.* The rules of Saltmere were useless here; she kept reciting them anyway, rosary-fashion, because the alternative was admitting she had no rules left. She had crossed the line. Magic had come back into her like water into a dried well—not gently, but all at once, a flooding that had dropped her to her knees in the snow while the girl stood over her humming that guttering melody. Three days later she still felt it: every charm she'd ever learned sitting in her chest like coins in a pocket, heavy, spendable, *spendable* after seven years of poverty.

She wanted to be sick. She wanted to sing. She did neither.

What she needed was information, and information in a border town lived in one place: wherever the charms were sold.

* * *

She found it by smell before sight—a sweetness under the woodsmoke, burnt sugar and something mineral, like lightning held in a jar. The shop occupied the ground floor of a leaning house wedged between a farrier and a pawnbroker, its window full of hanging things that turned slowly in the draft: knotted cords, bird skulls, wax tablets stamped with sigils she recognized from Maren's grimoire and a dozen she didn't. A hand-painted sign read **CHARMS BOUGHT & SOLD — FAIR RATES — NO QUESTIONS**.

No questions. In Lena's experience, places that promised no questions were the places that asked the most expensive ones.

"Stay by the door," she murmured. "Touch nothing."

The girl looked up at her—those eyes, gray as slate weather, old as anything—and touched nothing. She simply stood in the doorway and *hummed*, very softly, two notes of that melody, and every hanging charm in the shop swung toward her at once like iron filings finding a lodestone.

Lena's heart stopped, restarted wrong.

"Well now." The voice came from behind a curtain of beads, low and dry as paper burning. "That's either a very clever trick or a very stupid one."

The charm-seller who emerged was small, brown as a walnut, wrapped in so many shawls she seemed less dressed than accumulated. Her eyes were quick and wet-bright, and they went first to the girl, then to the swaying charms, then to Lena, and stayed there with an expression Lena knew intimately from mirrors: the look of someone recognizing a kind.

"You'll want to buy something," the woman said. "Everyone who comes in wanting nothing always ends up buying."

"I want directions," Lena said. "North. And I want to know who sold this child's soul."

Silence. The charms stopped swinging, all together, which charms should not do.

The seller crossed the room without hurry, took the girl's chin in one small hand, tilted her face to the light. The girl endured it with that terrible patience. On her collarbone, where the skin showed above her borrowed coat, the spiral sigil lay faint as a scar in milk—visible only when you already knew to look, which Lena suspected was exactly how it had been designed.

"Hm," said the seller. She released the girl's chin, wiped her hand on her shawl as if wiping away something more than dirt, and looked at Lena with new arithmetic in her face. "You're not her mother."

"No."

"Not her owner, or you'd have papers. Not court, or you'd have manners." She sniffed. "Hedge-witch. Valley-trained, by the cut of your wards—I can smell the salt on your craft even off the plain. So either you stole her, or you're trying to save her, and nobody steals a vessel. They're not worth the trouble until they're empty, and this one's—" she cocked her head, listening to something Lena couldn't hear— "this one's maybe half-full yet. Half-full and walking north in winter." Her mouth twisted. "So. Saving."

"Who brokered the sale?" Lena asked again.

The seller moved to her counter, unhurried, and began sorting wax tablets as though Lena hadn't spoken. "Information's a charm like any other. Has a price."

"Name it."

"Depends what I find when I answer." She glanced up. "Some answers cost the asker more than the seller. You sure you want to stand there looking brave? Brave is the most expensive thing a person can wear into my shop."

Lena thought of Maren's ledger, the neat columns, her own name written in a dead woman's hand: *collateral, held*. She thought of the girl's fingers in her ears against the noise of a world too loud to bear. She thought of the salt-line behind them and the mountain ahead and the solstice coming down on all of it like a closing door.

"Ask your question," she said.

* * *

The seller's name, eventually, was Odile. She poured tea that tasted of pine ash and iron, and made the girl drink it sweetened, watching with professional fascination as the child held the cup in both hands and drank with the gravity of someone performing a rite.

"Seraquine," Odile said at last, and the name came out of her carefully, the way you set down something hot. "Court factor. Third rank beneath the mountain—or she claims third rank; with factors you never see the whole ladder, only the rung they're standing on. She brokers for the court's creditors. Souls, mostly. Pieces of souls, when wholes aren't available. Memories, names, first loves, the sound of your mother's voice—anything a person carries that can be weighed." She sipped her own tea. "She trades under many seals. Dozens. Some of them belong to dead witches."

Lena's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Maren."

"Maren what?"

"Maren Ambrose. Hedge-witch of Saltmere. Dead seven years." Lena set the cup down before her hand could shake visibly. "Her seal was on the sale. I found it in her accounts."

Odile studied her for a long moment, and whatever she was weighing, it wasn't coin. "Then here's your answer and your poison both, hedge-witch. Seraquine doesn't steal seals. She *inherits* them. Buys them from estates, from debtors, from the desperate. A seal is a signature is a promise is a door, and Seraquine collects doors." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "If Maren Ambrose's seal sold that child's soul, then either Seraquine bought the right to use it—which means Maren owed the court before she died—or Maren sold it herself, and Seraquine merely carried the paper."

"She wouldn't have." The words came out of Lena flat and certain and seven years deep. "Whatever else she was, she spent her life teaching me to keep magic *out*. She buried her grimoire under the floor rather than use it. She—"

"—was a woman once," Odile said, not unkindly, "and women owe things. Especially women running from courts." She leaned forward. "How do you think the valley stays clean, girl? Saltmere's salt isn't weather. It isn't geology. Somebody *paid* for that deadness, and payments to the court beneath the mountain don't end. They compound."

The tea turned to ash in Lena's mouth. She thought of Maren counting stores every autumn, the ritual precision of it, the way she'd weighed each jar of rendered salt as if the numbers mattered more than the salt. Not hoarding. *Servicing.*

"How much?" Lena heard herself ask.

"That," said Odile, "is above my counter's pay grade. What's below my pay grade is this:" she tapped the girl's sigil with one fingernail, not quite touching skin, "Seraquine will be expecting you. Factors are always expecting someone; it's how they stay factors. If you walk north asking questions, you're not hunting her. You're delivering yourself." She sat back. "Still going?"

"Yes."

"Of course you are. They always are." Odile rose, joints crackling, and began pulling things from shelves with sudden brisk purpose. "You'll need passage. The road north past the tollstones is caravan country this time of year—bandits, wolves, and worse, the tax collectors. There's a wool caravan leaving tomorrow dawn, Master Corvin, bound for Thornmarket at the mountain's foot. He'll take passengers who can pay." She paused, a wax tablet in each hand, and looked at Lena over the counter with those wet-bright eyes. "Can you pay?"

Lena opened the bundle she carried: Maren's grimoire, the cipher-letter, three days' food, and a small wooden box she had sworn, seven years ago, never to open again.

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age, lay four charms of her own making—from before Saltmere, from the years she did not count. Charms made with the full flood of her gift, before she'd learned to dam it. Each one was worth, in the border markets, more than the house she'd lived in.

Each one was a piece of the person she had killed to become invisible.

"There's a storm-charm here," she said, keeping her voice level. "Worked at full strength. It will carry a wagon train through a whiteout untouched."

Odile's eyebrows climbed. "Full-strength work, unregistered, pre-kingdom technique by the knotting—that's not a charm, that's a *felony* in three jurisdictions." Her eyes gleamed. "I'll give you fair rates."

"No questions?"

"Oh, I'll have questions." Odile smiled, and it was not entirely unkind. "I just won't charge you for them."

* * *

They slept that night in the loft above the shop, among hanging bundles of dried angelica and the tick of cooling wax. The girl would not sleep until Lena lay down beside her, and even then she slept badly, turning, her breath catching on fragments of that melody.

Lena lay awake and counted costs, because counting was the only prayer she knew.

One charm gone. The storm-work, her finest surviving piece, made in another life with hands that had not yet learned to flinch. She tried to remember making it and found she could—vividly, horribly: the night, the rain, the reason she'd needed a storm to hide inside. Every charm in that box was a memory with a shape. Selling them was selling herself in installments, and she had a sickening suspicion that the road ahead would keep asking for installments until the box was empty and she was too.

And that was only the currency she could count.

*Maren owed the court.* The thought turned and turned. All those years of lessons—*keep the wards strong, girl, count the salt, never work beyond the hedge*—and Lena had thought she was being taught safety. She had been being taught *maintenance*. She was the caretaker of a debt, sweeping the floors of a prison and calling it a home. And somewhere in the mountain ahead, a factor wearing Maren's stolen seal was keeping the books, and had been waiting, patient as interest, for the collateral to come due.

The girl whimpered in her sleep. Then—softly, in a language with no living speakers—she said a word.

It sounded like *home* and like *hunger* and like neither. Lena felt it pass through her like cold water through cloth, taking something loose with it.

She held the child until morning.

* * *

Master Corvin's caravan assembled in the blue dark before dawn: eight wagons, forty-odd souls, wool and tin and fear of the road. Odile walked them out herself, bundled to the eyes, and pressed one last thing into Lena's palm—a wax tablet, blank except for a single spiral pressed near the edge.

"If you reach Thornmarket and need a friend, show that to any charm-seller flying a red lamp. Costs you nothing now." Her grip tightened. "Costs plenty later, likely. Everything does." She crouched suddenly to the girl's level, and her voice lost its merchant's edge. "Half-full," she said, to the child, as if the child could hear her. "Hold on to what's yours, little one. It's the only coin they can't mint."

The girl looked at her. Then, deliberately, she reached up and touched Odile's cheek—one finger, light as snowfall—and Odile flinched as though scalded, and stepped back, and said nothing more, and did not watch them go.

Corvin took the storm-charm with the reverence of a man accepting a live coal, and did not ask why a hedge-witch traveled with a mute child and no luggage worth naming. The wagons rolled north as the sun came up blood-colored over the plain, and Lena sat in the bed of the second wagon with the girl warm against her side, watching Saltmere's direction until the horizon erased it.

By midday the land changed. The salt gave out entirely; grass appeared, brown and stubborn; and the air grew thick with something Lena had forgotten the taste of—wild magic, unclaimed, blowing off the northern hills like pollen. The girl lifted her face into it and breathed deep, and for the first time since the doorway in the snow, she smiled.

It was not entirely a child's smile.

Toward dusk they passed the first tollstone—a leaning pillar carved with faces worn to suggestion, hung with ribbons and small bones, offerings to whatever collected at the crossings. The caravan slowed to pay its customary coin. And there, at the roadside, waiting with the patience of furniture, stood three figures.

They were adults. That was the only ordinary thing about them. Their clothes were laborer's rough-spun, their hands calloused, their feet in good boots—but their eyes were open and utterly vacant, fixed on the middle distance, and they stood in a row facing the stone as if awaiting instructions that had not come in years. One of them hummed. It took Lena a moment, ice spreading through her chest, to place the melody.

Two notes. Over and over. The same two notes the child had hummed at her door.

A caravan driver tossed them hard bread without looking, the way you'd feed stray dogs, and the three figures caught it and ate mechanically, and did not stop humming, and the wagons rolled on.

The girl had gone rigid against Lena's side. Her small hand found Lena's and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and her lips moved around a word in the old tongue, and this time Lena needed no translation, because some words arrive in the body before the ear:

*Not yet. Not me. Not yet.*

Ahead, far off through the failing light, the mountain had become visible at last—a black shape on the horizon wearing a crown of cloud, and around its base, scattered like embers, the lights of Thornmarket.

Somewhere under that crown, a woman with many seals was opening her ledger.

Lena tightened her hold on the child's hand and did not let go.
