## 9. The Hollow Choir

*The child's fragments:*

Cold. Not the cold of snow. The cold of a room where no one has spoken for years.

There are others here. She can feel them the way she feels the salt lamps gutter — a pressure, a wrongness, like standing near an open grave. They move. That is the terrible part. They move and they work and they breathe, and nothing inside them answers.

She presses her palm flat against the inn window and watches them in the yard below: men and women hauling barrels from a caravan of grey wagons, stacking wood, mucking stables. Their movements are smooth. Too smooth. No wasted motion, no hesitation, no little human stumbles. Water poured from a jug moves like that.

One of them looks up.

The child's breath fogs the glass. Her heart does something strange — not fear exactly, but recognition, the way you recognize your own face in a dark mirror and for a moment don't know whose it is.

The hollowed one smiles. It is a smile with nothing behind it, a lamp lit in an empty house to make passersby think someone lives there.

*Inside her chest, where the bright thing was taken, something aches in sympathy.*

* * *

Lena saw them first as figures in the yard of the Drowned Bell, the last inn before the mountain road turned cruel. She had noticed the caravan earlier — six wagons painted the grey of old ash, drawn by horses too calm, moving through the border town without stopping to trade. Debtor caravans were common enough on this road. What they carried varied. Sometimes goods. Sometimes people paying off their families' debts with years of labor.

Sometimes other things entirely.

"There," the charm-seller had said that morning, spitting into the road. "That sigil. Seraquine's mark, or one of her hundred faces. You want to find her factor-work, girl, follow the quiet ones." He had tapped the child's spiral sigil, drawn in spilled salt on his own counter, and looked at Lena with something between pity and greed. "Follow the quiet ones."

Now Lena understood what he'd meant.

The laborers worked in perfect silence. Not the silence of the weary or the sullen — the silence of things that had never been taught sound mattered. A woman stacked firewood with movements so fluid they seemed rehearsed over centuries. A man scrubbed the inn's doorstep again and again, though it was already clean, because cleaning was what he had been told to do and no part of him remained to wonder when to stop.

"Gods' teeth." Lena pulled the child back from the window. "Don't look."

But the child was already looking, had been looking since they arrived, and Lena realized with a cold slide in her stomach that the girl wasn't frightened. She was *familiar*. The way you are familiar with the house you lived in before you could speak.

"Nell." The name had come to Lena three nights ago, unbidden, and the child had gone still and then nodded, once, solemn as a judge. "Nell, look at me."

The girl turned. Her eyes were the same grey-brown as any valley child's, but there was a depth to them now that hadn't been there a week ago, a well-darkness, and Lena thought: *the closer we get, the more of her drains away.* The court beneath the mountain was calling its property home, and every mile north took another measure of whatever made Nell Nell.

"We eat," Lena said firmly, "and then we sleep, and at first light we're on the road. We don't stop here."

They stopped there anyway. The snow came down like a verdict.

* * *

*The child's fragments:*

The inn smells of tallow and wet wool and underneath it something older — the smell of the cold halls, the smell of the place where hands took the bright thing. She hides behind Lena's coat and breathes the smell and hates that some small buried piece of her finds it almost comforting. Almost like home. That is the worst thing the thieves did, maybe. They made the theft feel like a shape she fits.

The laborers come inside when the work is done. They file through the door in a line, and the line never jostles, never crowds, each one spaced exactly a hand's width from the next. They sit along the wall on a bench built long enough for all of them, and they eat what is put in front of them, and they do not taste it.

She can hear them.

Not with her ears. With the hollow place. They make a sound like a choir heard through a closed door — dozens of voices humming one note, except the note is absence, the note is a hole shaped like a person. Each of them is singing *I am here I am here I am here* without knowing it, the way a bell keeps ringing after it's been struck, and none of them know they've been struck.

One of them — a man with a farmer's shoulders and hands scarred from honest work done by someone who used to be him — turns his head toward her. Slowly. Like a door opening in a house where no one lives.

His mouth moves. No sound comes out. But the hollow place hears it anyway:

*You're new.*

She scrambles backward, knocks a stool over, and Lena's arm comes around her like a gate closing.

"It's all right," Lena says, to her, to the room, to no one. "It's all right."

It is not all right. Nothing about this is all right, and the man with the farmer's shoulders keeps looking at her with eyes like wells, and his mouth keeps moving, and the choir-note in the hollow place says:

*We were all new once.*

* * *

Lena got them a room at the back, up a narrow stair, and paid with the last of Maren's coin rather than touch the charm she'd sworn off. Through the floorboards she could hear the debtor caravan's handlers talking with the innkeep — flat voices, transactional voices, the voices of people discussing feed and fodder.

"—six more fit for the winter routes. The mountain court pays by weight of soul-stuff, and these have held their flavor better than most—"

Lena pressed her palms against her ears. It didn't help. Words like that went straight through skin.

Nell sat on the bed's edge, knees drawn up, watching the door. She hadn't eaten. Lena coaxed broth into her spoonful by spoonful, and the girl swallowed obediently, mechanically, and each obedient swallow frightened Lena more than refusal would have. Refusal meant a will. Obedience meant erosion.

"You're still here," Lena whispered. "You hear me? You're still here, Nell. I'm going to get the rest of you back."

The child looked up. And then, slowly, she reached out and took Lena's hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over the sternum, over the hollow place, and held it there.

Lena felt it.

Not a heartbeat — there was a heartbeat, steady, childish, alive. Underneath the heartbeat, something else. A hum. A single sustained note like a wet finger circling a glass rim, coming from nowhere, from everywhere under the girl's ribs. And threaded through the hum, faint as writing under water, dozens of other notes. Other hums. Other hollows.

"Oh," Lena breathed. "Oh, gods. You can hear them."

Nell's chin dipped. Yes.

"How many?"

The child held up fingers. Ten. Then she frowned, concentrated, and added fingers until she ran out, and then simply spread both hands wide, twice, and let them fall.

Twenty. Twenty souls in the yard below, singing their one empty note, and a child tuned to their frequency like a bell tuned to its sisters.

Lena sat down heavily beside her. Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere south, seven years of careful invisibility lay behind her like a shed skin; ahead, the mountain waited with its sealed names and its ledgers and its interest accruing for two centuries. She had known, abstractly, what hollowness meant. She had written it in her mind as a tragedy, a fate, a word.

It was not a word. It was a man scrubbing a clean doorstep forever. It was a choir of twenty voices with nothing left to sing but *here*.

"I'm not letting that happen to you," Lena said. "Whatever it costs. Whatever I have to sign."

Nell leaned against her shoulder. And in the hollow place, the choir shifted — just slightly — and the child felt something she had no language for yet, in any tongue, living or dead.

Hope has a sound. It sounds like one voice in a chorus trying, for the first time in years, to change the note.

* * *

*The child's fragments:*

Sleep comes, and with it the cold halls again, clearer now, nearer, the mountain pulling its property home through her dreams.

She stands in the corridor of white stone. The lamps burn with flames that bend toward her, hungry, attentive. Far off, water drips in a rhythm like counting.

She knows this corridor. She knew it before she knew anything, because it is where the knowing was taken out of her.

Ahead: the ledger room. She has never seen it awake, but she has seen it a thousand times asleep. Doors of black wood. A smell of ink and iron. And beyond the doors, a presence reading itself aloud — pages turning, names being weighed, interest calculated in a currency she understands in her bones even now, especially now.

*Two hundred years,* the presence murmurs, in a voice made of every voice it holds. *And the heir walks toward us at last.*

She wakes gasping.

The room is dark. Lena sleeps sitting up, one hand still resting on the child's shoulder, a knife loose in the other. Snow ticks against the window. And down in the yard, through the frost-feathered glass, the laborers stand motionless in the falling snow, twenty of them, faces tipped up toward the mountain, toward the inn, toward —

Toward her window.

They do not move. They do not shiver. The snow gathers on their shoulders like a second skin, and their mouths open in unison, and though no sound crosses the glass, the hollow place fills with the choir's note, louder than it has ever been, close as a hand on her chest:

*Come home. Come home. Come home.*

Beside her, Lena stirs. Her eyes open — sharp, immediate, awake the way hunted things sleep — and she follows the child's gaze to the window, and Lena goes very still.

"They're looking at you," Lena whispers.

The child nods. Then she reaches out and, in the frost on the glass, writes with one small finger. Not spirals this time. Letters — the pre-kingdom script, the language before names, the words she speaks fluently only in sleep.

Lena stares at it. She has spent seven years studying Maren's buried papers, and she knows these characters, knows them from a single page in Maren's deepest cipher, a page headed with a warning in Maren's cramped hand: *If ever she writes this word, run.*

"What does it say?" Lena asks, though her voice already knows.

The child traces it again, slower. Then she takes Lena's hand and presses it once more against the hollow place, and lets her feel the answer instead of speaking it, because the answer is not a word, it is a direction, it is a summons, it is the choir changing its note at last:

*Heir.*
