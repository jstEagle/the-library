## 10. Seraquine

The ink in the mountain's ledgers never dried. That was the first thing they taught a factor, before seals and sigils and the grammar of debt: *our ink is always wet, because our accounts are never closed.* Seraquine had been taught by a woman named Maren Ovesse, who had learned it from someone older still, and in thirty years of service she had come to believe it was the only honest sentence in the whole archive.

She sat now in her office above the counting floor, where the light came down through a shaft cut at such an angle that it touched her desk for exactly one hour each day—the hour of audit, the court called it, though Seraquine privately thought of it as the hour of confession. The light was thin and grey, the color of old pewter, and it lay across the open ledger like a blade laid flat.

She turned a page with two fingers, careful not to smudge.

*One vessel, female, age approximately six years,* the entry read, in her own hand, written eleven months ago in a border town whose name she had already forgotten. *Soul intact at time of purchase. Provenance: sold by guardians, reason recorded as famine. Price paid in full. Destination: the court beneath the mountain. Commissioned on behalf of—*

And there the line stopped, as it always stopped, dissolving into a seal of black wax that was not wax and would not break, not for her, not for anyone below the rank of sovereign. The true buyer's name was sealed even from her. She had brokered the sale; she had carried the soul south in a lead-lined reliquary no larger than a snuffbox; she had watched the clerks of the deep archive enter it into the master ledger with hands that trembled slightly, though whether from cold or reverence she could not say. And she did not know who wanted it.

That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. A factor who knew too much became an asset, and assets were collected.

Seraquine poured herself a measure of plum wine gone sharp with age and drank it without tasting. Around her, through the floorboards, she could hear the counting floor at its work: the scratch of a hundred pens, the soft chime of scales weighing things that had no weight—memories, shadows, first words, last breaths, all of it flowing down toward the vaults in pipes of hammered silver. The court beneath the mountain was hungry the way winter is hungry. It did not hate what it consumed. It simply could not stop.

*Eleven months,* she thought, and looked again at the entry. *And already the vessel walks free.*

That was the other thing sitting on her desk, beside the ledger: a report, delivered that morning by a courier who smelled of road dust and fear. The vessel had surfaced in Saltmere valley. Of all the godforsaken, salt-dead, magic-proof holes in the world, the child had walked out of the court's reach and into the one place in the northern countries where the court's fingers went numb.

Someone had taken her out. Someone had carried her across the wards, or found her wandering, and had not reported it, which meant they did not intend to return her.

Seraquine read the report a third time. It was thin. The court's eyes thinned to nothing at the salt-line, so everything beyond it arrived as rumor, secondhand and stale. But rumor said the child had appeared at a cottage door on the edge of the valley, barefoot in the first snow. Rumor said the cottage belonged to a hedge-witch dead these three years. Rumor said—

Rumor said the woman living there had come to Saltmere seven years ago and had not aged a day since, because time in a salt valley moves like syrup, and because some people choose valleys the way other people choose disguises.

Seraquine set down the report and pressed her fingertips to her closed eyes.

*Ishikawa,* she thought. *Lena Ishikawa.*

She remembered the name from Maren's papers—not the ledger Maren kept for the court, but the other one, the private one, the ciphered book Seraquine had inherited along with the seals and the debts and the particular loneliness of the trade. Maren had collected strays the way other brokers collected interest. Runaways. Burned witches. Girls who could do things with charms that made grown men weep and then forget why. And beside one of those names, in Maren's tight, upright script, a single annotation: *mine to teach. Not yours to take.* Underlined twice.

Maren had underlined very few things twice.

* * *

The courier had also brought weather, of a kind. Word moved up the mountain roads faster than horses when the couriers used the whisper-roads, the buried lines of silver thread that carried a spoken message the way a wire carries lightning. Seraquine had spent the afternoon listening to them, one ear pressed metaphorically to the ground, and what she heard was this: a woman and a small girl had crossed the salt-line at dusk two days past, heading north. The woman had paused at the boundary stone for the length of three heartbeats—and every ward within a mile had shivered like a struck bell.

That detail interested Seraquine more than all the rest. Wards did not shiver for ordinary travelers. Wards shivered for power returning to a body that had starved it for years, the way a limb screams when blood comes back. Seven years of suppression, released in a single step. The woman walking north toward the mountain was not merely brave. She was waking up.

Seraquine rose and went to the window. Her office overlooked the mountain's southern face, where the terraced courts descended in steps of black stone, each one colder and quieter than the last. Below, the border town of Vell's Crossing clung to the lower slopes like lichen—charm-sellers, debt-brokers, caravan masters, the whole gray economy of the court's appetite. Somewhere down there, if the reports held true, Lena Ishikawa was buying passage with charms she had sworn off, learning again how much everything cost.

*You'll reach me in four days,* Seraquine calculated. *Five, if the snows turn. You'll come to my office because you have nowhere else to go, and because I am the only person in these mountains who can tell you what your mentor never did.*

She wondered, watching the light die on the terraces, whether Maren had known this too. Whether somewhere in the ciphered book there was a page headed *if Lena ever comes south,* with instructions, warnings, perhaps even an apology. Maren had been thorough that way. Maren had been thorough about everything except honesty.

* * *

They had met only once, teacher and student—or rather, Maren and the apprentice she'd farmed out to the trade, which was what Seraquine had been at nineteen: a clever, hollow-eyed girl from a farm that had been sold out from under her, apprenticed to a factor because her family owed a debt and children were acceptable currency.

Maren Ovesse had come to the counting house to audit a colleague, and had found Seraquine instead, alone after hours, teaching herself the old notation from a stolen primer. Most factors would have reported her. Theft of notation was theft of power. Maren had stood in the doorway for a long moment, lamplight catching the iron-grey of her hair, and then she had sat down across the desk and said:

"You're holding the pen wrong. Here." She had reached over and adjusted Seraquine's grip with two cool fingers. "A factor's hand must be steady enough to sign away a life and gentle enough that the life doesn't feel the pen. Again."

For three winters, Maren had come when she could. She taught Seraquine the grammar of contracts, the ethics that passed for ethics in the trade—*never lie in a contract, never cheat the arithmetic, never take what isn't offered, because the court punishes bad faith in its servants worse than it punishes it in its victims*—and, once, late at night, over wine neither of them should have been drinking, the thing underneath everything:

"I got out," Maren had said, staring into the fire. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, girl? I was where you are. I signed where you sign. And I bought my way free, at interest that will outlive me, and I walked north until I found a valley where the salt swallows magic whole, and I built a life there on top of the grave of the one I should have had." She had laughed, short and bitter. "And then, because I am a fool of the first water, I started taking in runaways. Teaching them. Giving them the tools I used to escape, one at a time, like handing out knives to children and hoping they cut the right ropes."

"Did they?" Seraquine had asked. "Cut the right ropes?"

"Some. One or two." Maren's eyes had gone distant. "One of them I think I failed. Or saved. I've never decided which."

Seraquine had understood, even then, that she was being told something oblique—that *Ishikawa* was the name behind the words—but she had been nineteen and ambitious and she had let it pass. She had let a great many things pass, in thirty years. That was what weariness was: the sediment of things let pass.

Now she opened the drawer beneath her desk and took out the ciphered book—Maren's private ledger, willed to her successor as casually as a coat. She turned to the page she had avoided for seven years, the one with the double-underlined name, and read it through slowly, the way one reads a letter from the dead.

There were notes. Of course there were notes. Maren had annotated everything.

*Lena. Fled the court's reach 7 yrs past. Took nothing but her own name, which is more than most manage. Sealed her own power rather than surrender it—clever, costly, hers. If she comes south: she will bargain hard and blame herself for everything. Do not mistake guilt for weakness. Do not mistake kindness for naivety. She is the best thing I made and I broke her making her.*

Below that, in fresher ink, added in what Seraquine recognized as Maren's final year, the handwriting gone thin and shaky:

*If the vessel surfaces, protect her if you can. But know this: the buyer's name is sealed above my rank and yours both. There are powers in this court older than its ledgers. Whatever you do, do not promise the chorus anything you cannot unsay.*

Seraquine closed the book.

*The chorus.* Even the name raised the hairs on her arms. The sovereign of the court beneath the mountain was not a person; it was an accumulation, a choir of every soul the court had ever swallowed, speaking with ten thousand throats in perfect unison. Factors dealt with clerks. Clerks dealt with stewards. Stewards dealt with the sealed names. No one dealt with the chorus. No one came back from the audience chamber with their own voice entirely their own.

And somewhere below her feet, in the master ledger, was a page with a sealed buyer's name—a buyer who had purchased a six-year-old's soul and then let it walk out of the mountain's grasp, either through carelessness or design. Seraquine had served the court long enough to distrust coincidence the way farmers distrust a mild December.

*Either the buyer lost the vessel,* she thought, *or the buyer is fishing. And you do not fish with bait that expensive unless you expect a large catch.*

* * *

On the fourth evening, the whisper-road spoke her name.

She was at her desk, auditing a caravan's account of shadow-tolls—it had been a long day of small corruptions, the kind that wore a factor down grain by grain—and the silver thread in the wall hummed, and the voice that came through it was her junior clerk's, pitched low and fast:

"Factor. The traveler from the valley is at the gate. The woman. She has the child with her, sleeping. She's asking for passage north and asking after a broker named Seraquine." A pause, the sound of the clerk swallowing. "She says—" the clerk hesitated, clearly reading from a note, "—she says *tell her Maren's pen remembers her grip.*"

Seraquine sat very still.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north, carrying the smell of coming snow, and the light in the shaft above her desk had long since slid off the ledger and up the wall, leaving the accounts in shadow. She thought of Maren's fingers adjusting her grip thirty years ago. She thought of a girl sealing her own power shut and hiding in salt for seven years, and of a child humming songs that guttered lamps, and of a sealed name in black wax that would not break for her.

*She is the best thing I made and I broke her making her.*

"Well," Seraquine said aloud, to the empty office, in the dry tone she used for closing accounts. "So am I."

She rose, took her coat from its peg—heavy wool, lined with lead-thread against casual scrying—and lifted the lamp. At the door she paused, looking back at the ledger lying open on the desk, at the wet black line where the buyer's name should have been.

Then she went down to meet the woman Maren had broken, carrying the only inheritance either of them had ever truly received: the knowledge of exactly what everything costs.
