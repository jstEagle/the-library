## 11. Bargaining Weather

The border town was called Grimsel Hollow, though the sign at its edge had been repainted so many times that older names showed through like scars beneath new skin: *Grimsel*, and beneath that something in the pre-kingdom script that Lena could not read but which made the child go still and touch it with two fingers, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal.

They had taken a room above the charm-seller's shop because it was the only room, and because the charm-seller—Yeva, who smelled of cloves and old smoke—had looked at the girl's sigil and gone pale in a way that told Lena she would rather have them under her roof than out of her sight.

The weather turned while Lena counted out their last coins on the counter. Not snow. Something worse. The clouds came down off the mountain like a lid being fitted to a jar, low and bruise-colored, and the air developed a pressure that made her teeth ache and the salt lamp in the window burn with a flame that leaned toward the mountain no matter how many times she turned the wick.

"Bargaining weather," Yeva said, following her gaze. "You get it here when the court beneath is hungry. The mountain breathes out, and everything with a price in it feels the pull." She glanced at the child, who was sitting on the floor winding a length of red thread around her fingers, over and under, making a pattern that hurt to look at directly. "And everything with a debt."

Lena had not slept. She had spent the night with Maren's cipher spread across the floorboards, working the final substitution by candlelight, and what she had found had rearranged the furniture of her entire life: *the valley's salt was raised by your bloodline, not mine.* Maren's careful hand, Maren's confession, Maren's apology folded into a letter that read like a will. Lena had read it four times and still did not know what she felt, only that it was too large to fit through the door of any single feeling. Her hiding place had been hers all along. Her exile had been an inheritance. And somewhere behind both of those truths stood the fact that Maren had known—had always known—and had let Lena believe otherwise for seven years, perhaps to keep her safe, perhaps to keep her small, perhaps because even a woman as good as Maren could not resist holding one card back from every hand she played.

She was still carrying that when the knock came.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The kind of knocking that expected to be answered, that had never once in its life been ignored.

Lena blew out the lamp before she opened the door—a hedge-witch's reflex, deny them light to read you by—and found herself looking at a woman standing in the falling dark with snow settling on her shoulders and refusing, somehow, to melt.

Seraquine was smaller than Lena had imagined. The charm-seller's description had built her into something monumental—a factor of the deep court, a woman who traded under a hundred seals—but the person on the doorstep was slight, gray-eyed, dressed in traveling wool of a cut too fine to be anything but deliberate. She looked tired in the way that stone looks tired: worn smooth, not weakened. Her hands were bare despite the cold, and on the inside of each wrist, where a watchman might wear irons, she wore rings of black metal that seemed to drink the lamplight from the windows across the street.

"You're harder to find than Maren was," Seraquine said. Her voice was quiet, pitched for two people, though the street was empty. "She would have been proud of that. Or annoyed. With Maren it was usually both."

"You knew her."

"I trained her." A faint smile, without warmth or malice—just accuracy. "Before she ran. Before you ran. There's a tradition of it, apparently. May I come in? The cold is nothing to me, but it's rude to conduct business on a threshold, and thresholds are the only places I'm obliged to be honest."

That was either a courtesy or a trap, and Lena could not tell which, which she suspected was the point. She stepped aside. Behind her, the child stopped winding her thread and looked up, and Lena watched something pass between the girl and the visitor—not recognition exactly, but the way a compass needle recognizes north. The child's chin lifted. Her nostrils flared, as if she could smell the ledger on the woman's skin.

Seraquine knelt. It was a smooth, practiced motion, bringing her eyes level with the child's, and she studied the sigil that showed faintly at the girl's throat when the lamplight caught it—the spiral, the seven turns, the mark that had followed them from Saltmere to Grimsel Hollow like a signature chasing its document.

"Turn nine," Seraquine said softly. "They've begun turning her already. You're later than I thought." She rose and faced Lena, and the tiredness in her face sharpened into something almost like sympathy, which was more frightening than cruelty would have been. "Sit down, Ishikawa. What I have to offer will sound like mercy, and you'll want to be seated when you're tempted."

* * *

The room was small: one bed, one chair, a table scarred by generations of cups. Lena put the child on the bed with the red thread and sat in the chair herself, and Seraquine remained standing, declining the table's edge with a small shake of her head, as if sitting would constitute some concession she wasn't prepared to make.

"First, the facts," Seraquine said, "because Maren taught you to want those before anything else—I can see her hand in how you hold your shoulders. Facts. The child's soul sits in the master ledger at the mountain's heart, entered under purchase, held in trust for a buyer whose name is sealed even from me. That is unusual. In two hundred years of brokering, I have never been denied a buyer's name. Whoever wanted this soul wanted it badly enough to pay for silence, and silence at that price is never cheap." She paused. "The solstice is in six days. On the longest night, the court opens its books for settlement. Anything unclaimed reverts. Anything reverted is consumed. The child will hollow completely—not dead, worse than dead, an empty shape walking—and the part of her that is currently singing in her sleep will be poured into whatever vessel the sealed buyer intends. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Lena said. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was grateful to it, because underneath the steadiness her heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like. Six days. She had thought she had until spring.

"Second, the offer." Seraquine drew a slim book from her coat—not paper, something darker, pages like pressed slate—and laid it on the table between them. It fell open on its own, the way old books do when they've been opened to the same page ten thousand times. The page bore two entries. One was written in ink that shimmered like oil: *the vessel, turn eight, held.* The other was written in plainer stuff, older, and Lena's stomach dropped through the floorboards when she read it, because it was her own name, and beside it, in Maren's handwriting, a single word: *collateral.*

"You know about this page," Seraquine said, watching her face. "Good. Then you know that Maren pledged you against her own debts, and that her debts died with her unpaid, and that by the strict law of the ledger, you belong to the court's claim the moment you set foot past the salt-line. You've been walking around for three days as property, Ishikawa, and no one has collected, which tells you how patient the court can be when patience serves it." She turned the book slightly, angling it toward the light. "Here is my offer. The child's soul is purchased; purchases can be satisfied. If you assume the debt—if your name moves from collateral to principal—you free Maren's estate entirely. Every obligation she left behind, every loose thread of her old life, closes clean. And the court releases the vessel. The girl walks away whole. Her soul comes home before the solstice, and no one hollows, and no one consumes anyone."

"And me," Lena said. "What happens to me?"

"You bind yourself to the court." Seraquine said it simply, the way a physician says *it will hurt*. "Service. Term indefinite. Your magic—which, by the way, is considerable, and wasted on salt-thistle—is placed at the court's disposal. You would work in the archive, most likely. Ledgers, covenants, the law beneath the law. It's not a dungeon. It's not even unpleasant, after the first century. The court values precision, and you are precise. I know because I was made the same way, by the same teacher, and look how well I turned out." The smile again, brief and dry. "You would be trading one valley for another. But the second valley pays wages."

Lena looked at the page. At her name. Seven years of invisibility, of counting stores, of tending Maren's grave and keeping her magic buried so deep she'd nearly convinced herself it wasn't there—and now a stranger was offering to make her visible forever, to give her a place, a purpose, a wage, in exchange for the one thing she'd fled to keep.

It sounded like mercy. That was the terrible thing. It sounded exactly like mercy, warm and reasonable and structured, with terms and clauses and a deadline designed to make refusal feel like murder. Seraquine had probably delivered this speech a hundred times, in a hundred border towns, in bargaining weather, and every time she had made the cage sound like a career.

On the bed, the child had stopped winding her thread. She was watching Lena with eyes that were too old for her face, and her lips moved silently, shaping words in the pre-kingdom tongue, and though Lena couldn't translate them, she understood their cadence. It was the cadence of someone saying *don't*.

"The buyer," Lena said. "Whose name is sealed. Is it the sovereign?"

"No one knows if the sovereign has a name anymore. The sovereign is a chorus—all the souls the court holds, speaking together. Even I don't bargain with the chorus directly. I bargain with the ledger, and the ledger bargains with the chorus, and somewhere in between, decisions get made." Seraquine closed the book, and Lena felt the loss of it like a door shutting. "Why do you ask?"

"Because if the buyer were the chorus itself, you wouldn't need a seal. The chorus doesn't keep secrets from itself." Lena stood, slowly, and the chair scraped back, and the child's silent lips kept moving. "Someone else bought that soul. Someone who could afford to hide from you. And whoever they are, they'll still want it after the solstice whether I sign your page or not. So tell me honestly, factor—does my surrender actually save this child? Or does it just deliver a second vessel to the same buyer, gift-wrapped, with seven years of suppressed magic thrown in?"

For the first time, something moved behind Seraquine's gray eyes—something that might have been surprise, or might have been respect, or might have been the professional pleasure of a broker recognizing that she'd finally met someone worth the full truth.

"Maren," she murmured, "would have taken three days to ask that question. You took three minutes." She straightened her coat. "Honestly? I don't know. The release order would be genuine—the ledger cannot lie about its own transactions. But a released soul can be re-purchased, and the buyer's seal would still be waiting. You might be saving her. You might be clearing the road for whoever comes next. I am not permitted to speculate further, and unlike thresholds, speculation has no rules of honesty."

"Then my answer is no."

"Lena—"

"No." She said it again, firmer, and heard in her own voice something she hadn't heard in seven years: not fear, not calculation, but decision. "I won't trade my freedom for a maybe. I won't hand the court a trained witch and call it rescue. And I won't—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, "—I won't spend Maren's last pledge the easy way. She hid me for seven years. The least I owe her is to stay hidden from the things that deserve to find me, and to fight the ones that don't in the open, where I can see them coming."

Seraquine regarded her for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. Downstairs, faintly, Yeva was moving among her charms, and the smell of cloves rose through the floorboards like a memory of safety.

"There's a third option," Seraquine said at last, quietly. "I'm not supposed to mention it. It isn't in the ledger, and mentioning it costs me, so understand that I'm paying something to say it." She glanced at the child, then back. "Maren bought back her own soul once. Terrible interest. Ruinous terms. But there was a clause she used—a founder's clause, from the valley covenant. If it still exists, and if the bloodline still runs true, then a founder may assume a debt personally. Not as service. As surety. It's the oldest law the court honors, older than the chorus, older than the mountain, and it has never once been invoked successfully, because it requires the one thing brokers like me never have." She picked up her slate-black book and slid it back into her coat. "It requires someone willing to lose."

"And you're telling me because?"

"Because I trained under Maren for eleven years, and in eleven years she never once lied to me, and I find I've inherited the habit." Seraquine moved to the door, and the black rings on her wrists drank the lamplight one last time. "Six days, Ishikawa. The mountain is already breathing. Listen to the weather—it's telling you the same thing it tells everyone with a debt." She paused on the threshold, half in shadow. "One more thing, freely given. The buyer's seal—the one I'm forbidden to name. I saw it once, years ago, on a document I shouldn't have read. It was a spiral. Seven turns." Her eyes flicked to the child. "Whoever bought that soul used the girl's own sigil to seal the sale. Think about what kind of creature signs its work in the victim's handwriting."

Then she was gone, into the falling dark, and the snow where she had stood lay undisturbed, as if the night had agreed to forget her.

* * *

Lena sat by the window for a long time after, watching the bruised clouds lean against the mountain's shoulders.

The child climbed down from the bed and came to stand beside her, small and warm, and after a while she reached up and took Lena's hand—not pleading, not frightened, just holding it, the way you hold a rope in wind. Her lips moved again, shaping the pre-kingdom syllables, and this time Lena caught enough of the rhythm to guess at the sense: *six days, six days,* the girl was whispering, or perhaps counting, or perhaps reciting something the court was making her memorize for the long night ahead.

Lena looked at the mountain, and the mountain looked back, breathing its hungry weather over the town, and she thought about founders' clauses and seven-turn spirals and a mentor who had bought back her own soul at ruinous interest and never once said a word.

*Maren,* she thought. *What else did you bury?*

The cipher's last page was still spread on the floorboards upstairs, and there had been a line at the bottom she'd taken for a flourish—a spiral, drawn small and quick, seven turns, tucked into the margin like a signature.

Like a seal.

Lena tightened her grip on the child's hand and began, in her head, to plan a descent.
