## 12. What My Mother Buried

The cipher was stitched into the hem of Maren's winter shawl, and Lena had worn it for three days before she thought to look.

She found it the way she found most things now—by accident, by exhaustion, by the child's doing. Nell had been chewing on the frayed edge of the shawl where it hung on its peg by the door, a habit Lena should have broken her of and hadn't, when she'd gone suddenly still, the way she went still around anything written. Her small finger had hooked into the stitching along the border and pulled, once, gently, like plucking a harp string.

Lena took the shawl down and held it to the lamp.

The border was worked in cross-stitch, gray thread on gray wool, the kind of plain decoration Maren had put on everything. Lena had copied that border herself as an apprentice—thousands of tiny X's, hour after hour at Maren's table while the older woman sat across from her mending nets or grinding salt-thistle and saying nothing at all. *Patience is a hedge-witch's first tool,* Maren used to say. *Second is knowing which silences are load-bearing.*

Now, with Nell's finger pointing and the lamp angled low so the shadows made the stitches stand up like little walls, Lena saw what she had never seen.

Every seventh stitch was offset. Half a thread-width high. She counted them out along the border, heart going strange and slow: seven offset, then thirty-one even, then seven again. Seven and thirty-one. She knew those numbers. Every hedge-witch in the valley knew them, though nobody living remembered why—the salting-days of the founding year, the two numbers carved over the door of every root-cellar from here to the pass. Seven and thirty-one were the numbers of Saltmere itself.

Maren had stitched the valley's name into a cipher, and hidden it in plain sight on the shoulders of the woman she'd raised.

*Oh, you clever, cruel old woman,* Lena thought, and her eyes stung, and she didn't know if it was love or fury or both wearing each other's clothes.

* * *

It took her until well past midnight to work it out, because the cipher wasn't a substitution—it was a counting. The offset stitches marked positions; the even stitches between them gave intervals; and the whole thing only resolved when Lena stopped reading left to right and read instead the way salt crystallizes, spiraling inward from the edge. Nell watched her work with her chin on her folded arms, and twice reached out to turn the shawl for her without being asked, and Lena understood dimly that the girl could see the pattern the way she saw everything else—as something *true*, unhidden, humming under the surface of things.

The message, when it finally came clear, was four words long:

**UNDER THE HEARTH. NOT THE ASHES.**

Lena sat back. The fire had burned down to a red breathing glow. Outside, the snow had stopped and the sky had that scraped-clean blackness of deep winter, stars sharp as tacks.

Under the hearth. Not the ashes. Not the loose stone where Maren kept her emergency coin and her good knife—not the hiding place any sensible person would check first. Under the hearth itself. Under the foundation stone. The one place in the cottage that had never been moved since before either of them was born.

Lena looked at the hearth for a long time.

Then she woke Nell fully, wrapped her in blankets, and set her on the table with a heel of bread, because whatever was down there, she did not want to be alone when she lifted it.

* * *

The foundation stone came up easier than it should have. That was the first wrong thing. Stone that has sat undisturbed for decades grips the earth like a tooth in a jaw; this one shifted on the second pull, smooth as a drawer, and Lena felt the hair rise on her arms. Someone had prepared this. Recently. Within the last few years, certainly within the seven.

Beneath lay a cavity lined with lead sheeting—lead, the great deafener, the metal that even magic couldn't whisper through—and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, a bundle no bigger than a loaf of bread.

She carried it to the table. Nell leaned close, breath sweet with bread, and together they unwrapped it.

Inside were three things.

A ring of iron, plain and dark, sized for a woman's hand.

A ledger page, torn cleanly from a larger book, its edges browned with age but its ink still sharp.

And a letter, sealed with a smear of plain tallow rather than wax—Maren's way, always, of refusing ceremony—and addressed in a hand Lena knew better than her own:

*To the girl I stole.*

Lena's hands shook so badly she nearly dropped it.

*Not stolen from,* she read again. *Stolen. The girl I stole. As in—I am the thief.*

Nell made a small sound and pressed her palm flat against the back of Lena's hand, and Lena breathed, and broke the seal.

* * *

The letter was long. Maren had never been a woman who wasted words aloud, but on paper, in the privacy of a grave she'd dug for herself years in advance, she poured out like a burst cistern. Lena read it twice by lamplight while the fire died and Nell slept sitting up against her side, and by the end of the second reading Lena was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

*Maren had been a vessel.*

Not a hedge-witch who learned to guard against soul-brokers. A vessel—a hollowed child, sold before her sixth year to a creditor-house in the lowlands, emptied to a shell and set to labor, exactly as the adults in the debtor caravan had been. Exactly as Nell would be. She had been bought back at nineteen by a mistress whose name Maren had scratched out so violently the pen had torn the page—but the interest terms remained, itemized in Maren's neat accounting hand, and they were obscene. Forty years of labor. The surrender of her true name forever. And one clause more, circled twice in ink gone rusty-brown:

*the vessel shall render unto the house all works of craft made in service of concealment, in perpetuity, wherever such works are claimed.*

All works of craft made in service of concealment. Lena read the clause five times. Then she looked around her own cottage—at the wards knotted into the doorframe, the salt-lines under the windowsills, the deadening charms sewn into her own clothing, every quiet working Maren had ever taught her—and understood, with a lurch like the floor tilting, that none of it had been Maren's craft to give.

It belonged to someone else. It had always belonged to someone else. Every ward Lena had ever woven, every charm she would ever make, was already owned, already claimed, already entered in some ledger she had never seen.

*That is why I taught you to hide,* Maren wrote, further down. *Not because hiding is safe. Because hiding is owed. I could not give you freedom, child. I could only give you the shape of it, and pray you never learned the difference.*

But it was the last pages that broke her.

*I must tell you about the salt,* Maren wrote. *You will have heard, as everyone hears, that the valley's silence was raised by the founders' hedge-witch—that a great worker named Maren Ottelie called the salt up out of the earth two hundred years ago and bound it against all courts and creditors. You will have heard it because I let you hear it. I encouraged it. It was the kindest lie available to me, and I have never been a brave woman, only a careful one.*

*Here is the truth. The salt was not raised by me. It cannot have been. The covenant that calls the salt is blood-bound—it answers to a lineage, not a name, and I am not of that lineage. No hedge-witch is. When I came to this valley forty years ago, fleeing my creditors, the salt was already here, old and deep and sleeping, and it let me in because—*

*—because it knew you were coming.*

*Your bloodline founded this valley, Lena. Your grandmother's grandmother signed the founding covenant, and your line has kept the salt these two hundred years without one of you knowing it, because the knowledge was buried with each generation, passed down in ciphers and hems and hearthstones by women who understood exactly what the courts beneath the mountains do to families that know their own worth. Your mother knew. Your mother told me, the night she left you on my step, that the salt would keep you when nothing else could. She said: the valley is her inheritance. She said it like a warning. I have carried that warning for seven years like a coal in my chest, waiting for the day you would need it, and dreading that day, because an inheritance is only ever a debt with better lighting.*

*The ring in this bundle is yours. It was your mother's. It is the sign of the founder's line, and the mountain knows it, and I hid it in lead because I hoped you would live and die without ever needing to hold it.*

*If you are reading this, then the day came anyway. I am sorry. I was a coward, and I loved you, and those were the same thing in me, and I never learned to separate them.*

*One thing more, and then I will stop talking, which will surprise everyone including me. The child who will find you—because there will be a child, the ledgers always send a child, they did it to me—she is not your burden. Understand this. She is your key. What was taken from her was taken through a door, and doors open from both sides, and you are the only doorkeeper left. Do not go to bargain. Go to collect.*

*Burn this letter. Take the ring. And when you stand before whatever waits beneath the mountain, say your own name. Not mine. Never mine. Mine was bought and paid for and it bought me nothing. Yours was given.*

*Yours was given.*

The letter ended there. Below the signature—just *M*, no more—there was a final line in smaller script, cramped against the bottom edge as if written last, hastily, in the shadow of the grave she was digging:

*P.S. — If Tam Voss offers to come with you, let him. He knows why. Ask him nothing. Let him tell you himself, or not. Some debts are paid in silence.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Lena sat at the dead hearth with the letter in her lap and the iron ring in her palm, and Nell woke beside her and looked at the ring and reached for it and stopped, her fingers hovering, as if it hummed too loud to touch.

"It's mine," Lena said aloud. Her voice sounded rusted. "Apparently it's been mine the whole time."

The valley lay outside the window under its skin of snow, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys, the salt-thistle fields white and silent, the wards she had tended for seven years humming their borrowed tune. Her home. Her hiding place. Except it had never been a hiding place. It had been a stronghold, and she had been squatting in her own fortress, grateful to a ghost for walls that answered to her blood.

She thought of Seraquine's face when she'd spoken of Maren—the factor's careful courtesy, the flicker of something almost like respect. *Both women shaped by the same teacher.* And all the while Maren had been shaped by the same fear, the same machinery, the same ledgers. Runaway vessel. Debtor. Thief of a child she'd tried to protect by making her small.

"I'm not angry," Lena told the ashes, and found it was mostly true, which was somehow worse. "I just wish you'd told me I was allowed to be big."

Nell touched the ring at last. Where her finger met the iron, faint as breath on glass, a spiral bloomed—gold, brief, gone—and far off toward the mountain, so distant it might have been imagination, something answered. A sound below hearing. A turning of attention. The sense of a vast ledger opening to a fresh page.

Lena closed her fist around the ring. It was warm. It had been warm the whole night, she realized. Warm as a hand.

"Get your boots," she said to Nell. "We're done running north to beg. We're going to knock."

She was lacing her own boots when she heard it: footsteps in the snow outside, slow and uneven, and a lantern's glow swinging through the frost-furred window. A knock at the door—three raps, deliberate, familiar.

Tam Voss stood on her step, travel-cloaked, his old face grim as cut stone, and behind him in the dawn light the whole valley seemed to hold its breath.

"You've opened the hearthstone," he said. Not a question. His eyes went to her closed fist, and something in them cracked open—grief and relief fighting for the same ground. "Then you know what you are. And you'll be needing to hear what I've done."
