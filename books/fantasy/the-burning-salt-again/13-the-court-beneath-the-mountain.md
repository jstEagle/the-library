## 13. The Court Beneath the Mountain

*Cold. Cold like a mouth.*

The girl is walking but she is also not walking. Her feet are in Lena's footprints, small boots over big prints, and her body is warm under the wool coat Lena buttoned to her chin that morning, but some part of her is already *there* — beneath the white shoulder of the mountain, in halls where the dark is polished smooth as a spoon.

She knows the way without knowing it. That is the worst part. When the road forks at the frozen stream, her hand rises before she can stop it and points left, and Lena looks at her for a long moment — a look the girl feels on her cheek like a lamp held close — before taking the left fork too.

*Good girl,* says something far below, in a voice made of many voices folded together, like cloth woven from whispers. *Good vessel. Come home.*

She does not want to be a good girl for that voice. She wants to be a good girl for the woman who hums when she cooks, whose hands smell of salt and tallow, who calls her *little wren* even though the girl has no name yet, or has too many names, or had one name once and it was taken out of her chest like a coal from a fire and set down somewhere cold to be counted.

At night she dreams in the old tongue.

---

Lena heard it first on the third night north of the salt-line, in the loft above the waystation where they'd paid for a corner of straw with two charms and a lie about being cousins.

The child slept curled against her side, and in her sleep she spoke — fluently, urgently, in a language that predated every kingdom Lena knew from Maren's books. It was not babble. The syllables had weight and architecture. Vowels opened like doors; consonants fell like stones down wells. And though Lena could not translate a word of it, she understood, the way one understands a scream in any language, that the child was *describing something*. Narrating. Reporting.

To someone.

Lena sat up in the straw, heart hammering, and watched the girl's lips move in the dark. The firelight through the floorboards striped her face. Her eyes stayed closed, but beneath the lids they moved quickly, tracking things.

"No," the child said clearly — that one word Lena knew, because it was the same in nearly every tongue — and then a long rush of the old language, rising, pleading, and then silence, and then, softer, in the tone of someone reciting what they have been made to memorize:

*"Ashen-vel. Ashen-vel. Ashen-vel korath im."*

Lena did not sleep again that night.

By morning she had convinced herself it was a nightmare, nothing more, the residue of whatever had been done to the girl before she ever reached Saltmere. But on the fourth night it happened again, and on the fifth, and each time the old language came easier, fuller, the sentences longer, the pauses shorter — as if a door in the child were swinging wider and wider, and something on the other side was calling through it in a voice patient as dripping water.

And each time, the mountain was closer.

They saw it now even by day: a white-shouldered mass on the northern horizon, wearing clouds like a hood. The road bent toward it the way water bends toward a drain. Every village they passed was poorer than the last, houses shuttered against a cold that seemed to come not from the sky but from the ground itself, and twice Lena noticed doors marked with chalk spirals — wards, old ones, half-effaced — the same spiral the child had drawn in spilled salt on her kitchen floor a lifetime ago, back when Saltmere still felt like home.

On the sixth night, in the ruined chapel of a village whose name had been scraped off its own signpost, the child talked in her sleep for an hour without stopping.

Lena listened. She wrote down what she could catch, phonetically, in the back of Maren's ledger, and stared at the columns of alien syllables until her eyes burned. Then she did the thing she had been avoiding since the border town. She took the child's small hands in hers, waited until the sleeping eyes rolled and fluttered, and said, gently, in the plain trade-tongue:

"Who is speaking?"

The child's face went slack and serene. Her mouth opened.

"*We are,*" she said — in the old language, but Lena felt the meaning arrive behind her eyes like a draft through a cracked window, cold and unmistakable. "*We have always been. We are patient. We are owed.*"

Then the girl shuddered, turned her face into Lena's coat, and murmured — in a child's ordinary voice, small and frightened — *"don't let them count me."*

Lena held her until dawn.

* * *

The child dreams of the counting.

There is a room with no walls, only shelves, and the shelves go down farther than falling. On the shelves are jars, and in the jars are lights — no, not lights. Lights can be put out. These are *names*, burning slowly, the way names burn when no one is left alive to say them. Hands take her up. The hands are kind the way winter is kind: they do not hate you, they simply proceed. They open her chest — there is no blood, there is never any blood, there is only a seam, like the seam of a coat — and they lift out the bright thing that is her, her actual her, the part that would have grown into a woman with a name and a laugh, and they set it in a jar between a fisherman's last summer and a queen's forgotten middle name.

A voice reads numbers. The numbers are her. She is arithmetic now.

But sometimes — this is the part she holds onto, the splinter of herself she hides even from the hands — sometimes the jar grows warm, and she remembers salt on a stove, and a woman humming off-key, and being called *little wren*, and she thinks: *that one will come for me. That one counts differently. She counts me as mine.*

The voice below the mountain feels her thinking this, and is amused.

*Let her come,* it says, in the tongue before kingdoms. *Debtors always come. It is the nature of debt to be followed home.*

* * *

"We're being summoned," Lena said on the seventh morning, breath steaming, watching the mountain grow. "You understand that, don't you? It's not just pulling you. It's *inviting* us."

The child looked up at her. In daylight, awake, she was only a girl again — seven or eight years old, gap-toothed, solemn, her dark hair escaping its braid. But there were mornings now when she woke already speaking, mid-sentence, in the old tongue, and stopped only when she saw Lena's face. There were afternoons when she stood very still, staring north, and her breathing slowed to the rhythm of something vast and subterranean. There was an hour yesterday, crossing a frozen fen, when she walked three steps ahead without being asked, unerringly, along a path neither of them could see — and when Lena called her back, the girl turned with an expression that was, for one terrible instant, not a child's expression at all. Old. Assessing. *Patient.*

Then it was gone, and the little wren came back, and ran to take Lena's hand, and squeezed it hard, as if holding on to the edge of herself.

"I know you're still in there," Lena told her that night, over a fire of gorse and fence-posts, in the lee of a boundary stone carved with spirals so ancient they'd worn to suggestions. "I know it. Whatever they're saying through you, whatever they're making you say — you hold on. You hear me? You hold on to my voice and you pull the other way."

The child nodded. Then, hesitantly, she touched her own chest, over the sternum, and made a shape in the air with both hands: a jar. A small, round, closed thing.

"I know," Lena said quietly. "I'm going to get it back. I signed nothing yet, but I'll sign anything they put in front of me, if that's what it takes. Do you hear me? Anything."

The child studied her for a long moment with those too-old eyes, and then shook her head — slowly, deliberately — and pointed at Lena, and then at the ground, and then drew in the ash at the fire's edge a careful spiral, and inside the spiral, a smaller mark: a person-shape, standing.

*You belong here,* the gesture said. *Not down there.*

"You let me worry about where I belong," Lena said, and her voice cracked on the last word, because the truth was she hadn't belonged anywhere in seven years, and the one place she'd belonged was burning behind her in a valley that might already have voted her out of existence.

The child leaned against her shoulder. Above them the stars wheeled, hard and cold, and the mountain breathed its cloud-hood in and out, in and out, like something sleeping that was not asleep at all.

That night the child dreamed aloud again, and this time Lena caught a phrase repeated over and over, soft as a lullaby, terrible as a verdict. She wrote it down by firelight, sounding it out, and beside it, in the space where meaning should go, she wrote the only translation her bones would give her:

*Almost. Almost. Almost ours.*

* * *

On the ninth day they reached the last village before the mountain's shadow, a huddle of grey houses around a well that had frozen from the bottom up. The villagers watched them pass from behind shutters. An old woman crossed herself with the wrong hand — the old way, the pre-kingdom way, palm inward — and muttered something that made the child stop dead in the street and turn her head sharply, listening.

"What?" Lena asked. "What did she say?"

The child didn't answer. But that night, in the stable loft where they sheltered, she spoke in her sleep with a fluency that had stopped frightening Lena and started breaking her heart instead — because it was beautiful, the old language, it was music, it was the sound of a whole civilization folded into a child's mouth, and every fluent sentence was another degree of temperature the girl lost. She was warmer to the court and colder to the world with every night. Lena could chart it like a fever. Three nights ago the sleep-speech had been broken, halting, half-gasped. Tonight it poured out steady and silver, hour upon hour, and when the child woke at dawn she looked at Lena for a moment before recognition arrived — one moment, one long unlit moment, in which Lena was a stranger and a warmth-source and possibly a meal.

Then recognition came, and the girl flung herself into Lena's arms, and Lena held her and lied.

"It's all right," she said, over the child's head, staring at the mountain filling the window, close enough now to see the seams in its rock, the drowned mine-shaft Maren's cipher had described, the black slot in the snowfield like a pupil. "I've got you. I've got you."

But her hands were shaking, because she understood now what she had been refusing to understand since the chapel. This wasn't a rescue anymore. It was a race. Every mile north, the court's grip tightened and the child thinned, stretched between two anchors, and there would come a point — soon, days, maybe hours — when the anchor below outweighed the anchor beside her, and the girl who pressed close to her in the dark would simply not wake up as anyone at all, and the mountain would have collected what it was owed, politely, patiently, without ever lifting a hand.

"Tomorrow," Lena whispered into the child's hair. "Tomorrow we go down. I don't care what it costs. I don't care what they take. Tomorrow."

The child pulled back and looked at her — and smiled, a real smile, gap-toothed and crooked, the little wren blazing defiantly in her eyes — and then she raised one small hand and pointed past Lena's shoulder, at the window, at the snowfield, and said, wide awake, in the language before kingdoms, in a voice layered with a chorus underneath it:

*"She is already here."*

Lena turned.

At the edge of the firelight, standing in the trampled snow of the yard, was a figure holding a lantern — and it was not Tam Voss.
