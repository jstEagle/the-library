## 15. The Descent

The mine-shaft had been drowning for a hundred years before we found it.

It opened in the mountainside like a mouth that had forgotten how to close—a square-cut arch of black stone, half-swallowed by a waterfall that fell from a lip of ice above and vanished into the dark below. The water did not splash at the bottom. It simply went down, and kept going down, and the sound it made was not the sound of water falling but of something being counted.

Nell stopped at the edge and put her hand flat against the wet stone. Her fingers spread. She pressed her palm there the way a child presses a palm to a window when someone she loves is on the other side.

"It knows you," I said quietly. "I know."

She looked up at me. In the grey light of the mountain's shoulder her eyes were the color of deep water over pale stone, and they were more present than they had been in days—which frightened me more than the emptiness had. The court beneath the mountain was waking her up as we approached, filling her like a lamp being carried back to its table. Every mile north, she slept less and spoke more in that pre-kingdom tongue, syllables like river-stones turning over, and every mile north I understood that what woke her was not rescue. It was retrieval. The mountain was calling its property home, and the property was beginning to answer.

But she was still here. Still hers, in whatever fraction remained. When I took her hand she gripped back, and that grip was the whole of my map now.

"We go through the water," I told her. "There's a road under it. Maren's cipher said so, and Maren was right about everything except the things she lied about." I heard how that sounded and didn't correct it. Seven years of grief does not unravel in a single letter read by lantern-light; it unravels slowly, thread by thread, and some threads you have to leave knotted just to keep walking.

We stepped into the fall together.

The cold was total. It went through wool and skin and thought. But the shaft's throat was cut with stairs—old ones, miner's stairs worn to shallow curves—and the water sheeted down outside them in a grey curtain, so that we descended inside a column of falling silver with the mountain's black rock at our backs. Nell's hand in mine was the only warm thing in the world. I counted the steps because counting was what I did instead of screaming: two hundred and forty, three hundred and ten, four hundred and six. At five hundred the curtain of water ended and the stair kept going without it, and the dark below was no longer empty.

It was furnished.

* * *

Lanterns hung from iron hooks along the walls—dozens of them, hundreds, receding downward into a glow that never quite became light. Each lantern held a flame of a different color: amber, green, a bruised violet, one that burned white and utterly silent. And between the lanterns stood the courts.

I had imagined monsters. I want to be honest about that, because honesty was about to become the only currency I had left. I had imagined claws and teeth, the simple arithmetic of horror. What waited on the drowned stairs instead were clerks.

They were roughly human-shaped the way a coat is roughly body-shaped. They wore robes of ledger-paper, pages sewn edge to edge, and the pages moved when they moved, rustling with entries in scripts that hurt to look at sideways. Their faces were smooth as wax where a face should be, and where features should be there were only impressions—the suggestion of eyes, the ghost of a mouth—as if faces had been pressed into them long ago and peeled away. One turned toward us as we came down the last flight, and though it had no eyes I felt myself being read. Not seen. Read. Line by line.

"Traveler," it said. Its voice was the sound of a page turning. "Descending?"

"Passing through," I said. My voice sounded very small and very alive in all that paper rustle. "To the archive."

"Everything passes through," said the clerk. "Nothing merely passes." It extended a hand whose fingers were strips of vellum. "Toll."

"I don't have coin."

"Coin is for markets." The clerk's blank face tilted, courteous as a knife laid flat on a table. "This is a court. We take payment in kind. You are carrying memories. Everyone is. We will take one."

Nell's grip tightened on my hand hard enough to grind the small bones.

"Which one?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because asking meant negotiating, and negotiating meant I had already agreed.

"The court chooses. Not you." A pause, and the paper robes shivered. "Though I will tell you this much, traveler, as courtesy between parties: we do not take load-bearing memories. A soul stripped of those collapses, and collapsed souls make poor witnesses. We take ornaments. We take the small bright things." The clerk gestured, almost gently, toward the dark behind me—the way I'd come. "You may turn back. The toll is only owed by those who descend."

Behind me: the stair, the waterfall, the valley, seven years of safety bought with invisibility. Ahead: the archive, the ledger, a child's stolen self, and a deadline measured in the shortening of days.

I thought of Maren's letter. *Interest compounds, little bird. That is the whole of their power—they are patient, and everything you love accrues.*

"Take it," I said.

The clerk reached out and touched my temple with one strip of vellum, and it did not hurt, which was the worst part. There was a sensation like a bookmark being slid out of a well-read book. And then—

Gone.

I stood there grasping for it. Something warm. Something with rain in it, and a wooden porch, and—I couldn't find the edges of it. I knew I had lost something. I could feel the hole, smooth-walled as a pearl inside a shell. But the thing itself wouldn't come when called. I knew only that once, somewhere, there had been an afternoon with rain on a porch, and someone had laughed, and it had mattered, and now it was filed.

"What did you take?" My voice cracked on it.

"An afternoon," said the clerk, already turning away, already losing interest in me the way a bank loses interest in a paid account. "Rain. Laughter. It was a good one." The lantern nearest us guttered, and I saw—for one horrible instant—that its flame had taken on a new color, something golden and domestic, and I understood what fed these lights. "Next court is below. Mind the third landing. They deal in shadows, and they are less polite than we are."

* * *

The third landing dealt in shadows, and it was less polite.

The shadow-court occupied a chamber where the stair widened into a gallery, and its members did not wear paper. They wore darkness itself, draped and tailored, and they had faces—borrowed ones. As we entered, one of them wore my face. It sat behind a desk of black stone, wearing my tiredness, my crow's-feet, the scar on my jaw from a scythe gone wild in my first harvest year, and it smiled with my smile, and the smile was wrong the way a reflection is wrong when the mirror has opinions.

"Lena Ishikawa," it said in my voice. "Or the shape of her debts. Sit."

"I'll stand."

"You'll stand. They always stand at first." It laced my fingers together in front of my own face. "Your toll is heavier here. We are senior creditors. We hold senior claims." It leaned forward. "Give us a shadow."

"A shadow."

"Everyone casts one. Most people carry several. Regrets are shadows. Shames are shadows. That version of yourself you might have been, if you'd been braver or worse—shadow." My own face studied me with my own eyes. "We will take one. You will feel lighter. Everyone says they feel lighter. Lightness is the trap of our trade, but it is also real, and we do not apologize for either."

Nell tugged my sleeve. She pointed past the desk, down the gallery, and I followed her finger and wished I hadn't. Along the walls stood other travelers—or what remained of them. People who had paid too many times. They walked, they breathed, they obeyed the small motions of living, but their shadows lay flat beneath them like spilled ink, thin and starved, and their eyes had the particular vacancy of houses from which the furniture has been sold off room by room. Hollowed. Not the way Nell was hollowed—not emptied by theft from outside, but spent from within, installment by installment, each payment reasonable, each receipt signed willingly.

One of them, an old man with a peddler's pack still strapped to his back, looked at me and mouthed something. I read it more than heard it: *Keep count. Keep count or you won't know what's missing.*

"Your decision, Ishikawa," said the thing wearing my face.

I thought about which shadows I owned. There were so many. The night I ran from Seraquine's household with burns on my hands and another girl's screams behind me. The morning I found Maren dead and felt, beneath the grief, a shameful flicker of freedom. The letter in the cipher-box, and the way part of me had been relieved—*relieved*—to learn my hiding place had been built on a lie, because a lie meant the fear had been optional all along and I had chosen it anyway.

"Take the night I ran," I said. "The running itself. Not the reason. Not the girl who screamed. Just—" I swallowed. "Just the door closing behind me."

The shadow-court inclined my head, graciously, monstrously. "A clean extraction. You negotiate better than your teacher did."

It rose, crossed to me, and drew its hands through the air at my back like a tailor measuring. Then it pulled. There was no pain. There was a sound like a long exhale, and something detached from me that I had carried so long I'd mistaken it for a spine. For a moment I felt wonderful. That was the trap, and I knew it was the trap, and I felt wonderful anyway—light, unburdened, the door-closing gone, the guilt of survival gone, seven years of flinching gone.

Then the lightness settled, and underneath it I found the hole, and in the hole I found the truth: I had given them the night I became a survivor. Whatever was left of me now had survived without ever having run. The math of that would come due later. Debts always do.

"Descend," said the court, sitting back down behind its stone desk, my face folding away into darkness. "And traveler—the archive keeps late hours this season. Solstice hours. Hurry."

* * *

Below the shadow-gallery the stair gave up pretending to be a stair and became a road, wide and dry in the heart of the drowned mountain, walled with stone that glittered faintly like salt. And here the minor courts thickened until the road itself was a market of obligations.

They traded in first words.

That was the next toll, at a booth hardly bigger than a confessional, staffed by a creature like a folded letter with a voice like a struck bell. First words, it explained—the first word a person ever spoke, prized above rubies, because a first word is the moment a soul first shaped the world with its mouth. Mine had been "light," Maren told me once, teasing me across the herb-beds: *you came into the world asking for lamps, little bird.* I paid it. I don't remember paying it. That is the nature of the transaction—I know the receipt exists because I wrote it in my own journal that night, my handwriting slanting drunk with loss: *Paid the bell-thing. It took my first word. I no longer know what it was. Maren says it was 'light.' I believe her. Believing is not remembering.*

Then a court that traded in names-not-yet-given—the names our mothers considered and discarded, the selves we might have been christened into. I paid two of those and felt nothing at all, which frightened me more than grief would have.

Then a court that wanted a color. Not any color—a specific one, the exact shade of a sky on a day I could no longer fully summon. I argued with that one. I actually argued, standing in the salt-glitter dark while Nell hid her face against my coat, telling a folded mass of contractual appetite that it could not have the blue of a morning in the valley, that some things were not for sale. The court waited, patient as interest. And then I paid it anyway, because Nell's hand was cold, and the solstice was closer, and patience was a luxury of people who still had afternoons left to lose.

By the time the road began to slope down toward the great heatless glow of the mountain's heart, I had begun keeping the list the old peddler taught me. I wrote it by lantern-light in the margins of Maren's cipher-letter, my hand growing less certain with each entry:

*Paid: one rainy afternoon (laughter). Paid: the night I ran. Paid: my first word. Paid: two unworn names. Paid: one blue morning. Paid: the smell of Maren's kitchen—thyme, woodsmoke, burnt honey. Paid: the sound of the valley wind in the thistles, which I had thought was the sound of my own name being said kindly.*

I stared at that last line a long time. I could see the words. I could not find the wind. Somewhere below us, in a lantern or a ledger or a locked drawer of some clerk's gray attention, a wind was blowing in a field of salt-thistle, and it would blow forever without me.

Nell watched me write. Then she took the pencil from my hand—gently, the way she did everything, as if the world were made of eggshell—and beneath my list she drew the spiral sigil. But this time she added to it. Small marks, radiating outward, deliberate as stitches. And beside it, in her pre-kingdom tongue, she wrote a line of those river-stone characters, and then, slowly, copying the shapes of my letters, she wrote a word in mine:

*enough*

I looked at her. She looked at me, and her eyes were deep water over pale stone, and she was being pulled home with every step and she knew it, and she had drawn me a boundary in a language older than ledgers, as if to say: *this far, and no further. Whatever they take, keep enough.*

"Nell," I said—it was the name I'd given her, valley-named, hedge-born, mine to give—and her chin trembled. "When we get there, they're going to offer me deals. Terrible ones dressed as kind ones. I need you to remember something even if I forget it." I took her face in my hands. "Whatever they ask me to sign—whatever it costs—you are worth it. Say you understand."

She didn't say anything. She couldn't, not in my tongue, not yet. Instead she pressed her small fist against her own chest, over the place where something bright had once been taken, and then pressed the same fist against mine.

Understood. Filed. Witnessed.

* * *

The last court before the archive was the smallest, and it nearly broke me.

It consisted of a single figure seated on a stool at the final bend of the road, an old woman made of candle-smoke, tending a lantern that held a flame the color of a hearth in winter. She did not ask for a toll. She simply looked up at me with smoke-gray eyes and said, in Maren's voice:

"You've done well, little bird."

I stopped as if struck. "You're not her."

"No," said the smoke-woman agreeably. "I'm a collector of last words. Yours isn't due yet. But I hold others', and I'm permitted to lend one out, on occasion, to travelers who look like they're about to do something stupid and necessary." She lifted the lantern, and the hearth-colored flame bent toward me, and for one breath I smelled thyme and woodsmoke and burnt honey—wait, no, I had paid that, I had *paid* that—but the flame remembered it for me, lent it back to me for a moment like a coat returned from pawn. "Maren's last words," the smoke-woman said. "Spoken alone, in her cottage, to an empty room, three days before she died. Do you want them? Lending is free. Keeping costs."

"Yes," I said. "Yes."

The flame touched my ear like a fingertip, and Maren's voice, hoarse and unhurried and unbearably fond, said: *"Let them take the ornaments, little bird. Load-bearing memories only. And when they show you the ledger—read the margins. Nobody reads the margins. That's where I hid everything worth knowing."*

The flame withdrew. The hearth-smell faded. The smoke-woman nodded once, satisfied, and went back to tending her light.

The margins. Of course. Maren, who had hidden a cipher in a recipe for burn-salve and a confession in a seed inventory. Maren, who had bought back her own soul at terrible interest and spent her last decades teaching a runaway girl how to stay invisible—while all along knowing the girl was the one thing in the valley that could never afford to be.

Ahead, the road ended at a door.

It was not grand. That was the final obscenity of the place. After the drowned stair and the paper courts and the market of obligations, the door to the archive at the heart of the mountain was a plain oak door with an iron handle, the kind you'd find on a pantry, a schoolroom, a cottage. Above it, in script that predated kingdoms, hung a sign that translated itself directly into my understanding, the way the deepest magic skips language entirely:

*ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED HERE. NO ONE LEAVES UNBALANCED.*

Nell's hand found mine. Through the oak, faint as a pulse under skin, I could hear the sound of pages turning—thousands of them, millions, a sound like the mountain breathing. And beneath it, clearer now, rising like water in a lock, I heard the chorus. Many voices. Some of them children's.

One of them was humming the melody Nell had hummed on my doorstep, the night the salt lamps guttered.

I put my hand on the iron handle. It was warm. Everything down here that should have been cold was warm, and everything warm had a price.

"Read the margins," I whispered, and opened the door.
