## 19. Interest Due

The girl who was not yet Nell knew the taste of the mountain's hunger because she had been fed to it once already.

It came up through the floor of the ledger room like water through a grave. Not cold — the court beneath the mountain had no temperature, only *weight*, the pressure of ten thousand held things pressing down through stone and salt and the thin bright line of Lena Ishikawa's spine.

Lena stood at the center of the chalked circle, and the circle was drinking her.

The child saw it in fragments, the way she saw everything now — half of her soul had come home from the mountain's vault two nights past, and a half-soul sees like light through cracked ice. She saw Lena's hands first: they were going gray at the fingertips, color draining out of them in slow tides, as if someone were unpainting her from the ends inward. She saw the threads. Everyone else in the great dark hall saw only a woman standing very still while old voices murmured around her. The child saw what the threads were doing.

They came out of Lena's chest like spun glass. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a memory, a day, a name, a warmth — and each one was being wound onto a spool that had no shape anyone living had ever seen, turning and turning in the air above the master ledger, gathering the long bright rope of a life.

*This is how it was done to me,* the child thought, in the language before names, the language that had no word for *I* separate from *we*. *This is how it felt. This is the machine.*

She had forgotten that feeling on purpose. Now she remembered it whole, and it was worse than whole.

* * *

The chorus spoke from everywhere. It had spoken from everywhere since the moment Lena signed — the sovereign of the court beneath the mountain was not a king or a queen but an accumulation, every soul it held layered into one voice, and tonight the voice was almost gentle.

*"You feel it beginning,"* the chorus said. *"Good. Do not fight the winding. Fighting makes knots, and knots make pain."*

Lena did not answer. Her jaw was set the way it set when she was counting stores in winter, deciding how much could be spent and still survive. The child knew that look. She had watched it across a small kitchen for a season now, over salt-thistle tea and burnt bread, and she knew that behind it Lena was doing arithmetic with herself.

*How many memories can I lose,* the look said, *and still be someone who can walk out of here?*

The spool turned. A thread paid off it and vanished into the dark, and Lena swayed, and the child felt the loss arrive in her own chest like a door slamming somewhere far away in a house she loved.

She tasted what went. Not the content of it — the court kept the contents — but the *shape* of the absence. A morning-shaped absence. Salt-thistle and steam. A kitchen gone suddenly larger and emptier than it should be.

The child understood then, with the terrible clarity of the half-returned, that the court was not taking Lena's memories at random. It was taking them in order. Oldest first. The valley first. It was unwinding her from the place she had built her life inside, so that by dawn there would be nothing left anchoring the woman to anything but the debt itself.

*It is eating her home out of her,* the child thought. *So that when she leaves, she will not know why she weeps.*

Something in her — the piece that had been sold away and bought back, the piece that had spent seven years hollow and had learned exactly what hollowness cost — went hard as a salt crystal.

No.

* * *

The pre-kingdom tongue had a word the courts had never managed to tax.

It was not a large word. In the mouths of the people who first spoke it, before kingdoms, before ledgers, before anyone thought to weigh a soul against silver, it had been less a word than a gesture — the sound hands made when they closed around another hand. The sound a fire made when everyone finally sat down around it. The sound of *you are where you are supposed to be, and where you are supposed to be is here, with us.*

There was no word for it in any living language. There was barely a word for it in the dead ones. Maren's buried papers had called it, in a cramped marginal hand, *the belonging-word*, and warned in the next line: *never sing it where something hungry can hear. They cannot own it. That is precisely why they fear it.*

The child opened her mouth.

Her voice, since her soul had come half home, was still rough, still rusted from seven years of silence. But the old language did not live in the throat. It lived lower, in the place where the bright thing had been taken from her chest and returned, and when she sang, the note came out round and warm and utterly strange in the vast cold archive — a sound like bread, like lamplight, like a door opening instead of closing.

The spool stopped turning.

Every thread on it shivered.

*"What,"* said the chorus, and for the first time in two hundred years, the sovereign of the court beneath the mountain sounded uncertain. *"What is she doing."*

The child stepped forward, bare feet silent on stone, and put both her small hands against Lena's back, between the shoulder blades, right where the threads were being drawn out. And she sang the belonging-word again, and this time she did not sing it at the court.

She sang it *into* Lena.

* * *

Inside the winding, Lena heard it.

She was deep enough in now that the archive had begun to feel like home — that was the cruelest part, the way the dark grew comfortable, the way forgetting felt like rest. She had lost the smell of Maren's kitchen. She had lost the weight of the first snow on the morning the child arrived at her door. She was losing, thread by thread, the reason she had come down the drowned mine-shaft at all; the ledger room was becoming just a room, the debt just a number, the girl just a stranger crying behind her.

Then the song found her.

It did not push the darkness away. Nothing could have pushed it away; the contract was signed, the interest was due, the machine was lawful and ancient and patient. But the song did something the darkness could not digest. It *anchored*. Each thread, as it was wound away, hummed with the belonging-word on its way out — so that even the memories being taken left carrying her name in their pockets. Even the losses knew who they belonged to.

And the gray creeping up her fingers stopped.

Lena turned her head. She could not see well anymore — sight was one of the cheaper things, and the court had collected some of it already — but she saw the child pressed against her back, small and blazing, singing a word that made the spool tremble like a trapped moth.

"Nell," Lena said. Her voice came out in pieces. "Nell, don't — whatever you're giving me, don't —"

The child sang louder.

Because that was the unthinkable thing, the thing the chorus had not accounted for, the flaw in a machine two centuries perfect: the child was not merely singing. She was *paying*. Half of her soul had been restored, and a restored soul is not a fixed quantity — it is a fire, and fires can be shared. With every verse she gave a piece of her own hard-won brightness into the anchor-hold, feeding it into the threads, so that when the court pulled Lena's memories away, they pulled against something too full, too warm, too *held* to come loose cleanly.

She was buying Lena back with the only coin she had.

The coin she had been sold for.

*"Stop,"* said the chorus, and the gentleness was gone. *"That is not yours to give. That is not how the instrument works. Child, you are devaluing collateral—"*

The child sang the belonging-word a third time, and the stone floors rang with it, and far above, through two hundred fathoms of mountain, something answered.

* * *

Tam Voss had walked three days on legs that hated him, following a lantern he had carried to more funerals than weddings, and when the mountain's side opened for him — because it opened, the drowned shaft breathing him in like it had been waiting — he did not question it. Old men who have burned one decree learn not to argue with signs.

He climbed down into the dark with his lantern raised and his heart going like a boy's, and the minor creditors in the toll-courts looked at him and let him pass without taking anything, which frightened him more than taking would have. One of them, a thing shaped like a shadow of a shadow, leaned close as he passed and whispered, in a voice like paper:

"*They're singing down there. We haven't heard that in an age.*"

At the last door, the ledger room's threshold, Tam stopped. He was not a brave man. He had never been a brave man; he had been a *present* man, which in a valley like Saltmere amounted to nearly the same thing over a long enough life. He looked at his lantern. He looked at the dark beyond it, where a woman he'd quietly admired for seven years was being unwound like a sweater, and a mute child was holding the whole ancient machine at bay with a song.

"Well," Tam Voss said to nobody. "Can't let the little one do all the work."

He raised the lantern high.

Now — a lantern is a small light. Any honest person in Saltmere would tell you so. But Tam's lantern had lit forty years of council meetings, and vigils, and the windows of houses where people sat up with the dying. It had been carried, unextinguished, through the worst storm in the valley's memory. And it had been carried tonight by a man who had chosen, at the end of his life, to burn a decree rather than obey it — and a flame like that carries something with it. Not magic. The salt had seen to that, all these years. Something older than magic. Something the pre-kingdom tongue had a word for, and the courts had never managed to tax.

The lantern-light reached the ledger room and touched the child's song, and the song took it the way dry ground takes rain.

* * *

The machine strained.

The child saw it strain — saw the spool shudder against a rope of memories that would not come free, because every thread of Lena Ishikawa's life now ran through an anchor made of a child's shared soul and an old man's stubborn flame, and the anchor sang *belong, belong, belong* in a language older than debt.

*"You cannot,"* the chorus said, and it was not one voice anymore; it was many, fraying at the edges, souls within it stirring like sleepers hearing their own names. *"The contract is sealed. The signature stands. Interest is owed. You cannot simply —"*

The child stopped singing.

The silence was enormous.

Then she spoke — aloud, in the old tongue, her first fluent sentence in seven years, and the chorus translated it whether it wished to or not, because the court understood all languages and was therefore helpless before this one:

*"A soul freely given cannot be wholly owned. Your own law. Your oldest law. You wrote it yourselves, before you were a machine, back when you were only people who were afraid. Look at what you are pulling. Look at what it is tangled with. You cannot collect her without collecting us — and you cannot collect us, because we are not hers to lose. We gave ourselves. Freely."*

She lifted her chin at the spool, at the trembling threads, at the vast blind weight of the sovereign.

*"So. Renegotiate."*

* * *

For a long moment, nothing in the ledger room moved except the lantern-flame.

Then, deep in the chorus, something shifted — a settling, a grinding, the sound of an institution two hundred years old discovering a clause it had hoped everyone forgot. The threads of Lena's life slackened, just slightly. Just enough.

And beside the master ledger, on the great stone lectern where the court recorded everything it had ever held, a blank page began, slowly, of its own accord, to fill with writing.

Lena, gray-handed and half-unmade, squinted at it through failing eyes. Tam lowered his lantern closer. The child read it easily, in the language before names, and began, softly, to translate — because the words appearing on the page were older than the court's script, older than kingdoms, and they were not terms of forfeiture.

They were terms of *exchange*.

The chorus drew breath — ten thousand souls drawing one breath — and spoke, and its voice had changed: quieter now, warier, the voice of a creditor who has just realized the debtor has read the fine print.

*"Very well, daughter of Saltmere. Let us discuss what you will surrender — and what, in two hundred years, no one has ever asked us to return."*

The page turned itself.

Lena stepped toward it, Nell's hand locked in hers, and the child felt the woman's grip — and felt, beneath it, faint as a pulse, the shape of what the court wanted next, and knew it would cost Lena something no contract had ever named before.

The writing finished. The ink gleamed.

"Read it to me," Lena whispered. "Nell. Read it to me slowly."
