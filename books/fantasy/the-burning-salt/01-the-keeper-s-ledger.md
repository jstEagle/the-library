## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The trouble with knowing every stone on a road is that you notice when one of them changes.

Juniper Whitlock had walked the smuggler's path from the Saltgate down into Thornfell perhaps four thousand times in his thirty-one years, and he could have done it blindfolded, drunk, and bleeding. He had, in fact, done it all three at once once, though that was a story Bram told better than he did. The path dropped through pine shadow and scree, skirted the old millrace where the water ran white and cold even in high summer, and came out behind the tannery yards where the smell of lye and hides covered any number of sins. It was a good road for goods that didn't want to meet a toll ledger, and Juniper was a good man to walk it, because he was the keeper of the gate above and therefore the last person anyone would suspect of moving contraband beneath it.

That was the joke he never made out loud. The keeper running the smuggling routes himself. His mother would have had something to say about it. His mother had had something to say about everything, right up until the fever took her eleven years ago, and most of what she'd said had been warnings.

*Count them,* she used to tell him, when he was small enough to ride her shoulders up the switchbacks. *Every traveler, every face. A keeper who doesn't count is just a man standing in a doorway.*

So he counted. He counted the way other men prayed — without deciding to, without being able to stop.

This morning's tally, entered in his head with the same dry precision he gave his actual ledger back at the gatehouse: two drovers bringing salt pork down from the high pastures, legal, bored, arguing about a dog. One peddler with a cart of tinware who was definitely also carrying brandy under the false bottom, because Juniper had built three of those false bottoms himself and recognized the particular way a cart sat on its axles. Four pilgrims.

He slowed at that last entry. Pilgrims were rare. Pilgrims to *Thornfell* were rarer still, because Thornfell had no relics, no saints' bones, no famous spring. What it had was a scatter of old shrine stones along the valley roads — waist-high standing stones worn smooth as soap, carved with spirals no living mason could cut — and everyone knew those were just markers. Waystones. Nothing holy about them. The gods were gone; everyone agreed on this the way everyone agreed on the color of the sky. Whatever had once walked through the Saltgate before it was sealed had been gone so long that the sealing itself had passed out of memory and into the category of things that simply were, like winter, like taxes, like death.

But four pilgrims, walking the valley road with their hoods back and their faces tilted toward the shrine stone by the millrace as if they expected it to say something.

Juniper stopped in the alder shade and watched them, and felt the thing he always felt near the stones: a low hum behind his sternum, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Recognition. That was the word he never let himself use. He called it *affinity*, when he called it anything, and he kept it buried the way he kept everything else buried.

Because here was the truth of Juniper Whitlock, the truth he had never spoken whole to any living soul: the shrine stones liked him. They warmed under his palm. When he touched them, the spiral carvings seemed to turn, very slowly, the way a sleeping man turns toward a voice. Once, years ago, drunk on Bram's plum brandy, he had pressed both hands flat against the great stone by the ford and whispered *hello* — just hello, stupid and lonely — and the frost on the grass around it had melted in a perfect circle, in midwinter, in seconds.

He had gone home and been sick in the yard. Then he had gotten up the next morning and checked the road, and counted the travelers, and told no one, because his mother's last coherent words to him had been:

*If they ever learn what runs in you, they will not burn the god, Juniper. They'll burn the boy.*

He hadn't understood it then. He was fifteen and she was already half-gone, her mind swimming somewhere he couldn't follow, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise. He understood it now, mostly. There were people — the Order of the Iron Bell foremost among them — who had made a life's work of ensuring the gods stayed gone, and they did not distinguish finely between a god and a man with god in him. Ash was ash.

So he touched no stones where anyone could see. He healed nothing, warmed nothing, melted nothing. He was the gatekeeper: taciturn, reliable, faintly dull, a man whose greatest vice was an arrangement with the tannery to move untaxed wool. He had spent eleven years building that man, plank by plank, and he lived inside him the way a snail lives in its shell, and most days he almost believed the shell was him.

* * *

The gatehouse sat at the throat of the pass like a fist clenched around the world's oldest secret. Below it, the valley opened green and ordinary. Above it, the pass climbed between walls of grey rock streaked white with mineral salt — the burning salt, the old-timers called it, though none of them remembered why — until it reached the seal.

The seal looked, to any traveler, like nothing at all. A wall of rubble across the pass, ancient, unremarkable, the kind of collapse mountains make on their own. Only Juniper knew that not one stone of it had ever shifted, not in his lifetime, not in his mother's, not since the day it was laid. Rain fell on it and went nowhere. Snow piled on it and slid off in perfect sheets. Once a rockslide had come down the south face with enough force to shear a pine off at the roots, and the debris had parted around the seal like water around a prow.

And underneath the rubble, when he pressed his hand to it at night when no one watched, Juniper could feel a heartbeat. Slow. Vast. Patient in a way that frightened him more than hunger would have.

His mother had taught him the maintenance rites as chores, disguised as chores: clear the drainage channel, check the mortar lines, oil the gatehouse hinges, and *never, ever sing on the mountain*. He had thought the last one was grief or madness. He sang anyway, sometimes, alone, just to spite her ghost — and the first time he did, the heartbeat under the rubble had quickened, one single excited pulse, like a dog hearing its name.

He didn't sing anymore.

He reached the gatehouse by late afternoon and found Bram waiting on the step, which meant either business or trouble, and with Bram it was usually both.

"You've got pilgrims coming up your road," Bram said, without preamble. He was a big man going soft at the edges, with a smuggler's beard and a customs officer's memory for faces. "Four of them. And there's talk of more. Whole columns of them, coming up from the low country. Something about prayers being answered."

"Prayers are answered all the time," Juniper said. "Coincidence has good PR."

Bram grunted. "These are specific. Shepherd up past Highfell lost his flock — wolves, everyone said, dead loss, the man was going to hang himself over it. Prayed at the broken altar on the moor, the old one nobody's tended in a hundred years. Woke up next morning and his flock was standing in his field. Every ewe. Not one missing." Bram scratched his beard. "Wolves don't give back."

Juniper kept his face still, which took work, because something behind his ribs had gone cold and quick. "People embellish."

"People do. But the shepherd doesn't drink, and I bought a lamb off him yesterday, and it's real enough to eat." Bram shrugged. "Anyway. Word travels. Where word travels, pilgrims follow, and where pilgrims follow—" he spread his hands, "—the Iron Bell follows the pilgrims. You want to keep your ledger clean, Whitlock, this is the season to do it."

"I always keep my ledger clean."

"You keep it *quiet*. Different thing." Bram stood, joints cracking, and clapped Juniper on the shoulder on his way down the steps. His hand paused there a half-second longer than usual. "You look tired lately. Sleeping bad?"

"Frost on the windows," Juniper lied. "In summer. Keeps me up."

It wasn't entirely a lie. Three mornings that week he had woken with rime on the inside of the shutters, delicate fern-patterns of it, while the valley below sweltered. He had scraped it away and burned the cloth each time and told himself it was the mountain's breath through old stone. Mountains breathed. Everyone knew that too.

Bram laughed and went down toward the village, and Juniper stood on his own doorstep watching him go, counting out of habit — one friend, walking away, carrying news he shouldn't have shared so freely — and then went inside to light the lamp and open the real ledger, the honest one, the one with tolls and wool weights, because the ritual of it steadied him. Ink. Columns. Numbers that behaved.

He was halfway down the page when he realized he had written, in his neat keeper's hand, in the margin beside the day's date, a single word he had no memory of writing:

*Soon.*

He stared at it for a long time. The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there. And far below, down in the valley by the millrace, he could have sworn — though he couldn't have sworn it, he hadn't seen it, he'd only felt it through forty years of stone and his own unwilling skin — that one of the shrine stones had just turned over in its sleep.

He closed the ledger. Outside, the first stars were coming up over the pass, and the seal's slow heartbeat, for the first time in eleven years, was keeping time with his own.
