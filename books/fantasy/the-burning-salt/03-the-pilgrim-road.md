## 3. The Pilgrim Road

Sable counted heads at dawn the way other people counted coins — out of habit, out of need, and because if she didn't know exactly how many souls she was responsible for, she couldn't protect them.

Forty-one. It had been forty-one when they left the lowlands, and it was forty-one now, which felt like a small miracle all on its own. The Order of the Iron Bell had a way of thinning columns like hers. A way of asking questions in market squares with fire already stacked behind them.

She walked at the front, as she always did, her satchel heavy with the things that kept people alive: willow bark, honey, moldy bread wrapped in wax cloth, a bone needle for stitching flesh, and — folded into a false bottom where no inquisitor would think to look — a hand's-breadth of embroidered cloth bearing a symbol no one had dared weave openly in three generations. A circle broken by a vertical line. The old sign. The gate-sign.

Her grandmother had taught it to her in whispers, stitching it into hems and cuffs, into the lining of collars where only the wearer would feel it against their throat. *Wear it close,* Gran had said. *The gods read what's written on skin before they read what's spoken by mouth.* Sable had been nine years old and hadn't believed her, not really. She believed her now. Belief had a way of arriving late and staying forever.

Behind her, the column straggled along the old drover's road like a wound that refused to close cleanly. There were farmers with callused hands and soft bellies. Two sisters who'd fled a mill town after their brother was taken. An old man named Corvin who claimed to have dreamed the road to Thornfell three times before anyone told him Thornfell existed. Children — there were always children, because children dreamed loudest and the Order knew it, and mothers ran farther than anyone when the dreams started.

They were all running from the same thing. Not the Order itself — you could hide from soldiers, bribe captains, fade into crowds. They were running from the bells. The Order rang them in every town square at every hour, great iron throats that swallowed the air and spat it back out as doctrine: *There are no gods. There have never been gods. Anything that says otherwise is sickness, and sickness is burned out.*

Sable had watched a man burn in Harrowgate for humming a tune his mother had taught him. Just humming. The inquisitors said the melody was an old hymn. He said he didn't remember where he'd learned it. They said that was worse.

So they walked. Forty-one souls walking north and east, toward mountains most of them had never seen, following dreams none of them could explain, toward a pass called Saltgate that half of them couldn't find on any map because no mapmaker honest enough to draw maps had ever been allowed up there.

*Why Thornfell?* Sable asked herself this every morning, and every morning the answer shifted a little, like sand underfoot.

Because of the dreams. That was the true answer, whatever else she told the frightened ones at night when they needed a reason to keep putting one foot past the other. Everyone in the column had dreamed something. Not the same dream — the Order would have called that convenient, and convenient things got investigated — but similar ones. Dreams of a gate. Of salt-white stone. Of a man standing before it, tall, dark-haired, with eyes the color of wet slate, watching them come.

Corvin had dreamed him first. Then the mill sisters. Then a widow named Tessa, then two of the children, independently, in words that made Sable's spine prickle when she compared notes by firelight. *He'll be waiting,* the dreams said. *He knows we're coming.*

A gatekeeper with a face they all recognized and none of them had ever seen.

Sable didn't tell the column everything. A healer learns early that truth is a medicine with dosage instructions, and too much of it, given too fast, can kill as surely as nightshade. If she told forty-one exhausted refugees that they were following a shared hallucination toward a sealed mountain pass guarded by a man from their dreams, some of them would turn around. Some of them would panic. And one or two — the desperate ones, the ones whose faith had curdled into hunger — might start calling her a prophetess, and Sable had seen what happened to prophetesses. The Order didn't burn them any slower for being right.

So she let them believe what they needed to believe. That she had a map. That she had contacts. That the road ahead was hard but known.

Only the road ahead wasn't known. That was the trouble. Nobody she'd ever met had been to Thornfell. The valley sat above the timberline like a cupped hand holding a handful of stubborn villages, and the passes into it were either Order-patrolled or so steep that goats complained about them. The western route meant climbing. Days of climbing, with children, with the lame, with old Corvin whose lungs rattled like a dropped bag of coins.

But the eastern route — the low road, the easy one, the one that followed the river — that road went through Bellhaven, and Bellhaven had a garrison, and garrisons had lists, and Sable's name had been on a list since spring.

She chose the mountain anyway. Faith, she was learning, wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear that kept walking.

* * *

They came down out of the high scrub on the fourth day, and the valley opened below them like a secret finally told.

Sable had imagined Thornfell a hundred times during the climb — built it and rebuilt it in her head from the fragments in the dreams. White stone. Terraced fields clinging to slopes. Smoke rising straight in still air. What she hadn't imagined was the smell: salt, unmistakably, riding the wind up from somewhere below, though the sea was a hundred leagues away and no one could say why a mountain valley should smell of it. The old texts called the pass the Saltgate, and the name suddenly stopped sounding like poetry and started sounding like evidence.

The column stopped on the shoulder of the trail and looked down, and for a full minute nobody spoke. Forty-one people staring at the same green fold of earth, and Sable watching them instead of the valley, because their faces were the real country she'd crossed the mountains to reach.

Old Corvin began to cry. Quietly, the way men of his generation cried, as if embarrassed by the water. Tessa knelt and pressed her palm flat to the dirt of the path. One of the mill sisters — Marta, the younger one, the one who hadn't spoken since her brother was taken — turned her face up to the light and, for the first time in weeks, smiled.

"We're here," someone said, unnecessarily, the way people do.

"No," Sable said, and felt the word land wrong even as she said it, felt the hope in the column flinch. "We're near. There's a village between us and the pass. And villages have eyes."

She wasn't trying to crush them. She was trying to keep them alive, and alive required caution, and caution required that she be the one who said unwelcome things first, so that when the unwelcome things arrived anyway they'd arrive as confirmation rather than betrayal. A healer's trick. Break the bad news gently enough and often enough that the patient survives the diagnosis.

They came down into the valley in the late afternoon, strung out along the drover's track, and the first farmstead saw them before they saw it — a dog first, barking its head off from behind a fence, then a woman standing in a doorway with her arms crossed and her chin set like a fortification. Sable went ahead alone, as she always did at first contact. Alone, she was a pilgrim. With forty-one behind her, she was an invasion.

"Medicine," she said to the woman. "We're healers. We work for food and leave nothing behind but mended bones and delivered babies. You have my word."

The woman looked at her for a long moment — looked through her, more precisely, at the column strung out behind her on the road — and then said something Sable hadn't expected.

"You're not the first." Her voice was flat, uninterested, the voice of someone stating weather. "Others came through last month. Fewer. They went up toward the gate and I don't know what happened to them. Nobody comes back down from the gate."

"The gate," Sable said carefully. "The Saltgate?"

The woman shrugged, which was answer enough, and then added, unprompted, the detail that rearranged Sable's whole understanding of the journey: "The keeper lets people walk the pass. Always has. Smugglers mostly. But lately—" She paused, and her flatness cracked just slightly, like ice over moving water. "Lately there's been talk. Things growing where nothing grew. A shepherd's flock coming home overnight. The shrine stones warm to the touch, and it's midsummer." She shook her head. "I don't hold with talk. But my grandmother held with it, and she'd have said the mountain's waking up."

Sable felt something turn over inside her chest, slow and enormous, like a stone rolling in a streambed.

*The mountain's waking up.*

She'd spent her whole life believing the opposite. Believing it as doctrine, as grief, as inheritance: the gods were gone, the world was closed, faith was a memory you carried like a scar. The Order hadn't invented that belief — they'd just weaponized it. And here was a farmer's wife, a woman with no doctrine at all, saying casually, resentfully, over a fence, that the impossible was happening in the next valley over, and being annoyed about it.

"What's the keeper's name?" Sable asked.

"Whitlock. Juniper Whitlock." The woman's mouth did something complicated. "Good man. Fair trader. Keeps to himself." A beat. "Too much, maybe."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow above the river, and Sable walked the perimeter twice before letting herself sit, and then she didn't sit — she went from fire to fire, checking the sick, rewrapping the blistered feet of the children, mixing willow bark into the evening broth. This was the part of leadership no one sang about: not the visions, not the revelations, just the endless arithmetic of bodies. Who was feverish. Who was pregnant. Who hadn't eaten. Who was quietly planning to slip away in the night because the mountain scared them more than the bells did.

It was while she was kneeling at the second fire, lancing a boil on a drover's neck, that she heard the singing.

One voice. Thin. Coming from the direction of the road they'd climbed. Marta — the silent sister, the one who hadn't spoken since Harrowgate — was sitting apart from everyone, facing east, toward the pass, and she was singing. Softly. Badly. A melody Sable recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd you've never met: instantly, totally, wrongly.

It was the hymn. The one the man had burned for humming.

Sable set down her lancet and crossed the grass without hurrying, because hurrying would have been a kind of admission. She sat beside Marta in the dark and listened until the verse ended.

"Where did you learn that?" she asked.

Marta didn't answer right away. She was looking at the pass — at the notch of white stone visible even from here, catching the last grey light like a tooth of the moon — and when she finally spoke, it was the first time Sable had heard her voice in nineteen days.

"I didn't learn it anywhere. It's been in my head since we crossed the tree line. Like someone left it there." She turned to Sable, and her eyes were wide and calm in a way that frightened Sable more than madness would have. "He's real, isn't he? The man in the dreams. The one at the gate. I saw him today. On the road, far off, standing where the path forks. Watching us. Not following. Just — watching. Like he was counting us too."

Sable had seen him too. She hadn't said so. A figure on the ridge above the third switchback, dark-haired, alone, standing very still in the manner of someone who has stood in that exact place ten thousand times. He'd watched the column pass below him for the better part of an hour — she'd marked him through the trees, half-convinced he was a scout, a bandit, an Order outrider in plain clothes. But bandits didn't stand still. Outriders didn't stand still. Only men keeping watch stood still, and there was something in the way he watched them that Sable had turned over all evening without solving.

Not contempt. That was the thing she kept circling back to. Every stranger who'd ever looked at a column of pilgrims had looked at them with contempt, or fear, or the particular greedy blankness of someone calculating what forty-one tired people might be worth. This man had looked at them like — 

Like he'd been expecting them.

Like a man reading a letter he'd already memorized, checking it anyway for changes.

"He's the gatekeeper," Sable said to Marta. "Juniper Whitlock. The farmer told me about him."

"He's more than that." Marta said it simply, without drama, the way you'd report rain. "You know he's more than that. Why else would we all dream his face?"

And Sable, who had answers for everything, who had rationed truth in careful doses for forty-one people across four hundred miles, found that she had no answer at all. So she said the only honest thing available to her.

"I don't know. But tomorrow we knock on his door, and we ask."

* * *

Thornfell village woke slowly, the way mountain places do, and the column came down into it mid-morning looking exactly like what it was: forty-one road-worn strangers with dust in their hair and fear in their luggage. Sable had coached them on the walk in. No symbols shown. No hymns sung. No talk of dreams. They were travelers, displaced by drought, seeking work and passage west. Half-truths, all of them, worn smooth enough to pass.

The villagers watched from doorways and field edges. Not hostile — watchful. There was a difference, and Sable had learned to read it the way sailors read water. Hostility threw stones. Watchfulness waited to see which way the wind blew.

She found the headman — a broad, grey-bearded man named Aldous Vey, though the surname made her breath catch for reasons she didn't examine — outside the meeting house, and made her pitch: labor for lodging, healing for bread, gone within the week, up the pass and away, trouble for no one.

Vey listened with his hands clasped behind his back and gave her the answer she'd expected, which was neither yes nor no. "There's a council," he said. "Council decides who stays in Thornfell. Council meets in three days." A pause, and then, with the weary courtesy of a man delivering a warning he half-apologized for: "You should know the mood's turned strange here. Talk of miracles makes folk nervous. Nervous folk do stupid things. Keep your people quiet, mistress healer, and maybe we all get through this."

*Keep your people quiet.* As if faith were noise. As if the whole catastrophe of the last century had been a volume problem.

Sable was still turning that phrase over — *maybe we all get through this*, the conditional tucked inside the reassurance like a splinter — when she saw him again.

The gatekeeper. Juniper Whitlock. Standing at the edge of the market square, half in the shadow of the well-house, watching her column file past. Up close, the resemblance to the dream-figure was almost unbearable — the height, the dark hair, the slate-grey eyes — except that dreams had made him remote, monumental, a figure on a horizon, and the real man was tired. Tired around the mouth. Careful in the way he moved, as if he'd practiced not being noticed until the practice had eaten into his bones.

Their eyes met. Once. Briefly.

And Sable, who had spent a lifetime learning to read faces because lies lived there and so did fevers, saw something in his that she had never once seen directed at her people: recognition. Not of her — of them. Of the column entire, as if each face passing were a line in a letter he'd received long ago and answered, and now the answer had walked four hundred miles to see whether he'd meant it.

He didn't smile. He didn't approach. He looked away first, deliberately, the way a man looks away from something he isn't ready to want.

But before he turned, Sable saw his hand move — a small motion, half-hidden against his thigh, fingers tracing something in the air. A circle. Broken by a line.

The sign on the cloth hidden in the false bottom of her satchel. The sign stitched into her grandmother's hems. The sign no living soul outside her column should have known.

She stood very still in the middle of the market square, surrounded by her forty-one, in a valley that smelled of salt with no sea for a hundred leagues, and felt the ground tilt beneath a lifetime of certainty.

Marta was right. He was more than a gatekeeper.

And three days from now, a council would decide whether her people were welcome in the one place on earth that seemed to have been dreaming of them — while somewhere behind them, down the long road they'd climbed, she could almost hear the sound she'd been fleeing since Harrowgate.

Iron bells, ringing closer.
