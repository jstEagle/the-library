## 7. Veyra Wakes

The dream began with salt.

Not the coarse grey salt of the trade roads, not the bitter crust that whitened the gatehouse stones in dry summers, but the old salt — the kind my mother had kept in a stoppered jar on her shelf and never once explained. It glowed in the dream the way I'd always half-believed it did when no one was looking: a soft gold light, like lamplight through honey.

I stood on the mountain above the Saltgate, which was wrong, because I had climbed that face exactly twice in my life and both times my mother had dragged me back down by the collar before I reached the tree line. But in the dream my feet knew the way, and the scree did not slide under me, and the air was warm as bathwater though snow still lay in the hollows like spilled flour.

Below me the pass lay open.

That was the second wrong thing. The gate was a seam of black stone between two shoulders of the mountain, narrow as a coffin lid stood on end, and it had been shut since before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I had pressed my palm against it more times than I could count — testing, listening, the way you press an ear to a wall to hear what the neighbors are doing. In thirty years it had given me nothing. Cold stone, cold silence, and once, memorably, a spider.

In the dream it stood wide as a cathedral door, and light came through it the color of dawn on water.

"You climb well," said a voice behind me, "for a man who has spent his whole life pretending he has no reason to climb."

I turned. She was standing where the snow should have been, and there was no snow. That's how I'd describe her if you made me try, afterward, when I still tried: she was defined by absences. No frost where frost belonged. No shadow where the low sun should have thrown one. No age at all, which is its own kind of absence.

She wore grey robes the color of the pass stone, and her hair was dark shot through with white, and she had my mother's hands.

I knew those hands. I had watched them bind wounds, knead bread, press a sigil into wet clay by firelight while telling me stories about nothing — about weather, about smugglers, about anything except the things she actually believed. I had held one of those hands while she died, and it had been lighter than a bird's bones by then, and it had gripped mine with a strength that frightened me.

"Who are you?" I asked. My voice worked in the dream, which dreams allow.

"You know," she said. "You've known since the shepherd's flock came home. You just haven't let yourself say it."

"Say it, then."

She smiled, and the smile was kind, and kindness from something that vast is more frightening than cruelty. "I am Veyra," she said. "I am the thaw after the long winter. I am the first green shoot through the ash. Your mother called me the Lady of the Thawing, when she still called me anything at all. And you, Juniper Whitlock, keeper of a gate you were told was only a gate—" she spread my mother's hands, "—are the last living key."

"I'm a smuggler," I said. "I keep a ledger. I know which farmers water their beer and which priests don't pay their tithes. That's all I am."

"That is what you *do*." She came closer, and the warmth came with her, real warmth, the kind midsummer only promises and never keeps. "Come and see what you *are*."

* * *

She took my hand — my mother's hand around mine, which was a cruelty neither of us acknowledged — and we walked up through the pass together, and the world changed around us the way a page changes when you turn it.

The Thornfell valley filled with people. Not pilgrims, not refugees, not the thin and wary folk I catalogued every week in my ledger. Crowds. Cities' worth of crowds, pouring up the road toward the open gate, and every face turned upward, and every mouth moving, and the sound of ten thousand voices saying the same word resolved slowly into my name.

Not Juniper. The other name. The one my mother wrote in code in the margins of her books, the one I had never spoken aloud because speaking a thing gives it weight.

"They loved us once," Veyra said. Her voice had gone quiet, almost shy, which is a terrible trick for a goddess to play. "Not feared. Loved. They brought the first cut of every harvest to the shrines. They named their daughters for my sisters. When a child was born they carried it up the mountain so the old ones could breathe on it, and no child who breathed that air was ever sickly, ever lame, ever blind."

The crowd in the dream reached the gate, and the light took them, and they didn't burn. They *brightened*. I watched a man with a crutch walk through the threshold and come out the other side of it straight. I watched a woman carry in a bundle that must have been a dead infant and walk out with the bundle squalling and red-faced and alive.

"This is a lie," I said. My voice cracked on it.

"It is a memory," she said. "You have my mother's eyes too, little keeper. Look harder. Tell me it's a lie."

And because it was a dream, and because some traitor part of me wanted it to be true, I looked.

* * *

Then she showed me the rest.

The valley again, but empty. Not peacefully empty — *scoured* empty. The fields gone to scrub. The shrine stones I knew, the ones along the smuggling paths whose warmth I had spent my life explaining away as geothermal nonsense, standing cracked and toppled in waist-high weeds. A village I recognized as Lower Millstead, except there was no mill, because there was no stream, because there was no rain, and had not been for years.

"Famine," Veyra said, and now her voice had no shyness in it at all. "Three generations of it. Do you know what your mother's covenant cost, Juniper? Not her. Not her divinity — that was hers to spend. It cost *them*." She gestured at the empty fields, the dead mill, the bleached bones of a goat lying in a doorway as if it had lain down mid-step and simply ended. "When we walked the world, the land answered us. Rain came when we sang for it. The wheat stood when we willed it. We were not parasites on your world — we were its *circulation*, its breath in and out. Seal us out, and the world holds its breath. Forever."

"The Order says—"

"The Order says." She laughed, and the laugh was beautiful, and I hated how much I wanted to hear it again. "The Order burned the granaries in the Year of Ash and called it cleansing. The Order taught your valley that hunger is holy. Ask them why their cathedrals never want for bread."

I thought of Hale's bells coming down the pass road. I thought of the pilgrims Sable led, thin and stubborn and praying at ruined altars that had no business answering. I thought of the shepherd's flock, home by morning, every ewe accounted for, and the altar warm as a sleeping animal, and my mother's sigil carved fresh into stone that hadn't been touched in a century.

"And the war?" I asked. Because there had been a war; even the Order's own sanitized histories admitted a war, though they blamed it entirely on the gods.

Her face changed. For the first time she looked — not guilty. Something older than guilt. Tired.

"Yes," she said. "Show him," she said, to someone I couldn't see, and the dream turned its page again.

* * *

A battlefield. Of course. There is always a battlefield.

But this one was wrong in a way that took me a moment to name, and when I named it I wished I hadn't: there were no soldiers. Only villagers with farm tools and hunting bows, and across from them men in iron-grey cassocks with bell-shaped censers swinging on chains, and between the two lines the ground was black with a burning that gave off no heat.

"The War of the Silent Bell," Veyra said. "Your histories call it the Cleansing. Here is what happened. We grew proud — I will give your Order that much truth, and they will spend a hundred years denying they ever needed it. We grew proud, and mortal, and cruel in small ways, the way powerful things are cruel without noticing. And a man named Aldous Bell decided the world would be better managed by nothing at all than by flawed gods. He could not kill us. So he starved us. He burned our shrines, silenced our names, taught children to spit at our sigils — and gods, little keeper, are fed by belief the way you are fed by bread. He did not defeat us. He *fasted* us."

The burning-without-heat crept up the hillside toward the villagers. I watched a woman stand in front of a toppled shrine stone with her arms out, and I watched the flame take her anyway, and she made no sound, because the fire ate sound too.

"Your mother sealed the gate to save what was left of us," Veyra said. "I do not deny it. She was merciful and clever and she loved this world more than she loved heaven. But a seal is not a cure. It is a tourniquet. And tourniquets, left on long enough, kill the limb." She turned to me, and her eyes were the exact brown of mine, which I understood suddenly was not flattery but arithmetic. "The limb is dying, Juniper. The valley. The pass. The whole slow body of the world. And you are the last drop of blood in the keyhole."

"What do you want from me?"

"Nothing yet." She smiled again, and this time the sadness in it was genuine, and that was the worst thing she'd done all night. "Only that you remember this. Only that when the Order comes with its bells and its clemency and its clean fire, you ask yourself who taught you to be afraid of the dark — and what they keep locked in their own cellars. Open the gate when you choose. Not before. A god who is invited is a guest. A god who breaks in—"

"—is a conqueror," I finished. My mother's voice, saying it. Or hers. By then I couldn't tell.

"Wise boy," Veyra said softly. "Sleep now. Grow strong. And when you wake, look at your lashes."

* * *

I woke in my bed above the gatehouse with the grey light of false dawn coming through the shutters, and my heart going like a smuggler's mule on a downhill grade, and —

Cold.

On my face. On my *lashes*, exactly as she'd said, a fine rime of frost melting even as I felt it, running down my cheeks in threads so that for one disoriented moment I thought I was crying.

Midsummer. The night before, Bram and I had drunk ale on the gatehouse step and complained about the heat like every summer of our lives.

I sat up. My breath fogged. Across the room, the water in my wash basin had skinned over with ice, and in the center of the ice, perfect as a coin pressed in wax, was a single carved mark.

My mother's sigil.

I got up on shaking legs and crossed to the basin and looked down at it, and the ice creaked, and beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath the black seam of stone that I had pressed my ear against for thirty years, I heard — faint, patient, unmistakable —

a heartbeat.

It was not mine. Mine was hammering. This one was slow, and deep, and glad.

I dressed in the dark, took my ledger, and went down to open the gate for the morning traffic, because that is what I do, that is who I am, that is the whole of me: a man who opens a gate for carts.

But halfway down the stairs I stopped, because the second thing I heard that morning, rising up the pass road with the sun still below the ridge, was the sound of bells.

Iron bells. Many of them. Coming fast.
