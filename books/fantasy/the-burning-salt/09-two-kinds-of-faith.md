## 9. Two Kinds of Faith

Sable found him at the shrine stones below the gatehouse, the ones half-swallowed by gorse, where the old carvings had been worn to suggestions. He was kneeling in the wet grass with one palm flat against the largest stone, and he didn't hear her coming — or he did, and pretended not to, which was its own kind of confession.

She stopped ten paces off and watched him.

The boy he'd healed three nights ago — Tam, the cooper's son, the one with the crushed hand from the Order's raid on the smugglers' camp — was due for his bandage change this morning. Sable had walked up from the pilgrims' camp intending to do it herself. But she'd seen Juniper slip out of the gatehouse before dawn, moving like a man going to a meeting he hadn't agreed to, and she'd followed.

Now his hand was on the stone, and the stone was *glowing*.

Not blazing. Not the vulgar miracle-light of the traveling preachers who used oil lamps behind painted cloth. It was a low warmth, a gold like butter held near a fire, seeping up through the moss and the lichen and the centuries of rain-scars. Where his fingers rested, the frost that had crept over everything these past weeks — midsummer frost, impossible frost — melted into a ring of green.

Sable's breath went out of her slowly, so as not to make a sound.

She had known. That was the terrible part. She had known since the night of the raid, when she'd watched him cup that broken hand between his own and the swelling simply *leave*, drawn out like poison from a snakebite, and he'd looked around afterward with the wide blank eyes of a man checking whether anyone had seen him commit a crime. She'd said nothing then. She'd spent three days saying nothing, carrying it in her chest like a coal wrapped in linen, feeling it burn through every layer of caution she owned.

But watching him now, kneeling alone in the grey light with his mother's sigil carved under his palm — she'd seen that mark before, on the ruined altar where the shepherd's flock had come home — something in her chest cracked open past all saying.

"You can stop pretending," she said.

He flinched. The glow died. The stone went dark and cold as any other rock in any other valley, and Juniper Whitlock rose from his knees with the careful slowness of a man who has practiced, all his life, how to stand up without looking guilty.

"Sable." His voice was level. Almost. "You're up early."

"The dead don't sleep well," she said. "Neither do I."

* * *

They walked without speaking, up the switchback path toward the gatehouse, because he gestured toward it and she followed, and because some conversations cannot be had standing still in an open field. The mountain loomed above them, white-veined even in summer now, the Saltgate itself just a dark seam in the cliff face, sealed by masonry older than any kingdom Sable could name.

Inside the gatehouse, he built up the fire without asking if she wanted one. She watched his hands move through the ritual of it — kindling, bellows, the iron kettle — and thought: *he does everything like this. Like a man performing ordinariness. Like a man who learned early that being caught being extraordinary is worse than being caught stealing.*

"My mother kept this house," he said at last, to the fire rather than to her. "Before me."

"I know. The villagers talk about her. They talk about you more."

"What do they say?"

"That you're steady. That you're fair. That you've never once taken more than your share of the toll salt." She paused. "That you're lonely."

A ghost of a smile. "The last part's accurate, at least."

"Juniper." She said his name the way you'd set a knife down on a table between you — not threatening, just present. "I saw what you did for Tam. And I saw what you did just now, at the stones. I'm not here to accuse you. I'm here because I need to know if my people are safe with you. If *I'm* safe with you. Because there are men in this valley wearing iron bells on their collars who would do anything — *anything* — to get their hands on whatever it is you're hiding. And I can't protect anyone if I don't know what I'm protecting them from."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound like slow breathing.

He sat down across from her. For a long moment he just looked at his hands, turning them over, studying the palms as though the answer might be written there.

"Do you know what the Order calls people like me?" he said finally.

"God-touched."

"They have another word. They use it when they think no one's listening." His jaw tightened. "Abomination."

Sable felt the word land in her own chest, because she'd heard it too — screamed outside burning houses in the lowlands, spat at her column of pilgrims on the road, chalked on doors in villages they'd passed through. *Abominations.* Her people carried herbs and prayers and a faith the Iron Bell had outlawed, and for that they were hunted like wolves.

"And is it true?" she asked quietly. "What they'd call you?"

He laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it at all.

"My mother was Veyra's daughter," he said. "Or her granddaughter. The records don't agree, and she never told me which, only that the blood came down to her and from her to me. When the gods were cast out, someone had to hold the door shut behind them. That someone was her. The Saltgate isn't just a wall, Sable. It's a seal. And the seal is bound to her blood. To mine." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of deep water over stone. "I'm not god-touched. I'm god-*born*. Half of what's behind that gate is half of what's in me. Every prayer that gets answered in this valley lately gets answered through a crack in a door that opens for my family and no one else."

The silence after that was enormous.

Sable realized she had stopped breathing. She made herself start again.

Outside, the world continued — a bird somewhere, the creak of the gatehouse timbers, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary morning — and inside, everything had changed shape. The man sitting across from her was not a keeper of a pass. He was the pass. He was the hinge and the lock and the key, walking around in a worn coat with salt dust on his boots, smuggling brandy and untaxed wool through a doorway that led to the graves of gods.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. Not accusing. Genuinely needing to know.

"Because you asked," he said. "And because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I've been alone with this since I was nine years old, when my mother made me swear on her deathbed never to tell a living soul. And because you looked at me at the shrine stones just now, and you weren't afraid. You were *sad*. And I don't know why that undid me, but it did."

"It undid you because you've been waiting your whole life for someone to be sad instead of afraid," Sable said. "Sad means they understand. Afraid means they're already reaching for a rope."

His face did something complicated. "Is that what happened to your people? In the lowlands?"

"Some of it." She wrapped her hands around the mug of tea he'd pressed on her without her noticing — he was always doing things like that, small kindnesses disguised as habit. "My grandmother taught the old prayers back when they were merely unfashionable. My mother died in a cell in Corvane for continuing. I lead forty-three people who believe that the divine isn't gone from the world, only sleeping, and that someday it would wake. We've been walking toward Thornfell for two years, Juniper. Running, mostly. Hiding in cellars, crossing borders at night. Do you know why we came *here*? Of all the valleys in all the mountains?"

He shook his head slowly.

"Dreams," Sable said. "Starting last winter, dozens of us began dreaming the same dream. Not similar. The same. A gate in a high white pass. A man standing before it with his hand on the stone. Dark hair, grey eyes, a scar—" she reached out, hesitated, then touched the faded line along his jaw where a smuggler's hook had caught him years ago, "—there. Everyone dreamed the same scar. Forty-three people, from villages that have never met, dreaming one man's face. We didn't come to Thornfell for safety. Safety was in the south, among the marshes, where the Bell's reach thins. We came because we were *called*. By a gatekeeper with your face."

Juniper had gone very still. The kind of stillness that isn't calm — the stillness of a man listening to something enormous approach from very far away.

"That's not possible," he said, but his voice shook.

"Isn't it?" Sable leaned forward. "You told me yourself the seal is bound to your blood. You told me prayers are getting through. What if it goes both ways? What if the gate doesn't just keep the gods out — what if it reaches out, through you, into the sleeping minds of anyone who ever loved the old ways? What if you've been calling us, Juniper, without knowing it? Calling us home?"

"I would know. I'd—"

"Would you?" she said gently. "Did you know the stone glows under your hand? Did you know you healed Tam? Did you know your mother's covenant was dying until the shepherd's flock came home?" She let that sit. "You've spent your whole life not-knowing, love. It's the family trade."

The endearment slipped out before she could catch it. She saw him hear it — saw the word land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there, glowing faintly, like the stone.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire settled. The light through the window shifted from grey to the pale gold of actual morning, and in that light Juniper Whitlock looked, for the first time since she'd known him, like a young man rather than a fortress.

"If I called you," he said at last, hoarsely, "then I'm responsible for you. All forty-three. And for whatever answers the valley's prayers — the merciful ones and the monstrous ones both. And for whatever comes next through that crack I can feel widening under the mountain like a tooth coming loose." He dragged a hand through his hair. "Sable, there's an inquisitor captain in Thornfell right now who looks at me like he's reading a ledger. Hale. He knows more about my bloodline than I do. And yesterday he offered me a bargain I haven't answered yet, because answering it either way burns something I can't replace."

"Then don't answer yet," Sable said. "Answer me instead. One question."

"Anything."

"When you dreamed — if you dreamed — before we came. Before the miracles started. Did you ever dream of us? A column of ragged people walking toward you through snow?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And she watched the truth arrive in his face like weather.

"Yes," he whispered. "Every night since midwinter. I thought it was longing. I thought I'd invented you."

"You didn't invent me." Sable reached across the table and took his hand — the one that glowed, the one that healed, the one that trembled slightly now against hers. "We invented each other, maybe. Or something older than both of us did. Either way—" she laced her fingers through his, deliberately, watching his breath catch, "—I'm not afraid of you, Juniper Whitlock. I'm afraid *for* you. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

He looked at their hands. Then at her. And whatever wall he'd built between himself and the world — the wall his mother built first, and he'd inherited along with the blood — Sable watched it develop its first real crack.

"No one knows," he said. "What I am. No one except you, now."

"Then let me be the one who knows," she said. "Let me carry half of it. That's what faith is, in the end. Not believing the doctrine. Believing *someone*."

* * *

They were still sitting that way, hands joined across the table like a bridge, when the knocking started.

Three blows, iron-weighted, on the gatehouse door. Then a voice Sable knew from nightmares — smooth, patient, reasonable, the voice of a man who burned people and slept soundly after.

"Keeper Whitlock." Inquisitor-Captain Hale, pleasant as morning. "Open the door, please. We need to discuss the woman currently hiding in your house — and the forty-two others camped in your valley who were named in last night's edict."

Juniper's hand tightened around hers.

Above them, far up in the sealed pass, something vast turned over in its sleep — and the frost on the window bloomed outward, all at once, in the shape of an open eye.
