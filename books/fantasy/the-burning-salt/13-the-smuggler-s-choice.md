## 13. The Smuggler's Choice

Bram came up the goat track at dusk with his hood back and his hands empty, which was how I knew it was serious. In twenty years of knowing him, Bram Tolliver had never once approached me without something in his hands — a jug, a ledger, a stolen reliquary, a lie. Empty hands meant he'd come to say something he didn't want to be holding when he said it.

I was mending the hinge on the west postern when he crested the ridge. The gatehouse behind me hummed its low note, the one only I could hear, like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop ringing. Three days since I'd bled on the seal and opened that hand's width of light. Three days since Sable had been dragged out of the mob's reach by smugglers who owed her for stitching their wounds, and since half of Thornfell had watched their gatekeeper glow like a lamp with a cracked shade.

"You shouldn't be seen coming here," I said.

"Everyone's seen everything already." He dropped onto the mounting block like a man twenty years older than he was. "That's rather the problem."

I set down the hinge pin. My hands weren't steady. They hadn't been since the herald's fire had run through them — stolen divinity leaves a residue, like the smell of smoke in wool. "How bad?"

"The Order's pulled out of the valley proper. Hale's men are fortifying the shrine road instead." Bram turned his face up toward the pass, where the last light was going copper on the snowline. "They're not leaving, Junie. They're digging in. Like they're waiting for something."

"They're waiting for the gate."

"I know what they're waiting for." He said it flatly, no fear in it, which frightened me more than shouting would have. Bram had always been brave about the wrong things. "The whole valley knows what they're waiting for now. Half of them want to help."

* * *

He'd brought food after all — it appeared from inside his coat, because Bram was constitutionally incapable of arriving anywhere without contraband — hard cheese and a loaf still warm from the widow Marsh's oven, which meant he'd walked through a village that wanted my head carrying bread baked for me. I didn't know what to do with that. I ate some of it anyway. Grief and hunger share a table more often than people admit.

"Sable's asking for you," he said, around his own mouthful. "Not asking. Refusing to leave until she sees you. Maren's about ready to dose her tea with poppy just to get her moving."

"She should move. All of them should." I looked at the pass. The evening star was out above the Saltgate's towers, sharp as a nail. "Hale will come back for them. For her especially — she's the one who broke the boy out of the cell, she's named in the writs."

"She is. And we can get her out." Bram set down the bread and wiped his hands on his thighs, and I saw then that this was the thing he'd carried up the mountain, the weight in his empty hands. "There's a way over the western passes. Old drover roads, pre-Order, pre-everything. Hard going, but my people know every switchback. Snowmelt's late this year; the high fords are passable for another fortnight, maybe three. We take the pilgrims in small groups, mixed in with salt caravans, split across six different routes so no one pattern shows. Ten days, everyone's over the divide and into the free cantons where the Bell has no writ and less sense."

I stared at him. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, an excellent plan — the kind of plan I would have built myself if I'd had the luxury of thinking about anything except the seal cracking one hair-width wider each night, the frost creeping further down the shrine stones each morning, the voices behind the gate growing louder and hungrier and, worse, more familiar.

"And the cost," I said, because there was always a cost, and Bram's face told me he'd rehearsed this part.

"The cost," he agreed. "You come with us."

"Bram—"

"Hear it all before you argue. You're the most wanted man between here and the coast. Every route I've mapped has checkpoints, and every checkpoint has someone who'd sell their mother for the reward on your name. But you're also the only person alive who knows these mountains better than I do, and the western passes eat strangers. So: you walk point. You get them over. You disappear into the cantons, change your name, grow a beard, become a boring man who fixes fences. Hale can't burn what he can't find." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped into the register he used for talking men off ledges. "Junie. It works. It actually works. For the first time since your mother died, there's a door open that isn't that one."

I looked at the gatehouse. Of course I did. I couldn't not.

"It doesn't work," I said quietly. "Because I'm not walking away from the gate."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him, standing there in the blue dusk with the cheese going dry between us:

Every night since the riot, I climbed to the seal chamber and pressed my palm to the stone, and every night the crack under my hand was a little wider. A hair's width became two. Two became the width of a straw. Through it came warmth, and light the color of deep water, and the sound — saints, the sound. Ten thousand voices had become a tide. Some of them sang. Some of them prayed. Some of them simply said my name, over and over, in my mother's voice, in Sable's voice, in mine, patient as erosion.

And every morning the frost reached a little further down the mountain. The shepherd's ewes were lambing in midwinter coats. The millpond had frozen solid on Tuesday, in high summer, and the children of Thornfell had skated on it laughing, and their parents had stood on the bank looking at the ice and then at each other, and nobody had laughed.

If I left, the seal would fail in a season. Maybe less. Whatever my mother had bound into that stone was bound to her blood, and I was the last drop of it, and blood that walks away from a covenant might as well have never signed it.

But if I stayed—

If I stayed, Sable went over the passes without me, into a country where I could never follow, and lived, and grew old, and somewhere in the middle of that long life she stopped waiting at gatehouses for a man who glowed. That was the version where she lived. I held onto it the way a drowning man holds a spar: not because it floats well, but because letting go is unthinkable.

"Say it plainly," Bram said. "Whatever it is. Say it plainly and I'll tell you if it's madness."

I almost laughed. Twenty years of friendship and he still thought my silences were puzzles rather than mercy. "If I leave, the gate opens. Not a crack. All the way. Everything behind it comes through, and there'll be no cantons to hide in, no valleys, no anything. If I stay, I can hold it. Barely. While my blood holds out."

"Your blood." He rubbed his jaw. "Which is the thing Hale wants. Which means staying makes you the bait that keeps the trap sprung right here, instead of letting him chase it over half the world."

"That's one way to put it."

"It's the only way to put it." Bram stood, restless, and paced the length of the mounting block like a caged thing. "Junie, listen to me. I've moved contraband through nine kingdoms. Do you know the first rule? Never guard the cargo. Guard the route. The moment you stand between the soldiers and the thing they want, you die, and then they take the thing anyway. You're doing it backwards. Get the cargo — yourself, Sable, all of it — onto the road, and let the gate be somebody else's problem."

"There is no somebody else."

"Then let it be *no* somebody! Let it—" He caught himself. Stopped. When he turned back, his eyes were wet, and Bram Tolliver crying was a rarer sight than the miracles rotting the valley below us. "Let it break. Saints forgive me, but let it break. What's behind it can't be worse than what's in front of it. I've seen the square. I've seen what Hale left of Tam Fletcher. I've seen good people who'd have hidden a fugitive last month now drawing up lists of their neighbors. Your gods can't do worse to this valley than your neighbors are already doing to themselves."

I thought about telling him he was wrong. About Veyra's dream, the famine years, the war that had followed her imprisonment, the ten thousand starving mouths behind the seal. About the herald, vast and patient, offering to wear me like a coat. But the words wouldn't come, because the truth was that he wasn't entirely wrong, and a man can't shout down an argument that's half true.

Instead I said: "Sable won't go without knowing why I'm not coming."

"No," Bram agreed. "She won't. She's got your same disease — thinks love means standing in the doorway."

* * *

We went down together as far as the shrine fork, where the paths divided — his toward the smugglers' camp in the hollow, mine back up toward the gatehouse and the humming stone and the widening crack. The stars were fully out now, and the frost had begun its nightly climb down the mountainside, silvering the gorse, silvering the milestone that read THORNFELL, 3 LEAGUES in letters my grandfather had cut.

Bram stopped at the fork. "Come with us," he said one more time, and it wasn't logistics anymore. It was a boy's voice, from thirty years ago, from the riverbank where two fatherless kids had sworn they'd look out for each other. "Please, Junie. Come with us."

"If I could," I said, "you know I would."

"That's the worst thing you've ever said to me. Because I believe you." He pulled his hood up. "Ten days. The first caravan moves in ten days, moonless, from the hollow. If you're there, you're there. If you're not—" He didn't finish it. He squeezed my shoulder once, hard enough to hurt, and went down into the dark.

I stood at the fork a long time.

Then I climbed back up, past the postern with its mended hinge, past the shrine stones wearing their new frost like lace, up to the seal chamber, and put my hand against the stone. Warm. Warmer than yesterday. Through the crack, faint as a pulse, the voices — and among them, unmistakably, one voice that was not ancient, not divine, not hungry. One voice praying in the plain speech of the smugglers' hollow, in words I knew because I'd heard her pray them over dying men:

*Sable.*

She was praying for me. Down in the hollow, refusing the poppy tea, refusing to move, praying for a man who had already chosen the stone over her — or hadn't, not yet, not finally, the choice renewed every hour like breath.

I took my hand off the seal. It left a print in the frost, five fingers, glowing faintly gold, fading slow.

Ten days, Bram had said. Moonless. From the hollow.

Below me, far down the valley, a bell began to ring — not the Iron Bell's harsh iron voice but the old chapel bell, cracked and sweet, ringing the hour, or perhaps ringing something else. And on the wind, threading through it, I heard hoofbeats on the shrine road. Many horses. Moving fast. Moving up.

Toward the gate.

Toward me.

I counted the riders by their torchlight as they wound through the dark — twelve, fifteen, twenty — and at their head a banner I knew too well, white on black, and my stomach went cold as the stones beneath my feet.

Hale hadn't been digging in.

He'd been sending for reinforcements.
