## 15. Riot at the Gate

The bell began at dusk.

Not the Order's iron bells — those had a voice like a dropped anvil, all clang and authority. This was the chapel bell of Thornfell itself, rung hard and fast, the alarm pattern every child in the valley learned before they learned their letters. *Fire. Flood. Come armed.*

Juniper heard it from the gatehouse and knew, in the marrow-deep way he now knew most things about his own ruin, that it was for them.

He came down the switchback road at a pace that would have scandalized any keeper who'd held the post before him — and there had been only one, and she had taught him to walk everywhere slowly, so the valley would never see him hurry, never see him care too much, never see anything at all but a bored man with a ledger. He could hear her voice even now, dead eleven years and still lecturing: *A gatekeeper's first weapon is tedium, Juno.*

But the bell kept ringing, and below him Thornfell was spilling into its streets with torches, and tedium was not going to save anyone tonight.

* * *

Sable heard the bell from the tannery loft where the pilgrims had been hidden since the burning.

They had buried what they could carry of Marta's ashes — Sable refused to think of it as anything else, though Hale's men had left little enough to bury — and after that the column had stopped pretending to be travelers. Forty-one souls crammed into a building that smelled of lime and old hides, waiting on Bram's word about the western passes, waiting on Juniper, waiting on a God they couldn't see to do something merciful for once.

"Is it the Order?" whispered Doss, the drover whose hands still shook three days after the raid. "Bell means fire."

"It's not fire," Sable said, and then she heard the shouting, and understood that she had lied, because the valley was shouting one word over and over, and the word was *gatehouse*, and the second word, when it finally resolved through the panic, was worse.

*"Witch-lovers."*

She got the pilgrims moving toward the back wall, toward the drainage culvert that ran under the palisade — Bram's route, marked in charcoal on a beam by one of his boys that afternoon. She was counting heads — thirty-eight, thirty-nine — when the tannery door burst open and the night came in wearing torchlight.

There were maybe two dozen of them. Farmers mostly. Men whose animals she had healed, women whose children she had splinted and poulticed. She knew their faces. That was the horror of it; she knew every single face, and behind them stood four men in gray with iron bell badges, and none of the farmers were holding weapons, just tools, which was somehow worse, because tools meant they hadn't planned this, meant they'd simply been *led*, the way a flock is led, and Sable thought of Marta and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very cold.

"There," said a woman Sable had delivered twins for. "That's her. The healer."

"I'm no witch," Sable said, stepping forward before anyone could grab her, because if they were going to take someone, better it be her with her hands up than some trembling drover caught between torches. "I've mended your bones. I pulled your son back from fever, Aldith. You know me."

"That's how they do it," Aldith said, and her voice broke on the words, and Sable saw that she was crying, that half of them were crying, that fear had done to these people what no god or devil ever managed. "That's how they get their hooks in. The Inquisitor preached it plain. Kindness is the bait."

Behind Sable, the culvert grate clanked softly shut. One of the gray men had gone around back. Of course one of them had gone around back. The Order didn't mob; the Order *positioned*.

"The pilgrims come with me," said the gray man in front, a lean one with a scarred lip. "The healer comes too. Anyone who interferes will be named at Sunday sermon."

And the crowd — her valley, her patients, people she'd bled for — parted to let them through.

Sable went. What else was there? She looked back once and saw Doss weeping openly and doing nothing, and she did not hate him for it, and that was somehow the worst part of the whole night.

* * *

They took her to the gatehouse square.

Of course they did. Hale wanted the gatehouse. Hale had always wanted the gatehouse — she'd understood that much from the sermons, from the questions the gray men asked farmers about the keeper's habits, his mother, his comings and goings. The mob surged up the switchback road carrying torches and a felled pine trunk for a ram, and the Order walked among them like shepherds among sheep, and above them all the Saltgate stood black against the stars, sealed, silent, patient as it had been patient for three hundred years.

Juniper met them at the gatehouse door.

He was alone. He had sent his two hired hands down-valley at dusk — Sable learned later that he'd paid them a month's wages and told them to visit their mother in Lowmarsh, and she understood that too, eventually, everything about that night, the way you understand a wound whole.

"Go home," he told the mob, and his voice carried the way it always did, flat and unhurried, a man reading a ledger aloud. "The gate is closed. There's nothing here but stone and salt."

"The witch is in your house," shouted a man with a smith's hammer. "Bram's boys seen her. Seen *them*. You've been hiding god-touched filth behind the seal—"

"The Order requests the keeper's presence," said the scarred-lip man smoothly, "and the Order thanks the valley for its zeal. Stand aside, keeper."

"You'll have my presence tomorrow at noon, as your captain requested." Juniper hadn't moved. His hands hung loose at his sides, and Sable, watching from between two gray men's grips, saw what no one else in the torchlight seemed to see: that his shadow fell wrong, stretched up the gatehouse wall toward the pass, as if the mountain were pulling on it. "Tonight, go home."

Something in the crowd wavered. Torches dipped. And then a voice from the back — young, cracked, frightened — called out:

"He healed a boy! At the shrine stones! I seen it — touched the lad's head and the fever just — my cousin swears it, the keeper's *touched*—"

The word went through the crowd like sparks through dry grass. *Touched. Touched.* And Sable watched the faces around her turn from doubt to certainty in the space of a breath, because a mob does not need proof, only permission, and someone had just given it to them.

The pine trunk swung.

The gatehouse door was oak, iron-banded, built by Juniper's grandfather. It held for four blows. On the fifth it gave, and the mob poured into the courtyard like water finding a breach, and the gray men dragged Sable up onto the mounting block where everyone could see her, because the Order understood theater as well as any priest.

"Here!" scarred-lip shouted. "The witch of the pilgrim road! And the keeper who harbored her!"

Juniper stood in his broken doorway with his ledger still under his arm — he had actually picked it up, Sable realized later, some absurd instinct of office — and looked out at perhaps two hundred people he had known his whole life, and she saw him understand, in real time, that it was finished. The mask. The slow walks. The bored voice. Eleven years of careful nothing, ending in a courtyard full of torches.

"Take her down the road," he said quietly. "Do what you want with me. She mended your children."

"And opened the gate!" someone screamed. "The ash on the wind — the frost in summer — it's *him*, it's the keeper's blood—"

Hale stepped out of the dark.

Sable hadn't seen him arrive. He wore no armor, just gray wool, and he walked through his own mob the way a man walks through his own house, and when he reached the mounting block he smiled up at Sable almost kindly.

"You see the difficulty," he said, loud enough for the square. "The valley is frightened. Frightened people do regrettable things." He turned to Juniper. "I offered you terms, keeper. Tomorrow noon. But the valley has moved up our appointment." He gestured, lazily, at Sable. "This one burns at dawn unless the gate opens. That's the demand from your neighbors, not from me — I merely lack the cruelty to refuse them. Open the Saltgate, surrender yourself and whatever runs in your veins, and the healer walks free. Refuse—" he spread his hands at the mob, at the torches, at the pine trunk dripping splinters of the door— "well. Crowds are impatient."

"They can't open the gate," Juniper said. "No one can. It's sealed by covenant—"

"By *bloodline*," Hale said, gently, terribly. "Yes. We've read the same histories, you and I. Different libraries, perhaps. But the same page." He looked at Juniper for a long moment, and his voice dropped so only the front ranks heard, and Sable heard it because the night had gone utterly still. "Open it, or watch her burn. And know that either way, keeper, I get what I came for. The only question is how much of this valley I let survive the lesson."

* * *

Later — in the years after, when people asked her, and they always asked — Sable would try to explain what Juniper did next, and she would fail, because there was no explaining a man walking calmly past two hundred enemies as though they were furniture.

He set the ledger down on the mounting block. Neatly. Squared its edges.

Then he walked past her, and as he passed he touched her bound wrist, just briefly, two fingers, the way he'd touched the fevered boy at the shrine stones, and warmth went through her like sunrise, and the ropes fell away, and she understood that he was saying goodbye in the only language left to him.

"Juno—" she started.

"Don't," he said. Not unkindly. "Watch the gate."

He climbed the steps to the seal.

It sat in the rock of the pass itself, behind the gatehouse's inner wall — a slab of white stone veined with gray, tall as three men, carved over every inch with his mother's sigil, the interlocking rings that every soul in the valley had grown up calling mere decoration. Juniper pressed his palm against it, and Sable saw him take out a knife — his belt knife, the plain one he used for cutting cheese and whittling kindling — and she understood one heartbeat before the crowd did, and screamed his name.

He drew the blade across his palm, deep, deliberate, and pressed his bleeding hand flat against the white stone.

"For the valley," he said. Not to the mob. To the mountain. "For the ones I keep. Open a crack, Mother. Just a crack."

The sigil drank the blood.

Every carved ring lit at once, soft gold, spreading outward from his hand like frost blooming across a window, and the sound that came out of the Saltgate was not mechanical — it was vast and mineral and ancient, stone grinding against stone deep in the heart of the mountain, a groan that Sable felt in her teeth and her ribs and the soles of her feet. The mob's torches guttered. Horses somewhere down the road screamed. Two hundred people fell to their knees without deciding to.

And the gate — the sealed, three-hundred-years-dead gate — moved.

A hand's width. No more. A seam of light split the white stone down its center, and through it came a radiance that was not fire and not sun and not anything the world below had words for, warm and gold and impossibly far away, like looking at dawn through a keyhole in heaven. Wind breathed out of it, smelling of high snowfields and — faintly, horribly, wonderfully — of salt and roses.

Through the gap, thin as a blade, came a voice. Or ten thousand voices braided into one, so that each listener heard only their own name spoken with unbearable tenderness.

Sable heard hers. She was still hearing it when the light died — when Juniper tore his hand off the stone and staggered back, grey-faced, clutching his ruined palm, and the seam ground shut again with a boom that rolled down the valley and woke every dog in Thornfell.

Silence. Total. The torches burned. Nobody spoke.

Juniper turned around to face them all, blood running off his fingers onto the white stone steps, and his eyes had gone the color of the light — gold shot through with gray — and when he spoke, his voice was his own and also something older, something that made the hair stand up on two hundred necks:

"She's awake. And she knows the way in now."

He looked straight at Captain Hale, standing very still amid his kneeling flock, and Sable saw the inquisitor's smile return — slow, satisfied, triumphant — and understood with sickening clarity that everything had just gone exactly according to the Order's plan.

"Thank you, keeper," Hale said softly. "That's all we needed to see."
