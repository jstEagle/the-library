## 17. Abomination

They built the pyre before they finished the trial.

Sable watched it from the pilgrims' camp on the mill side of the river, a black lattice of fence posts and door planks rising in the valley square like a ribcage offered up to some new god — the god of being safe, of never again having to be afraid. The Order's iron bells hung from the meetinghouse eaves where the harvest garlands used to go, and every hour they rang, and every hour the sound scraped something raw out of the valley and left it flatter.

She had healed these people. She had set the cooper's arm after the raid, stitched the ferryman's scalp, sat three nights with old Maren while the fever burned her down to embers and then, mercifully, out. They had wept over Sable's hands. They had called her blessed.

That was four days ago. Before the herald came through the gate. Before Juniper stood in the gatehouse yard with light pouring off him like heat off a forge and threw it back into the dark with his bare hands.

Now the same hands that had pressed bread into hers were chalk-drawing an X on their doors — the mark for *harbored*, for *touched*, for *burn*. The cooper whose arm she had mended spat at her feet in the market lane and did not even look at her while he did it. The ferryman refused her passage and charged her double to say why: *because you brought it here.*

*It.* Not the herald. Not the Order. Him. Juniper Whitlock, who had walked this valley's smuggler paths for twenty years, who had carried their grain past the tolls in famine years and never once asked credit, who had bled on a seal to save a woman he'd known one season — because that was what the mob had decided he was now. An it. An abomination. A thing wearing a man's shape.

Sable stood at the edge of the square as they raised the first crossbeam, and she let herself feel the whole weight of it, because she had learned on the road that fear you refuse to name comes back with teeth.

Then she went to find Bram.

* * *

The smuggler's cellar under the tannery smelled of oak bark and lamp oil, and Bram sat in it like a stone sits in a streambed — immovable, worn smooth, letting everything flow around him. He was older than Juniper by ten years, gray coming into his beard, hands like knotted rope. He had been the first person in Thornfell to call Sable by name instead of *pilgrim*, and she had not forgotten it.

"You heard," she said.

"Everyone's heard." He didn't look up from the blade he was sharpening. "They're holding him in the Order's cell beneath the tithe barn. Hale wants a public burning. Wants it at dawn tomorrow, so the whole valley can watch the sun come up on a world with no gods in it." The whetstone paused. "Wants it bad enough that I think he's afraid of something else."

"Of what?"

"Of what happens if the gatekeeper dies and the seal dies with him." Bram finally looked at her, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I've run that pass since I was fourteen. My mother ran it before me. There's a feeling you get up there, when the wind comes through the crack in the world. Like standing next to a held breath. You don't need to believe in gods to know something is being *held*." He set the knife down. "The valley thinks killing him closes the wound. I think it opens it."

"Then help me get him out."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Outside, faint through the floorboards, the bells rang the hour, and Sable watched the sound move through him like weather.

"You understand what you're asking," he said finally. "Not for him. For you. If you walk out of this valley with him, you don't walk back. Not in a year. Not in twenty. Every bridge behind you burns, and most of them you lit yourself."

"I know what I'm asking."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. "Because I've known Juniper Whitlock since he was a boy trailing me along the salt paths, and I will tell you the truth about him, healer, since no one else in this valley will: he has spent his whole life choosing other people over himself, and he is very, very good at it, and it has made him a man who does not know how to be chosen *back*. He'll tell you to leave. He'll mean it. He'll believe he means it. And if you leave, he will forgive you, and he will die tomorrow morning, and he will consider the whole arrangement fair."

"Then I won't leave."

"No," Bram said softly. "I don't suppose you will." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and pulled a ring of keys off a nail behind a loose brick. "Tannery cellar, third door, the one that doesn't lock from the outside anymore because I broke it myself two winters back. There's a tunnel runs up under the tithe barn. Old salt-smugglers' route, predates the Order, predates the tolls. Comes up inside the foundation wall, behind a stack of grain sacks nobody's moved since the last famine." He wrapped the keys in oilcloth and put them in her hand and closed her fingers over them. "The cell door takes an iron key. This one's filed to fit. Don't ask me how I know."

"Bram—"

"Don't thank me yet." His voice roughened. "Thank me when you're over the western passes and my oldest friend isn't ash on the morning wind. Until then, you're just a fool with stolen keys, and so am I."

* * *

The tunnel was exactly as wide as a person carrying nothing, which meant it was exactly too narrow for a person carrying hope, which was heavier.

Sable crawled by lamplight through air that tasted of salt and rat droppings and years, elbows scraping stone, the oilcloth-wrapped keys pressed flat against her sternum where she could feel them with every heartbeat like a second pulse. Above her, muffled through six feet of earth and the barn's plank floor, she could hear the Order moving — boots, orders, the creak of a winch hauling something heavy. Building materials, she thought. More pyre. They were building it bigger.

She thought about the night on the pilgrim road when she had first seen him — the gatekeeper on the ridge above the pass, watching her column come down out of the snow with an expression she had spent weeks afterward trying to name. Not contempt. Not suspicion. *Recognition*, she had decided eventually. As though he had been waiting for them without knowing it, the way you wait for a letter you sent before you remember writing it.

She had dreamed of him too. All of them had — the whole column, hundreds of miles apart, all dreaming the same face at a gate. She had told herself it was the faith moving through them, the shared hunger taking a shape. It had taken her months to understand that the dreams had a direction. That they flowed *from* somewhere. From him. From whatever slept in his blood and reached out through the mountain toward anyone who might answer.

And now the valley wanted to burn him for it. For answering. For being what the mountain made him.

The tunnel bent upward. Light leaked through gaps between grain sacks ahead, thin and dusty, and Sable killed her lamp and went the last stretch blind, by feel, palms flat against cold stone, until her fingers found the edge of the last sack and she lifted it an inch and looked out.

A cellar. Grain sacks in towers, barrels stenciled with the Order's bell-and-flame mark, and across the room, a door — iron-banded, with a small barred window at eye height, and beyond the window, darkness, and in the darkness, breathing.

She waited. A guard passed above; dust sifted down through the floorboards. Boots crossed, paused, crossed back. Then silence, long enough to count to two hundred in.

She slipped out from behind the sacks, crossed the cellar, and put her eye to the barred window.

He was sitting on the floor with his back to the far wall, wrists bound in front of him with iron wire that had bitten through skin, head bowed. In the dark she could see only the outline of him — the broad shoulders she knew, the hands she had held, the stillness of a man who has already argued with death and lost and is now just waiting for the appointment. There was frost on the floor around him. In a windowless cell, in high summer. It spread outward from where he sat in delicate fern-patterns, and where it touched the iron wire, the metal had gone white and brittle.

He was freezing the iron without knowing he was doing it. Or knowing, and unable to stop. His power leaking out of him the way grief leaks out of people — everywhere, unasked, staining everything it touched.

"Juniper," she said through the bars.

His head came up fast, and for a moment she saw his eyes in the dark, and they were the eyes from the ridge, from the dream, from the gatehouse yard full of light — and then they weren't. They were just a man's eyes, wrecked and grateful and already apologizing.

"Sable." His voice was gravel. "No. No, you can't be here. They'll have watchers—"

"They don't. Bram checked. Bram gave me the route." She worked the key into the lock, fingers shaking, the filed iron catching twice before it caught true. "He says hello. He says you're an idiot. He says you always were."

A sound came out of Juniper that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed. "Sounds like him." The lock turned. The door swung, and she stepped into the cell, and he tried to stand and couldn't — the wire had held his arms bent too long, and the cold had gotten into his joints — so she knelt beside him instead, in the frost, and took his bound hands in hers.

The wire snapped like ice on a pond. Just — broke. Under her fingers, where she was touching him, where whatever he was reached out through his skin into hers. She felt it happen and did not pull away. She had stopped pulling away somewhere on the pilgrim road, and there was no road back to the person who hadn't known yet, and she didn't want one.

"They're going to burn you," she said quietly. "Dawn. The whole valley's coming. People I healed. People who fed us, sang with us—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "Maren's grandson drew the X on our door himself. He's twelve, Juniper. He carried water for me when I treated his grandmother. Twelve years old, and they've taught him your name means monster."

"I know." He flexed his freed hands, staring at them like they belonged to someone else. "I voted with them, you know. At the council. When they were deciding whether you were welcome here. I voted to turn you away, because I was afraid, and I told myself it was duty." He looked at her. "Everything I ever called duty was just fear wearing its good coat. And now they're going to burn me in a square I helped keep safe, and the worst part — the part I can't make peace with — is that I understand them. Completely. I'd have done the same, once. I nearly did."

"Then we're both cowards," Sable said. "Good. Cowards survive. Heroes burn at dawn." She stood and pulled him up. "Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Lie better."

"...Mostly."

* * *

Getting him out was the easy part, because the Order had made the mistake of believing its own sermon: they had locked the abomination away like a thing, and things don't have friends with tunnels. They crossed the tithe barn's cellar, went down through the grain-sack gap into the salt tunnel, and crawled westward under the valley while above them the bells rang the hour and the hammering continued — more planks, more nails, a pyre growing fat for a body that would never arrive.

It was in the tunnel, in the dark, crawling single file with his breath loud behind her, that Sable finally said the thing she had been carrying since the gatehouse yard.

"I saw you," she said, not stopping, because if she stopped she wouldn't start again. "When the herald came through. I saw what you are. All of it. The light, the fire you stole out of the air, the way the mountain answered you like you were its own voice." The tunnel narrowed and she dragged herself through it, words scraped thin by stone. "And I want you to hear me say this while you can't look at me, because then you'll know I'm not saying it to your face to soften it:

"It didn't frighten me.

"The valley is frightened. Let them be. But I grew up in a country where the Order burned my teacher for keeping a herb-lore older than their church, and I have been afraid of the wrong things my whole life, and I am done. What I felt, watching you fight that thing — it wasn't fear. It was like standing in a doorway during a storm and realizing the storm knows your name and is being *gentle* anyway."

Behind her, silence. Long enough that she thought he hadn't heard, or worse, that he had.

Then, low, almost lost under the scrape of stone: "You should be afraid of me. Everyone should be afraid of me. My mother was right about that much."

"Your mother was afraid," Sable said. "There's a difference, and it cost her everything, and it nearly cost you the same. Fear isn't wisdom, Juniper. It's just fear with a good coat on." She smiled at the dark ahead of her, bitter and fierce. "You taught me that phrase, or something like it. The night you told me about the council vote. So I'm giving it back."

He said nothing more for a long time. But his breathing changed — evened out, deepened, the way a man breathes when a weight shifts from his chest to someone else's hands — and Sable crawled on through the dark toward the tunnel mouth and the western passes and the rest of a life she could not yet see, carrying his breath behind her like a lamp.

* * *

They came up out of the ground an hour before dawn, in the birch scrub below the western crags, and the sky over Thornfell was going gray at the edges, and somewhere behind them the pyre stood finished and waiting, and the bells began to ring the hour, and kept ringing, because no one had come to stop them and no one would.

Bram was there, as promised, with two pack ponies, dried meat, a coil of rope, and a face like a cliff. He looked at Juniper for a long moment — the frost-scalded wrists, the hollowed eyes, the man-shaped ruin of him — and then he did something Sable had not expected. He embraced him. Briefly, hard, the way men embrace at departures they don't believe are temporary.

"Western passes are open till the first snow," Bram said against his shoulder. "Three weeks, maybe four. After that, no one crosses, including fools. Get to the coast, take ship south, don't send word, don't come back, don't be noble about it." He stepped back and gripped Juniper's neck, forehead to forehead, an old smuggler's blessing. "You were the best of us. That's not the valley's opinion. That's mine, and mine's the one that counts, because I've known you longest and robbed with you best."

"Bram—"

"If you say *thank you* I'm leaving you here." He turned to Sable and pressed something into her hand: a small tin of salve, tannery-made, for the rope burns she'd get on the crags. Then, quieter, so only she could hear: "Keep him alive. Not safe. Alive. There's a difference, and I think you know it."

"I know it."

The east was lightening. Down in the valley, faintly, they could hear voices gathering in the square — hundreds of them, murmuring, arranging themselves around a lattice of planks and a stake and a bundle of faggots, coming to watch a man burn who was already climbing away from them into the birches with frost still melting off his wrists.

Juniper stopped once, at the treeline, and looked back. Sable watched him do it — watched him take in the whole valley in one long breath: the roofs, the fields, the smoke, the pyre, the distant glint of the Saltgate high on the mountain where the seal he had wounded still stood, groaning, half-open, leaking light into a world that had decided to kill him for it.

"We can't fix it from here," Sable said gently. "Whatever's coming through that gate — running won't close it. We both know that."

"I know." He didn't turn from the view. "But I need to see it once more before I decide what I am." His jaw tightened. "Because I'm done letting other people decide. My mother. The council. The Order. Even you, Sable — even love. I'm walking up that mountain because I choose to, and God forgive me, but I think I always was going to. I think I was born walking up it."

Above them, far up the forbidden face of the mountain, the gate's light pulsed once — slow, patient, hungry — like a heartbeat in the rock.

Like something up there, in the dark behind the seal, had felt him look.

And was looking back.
