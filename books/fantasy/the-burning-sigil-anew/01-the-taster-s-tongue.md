## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first thing Beatrix Thornbury did every morning, before the ovens were lit and before the kitchen boys arrived with their buckets of ash, was poison themselves.

Not fatally. Never fatally. A drop of bitterleaf tincture in cold tea, a sliver of graycap mushroom folded into bread, a pinch of ground wraith's-bone stirred into porridge. Small doses, measured to the grain, the kind of doses that taught the body a poison's signature without ever letting it sign the guest book. Five years of this. Beatrix's tongue had become a library of suffering: it knew the copper kiss of ratbane, the almond ghost of bitter almond oil, the green rot-smell of hemlock steeped too long in wine. It knew forty-one poisons by taste and another dozen by smell alone.

It did not know what had been put in the regent's soup yesterday, and that fact sat in Beatrix's chest all through breakfast like a swallowed stone.

"Thornbury." The under-steward's voice carried down the kitchen corridor, flat as a slate. "His Grace requests the morning service."

"Of course he does," Beatrix said, and smiled the smile that made them invisible: pleasant, slightly dull, the smile of a person who has never once been interesting.

* * *

The regent's private dining room overlooked the eastern gardens, where gardeners were already trimming hedges into shapes that pretended to be swans. Regent Casimir Vell sat at the head of a table set for twelve, eating alone at one place setting, as he always did. He was not an old man, but he ate like one—slowly, deliberately, each spoonful lifted as though the air itself might have opinions about his food.

Beatrix stood at the sideboard with the tasting spoon and worked through the morning's courses the way a scribe works through a ledger. Cold pheasant with quince. Porridge with honey and toasted hazelnut. The soup—the same pale broth as yesterday, made from white beans and smoked ham bone.

Beatrix tasted it and stopped.

There it was again. Not a poison. Poisons announced themselves; this thing *withheld*. It tasted like the moment when you walk into a room and forget why you came. It tasted like a word on the tip of the tongue that will not come loose. It tasted, if Beatrix were pressed to describe it to anyone—which they never would be—like forgetting itself, poured warm into a bowl.

"You hesitate." Casimir did not look up from his plate.

"The beans are excellent this morning, Your Grace," Beatrix said. "I was savoring."

Casimir's eyes came up then. They were pale eyes, the color of watered milk, and they rested on Beatrix for exactly as long as they needed to and no longer. "You are the best taster I have employed," he said. "Do you know why?"

"Because I haven't died, Your Grace."

Something moved behind the regent's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a man who still owned one. "Because you taste *everything*, Thornbury. Even what isn't there." He returned to his soup. He ate all of it.

Beatrix stood very still while the serving footmen cleared, and thought about the ledger hidden beneath the third floorboard of their rented room, and about how many entries in that ledger began with someone saying *I can't remember*.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the kitchens proper, which was where Beatrix did their real work.

Officially, Beatrix Thornbury was the regent's food-taster, a position of modest honor and modest pay, housed in a modest attic room in the palace annex. Unofficially—and here was the thing that would have ended them, slowly and then quickly, if the wrong person learned it—Beatrix was also a charm-seller of the unregistered variety.

Magic in Caelmere required registration. Registration required a name entered in the Crown Rolls, a seal, a fee, and a sponsor of good standing. Beatrix had none of these things, or rather, had none of them *on paper*. What Beatrix had was a grandmother's training, a talent for small workings, and a deep, abiding conviction that the safest life was the life no one looked at twice.

So the charms were small. Deliberately, artfully small.

A knot-charm tied into a fisherman's net so the knots held through storm weather. A warming stone that kept a feverish child's bed gentle through the night. A finding-token shaped like a tin whistle that would grow faintly warm when its lost object was within twenty paces. Nothing that bent the world. Nothing that sang or flared or left a residue the wardens could trace. Just tiny corrections to the day's unfairnesses, priced at whatever the customer could pay, which was often nothing more than a loaf of bread or a promise.

The bread baskets were the delivery system, and they were beautiful.

Every second day, the palace kitchens sent out baskets of day-old loaves to the parish charities along Tanner's Row. Beatrix had long ago arranged—with a bribe here, a favor there, a charm of good-rising slipped to the head baker—to be the one who packed those baskets. And tucked into certain loaves, in wax paper folded to look like nothing, went the week's orders.

Today's orders numbered nine.

For Widow Sarelle on Cooper's Lane: a sleep-charm, because her dead husband's ghost was not a ghost at all but her own grief keeping her awake, and the charm wouldn't fix that, only let her rest enough to start fixing it herself. For the cooper's apprentice, Tam: a steadying charm for his hands, sewn into a leather wristband, because a boy who dropped a chisel on his master's foot once too often would soon find himself out of a trade. For old Fenwick the lamplighter: a joint-easing salve that smelled of rosemary and, faintly, of magic—but only faintly, only if you already knew what you were smelling for.

And for a woman named Odile Ferrant—who had left no name, only a note pushed under Beatrix's door three nights ago, written in the precise hand of someone who drew letters the way other people drew maps—a plain linen pouch containing a charm of true-seeing, priced at nothing, because the note had said *I need to see what is in front of me*, and Beatrix had recognized the tone of a person standing at the edge of something.

Beatrix packed the basket. Wrapped the loaves. Slid the wax-paper packets into the hollows baked into the crust bottoms—a trick of the head baker's, who never asked questions because asking questions was how bakers stopped being employed. Nine packets, nine lives touched, none of it recorded anywhere except—

Except the ledger.

That evening, in the attic room with the slanted ceiling and the view of nothing but chimney pots, Beatrix unlocked the floorboard and took out the book.

It was an ordinary book. That was the point. A merchant's day-book, bought secondhand, its first hundred pages filled with someone else's boring accounts of flour and tallow. Beatrix's entries lived in the back half, in a cipher of their own devising, each entry a small portrait:

*Sarelle, Cooper's Ln. Sleep. 3rd charm. She laughed today—first time since the funeral. Told me my bread was overpriced. Good sign.*

*Tam. Hands. He hasn't dropped anything in two weeks. His master called him "steady" in front of the whole shop. Steady. As if he'd always been.*

*Fenwick. Joints. Says the pain is "down to a whisper." Asked if I could do something about his hearing next. Told him miracles cost extra.*

Beatrix wrote tonight's entry by candlelight, the nib scratching in the quiet:

*Nine delivered. The Ferrant woman's charm is the strongest thing I've sent out in a year—true-seeing is not small magic, whatever I told myself when I made it. Something has her frightened. Something has everyone frightened, if you stand close enough and listen. The baker's wife asked me today whether I'd noticed the birds leaving. I said birds leave all the time. She looked at me like she knew I was lying, and maybe she did, because I have noticed. Three days now. No birds.*

Beatrix paused, nib hovering.

*I am invisible. This is the achievement of my life. I have made myself so useful and so unremarkable that the most powerful man in Caelmere trusts me with his throat, and the poorest people on Tanner's Row trust me with their griefs, and no one—not one soul in this city—knows my true name's worth, or what I could do if I stopped being careful. This is safety. This is competence. This is the loneliest thing I have ever built.*

They closed the book. Sat with their hand flat on its cover for a long moment, feeling the texture of the leather, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you loved and could not keep.

Then they blew out the candle, dosed themselves with tomorrow's harmless poison, and lay down to sleep the dreamless sleep of the carefully invisible.

* * *

They woke to bells.

Not the dawn bell—that was hours off, and anyway the dawn bell rang six times, always six times, had rung six times for as long as anyone in Vellamere could remember. These bells were ringing now, in the black heart of the night, and Beatrix lay in the dark counting against their own heartbeat.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

Below the attic window, the city was waking in confusion. Lamps bloomed in windows. A dog began to bark somewhere near the river, then another, then a chorus of them, a ragged alarm spreading street by street.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Beatrix sat up. The floorboards were cold under their bare feet. Through the little window, above the chimney pots and the slate roofs of Vellamere, the sky waited.

Twelve was the death knell count. Twelve was for the death of a sovereign. Twelve was the most the bells had ever rung in living memory, and Beatrix's mouth had gone dry, and some animal part of them wanted to dive back under the blankets and be nobody until morning.

The thirteenth bell rang.

It did not sound like the others. The others were bronze and certain; this one was thin, and high, and it went on far longer than a bell should, a note stretched like wire drawn to breaking, and Beatrix felt it not in their ears but in their teeth, in the roots of their teeth, in the marrow behind them.

And then the stars began to go out.

Not all at once. One by one, in no order Beatrix could read, the way candles are snuffed by an unseen hand moving through a great dark hall. The Shepherd. The Loom. The Sisters Three, which every child in Caelmere learned before they learned their letters. Each star held for a breath after the bell-note faded, flickered like a lamp low on oil, and then was simply not there, and the dark where it had been was not the dark of empty sky but a *flat* dark, a dark with no depth to it, as though a piece of the night had been cut away and taken somewhere else.

Beatrix stood at the window with their arms wrapped around themselves and watched the whole sky emptied, star by star by star, and the taster's tongue—the famous tongue, the tongue that knew forty-one poisons—tasted something new on the air.

It tasted like the soup.

It tasted like forgetting, poured across the heavens, and somewhere below in the streets people were shouting, and somewhere beyond the walls—Beatrix felt it rather than heard it, a sensation like a great door closing at the end of a very long corridor—the border wards of Caelmere flared once, silver and desperate, and died.

The last star went out. The moon hung alone in a sky with no depth at all, and it looked, Beatrix thought with a calm that frightened them more than panic would have, like an eye that had just opened.

They reached for the ledger without deciding to. Their hands found the floorboard, the book, the familiar worn cover, and they held it against their chest in the starless dark like a talisman, like a child's blanket, like the last record of a country that might, even now, be forgetting itself.

Outside, the bells began again.

They were ringing thirteen.
