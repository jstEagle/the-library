## 2. Thirteen Bells

The trouble with tasting poison for a living is that you stop trusting anything else.

I stood on the roof of the tannery on Cordwainer's Row with a cup of stolen wine and told myself the shiver down my spine was just the cold coming off the river. It was nearly midnight. The city below me was doing what Vellamere always did after dark: banking its fires, shuttering its windows, letting the watch call the hours in their flat bored voices. A dog barked itself hoarse near the fish market and gave up. Somewhere a drunk was singing a song about a miller's wife, badly, with feeling.

Ordinary. I made myself say the word in my head like a charm. Ordinary, ordinary, ordinary.

Because I'd felt it at supper. Not in the regent's food—that had been clean, every dish, which was its own small oddity because Master Corvel's sous-chef had been in a mood all day and moody cooks salt carelessly or worse—but in the air of the kitchens themselves. A taste like the moment before lightning. Like licking a copper coin while standing too close to a struck bell. I'd kept my face smooth and my hands busy and said nothing, because saying something is how you stop being invisible, and being invisible is how I've stayed alive five years in this city.

But I couldn't shake it. So I'd climbed.

From the tannery roof you can see most of Vellamere: the crooked spine of the Guildhall, the green-black bulk of the Regent's Keep on its hill, the walls—grey granite, forty feet, older than anyone could say—and beyond them, if you leaned out over the gutter, the dark country rolling away to the horizon where Caelmere ended and, presumably, other places began. I never looked past the walls much. There was nothing out there for me. Everything I had was inside these stones: my room above the chandler's, my ledger under a loose floorboard, my customers who bought luck-knots and fever-charms and pretended, when they paid me, to be buying nothing at all.

The bells began at midnight. That was right. That was correct. The Cathedral of Saint Aldhelm rang twelve for the turning of the day, and the sound rolled across the rooftops like a tide, and every night of my five years here I had counted it without counting—one through twelve, the great bell and then the answering peals, done.

Tonight the twelfth bell rang.

And then, after a pause long enough that the whole city seemed to lean toward the cathedral and hold its breath—

A thirteenth.

* * *

I have heard bells ring wrong exactly once before in my life, and that was the day my mother died, though I couldn't tell you what was wrong about it, only that my body knew before my mind did. The thirteenth bell did that to me now. It wasn't cracked. It wasn't off-key. It was simply a bell that should not exist, tolling a note so deep I felt it in my back teeth, in the wine in my cup, in the mortar between the bricks under my boots. The ripples it left in the rain barrel beside me were perfect concentric circles, unnaturally slow, like the water had been asked to be beautiful and didn't dare refuse.

Below me, doors opened. Shutters banged. The drunk stopped singing mid-verse.

"Count them again," someone hissed in the street. "Somebody count them again."

"I counted. I counted thirteen."

"That's not—" A woman's voice, high and thin. "That's not possible."

No one said what everyone was thinking, which was: *thirteen is an unregistered number*. That's a joke, mostly. Children say it. Thirteen bells, thirteenth step, the thirteenth loaf in a baker's dozen given to the devil—or to whatever came before the devil, depending on how old your grandmother is. In my trade we have a saying too: *the thirteenth charm is the one that costs you*. I'd never known what it meant. I'd written it in my ledger once, early on, under a list of folk-cautions, and reading it back later had given me the same cold slide down the spine as the bell was giving me now.

The bell finished dying. The silence afterward was worse.

And that's when the stars started going out.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only weapon I own.

They didn't flicker. They didn't dim. They went out the way a candle goes out when pinched between wet fingers—decisively, completely, each one leaving behind not darkness but absence, a hole in the sky with edges. And they went out in order. I wouldn't have believed stars could go in order; they're scattered like spilled salt, no order to be had. But these did. Starting in the east, low over the wall, a line of them snuffing westward like a row of lamps being walked past by someone with a snuffer, methodical, unhurried, thorough.

The Shepherd first—I knew him instantly, the bright knot of seven that Pip Ashdown would later swear he remembered better than his own mother's face. Then the Loom. Then the Long Oar that fishermen steer home by. Each one gone, and the sky where it had been not black but *empty*, a nothing that pulled at the eye the way a missing tooth pulls at the tongue.

I dropped my wine. I don't remember dropping it. I heard it burst on the tiles below and I was gripping the parapet with both hands and my mouth was moving around words I didn't know I knew—not spells, I don't cast spells, charms are different, charms are small and legal-adjacent and safe—but old words, cadenced words, words shaped like the inside of a prayer. My grandmother's voice, maybe. I never met her. She died in the provinces before I was born, in a village whose name I have somehow never been able to recall, though I've tried, on slow nights, more times than I can count.

The last star in the east went out, and the border wards woke up.

You've seen them if you've ever left Vellamere by the gates, though most people haven't, because why would you—the wards sit in the stone of the wall like veins of silver in ore, visible only when light catches them sideways, humming a note below hearing. Every child presses a hand to them once, on a dare, and jerks back laughing at the static sting. They are as much a part of the city as the bells. More. Bells you could melt down and recast; the wards are the reason Vellamere is Vellamere and not just a town waiting to be somebody's province.

They flared white.

Every vein of silver in forty feet of ancient granite lit at once, top to bottom, gate to gate, a cage of light thrown up around the whole city so suddenly that the rooftops threw shadows from a sky with no sun in it. The hum climbed until I heard it, until everyone heard it, a note like the thirteenth bell's twin, and for one heartbeat—one—the whole of Caelmere glowed like a coal breathed on.

It was, I am ashamed to write, beautiful. For one heartbeat I thought: *they're renewing themselves, the old things are waking, something is keeping us.*

Then they died.

Not faded. Died—all at once, everywhere, the way a man dies when the axe falls, with a finality you feel in your stomach. The light went out of the walls and the hum went out of the air and the silence that followed had weight, pressing down on the city like deep water. And I swear on my ledger, on every charm I ever sold, on the loose floorboard over my secret life: in the instant the wards died, the land beyond the wall changed.

There had been hills out there. Low blue hills, wooded, with a road winding over them toward the coast towns—everyone knows them, everyone has a cousin in the coast towns. What lay beyond the wall now was a grey flatness, featureless, like fog that had decided to hold still and pretend to be ground. No hills. No road. No lights of any farmstead or ferry crossing. Just nothing, smooth as poured milk, all the way to the edge of seeing.

And the edge of seeing was closer than it used to be.

* * *

I stayed on that roof until my knees unlocked, which took longer than I'd like to admit. Around me the city made its noises of fear: dogs first, then babies, then people, voices overlapping in the streets, shutters slamming, the watch whistles blowing in patterns I'd never heard—three short, three short, three long—which I later learned was the old ward-alarm, dug up from some manual nobody had read in a century, blown by men who didn know what it meant any better than I did.

I did what I always do. I inventoried.

Facts: the stars were gone. The wards were dead. The land beyond the wall was wrong. The bells had rung thirteen. None of these facts could be poisoned, spat into a napkin, or quietly replaced with a harmless substitute. All of them were true, and truth, tonight, was the one thing in Vellamere I couldn't render safe.

I got down off the roof the way I'd come up, hand over hand down the tanner's ladder, and I walked home through streets full of people standing in their doorways holding candles, and here is the thing I will remember longest, the thing that curdled the awe in me into something colder: nobody looked at each other. Five years I have lived among these neighbors, sold them charms, tasted their fears in the bread they brought me, and tonight, with the sky falling to pieces overhead, they stood shoulder to shoulder in the lamplight and studied the cobbles. Because looking at each other would mean asking questions, and questions in Vellamere have always been expensive, and some habits outlive even the end of the world.

An old woman grabbed my sleeve outside the chandler's. Widow Sarei, buys a hearing-sharpener every winter, pays in buttons.

"You're the one," she said. "The one who knows things."

"I sell charms," I said. "Charms for luck. Nothing else."

"The stars," she said, and her hand shook on my arm, and her eyes were wet and furious. "The stars are *gone*, girl. Where do we go to get them back?"

And I—who had spent the evening on a rooftop watching the sky get erased pane by pane, who had felt the wards die through the soles of my feet, who had seen the hills outside the wall become milk—looked her in the eye and lied.

"There's been a mistake," I said. "Bells miscount. Clouds move. Go home, widow. Lock your door. It'll look different in the morning."

She let go of my sleeve. She looked at me the way you look at a door that has just been locked in your face, and she went home, and I climbed my stairs and sat in the dark with my ledger open on my knees, and I did not light a lamp.

Here is what I wrote, by feel, in the column reserved for omens, which until tonight held only weather-notes and one entry about a two-headed calf:

*Night of the thirteenth bell. Stars extinguished in sequence, east to west. Wards flared and failed. Land beyond wall: absent. City unchanged. City unchanged. City unchanged.*

Three times, like a charm. As if writing it could make it so.

Then, underneath, smaller, the honest entry:

*I tasted this coming at supper. I said nothing. I always say nothing.*

I closed the book and put it back under the floorboard and lay down on my bed with my clothes on, listening to Vellamere breathe around me—a thousand households pretending to sleep, the watch whistles still calling their borrowed alarm, and beneath it all, so faint I might have invented it, a new sound from the direction of the Regent's Keep. A grinding, patient sound. Like a very large door being unbarred somewhere far underground.

In the morning, I told myself, I would go to the kitchens as always. I would taste the regent's breakfast. I would be useful and unremarkable and invisible, and the sky would be full of stars again, and Widow Sarei would buy her hearing-sharpener, and this would be a story we'd all laugh about at Midwinter.

The knock came at my door at dawn. Three blows, official spacing. A voice I knew from the palace corridors, flat as a summons:

"Taster. His Excellency requires you. Wear your good apron."
