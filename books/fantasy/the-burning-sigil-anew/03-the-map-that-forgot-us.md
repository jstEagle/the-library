## 3. The Map That Forgot Us

The ink was gone.

Not faded—gone. Odile Ferrant stood over her drafting table in the grey hour before dawn and stared at the border of Caelmere as if staring could argue it back into existence. Twenty years ago she had drawn that line herself: the Thornmark passes to the north, the river Vell running east to the sea, the salt marshes bleeding south toward a coast she had walked with her boots full of water and her heart full of certainty. She had drawn every mile of it twice, once in pencil and once in iron-gall ink, because a map that couldn't survive rain wasn't worth the vellum it shamed.

Now the line simply stopped. Her pen had, apparently, run out of country.

She touched the blank space with one finger, the way you'd touch a bruise. The parchment there was smooth, unblemished, exactly as blank as the day the calfskin was scraped. No ghost of ink. No watermark where the line had been lifted out. It was as though she had never drawn it at all—as though some hand had reached back through twenty years and unmade the work rather than erased it.

Odile had been stripped of her license seven years ago for "cartographic fraud," which was what the Guild called it when a woman corrected the Guild's maps and was right about it. She had appealed. She had shouted. She had finally done what proud people do when the institutions close their doors: she had gone home and kept working anyway, alone, at a smaller table, on maps no one would buy. This one—the Grand Survey of Caelmere, her masterwork, eleven years in the making—had never been submitted anywhere. It existed for her own satisfaction, which meant it existed for nothing, which meant she worked on it anyway, because pride is the last thing to starve.

And now its borders were eating themselves.

She lit a lamp and pulled down her other maps. The trade atlas from the Guild press: blank at the borders. Her field notebooks from the Thornmark survey: the sketches of the pass were intact, but the marginal notes—"here the ward-stones begin, old and humming"—had gone pale as watermarks. Even the cheap schoolroom map she'd bought off a street vendor to mock had lost its edges. Caelmere was shrinking on paper everywhere, retreating inward like a tide going out, and Odile stood in the middle of the room holding four different documents that all agreed on one impossible thing.

*The country is being forgotten,* said a voice in her head that sounded disgracefully like her mother's. *And you are the only fool keeping records.*

She sat down. She did not cry. Cartographers don't cry over lost territory; they re-survey.

* * *

By midmorning she had a system, because grief without method is just drowning. She laid out every map she owned in chronological order across the floor of her narrow house—her house was mostly drafting table, mostly always—and walked the timeline barefoot so she wouldn't scuff the vellum.

The oldest maps, forty years and more, were untouched. The Guild's grand atlas of the last century: borders crisp, mountains shaded, the whole country fat and confident on the page. Maps from thirty years ago: fine. Twenty: fine. Ten years ago, the year of her disgrace: fine.

Seven years ago—the year after her license was revoked, the year she'd stopped submitting anything to anyone—the rot began. Faintly. A softening at the northern passes, as if the engraver's hand had trembled. Five years ago, the edges of the salt marshes went vague. Three years ago, the river Vell's mouth dissolved into the sea it fed. And last night, sometime between her lamp going out and the grey hour, the rest had followed, all at once, like a held breath finally released.

Seven years. She turned the number over. Something had started then—or something had *stopped*. She thought of the Guild hall, the tribunal, the three men who'd looked through her corrections as if she were glass. She thought of how, in those same years, fewer and fewer travelers came through Vellamere claiming to have crossed the Thornmark. She'd noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping—not the moment itself, but the silence afterward. Caravans thinned. Foreign coin grew rare. The harbor pilots complained that ships no longer bothered with Caelmere's coast, sailing past as if the coastline weren't there.

She had blamed the Guild's bad charts for the decline in traffic. She had written an angry monograph about it. The Guild had not responded, because the Guild had stopped acknowledging that Odile Ferrant existed at all, which at the time had felt like the cruelest thing that would ever happen to her.

It was, she understood now, standing barefoot among her dying maps, merely practice.

* * *

In the afternoon she did something she hadn't done in years: she left the house.

Vellamere in autumn smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and the tanneries downriver, and Odile walked through it with her collar up, feeling the city look at her sideways. Seven years of disgrace had taught her exactly how much space to give people on the street. She went first to the map-sellers' row, where three licensed shops displayed the Guild's wares behind glass, and asked, in her most harmless voice, to see their newest survey of the realm.

The clerk—a boy barely old enough to shave—produced it proudly. Odile opened it on the counter with hands that wanted to shake and didn't.

Blank. All around, blank. The city of Vellamere sat in the center like an island, and around it the parchment faded into nothing within fifty miles. No Thornmark. No Vell. No coast. The boy saw her looking and frowned. "Is something wrong, madam?"

"Your border work," she said. "It's very... minimal."

"That's how they've always printed it." He sounded puzzled at his own words even as he said them, and Odile watched the puzzlement cross his face and get put away, unexamined, the way people put away things that don't fit. "The Surveyors' Guild keeps the interior detailed. The borders are, ah. Administrative."

"The borders are *blank*," she said.

"They're administrative," he repeated, with the serene confidence of a man reciting something that had been recited at him, and Odile paid him for the map and left before she made a scene, because scenes were how she'd lost everything the first time.

Outside, she stood under the eaves and let herself think the thought properly, the way you take off a bandage slowly.

Either everyone in Vellamere had agreed overnight to print empty maps—which was absurd—or everyone had always printed empty maps and none of them had ever noticed, which was worse than absurd, which was *wrong*, wrong in the deep structural way that made her professional instincts scream. A map is a memory of a place. If the memory has a hole in it, someone made the hole. And holes this clean, this unanimous, this *unnoticed*—

That wasn't cartography. That was magic. And magic, in Vellamere, was a thing that happened quietly, in back rooms, for small coins, among people who officially didn't believe in it.

Which brought her, by evening, to the rumor.

* * *

She heard it from a laundress, because laundresses hear everything; it was one of the few kinds of intelligence Odile's profession had taught her to gather, back when she'd still imagined she might petition the Guild with evidence of its own errors. The laundress was wringing sheets in the communal wash-house while Odile waited with her bundle, and complaining, as laundresses must, about prices.

"—and it's not just flour, it's everything, my sister swears the bread tastes less than it used to, and don't laugh, Marta, taste can be *less*, ask anyone who's sick and eating—"

"Superstition," said Marta.

"It's not superstition when you pay for it. Old Sefa down our lane, her boy had the night-terrors, screaming till the neighbors banged the wall, and she took him to the charm-woman and it cost her two coppers and a heel of bread and the boy slept. Two coppers! The physician wanted a silver just to look at him."

"What charm-woman?" Odile asked, before she could decide whether to sound interested.

Both women looked at her. She arranged her face into something harmless: a thin, tired woman with ink stains she'd failed to scrub from her fingers, holding laundry she clearly resented having to wash herself. Disgrace had its uses. Nobody suspected a fallen woman of anything except falling further.

"There's a taster up at the regent's kitchens," the laundress said, lowering her voice as if the walls had licenses too. "Thornbury. Quiet one. Does a bit of—work. On the side. Little charms, blessings, that sort of thing. You leave word with the baker on Coriander Street, third door past the shrine, and if the wind's right, you get an answer."

"Does it work?" Odile asked.

Marta snorted. The laundress shrugged with elaborate unconcern. "Sefa's boy sleeps."

* * *

Coriander Street at dusk was all shutters and cooking smoke, and the bakery's windows glowed the particular gold that means someone is working late and loves it. Odile stood across the lane for a long moment, feeling ridiculous. She was fifty-one years old. She had surveyed mountain passes in winter. She had faced down a tribunal of three men with her evidence spread out like a battle plan. And here she was, hovering outside a bakery, about to beg a kitchen servant for a spell.

But the maps were blank. The maps were blank, and she was the only person in Vellamere who seemed to have noticed, and a person who notices alone starts to doubt her own eyes, and Odile Ferrant had lost enough to doubt already. She needed a second pair of eyes. Preferably eyes attached to someone who dealt in the kind of quiet wrongness that eats borders off parchment.

She went in.

The baker was a broad, floury man who took her measure in one glance and said, "Closed," with the warmth of a slammed door.

"I'm told," Odile said carefully, "that if the wind's right, one can leave word."

The warmth did not improve. But something behind his eyes shifted—a flicker, a weighing. He looked at her ink-stained fingers. He looked at her face, which she tried to make into the face of a desperate grandmother instead of a disgraced cartographer with a theory.

"You'll leave the word with me," he said, "and I'll pass it along if I pass it along. No promises. No names. What's the need?"

"A map," Odile said. Then, because that sounded insane, she amended: "A question about maps."

The baker's eyebrows performed a small skeptical dance. "That's a new one."

"It's an old question," Odile said. "It's just newly urgent."

He grunted, wiped his hands on his apron, and tore a corner off a scrap of paper. "Write it. Small. I'm not a post office."

She wrote: *The borders are going blank. I need someone who knows why.* She hesitated, then added, because pride is also honesty when you're this tired: *I used to be a cartographer. My name is Odile Ferrant.*

The baker read it, and she watched him arrive at her name and flinch slightly—the way people flinch at names they half-remember from scandal—and she stood there and absorbed the flinch, because that was the tax on being Odile Ferrant, and she had paid it so long it barely stung anymore. Barely.

"He'll come or he won't," the baker said, folding the scrap into his apron pocket. "Don't wait up."

* * *

She waited up. Of course she waited up. She spent the hours doing what she always did with unbearable waiting: working. She cleared a space on the big table and began the only sensible response to a vanishing country, which was to document it. She laid the blank-edged survey flat and traced its outline onto fresh paper, marking each point where the old line had died, dating it, annotating the sequence of loss. North passes: seven years. Marshes: five. River-mouth: three. Everything else: last night.

A pattern. Loss moving inward, slow for years and then sudden, like ice going out on a river—one warm day and the whole sheet lets go. Whatever was happening had been happening for a long time, patiently, and had recently stopped being patient.

She was drawing a small, careful moon in the margin—it was nearly full tonight, hanging fat and yellow over the rooftops when she'd checked, and she marked its phase the way she marked all celestial data, automatically, because a good map recorded the sky above the land as well as the land beneath the sky—when she realized she couldn't remember what tomorrow's phase should be.

She checked her almanac. The almanac gave the moon's phases through the twelfth turn of the year and then simply stopped, the tables ending mid-column, the printer having evidently lost interest. She checked another almanac. The same. Every astronomical table in her possession ended at the twelfth turn, as if the year itself had decided to stop being counted.

"That's not possible," she told the empty room. "Years turn. That's what years *do*."

The empty room declined to reassure her.

* * *

Near midnight, a knock. Not on her door—on her *window*, two floors up, which was impossible, and Odile went to it anyway because her life had recently renegotiated its relationship with the impossible.

A figure crouched on the roof of the coal shed below her window, hooded, slight, balanced with the ease of someone who climbed things for a living or for fun. Moonlight caught a sharp, watchful face beneath the hood—youngish, maybe thirty, with the careful blankness of a person who had practiced not being noticed until the practice became the face.

"Odile Ferrant," the figure said. Not a question.

"You got my note."

"I got your note." A pause. "You wrote your real name. On a note. Passed through a bakery."

"I assumed anonymity was pointless if I wanted an answer."

Something moved at the corner of the stranger's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile lived. "Fair. Most people lie. It's refreshing when they don't bother." The figure glanced up at the sky, at the fat yellow moon, and Odile saw the glance linger a beat too long, the way you look at a clock you suspect is wrong. "You said the borders are going blank."

"On paper," Odile said. "Every map I own. Every map in the shops. Seven years of erosion, finished in one night. And the almanacs end at the twelfth turn, and the trade routes dried up years ago, and nobody notices, and I need to know if I'm mad."

The figure was quiet for a moment. When they spoke again, the careful blankness had cracked, just slightly, and underneath it was something tired and alert and terribly young. "You're not mad. Last night the bells rang thirteen times. Thirteen. And the stars went out, one by one, and I watched from a rooftop because I couldn't sleep, and this morning the wards on the wall were dead—cold, dark, like a hearth after the fire's been raked. And nobody in the kitchens mentioned it. Nobody anywhere mentions it. Except—" the figure hesitated, "—a child drew me the old constellations today. Stars I'd never seen. Stars nobody remembers. Except her."

Odile's heart was doing something undignified. "Who are you?"

"Nobody," the figure said promptly. "That's rather the point of me. I taste the regent's food and I sell charms that officially don't exist and I am extremely good at being unremarkable, and I climbed up here because your note was the first interesting thing to happen to me in five years, and I hate myself for finding it interesting." A breath. "Show me the maps."

Odile stepped back from the window. "There's a door."

"There's always a door," said the charm-seller, and dropped lightly from the coal shed to the alley, and Odile went down to open it, and found herself thinking, with a hope so rusty it squeaked, that for the first time in seven years, someone believed her.

The knock came at her door a moment later. She opened it on the hooded figure, who pushed the hood back and looked past her shoulder at the great survey spread across the floor—at the country with no edges, lying there like a body missing its skin.

"Oh," Beatrix Thornbury said softly. "Oh, that's what it looks like from the outside."
