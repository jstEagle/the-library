## 4. Neighbors Who Pretend

The morning after the stars went out, Beatrix did what they always did: they lit the stove, set the kettle on, and opened the shutters of the little shop-front room exactly as far as they always opened them—two hands' width, no more. Enough to show the bread baskets and the bundles of dried herbs and the row of small clay charms that were, officially, decorative.

Unofficially, by noon, every charm was gone.

That was wrong. That was the first thing wrong. Beatrix had sold charms in Vellamere for five years at a rate of perhaps three a week, always to people who arrived with elaborate pretexts—a remedy for a cough, a blessing for a wedding—and left with a knotted thread or a salted coin cupped in their palm like something shameful. Vellamere did not believe in magic. Vellamere *pretended* not to believe in magic, which was a different thing entirely, and Beatrix had built their whole survival on knowing the difference.

But today the customers came straight up the stairs, no pretext, faces gray as unlit coals, and asked for charms the way drowning people ask for rope.

"The night one," said Widow Sarel from the third floor, gripping her shawl. "The one you sold me last winter. The one for bad dreams."

"It's a decorative knot," Beatrix said mildly, wrapping a fresh loaf for the man behind her. "I'm afraid I can't sell you superstition twice."

"Beatrix." Her voice cracked on the name. "The stars are *gone*."

"They're cloudy," Beatrix said. "It's autumn. It's always cloudy." They slid the loaf across the counter and did not meet her eyes, because meeting her eyes would have been an act of mercy, and mercy was a door you couldn't close again.

Widow Sarel bought the knot anyway. She paid double without being asked. She left clutching it like a child's hand.

* * *

By mid-afternoon Beatrix had sold out of everything—the knots, the salted coins, the pressed-flower wards, even the three plain river-stones they kept in a dish for people who wanted to buy magic but couldn't admit it. The dish was empty. The ledger under the floorboard had four new entries in their small, slanted hand:

*Sarel, W. — night-knot, doubled price, didn't haggle. Afraid.*
*Tomas the cooper — hearth-thread. Said "for my daughter" but has no daughter living. Afraid.*
*Two brothers off Tanner's Row — road-salt each. Asked if it works against "what's coming." Didn't say what. Afraid.*
*A man I didn't know — bought nothing. Stood in the doorway a long time. Asked if I'd seen the sky.*

They had seen the sky. They had stood on the bakery roof until their fingers went numb, watching the last star gutter out like a wick pinched between godly fingers, and they had felt the border-wards die—not heard, not seen, *felt*, the way you feel a tooth go quiet after days of ache. Five years of dosing themselves with harmless poisons had given Beatrix a tongue that noticed things. The whole city had a taste this morning, faint and wrong, like water from a pipe that had been shut off too long.

And underneath it, almost too faint to catch: the taste of forgetting. The same taste that had been in the regent's soup once, two years ago, a soup Beatrix had spat into a napkin and never reported, because reporting it would have meant explaining how they knew, and knowing things was dangerous, and Beatrix had survived five years in Vellamere precisely by not knowing anything out loud.

Now the whole city tasted of it, and there was nowhere to spit.

* * *

The knock came at dusk. Three soft raps, then a pause, then two more—an old signal, half-forgotten, meaning *I know what you are and I need help*. Beatrix hadn't heard it in over a year.

They opened the door on Odile Ferrant.

Beatrix knew her by sight the way everyone in the quarter did: the disgraced cartographer, tall and angular, with ink permanently shadowing the cuticles of her right hand and a way of looking at people as though measuring them for inclusion on a map she'd never finish drawing. She'd been stripped of her license six years back—for falsifying borders, the story went, though everyone whispered the real crime was that her maps had been *accurate*.

"I need—" Odile began, and stopped. She looked past Beatrix into the room, at the empty charm-dish, at the ledger-shaped gap beneath the loose floorboard that she absolutely should not have known about. "I need to ask you about the sky."

"The sky," Beatrix repeated.

"My maps are blank." The words came out flat and fast, like stones dropped down a well. "Not faded. Not damaged. Blank. Every map I've ever drawn of Caelmere—the borders are gone. Not erased. *Gone*, as if the paper never held ink there. And I've been asking myself all day who in this city would know whether that's happened before, and every answer came back to the same door." She lifted her chin. "Yours."

"You've mistaken me for someone useful," Beatrix said. "I sell bread and decorations."

"Last spring you sold my neighbor's boy a fever-charm that worked."

"A warm cloth and chicken broth work wonders."

"He told me it hummed."

"Children imagine things."

Odile's eyes narrowed. She had the particular patience of someone who had spent years hunched over parchment waiting for ink to dry, and she deployed it now, standing in the doorway, saying nothing, letting the silence do her arguing for her.

Beatrix hated her a little for it.

"Come in before someone sees you," they said finally. "You're making my shop look interesting."

* * *

They sat across the table from each other with tea neither of them drank, and Odile laid out her evidence like a case before a magistrate: the blank maps, the thirteen bells, the wards she claimed she could feel dying the way Beatrix could taste them—though she said *feel* where Beatrix would only ever think *taste*, and both of them pretended not to notice the other flinch.

"So you're unregistered too," Beatrix said at last.

"I'm licensed," Odile said bitterly. "Or I was. Which is worse. At least the unregistered get to choose their own small crimes." She turned her teacup in its saucer, around and around. "The maps aren't just blank at the edges. I checked the archives today. Every cartographic record of Caelmere in the city is intact. It's only mine. Only the ones drawn by hand, by memory, by *me*." Her voice thinned. "As if the country is erasing itself outward from wherever people remember it hardest, and I remember it too well."

"That's not how magic works."

"How would you know?"

"I don't," Beatrix admitted. "That's rather the point of me."

Outside, the city bells rang the evening hour—six strokes, seven—and Beatrix counted them automatically, the way they counted everything now, waiting for a thirteenth bell that didn't come. Yet.

"What do you want from me?" they asked.

"Honestly? Confirmation that I haven't gone mad." Odile smiled without any humor in it. "Failing that, charms. Whatever you have left. For the road."

"What road?"

"The one out of here. There's an old legend—I found it in a marginal note in a surveyor's journal from before the Regency. A Doorway. Under the Salt Cathedral, if the journal's geography means anything, which is itself a question, since the Salt Cathedral burned down forty years ago and nobody remembers why." She looked up. "You're going to tell me legends aren't your trade either."

"I was going to tell you the Salt Cathedral is a ruin full of pigeons and unstable masonry."

"And after that?"

Beatrix looked at the empty charm-dish. At the ledger hidden under the floorboard, four entries heavier than yesterday. At the window, where the dark outside had begun to seem less like night and more like absence.

"After that," they said carefully, "I'd tell you to come back tomorrow. I don't sell to strangers on the same day they accuse me of crimes."

"I haven't accused you of anything."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "You've done something much ruder. You've *noticed* me."

* * *

Odile had barely gone when the second knock came, and this one Beatrix recognized instantly, because it belonged to nine-year-old Pip Ashdown from the fourth floor, whose knock was less a knock than a drumroll performed by someone with more enthusiasm than coordination.

Pip burst in before the door was fully open, which was also characteristic.

"Mistress Thornbury, Mistress Thornbury, Mama says the stars fell down and Papa says they're just behind clouds and Grandda says he saw this before, in the war, except nobody knows what war he means, and *nobody will tell me anything*—" Pip ran out of breath, stood panting, and then, recovering with the resilience unique to children and certain species of weed, planted both hands on Beatrix's counter. "So I've come to you, because you know things."

"I sell bread," Beatrix said.

"You sell *bread*," Pip agreed solemnly, "and also my mama's headache went away after she wore that flower you gave her, and also Mr. Cooper's dog stopped howling at ghosts, and also—" Pip dug into a coat pocket and produced a folded piece of paper, crushed and re-folded many times, soft as cloth. "Also I drew this."

Beatrix unfolded it.

Charcoal on cheap paper. A sky. Not the sky outside the window—that was a flat black nothing, starless as a closed eye—but a sky remembered. Pip had drawn constellations: careful, looping figures connected dot to dot, labeled in a child's uneven hand. *The Plowwoman. The Nine Sisters. The Hare Chasing Its Own Tail. The Lantern-Bearer.* And at the top, drawn largest of all, a great wheeling figure with arms spread wide, marked with a single word:

*THE BURNING SIGIL.*

Beatrix's hands went still.

Because these were not made-up shapes. These were not a child's inventions. Beatrix had grown up in a household where an old woman—dead now, name half-lost even then—had pointed at the winter sky through a smoke-hole and named the stars one by one, and those names had been *these names*, and those shapes had been *these shapes*, down to the tilt of the Hare's ears and the gap-toothed smile of the Lantern-Bearer.

Stars no one else in Vellamere seemed to recall. Stars that had gone out two nights ago while the whole city slept through it, or woke and called it weather, or woke and said nothing at all.

"Pip," Beatrix said, and their voice came out strange, "who taught you these?"

"Nobody." Pip shrugged. "I just know them. Like I know my own teeth. Don't you?"

Don't you.

The question hung in the lamplight. Beatrix thought of the ledger under the floorboard, five years of small magics recorded in secret. Thought of the regent's kitchen, the soup that tasted of forgetting. Thought of Odile's blank maps, erasing themselves outward from the places people remembered hardest—and if that was true, then the opposite must be true as well, and here stood a child holding the proof of it in charcoal and cheap paper.

Here stood a child who remembered the sky so hard it might hold.

"Yes," Beatrix said quietly. "I do know them."

Pip beamed, radiant, triumphant, utterly unaware of having just detonated something at the center of Beatrix's careful, invisible, five-years-in-the-making life.

"Can I leave the picture with you?" Pip asked. "Mama says I shouldn't talk about the stars anymore. She says it makes the neighbors nervous. But somebody ought to keep them somewhere safe, don't you think? Until they come back?"

Somebody ought to keep them somewhere safe.

Beatrix looked at the child—at the smudged cheeks and the fierce, unguarded certainty—and felt the cost of invisibility come due all at once, with interest. Five years of pretending not to know. Five years of selling courage in secret and denying it in daylight. And now the city was fading at its edges, and the people who knocked on doors were afraid, and the only person in Vellamere carrying the old sky around in their head was nine years old and being told to hush.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Leave it with me. I'll keep it safe."

Pip pounded back up the stairs, drumming on every banister along the way.

Beatrix stood alone in the lamplight, the drawing open on the counter, the Burning Sigil wheeling overhead in charcoal above a city that had forgotten it. Then they pried up the floorboard, took out the ledger, and added a fifth entry—the first one that wasn't a sale:

*Pip Ashdown, age 9. Remembers the old sky entire. This is either the most dangerous thing in Caelmere or the most precious, and I no longer believe there's a difference.*

Below it, after a long moment, they wrote one line more:

*Tomorrow I stop pretending.*

Somewhere across the rooftops, faint as a held breath, the evening bells began to ring again—and Beatrix counted, heart slowing with each stroke—six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve—

Thirteen.
