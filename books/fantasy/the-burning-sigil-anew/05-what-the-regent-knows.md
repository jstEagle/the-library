## 5. What the Regent Knows

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a page who wouldn't meet my eyes.

"His Grace requests the taster," the boy recited, staring hard at a point somewhere past my left ear. "Before the mid-morning meal. The Gray Salon. Alone."

*Alone.* In five years of service, I had never been summoned alone. Tasters were furniture—useful, unremarkable, present in clusters like chairs or candlesticks. A regent did not dine privately with his furniture.

I pressed a copper into the page's palm and he fled down the stairwell as if I'd offered him something cursed instead.

My rooms above the chandler's shop were small enough to cross in four steps, and I crossed them twice, gathering what I'd need. Not much. A taster travels light: clean hands, a neutral face, and a stomach trained to hold anything. I checked my hidden ledger one last time—slid it beneath the loose floorboard, beneath the false bottom, beneath the charm that made searching eyes slide off it—and told myself the tremor in my fingers was just the cold.

It wasn't cold. It was the thirteenth bell still ringing somewhere behind my ribs, three days after the night the stars went out.

Vellamere had not recovered. The city moved through its mornings the way a sleepwalker moves through a house—familiar motions, wrong reasons. Bakers baked. Guards marched. But the lamplighters no longer sang their rounds, and the fishwives had stopped arguing about prices, and everyone, everyone, kept glancing up at a sky that gave them nothing back. Just gray. Just an even, sourceless gray, like the inside of a closed lid.

And now the regent wanted me.

I dressed in my service grays, pinned the small silver spoon of my office to my collar—the only ornament permitted me—and went to be looked at by power for the first time in my life.

* * *

The Gray Salon earned its name honestly. Everything in it was gray: gray marble, gray silk, gray tapestries so faded they might have depicted battles or banquets or nothing at all. Even the windows had been fitted with smoked glass, so that the light coming through was the same sourceless gray as the sky outside. Someone had designed this room to match the world, or perhaps the world had simply learned its manners from this room.

Regent Casimir sat at the far end behind a table set for two.

He was smaller than I remembered from the feast-day viewings—a thin man in dove-gray robes, with a long, patient face like something carved for a church and then forgotten. His hair was white without being old-white; it had gone colorless the way paper goes colorless, bleached rather than aged. He was pouring tea when I entered, and he did not look up until the cup was perfectly full.

"Beatrix Thornbury." My name in his mouth sounded strange, like a coin from a dead country. "You'll forgive the theater of a formal summons. I find that if I ask for things quietly, people pretend not to hear."

"Your Grace honors me." I bowed the exact depth required—not deep enough to suggest fear, not shallow enough to suggest pride. Five years of practice in a single motion.

"Honors you." He considered this. "Sit. Eat with me. That is not an order, but it is very nearly one."

There were two places set. Two. And between them, on a covered silver dish, sat the reason I had been brought here—I knew it the way I knew poison, which is to say instantly and in my bones.

I sat.

Casimir lifted the cover himself. No servants. Of course no servants. Under the dome lay a small portion of quail in a dark sauce, arranged with the kind of precision that spoke of a kitchen terrified of the man eating it. The smell reached me first, and my tongue—the tongue that had been ruined for ordinary food years ago, the tongue that could find arsenic in honey and heartsbane in wine—that tongue went quiet.

Not silent. *Quiet.* The way a room goes quiet when someone important enters.

"You will taste this," Casimir said, "and tell me what you find."

"Your Grace, standard practice requires—"

"I know your standard practice. I wrote half of it." He pushed the dish toward me with one finger. "There are no protocols today. There is only you, and me, and this. Taste it."

A dozen escape routes presented themselves and I dismissed them one by one. Refusal was itself an answer. Feigning illness would buy hours, not safety. And underneath all my careful reasoning, something older and stranger was pulling me forward—the same pull that had drawn me to the rooftop the night the bells rang thirteen times. When the world cracks, some part of us wants to look.

I took the fork. I took a piece of quail, no larger than a thumbnail, and I put it on my tongue.

For a moment there was nothing. Quail, salt, a sauce built on dark stock and dried plum. Competent palace cooking, slightly over-reduced because someone in the kitchens had been distracted—which, given the state of Vellamere, was forgivable.

Then the second taste arrived.

It didn't taste like anything. That was the horror of it—it tasted like the *absence* of things. Like drinking cold water after crying, except the thing being washed away wasn't salt. It was— I reached for the word and watched the word dissolve as I reached for it. Something had been in my mind a moment ago. Something with a shape. A shape like—

Like a kitchen. Warm bread. A woman's voice saying a name—

Whose kitchen? Whose voice? Whose—

I gripped the edge of the table. My own name. I held onto my own name the way you grip a rail in a storm. *Beatrix Thornbury. Beatrix Thornbury.* The words felt suddenly like a rope thrown across a gap, and I could not have said why, only that if I let go of them I would fall into a gray place where there had never been a Beatrix at all.

"There it is," Casimir said softly. He was watching me with an expression I had never seen on a ruler's face. It looked almost like grief. "You can taste it. I hoped someone could. They tell me the palatine chefs can't detect it at all anymore. The guild masters can't. Either the stuff has grown too subtle, or everyone else has already stopped noticing what it takes away." He folded his hands. "Tell me what it tastes like, taster. Use whatever words you have left."

"It tastes like forgetting," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It tastes like the space where something used to be. Your Grace—" I swallowed, and the swallow hurt. "What is it?"

"What is it," he repeated, and laughed—a dry, papery sound with no pleasure in it. "That is the question, isn't it. I've asked it for thirty years." He rose, walked to the smoked window, and stood with his back to me, a thin gray figure against gray glass. "Do you know what a regent is, Beatrix Thornbury? Do you know what the word means?"

"One who rules in another's stead. Until the true heir—"

"Yes. Until the true heir returns. Until the king comes home. Until the war ends. Every country has its waiting story." He turned his head, just slightly. "Ours is older than most. Tell me—what do you remember of the founding of Caelmere? Who was the first king? What was Caelmere called before it was Caelmere?"

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out.

Not the way a name escapes you when you're tired—this was different. This was a door in my mind, and behind the door there was a room, and the room was *painted over*. Smooth gray plaster where history should have been. I could feel the edges of the answer, the way your tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. There had been a name before Caelmere. There had been a first king, or maybe there hadn't been a king at all, maybe there had been something else entirely—and every trace of it had been scoured out of me so cleanly that I hadn't known anything was missing until this moment.

"No," I whispered.

"Mm." Casimir returned to the table and sat, folding himself back into his chair with the care of a man conserving his last strength. "Every citizen of this country carries that same painted-over room. Scholars included. Especially scholars—they've written forty treatises on the early history of Caelmere, and every one of them is a circle drawn around an absence. We are a nation with a hole where its beginning should be, and we have agreed, all of us, without ever discussing it, not to look at the hole."

"The dish," I said. My hands would not stop shaking now, and I let him see it. "The dish is full of it. Why serve it to me? Why serve it to yourself?" Because I saw it now—there was only one dish, and he had eaten from it too, a small precise bite while I was still finding my courage.

"Because I needed to know if anyone could still taste the truth of it." He looked at me, and for the first time I saw how tired he was—tired down past the bone, tired in whatever part of a person holds their name. "And because tomorrow the kitchens begin serving it to the city. Bread, soup, the water supply if it comes to that. A gentle dose. Enough to smooth the panic. People are frightened, taster. Frightened people do dangerous things—pray to vanished stars, gather in the streets, start remembering things that were sold a long time ago. A little forgetting is a mercy. I have told myself that for thirty years." He smiled, and it was the loneliest smile I have ever seen. "I have gotten very good at telling myself things."

"Sold," I said. "You said *sold*."

Did he flinch? A ruler doesn't flinch. But something behind his face shifted, the way tapestries shift in a draft.

"A bargain has two sides," he said. "One side gives. One side receives. And both sides sign." He touched the rim of his teacup without lifting it. "When I was young—before the gray, before any of this—I was not the regent. I was the regent's understudy, a spare part of the court, and I found a document. In the under-croft below the Salt Cathedral, if you must know, though the way down has been sealed since my predecessor's time. A contract. Old beyond reading, except that it read *me*, if you understand. It wanted to be understood." His voice dropped. "Caelmere was dying once, long ago. Plague, invasion, famine—I don't know which; the document was mercifully vague on our sufferings. And someone—kings, priests, I don't know—went to a party whose name is also painted over, and bought protection. Wards at the border strong enough to keep out the end of the world. And the price—" He laughed again, that papery laugh. "The price was the country's name. Its true name, its old sky, its memory of itself. All of it, signed away, so that no enemy could ever curse what could not be named. We became Caelmere, a placeholder, a word meaning *the country that remains*. And the wards held. For centuries, the wards held beautifully."

"And now they've fallen," I said.

"Now the term has expired." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than drama. "Contracts end, taster. Ours ran out three nights ago, when the bells rang thirteen. Whatever we bargained with has collected. The stars were the first installment. The rest follows—the moon is keeping count, if you've looked up lately. And so I am left with a country full of frightened people and one asset remaining." He gestured at the dish between us. "Forgetting. The last dregs of the old magic, distilled. If they cannot remember what they lost, they cannot grieve it. If they cannot grieve it, they will not riot. Vellamere stays calm. Order holds. And perhaps, in a generation or two, no one will mind living inside a gray lid."

"You'd erase them," I said. "To save them."

"I'd *anesthetize* them," Casimir said, with sudden sharpness, and for a moment the carved-church face cracked and showed me the man underneath—young once, reading a forbidden contract by candlelight, understanding too late what his predecessors had signed. "I have watched this country forget for thirty years, taster. I have forgotten things myself. Do you imagine I don't know what I'm serving?" He straightened, and the crack sealed, and he was the regent again, smooth and gray and terrible. "You will speak of this to no one. You will return to your kitchens and taste as you've always tasted, and when the new shipments arrive you will find them, as you always do, unremarkable."

"And if I don't?"

The question was out before I could weigh it. Terror does that—loosens the tongue at exactly the wrong moment.

Casimir regarded me for a long moment. Then he did something I will think about for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. He leaned forward and, in a voice stripped of all authority, all rank, all gray, he said:

"Then you will do what I should have done thirty years ago, and I will be unable to stop you, and I find, sitting here, that I am not certain I want to." He rose. The audience was over. "Go home, Beatrix Thornbury. Hold onto your name. It's worth more than you know."

* * *

I walked back through Vellamere in a daze, and the city had changed for me, the way a face changes once you've seen the skull underneath.

The baker hauling his shutters open: how much of his childhood had already been skimmed off him, gently, generation by generation? The wardens at the corner, bored and gray-uniformed: guarding borders that no longer existed, protecting a name that had been pawned before their grandmothers were born. The smoked-glass sky pressing down on all of it like a lid on a dish.

*A lid on a dish.*

I had spent five years tasting for this crown. I had dosed myself with harmless poisons until my tongue became an instrument, and I had told myself it was craft, discipline, competence—loneliness wearing a uniform. And all the while I had been training, without knowing it, to taste the single most important flavor of my lifetime: the flavor of a country swallowing its own name.

At my door, I paused. Through the chandler's window across the lane, I could see a customer waiting—little Pip Ashgrave, the neighbor's child, clutching something rolled up tight in one fist. Paper. A drawing, by the size of it. Pip saw me and waved, urgent, unafraid, the way children wave when they have something burning in them.

Everyone in this city was pretending. The regent pretended the forgetting was mercy. The neighbors pretended the charms were nonsense. The scholars pretended the hole in our history was a scholarly disagreement.

But the child in the window wasn't pretending about anything. Children never got the memo.

I unlocked my door, and let Pip in, and began—for the first time in five years—to tell the truth.
