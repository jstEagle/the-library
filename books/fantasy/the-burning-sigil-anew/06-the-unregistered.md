## 6. The Unregistered

Odile had spent three days doing the one thing a licensed cartographer was trained never to do: drawing from memory.

She sat at the scarred table in her rented room with a sheet of cheap paper before her, charcoal in hand, and tried to render the city she had mapped for twenty years. It should have been easy. She had drawn Vellamere's streets so many times she could do it blind, in the dark, in a moving cart. She had once reproduced the entire Salt Road from a two-second glance at a rival's atlas, and the guild had called it theft and stripped her license for it, and she had hated them for years, and now—

Now she would have given anything to steal a map that still existed.

The paper came out wrong. Not blank—wrong. The streets she drew curved where they should have run straight. The river bent like a question mark. When she drew the border, her hand stalled, the charcoal skidding into a smear, and the smear looked less like ink than like fog. Like the paper itself refusing to hold the line.

She tried again. And again. By the fourth sheet her hands were shaking, not with fear exactly, but with the specific vertigo of an expert discovering the ground had been pulled out from under her expertise.

*You are not a cartographer anymore,* the guild had told her, five years ago, when they took her seal. *You are a woman who draws pictures.*

Fine. Then let the woman who draws pictures admit what she was seeing: the city was forgetting its own shape. And it was forgetting from the edges inward.

She had gone out that morning to check. Walked to the Tanners' Gate, the easternmost point, where the wall met the marsh road. The gate was still there. The wall was still there. But the road beyond it—the road she had walked a hundred times, the road to Ferrow's Crossing with its toll bridge and its chestnut seller—was simply *not*, in the way a word on the tip of your tongue is not. She stood at the gate and reached for the memory of what lay outside and found her mind sliding off it like rain off wax.

A boy had been sitting on the gate steps, drawing. That was the strange part. Everyone else in Vellamere was pretending nothing had happened—shuttering their shops, doubling their prayers, talking loudly about the harvest as if the sky hadn't gone black and starless three nights ago. But this boy, maybe nine years old, skinny as a fence rail, was sprawled on the steps with a stub of charcoal, drawing the sky.

Not the sky that was. The sky that had been.

Odile had stopped. She couldn't help it. The boy had drawn a scatter of stars, and among them a shape she knew—the Wagon, the old farmers' name for it, seven stars dragging a crooked line—and the Heron, and low on the horizon the pale smudge he'd labeled, in a child's careful letters, *the Lantern.*

"You drew that from memory?" she'd said.

The boy had looked up with the flat, unimpressed stare of children everywhere. "Everyone's forgot. I didn't."

"Everyone's forgot," she'd repeated.

"Ma says don't talk about it. Says talking about what's gone makes the gray come for you." He shrugged. "I'm not scared of gray."

He was, though. She could see it in the way his charcoal had pressed too hard, snapping twice, in the way he'd drawn the same constellation three times over, bigger each time, as if size could hold it in place.

"What's your name, boy?"

"Pip." Then, quickly, with the reflex of the poor: "I ain't done nothing."

"You've done something nobody else in this city has managed." She'd crouched to his level, which her knees protested. "Pip. Do you know where these came from?" She'd tapped the paper. "Who taught you the stars?"

"Nobody. I just know them." He'd frowned, and added, in the tone of a child reporting a fact he's been told is impossible: "I know them better than I know my own street. That's wrong, isn't it?"

It was wrong. It was exactly wrong, and wrong in a way that meant something, and Odile Ferrant—who had spent five years telling herself she was finished with maps, finished with the guild, finished with caring—felt the old hunger open in her chest like a door.

"Where did you get the charcoal, Pip?"

"Traded for it. Bread and a charm, off the taster." He said it casually, the way you'd say *off the baker*, and Odile's whole body went still.

"The taster."

"Beatrix. Sells charms out of the bread baskets, if you know to ask. Ma says they're nothing. Ma buys them anyway." Pip went back to his drawing. "You want one? Beatrix does a forgetting-charm. For bad things." His voice went small. "I asked for one once. Beatrix wouldn't sell it to me. Said some things you have to keep."

Odile stood there on the gate steps with the fog where a country used to be and a child's sky in her hands, and thought: *a taster who knows which things must be kept.*

She had a direction now. She had, for the first time in three days, a direction.

* * *

Beatrix knew something was wrong with the bread before they knew it was wrong.

They had been a taster for eleven years; their tongue was an instrument, calibrated daily against the harmless poisons they dosed themselves with—faint bitterings of gelsemium, a breath of foxglove, nothing that would kill, everything that would teach. Their tongue knew the difference between flour milled in Vellamere and flour carted in from the valley. Their tongue knew when the regent's cook was nervous, because nervous cooks oversalted.

This morning the bread tasted like nothing.

Not stale. Not bland. *Nothing*—the way the sky had gone nothing, the way the road beyond the Tanners' Gate had gone nothing, if the boy Pip's terror was anything to go by. Beatrix chewed and swallowed and chewed again, and stood in the regent's kitchen with a loaf in their hands, feeling the floor tilt.

"You'll ruin it, standing there," said Cook, not looking up.

"It's fine," Beatrix said, because that was the job. The bread was fine. The bread was always fine. Beatrix had kept the regent alive through two poisonings, one attempt at slow arsenic, and a truly ambitious attempt involving a snake in a pie, and the bread was *fine*.

But their tongue, their instrument, their one true thing, was reporting the world going quiet.

They finished the shift in a haze. They walked home through streets that felt thinner than usual, as if the city were a fabric worn to gauze at the elbows. And when they climbed to their room above the chandler's and lit the lamp and opened the ledger—the little book of small magics, eleven years of entries, *charm for Marta's cough, charm for the Ostler boy's nightmares, charm against the gray, sold to no one, kept for myself*—they understood, with the calm that had always lived underneath their fear like a stone under moss, that the time of small magics was over.

Someone knocked. Three quick raps, then one, then three. Not a customer's knock. Customers knocked like beggars, apologetic, muffled.

Beatrix blew out the lamp before answering. Old habit. They opened the door on a chain, and looked at the woman on the landing: fifty-ish, travel-stained, with the ink-stained fingers of a clerk and the eyes of someone who had been walking for days and hated every one of them. She was holding a piece of paper the way you hold a warrant or a love letter.

"You're the taster," the woman said. "Beatrix."

"I'm a baker's assistant."

"You're a poison-taster for the regent who sells charms in bread baskets, and a child named Pip drew me a map of the old sky and said you were the one who told him to keep it." The woman held up the paper. On it, in charcoal, the stars wheeled—wrong constellations, old constellations, constellations Beatrix's grandmother had sung to them before the singing was forbidden. "My name is Odile Ferrant. I was a cartographer. My maps have gone blank at the borders, and I want to know if you taste it too."

Beatrix looked at the stars. Something in their chest, something they had kept shut for eleven years, rattled on its hinges.

"Taste what?" they said, which was as good as a confession, and they both knew it.

"The country," Odile said. "It's going out. Like a lamp. And I think someone in this city knows why, and I think it's you."

Beatrix unhooked the chain. It was, they would think later, the most dangerous thing they had ever done—more dangerous than the snake in the pie, more dangerous than the first charm they'd ever sold. They opened the door.

"You'd better come in," they said. "And you'd better tell me about the boy."

* * *

They gathered, the way sparks gather toward a chimney's draft.

It was Odile's doing, mostly—Odile, who had spent her disgrace learning the other disgraced, the unlicensed, the unregistered, the people the city pretended not to see because seeing them would mean admitting the city had edges and people fell off them. She had a list in her head that was almost a map.

The hedge-healer first. Maren Ostwick worked out of a cellar in the Weavers' Quarter, treating fevers and broken bones and bad marriages with poultices and tinctures and a bluntness that had gotten her formally warned by the Physicians' Guild four times. When Odile and Beatrix climbed down her stairs, they found her packing.

"You heard," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"Heard what?" Beatrix asked.

"Heard the wards die." Maren didn't stop folding. She was a broad, gray-streaked woman with hands like tree roots, and she moved like someone who had done a great deal of leaving in her life. "I've worked the marsh villages. I know what the border wards sound like when they're healthy—a hum, low, in your back teeth. You stop hearing it after a while, it's just the world. Three nights ago it stopped." She looked up. "You're the taster. And you're the thief-cartographer. I know both of you by reputation, and my reputation for knowing things is the only one I've got left, so believe me when I say: I'm not packing because I'm scared. I'm packing because I've been waiting five years for the wards to fail, and now they have, and I intend to be useful before the end."

"The end of what?" Beatrix asked.

Maren tied off her bag. "That's the question, isn't it. That's the only question."

The bell-ringer came to them, which surprised everyone. Tam Wicke presented himself at the chandler's door two evenings later, a long, stooped man with rope-burned palms and the apologetic posture of someone who had spent thirty years asking to be forgiven for making noise. He rang the bells of the Salt Cathedral—the low hours, the ones nobody listened to.

"I heard the thirteenth," he said, standing in the doorway, not entering, as if he might still be turned away. "Night the stars went. I counted. Everyone counts the bells, it's the first thing they teach you—twelve and stop, twelve and stop, the city's whole rhythm. And the bell went thirteen." His hands worked against each other. "And I rang it."

"You rang it?" Odile said sharply.

"The rope pulled *me*." He said it fast, the words of a man who had rehearsed being disbelieved. "Thirty years, I've rung those bells, and I'm telling you the rope went taut on its own, and the bell spoke a note I've never heard it have, and every ward-stone in that tower lit up blue and then went out like snuffed candles. And then—" he swallowed— "then the bells forgot me. I rang the dawn hours and nobody came to relieve me. I rang the noon hours and the sacristan looked straight through me. It took until yesterday for my own sister to remember my name." He finally looked up. "I'm unregistered now. Properly. The city's started doing to me what it's doing to the country. And I thought— I thought if anyone would know what that means—"

Beatrix stepped aside and let him in. "It means you're one of us," they said. "Come in, Tam. We're making a list."

"What kind of list?"

"The kind that keeps."

The soldier was last, and the soldier was hardest.

Odile found him the way she found everything now, by drawing: she sketched the city from memory each night, and each night the map showed her a little less, and each night one place glowed in her mind like a coal—the Tanners' Gate, where a boy drew stars. She went back to the gate on the fourth day and found a man camped under it. Deserted uniform, sword still belted at his hip because he hadn't been able to make himself throw it in the marsh, eyes like a man who hadn't slept since the sky changed. Dain, he said. Dain Merrick. Warden of the Third Gate.

"I know you," Beatrix said, when the company had assembled in Maren's cellar, all six of them crowded among the drying herbs and the smell of camphor. "You were at the palace. You stood post outside the tasting chamber."

"I did." Dain's voice was flat. He sat apart from the others, on a crate, with his hands hanging between his knees. "You want to know why a warden deserted. Everyone wants to know. Here's why." He looked at the floor. "That night, the order came down. Open the Third Gate. Full ward-release. Signed with the regent's seal." His jaw worked. "I've opened gates a hundred times. It's a ritual, you know? You speak the ward-words, the stones light, the road opens. I put my hand on the release and the ward-words wouldn't come. Not wouldn't—*were gone*. Out of my head. Words I've said since I was sixteen, gone like they were never in me. And the gate—" he looked up, and his eyes were the worst thing in the cellar— "the gate was already open. It opened itself. Something came through, or something went out, I don't know which, and the stars went out one by one from the horizon in, like a hand closing. And I ran." He said it without flinching, having clearly decided flinching was a luxury. "I ran from my post. That's the confession. That's the whole of it. Court-martial me if the country lasts long enough."

Silence. The herbs turned on their strings in a draft.

It was Pip who spoke—Pip, who had followed Odile to the cellar because Odile had not been able to bring herself to leave him on the gate steps, and who had been sitting on the floor drawing the entire meeting, because drawing was how he held on to things.

"You didn't run from the gate," Pip said.

Everyone looked at him.

"You ran *at* it," Pip said, "and it was already too late. That's different." He held up his paper. On it he'd drawn six figures in his blunt child's hand, standing in a row under a sky full of stars. "I draw what I remember. I remember you all now. That means something. Beatrix says things you keep can't be taken."

Beatrix felt every eye in the cellar turn to them, and did not remember saying that to the boy, and knew perfectly well that they had.

"That's charm-talk," Maren said, but gently. "Folk belief."

"It's *ledger*-talk," Beatrix said. They took out the little book—eleven years of entries, small magics, small mercies—and held it up. "I have written down every charm I ever sold and every person I ever sold it to, because I believed that a thing written down stays true. I believed it so hard I made it work. That's what magic is, out here among the unregistered. It's not the guilds' fire and lightning. It's attention. It's keeping." They looked around the cellar: the healer with her packed bag, the bell-ringer with his ruined name, the soldier with his confession, the cartographer with her fading maps, the child with his sky. "And something is un-keeping this country. From the edges in. And I think the regent knows why, because three days ago I tasted forgetting in the palace bread, and Casimir watched me taste it, and he *smiled*."

Dain stood up. "You're saying we go back. Toward the palace."

"I'm saying," Beatrix said, "that we are the people this country forgot on purpose, and we are the only ones who noticed. That's not nothing. That might be the only thing that's something."

Odile was already unrolling paper. Maren was already unpacking her bag. Tam was flexing his rope-burned hands as if counting bells only he could hear. And Pip drew on, six figures under the old stars, keeping them the only way he knew.

"Then we need a plan," Odile said, charcoal moving. "And a road. And—" she stopped. Her hand had gone still on the paper. She stared down at the map taking shape under her fingers, at the city she'd drawn a hundred times, and her face went the color of tallow.

"What?" Beatrix said.

"The Salt Cathedral," Odile said slowly. "I've drawn it every night since the stars went out. And every night it's the one thing that comes out *clearer*." She looked up. "Everything else is fading. It's getting darker. Like something under it is—"

"Calling," said Tam Wicke, in the voice of a man who has heard a bell ring thirteen.
