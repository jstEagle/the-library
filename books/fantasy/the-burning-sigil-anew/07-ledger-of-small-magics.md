## 7. Ledger of Small Magics

The safehouse was a tannery that had gone out of business two years before the stars did, which meant it smelled like old death and no one wanted it. Odile called it tactically undesirable. Maren called it honest. Beatrix called it home for exactly as long as it took the wardens to stop searching door to door, and not one hour more.

They had been there four days. Four days of Dain sleeping in shifts with a knife under his hand, of Maren grinding willowbark for the headaches the whole company seemed to share, of Odile spreading her ruined maps across a curing table and staring at them like a physician staring at a body that refused to say what killed it. Four days of Pip being Pip, which was to say: asking questions no one could answer and answering questions no one had asked.

Beatrix spent the fourth evening doing the thing they had done every night for five years in Vellamere, the thing that had kept them sane when tasting the regent's supper had kept them fed: they took out the ledger and read it.

It wasn't much to look at. A book the size of a palm, bound in gray leather gone soft as ear at the spine, its cover stamped with nothing at all. Unregistered goods could not bear marks. Inside, in Beatrix's small slanted hand, were five years of entries.

*Third of Harvestmonth. Wren the cooper, fever-charm, two coppers. His wife thinks it was the tea.*

*Ninth of Harvestmonth. Sela Ostrow. She wanted a charm to make her mother forget the debt. I gave her one to make the debt forget her mother instead. It worked sideways. They both laughed for the first time in a year.*

*Midwinter. Bread for the Ostrow girl, no charge. She is growing like a weed and weeds should eat.*

And so on, page after page. Two hundred and eleven entries. Beatrix had counted them the way other people counted their savings, because that was exactly what they were. Not money. Not power. A record. Proof that a person who had made themselves deliberately unremarkable had, in small and deniable ways, been remarkable to somebody.

They were reading the entry about the cooper's wife when the floorboard by the door sighed.

Beatrix had the ledger under their apron before the sound finished. Old habit. The book had spent more of its life hidden against Beatrix's ribs than in any bag, and it knew the way home.

It was Pip, of course. Pip moved like a cat who had been told about cats but never met one — all elbows and sudden decisions. The child dropped onto the curing bench across from Beatrix, legs swinging, and watched them with the frank, terrible attention of the very young.

"You hide it when I come in," Pip said.

"I hide it when anyone comes in."

"You hide it fastest when it's me."

Beatrix turned a page they hadn't read. "Children repeat things. It's not an insult. It's a survival trait."

"I don't repeat things." Pip said this with such conviction that Beatrix almost laughed. "I *keep* things. There's a difference. Keeping is when you hold it so tight it can't get out. Repeating is when it wants to."

"That's not a difference. That's the same thing wearing different shoes."

Pip considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. "No. It's a real difference. Ask me what the third star of the Shepherd's Crook is called."

Beatrix looked up. Outside the tannery's grimy high windows, the sky was the color of a scoured pot. No stars. No moon yet either — the moon had gone into its impossible thirteenth turn somewhere over the edge of the world, and the scholars in the capital were reportedly tearing their hair out in neat scholarly rows. The dark up there was not night-dark. It was absence-dark, the dark of a page where writing had been.

"I don't know what the third star of the Shepherd's Crook is called," Beatrix said. "I don't know the Shepherd's Crook."

"Nobody does." Pip's voice went quiet, and that was worse than loud, because Pip quiet was Pip remembering. "Except me. The Shepherd's Crook, the Salt Gate, the Nine Sisters, the Wagon That Fell. I keep them. I lie in my blanket and I say them in order so they don't get out. But—" the child's brow furrowed, working at something too big for the words available, "—keeping them so tight might be the same as losing them. If nobody else knows them, then when I forget, that's the whole sky gone."

Beatrix's hands had gone still on the ledger. "You won't forget."

"Grandmother said she'd never forget the name of her village." Pip swung their legs once, hard, and stopped. "I don't remember the name of her village. She died before the stars went out. I don't even know if the village is still *there*, or if it went when the wards did, or if it was never—" Pip stopped again, jaw tight. "Anyway. That's not why I came down."

"No?"

"No." Pip pointed at the shape under Beatrix's apron, at the ledger's corner just visible against the cloth. "That's why I came down. I want to see it."

"No."

"I kept the sky for you. You can show me the book."

"That's not a trade, Pip. That's a hostage negotiation."

"Odile says everything is a hostage negotiation, you just don't always know what's being held."

Beatrix pinched the bridge of their nose. Five years of careful, professional invisibility, and they had been undone in a week by a disgraced cartographer and a nine-year-old who negotiated like a fishwife. "The ledger isn't a toy."

"I know it's not a toy. It's your sky."

Beatrix looked at the child for a long moment. Above them, through the broken skylight, the empty dark went on being empty. Somewhere across the city — their city, their small gray city of bells and bread and careful poisons — wardens were still knocking on doors, still asking the question that had emptied the streets: *where are the unregistered ones?* They had Odile's description. They had Dain's. They had, Beatrix was nearly certain, a boy's drawing of a charm-seller's mark copied from a bakery wall.

The ledger was not a toy. The ledger was a confession with a spine.

* * *

They burned it at midnight, and it was Odile's idea, which Beatrix would hold against her for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be.

She laid it out plainly, the way she laid out maps, with everything at its correct distance. "The wardens took the baker's boy for questioning. He'll give them your mark, Beatrix. He's nine and they'll be gentle about it, which is worse. When they have your mark they'll search for your goods, and your goods are charms, and charms leave traces — you told me that yourself, the night we met. Traces they can read." She tapped the curing table. "But you didn't just sell charms. You *wrote them down*. Names, streets, prices, dates. If they find that book, they don't find one unregistered charm-seller. They find two hundred and eleven unregistered *people*."

"I know what's in the book."

"Then you know what has to happen to it."

"I know what you *want* to happen to it." Beatrix's voice came out flatter than they intended, and they heard it flatten further as they went on, the old kitchen voice, the voice that had said *yes, my lord* and *no, my lord* and *the soup is not poisoned, my lord* ten thousand times without once cracking. "You want it burned because it's dangerous. Fine. It is dangerous. Burn it. But don't stand there and call it tactics. That book is the only place in the world where any of those people exist as themselves. Wren the cooper isn't in any census — unregistered, remember, that's the whole point of us. Sela Ostrow isn't anywhere. If the book burns, they were never helped. They were never *met*. I was never—" Beatrix stopped. The kitchen voice held, barely. "I was never anything at all."

The tannery was quiet. Dain had paused in the doorway with an armful of firewood, and Maren had stopped grinding, and even Pip, who was supposed to be asleep on the loft, was sitting up among the sacking with their eyes wide open.

Odile didn't flinch. She was a woman who had lost her license, her guild, and her country's place on the map, and she had learned that flinching was just pain wasted. "You're right," she said. "It isn't tactics. It's a funeral. And funerals are still necessary." She held out her hand for the book. "I'll do it, if you can't."

And that — that offer, gentle and terrible — was what decided it. Beatrix had spent five years being useful so that no one else would ever have to carry anything heavy on their behalf. They were not going to stop now, with the country itself coming apart.

"No," Beatrix said. "It's mine. I'll do it."

They took the ledger to the tannery's old curing fire, which Maren had coaxed back to life against the cold, and they stood there a moment with the book in both hands, feeling its weight, which was nothing, feeling its true weight, which was two hundred and eleven people.

"Beatrix," Maren said softly, from her stool. The hedge-healer had a way of speaking that made everything sound like it was being poured. "You don't have to watch it burn. You can hand it to the fire and look at the sky instead."

"I watched every one of them happen," Beatrix said. "I'll watch this too."

They opened the cover one last time. The first page, five years old, the ink gone brown: *First of Plantingmonth. Charm for the miller's dog, who is afraid of thunder. No charge. Dogs shouldn't have to pay.*

Beatrix almost smiled. Then they fed the book to the fire, a few pages at a time, the way you feed anything you love to something that destroys it — slowly, so it doesn't suffer, or so you don't, it's hard to say which.

The pages went up green and then gold. The entries curled. Wren the cooper became a black lace edge, then a fleck, then nothing. Sela Ostrow and her sideways debt lasted a breath longer, as if the ink knew it was the best sentence Beatrix had ever written. The miller's thunder-frightened dog went first of all, and Beatrix watched it go, and did not look at the sky, and did not let their face do anything at all, because the face was the last thing they owned that the regent had never touched, and they were not going to spend it here.

When it was done, the fire settled, and there was a small gray ash-shape in the grate like a moth with its wings folded, and Beatrix understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that they had made a mistake.

Not the burning. The burning had been right; Odile was right; the wardens could never be allowed to read that book. The mistake was older. The mistake was five years of writing everything down and telling no one, of keeping the ledger the way Pip kept the sky — held so tight it couldn't get out — as if a memory locked in one skull were the same as a memory alive in the world. The stars had gone out because they had been remembered by exactly one person, and that person was a child. Beatrix had built a sky of their own, two hundred and eleven stars of it, and hung it where no one could see, and now the fire had it, and that was that.

They had made themselves unremarkable so well that they had made their whole life unrecorded. Unregistered. Erasable.

The same thing, wearing different shoes.

* * *

Pip came down after the others had gone to their shifts of sleep. Beatrix was still at the fire. They were not crying. They were doing something worse, which was sitting perfectly still and cataloguing, entry by entry, from memory, everything the fire had taken — Wren, fever-charm, two coppers; Sela, the debt that forgot; the dog, no charge — as if by reciting the index they could keep the book. They were at entry forty when small feet hit the floor of the loft and a small body arrived beside them, wrapped in sacking like a burrito with opinions.

"You're doing it wrong," Pip said.

"It's the middle of the night. There's no right way to do anything at this hour."

"You're keeping them. I can see it. You're saying them inside your head and holding them so tight they can't get out." Pip held up one hand, fingers spread, demonstrating tightness. "That's how you lose them. I told you."

"You told me about stars."

"Stars, charms, same thing. Things that have to be said out loud or they go out." Pip scooted closer to the fire, to Beatrix, until there was no gap left at all, which was a liberty no adult in five years had taken and which Beatrix found, to their considerable annoyance, that they did not mind. "So say them out loud. I'll keep them."

Beatrix looked down at the child. "You can't keep two hundred and eleven."

"I kept the whole sky."

"The sky is—" Beatrix stopped. Recalculated. "How many stars do you actually have in there?"

"Four hundred and six. Grandmother counted with me every clear night. She said a sky you can't count isn't yours yet." Pip said it simply, without boast, the way you'd report the number of stairs in your house. "Two hundred and eleven is easy. It's not even a whole sky."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the gray fields beyond the city, the moon was turning its impossible thirteenth turn, and somewhere under the Salt Cathedral — if Odile's half-remembered legend was worth anything — there was a Doorway with a burning sigil that had once led out of the world, and the whole country had until that turn completed to remember itself or be forgotten. It was a mad plan. It was the only plan. And it rested, in the end, on memory — on things said aloud, held in common, kept by more than one skull.

Beatrix had spent five years believing that safety was smallness. Sitting in a dead tannery at midnight with a child leaning warm against their arm, they felt the belief crack down the middle like a ward-stone.

"First of Plantingmonth," Beatrix said, out loud, into the fire. "A charm for the miller's dog, who was afraid of thunder. No charge. Dogs shouldn't have to pay."

Pip's eyes were very wide and very bright. "Again," the child said. "Slower. I want to get it exactly."

"Third of Harvestmonth. Wren the cooper, fever-charm, two coppers. His wife thought it was the tea."

"Again."

They went through the night that way. Beatrix spoke and Pip kept, and when Beatrix's voice wore out around entry ninety, Pip recited the entries back, all of them, in order, without a single error, and Beatrix sat there with their heart going strange in their chest, listening to their hidden life come out of someone else's mouth — alive, out loud, no longer theirs alone.

Near dawn, Odile appeared in the doorway with a rolled map under one arm and an expression that meant she had been listening at the loft for some time and had decided not to apologize for it.

"Good," she said. "Because if memory is what we have, we're going to need all of it." She dropped the map on the curing table and weighted its corners. "I found it. The Doorway. Under the Salt Cathedral — the legend is real, and there's more than a legend down there. There's a sigil. And Beatrix—" she looked at them across the dying fire, across the child full of stars and charms, "—I think you've seen it before."

Beatrix had not seen it before. Beatrix was almost sure of that.

But their hand had gone, without their permission, to the old brand-shaped scar beneath their collarbone — the mark every taster was born to, the mark the regent's household called a blessing — and it had gone warm for the first time in their life.
