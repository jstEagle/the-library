## 8. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The Observatory of Vellamere had been closed for eleven years, and Odile Ferrant had picked its lock in under four minutes.

She was not proud of that. She was proud of very little these days. But her hands remembered locks the way they remembered coastlines—by feel, by the small betrayals of resistance—and the Observatory's gate had a cheap tumbler mechanism, installed by someone who had never expected anyone to want in.

"Careful of the third step," she said over her shoulder.

Pip hopped it without looking, which was either trust or recklessness, and Odile had stopped trying to tell the difference in children. Behind them came Beatrix, hood up, moving with that particular economy Odile had come to recognize as the walk of a person who had spent five years being deliberately forgettable. Dain took the rear, one hand on the knife he hadn't sheathed since the stars went out.

Inside, the great dome smelled of dust and cold brass and, faintly, of salt. The orrery at the center still stood—a thing of nested rings and silver spheres, built when Caelmere had still believed itself worth mapping. Odile had drawn it once, years ago, for an atlas that no longer existed.

"Light," she said, and Beatrix produced a stub of candle from somewhere inside their coat, because of course they did. Beatrix carried everything. Charms, bread, rope, a tin of honey. Odile suspected that if you cut them open you'd find a fully provisioned kitchen.

The candle flame caught the orrery's rings and threw slow shadows across the dome, and Pip made a sound Odile had never heard from them before—a small, punched-out breath of pure want.

"It moves," Pip whispered. "It really moves."

"It did," Odile said. "We'll see."

* * *

She had come here first, two nights ago, alone, before she'd gone knocking on charm-sellers' doors. She hadn't told the others that. There were things a disgraced cartographer kept to herself out of habit, and things she kept to herself out of shame, and this had been both: she had wanted to see the sky fail before she told anyone it was failing.

Because Odile Ferrant had known, in the professional part of her brain, since the second night after the bells. The night the stars went out, everyone in Vellamere had looked up and grieved, or panicked, or prayed. Odile had done all three, briefly, and then she had done what she always did with catastrophe: she had measured it.

The moon had not gone out. That was the first wrongness. Whatever had eaten the stars had left the moon burning, pale and full and close, hanging over the city like an eye that refused to blink. And the moon had been full then. It should have begun to wane.

It had not waned. For six nights it had hung there, exactly full, exactly round, and every astronomer in the city who still dared look up had begun to say the word *impossible* in increasingly frightened voices.

Tonight, standing beneath the dead orrery, Odile cranked the main ring until the gears groaned awake. The mechanism shuddered, shed a decade of dust, and turned—the innermost sphere swinging on its arm to mark the moon's position against the ring of houses, the zodiac band, the thirteen stations of the lunar calendar.

Thirteen. Every schoolchild in Caelmere learned the count on their fingers: twelve turns of the moon to the year, and a thirteenth station at the top of the wheel that the moon never entered. The Empty House, the teachers called it. A place on the calendar the way a locked room is a place in a house. Everyone knew it existed. No one had ever seen it used.

Odile set the arm to tonight's position and let go.

The sphere swung, settled—and kept going. Past the twelfth station. Up the long empty arc toward the thirteenth.

"No," Dain said behind her. Not a question. Just the word, flat, like a man setting down something heavy.

"It's climbing," Odile said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Slowly. But it's climbing. Six nights at full, and now—" She watched the silver ball creep along its rail. "Now it's entering the Empty House."

"What happens when it gets there?" Pip asked.

And there it was. The question. The child asked it the way children ask everything—simply, directly, with none of the adult machinery of denial—and Odile found she could not dress the answer up.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know someone who might."

* * *

Master Ellery Vance had been the Royal Astronomer for thirty-one years and had been pretending not to be for six days. When Odile hammered on the door of his narrow house near the Salt Quarter—at an hour no respectable person would choose—he opened it already dressed, already holding a lantern, already looking like a man who had been waiting for someone brave enough or desperate enough to knock.

"You're the Ferrant woman," he said.

"I'm the Ferrant woman."

"They stripped your license."

"They did."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he stepped aside and let her in, and Odile understood that this was how the world worked now: the licensed were useless and the unlicensed were the only ones still doing the work.

His study was a cave of paper. Star charts layered on star charts, some of them decades old, some of them—she saw with a lurch—drawn in the last week, in a hand gone shaky. He poured tea nobody drank and spread the newest chart across his desk, weighting the corners with anything heavy enough: an inkwell, a bust of some forgotten mathematician, a brick.

"Look," he said.

Odile looked. It was a chart of the night sky over Vellamere, and it was mostly empty. Where the constellations should have been, where the Shepherd's Crook and the Two Sisters and the Long Whale should have wheeled, there was nothing but blank vellum and, here and there, faint ghost-lines where Vance had tried to draw stars from memory and couldn't hold them.

"The sky's not dark," he said. "That's what I keep telling people and they don't understand me. Dark would be fine. Dark is just an absence of light. This is an absence of *record*. The stars aren't hidden. They're unwritten." He tapped the chart with a trembling finger. "I have looked through the great refractor every night since the bells. There is nothing to see. Not blackness. Nothing. My eye slides off it. I have begun to doubt my own charts of the last forty years, do you understand? I checked them against the almanacs and the almanacs are blank too. The whole archive. Every recorded observation of the Caelmeran sky, gone, as if we never—"

He stopped. His hand was shaking too hard to tap anything.

"And the moon?" Odile asked quietly.

Vance sat down heavily. "Ah," he said. "The moon."

* * *

Here is what Master Ellery Vance knew, laid out across his desk in charts and calculations while the others crowded close and the candle burned down:

The moon had held full for six nights because it was no longer following the old calendar. It had left the twelve-stationed wheel entirely and was traveling the thirteenth arc—the Empty House—for the first time in the whole recorded history of the country. Except there was no recorded history anymore, so perhaps it had done this before. Perhaps it had done this every time. Perhaps, Vance said, his voice dropping to almost nothing, this was simply what happened at the end, and every country that had ever ended had ended this way, and no one had survived to write it down.

"How long?" Odile asked. She had her notebook out. She always had her notebook out; it was the last professional habit she had left.

Vance showed her the calculation. It was elegant, actually—she could admit that even now, even with her stomach turning. The moon moved along the thirteenth arc at a measurable rate, a few degrees a night, and the arc was finite, and the end of the arc was marked on the oldest orreries with a symbol no scholar had ever satisfactorily explained: a circle bisected by a line, like a door seen edge-on.

"Twenty-two nights," Vance said. "Give or take. On the twenty-second night, the moon reaches the end of the Empty House and—" He spread his hands. "And the turn completes. And whatever the turn does, it finishes doing."

"And what does it do?"

"I don't know." He said it like a confession, like the failure of his entire life. "But I can tell you what it has done *so far*. It erased the stars. Then it began erasing the borders—I've had reports, do you understand, reports from the garrison captains at the ward-gates, saying the land beyond the walls has gone... smooth. Gray. Like unfinished country. Like a map before the mapmaker arrives." His eyes came up to hers, and Odile felt the old shame rise in her throat, because she knew exactly what he meant, she had dreamed in that idiom her whole life. "You're a cartographer, madam. What happens to a country when the edges go first?"

"The edges go first," she said, "and then the middle forgets it was ever connected to anything."

"Yes," Vance whispered. "Yes. That's precisely it."

Dain shifted by the door. "Twenty-two nights," he said. "To do what? You're telling us the hourglass exists. Fine. What's the sand for?"

And Odile took a breath, because this was the part she had been carrying alone for three days, the part she'd walked across half of Vellamere to find a charm-seller about, the part that had cost her the last shred of the careful anonymity she'd wrapped around herself since the license board broke her.

"There's a legend," she said. "An old one. Older than the regency. My grandmother told it to me when I was learning to draw coastlines, and I thought it was a story for children, and I have spent three days wishing I had written it down properly instead of just—remembering it." She laughed, short and bitter. "Remembering. That's all any of us have now, isn't it?"

"Odile," Beatrix said softly. Just her name. An invitation.

"The legend says that Caelmere was not always shut in," Odile said. "That once there was a Doorway. Not a ward-gate—not one of Casimir's checkpoints with their stamps and their fees. A true Doorway, made before the country had walls, a stone arch cut with sigils that stood open onto everywhere else. Roads to other kingdoms. Other skies. And when the country sealed itself—when whatever bargain was struck that put the wards around us—the Doorway was closed and buried rather than broken, because a thing like that can't be broken, only hidden." She looked around the candlelit study, at the astronomer and the soldier and the child and the taster, at the faces of the only people in Vellamere who believed her. "And the legend says it was buried beneath the Salt Cathedral."

"The cathedral burned down forty years ago," Dain said.

"Burned down, yes. But nobody ever dug out the foundations. Nobody ever builds over consecrated ground in this city—they're afraid of it. So it's still down there. Under the rubble. Under the salt." She closed her notebook. "A door buried under a church, in a city running out of nights. If that isn't a legend worth digging up, I don't know what is."

* * *

They argued, of course. Dain thought it was a trap, or worse, a waste of their remaining time on a fairy story; Beatrix said little but watched Odile with those steady, tasting eyes, as if weighing her words for poison and finding them, against all expectation, clean. It was Pip who ended it—or rather, it was what Pip drew.

The child had been quiet the whole evening, sitting cross-legged on the floor among Vance's fallen charts with a piece of charcoal scavenged from the desk. Now Pip held up the paper.

Stars. Dozens of them, arranged in shapes Odile didn't recognize—the Shepherd's Crook, yes, but drawn wrong, or rather drawn *right*, with stars that appeared on no surviving chart. And above them, drawn large and careful, a circle bisected by a line.

"That's the mark," Pip said. "From my dream. The door seen from the side. It's on the arch, under the church. I've seen it." Pip's chin lifted, defiant, daring any of them to say *children dream nonsense*. "I've seen the old sky. Someone has to remember it, so I do."

Silence, in the paper-cave study, while the candle guttered.

Vance broke it. The old man crossed to his shelf, pulled down a leather tube, and drew out a document Odile recognized by its seals: a royal warrant of observation, signed with the regent's own hand, granting the Astronomer authority to record and report. He set it on the desk between them.

"Twenty-two nights," he said. "I am too old to dig and too honest to lie about what I've seen. So I will do the one thing left to me. I will keep counting. Every night, I will measure the moon's climb, and I will send word to you through—" he glanced at Beatrix, "—whoever it is you people use. And you will find that Doorway, madam Cartographer, if it exists. Because I have read every book in the royal archive this week, and I will tell you what I found in the marginalia of a ninth-century surveyor's journal, one line, in a dead hand: *the moon climbs the Empty House but once, and what the turn completes, the turn unmakes.*"

He looked at each of them in turn.

"Unmakes," he repeated. "Not destroys. Unmakes. As though we were written down to begin with."

* * *

They walked back through the sleeping streets in a loose knot, no one speaking, the full moon overhead riding its impossible arc. At the corner where their paths divided—back to the safehouse, back to the kitchens, back to whatever thin coverings they each maintained over their lives—Beatrix touched Odile's sleeve.

"The Salt Cathedral," Beatrix said quietly. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow," Odile said. "Before the moon climbs another degree. Before Casimir notices that his astronomers have stopped reporting." She looked up. The moon hung over the rooftops, white and patient and full, and for the first time in her life Odile Ferrant saw it for what it was: not a light, but a countdown. A deadline written across the sky in a language everyone could read and no one could stop.

"Twenty-two nights," she said. "We dig tomorrow."
