## 9. Road Through the Pale Fields

The gate wardens didn't look at us. That was the first miracle of the morning, and I distrusted it immediately.

Five years in Vellamere had taught me that attention was a currency and invisibility was bought with it, coin by coin, day by day. But the Westgate sentries stood at their posts like candles burned down to the stub, their eyes fixed on some middle distance where nothing lived. One of them was chewing. Slowly. The way you chew when the food has stopped meaning anything.

"Walk," Odile murmured. "Don't run. Running is interesting."

So we walked. Dain carried Maren's pack along with his own because she'd spent half the night packing her herbs and the other half arguing with Pip about whether starlight could be pickled. The bell-ringer, Sef, had come with us wearing his city coat over road-stained trousers, looking like a man who'd stolen his own clothes. And Pip walked between all of us, small hand hooked into the strap of my satchel, not letting go.

I looked back exactly once, at the top of the rise.

Vellamere sat in its bowl of hills the way it always had—gray stone, red roofs, the palace crown on its hill with the regent's banners hanging limp. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys. Bells were ringing the hour, though they sounded wrong from outside, thinner, as if the sound itself had been stretched too far to reach us. It was the first time in five years I had seen the city from beyond its walls. It was smaller than it felt from inside. Everything that keeps you safe looks small once you're out of its shadow.

"Beatrix." Odile's voice, low. "The horizon."

I turned. And my stomach did something complicated and cold.

There was no horizon. Not properly. Where the fields should have rolled away into the blue haze of distant country—the river provinces, the salt marshes, all the places I'd tasted spices from but never seen—there was only a pale wash, like fog pressed flat against the land, like the sky had been let down too far and left to pool. The world simply *stopped*, gently, without an edge.

"That's where Caelmere used to be," Odile said. "Everything past that line." She said it the way a surgeon says *the wound*.

"Then we walk toward it," Dain said, and shouldered his pack, and we went.

* * *

The first farmstead was abandoned, but not recently. Doors stood open. A dog's chain lay coiled by the well, empty. Laundry still hung on a line, gone stiff and colorless as parchment, flapping in a wind that smelled of nothing at all.

I noticed the smell—or the absence of one—before I noticed anything else. A farmyard in high summer should have reeked: manure and dust and pig and hot wood and the green crush of weeds. This yard smelled like clean paper. Like a room no one had lived in.

"The fields," Maren said quietly, and pointed.

Wheat grew in the near field. It stood in rows, golden-gray, each stalk perfect, each head full. It should have been ready for harvest. It swayed in the breeze with a sound like sand sliding over sand.

I broke off a head because I couldn't help myself, because I am who I am, because my whole life has been organized around the principle that if something might be poisoned or wrong, someone has to put it in their mouth.

I chewed.

Nothing.

Not bitterness. Not staleness. Not even the papery blandness of old grain. *Nothing.* My tongue reported a complete absence, a hole shaped like wheat. I have tasted two hundred poisons and trained myself to find flavor in anything—I can taste the difference between well water drawn at dawn and at dusk. This was like chewing silence.

"Beatrix?" Pip had gone very still beside me, watching my face with those eyes that remembered stars nobody else could see.

"It doesn't taste," I said. My voice came out strange. "Pip, listen. Food always tastes. Even bad food. Even gray, moldy, burnt food. Taste is the world talking to you." I held up the wheat head. "This isn't talking."

Odile took a stalk, examined it with her cartographer's squint, then pulled out her journal and began to sketch—not the wheat, but the field, the fence lines, the angle of the abandoned farmhouse against the pale wall of the horizon. She drew the way other people prayed.

"Gray crops," she said. "Empty farms. No bodies, no struggle. They didn't flee. They—" She stopped.

"They forgot to be here," Pip said.

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. It sounded true.

We walked on through the morning, and the pattern repeated until it stopped being a pattern and became simply the world. Farm after farm, standing open and unhaunted. Orchards heavy with fruit the color of ash. A vineyard where the grapes hung like dull beads of glass. Once we passed a shrine at a crossroads—a little hooded stone figure, offerings long since scattered—and Maren stopped and righted a fallen candlestub and said a word in her hedge-healer's dialect that I suspect was a curse and a blessing at once.

By midday I had tasted six things. Wheat, an apple, well water, a blackberry, a handful of sorrel, and, in a moment of desperation I'm not proud of, dirt.

All of it tasted of nothing.

Except—

"Wait." I crouched at the edge of the road, heart going hard. "Everyone stop. Give me a minute."

They gathered around while I knelt there with my palm flat against the ground like a madwoman, which is more or less what I was. Because the dirt hadn't tasted of nothing. The dirt had tasted of *almost*. Of a ghost of a ghost. Faint as memory of a smell: mineral, wet, alive. Something.

"There's still taste here," I said. "Barely. Underneath."

"Taste of what?" Odile asked.

"I don't know how to describe it except—" I closed my eyes. Five years of tasting the regent's food had taught me to read flavors the way Odile read maps. Every poison leaves a signature. Every spice leaves a history. "It tastes like it's being pulled. Like a current. You know how water tastes different downstream of a tannery? Everything here is downstream of something."

Maren crouched beside me, laid two fingers on the soil, and frowned. "The magic's moving," she said slowly. "I felt it last night at the wards—they didn't just fail, they *drained*. Like a cup with a crack in the bottom." She looked up, and her eyes went down the road, back the way we'd come, toward the distant smudge of Vellamere. "Toward the capital."

"Magic drains downhill," Sef said. He'd been quiet all morning, a big careful man out of his tower. "That's bell-logic. Sound runs to the low places. So does everything else, given time."

"So the capital is drinking," Odile said. Her pencil had stopped moving. "Something in Vellamere is pulling every drop of magic out of the country, and the land is going gray from the edges inward. The wards fell because there was nothing left to hold them up."

"And Casimir's astronomers stopped reporting," Dain said grimly, "because he already knows."

We stood in the middle of the pale road, five people and a child, and the wind moved the gray wheat around us with its sound of sand, and above us the moon hung fat and white in a sky with no stars, counting down.

"Twenty-one nights," Odile said. "Keep walking."

* * *

We camped that night in the shell of a barn, and I cooked, because cooking is the only prayer I know.

I'd brought what I could carry from my kitchen: flour, salt, a knob of rendered fat, dried apples from my own cupboard—the last food in the world I trusted, because I'd tasted every batch myself. I built a small fire in the barn's corner where the roof still held, and I made flatbread the way my mother made it, or the way I remembered her making it, which after twenty years may amount to the same thing.

The smell filled the barn. Real smell. Butter and browning dough and woodsmoke. Pip appeared at my elbow before the first bread came off the stone, drawn by scent the way iron filings are drawn to a lodestone.

"It smells *right*," Pip said, with the fierce emphasis children use when they've found one true thing in a day of false ones. "Everything today smelled wrong. Or didn't smell. But this—"

"This is mine," I said. "I carried it out of the city. It hasn't forgotten what it is yet."

I handed Pip the first bread, too hot, and watched them juggle it hand to hand and blow on it and bite it anyway, and close their eyes. And I stood there in a dead barn in a graying country with the moon counting down outside, and I was so homesick I could hardly breathe.

Not for Vellamere, exactly. Vellamere was a walled box where I had spent five years being carefully, profitably invisible. I had sold charms to neighbors who needed them and pretended otherwise; I had swallowed poison on schedule; I had kept a ledger of small magics in a code only I could read. It had been a good hiding place. Hiding places aren't home.

But the kitchen had been home. The dark before dawn, the ovens ticking as they heated, the particular silence of a city not yet awake. Kneading dough while the regent's household slept, knowing exactly what was in every loaf because I had put it there. My hands knew work. My tongue knew truth. And now I was walking away from both, toward a Doorway under a ruined cathedral, in a country that was being drunk dry, carrying flour and salt and a child who remembered the old sky.

"You're doing the face," Pip said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're fine." Pip bit their bread. "Sef does it too. Everyone does it. Except Maren. Maren just says when she's sad."

I almost laughed. "And when are you sad, Pip?"

Pip considered this with great seriousness, chewing. "When I look up," they said finally. "At night. I keep forgetting, and then I look up, and they're not there. The stars. I know all of them. Did you know that? I know all of them, and there's nowhere to put them." They pointed at me with the bread crust. "You should say when you're sad too. It works better."

"Noted," I said, and meant it more than I expected to.

Later, when the others slept, I sat by the fire and got out the little tin I'd brought and opened it, and looked at what was inside: a single pinch of powder, gray-green, wrapped in waxed cloth. Harmless. I'd dosed myself with harmless poisons for five years to build the tolerance that kept me alive and useful. Old habit. I weighed the tin in my hand and thought about the wheat that tasted of nothing, and the dirt that tasted of *pull*, and the sentry at Westgate chewing his flavorless ration with dead eyes.

If the magic was draining toward the capital—if everything living was being pulled downhill like water—then how long before the food in Vellamere went silent too? Before the bread stopped tasting? Before the whole city became a beautiful gray model of itself, full of people who chewed and swallowed and forgot?

How long before *I* forgot? My ledger was ash. My mother's face was mostly gone already, worn smooth like a coin handled too long. What was holding me together except habit and flour and salt?

I closed the tin. I didn't take the dose. Some habits deserve to die with the life that needed them.

Instead I woke Odile for the second watch, and told her what I'd tasted in the dirt, and watched her map it by firelight—marking the pale fields, the empty farms, a small arrow pointing back toward Vellamere labeled, in her cramped precise hand, *the drain*.

"If we can learn what's pulling," she said, "we can learn how to push back."

"Or what to feed it," I said, and didn't sleep much after that.

* * *

On the third morning we reached the river.

Or what had been a river. The maps in Odile's head called it the Ammel, wide and brown and busy with barges; she talked about it the night before with something like longing, the ferry crossing at Tarn's Ford, the eel pie at the towpath inn. What we found was a channel of gray glass, motionless as poured metal, reflecting the starless sky so perfectly that Pip refused to look at it and walked the whole ford with their eyes squeezed shut and a fist knotted in my sleeve.

Dain waded in first, testing, and the water parted around his legs without a ripple. Cold, he reported. Flavorless, I confirmed, when he brought me a cupped handful. Dead water. But water nonetheless, and we crossed.

On the far bank, the land changed.

Not gray anymore—*paler*. The grass crunched faintly underfoot like frost that wasn't cold. The road ahead ran straight toward the pale wall of the horizon, toward the Salt Cathedral somewhere beyond it, and the light coming off that washed-out distance had a quality I had no name for. It wasn't dim. It was *thin*. Light with the taste boiled out of it.

And there, on the road ahead, maybe half a mile on, someone was walking toward us.

One figure. Traveler's cloak, staff, unhurried. In three days of empty country it was the first living soul we'd seen, and every instinct I owned—the taster's instincts, the survivor's instincts, the instincts that had kept me breathing through five years of a regent's kitchens—all of them stood up at once and shouted.

Dain's hand went to his knife. Odile's pencil stopped. Maren stepped slightly in front of Pip, casual as arranging laundry.

The figure stopped too, and stood, and waited for us on the pale road, and I realized as the wind shifted that I could smell them from thirty paces—which should have been impossible, in air that carried no smells at all.

Woodsmoke. Bread. And underneath, sharp and unmistakable, the green mineral bite of *magic*, strong enough that even my drained and doubting tongue caught it clear as a bell.

Whoever was walking toward us out of the pale fields was soaked in the very thing our country was running out of.

"Don't," Odile breathed, as Dain started forward. "Wait. Look at the road behind them."

I looked. And my mouth went dry.

Behind the walker, faint but unmistakable, the gray grass was *greening*—a narrow ribbon of color trailing in their wake like a wake behind a boat, closing slowly, fading even as we watched.

The stranger raised one hand—not a threat, a greeting—and called out across the thin bright distance in a voice pitched to carry:

"You'll want to hear what happened at the wardenhouse. Before you go another step toward the cathedral."
