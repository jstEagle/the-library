## 10. The Soldier's Confession

Dain had not slept properly in nine days. He knew the count precisely because he'd been counting since the stars went out, the way he'd once counted the seconds between a gate alarm and the arrival of reinforcements—a habit drilled into him at the Wardenhouse until it lived in his spine like a second pulse.

Nine days. Nine nights of lying awake in the cold while Maren's hedge-healer snores and Odile's pen scratched at nothing and Beatrix sat watch with that stillness that wasn't quite sleep.

Tonight it was his watch. He was grateful for it. A watch gave you something to do with your hands besides shake.

They were camped in the lee of a collapsed waymarker, one of the old stone pillars that had once lined the pilgrim roads out of Vellamere. It had a face carved into it, worn smooth by weather, and Dain kept his back to it because the face looked like it was listening. The pale fields stretched out around them, gray rye and gray barley and gray weeds, all of it still growing, none of it quite alive. In the dark it looked like a sea that had decided to hold still forever.

The moon hung low and wrong. Dain had served eleven years on the border wards. He knew the moon's phases the way a farmer knew rain. This was not any phase. It was swollen past full, edged in a faint bruised color, and it was getting worse by the night.

He was sharpening his sword when Beatrix came to relieve him early.

"You're supposed to have two more hours," Dain said.

"And you're supposed to be asleep." Beatrix lowered themselves onto the waymarker's base with the ease of someone who could sit anywhere, in any company, and belong to none of it. "You breathe wrong when you're awake. Too controlled. Soldiers breathe like that."

"I'm not a soldier anymore."

"You deserted. That's different from not being one."

The words landed the way a boot lands on ice that's already cracking. Dain looked down at his sword, at the edge he'd been working, and found his hands had gone still. "Why are you out here, taster?"

"Because you've been circling it for nine days." Beatrix's voice was quiet, level, the voice they used in the regent's kitchens, Dain realized—the voice of someone who had learned that the safest thing in a room full of knives was to sound like you had nothing to lose. "You look at me like you're waiting for me to say something. Like you need me to say something first. I'm tired of watching you rehearse a conversation you never start."

Dain set the whetstone down. His hands were shaking now, openly, and there was no watch to hide them behind.

"You want to know why I deserted," he said. "The actual night. Not the story I gave Odile."

"The story you gave Odile was that you ran when the stars went out because the wards died and you knew what was coming."

"That part's true." He swallowed. "But I didn't run because the wards died. I ran because of what I did before they died."

The gray fields made no sound. No wind, no insects, none of the small noise that a living night is made of. It was the silence that made him say it, he thought later. In Vellamere there had always been bells and carts and neighbors pretending not to listen. Out here the world had gone quiet enough that a confession could actually be heard.

"Three hours before the bells," Dain said. "I was stationed at Ward-gate Nine. East wall, the old pilgrim gate, the one nobody used anymore except smugglers and the occasional desperate pilgrim. It was a dead posting. Everyone hated it. I'd had it for two years." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what I was happy about that night? That it was quiet. That I'd get through another shift reading dispatches and drinking bad tea. That's what I was thinking about when the order came."

"What order?"

"Open the gate." His mouth was dry. He made himself keep going. "A runner came from the Wardenhouse. Sealed orders, regent's cipher, the whole ceremony. The order said the gate was to be unsealed and opened at the first bell of midnight. No reason given. No duration. Just: open, and hold the gatehouse, and let no one through who isn't on the list."

"What list?"

"There was no list. That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was that the order was signed with the regent's personal seal, not the Warden-commander's. Gate orders come from the Warden-commander. Always. Even the regent's household guard goes through the commander. That's not tradition. That's the whole point of a chain of command—you don't let one man open the country's throat because he had a bad night."

Beatrix said nothing. They were good at that, Dain was learning. They listened the way a taster listens—with their whole body, cataloguing everything, giving nothing away.

"So what did you do?" Beatrix asked.

"I did what eleven years of service tells you to do with an order that smells wrong. I sent the runner back for confirmation. And I sat with the sealed order in my hands for three hours, and I told myself I was being diligent." His voice cracked on the last word. "I wasn't being diligent. I was being afraid. Because confirming it meant either disobeying the regent or finding out the regent really had ordered it, and both of those were worse than the tea and the dispatches and the quiet. So I waited. I counted seconds. I was so good at counting seconds."

The moon had climbed a little. Its bruised light lay over the fields like a sheet over a body.

"The first bell rang," Dain said. "And I stood up, and I put my key in the gate lock. Because the order was signed and sealed and I was a soldier and soldiers open gates when they're told to open gates. And the lock turned. And the counterweights started moving. And the gate began to open."

He stopped. Beatrix waited. The carved face of the waymarker seemed, in the corner of his eye, to lean closer.

"And then?" Beatrix said, gently.

"Then the stars started going out." Dain's hands were flat on his knees now, pressing down, as if he could pin himself to the earth. "Not all at once. One by one. Like someone walking along a row of candles and pinching each one. And I knew—I knew in my gut, the way you know a taste is wrong before you know what the poison is—I knew the gate and the stars were the same thing. That the order and the dark were one bargain. And I ran to the winch and I hauled it back and I got the gate closed."

"How long?"

"Four minutes. Maybe five. It was open four or five minutes." He looked at Beatrix at last, and it cost him everything he had left to do it. "The gate was open when the stars went out. I don't know what came through in four minutes. I don't know what the gate being open *did*. I only know that I was the one who turned the key, and I was too late by four minutes, and nine days later the moon is doing something that has never happened in the history of the sky, and every person I ever served with is probably gray dust by now, and it's my—"

"Stop."

It wasn't harsh. That was the strange thing. Beatrix said it the way they'd have said *the soup needs salt*—a simple correction, offered without cruelty.

"Stop," they said again. "Because you're about to do the thing I watched the regent's courtiers do every day of my life, which is take a whole country's catastrophe and fold it up small enough to fit inside one person's guilt. It doesn't fit. I've seen it tried."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know you turned a key." Beatrix's eyes caught the moon's bruised light. "I know you refused first. I know you sent the runner back for confirmation, which is more courage than most of the Wardenhouse has shown in eleven years, and I know that when your gut told you the truth, you ran to the winch. Four minutes, Dain. You were four minutes too late on a night when every other soldier in Caelmere was exactly on time for every wrong thing they were ordered to do. You want to know what the other gates did?"

Dain's throat worked. "What?"

"I've been asking Odile to cross-check her old maps with what we've seen. The ward-line didn't fail everywhere at once. It failed from the gates outward. Every gate. All of them, opened or not—Ward-gate Nine's wards died at the same second as every other gate on the line. Whatever came through your gate, or didn't, the wards were dying regardless. The order wasn't the knife, Dain. The order was the *door*, and the knife had already been sharpened decades before you were born." Beatrix paused. "You didn't kill the stars. You were a man standing in the wrong doorway on the wrong night, and you've been executing yourself for it ever since."

Dain wanted to argue. He could feel the argument in his chest, the old drilled shape of it: *the key was mine, the gate was mine, the seconds were mine to count.* But underneath the argument was the thing Beatrix had just said, and it fit a shape he'd been refusing to look at for nine days—the shape of the dispatch he'd read that same evening, the one reporting that Ward-gates Three through Eight had all received identical sealed orders. He'd told himself it was coincidence. He'd told himself a lot of things.

"If I'd refused outright," he said, and his voice was smaller now, "if I'd broken the seal and sent it back unread—"

"Then a runner would have come with a second order, and a squad after that, and the gate would have been opened by someone who didn't run to the winch afterward." Beatrix shrugged, and it was a terrible shrug, matter-of-fact as arithmetic. "You were one man with one key on a night with a lot of keys. The only thing you controlled was which kind of man you were when the turning came. And you turned back."

The silence after that was long. Dain found he was crying, quietly, the way he hadn't cried since he was a recruit—no sound, just the salt of it, and Beatrix watching with the patient neutrality of someone who had tasted a hundred poisons and knew exactly how long the worst part of any of them lasted.

"I keep seeing the gate opening," he said finally. "The dark coming through it. Like the night outside the walls was a thing with a body."

"I know."

"You can't know."

"I taste the regent's food," Beatrix said. "Do you know what that means, eleven years of it? It means I have swallowed things that should have killed me, because someone paid me to, because it was safer to be the one who swallowed than the one who ordered. I have spent my whole life being useful to a man I suspected was rotten, because the alternative was being poor and dead. You held a gate for eleven years for a crown you suspected was rotten. We're not so different, soldier. We both kept the machine running because the machine fed us." They looked out at the gray fields. "The difference is you stopped. Four minutes late. But you stopped."

Dain wiped his face with the back of his hand, a soldier's gesture, rough and efficient, as if grief were mud to be cleared from the eyes before the next watch.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Really. You didn't come out here for my confession. You came out here for something."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. When they spoke again, the kitchen-voice was gone, and underneath it was something rawer.

"Because tomorrow we reach the Salt Cathedral. And because when we do, someone in this company is going to have to touch that Doorway, and I've watched you all week, and you're the only one of us who's ever stood in front of a sealed gate and made a decision with the country's life in his hands." They stood, brushing the gray dust from their coat. "I need to know if the man who turns the key is someone I can trust. So I asked. That's all. I asked, and you answered, and here's my answer: four minutes late is still a refusal. I've seen what a real betrayal looks like. It wears a crown and signs its orders in its own hand."

They turned to go back toward the cold campfire, and Dain, on impulse, caught their sleeve.

"The list," he said. "The order said *let no one through who isn't on the list.* I told you there was no list attached. But there was one thing written on the back of the order. A single line. I've been trying not to think about it for nine days."

Beatrix went very still. "What line?"

"A name." Dain closed his eyes, calling it up from the place he'd buried it. "Written in the regent's own hand, under the seal. Not a list. Just one name, marked *admit.*" He opened his eyes. "It was *Ferrant.*"

Beatrix's face did something complicated and fast—shock, then calculation, then a cold settling, like a lock turning.

"Odile," they said. "The regent ordered his own licensed cartographer admitted through the gate the night the country was erased." They looked east, toward the black line of the horizon and whatever waited beyond it. "She told us she was stripped of her license years ago. She never told us why. Or who stripped it."

Far off, at the edge of the pale fields, the moon slid behind a cloud, and for a moment the night was honest and dark—and then the cloud passed, and the thirteenth moon came back, a little fuller than it had been at sundown.
