## 11. Salt Cathedral

The cathedral announced itself a mile before we saw it, the way grief does.

First there was the smell—brine and old stone, the sea's memory soaked into land that hadn't touched water in three hundred years. Then the ground changed under our boots, packed white earth that crunched faintly, like walking on sugar. Maren knelt and pressed two fingers to it, then to her tongue, and made a face.

"Salt," she said. "The whole plain is salted."

"Deliberately?" Dain asked, hand drifting toward the axe at his back out of habit. He'd been doing that less since the wardenhouse road, since Beatrix had looked at him across a dead campfire and said *you were one man given an impossible order*, but old habits surface when the sky goes strange. And the sky had been going strange all morning—the moon fat and wrong overhead, its thirteenth phase swelling like a bruise.

"Deliberately," Odile said. She was walking ahead of us with her license-less hands sketching in the air, mapping by instinct because she no longer trusted paper. Paper had betrayed her once; every border on every chart she'd ever drawn had gone blank the night the stars died, as if the country itself had been rubbed out of the world's ledger. "You salt a field so nothing grows there. You salt a plain so nothing builds there. You salt the ground around a cathedral so no one forgets that this place is finished."

Pip walked beside me, which surprised me more than anything else that day. The child had spent five days clinging to Maren's hem or Odile's elbow, and now, suddenly, they wanted to walk with me. They kept reaching up to touch my sleeve—not grabbing, just touching, the way you'd touch a doorframe for luck.

"You're quiet today," Pip said.

"I'm thinking," I said.

"About what?"

"About whether I've been lying to myself my whole life."

Pip didn't understand that, of course. Children rarely understand the specific shape of an adult's ruin. But they nodded anyway, solemn as a bell-ringer, and kept their hand on my sleeve, and I found I didn't want them to let go.

* * *

We saw the cathedral at noon, and it was wrong in a way that took me a long time to name.

It wasn't ruined the way ruins are supposed to be. There were no collapsed walls, no toppled spires, no ivy eating the masonry. The Salt Cathedral stood complete—every buttress, every rose window, every finial on every pinnacled tower—and it stood in a state of perfect, terrible preservation, like a body kept from decay by cold. Except nothing about it was cold. The air around it shimmered faintly, the way cobbles do in high summer, except this was autumn and the shimmer came from the stone itself.

"It's not decaying," Odile said quietly. "It's being held."

"Held by what?" Dain asked.

"By whatever's underneath it."

We approached from the west, through what had once been a pilgrim's gate—Odile knew this from maps she'd drawn as an apprentice, maps of a country that officially did not exist anymore, maps she'd been stripped of her license for insisting upon. The gate arch still bore traces of paint: faded blue, faded gold, the ghost of a sigil burned into the keystone. I stopped beneath it and stared up without meaning to.

I knew that sigil.

Not the way you know a symbol you've studied. Not the way you know a heraldic lion or a merchant's mark. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The way you know the shape of a scar you've carried since childhood.

There is a brand on my left shoulder blade. I have had it since before I can remember—since before the orphanage, since before the kitchens, since before the first time a matron told me I was lucky, *lucky*, to be chosen for the taster's program because children with the mark are sensitive to poison, sensitive to essence, sensitive to things ordinary tongues cannot parse. I used to trace it in the polished surface of the regent's silver trays when I thought no one was looking: a small burning figure, a flame inside a circle, ringed by seven small marks like teeth or stars or words too worn to read.

The sigil on the keystone was that same burning figure. Larger. Older. Whole, where mine was partial—a fragment, a shard, a single piece broken off something vast.

"Beatrix," Odile said. "You've gone white."

"I know this mark," I said.

"From where?"

"I don't know." My voice came out flat and strange. "That's the problem. I don't know *where*. I only know that I've had it on my skin since before I had memories."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the salt grass, making a sound like paper turning pages, and above us the wrong moon hung in the pale sky like a lamp someone had forgotten to snuff.

Maren broke the silence, because Maren always breaks silences; it's the healer in her, the compulsion to treat whatever's bleeding. "Inside," she said. "Whatever answers we're looking for, they're not going to be standing out here in the wind."

* * *

The interior of the cathedral should have been dark. It was noon outside, but cathedrals are built to be dim even at noon, light falling through colored glass in long slanted shafts. This one was lit from below.

A soft gold glow seeped up between the flagstones, up the pillars, along the ribs of the vaulting, as if the whole building were a lantern with the candle lit inside its floor. Dust motes drifted upward instead of down. When Pip dropped a pebble to test it, the pebble fell slowly, reluctantly, like something swimming against a current.

"The magic's draining toward the capital," I said, remembering the gray fields, the tasteless wheat only I could taste. "Everyone said so. So why is it pooling here?"

"Because this is where it comes *from*," Odile said. "Or where it went to hide."

We crossed the nave in a loose line, boots crunching on salt crystals that had blown in through the broken doors and never melted, never washed away, just accumulated year over year like snow in a country where winter forgot to end. Our breath fogged. The glow from below grew stronger.

At the far end, past the shattered altar, past the rood screen with its saints' faces worn smooth as river stones, the floor opened.

Not collapsed—*opened*. A stairway spiraled down into the earth, wide enough for four abreast, its steps hollowed by centuries of feet. The gold light poured upward out of it like smoke. And carved into the first pier beside the descent, deep enough to cast its own shadow, was the sigil again. The burning figure in the circle. Seven marks around it.

My brand had three of those marks. Only three. As if whoever marked me had taken a whole thing and broken it, kept a piece, passed the piece down.

"Tasters," I said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "They choose tasters from bloodlines that used to matter. That's what the kitchens say—that it's tradition, that marked children make better instruments. Nobody says *what* the bloodlines used to do."

"Maybe nobody knows," Dain said.

"Maybe somebody knew and decided we were safer not knowing." I put my hand against the carved pier. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm—there was no sun down here—but warm like a living thing, like the flank of an animal. "Or maybe they knew exactly what we were for, and they've been using us for it for generations without telling us."

* * *

The stairway descended farther than any stairway has a right to descend. We counted steps out of habit—Dain counted for ambushes, Odile counted for scale, Pip counted because counting was how they kept themselves brave—and somewhere past two hundred the air changed. Warmer. Wetter. A sound reached us from below, a sound I will hear in my head until I die: a low, rhythmic hum, like a hive the size of a city, like a choir holding one note forever.

Then the stair opened into the chamber, and I understood why pilgrims had salted the earth above it.

The Doorway was an arch.

That sounds like nothing—an arch, stone, architecture, fine. But standing beneath it, before it, I felt what Odile must have felt the night her maps went blank: the vertigo of realizing the world is larger and stranger than the version of it you were permitted to carry.

The arch stood free in the center of a vast round chamber, unsupported by wall or pillar, thirty feet tall if it was an inch, carved from a single seam of stone that might have been quarried or might have been grown—it bore whorls and grain-marks like wood, like petrified root. Its surface was covered edge to edge in sigils. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The burning figure repeated at intervals like a refrain, and around it spun constellations I didn't recognize, glyphs that hurt to look at directly the way a bright lamp hurts, words in a script that predated every script Odile had ever catalogued.

And it was cold.

Everything else down here was warm—the walls, the air, the humming stone underfoot—but the arch itself radiated cold the way a frozen lake radiates cold, the kind that climbs into your bones and stays. Frost feathered its base despite the warmth of the chamber. When Pip crept close and laid one small palm against it, they snatched the hand back with a yelp.

"It's locked," Pip said, stating the obvious because stating the obvious was how they managed fear.

"Obviously," Dain said, but gently.

"No—I mean—" Pip pressed their palm to the stone again, longer this time, jaw clenched with effort, and I watched a child do something I couldn't do and didn't fully understand. "It doesn't feel like a wall. Walls don't mind being touched. This minds. It's *holding* something shut, and it's tired, and it wants—" Pip groped for the word. "It wants to be *opened properly*. Not forced. Opened the way a door is supposed to open, by someone who knows the way."

Odile stepped forward with her sketchbook—the new one, the one she'd started filling since the blank night, drawing from memory and guesswork because the old maps were dead—and began to copy the sigils. Her hand shook. I'd never seen Odile Ferrant's hand shake; she was the most precisely controlled person I'd ever met, a woman who'd had her livelihood revoked and rebuilt herself around the loss like a tree growing around a fence post.

But her hand shook now, and I understood why when she reached the central panel of the arch and stopped copying and simply *looked*.

The burning figure. The circle. The seven marks.

"Beatrix," she said, and her voice cracked on my name, which it never does, because Odile uses names carefully, sparingly, like currency. "Look at the seventh mark."

I looked. The seven small glyphs ringing the central sigil were not decorative. They were words—or names, or parts of names—worn shallow by centuries of touch, except one, which was worn *deep*, polished smooth, the way a stair hollows where ten thousand feet have trod. Someone had touched that mark so often, over so many years, that they'd erased its edges.

I raised my own hand without deciding to. My fingers found the seventh mark the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. And the moment I touched it—

*Cold. A kitchen. A woman with flour on her hands pressing a hot iron to a screaming child's shoulder, saying hush, hush, this is how we serve, this is how we're remembered, and the child is me, and the iron's face is this exact shape, and the woman is saying a name as she presses it, a name I can't hold onto, a name that slides away like water—*

I came back to myself standing in front of the arch, gasping, Pip's hand pressed flat over my heart like a small shield. The vision—if it was a vision, if it was memory, if it was the arch itself reaching through my skin into whatever the brand had wired into me—took less than three seconds and unmade twenty-five years.

"That's not a memory I have," I said. "I was an infant. Nobody brands infants in the Vellamere kitchens. The matrons said— they said the mark was congenital, a birthmark shaped like—"

"A burning sigil," Odile finished quietly. "Shaped like the seal on the Doorway of Caelmere."

The hum of the chamber seemed to deepen. Or maybe that was my own blood in my ears.

Dain said, carefully, soldier's cadence, each word set down like a boot on ice: "Beatrix. The tasters. How many generations?"

"Nobody counts. The program's older than the regency. Older than the records anyone admits to."

"And what do tasters actually *do*? Beyond the obvious?"

"We taste. We detect poison. We—" I faltered, because I had never once asked the question behind the question, because asking it would have cost me the only safety I'd ever built. "We calibrate. That's the real word for it. The kitchens say the regent's palate needs calibrating against ours, that marked tongues set the standard for essence-detection across the ward-network. I always thought it was bureaucratic nonsense. A justification for keeping a stable of half-orphaned children with strange brands."

"Or," Maren said softly, "it's maintenance."

Everyone looked at her.

"Healers see patterns," she said, with the mildness of a woman describing weather. "The wards that fell on the night of thirteen bells—they didn't break. Breaking is sudden, violent, external. These *thinned*. Like a wound closing wrong, like a body slowly forgetting how to knit itself. And you're telling me there's a lineage of people, branded with fragments of the ward-sigil, whose entire institutional purpose is to stand in the capital and *taste*—to sense essence, to calibrate, to keep some vast invisible system balanced through their own bodies." She spread her hands. "I mend bones for a living. If someone told me the kingdom's skeleton was a network of people, and the healers' guild had been quietly dissolved and replaced with a tasting program, I would call that not nonsense. I would call that a cover story."

The silence that followed had weight. Salt ticked and settled somewhere in the dark above us. The arch hummed its one endless note.

"So the bloodlines bound to the wards," Odile said slowly, working it through the way she worked through a difficult survey, one bearing at a time, "weren't destroyed when the old order fell. They weren't erased. They were *repurposed*. Bound servants became food-tasters. Ward-keepers became kitchen staff. The most important magical office in the country was dressed up as the humblest one, because—" and here she laughed, short and bitter and full of admiration, "—because who suspects the servant carrying the bread basket? Who looks twice at the person whose job is defined by swallowing?"

I thought of my ledger. My secret ledger of small magics, the charms sold to neighbors who pretended not to believe, the tiny workings I'd performed for years while telling myself I was a fraud with a sensitive tongue. I thought of the dosing—my private regimen of harmless poisons, building tolerance, keeping the instrument calibrated. I had thought I invented that. I had thought it was cleverness, survival instinct, my own invention.

It was training. It was the job. I had been doing the work of a ward-keeper my entire adult life without knowing the word for it, in a kitchen, in an apron, in perfect obedient invisibility.

"They didn't erase the bloodlines," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "They erased the *knowledge*. They kept the hands and cut out the understanding. Generation after generation of people like me, born marked, taken young, taught to serve, and never once told that the brand on our shoulders is a key."

"Is it?" Pip asked. "A key?"

I looked at the arch. At the seventh mark my fingers still rested against, worn deep by centuries of other thumbs, other palms, other branded people who had come down here before me—pilgrims, warders, tasters sent on some errand history forgot to record—and I felt the cold of the stone reach up my arm and settle over my heart like a hand.

"I think," I said, "that it's part of one."

* * *

We made camp in the nave rather than descend again in the dark. Dain insisted on watch rotations; Maren insisted on food; Odile insisted on sketching until her candle drowned in its own wax. Pip fell asleep against my side halfway through a bowl of barley soup, mid-sentence, mid-question—*do you think the arch dreams about us too*—and I sat very still for a long time so as not to wake them.

I should have felt afraid. Everything I'd learned that day should have terrified me: the brand was a key, the kitchens were a disguise, my whole life had been a lie so well-built I'd lived inside it comfortably for twenty-five years. Instead I felt something closer to grief, and underneath the grief, absurdly, relief.

All those years of being useful and unremarkable. All those years of believing my invisibility was a talent I'd cultivated, a discipline, a virtue. It was none of those things. It was a cage built before I was born, and I had decorated it lovingly, the way prisoners do, hanging charms on its bars.

But the cage had a door. The door had been under my feet—in my skin—all along.

Near midnight, when the others slept, I climbed alone down the spiral stair to the chamber. I didn't touch the arch. I stood before it with my arms folded, letting the cold pour off it, listening to the hum, and I traced my brand through my shirt with two fingers and thought about the woman in the vision, the flour on her hands, the name she'd spoken as the iron came down—a name that slid away like water.

Whose name? Mine? The bloodline's? The country's?

The arch's frost crept a little nearer to my boots, and in the gold light rising up the stairwell behind me, I saw something I hadn't seen before, something too faint for daylight: among the thousands of sigils covering the arch, near the base, at the height of a kneeling person's hand, one glyph was not worn smooth like the rest.

It was fresh. Recently cut. Days old, maybe weeks.

And beneath it, in the old prehistoric script that hurt to read, someone had added a single line in a newer hand—a hand I recognized, because I had seen it on requisition orders and execution warrants and the regent's official seal for fifteen years.

Casimir had been here.

Casimir knew about the Doorway. Casimir had come down into the dark, alone or nearly alone, and left his mark on the lock of the world.

I climbed back up the stair with that knowledge sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal, and I woke Odile before dawn, because some things are too heavy to carry alone until morning.

"Odile," I said. "The regent found it first."
