## 12. A Kitchen in Exile

The hollow they found was an old quarry, half-swallowed by birch scrub, with a shelf of overhanging stone that kept off the worst of the wind. Dain called it defensible. Maren called it damp. Beatrix called it, after a long look at the fire ring some forgotten stonemason had left behind, *a kitchen*.

That was the word that changed the evening. Not camp. Not shelter. A kitchen.

They had been on the road four days since the Salt Cathedral, and four days of hardtack and foraged greens had worn everyone thin in ways that had nothing to do with hunger. Odile flinched at her own sketches. Dain checked the treeline every ninety seconds like a metronome. Pip hadn't laughed once since the wardens took Maren—no. Since before that. Since the stars went out.

But a kitchen was something people knew how to be in.

Beatrix had carried flour from Vellamere wrapped in oilcloth, hoarded against exactly this kind of night, though they'd never admitted it even to themselves. They unpacked it now with the ceremony of a surgeon laying out instruments: flour, a clay jar of salt, a twist of dried yeast, a small crock of honey traded from a farmwife who'd taken a charm against gray dreams and paid without asking questions.

"Where did you get all this?" Odile asked.

"I'm a taster," Beatrix said. "I steal food professionally. This is just the honest version."

Dain snorted. It was the first sound he'd made all day that wasn't a warning.

They built the fire small, the way Beatrix liked it—big enough to bake, too small to see from the ridge. While it caught, Beatrix measured flour by feel, by the weight of it in the bowl, the way their mother's mother must have done in some kitchen now erased from every map but one. Water from the stream. Salt. Yeast bloomed in honey-water until it smelled faintly of apples going soft.

Pip appeared at the edge of the firelight the way Pip always did—sideways, watchful, as if permission were a door that might be locked.

"You can come closer," Beatrix said. "It's not my kitchen. It's ours."

"It's not really a kitchen."

"It has fire, flour, and someone bossy. That's three more than most kitchens have."

Pip came closer.

* * *

Teaching a child to bake is mostly teaching them to be unafraid of mess, and Pip was afraid of everything now, so Beatrix started there. Flour on the hands first. Deliberately. *Look, I've ruined myself already and the world continues.* Then flour on Pip's hands, which produced a small gasp of horror and then, unexpectedly, a giggle—the first one in days, high and startled, like a bird flushing from cover.

"That's the whole secret," Beatrix told them. "Bakers are just people who've made peace with being covered in it."

Pip pressed their palms flat into the dough and pushed, and the dough pushed back, and something in the child's shoulders loosened that had been locked since the thirteenth bell.

"Harder," Beatrix said. "It can't feel pain. That's the other secret. Dough forgives everything."

"What if I do it wrong?"

"Then we eat it wrong. I've eaten worse for the regent. Once I tasted a pheasant pie that had been sitting under a window in July. You know what happened? Nothing. My tongue is a graveyard of things that could have killed kings." Beatrix turned the dough, folded it. "Your hands won't kill anyone either. Fold from the far side, turn, fold again. Like you're closing a book you don't want anyone else reading."

"Why would you close a book?"

"Because some books are only yours."

Pip thought about that with their whole body, the way children do, kneading in slow rhythm while they decided what they believed about secrets. Eventually: "Is your book about magic?"

"My book is about bread, mostly. Magic got in by accident."

"Everything gets in by accident," Pip said, with the terrible authority of nine years old. "That's what my mama said about me."

Beatrix felt that one somewhere behind the ribs. They didn't answer. Some statements you just let stand, like a finished wall.

Across the fire, Odile had spread her papers on a flat stone and was working with a stub of charcoal she'd burned herself from willow twig. She drew slowly, checking each line against memory twice before committing it, because her paper had betrayed her once already—every border gone blank overnight, every label faded to ghost-text. Now she drew a country no mapmaker would ever certify: Caelmere as it had been, or as she remembered it being, which might be the same thing or might be better.

She drew the river first. Then the capital, then the road they'd walked, then the pale fields where the crops grew gray and tasteless to everyone but Beatrix. When she reached the Salt Cathedral she paused, and drew its arch, and marked it with a small burning shape—a sigil sketched from a single glimpse underground, held in her head like a coal cupped in bare hands.

"What are you drawing?" Pip asked, peering over, trailing flour.

"A country."

"There isn't one. There's just us and the fog past the fields."

"There used to be," Odile said. "There still is. Maps are just memories with lines around them." She tapped the blank margin at the edge of her page. "This part here—that's where the rest of the world is supposed to be. Every map I ever made had this hole in it, and I thought the hole was my failure. Turns out the hole was the truth. We were drawn inside a lie so big it looked like paper."

Dain drifted over, drawn despite himself. He stood at Odile's shoulder for a long time, saying nothing, arms crossed, a man auditing a report he couldn't refute.

"The ford at Marrow Bridge," he said finally. "You have it wrong. It's two miles downstream."

Odile looked up at him. "You know Marrow Bridge?"

"I garrisoned it. Three years." His voice did something complicated. "I fished there. Sundays."

"Then correct it," Odile said, and handed him the charcoal.

He held it like it might go off. Then he knelt, and in careful blocky strokes, a soldier's hand making a soldier's mark, he fixed the ford. And then, because no one stopped him, he added the fishing pool below it, and the willow that leaned wrong over the water, and the flat stone where he'd sat and gutted trout and thought, in those long empty Sundays, that his life was small and fine.

The map filled the way a fire fills a dark room—one person's memory catching another's.

Maren limped back from gathering yarrow in time to see it. Her ankle had been bad since the wardenhouse; she'd refused to say how bad, and Beatrix had refused to ask, both of them practicing the same mercy of silence. She lowered herself onto a stone, looked at the growing country on the page, and said, "Put the hot spring above Hallowmere. The one that smells of sulfur. I pulled a boy out of a fever once because his grandmother swore by that water."

Odile wrote it in. *Hallowmere springs — healing.* In Maren's honor, though nobody said so.

By the time the loaves went into the Dutch oven Beatrix had brought—buried in coals, lid stacked with embers, the old trick—the map showed rivers none of them had seen together, roads each of them had walked alone, and a border drawn in dotted line, labeled in Odile's small fierce hand: *here the forgetting stops.*

* * *

The bread came out as the light failed completely. Four misshapen loaves, one of them unmistakably Pip's—lopsided, split along one side where the child had scored it too deep, steam venting from the wound like a little whistle.

"It's ugly," Pip announced.

"It's perfect," Beatrix said. "Ugly bread tastes better. Ask any baker. The pretty ones are showing off. Yours worked for a living."

They ate with their hands, passing loaves across the circle, tearing, buttering with a scrape of salt pork Maren had been saving. Honey on the burnt ends. Nobody spoke much while they ate. Good bread imposes a silence of its own, and after days of fear-talk—routes, wardens, the moon climbing toward its impossible fullness—it was a different silence, a full one.

It was Dain who broke it, the way quiet men break things: accidentally, and all at once.

"We had a cook," he said, staring at his crust. "At Marrow Bridge. Old fellow named Sarel. Made a soup on feast days with river fish and wild garlic. Whole garrison went quiet when it came out. Same as this." He swallowed. "I keep expecting him to walk out of the trees and complain about my fire."

"Tell us about him," Pip said.

And that was the evening's second miracle: stories. Not plans, not arguments about the Doorway, not calculations of how many turns the moon had left. Stories. Maren told about the fevered boy and the sulfur water, and how his grandmother had paid her in a knitted shawl she still wore under her cloak. Odile told about her licensing exam, the map she'd drawn of the northern coast at nineteen, and the examiner who'd failed her—not for error, she was sure now, but because her coastline had included villages the official maps omitted, and omission, she understood now, had been policy long before it was her crime.

"They didn't strip my license for bad cartography," she said, and her voice cracked on it, years late. "They stripped it for accuracy."

Beatrix told the story of the pheasant pie, properly this time, with gestures, and Pip laughed until they had to put down their bread. And then, because the fire was low and the honesty was high, Beatrix told one they'd never told anyone: about the first poison they'd ever dosed themselves with, at fourteen, alone in a rented room, practicing on purpose because a taster who feared bitterness was a taster who'd hesitate, and hesitation kills. About how they'd lain on the floor for six hours sweating, cataloguing the symptoms like a scholar, writing them later in the ledger in code, entry forty-one, *bitterwillow, mild, survivable, note the ringing in the ears lasts two days.*

"I built my whole life out of entries like that," Beatrix said. "Small magics. Small harms survived. Small kindnesses sold for coppers. I thought if I kept everything small enough, nothing could take anything big away from me." They looked at the circle of them—the deserter, the disgraced mapmaker, the hedge-healer on a bad ankle, the child who remembered the sky. "And then everything got taken anyway. All at once. The whole country. So it turns out small wasn't protection. It was just... less to love."

"That's sad," Pip said.

"It's true," Beatrix said. "Those can be the same thing."

"But you love stuff now. Big stuff. Us."

Beatrix looked at the child across the fire, flour still in their hair, holding a heel of lopsided bread like a scepter.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Apparently I do. It happened by accident. Everything does."

* * *

Later, when Pip slept against Maren's good side and Dain took first watch facing the ridge, Beatrix sat with Odile at the map.

The moon was rising over the quarry rim, swollen and strange, well into its thirteenth turn now. You could see it if you knew to look: a sliver of phase that had no name in any almanac, a shape the sky had never held before. It lit the birches blue-white and made the quarry floor look like the bottom of a drained sea.

"How long?" Beatrix asked. Odile had done the scholars' arithmetic back at the cathedral; she carried the number like a stone.

"If the pace holds—eleven nights. Maybe twelve."

Eleven nights. Eleven more fires. Eleven more chances to teach a child to bake, to fix a ford on a map, to tell a story that had never been told aloud before.

"Can I ask you something selfish?" Beatrix said.

Odile raised an eyebrow.

"When this is over—if it's over, either way—will you draw the bakery? On one of your maps. The real one, the new one, whatever it turns out to be. Mark it. A little square with a chimney." Beatrix fed a stick into the coals. "I spent five years being nowhere on purpose. If the country survives, I'd like to be somewhere on purpose. Even if it's just ink."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she took out the charcoal stub, the same one Dain had held, and on the fresh page she'd started—the new Caelmere, the one with open borders she only half-dared imagine—she drew a small square near the river bend below the capital. A chimney. Smoke curling from it in two lazy lines.

She didn't label it. Labeling felt like jinxing, and Odile had lost enough to jinxes.

"There," she said. "Unlabeled. The best places usually are."

They sat with that, two adults watching a fire and a moon that shouldn't exist, and Beatrix felt the strangest thing settle over them—not hope exactly, hope was too loud a word—but something quieter. The feeling of dough risen under cloth. Of a thing quietly becoming what it was going to be.

Then Dain's voice cut across the quarry from the ridge, low and flat, the voice of a man whose Sundays at a fishing pool had taught him exactly what an interrupted silence sounds like:

"Firelight. North ridge. Two riders, maybe three. Moving slow."

The warmth went out of the evening like a snuffed candle. Beatrix was on their feet before they'd decided to stand, heart doing the old taster's trick—cold, fast, cataloguing. Riders on the north road meant Vellamere. Vellamere meant Casimir. Casimir meant the hunt had found them eleven nights early.

"Pip stays asleep," Maren whispered, already moving to shield the child with her body. "Whatever happens, Pip stays asleep."

Beatrix raked dirt over the coals with one boot, killing the light, and stood in the sudden dark listening to hoofbeats that might be wardens—or might, in a country where everything familiar had become uncanny, be something else entirely.
