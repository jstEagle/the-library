## 13. Casimir's Hunters

Dain heard the horses before anyone else. He'd spent nine years listening for them—first as a warden himself, then as a man the wardens wanted—and he knew the difference between a patrol and a hunt the way he knew the difference between rain coming and rain arriving.

Patrols rode loose. Hunts rode in formation: four hooves to a rhythm, then four more offset behind, spread wide enough to close a road but tight enough not to lose each other in the dark. The sound coming up from the north was a hunt.

"They're fanning out," he said quietly, crouching beside the dead fire. "Three riders on the road, at least two more cutting through the fields to flank us. Warden tack—I can hear the chain-mail under the saddlecloths."

Beatrix didn't ask how he knew. That was one of the things Dain had come to trust about them: they never wasted breath on questions that had answers standing right in front of them. They just nodded once and started moving—kicking dirt over coals, waking Odile with a hand on her shoulder, gathering the ledger-scarred satchel of charms like it was a child.

Maren was already awake. She'd been awake since dusk, she admitted later, because the feverfew by the stream had curled its leaves shut two hours early, and plants only did that when something wrong was walking toward them. She had Pip bundled against her chest before Beatrix finished raking the fire, and the child—mercifully, miraculously—stayed asleep, small fists knotted in the healer's collar.

"South," Odile whispered. "There's a drainage cut past the hedgerow. It runs toward the old millroad."

"South is where they want us," Dain said. "Flankers will be there first." He pointed east, where the pale fields gave way to a stand of black poplars. "Trees. We move quiet, we move now, and if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," Maren said.

It went wrong.

* * *

The poplars should have been cover. Instead they were a trap with leaves on it, because the wardens weren't tracking hoofprints or campfire smoke or any of the things Dain had been trained to track. They were tracking magic. Dain understood this only afterward, piecing it together, but in the moment all he knew was that the trees lit up.

Not fire. Light—the old kind, the kind the border wards used to throw off before they died, blue-white and cold, crawling along the bark of every poplar in the grove like veins filling with ice. And in that light, six figures in warden gray stood among the trunks where no one had been standing a heartbeat before.

"Ward-gate," Dain breathed. His stomach dropped through the ground. "They still have working gates. The regent kept some alive."

The lead warden raised a hand and the light bent toward it, gathering into a net of sigils that hung in the air between the trees. Dain knew that net. He'd helped string it, once, across a mountain pass full of people who'd only wanted to leave.

"Run," he said, and shoved Odile west.

What followed was chaos in the dark, and Dain's memory of it would always come back to him in pieces. Odile's elbow catching him in the ribs as they crashed through bramble. Beatrix hurling something small and clay from their satchel—a charm that burst into a wall of thick white smoke smelling of burnt rosemary, buying them ten seconds. Pip finally waking, crying Maren's name into the night. The whistle of a capture-net unfolding. The wet, heavy sound of a body hitting the ground hard.

And Maren, turning around.

She was the slowest of them—fifty-three years old, bad knee, a child in her arms—and she was the one who stopped. Dain saw it happen and screamed at her not to, and she looked at him with an expression he'd seen on soldiers holding a breach: calm, decided, already gone.

"Take Pip," she said, and passed the child to Beatrix, and turned to face the wardens with her hands raised—not surrendering, healing. Dain felt it even from thirty feet away, that wash of warmth, the hedge-healer's gift pushed outward for the first time as a weapon: the nearest warden staggered as his migraine bloomed and crested; another doubled over, nose gushing; a third simply sat down in the nettles, weeping, unburdened of a grief he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten whose it was.

"You can't fight them all," Dain shouted.

"I'm not fighting," Maren called back, and there was almost laughter in it. "I'm making them feel better. It's slowing them down wonderfully."

It bought them thirty seconds. Maybe forty. Then the lead warden—Dain recognized the voice before the face, a captain named Hesk, who'd once shared a watch-fire with him and complained about his wife's cooking—threw the sigil-net himself, and it came down over Maren like a shroud, and the light ate her warmth out of the air, and she folded to her knees with her hands still open, still giving, until the net took that too.

"MAREN—" Pip shrieked, and Beatrix clamped a hand gently over the child's mouth and ran, and Dain ran too, hating every step of it, hating the arithmetic of it—one healer traded for four lives—and knowing, with the cold clarity of a man who had made this exact calculation once before and refused it too late, that Maren had made it first.

The last thing he saw through the trees was Hesk kneeling beside her, and Maren, even netted, even kneeling, reaching up to touch the man's temple and take away his headache anyway.

* * *

They didn't stop until the sky went gray at the edges. Then they stopped because Odile collapsed against a fencepost and couldn't take another step, and because Pip was sobbing in great silent gulps that were worse than screaming, and because Beatrix sat down in the middle of the pale field and said, very quietly, "No," as though the word could be appealed to someone.

Dain stood watch. It was what he was for. He faced back the way they'd come and let the others break, and while he watched the empty road he did the other thing he was for, which was remember.

Hesk. Wardens with live ward-gates. A net keyed to suppress healing. None of that was standard issue. Standard issue had died with the stars. Which meant the regent had kept a private stock of working magic—hoarded it, hidden it, fed it—and had now unleashed it not on invaders, not on the erasure eating the country from the edges inward, but on five unarmed travelers and a child.

"Why us?" Odile asked the fencepost, or the dawn, or herself. "We're nobody. We're *unregistered*. We're the least magical people left in Caelmere."

"We know where the Doorway is," Beatrix said. Their voice was flat and terrible. Dain had heard tasters talk like that once, describing poison symptoms to a cook. Clinical. Because feeling it would stop the work. "Pip dreamed the second sigil-site. Odile mapped the route. I—" They stopped. Started again. "There are four people alive who can find the Doorway beneath the Salt Cathedral, and three of them are sitting in this field. Casimir doesn't want to catch us. He wants to catch *one of us*, and he'll question whichever one he gets."

"He has Maren," Pip said. Small voice, wrecked. "Maren doesn't know anything about doors."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "But she knows everything about us."

The silence after that was the worst silence Dain had ever stood inside, and he had once stood inside a ward-gate house while his commanding officer read him orders he refused to carry out.

* * *

It was Odile who broke it, because Odile's mind worked like her maps: when one road failed, she drew another.

"Hesk," she said. "You knew him. You said his name like you knew him."

"Shared a watch-fire. Two years." Dain kept his eyes on the road. "He's not cruel. He's loyal. There's a difference, and it gets people killed either way."

"Loyal men follow orders," Odile said. "Orders come from somewhere. If we know what Casimir told them to do with prisoners—if we know where they're taking her—we can—"

"We can't storm a wardenhouse," Dain said. "Four of us. One of whom is eight years old."

"I'm nine," Pip said, with the ferocity of the very small.

"We can't storm a wardenhouse," Dain repeated, gentler. But his mind was already doing it, because his mind had always done exactly that, drawn floorplans of places he'd sworn off, counted guards, marked the shift change at dusk. He hated himself a little for how easily the skill came back. Like a sword arm. Like a reflex.

It was Beatrix who said what everyone was thinking.

"The wardenhouse at Crossmere is the closest holding station. Half a day back the way we came. If Hesk follows protocol, Maren goes there for processing before transfer to Vellamere." They were speaking in their kitchen voice, the one from the regent's pantry, cataloguing. "Half a day gives us a window. And a wardenhouse garrison eats."

Odile stared at them. "What does that have to do with anything?"

"Everything," Beatrix said, and for the first time since the poplars, something moved behind their eyes that wasn't grief. "I spent five years learning what poisons do and how they taste and which ones sleep a grown man for six hours without stopping his heart. Every garrison kitchen in Caelmere buys from the same three suppliers. I know those suppliers. I know their routes. I know which deliveries arrive at Crossmere before dusk." They looked down at their hands, the taster's hands, steady as ever. "I told myself I left that life behind. Apparently I brought it with me."

* * *

They learned the rest from the prisoner.

That was the part Dain would think about later, turning it over like a stone with something underneath. They hadn't gone looking for answers. The answers had ridden into their camp wearing warden gray, same as the hunters had.

An hour past dawn, with the company packing to move, a horse came stumbling down the millroad riderless—or nearly riderless. Its rider was slung across its neck, groaning, one arm hanging wrong, and Dain had his knife out and Beatrix had a charm half-drawn from the satchel before they recognized him: the third warden from the grove, the one who'd sat down in the nettles and wept. Maren's gift had gotten into him deep and stayed there, and somewhere between the trees and here, his horse had thrown him, or he'd fallen, and his comrades—hurrying their prisoner toward Crossmere—hadn't stopped.

He was young. Twenty, maybe. He cried when he saw them, which Dain found worse than fighting.

"Please," the boy said. "Please, whatever she put in me, don't take it out. I can *think* for the first time in three years. Do you know what it's like? Serving him? Knowing—" He hiccupped, swayed in the saddle, and told them everything, the way people do when a healer's mercy has cracked them open like an egg.

They learned that Maren was alive, bound and netted, due at Crossmere by nightfall. They learned that the garrison there was twelve strong, expecting reinforcement from Vellamere within the week. And they learned the thing that turned Dain's blood to well water:

The boy had served in the regent's own household guard before Crossmere. He'd stood post outside the map room. He'd heard the regent speak to his cartographers-at-arms, the ones who still kept the secret charts.

"He's not trying to escape the erasure," the boy said, words tumbling, tears freezing on his cheeks in the morning cold. "He's *bargaining* with it. The Doorway isn't a way out—it's a mouth. Old stories say it eats. And the moon-turn ends soon, and when it does, everything unclaimed goes. So he means to feed it. Feed it the last of the country's magic—all of it, the wards' residue, the hedge-craft, the charms, everything that's left living in Caelmere—in exchange for keeping one thing un-erased." The boy swallowed. "His rule. His name in the world beyond. He'd trade every hedge-witch and charm-seller and unregistered soul in the country for his own chair."

Nobody spoke. Overhead, through the gray morning cloud, the thirteenth moon showed as a pale bruise, one phase from full.

"That's why the hunters," Dain said slowly. "We're not witnesses. We're *ingredients*. People who remember the old magic—he needs us delivered to the Doorway, or erased before we can interfere."

"He needs the child most," the boy whispered, and flinched as if his own words had struck him. "The one who remembers the sky. The captain said—orders came down—the child is to be taken *unharmed above all*. The regent said memory like that is worth ten sigils."

Pip, who had been standing at the edge of the group with Odile's coat wrapped around their shoulders like a cloak twice borrowed, stepped forward.

"My ma remembered the sky," Pip said. "She taught me every star before they went out. She's gone now, and the stars are gone, and Maren's gone, and I'm the only one left who knows them." The child's chin came up, and Dain saw, with a jolt, not a frightened eight-year-old but something older looking out—the last librarian of a burning library, deciding not to run. "So he can't have me. And you can tell him I said so."

The boy warden looked at Pip for a long moment. Then he slid off his horse, carefully, favoring the bad arm, and knelt in the pale grass, and said, "Tell me what to do."

* * *

They tied him loosely and left him with water and half their bread, because Beatrix insisted and because, Dain admitted privately, the boy's testimony was worth more than his captivity. Then the four of them stood in a circle around Odile's map—the route to Crossmere inked fresh that morning, the wardenhouse drawn as a square with a yard and a kitchen door, because Odile had surveyed such buildings once, in her licensed life, and never forgotten one.

"Twelve garrison," Dain said. "One gate, one kitchen door, cells below. Shift changes at dusk. Reinforcements within the week, so our window closes fast." He tapped the kitchen door. "Beatrix goes in through the suppliers' delivery. I go over the wall. Odile keeps Pip and the horse ready at the millroad, and if we're not out by moonrise—"

"We will be out by moonrise," Beatrix said.

"—if we're not," Dain repeated, "you ride for the Salt Cathedral without us. That's not defeat. That's the mission. Maren would say the same."

"Maren would say it and then turn around and come back for us anyway," Odile said, and no one laughed, and everyone agreed.

Dain looked east, toward Crossmere, where the sun was climbing and the thirteenth moon was fading into daylight like a debt waiting to be collected. Somewhere in a cell beneath that wardenhouse was a woman who had healed her own captor even as the net closed over her, and somewhere south of here was a regent hollowed out by an old bargain, preparing to feed a country to a door.

Dain had refused one order in his life, too late, and carried it ever since like a stone in his boot.

This time, he thought, tightening the strap of his knife, we refuse it early.

"Mount up," he said. "We have until dusk."
