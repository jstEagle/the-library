## 14. The Price of Names

The wayshrine appeared out of the gray fields like a tooth left in an empty jaw.

Odile spotted it first—she had a cartographer's eye for anything that broke a horizon—and she called a halt while the light was still good. The shrine was small, older than any building Beatrix had ever entered, its stones worn round as river pebbles and furred with lichen that should have been green but came up pale as ash when Maren touched it.

"Wayshrines used to dot every road," Odile said, running her fingers along the lintel. "Travelers left offerings. I mapped three of them, once, before—" She stopped, the way she always stopped when she reached the edge of her own disgrace. "They're not on my maps anymore either."

"Nothing is," Dain said.

"Nothing is," Odile agreed, and went inside first, because that was the kind of courage she had—the quiet, stubborn kind that kept walking after everything it drew had been erased.

Beatrix followed with Pip's hand in theirs. The child had been quiet all day, watching the sky the way other children watched a pot about to boil over. The moon hung above them now in its impossible thirteenth turn, swollen and wrong, a coin bitten by some enormous mouth. Pip kept glancing at it and then deliberately looking away, which was more discipline than most soldiers Beatrix had known could manage.

Inside, the shrine was one room, domed, lit by nothing they could name. There was no lamp, no window, and yet the walls gave off a faint pearlescent glow, like the memory of light rather than light itself. In the center stood a basin of black stone, dry as bone. And beside the basin sat a figure.

Beatrix's hand went to their knife before their mind caught up. The figure was old—not old like a grandmother, but old like weather, like stone, like the idea of age itself. It wore robes that might have been fine once and were now simply *time-colored*. Its face was kind and unfinished, the way faces are in dreams, and when it turned to look at them, Beatrix felt the particular chill of being seen by something that did not use eyes.

"Well," the figure said. Its voice was dry leaves and bell-metal. "Tasters' blood. I wondered when one of you would come."

Maren stepped forward, hands already glowing faintly with her hedge-healer's warmth. "We mean no harm."

"No," the ghost said. "You mean questions. Harm is what you're carrying whether you mean it or not." It gestured at the basin. "Sit. All of you. Standing visitors make me feel like a debt collector."

They sat—or arranged themselves around the basin, because there was little enough to sit on. Pip pressed close against Beatrix's side. The bell-ringer, Tam, who had barely spoken since they'd left Vellamere, drifted toward the wall where a rope hung, frayed and purposeless, and put his hand on it anyway, the way he probably touched every rope he met.

"You are the ward-ghost," Odile said. Not a question. She'd been carrying this theory for two days, turning it over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. "The shrines were built on the anchor-points of the border wards. When the wards died, you should have died too."

"I did die," the ghost said cheerfully. "Some centuries ago. But I was bound to the wards, and the wards were bound to the bargain, and the bargain—" it spread its unfinished hands, "—has not yet finished expiring. So here I sit. A clerk at a closed office, waiting for someone to come stamp a form."

"What bargain?" Beatrix asked.

The ghost looked at them for a long moment. Beatrix had spent five years reading faces across a regent's table—distinguishing fear from hunger from poison—and there was nothing in this face to read. That frightened them more than hostility would have.

"The bargain," the ghost said, "is why your country has no borders on any map beyond these walls. Why the stars over Caelmere went out. Why the moon has begun a turn that does not exist." It folded its hands. "Caelmere sold its name."

* * *

The silence that followed had weight. Pip's grip tightened on Beatrix's sleeve.

"Sold it," Odile repeated flatly. "Countries don't have names to sell. Names are just—words. Marks on maps."

"Then what is a country?" the ghost asked. "Land? Land doesn't know it's Caelmere. People? People forget; people die; people move. A country is a story the world agrees to keep telling. And the world keeps stories by their names. Take away the name, and the world stops telling. Stops remembering. Stops *seeing*." The ghost leaned forward. "Your neighbors didn't erase you out of malice, cartographer. They erased you because Caelmere asked them to. Paid them to, in the only currency that mattered."

"Paid with what?" Dain said. His voice was rough. He'd been getting rougher the closer they came to answers, as if each truth scraped him rawer.

"With its name," the ghost said patiently. "Generations ago—before your grandparents' grandparents—the kings of Caelmere looked at the wars chewing up the world beyond the mountains and made a choice. They went to the old powers, the ones under the wards, and they traded. The name of the nation, freely given, in exchange for protection. No army could find them. No famine could be sent to them. No map beyond the walls would ever show them, no foreign tongue would ever speak of them. A country hidden so completely that even history forgot to hate it."

"And the price?" Maren asked quietly. She was holding her own hands now, the healer-glow dimmed. "There's always a price."

"The price was paid in advance," the ghost said. "Every child born inside those walls was born into the bargain. Bound without consent. That's the flaw in it—that was always the flaw. A protection bought with names that were never offered can only last as long as the original offering holds true. And the kings who gave it—" the ghost's unfinished face did something that might have been a wince, "—did not give it freely. They gave it desperately. Desperation sours a gift. The old powers knew it. They accepted anyway, because even a souled-out name buys real protection, for a time."

"How long?" Beatrix asked.

"Longer than it lasted," the ghost said. "The protection expired on the night the bells rang thirteen times. What you've been walking through since—the gray fields, the tasteless crops, the fading streets—is not punishment. It's simply the world forgetting faster now that the bargain is void. Erasure accelerates when a contract breaks. And the thirteenth turn of the moon is the clock running down. When it completes, Caelmere will not be destroyed. Destruction requires having existed. Caelmere will simply never have been."

Pip made a small sound. Beatrix pulled the child closer without thinking about it, the way they'd once steadied a trembling tray.

* * *

"There's a Doorway," Odile said. "Under the Salt Cathedral. An arch covered in sigils. Locked."

The ghost turned to her with something like approval. "Cartographers. Always the practical ones. Yes. The Doorway is the lock's keyhole, if you like. The sigils on it are the terms of the original contract, written in the language the old powers speak. And the contract can be rewritten—but only in the same currency it was drawn in."

"A name," Beatrix said.

Their own voice sounded strange to them. The ghost looked at them, and Beatrix had the sudden, vertiginous sense that the thing had been waiting for them specifically—that "tasters' blood" hadn't been a greeting but an identification.

"A name," the ghost agreed. "Freely given. That is the whole of it and the whole of the difficulty. The original bargain failed because it was taken, not given—taken from generations who never agreed. To rewrite it, the old powers require the one thing desperation cannot counterfeit: a true name, offered freely, by someone who understands exactly what they are giving away." The ghost's gaze held Beatrix's. "Not a false name. Not a street name, not a kitchen name, not the name you go by among strangers. Your true name. The one written into you before you could choose anything at all. And once given, it cannot be taken back. Every record of you—every ledger line, every memory in any mind outside the circle of those who witnessed the giving—will let you go. You will become unrecorded. Unwritten. You will walk out of the world's accounting entirely."

"That's not sacrifice," Tam said suddenly, from his place by the rope. His voice cracked on it. "That's erasure. You're asking someone to do to themselves what the bargain did to all of us."

"I am asking no one," the ghost said. "I am describing a door. Doors don't ask. They only open or stay shut." It rose, and rising, seemed somehow both taller and smaller, as though size were another thing it had half-forgotten. "But hear the rest, since you've come this far. A name given freely does not only pay a debt. Freely given, it becomes an *anchor*. The old powers do not want Caelmere dead—they want it *held*. A willing name can hold a whole country's memory in the world beyond these walls. One person, unwritten, becomes the thread by which everything else is rewoven. The country returns changed—open, named anew, remembered—but only if someone pays the toll of their own self."

"And if no one does?" Maren asked.

The ghost gestured outward, at the gray fields, the bitten moon, the tasteless air. "Then the turn completes, and the question answers itself."

* * *

They made camp in the shrine that night, because none of them could bear to leave it yet, and because the ghost—clerk at a closed office—seemed to find their company less tedious than solitude.

Beatrix sat awake by the dry basin long after the others slept. Pip was curled against their shoulder, breathing slow, one fist still knotted in their sleeve. Across the room, Odile slept sitting up with her sketchbook open on her knees, a half-drawn coastline bleeding into shadow. Dain had taken first watch out of habit, though there was nothing left to watch for. Tam had finally let go of the rope.

*A true name, freely given.*

Beatrix turned the words over the way they'd once turned unknown substances on the tongue—carefully, respectfully, cataloguing every note. Fear, yes. Bitterness at the back. But underneath, unmistakable, something that tasted almost like relief.

Because Beatrix had understood, somewhere between the ghost's fourth sentence and its last, what the ghost had been too courteous to say outright. Tasters were chosen from bloodlines once bound to the wards. Their whole life—the tasting, the dosing, the careful invisibility, the ledger of small magics—had been preparation they'd never consented to either. The bargain had shaped them before they were born, same as it had shaped everyone inside those walls. The difference was that Beatrix, alone of all of them, had spent five years practicing the exact skill this moment required: knowing precisely what a thing cost before putting it in your mouth.

And knowing, still, choosing to swallow.

They thought of the ledger, burned in a Vellamere alley to protect these same sleeping people. Every entry a person helped, gone in smoke—and yet not gone, because Pip had promised to remember them aloud, and had been doing so every night since, reciting charm-sellers and fevered grandmothers like a rosary until sleep took them. Memory spoken freely had already proven stronger than ink. Maybe that was all a name was, in the end. A memory the world agreed to keep.

Maybe that was all a country was, too.

The ghost stirred beside the basin. It had not truly slept—it was not clear it could—but it opened its unfinished eyes and regarded Beatrix with something that had warmed, very slightly, since morning.

"You understand," it said. Not a question.

"I'm a taster," Beatrix said. "Understanding the price is the job."

"Mm. And do you intend to pay it?"

Beatrix looked at the child asleep against their shoulder. At Odile's half-finished coastlines. At the door of the shrine, beyond which lay a country forgetting itself one gray field at a time.

"I intend to reach the Doorway first," Beatrix said. "And then I intend to decide. Freely. That's the point of it, isn't it? It only works if it's chosen. So I'm choosing to wait until I'm certain."

The ghost laughed—a sound like distant bells rung softly, thirteen times, and stopping. "A taster's answer. Weigh everything. Swallow nothing unexamined." It settled back against the stone. "For what it's worth, clerk to customer: the old powers will not offer again after this turn. Whatever you decide, decide before the moon fills."

"It won't take that long," Beatrix said. "It never does. Someone always ends up hungry."

The ghost tilted its head. "Meaning?"

Beatrix looked east, toward Vellamere, toward the palace where Regent Casimir sat behind walls that were graying at the edges, guarding a throne in a country that was learning how to stop existing. They thought of the dish they'd tasted in that throne room, the one that tasted like forgetting, and the way Casimir had watched them swallow it—hungry, hopeful, hollow.

"Meaning," Beatrix said, "that we're not the only ones who know what the Doorway costs. And I don't think the regent intends to let anyone else pay it first."

Far off, past the fields going gray, the bells of Vellamere began to ring—and Beatrix counted, heart slowing with dread, as they rang thirteen.
