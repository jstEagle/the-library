## 15. Rescue at the Wardenhouse

They took her boots first.

Maren noticed that, in the way she noticed everything about a body now—what it lacked, what it needed. Boots meant they expected her to stay. Chains at the wrists and ankles meant they feared her leaving. But no gag, and that was an insult, or a mistake, or both: a hedge-healer's voice was half her craft, and hers was still her own.

The cell in the Wardenhouse of Vellamere's Third Ring was a converted wine cellar, which told her something too. The regent's men had built this place in a hurry, out of what they had. Stone walls sweated cold. A slot in the door for bread and water. A single lantern burning behind iron mesh, its light the color of old tallow.

She sat on the straw pallet and did what healers do when there is nothing to heal: she inventoried herself. Ribs bruised where they'd shoved her against the gatepost. A split lip. Wrists chafed raw. Nothing broken. She'd bought her capture dearly enough—a quarter hour of slowing three wardens with cramps and vertigo while Beatrix and the others ran—and she'd do it again, though the arithmetic of it sat poorly in her chest. A quarter hour for a life spent in a cell. Fair trade, said the part of her that had always undervalued herself. Poor bargain, whispered another part, quieter, newer, the one that had learned to sit at a fire with people who passed her the salt without being asked.

*They'll come back for you,* that second part said.

*Don't be foolish,* said the first.

The door clanged. Two wardens entered, and behind them a third man she hadn't seen before: soft-bellied, ink-stained fingers, a clerk's chain of office around his neck. An interrogator who had never dirtied his hands, sent because Casimir's hunters had failed to bring back anything useful from the road and someone had to extract value from what they *had* brought back.

"Maren Ostwick," the clerk said, consulting a slate. "Unregistered practitioner. Hedge-craft. The charge is sedition by enchantment."

"I'm a healer," Maren said. "I set bones. I brew feverfew. The most seditious thing I've ever done is tell a lord his gout was caused by his own table."

The clerk smiled thinly. "Then this will be simple. You'll tell us where your companions are bound."

"The Salt Cathedral," she said, because lying badly was worse than silence, and because Odile would have already accounted for the fact that Maren might talk. The cartographer planned like water finding its level; she assumed every container would leak eventually. "We were going to the Doorway. You know that. Your wardens followed us from the pale fields."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—not surprise. Confirmation. He wrote on his slate. "And what did you find there?"

"An arch. Cold stone. Locked." She shrugged with her chained hands. "If you're hoping I'll describe how to open it, I can't. They didn't tell me. I'm the one who gets captured, remember? That's my whole role."

It was a small lie wrapped around a large truth, and the clerk swallowed it whole, because men like him believed that everyone in a company had a rank, and that the one taken prisoner must be the least of them. Let him believe it. Let him write it down. Every wrong line on his slate was a wall between Casimir's understanding and theirs.

He asked questions for an hour. Where had they camped, who kept watch, what supplies remained. She answered some, deflected others, lied about the rest with the flat calm of a woman describing weather. And through all of it, she watched—not the clerk, but the two wardens flanking the door.

The tall one had a headache. She could see it the way she could see fever: the tightness around his eyes, the slight favoring of one side of his jaw, the way he blinked too often in the lantern light. The short one had a bad tooth, lower left, and hadn't slept properly in days. Wardens worked long shifts since the stars went out. The city graying at its edges frightened them as much as it frightened anyone, and fear is exhausting, and exhaustion lives in the body long before it reaches the mouth.

Maren had been healing headaches since she was nine years old, crouched beside her grandmother's hearth, learning to pull pain out through the temples like drawing a splinter. It was small magic. Harmless magic. The kind the registry never bothered to list.

She began, very gently, to work.

Not cure—the tall warden's headache had a cause, tension knotted at the base of his skull, and curing it outright might make him suspicious of his own sudden comfort. No, she just... eased it. Loosened the knot a finger's width. Let warmth spread where the ache had been. The warden straightened almost imperceptibly. His shoulders dropped. His breathing deepened.

For the tooth, she dulled the nerve's complaint to a distant murmur. The short warden stopped pressing his tongue against it. He looked, for the first time since entering, like a man who might get through his shift.

Neither of them knew why they felt better. Neither of them would question it. People rarely questioned feeling good; that was the whole tragedy of the profession.

The clerk left unsatisfied. The wardens escorted her back to her pallet, and as the tall one locked the door, Maren said quietly, "You should drink more water. And sleep on your left side tonight."

He stared at her. Then, slowly, he nodded, and was gone.

* * *

Three days. Or four—the cellar gave her no windows, only the rhythm of bread through the slot and the changing of boots outside the door, so she counted meals and called them days.

On the third meal-day, the tall warden brought her bread and, instead of setting it down and leaving, lingered. "Ostwick," he said. "My mother has the same affliction you mentioned. In her temples. Twenty years of it."

"There are exercises," Maren said. "I could show you, if these chains were off."

He didn't take the chains off. But he came back the next day, and the day after, and on the fourth day he sat on the floor across from her cell bars with his helmet off, a big tired man named Corren, and let her talk him through the pressure points while the short warden—Bett, who had a daughter in the Second Ring—stood watch and pretended not to listen.

This was the thing about healing that the registry never understood, the thing that made hedge-craft dangerous in ways fireballs were not: nobody guards against kindness. A warden would die before opening his post to a witch's hex. He would open his heart to a witch's *help* without ever noticing the door swing wide.

By the fifth day, Corren had told her he didn't sign up for this. By the sixth, Bett had mentioned that the Wardenhouse garrison was thin—half the men pulled to the palace for the regent's preparations, whatever those were, because the moon hung fat and wrong in the sky and everyone in Vellamere could feel the country holding its breath.

By the seventh day, Maren had mapped the entire building through conversation the way Odile mapped coastlines: the armory off the east corridor, the records room above, the kitchen with its single well, the garrison sleeping quarters, the rotation of watches. She knew which wardens had headaches and which had fevers brewing and which had hearts soft enough to look away at the right moment.

She also knew, with a healer's certainty, that it wouldn't last. Sooner or later the clerk would return with sharper tools than questions. She'd seen the new orders pinned to the records-room door when Corren carried a file past: *Subject to be transferred to the Palace Cells upon completion of preliminary interrogation.*

Preliminary. As if everything until now had been an appetizer.

That night she lay on the pallet and let herself want, openly, for the first time in years. Not freedom in the abstract. Specific things: Odile's dry voice correcting her grammar. Dain's terrible whittling. Pip asleep against her shoulder on the road, trusting her completely, the weight of the child like an anchor dropped into the dark water of her chest. Beatrix's careful hands measuring herbs, always measuring, as if generosity required exact portions.

*I am worth rescuing,* she practiced silently, the way one practices a difficult incantation. *I am worth rescuing.*

It still didn't quite take. But she was getting closer.

* * *

They came on the eighth night, during the change of watch, because of course they did—Beatrix would have counted the guard rotations from a rooftop the way Maren counted pulses.

She woke to the sound of the outer door opening without its usual groan, and thought at first it was a dream, until she smelled it: a faint bitterness threading through the cellar's damp, familiar as her own name. Valerian. Something else beneath it—she catalogued it automatically, the taster's craft rubbing off on her after weeks on the road—poppy, maybe, and a third thing she couldn't name, something that made her tongue feel briefly numb though she'd tasted nothing.

Footsteps above. Soft. Many. Then a thud, gentle as a sack of flour set down by someone considerate. Another. A third.

The lantern behind its mesh flickered as a shadow crossed it, and then Beatrix's face appeared at the cell door, framed in the slot's worth of lamplight, looking exactly as unremarkable as ever—which was, Maren had learned, the most remarkable thing about them.

"You're late," Maren whispered.

"We stopped to buy pastries," Beatrix whispered back, working a pick into the lock. Their hands were steady. Their eyes weren't. "There was a queue."

The lock clicked. The door swung, and Maren stood on legs gone stiff and stupid, and Odile caught her elbow before she could stumble, and that—that small steadying hand—undid her more thoroughly than any interrogation could have.

"How many?" Odile murmured, all business, but her grip said other things.

"Fourteen in the garrison, six on duty tonight. Four are asleep in the east quarters. Two at the main door. Corren and Bett are walking the corridor—" Maren paused. "Corren won't stop you. I need you to trust me on that."

Beatrix's eyebrows rose. "You made friends with our jailers."

"I treated their headaches. It's what I do." She flexed her chained hands. "Also, I know where the keys are. Records room, hook by the door, third ring. And there are transfer orders with my name on them—I'm due at the palace cells within days. We should assume the clerk reported everything I told him."

"He did," Odile said grimly. "We found his notes. You told them we were going to the Salt Cathedral."

"I told them the truth. Badly, on purpose."

Odile's mouth twitched—the closest thing to laughter she permitted underground. "Good. Because we're not going to the Salt Cathedral anymore. Pip had a dream."

"A dream."

"We'll explain on the move. Dain's watching the well-house. Pip's with him." Odile pressed a bundle into Maren's arms: her boots, her herb-satchel, her coat with the mended elbow. Someone had packed them carefully. Someone had remembered the mended elbow.

Maren sat down right there on the cold floor and put her boots on, and her hands shook, and she let them shake, because there was no one in this cellar she needed to be strong for. That was the discovery, arriving not with trumpets but with laces: strength shared was strength doubled. She had spent thirty years being the one who held others together. These people had simply picked her up and carried her, pieces and all, without ever making her feel like a burden.

"Who disabled the guards?" she asked, standing, testing her ankles. "The ones upstairs. I heard them fall."

"No one fell," Beatrix said. "They're sleeping. Deeply, comfortably, and they'll wake with mild headaches and no memory of the last hour." A pause. "I dosed the evening stew in the garrison kitchen. Taster's trick—you don't poison the dish, you poison the one spoonful nobody watches. The cook's portion stays clean, so the cook tastes nothing wrong, and the shift change eats before the watch does." They shrugged. "Nobody dies. Nobody even bruises. Killing fourteen men who are mostly tired boys from the Second Ring seemed like poor accounting."

Maren looked at Beatrix—this careful, invisible person who had spent five years making themselves harmless, and who had just walked into a wardenhouse and emptied it like a nurse emptying a bedpan, with the same brisk mercy—and felt something in her chest rearrange itself.

"You could have killed them," she said. "Easily."

"Yes," Beatrix said simply. "That's rather the point of knowing poisons. Knowing when not to use them is the whole profession." They glanced toward the stairs. "Come on. Corren's due to round the corner in ninety seconds, and I'd rather he found an empty cell than a fight."

They went up through the cellar's false quiet, Odile leading, Beatrix drifting behind Maren like a shadow that had decided to be a person. At the top of the stairs, the corridor stretched dim and empty, four sleeping shapes visible through the barracks doorway, breath rising and falling in drugged peace.

And there, at the far end, stood Corren—helmet under his arm, lantern raised, staring at the open cell door below and then at the three figures on the stairs.

Maren stepped forward, putting herself between.

For a long moment, nobody moved. The lantern flame bent in a draft. Somewhere above, the fat wrong moon pushed its light through a high window, gray as ash.

Then Corren set his helmet down on the floor. Deliberately. Loudly. And turned his back, and began to walk away down the corridor, unhurried, a man with rounds to complete and nothing whatsoever to report.

"Third ring, hook by the door," he said over his shoulder, without turning. "Take the north well-road. The palace gates double their watch after midnight bell." A pause. "And Ostwick—the exercises work. My mother slept through the night for the first time in years."

Then he was gone around the corner, and Maren stood with tears she refused to shed running hot behind her eyes, and Odile squeezed her arm once, hard, and said, "Well. Now we run."

They ran—out the well-door, past Dain's silhouette waving them on, into the gray streets of a city fading at its edges, Pip's small hand finding Maren's in the dark without either of them looking, as if the child had known exactly where she'd be.

They were three streets clear when Pip stopped dead, tugging Maren's sleeve, pointing up.

Above the rooftops, where the moon hung swollen and impossible in its thirteenth turn, the stars were beginning—not to return, but to *stir*. Faint lights moving in patterns no astronomer had charted, arranging themselves, waiting.

"Pip?" Maren breathed. "What are they doing?"

Pip's face, tilted to the sky, was lit with a wonder that hurt to look at.

"They're learning our names," Pip said.
