## 16. What the Stars Remembered

Pip dreamed of the sky the way other people dreamed of their mothers.

Not a picture of it — the *thing itself*, vast and turning, close enough that Pip could have reached up and hooked a finger through the Hunter's Belt. In the dream there was no moon at all, or if there was one it was small and shy and off doing its own business somewhere near the horizon, because this was the old sky, the true sky, the one from before, and in the old sky the stars did not need a moon to keep them company. There were thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They came in rivers. The whole dome of the night was braided with light, and Pip lay on a hillside of dry sweet grass — grass that smelled like something, everything smelled like something in the dream — and named them all.

The Ladle. The Shepherd's Crook. The Nine Sisters walking single file across the dark, the littlest one trailing behind because she always stopped to look at things. The Wagon with its broken wheel. The Kettle pouring steam. The Long Fish swimming up the river of stars toward the place where the sky met the sea, which was also a river, which was also the same thing if you looked at it long enough.

Pip knew these names the way Pip knew their own hands. Not learned. *Remembered.* That was the strange part, the part that made the dream feel less like a dream and more like a door left open somewhere: Pip had never seen this sky. Pip was nine years old, and nine years ago the stars over Caelmere had already been the wrong stars, the thin stars, the handful the regent's astronomers said had always been there. But the dream-sky didn't care what Pip had seen. It poured itself into Pip anyway, generous as spilled milk, and said: *you hold this now. You've been holding it. Say it back.*

So Pip said it back. All night, in the dream, Pip walked the constellations like a child reciting a lesson for a beloved teacher, and the stars leaned down to listen, and somewhere very far away — so far it was almost a feeling instead of a sound — something enormous and patient turned its attention toward a small sleeping figure wrapped in a gray blanket under a leaning cart in the pale fields of a country that was being forgotten.

* * *

Pip woke before dawn with the names still hot in their mouth.

That was the first odd thing. Dreams usually went out of Pip like water through fingers; by breakfast they were down to a smear, a color, a feeling of running. But this one stayed whole. Pip lay still under the blanket and said the names over silently, testing each one like stepping stones, and every stone held. The Ladle held. The Shepherd's Crook held. The Nine Sisters held, and the littlest sister — Pip could see her exactly, a faint gold star just south of the Wagon's broken wheel, so faint you'd miss her unless you knew to look — the littlest sister held best of all.

The second odd thing was the taste. Everything in the pale fields tasted like nothing — Beatrix had said so, making a face at a gray turnip two nights ago, and Maren had said the healing felt like pushing rope, and Odile had said the ink came out of her pen dead. But when Pip sat up and breathed the cold morning air, Pip could taste the fields. Faintly. Like a soup made from the memory of soup. Like someone had described bread to the wind and the wind had tried its best.

Pip scrambled out from under the cart, bare feet in frost-gray grass, and looked east.

There. Low on the horizon, where the sky went from charcoal to ash to the bruised yellow of coming morning, stood a hill. It wasn't much of a hill. Round-shouldered, lone, with something on top of it that might have been a standing stone or might have been a trick of the light. And rising out of it, straight up into the fading stars, was a thread of darkness — not smoke, nothing moved, but a line where the dawn light didn't quite land, the way a crack in a wall doesn't quite catch the lamplight.

Pip knew that hill.

Pip had never seen that hill.

Both of those things were true at once, and Pip was used to both-of-those-things-at-once, because that was what remembering felt like now. It wasn't like knowing. It was like being known *by* something.

"Pip." Beatrix's voice, low and warm, from the banked fire. "You're up early. Cold out here — come sit."

"I dreamed the sky," Pip said.

Beatrix went still. Pip had learned to read Beatrix the way Pip read weather: the taster's face gave away almost nothing on purpose, but the pauses were loud. This pause was very loud indeed.

"Tell me," Beatrix said.

* * *

They gathered around the fire while the light came up gray, all six of them, and Pip told it standing up because sitting felt wrong, because this was important and important things deserved a person on their feet. Dain kept glancing at the tree line out of habit, but even he stopped checking the shadows after a while. Even he listened.

"The Ladle," Pip said, pointing at the empty eastern sky where the Ladle would be, if stars remembered themselves. "It pours into the Long Fish. The Fish swims up the Sky-River. And past the Fish — you have to go past the Fish, past where the sky meets the water — there's a hill. With a stone on it. And a crack in the air above it that doesn't shine."

"A crack that doesn't shine," Odile repeated. Her pen was already out. She'd been sketching since the second sentence, fast, messy, the way she sketched when her hands believed her ears. "Pip. Look at me. Has anyone told you about sigil-sites? About the Doorway, about—"

"No. But I know where another one is." Pip pointed again, arm steady, finger finding the hill without hunting for it. "There. Two days' walk, maybe three. Northeast. Past the Fish."

"Past the—" Odile stopped writing. She stared at the horizon, then at her half-finished map, then at Pip, and Pip watched something move across her face like weather. Hope, maybe. Or fear wearing hope's coat. "I have surveyed this region. Twice, before my license was stripped. There is no marked hill northeast of here. There is no *hill* northeast of here — it's floodplain, I mapped it myself, flat as a griddle for twenty miles—"

"Your maps forgot," Pip said simply. "I didn't."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere behind them a gray bird made a sound like a hinge.

It was Maren who broke the silence, and she did it the way she did everything, gently, kneeling so she was smaller than Pip even though she wasn't. "Sweetling. When you say you know the hill — do you mean you've heard stories? Seen a drawing? Sometimes dreams stitch together things we've—"

"No." Pip shook their head. "It's not a story-dream. It's a *remembering*-dream. Like when you smell bread and you're suddenly in your grandmother's kitchen even though your grandmother died before you were born." Pip hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out anyway, the way true things did lately, and Maren's hands went still in her lap. "The sky taught me. All night. It said, *say it back.* So I'm saying it back."

"The sky taught you," Dain said flatly. He wasn't mocking. That was the strange new thing about Dain since his confession — he treated impossible statements like reports from scouts, weighing them for truth. "And the sky wants us to walk northeast."

"The sky wants us to *remember*," Pip said. "Walking northeast is just — it's how remembering looks from out here."

Beatrix laughed. Just once, short and startled, like a hiccup of joy escaping custody. Everyone looked at them. Beatrix pressed their knuckles to their mouth, and when they took the hand away their eyes were bright. "Forgive me. It's only — I spent five years selling charms to people who swore they didn't believe in magic. Tiny things. A luck-knot. A wart-charm. And the whole time, the counter-magic to the end of the world was going to be a child who paid attention to the sky." They shook their head. "Memory. Of course it's memory. The wards were built on oaths, oaths are spoken memories, the erasure eats records and names and *maps*—" they nodded at Odile, who flinched and then, visibly, decided not to mind, "—but it can't eat what's carried in a person. It's been trying. The gray is creeping in everywhere else. But it can't get into heads that refuse."

"It got into mine," Odile said quietly. "My borders went blank."

"Your *ink* went blank," Beatrix said. "You didn't. You came looking for a charm-seller off a rumor and a hunch, in the middle of the worst night this country has ever had. That's not forgetting, Odile. That's a memory with teeth."

Odile looked down at her map — the new one, the one she'd been building since the Salt Cathedral, drawn from testimony instead of survey, every road and river argued into existence by people who refused to let go of them. Then she looked at Pip. Then she turned to a fresh page and wrote, in careful letters, at the top: *What Pip Remembers.*

"All right," she said. "Dictate. Everything. The constellations first, then the hill, then anything else the sky gave you. Leave nothing out. Small details especially — the small details are what make a memory too heavy to steal."

So Pip dictated. The Nine Sisters and the littlest one who stopped to look at things. The Wagon's broken wheel and how it got broken (a star fell out during the war against the dark, and the wagon never stopped moving, and if you were very good the fallen star would someday catch up). The Kettle. The Shepherd's Crook with the lost lamb hanging off the hook end. The Ladle, the Long Fish, the Sky-River, the seam where sky became sea. Pip talked until their voice went hoarse and Maren made them sit and drink something warm that tasted, faintly, wonderfully, like chamomile that remembered being chamomile.

And the others worked while Pip talked. That was the marvel of it, the thing Pip would remember later, in the time after: watching the company bend around the dictation like a fire bending around new wood. Odile drew the constellations freehand, no instruments, her pen flying. Beatrix packed the camp with one ear cocked, asking questions — *how high was the stone, was the crack wide as a door or narrow as a knife, was there a path or did the grass grow right up to it.* Dain studied the northeast horizon and started, unasked, to plan the route aloud, counting streams and hedgerows from patrols he'd once walked. Maren refilled cups and listened with her eyes shut, mouthing the star-names to herself like prayers, and Pip understood that for her they were prayers, or near enough.

By full morning they had it: a page of stars that existed nowhere else in the world, and a route to a hill that officially did not exist, and a theory that had started as a child's dream and hardened, over the course of an hour, into the closest thing any of them had to a plan.

"Two sigil-sites," Odile said, tapping the Salt Cathedral on her map and then the blank space northeast where the hill should be. "The Doorway below the cathedral is locked and cold. If the sigil-work is split between sites — anchor points, a lattice — then re-inscribing one without the other is like mending half a net. We couldn't have done it. We *can't* do it yet. But if the second site holds the other half—"

"Then we need both," Beatrix finished. "And we need to speak our memories at both. Every charm sold. Every meal cooked. Every star." They looked at Pip, and their expression did the complicated thing it did sometimes, pride and grief sharing a chair. "Every star."

"Me too?" Pip asked. "At the stone?"

"You most of all." Beatrix crouched to Pip's height, the way Maren did, and Pip noticed that everyone was starting to do that. "The erasure took the records, the maps, the names, the wards. It's taking the streets of Vellamere as we stand here. But it hasn't managed to take the sky out of one nine-year-old's head. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Small?" Pip offered.

"Essential," Beatrix said. "The smallest and the most essential. That's usually how it works."

* * *

They walked northeast all day, and Pip led.

Not alone — Dain ranged ahead, and Beatrix kept a hand near Pip's shoulder whenever the ground roughened, and Maren called out roots and ruts from behind like a mother hen with a soldier's vocabulary. But when the track forked, they looked to Pip. When the land flattened into gray floodplain exactly as Odile's dead maps promised, and there was no hill anywhere in sight, and Dain said carefully, "Pip, are you certain of the bearing," they looked to Pip.

And Pip closed their eyes, found the littlest Sister in the theater behind their eyelids, followed her gaze south of the Wagon's broken wheel, and opened their eyes and pointed. "That way. She stops to look at things, and the hill is what she's looking at."

Dain looked. For a long moment there was nothing but gray grass and gray sky. Then he said, very quietly, "There's a haze on the horizon. Could be distance. Could be—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They all saw it now: a smudge of not-quite-light where the plain should have run clean to the sky's edge, a place where the world seemed slightly less finished than the world around it.

They made camp within sight of it, close enough that the smudge filled half the northern sky, far enough that nobody suggested getting nearer in the dark. The fire was small. The mood was not grim, though — that was the miracle Pip kept turning over like a smooth stone. After weeks of dread, of deadlines written in the moon, of Maren's empty chair at supper, the company sat around the flames and something in them had loosened. They had a direction that came from inside the country instead of outside it. They had a counter-magic they could carry in their own skulls, unstealable, unerased. They had a star-speaker, and the star-speaker had fed them stew.

"Tell us one," Odile said, when the plates were scraped. She was sharpening her pencil with a knife, not because it needed it but because her hands thought better that way. "A star-story. The ones that go with the pictures. You must know some."

Pip did know some. They came up easily, warm from the same deep place as the shapes: why the Wagon never stopped (it was carrying the harvest-souls home, and if it stopped, the year would stop with it); why the Littlest Sister lagged (she'd promised the dark she'd look at it, really look, because even the dark wanted to be seen once); how the Ladle was stolen from a giant's kitchen by a cook who was tired of everyone going hungry, and the giant chased her into the sky, and she tipped the Ladle out behind her as she ran, and everything that spilled became the Milky River, and that is why the river tastes of milk to this day, if you are a star.

The company listened the way people listen to fires. And when Pip finished, Beatrix said, softly, mostly to the flames:

"That's how we'll do it. At the stone. Stories, not just lists. A list is a record — the erasure eats records. But a story is a debt. You can't erase a debt; it just transfers." They looked around the circle, at each face in turn. "We don't recite at that hill. We *tell*. Everything we love, everything we've done, everything this country was — told properly, with beginnings and ends, so it lodges so deep in the world it can't be pried out."

"And the moon?" Dain asked. Always the moon, with Dain. He tracked it obsessively now, measuring its impossible swelling phase against his thumbnail each night like a man counting coins he owed. "How many nights left?"

"Four," Odile said. "Perhaps five."

"Then tomorrow we reach the hill," Pip said.

Everyone looked at Pip again. Pip shrugged, hugging their knees. "The Littlest Sister told me. She says the hill has been waiting a long time, and it's lonely, and lonely stones remember best." Pip hesitated, then added the rest, because the others had earned it and because saying it out loud made it more real, more heavy, harder to steal: "She says there's a door in the stone. And behind the door is the other half of the burning sigil. And it isn't cold like the cathedral one." Pip swallowed. "She says it's *warm*. It's been warm this whole time. It's been waiting for somebody to come and be remembered at."

The fire cracked. The smudge on the northern horizon seemed, very slightly, brighter than it had been at dusk — or perhaps that was only the eyes adjusting, or hope, or a star that had fallen out of a broken wheel finally catching up.

"Four days," Beatrix murmured, gazing north at the not-quite-light. "A warm door, a lonely stone, and four days." They rose, brushing off their knees, and Pip saw the taster's face set into the expression Pip had come to think of as the cooking-face — the one Beatrix wore when a dish was nearly ready and only the dangerous part remained. "Then we sleep well tonight. Tomorrow we walk fast. And when we reach that stone—"

"—we tell it everything," Pip said.

Above them, through the thinning gray, one star — faint, gold, stubborn, just south of where the Wagon's broken wheel ought to be — shone a little harder, as if it had stopped to look at them, and liked what it saw.
