## 17. Return to Vellamere

The city smelled wrong before we could see it.

We came down off the ridge at dusk, six of us now—five, I kept correcting myself, and the correction sat in my chest like a swallowed stone—and the wind off the pale fields carried something that should not have been there. Nothing. The smell of nothing. Bread without yeast, water without mineral, air scrubbed of every living note until it was just cold and distance.

"That's Vellamere," Dain said, and his voice had the flatness he got when he was trying not to feel something.

Below us, the capital lay in its bowl like a coin at the bottom of a well. From this far you couldn't see what was happening to it. You saw the walls, the bell towers, the smoke—or what used to be smoke, because even the chimney-smoke had gone thin and gray, rising in threads that dissolved too quickly, as if the sky were refusing to hold onto it.

The moon hung over the eastern wall, swollen and wrong. Eleven nights left on the thirteenth turn. Odile had done the arithmetic with a charred stick on the back of her last surviving map, and none of us had asked her to do it twice.

"We go in through the tanners' gate," I said. "It's warded lightest. Half the wardens there owe me favors, and the other half owe me headaches."

Maren would have laughed at that. Maren was in a cell beneath the Wardenhouse, or she had been three days ago, and every hour since, Pip had been walking with his chin up and his jaw set like a small soldier, refusing to cry where anyone could see. He carried her satchel. Nobody had offered to carry it for him. Some things you don't offer.

Odile spread the map across a flat stone. "The streets near the river are already—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way she did when precision hurt. "The outer ring is fading. Not damaged. *Fading.* The tanners' quarter went gray two days ago according to the refugees on the road. If it's spreading inward—"

"Then the kitchen's still there," I said. "The palace kitchens sit at the center of everything. Casimir would keep himself whole longest."

"Is that why we're going?" Pip asked. "For the regent?"

I looked at him—at the soot still smudged along his jaw from the wayshrine fire, at the constellation drawings he kept folded against his heart like a second set of ribs—and I told him the truth, because he'd earned truth from everyone by now.

"No," I said. "I'm going for a bread basket."

* * *

The tanners' gate stood open.

That was the first horror. In five years I had never seen that gate unbarred after sundown; the tannery stink was considered a defense all its own, and the wardens treated the quarter like a lung they'd rather the city didn't breathe through. Now the gate yawned, its iron bars spotted with rust that hadn't been there a season ago, and no one stood in the archway except a single warden who was staring at his own hands.

Dain moved to handle him. I put a hand on his arm.

"Wait."

The warden wasn't asleep. He was counting his fingers. Slowly, one to ten and back again, mouthing the numbers, and when he reached ten he frowned as though the answer had disappointed him, and began again. His spear leaned against the wall, forgotten. His uniform was gray at the cuffs—not dirty gray. *Fading* gray, the dye going out of the cloth the way color goes out of a face.

"He's not guarding anything," I whispered. "He's trying to remember how many fingers a man has."

We slipped past him. Our boots made no echo. That was the second horror: Vellamere had always been a city of echoes, stone answering stone, every footstep doubled back at you by alleys that had heard a thousand years of footsteps. Now the stones took our sound and gave nothing back. We walked through our own city like ghosts walking through a portrait of it.

The tanners' quarter was worse.

I had sold charms on these streets for five years. I knew which doorposts had my marks scratched into them—a little spiral, a promise of luck, three coppers' worth of hope. I knew Widow Sarelle's shutters and the crooked step outside the cooper's yard and the exact spot where the gutter overflowed and froze into a small silver waterfall every winter, which children dared each other to lick.

The waterfall was gone. Not frozen-over. *Gone*, along with the gutter, along with half the street it ran down. Where the lane should have bent toward Sarelle's door, there was simply less city—a softness at the edge of vision, a place your eye slid off of, the way you can't look directly at the inside of your own eyelid. Beyond it, where the tanning yards had been, was a pale flatness like fog that had decided to be ground.

Pip grabbed my sleeve. "Beatrix. The spiral. My doorpost—"

"I know." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Hold onto me. All of you. If the edges take anything else, I want to feel it try."

Odile was sketching as we walked—fast, furious strokes, her pencil scratching the only sound in the whole quarter. "If I draw them," she said, half to herself, "they hold a little longer. Look." She'd drawn the cooper's yard an hour before, and where the actual yard had gone misty, her drawing remained crisp, stubborn, *insistent*. "Memory has weight here. Memory is the only thing with weight."

"Then the city's being eaten from the outside in," Dain said, "because nobody remembers the edges. Everyone remembers the palace. The cathedral. Their own street, maybe. But who remembers a tannery?"

"Widow Sarelle remembered her gutter," I said quietly. "She complained about it every market day for thirty years."

And Sarelle's house was the next thing to go.

We watched it happen. I will carry that watching for whatever remains of me to carry things. One moment her shutters were there, brown wood, peeling paint, the window box where she grew thyme she never used. Then a grayness moved through it—not fast, not violent, just *thorough*, like water soaking through cloth—and the shutters became suggestion, and the suggestion became nothing, and where her home had stood there was a smooth pale blankness that our eyes refused to land on.

Pip made a sound I never want to hear a child make again.

"It doesn't hurt them," Odile said quickly, gripping his shoulder. "Pip. Listen to me. The people are gone ahead of the houses—they evacuated, they're on the roads, they're safe. It's only the *remembering* that's failing. A house isn't a person."

But I was thinking about Sarelle's thyme. About whether anyone on any road out of this country was carrying the memory of that unused window box, or whether it had simply ended, unwitnessed, when the last person who knew it stopped knowing.

Five years I had lived small and useful in this city, telling myself that invisibility was safety, that the ledger of my small magics was enough—that changing one life at a time, quietly, secretly, was a kind of heroism available to people like me. And now the city itself was teaching me the lesson underneath the lesson: that nothing survives unwitnessed. That a hidden life is a life half-lived, and the other half can be erased.

"Come on," I said. "The center holds longest. Let's see how long the center holds."

* * *

We crossed the middle city as the moon rose fully clear of the walls, and I understood then why the old astronomers had called the thirteenth turn a *turning*. The moon didn't move like a moon anymore. It hung and it *rotated*, slowly, showing us faces it had never shown us, and each new face was a shade paler than the last, like a coin being rubbed away between enormous patient thumbs.

The streets near the palace were still themselves. Mostly. The Fountain of the Nine Daughters still trickled, though only seven daughters remained carved above the basin, and when I pointed this out, Odile checked her old survey maps and found that there had only ever been seven, and then she sat down on the fountain rim and put her head in her hands for a long moment, because if the erasure could reach backward into records and memory alike, then nothing any of us trusted about the past was safe either.

"The kitchen," Pip said gently, when she could stand again. "You said bread basket."

The palace service gate was where I had entered every morning for five years, and the guard there—Brammel, a heavy, kind-eyed man who pretended not to notice the charms I slipped into the scullery maids' apron pockets—was not at his post. The gate was barred but the bar was old and I knew its trick, and we went through the way I'd gone through ten thousand mornings, except this time my hands shook.

The kitchens were lit.

That undid me more than the empty streets. After all that grayness, the great kitchen blazed with ordinary firelight—roasting fires banked low, lamps turned down but burning, copper pots hanging in their rows, catching the light in warm crescents. And the smell. After the nothing-smell of the dead quarters, the kitchen smelled like *life*: stock and onion and scorched flour and the ghost of a thousand breakfasts soaked into the beams overhead.

Three cooks remained. Old Tolla, who had taught me the regent's palate; a boy named Fen I'd trained myself; and the pastry mistress, whose name I realized with a lurch I had never learned, because pastry had its own kingdom and I had never needed to cross the border.

They looked up when we came in, and Tolla's face did something complicated—relief and fear and reproach all at once.

"You're alive," she said. "Half the staff walked out when the stars died. Half the rest walked out when the moon started doing *that*." She jerked her chin at the high windows, where the pale disc crawled past. "Casimir eats alone now. Sends the tray back untouched most nights. Says he can't taste anything anyway."

My stomach dropped. *Can't taste anything.* A man who had traded his name for power, hollowed out decade by decade—I hadn't let myself think it through, what the bargain must have cost him at every meal, every morning, every touch of fabric or warmth of sun. The regent of Caelmere had been living in the same erasure we were walking through, only slower, and from the inside.

"I need to bake," I said.

Tolla blinked. "You break into your own kitchen to *bake*?"

"I need to bake one last time in this room, with this flour, in front of witnesses. And then I need you to remember it. All of you. Forever, if forever lasts." I was already pulling my apron from its hook—my apron, still there, still smelling faintly of the harmless poisons I dosed myself with daily, bitter greens and milk-thistle—and tying it on felt like putting on armor that had always secretly been a skin. "Because tomorrow night we go under the Salt Cathedral, and I don't know what comes out the other side of that. But tonight there's bread."

Fen started the ovens without being asked. He'd learned that much from me, at least: when someone ties on an apron in this kitchen, you feed the fire.

* * *

I baked the way I hadn't baked in years—not for disguise, not for dosing, not for the regent's suspicious palate, but the way my grandmother taught me before I understood that grandmothers could be erased. A simple loaf. Flour, water, salt, the wild yeast Tolla kept in a crock by the window like a pet. I let my hands do the remembering while my mind wandered the dangerous country of *last times*.

Last time kneading at this bench, where the wood had worn a shallow dip exactly the shape of my hip. Last time hearing Fen swear at the oven dampers. Last time the particular squeak of the third floorboard by the pantry, which I had used for five years as a warning signal and which no one else had ever known was a language.

"This dough needs to rest," I told Pip, who had climbed onto a stool to watch, flour dusting his nose. "Not because the recipe says so. Because the gluten needs time to relax, and because rushing bread is how you get a loaf that looks fine and eats like punishment. Everything true takes resting."

"Is that about bread or about us?"

"Yes," I said, and he grinned, and I thought: *there*. Whatever happens under the cathedral, that grin exists now in four other heads besides mine. Witnessed. Weighted. Harder to erase.

While the loaves proved, I did the thing I had actually come to do.

The bread baskets stood in their rack by the servery door—the ones that went up to the regent's table, and to the ministers' tables, and out to the households of everyone who mattered in what remained of Caelmere's government. For five years I had slipped charms into those baskets. Tiny things. A knot of thread for courage. A pinch of salt charmed for honest sleep. A button that would grow warm in the presence of a lie. Small magics for small protections, sold and smuggled and given free to those who couldn't pay, all of it logged in a ledger I had burned in a farmhouse two weeks ago to keep it from Casimir's hunters, and regretted burning every day since, because the ledger hadn't been a record of charms. It had been a record of *people*. Every entry a neighbor. Every line a hand I had held without holding it.

Tonight I filled the baskets one last time—but differently. No hiding. I walked through the kitchen and told Tolla, and Fen, and the pastry mistress whose name I finally asked (Ondine; her laugh at my five years of ignorance was the first real laugh any of us had had in days), exactly what I was doing and why.

"Tomorrow night," I said, "either Caelmere becomes something new, or it becomes nothing. If it becomes nothing, then these baskets are the last magic this kitchen ever makes, and I want it to be honest magic. Out loud. Witnessed. Given, not smuggled."

Tolla took a charm of safe passage from my palm and closed my fingers back around it instead. "Keep it," she said. "You'll want it more than the ministers. They never believed in anything anyway—that's why the country's in this state, if you ask me. Too many people being useful and unremarkable." Her eyes were sharp and old and kinder than she'd ever let them be on duty. "Including you, taster. Especially you."

The loaves came out golden. We ate them standing up around the big table, six of us plus three cooks, tearing the crusts while they were still too hot, and it was the best thing I have ever tasted, and I am including in that count every dish I ever evaluated for a regent. Because taste, I realized, mouth full, eyes stinging, was the sense that erasure couldn't fake. The gray fields had been tasteless. The dead streets had been scentless. But this bread, in this kitchen, on this impossible night, was *real* all the way through, and everyone tasting it was tasting the same reality together, and that—more than any sigil, more than any ward—was the thing Casimir had bargained away without understanding its price.

A country is a flavor shared.

"Beatrix." Odile touched my arm. She was looking at the high window, and her face had gone the color of the moon. "Come and see."

I wiped my hands and followed her to the courtyard, and the others trailed after, and we stood in the cold among the herb beds I had stolen cuttings from for years, and looked up.

The moon had stopped turning. It hung full and white and utterly round, directly over the palace spire, and across its face—for the first time since the stars went out—we could see markings. Not craters. *Letters.* Sigils, vast and slow-burning, written across the moon's skin in script older than any of Odile's maps, older than the Doorway, older perhaps than the bargain itself. And they were moving. Rearranging. As if something on the other side of the sky were editing a document.

"It's a countdown," Odile whispered. "Or a contract. Or—" She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "Beatrix, the sigils. The ones on the arch under the cathedral. They match."

Of course they matched. The Doorway below the Salt Cathedral had always been a copy of something larger. And tomorrow night, when the turn completed, the original finished writing.

Behind us, in the warm lit doorway of the kitchen, Pip stood with a plate held out—one last slice of bread, saved for me, butter melting into it—and he didn't call out, didn't hurry me. He just waited, patient as a star, holding the ordinary world in his two hands.

I took the bread. I ate it slowly, watching the sky write its final lines.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we go down."

And somewhere beneath our feet, deep under salt and stone, I felt—taster's tongue, taster's blood, blood bound to the wards before I ever knew what wards were—the Doorway begin, very softly, to get hungry.
