## 18. The Regent's Bargain

The palace had gone gray at the seams.

Beatrix noticed it first in the marble. The great stair that rose from the entrance hall to the throne room had always been veined with gold—thin rivers of it, catching lamplight, running like honey through white stone. Now the gold was dull. Not tarnished. *Faded*, the way a word fades from a page left too long in the sun, and Beatrix's taster's tongue, which could find poison in a drop of wine or rot beneath the sweetness of a preserved plum, kept snagging on something else: the air itself tasted thin. Like broth made three times over from the same bones.

They climbed anyway. Dain walked behind them, armor stripped of insignia, sword wrapped in sacking. Odile had wanted to come; Beatrix had argued her out of it. If this went wrong—if Casimir decided the unregistered were better erased than answered—someone had to carry the map, and Pip, and Maren still limping from the wardenhouse, out through streets that were forgetting their own cobblestones. So it would be Beatrix alone who walked into the throne room. It felt fitting. They had spent five years walking into rooms alone. Tasting alone. Swallowing alone. Being useful and unremarkable, which was its own kind of armor, though tonight the armor felt thinner than ever.

The doors at the top of the stair stood open. That should have been the first warning. In five years of service, Beatrix had never once seen those doors open before they reached them. There was always a chamberlain, always a wait, always the ritual of being announced as though announcing were necessary for someone whose whole art was going unnoticed.

Tonight there was no one. Just the dark mouth of the throne room, breathing cold air, and beyond it a single lamp burning on a table set where the throne should have been.

Not beside the throne. *In place of* it.

Beatrix stopped on the threshold. The throne itself—the high-backed seat of black oak and silver, the one Casimir had occupied every audience day for thirty years—was gone. Not moved. Gone. Where it had stood there was only pale floor, swept clean, and above it the vaulted ceiling with its painted sky, and the painted sky was wrong too. Beatrix remembered it from tasting days, remembered glancing up between courses: constellations, gilded stars, the old sky of Caelmere rendered by some dead master's patient hand. Now half the gilding had lifted away like ash. What remained hung in patches, constellations amputated mid-figure, and Beatrix felt something twist in their chest because they knew those figures. Pip had drawn them. Pip had *remembered* them, when no one else in Vellamere could.

Casimir sat at the plain table. One chair. One lamp. A carafe of wine, two cups, and a covered dish, silver, domed, small enough to hold a single serving.

He looked smaller than Beatrix remembered. Five years of audiences, and Beatrix had always seen him at a distance, elevated, robed in midnight blue, framed by guards and ritual so that he seemed less a man than an institution wearing a man's face. Now he wore no robe of state. He wore gray wool, plain, belted, the kind of thing a clerk might wear, and his hair—which had been iron-gray and immaculate—hung loose and unwashed past his shoulders. His hands lay flat on the table like a man showing he held nothing.

"Thornbury," he said. "Sit."

There was only one other chair. Beatrix took it. Across the table, close enough now to see what five years of distance had hidden: the hollows under his eyes went all the way down to something. Not exhaustion. Exhaustion has color to it, a bruised purple, a living darkness. This was different. This was the flatness of a page where writing used to be.

"You've been busy," Casimir said. "My wardens report a taster, a cartographer, a deserter, a healer, a bell-ringer, and a child moving through my country toward my Doorway." He said *my* twice, and both times the word landed strangely, like a coin bitten and found soft. "They lost you at the wardenhouse. I'm told you poisoned forty men with a soup."

"Forty-one," Beatrix said. "One of them was already sick. I didn't want him feeling left out."

Casimir almost smiled. Almost. The motion began at the corner of his mouth and died somewhere in the middle, and Beatrix watched it die and understood, suddenly, that smiling was not something this man could do anymore. It wasn't policy. It wasn't coldness. It was that the muscles of it had been—*unwritten*. The way Odile's maps had gone blank at the borders. The way the stars had lifted off the ceiling above them even now.

"I didn't summon you here to punish you," he said. "I summoned you because you're the only person left in Caelmere who might understand what I'm about to tell you. You taste things. You taste what things truly are, beneath what they pretend to be. So taste this."

He lifted the dome off the dish. Beneath it sat a single pear, poached, glazed, arranged on a plate that had once been palace porcelain and was now—Beatrix could see it plainly—fading at the rim, the painted border dissolving into bare white clay.

Beatrix had tasted ten thousand dishes in this palace. They knew the regent's kitchen the way a sailor knows currents. They knew the poached pears came from the orchards south of the city, knew the glaze was honey and wine reduced with a blade of mace, knew the exact weight of the silver spoon the under-cooks served them on. This pear was none of those things. It was heavier than it should be. The glaze smelled right and tasted of nothing at all—not of badness, not of spoilage, but of *absence*, the same absence Beatrix had tasted in the gray fields outside the walls, the draining of everything toward—

Toward what? Toward the capital, they'd thought, on the road. Magic pooling in Vellamere like water finding low ground. But sitting here, tasting nothing where a pear should be, Beatrix understood the truth ran the other direction. Nothing was pooling anywhere. Everything was *leaving*. And the man across the table was the lowest ground of all.

"Eat," Casimir said. "Or don't. You'll taste it either way. That's your curse, isn't it? You can't not know."

Beatrix cut a slice with the spoon's edge and let it rest on their tongue without swallowing.

Nothing. Sweetness with the sweetness removed, like a photograph of sugar. And beneath the nothing, faint as a fingerprint in dust, something else—a flavor Beatrix had tasted exactly once before, years ago, in a dish that had been sent back to the kitchens half-eaten with a note in the regent's own hand: *Never serve this again.* They had assumed poison then. They had dosed themselves carefully, found nothing harmful, logged it in the ledger they would later burn, and moved on. But they had remembered the taste. Tasters remember. It was the one luxury of the trade: memory, hoarded against erasure.

It tasted like forgetting. Like the specific flavor of a name being taken out of a person.

Beatrix swallowed, because swallowing was the job, and because refusing would end the conversation, and they needed the conversation more than they needed safety. Then they set down the spoon and looked at Casimir and said the thing they had crossed a fading country to say:

"It was you. Thirty years ago. You opened the bargain."

Something shifted in the room. Not movement—pressure. The lamp flame leaned though there was no draft.

"The wards were failing," Casimir said quietly. "You wouldn't know this. No one knows this. The records were the first thing traded away—what good is a record of a bargain if the bargain requires forgetting? But I was young then. Not regent yet. Heir to a regency built on walls, and the walls were dying, and the mages came to me with terms." He poured wine into both cups but drank from neither; he only held his, turning it slowly, watching the surface. "Caelmere was being eaten. Not invaded—eaten. Something at the edges, something that unmade. The old wards were bought with blood, generations of it, and the price had come due, and we couldn't pay it anymore. So the mages offered new terms. A name instead of blood. The country's name, given freely, to seal the borders against the thing outside. And inside the sealed country, time would pass differently. Safely. Slowly. We would be a jar with a lid on it, and the lid would hold for as long as the name held."

"And the name held for thirty years," Beatrix said.

"The name held for twenty-nine years, eleven months, and thirteen days." He set the cup down. The number had the precision of a man who had counted every day of it, who had woken each morning and counted, the way a prisoner counts. "I know because I counted. Because I gave more than the country's name, Thornbury. That was the part the mages didn't ask for. That was the part I offered."

Beatrix leaned forward. "What did you offer?"

"My own." Casimir's voice didn't change. That was the frightening part. It stayed level, dry, administrative, a voice reading terms aloud, while underneath it the man was coming apart like wet paper. "The bargain required a freely given name to anchor it. The country's name was the seal. But a seal needs a hand to press it, and the hand must be bound to the seal, and so I gave mine. Casimir. My name. The one my mother whispered over my cradle. The one I was called in schoolyards. The one written in the margin of my father's books. Given freely, fully, irrevocably, into the ward-work. And in exchange—" and here his voice did finally move, dropped half a tone, the sound of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for decades, "—in exchange, Caelmere would live. My people would be safe. Every child born inside the walls, every harvest, every bell, every ordinary morning—for as long as the seal held, no one would ever have to know what waited outside."

"And you forgot yourself."

"No." He said it fast, sharp, the only fast thing about him. "That's what everyone assumes, and it's wrong, and the wrongness matters. Forgetting would have been mercy. Forgetting is clean. You lose the memory and go on, lighter. What happened to me was worse. I *kept* everything. Every memory. Every name-day. Every face. I remember my mother's voice. I remember being called Casimir. I remember the shape of the name in my mouth, the weight of it, the way teachers used to say it with the little honorific, the way enemies cursed it. I remember all of it—and none of it belongs to me anymore. Do you understand? The name was taken out of me the way a stone is taken out of a wall, and the wall stands, and the stone sits in a museum case somewhere, labeled, preserved, and I can visit it. I can look at my own name through glass. I can't touch it. I can't wear it. When people address me, the words slide off. 'Regent' works—it's an office, not a name, offices are hollow by design. But anything true just... passes through. I have spent thirty years being addressed by everyone in this country and known by no one."

Beatrix sat very still. Their whole life had been built on being unknown—useful and unremarkable, invisible on purpose, invisible as survival. They had thought, all these years, that they understood invisibility. They had pitied themselves for it in the quiet hours, in the kitchen after service, eating alone over the ledger. And now across a table sat a man who had made himself invisible in the deepest way a person could, permanently, deliberately, as a gift to strangers who would never know his name because his name no longer existed—and Beatrix felt hatred drain out of them like heat out of a cooling coal, and something else pool in its place, something heavier and much harder to carry.

Pity. Pity, and recognition.

"You sealed us in," Beatrix said. "People died at the borders. The wards fell and the stars went out and the moon started counting turns that shouldn't exist, and you knew—you must have known the seal was expiring. Why didn't you tell anyone?"

"Because telling would break it faster." He folded his hands again. "A secret held is a load-bearing wall. Announce that the house is falling and the house falls sooner. I told myself that for a year. And then the thirteenth turn began, and I stopped telling myself stories, and I started doing arithmetic. The seal expires when the turn completes. Everyone in this country becomes what I have been for thirty years—or worse. The thing outside doesn't take names politely, Thornbury. It takes the *person*. I got the gentle version, because I gave mine freely. Freely given names leave the giver standing. Names taken by force leave nothing."

"So your plan is to feed it the Doorway."

"My plan is to make sure there is never a Doorway." He turned the wine cup again, around and around, a small orbiting, like a moon. "The company you keep wants to open it. Re-inscribe the sigil. Speak the memories aloud. Give a name freely and walk out into the world beyond, carrying Caelmere's memory like embers in a basket. I know. I've read Odile Ferrant's old maps—I had them brought to me, the blank ones, and I sat with them for a night, and I understood exactly what she'd drawn and why it terrified her. It's a beautiful plan. It might even work. And I am asking you to stop it."

"Why?" Beatrix said. "Truly. Not the arithmetic. The reason underneath."

And Casimir looked at them across the table, across the pear that tasted of nothing, across thirty years of counted mornings, and said:

"Because I know what it costs. Not from doctrine. From the inside. I have been a hole in the shape of a man since before your parents met, Thornbury. I know precisely, intimately, in my bones, what it is to give a name away. And I am telling you that whatever you think you're prepared for—you are not. You imagine sacrifice as a grand gesture, a burning moment, a hero's price paid in firelight with witnesses. Mine was paid in a locked room with one mage and a contract, and it has lasted thirty years, and it will last until I die, and when I die there will be nothing left of me to bury because there was nothing left to begin with. You want to do that *on purpose*. Willingly. With people you love watching. And I look at you—at the taster who poisoned forty-one men to save a healer, at the charm-seller who burned their own ledger rather than let it be seized, at the person who has spent five years making themselves small to protect others—and I think: this one might actually do it. This one might actually pay it. And I cannot allow it. Not because I hate you. Because I *know*."

The lamp flickered. Above them, another patch of gilt star lifted off the ceiling and drifted upward into shadow, and Beatrix watched it go and felt the room get slightly lighter, slightly emptier, slightly closer to ending.

"You could have warned us," Beatrix said. "You could have told us the cost. We'd have chosen with our eyes open."

"I tried." Casimir's voice cracked here, actually cracked, one hairline fracture in thirty years of level delivery, and Beatrix heard it and heard what lived inside the crack: not anger. Grief. "I sent the wardens. I sent terms. I sent warnings dressed as threats, because threats are the only language I have left—an office speaks in threats; a man would speak in pleas, and there is no man left here to speak them. Every message I sent you was a plea wearing armor. *Turn back. The Doorway is a wound.* And you read them as a tyrant's orders, because that's what they looked like, because I made sure they looked like that, because if I showed you what I actually was—a hollowed man begging strangers not to make his mistake—you'd have seen the weakness, and weakness invites the thing outside faster than any broken ward. Fear is a door too, Thornbury. I've spent thirty years keeping my fear behind stone."

Beatrix thought of Maren in the wardenhouse, healing her captors' headaches until they softened. Thought of Dain confessing at the fire. Thought of Pip drawing stars nobody else could see. Thought of the ledger burning, and how each entry had been a person helped, and how Pip had offered to remember them aloud, and how that had felt like being handed back something they hadn't known they'd lost.

Then they thought about what Casimir had just described. Thirty years. Kept memories, stolen name. Visiting yourself through glass.

"Hatred would be easier," Beatrix said slowly. "I came here ready for it. I had it loaded, like a dose measured out. And you've gone and dissolved it, and I resent you for that almost as much as I resent everything else."

"That's the most honest thing anyone has said to me in thirty years." He almost smiled again. The smile got further this time—reached his eyes for a single second—before the hollowness closed over it. "Ask me what you came to ask. You didn't climb my stairs to trade confessions."

Beatrix took a breath. "The sigil. The re-inscription. Memories spoken aloud, a name freely given. Would it work? Truthfully. You've studied this longer than any of us. You've *lived* the mechanism."

Casimir was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Beatrix counted the lamp flickers—seven, eight, nine—and watched the wine settle in his untouched cup.

"Yes," he said finally. "It would work. The memories spoken aloud would rebuild the pattern. Memory is the counter-magic—I've known that for thirty years and done nothing with the knowledge, because using it would require admitting the seal was temporary, and admitting that meant admitting I'd only postponed the eating, not ended it. A freely given name would anchor it. It would hold. Not forever—nothing holds forever—but long enough. Long enough for children born under a restored sky to grow old under it. That's the truth. That's what I know and couldn't say." He lifted his eyes from the cup. "Now ask me the real question. The one under that one."

Beatrix asked it. "Why seal it forever, then? If opening it works, if the price is survivable, if the country can be reborn—why stop us?"

"Because the price isn't survivable." And here Casimir stood, slowly, unfolding from the chair like something that had been seated far longer than a meeting requires, and walked to the edge of the pale floor where the throne used to be, and stood on the spot where his throne had been, on the spot where his name had been pressed into the world like a seal into wax, and spoke facing the empty space. "Survivable means you live through it and continue. I live through it every day and do not continue. There is a difference, Thornbury, and it is the entire difference, and no one told me it before I signed. The mage who took my name wept while she wrote it down. Did you know that? She wept, and she told me it was honorable, and she was right, and it changed nothing. Honor is a story survivors tell about the dead. I won't watch you become a story."

"Then help us instead."

The words came out of Beatrix before they decided to say them—out of the place where the pity pooled, out of the taster's instinct that had learned, over five years, to detect the difference between poison and medicine by holding both on the tongue at once. Casimir turned from the empty throne-space. He looked older in that instant, or perhaps simply more present, more *there*, than he had been in thirty years, because someone had just offered him a role that wasn't office.

"Help you," he repeated. Flatly. Testing the words the way he tested everything, turning them, weighing them, checking for the hollow spot.

"Your name is gone. Fine. But your *memory* isn't. You said it yourself—you kept everything. Thirty years of counted days. The terms of the original bargain, the mage's face, the wording of the contract, the way the ward-work accepted the offering. You are the only living person who remembers how the seal was made from the inside. We have memories of charms and meals and stars. You have the *mechanism*. The sigil needs both—the pattern and the power, the spoken past and the precise terms. You could stand at the Doorway and speak the bargain aloud, every clause, every condition, the exact words the mage wrote down. Not a name. Not a sacrifice. Just testimony. The one thing you have left that's worth anything, and the one thing no one else alive can give."

Casimir stood very still. The lamp steadied, as if the room itself were listening.

"They will hate me," he said. "The soldier whose brothers died holding a gate I ordered sealed. The healer my wardens took. The child who grew up under a sky I extinguished. They will look at me and see the regent who let the stars go out, and they will be right to."

"They'll see a man who came," Beatrix said. "At the end. When it counted. When he could have sat in a fading palace and let the country dissolve around him and died comfortable and hollow and safe. You taught me something tonight, Regent. You taught me that freely given is the only currency the Doorway respects. So I'm giving you this freely: a way to matter. Not a name. Not absolution. A task. Take it or leave it."

For a moment—one breath, maybe two—Beatrix saw it happen. Saw the hollowness ripple, saw something move underneath it like a fish under ice, saw the man reach toward the offer the way a starving man reaches toward bread he doesn't quite believe is real. And then it passed. Whatever lived in Casimir now—whatever the bargain had left behind when it took the name out—looked at the offer, and weighed it, and found itself unable to lift it.

"No," he said. Softly. Without cruelty. "No. I can't. You don't understand what I am now. I am not permitted to help. That was in the terms too—the part no one reads because it's the part written smallest. A name given to the seal binds the giver to the seal's *purpose*. While the seal stands, I maintain it. I defend it. I cannot act against it, not by word, not by deed, not by omission I could have prevented. I have spent thirty years unable to warn my own country, unable to save my own wardens' consciences, unable to weep where anyone could hear me. And when the seal breaks—when the turn completes—" he paused, and Beatrix watched him do the arithmetic again, the counting he'd done every morning for thirty years, "—when it completes, whatever is left of me goes with it. The seal's purpose dies, and the thing bound to the purpose dies with it. I am not a man refusing your offer, Thornbury. I am a clause in a contract, standing in a room, explaining its own terms."

The room had gotten colder. Beatrix could see their breath now, faintly, in the lamplight. Another star lifted from the ceiling. Somewhere far below, a bell rang—wrong, arrhythmic, the bells of Vellamere losing their count the way the streets were losing their stones.

"One question more," Beatrix said. "Then I'll go."

Casimir inclined his head.

"The pear. The dish you served me. The one that tastes like forgetting. Was it a test?"

"It was dinner." He looked at the plate, at the pear he had poached himself—in a palace kitchen, alone, at midnight, because the cooks had all fled or faded and the regent of Caelmere had cooked his own final meal the way a condemned man does. "I wanted to eat one last meal with someone who could taste it. Truly taste it. You're the only one left who can. Everyone else eats nothing-flavor now and calls it food. You tasted the absence and named it. That's the closest thing to being *known* that I've experienced in thirty years." He lifted his cup at last, the toast he'd been not-making since Beatrix sat down. "To being tasted, Thornbury. It's more than most of us get."

Beatrix raised their cup in return and drank, and the wine tasted of almost-nothing too, and somehow that was right.

Then they stood, and thanked him—formally, coolly, the way one thanks a host who has confessed to a crime and a wound in the same breath—and turned for the doors.

At the threshold, Beatrix stopped. Turned back. Casimir sat alone at his table in the empty throne room, under the amputated sky, beside the meal for two of which he had eaten nothing, and Beatrix understood that they were looking at the future the Doorway offered everyone: a self preserved in glass, visited but never worn.

"Regent," Beatrix said. "When the sigil burns anew—when the memories are spoken—we'll speak yours too. All thirty years of counted days. Someone will say them aloud at the arch. Not your name—that's gone, and gone is gone. Your *witness*. The clauses. The mage who wept. The pear you poached yourself. It will be testimony, freely given, and testimony isn't bound by the seal's purpose. Testimony is just truth leaving a person. Even a clause can testify."

Casimir looked at them across the darkening room. And for the first time in thirty years—Beatrix would swear this later, to Odile, to Dain, to anyone who asked—he moved without the seal moving him. A small motion. A nod. Half an inch of the head, toward the door, toward the night, toward the company waiting in the fading streets below.

Permission, Beatrix thought. Or blessing. Or simply a hollow man, hearing for one moment that even a clause in a contract might still be *heard*.

They walked out. Down the gold-veined stair, the veins duller than they'd been an hour ago. Out through the doors no one guarded anymore. Into streets that were forgetting their own names, where the company waited—Odile with her blank maps, Dain with his wrapped sword, Maren with her healing hands, Pip with the old sky folded in a pocket like a letter.

Behind them, high in the palace, a single lamp burned in an empty throne room, over a meal for two, one portion eaten by the only person in Caelmere who could taste it.

And below the Salt Cathedral, beneath the ruined arch, the Doorway waited—cold, locked, hungry for a name.

The moon was two nights from full thirteenth phase.

Two nights.

* * *

Beatrix descended the palace stair with the taste of nothing-wine still coating their tongue, and found the others where they'd agreed to meet: the sunken doorway of a chandler's shop three streets from the cathedral square, a recess deep enough to hide four people and a child from patrols that had grown sparse and strange, wardens walking their routes like sleepwalkers, boots finding cobbles that weren't always there.

Odile saw them first. She was sketching by lamplight—of course she was, the woman drew the way other people breathed—and what she was drawing stopped Beatrix's breath for a moment: the throne room. She'd never seen it. She was drawing it from Beatrix's description, given in fragments over the last hour, and she had drawn the empty space where the throne had been, and above it the amputated sky, and she had labeled the empty space, in her small precise cartographer's hand: *the seat of a man who is not there.*

"He told you everything?" she asked.

"Everything except how to stop him." Beatrix slid down the wall to sit, because their legs had decided the conversation was over whether they were or not. "He can't be stopped. That's the discovery. He's not a man choosing to oppose us—he's a clause defending its own terms. You can't argue with a contract. You can only outlive it."

Dain crouched opposite, sword across his knees, and his face had the gray look of a man doing sums he doesn't like. "Then the wardens—"

"Were following his orders. Which are the seal's orders. Which expire when the turn completes." Beatrix looked up at the strip of visible sky above the street, where the swollen wrong moon hung like a lantern with the light going out of it. "Here's what I learned tonight, and I need you all to hear it, because it changes what we do next. The seal isn't failing because it was badly made. It's failing because it was *temporary by design*—thirty years, bought with a name, and the name was always going to run out. Casimir didn't betray us. He *postponed* us. He bought this country one generation of safety at the price of himself, and the bill has come due, and he is bound—bound by the terms—to fight us to the last hour, because fighting us is the last function his borrowed authority can perform."

Maren stirred. Her ribs were still strapped from the wardenhouse; she shifted carefully and said, "Then when we open the Doorway—"

"When we open the Doorway, he'll be there. At the arch. He has to be. The seal's purpose requires its witness present at the moment of breaking." Beatrix heard themselves say it and realized they believed it the way you believe a taste: completely, bodily, the way you know milk has turned. "He'll stand on the far side of the arch, the clause facing the counter-clause, and he will speak the old terms one last time because the terms compel him, and then—" Beatrix paused, and the pause had weight, "—and then the turn completes, and the seal dies, and everything bound to it dies with it. Including him."

Silence in the chandler's doorway. The kind of silence that has a texture.

"He's already dead," Pip said.

Everyone turned. The child sat cross-legged against the doorframe, knees up, arms wrapped around them, the old sky drawn in charcoal on the backs of both hands where Pip always kept it, and Pip looked at Beatrix with the direct unsparing gaze that children and cats share, the gaze that sees the thing and does not flinch from it.

"He's been dead for thirty years," Pip said. "He just kept talking."

And Beatrix, who had climbed a palace stair to hate a man and come down having pitied him instead, found that a child had said in six words what they had needed a chapter of prose to arrive at, and nodded, and said, "Yes. That's exactly it."

Odile capped her ink. "So we have two nights, a hostile clause, a Doorway that demands a name, and a company of six." She looked around the doorway at each of them—Dain, Maren, herself, Beatrix, and Pip, five faces and a child's charcoal hands. "Someone should say the plan aloud. Saying it aloud is how we do things now. It's the whole magic."

Beatrix looked at the moon through the gap between rooftops. Two nights from full. Two nights from the turn completing. Two nights from the moment when a hollow man would stand at an arch and speak terms written before Beatrix was born, and a taster would stand on the near side holding the only currency the Doorway respected.
