## 19. Sigil Anew

The Doorway did not want blood. That was the first thing they learned, and the last thing they had left to give.

It stood in the deep crypt beneath the Salt Cathedral, older than the cathedral above it, older perhaps than the salt itself—a stone arch the height of three men, its surface carved with sigils that had once burned and now sat dark as old scars. The air around it tasted wrong to Beatrix: not poisoned, not spoiled, but *unseasoned*. Like a broth made of nothing. Like water that had forgotten it could carry flavor.

They came down into the crypt at moonrise, all six of them, carrying everything they owned and everything they remembered.

Odile went first with her lantern and her maps—the good ones, the ones she'd redrawn from memory on the road, every river and road and village of Caelmere laid down in ink because ink was the only way she knew how to love a place. Dain carried the ward-ghost's instructions scrawled on paper he kept checking, as if the words might desert him like he'd deserted his post. Maren walked without limping for the first time since the wardenhouse, though Beatrix noticed she still favored the leg when she thought no one watched. Pip carried a jar of fireflies caught on the road back to Vellamere, because Pip said stars should be represented, even by proxy.

And Beatrix carried the bread.

It had been Pip's idea, two nights ago, in the camp where they'd told their stories until the fire burned to embers. *The sigil is made of memories,* the ghost had said. *Spoken aloud, freely given, held in common.* And Pip, who remembered the old sky better than any astronomer alive, had said: *Then we should bring food. Nobody ever remembers a country without remembering what it tasted like.*

So Beatrix had baked through the last night in the kitchen below the regent's palace—slipped out past sleeping guards with a sack of loaves still warm, the morning fold done properly for the first time in front of an audience of one solemn child. The bread smelled like the life Beatrix was about to spend. It smelled, Beatrix thought, like the only part worth keeping.

The arch waited.

"Everyone remembers the plan," Odile said. Not a question. She checked anyway, touching each name like a cartographer confirming a landmark. "Maren first. Then me. Then Dain. Then Pip. Beatrix last."

"Because mine costs the most," Beatrix said.

"Because yours finishes it," Odile corrected, and would not look at them while she said it, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Maren stepped up to the arch and put her palm flat against the cold stone.

"I'm Maren Ostwick," she said, "of the fen-road villages, daughter of a midwife and a man who mended nets." Her voice shook and steadied. "I remember the fever winter, when I was eleven, and my mother worked four days without sleep, and I carried water and boiled rags and learned that healing is mostly showing up. I remember every headache I eased in the wardenhouse. I remember the soldier who brought me water when I was in the cell, and pretended it was orders." She glanced at Dain. "I remember that magic is just care, made visible. I give it freely."

The sigils under her hand flickered. One line of the great burning pattern woke—a thread of amber light tracing up the arch like sap rising—and stayed lit.

Maren stepped back crying and grinning at once. "It works," she whispered. "Oh, it *works*."

* * *

Odile took her place. She unrolled one of her maps against the base of the arch, weighting the corners with stones, so the country lay at the Doorway's feet.

"I'm Odile Ferrant," she said, "and I was stripped of my license for drawing a border that moved, which turned out to be the truest thing I ever drew." She laughed, short and wet. "I remember Caelmere entire. Every road I surveyed and every road I was too tired to survey and guessed at anyway. I remember the Salt Cathedral before it fell, because my grandmother took me up the tower and taught me the bells' names. I remember that a map is a promise: *this place exists; you can stand here.*" She pressed both hands to the stone. "I remember this place exists. I give it freely."

Two more lines ignited. The light spread along the arch's inner curve, and where it passed, the sigils began to hum—a low note Beatrix felt in their teeth, in the taster's tongue that had never once been wrong.

* * *

Dain stood before the arch longer than the others. His jaw worked. The paper with the ghost's instructions trembled in his fist.

"I'm Dain Corvess," he finally said. "Ward-gate third watch. I was ordered to open the gate the night the stars died, and I refused too late, and the gate opened anyway, and I have spent every day since trying to decide if that makes me a coward or a witness." He swallowed. "Here's what I've decided. I remember the gate. I remember the sound it made—not the order, not the fear, but the *sound*, like a bell underwater. I remember that refusing late is still refusing. I remember the faces of the company I deserted, and I will carry them whether the world records me or not." He set his palm against the arch. "I give it freely. All of it. Even the shameful parts. Especially those."

A third of the sigil burned now, and the humming deepened, and the fireflies in Pip's jar rose against the glass as if called.

* * *

Pip needed no coaching. Pip had been waiting for this the whole way, Beatrix realized—every star named aloud on the road, every constellation drawn in flour on a camp table, had been rehearsal.

"I'm Pip," Pip said. "Just Pip. I don't have a family name because my family got erased first, but I remember them anyway." Pip lifted the jar of fireflies high. "I remember the old sky. The Shepherd's Crook, the Long Kettle, the Seven Sisters and their eighth sister nobody talks about, the Wandering Ox—" Pip looked at Beatrix, quick and fierce, "—the Wandering Ox that stands over the north fields in autumn. I remember forty-one stars by name and I'll say every single one if the arch wants them, starting with Alder-bright, starting with the Kettle's handle, starting with—"

The arch drank it in. Line after line of the sigil caught, the burning spreading like dawn across the stone, and Pip's voice rang off the crypt walls naming stars that existed nowhere else in the world except inside a child's head—and the arch *wanted* them, the hunger in its hum turning to something almost like joy.

Four-fifths burned. The crypt was full of amber light and the smell of warm bread and the sound of a child reciting heaven.

Only the keystone remained: the sigil at the very top of the arch, the one shaped like a name being spoken.

Beatrix stepped forward.

* * *

They had agreed there would be no ceremony. But Odile caught Beatrix's sleeve, and Maren touched their shoulder, and Dain stood straight as a wall between Beatrix and the door they'd come in by—as if even now some part of him expected to be ordered to hold it shut.

"You don't have to explain yourself," Odile said. "Say what you remember. That's all it asks."

"That's all it asks," Beatrix repeated softly. "That's the trouble with doors. They ask so little and take so much."

But Beatrix had been a taster for five years. Beatrix knew exactly what things cost, because tasting was nothing else. They set the bread down at the foot of the arch where everyone could reach it, and they put their hand on the stone, and the stone was warm now, warm as a hearth, warm as the top of a morning loaf.

"I'm Beatrix Thornbury," they said, "and here is what I remember. I remember the regent's kitchens—the steam, the shouting, the sous-chef Rennick who sang off-key and shared his onions with me. I remember every charm I ever sold: the sleep-charm for Widow Cassel, the finding-charm for the cooper's boy lost in the fog, the courage-charm I sold to a girl going to tell her father the truth, and she came back the next week and paid double and wouldn't say why. I remember dosing myself with harmless poisons until my tongue could taste a lie in a soup. I remember standing on a rooftop watching the stars go out and thinking: *I know that feeling. I have spent my whole life being unremarkable on purpose.*"

The sigil climbed toward the keystone. The humming was a chord now, vast and patient.

"I remember this company," Beatrix said, and their voice finally broke, and they let it. "A mapmaker who draws countries out of love. A healer who heals her captors. A soldier who chose guilt over comfort. A child who carries the sky in a jar." They looked at each of them. "I remember all of it. I give it freely."

The keystone sigil flared—

—and paused.

The arch hummed, waiting. Everyone felt it: the shape of one more thing wanted. Not memories. Memories were the mortar. The keystone wanted the brick.

*A true name, freely given.*

Beatrix closed their eyes. Behind them, Pip made a small sound and stopped it. Odile said, very quietly, "You don't have to—"

"Yes I do," Beatrix said. "That's rather the point of freely."

They opened their eyes and looked up at the burning arch—at the sigil that had branded itself into their bloodline generations ago, into every taster ever chosen, the mark of a family bound to the wards since before anyone remembered why. This was the why. This had always been the why. Tasters were raised to the work because somewhere far back, someone in Beatrix's line had promised that when the Doorway needed opening, a Thornbury would stand here and pay.

"Here is my name," Beatrix said to the arch, to the country, to whatever listened beyond the borders of the world. "Beatrix Thornbury. Given freely—not to escape, not to save myself, but to anchor what we remember into the world outside these walls. Let them forget me. Let every record, every ledger, every memory in any mind that isn't standing in this room let me go. But let what we spoke tonight hold. Let Caelmere be remembered by the ones who choose to remember it. Take my name. It's the only thing I own that's big enough."

The keystone drank it in.

For one heartbeat, nothing. Beatrix felt the absence already beginning—like a tooth pulled, like a word on the tip of the tongue, like reaching for the ledger and finding ash. Who had they been? Someone. Someone standing in a crypt with people they loved, holding—

No. Not gone. *Held.* Held by the others, the way Pip had promised to hold the ledger's entries aloud. The name wasn't destroyed. It was *deposited*—carried outward through the reopening wards, past the vanished borders, written into a world that had erased Caelmere, so that somewhere beyond, in minds and maps and mouths that had never heard of this country, a name would arrive like a seed.

The arch ignited.

Every sigil burned at once, white-gold, roaring silently, and the Doorway opened—not onto another land, but onto *possibility*, onto the raw space where a country decides what it will be. Wind rushed through the crypt though no wind could reach this deep. The fireflies burst from their jar and scattered upward into the light like sparks returning to a forge.

And beneath the roar, Beatrix—who no longer had a name and was therefore listening with everything else—heard the wards of Caelmere answer. Heard them all across the country, in the border stones and the city gates and the old wayshrines: not restoring, not rebuilding the old bargain, but *asking a question*.

*What shall we be instead?*

Beatrix turned to the others. Their face was wet and they were smiling and they could not, for the life of them, have said whose face it was—but the four people looking back at them knew, and that was enough, that was everything, that was the whole trick of it.

"It's asking us," Beatrix said. Voice strange and new and entirely their own. "The country's asking what we want it to be."

Odile stepped forward, map in hand, eyes blazing. "Then we'd better answer fast," she said, "because something else is answering too—I can feel it pulling the other direction. Casimir. He's at the top of the cathedral right now, and he's giving the old wards his terms."

Above them, through the salt-crusted vaults, came the tolling of the cathedral bells—and Beatrix's unnamed heart sank, because the count was wrong again.

Thirteen.
