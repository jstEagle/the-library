## 20. A Name Freely Given

The Doorway had been burning for an hour, and still the arch demanded more.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the sigil-circle with their hands black to the wrist, ash and old salt ground into every crease of their knuckles. Around them, the others spoke the country into being—Maren's voice cracked from the wardenhouse but steady, naming every fever she had ever broken; Dain reciting the roads he'd marched, gate by gate, mile by mile; Odile reading from her sketched map the way a priest reads scripture, every river and fold of hill given back its name. Pip sat cross-legged at the center, small and fierce, pointing up at a sky that was not yet there and calling stars into it one at a time.

*The Ladle. The Shepherd's Dog. The Long Knife. The Wandering Queen.*

Each name landed on the arch like a struck bell. The sigils drank them in and burned a shade brighter, a shade redder, and the cold that poured off the stone eased by a degree, then another.

It was working. That was the terrible thing. It was working, and Beatrix had known since the wayshrine that it could not work all the way.

The ghost's words still hung in them like a swallowed coal. *The Doorway opens to a true name, freely given. Not spoken. Given. There is a difference, taster, and you of all people should know it—you have spent your life tasting what was never meant to be yours.*

Beatrix had spent the walk from the wayshrine telling themselves there would be another way. That Odile would find a loophole in her maps. That Maren would know a herb. That the ghost had been wrong, or lying, or speaking in the riddles ghosts used to amuse themselves across the centuries. They had watched the others work all night and let themselves believe it, the way they had once let themselves believe that a person could be useful enough to be safe, small enough to be spared.

But the arch had reached the edge of what memory alone could do. It burned a deep, hungry orange now, and beneath the orange Beatrix could see the stone itself going translucent, like a window iced over, like a thing seen through water. Beyond it was not the other side of a wall. Beyond it was *absence*—the shape Caelmere had left in the world when it was erased, a country-shaped hole with nothing in it, waiting.

Waiting for an anchor.

"Beatrix." Odile's voice, low, from the edge of the circle. She had stopped reading. She was looking at them the way she looked at a map that had just shown her something she didn't want to know. "The sigils aren't taking the names anymore. They're just—holding. Like a cup that's full."

"I know."

"You know something."

Beatrix wiped their hands on their apron—an old habit, the apron they had worn out of the regent's kitchen for the last time two nights ago, flour still ghosted into its weave. "I know what the ghost told me. All of it. I should have said so at the wayshrine."

Dain's head came up. Maren went still. Even Pip, mid-star, turned.

"The Doorway doesn't just want memories," Beatrix said. "Memories are the mortar. But a door needs a hinge. It needs something true, given freely, from someone who belongs here—and it has to be the one thing a person can't take back." They looked at the arch. It was easier than looking at the others. "A true name. Given, not spoken. Given means it goes. All the way. Every record, every memory that isn't deliberately kept, every mark I've ever left on paper or stone or someone's ledger. The country keeps its shape because I stop having one."

The silence that followed had a texture. Beatrix had tasted enough silences to know them—the silence of a kitchen before the regent's verdict, the silence of a neighbor deciding whether to believe in a charm. This one was grief, still warm, not yet shaped into argument.

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"No." The child was on their feet, fists at their sides, and Beatrix thought: *there is the old sky in that spine.* "You don't get to. You taught me to bake. You can't give away your name, that's—that's like giving away your hands."

"It's not like that."

"It's exactly like that."

"Pip." Beatrix crouched, brought their eyes level with the child's, and did the thing they had practiced for five years in a walled city: they made their face kind and steady when it wanted to be neither. "Listen. I've been nameless most of my life already. Not the way you think—everyone called me Beatrix, the regent's taster, the charm-seller on Tallow Lane. But a name that's given to you so you can be *used*—that's a leash, not a name. The Doorway isn't asking for that. It's asking for the true one underneath, the one my mother's mother carried, the one that was bound into the wards before I was born. And here's what the ghost said that I haven't told you." They took a breath. It shook. They let it. "It said the name doesn't vanish. It *travels*. It becomes the hinge—the thing that holds the door open between Caelmere and everything beyond. Which means every person who walks out of this country, every trader, every letter, every story that crosses the new border, will carry a piece of me with them without knowing it. I won't be erased, Pip. I'll be *everywhere*. I'll just never get to sign my work."

"That's not fair," Pip said, and their voice cracked down the middle, and Beatrix gathered them in before the crack could become a break.

"No," Beatrix said into the child's hair. "It isn't."

* * *

They argued for another hour. Beatrix would give them that—they were thorough. Odile laid out the ghost's exact words, parsed them, hunted for the loophole with the same ferocity she'd once used to chart a coastline, and found none. Maren offered her own name and Beatrix refused it: the wards had been bound to taster bloodlines, to the families bred to keep the regent's body and the country's borders safe with the same tongue. It had to be a taster. It had to be them. Dain offered to hold Beatrix down and refuse to let them approach the arch, and Beatrix looked at him—big, gentle, guilt-carrying Dain, who had refused one order too late and had been paying for it ever since—and said, "You taught me something on the road. Refusing is a thing you do *before*, not after. Don't make me your after."

In the end it was Odile who stopped arguing, not because she ran out of arguments but because she ran out of the will to make them. She sat down on a fallen pillar, unrolled her map of a country only they remembered, and was quiet for a long time.

"I spent six years hating myself for drawing maps of a place that didn't exist," she said finally. "Every blank border felt like a confession. And now I understand—the maps weren't wrong. They were the only true thing left. Memory is the counter-magic. You taught me that, or Pip did, or the moon did." She looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "If your name is the hinge, then I want it in my map. Not your name—your *place*. A blank space, labeled. So that when Caelmere is reborn and someone asks why the map has one empty square, I get to tell them about the taster who fed a whole country through a door."

Beatrix laughed. It came out wet. "You'd label me on a map."

"I'd label you on a *good* map. Better than a statue."

* * *

They did it at moonrise, because the thirteenth turn would be full within the day and there was no more time to be gentle about it.

The others stood back at the edge of the circle. Pip had demanded the right to hold Beatrix's hand until the last possible moment, and Beatrix had granted it, and now the child's grip was crushing and Beatrix treasured every ache of it.

"Tell me what to expect," Beatrix said to the arch, or to the ghost they half-hoped was listening, or to themselves. "I've tasted everything this country can poison me with. I'd like to taste this one sober."

The arch flared. And Beatrix felt the Doorway *answer*—not in words, not in the ghost's dusty voice, but in the way the sigils turned toward them like faces, in the way the cold became a kind of attention. It was the feeling of the regent's table, the moment before the first dish arrived, when the whole room waited to see if you would live. But inverted. Reversed. Not *will you survive this bite* but *will you give this bite freely*.

It was asking permission. Even now. Even hungry.

"Well," Beatrix said. "Aren't you polite."

They thought about what they were giving away. They made themselves think about all of it, because a gift given carelessly wasn't free. The ledger they'd already burned—every charm, every customer, every small kindness recorded in their cramped hand, gone in chapter seven of this same story, and Pip remembering them aloud ever since. Their mother's lullaby, hummed to a tune whose words had worn away. The first time they'd tasted arsenic in the regent's wine and lived, and the strange pride of it. Tallow Lane. The bread baskets. The neighbors who pretended not to believe. The regent's hollow eyes, which they understood now, which they pitied, which they would forget pitying.

And Beatrix Thornbury. The name itself. The one their mother had given them in a small room that no record would remember, the name that had kept them *someone* through five careful invisible years.

They had spent those years being useful so no one would look at them. And here was the joke the universe had been building the whole time: the way to save everyone was to finally, fully, be *seen*—by a door, by the world beyond the world, by the found family standing at the edge of the fire with their faces lit orange and wet.

Not a sacrifice, they told themselves. Say it right. A gift. You don't lose a gift. You give it.

"Pip," Beatrix said. "Let go when I say. Not before."

"Okay." The child's voice was very small. "Beatrix?"

"Yes?"

"When it's done—I'm still going to set a place for you. At every table. Forever. That's not a memory that fades, that's a *rule*."

Beatrix's chest did something complicated and painful and warm. "Then it's a rule I accept."

They stepped into the circle. The heat should have been unbearable and was not; it was the heat of an oven at dawn, of bread about to become bread, of a kitchen in exile on the best night of its life. The sigils rose up the arch like flames standing still. And Beatrix opened their mouth and did the one thing a taster is trained never, ever to do.

They gave away the taste of their own name.

They didn't speak it. Speaking would have kept it. They *gave* it—the way you give bread to a hungry stranger, the way you give a charm to a neighbor who'll pretend it's nothing, the way you give your last coin not because you can afford it but because the giving is the point. The name left them like a flavor leaving the tongue after the swallow: present, then fading, then a warmth, then only the *knowledge* of warmth, then only the habit of reaching for a word that wasn't there.

The arch drank.

Light went through it like dawn through ice. The translucent stone cleared from the center outward, and beyond it Beatrix saw—not absence, not anymore—a horizon. Grass. Sky. A road going somewhere that had roads. The country-shaped hole in the world filling in at the edges like water returning to a dry bed, and at the center of it, holding the door open, a hinge of light that hummed with a frequency Beatrix's bones recognized as their own.

"Now," they said, and their voice came out strange, thinned, like a bell with a crack that somehow made it sweeter. "Pip. Let go."

Pip let go.

Beatrix walked toward the light—not through it, they weren't leaving, that had never been the point—but close enough to touch the threshold, close enough that the new dawn on the other side fell across their face. Behind them the others were shouting their name, all of them at once, *Beatrix, Beatrix, Beatrix*, over and over, deliberately, furiously, carving it into the air the way Odile had promised to carve it into the map. Keeping it. Choosing to keep it, each of them, one by one by one.

That was the trade, then. The world would not record them. But these four would. And a rule was a rule.

Beatrix turned around one last time and smiled at them—at the hedge-healer with the cracked voice, the deserter with the steady hands, the cartographer with her blank square waiting, the child who remembered the old sky and had just remembered one more thing in it.

"Go on, then," Beatrix said, though the name that should have signed the words was already gone, already everywhere, already the hinge holding a whole country open to the morning. "Let's see what we made."

The light took the arch. The light took the thirteenth moon. And on the far side of the Doorway, in a world that had never heard of Caelmere, a traveler on an empty road stopped, and frowned, and felt—without knowing why—the sudden, certain sense that somewhere behind them a door had opened, and that they were expected, and that there would be bread.
