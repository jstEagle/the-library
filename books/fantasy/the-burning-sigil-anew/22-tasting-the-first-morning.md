## 22. Tasting the First Morning

The oven wakes before I do.

That's not quite true. The truth is that I wake before dawn out of five years of habit, lie in the narrow bed above the shop listening to the coals settle, and then remember—again, every morning, like a gift I keep re-receiving—that no one is going to ring a bell and summon me to taste anything. That the only tongue my work requires today is my own.

I get up anyway. Some habits are worth keeping.

The bakery-teahouse sits on Larkspur Row, which used to be called something else before the wards came down and the city remembered it was allowed to change. It has a crooked chimney, a door that sticks in damp weather, and a front window wide enough that Odile declared it "architecturally honest" the first time she saw it, which I have decided is a compliment. The sign above the door has no name on it. Just a sigil—the burning one, redrawn by six hands in gold leaf—and beneath it, in Pip's careful letters: *OPEN. BREAD. TEA. CHARMS. ASK ABOUT THE STARS.*

Pip insisted on that last line. Business has been excellent.

* * *

The first customer of the morning is Maren, who does not need bread but comes anyway because she says my kitchen is the only place her hands stop itching to heal things. She takes her usual seat by the window—the one with the cushion she pretends not to have brought herself—and wraps her fingers around a cup of nettle tea while I shape the morning's loaves.

"You burned the first batch," she observes.

"I did not burn it. I *caramelized* it."

"You burned it."

"Charm of confidence," I say, tapping the little woven knot hanging over the oven door. "It works better if you don't undermine it."

Maren smiles into her tea. She wears color now—actual color, a scarf the shade of new leaves—and when the bell over the door rings and a young man shuffles in cradling his bandaged hand, she sets the cup down and gets to work without being asked. He pays her in eggs. She accepts them like treasure. Under the old regime, hedge-healing was a flogging offense. Now there's a queue outside her door most afternoons, and last week the city council asked her—asked *her*—to advise on the rebuilding of the hospital ward.

She still flinches at loud footsteps. We all carry something. But she laughs more easily than she did on the road, and when the young man winces as she unwraps the bandage, she tells him a joke so terrible he forgets to hurt for a full three seconds.

That's healing too. I'd put it in the ledger, if the ledger still existed.

* * *

Dain arrives at the second bell, as he always does, carrying a crate of flour from the miller on his shoulder like it weighs nothing. He set himself up as our delivery man within a week of the rebirth, and no one had the heart to tell him we could manage. The truth is the crates are heavy and my arms are better suited to tasting than hauling, so nobody tried.

He stacks the flour by the cellar door, wipes his hands on his trousers, and stands there a moment in the doorway with the particular expression he gets when he wants to say something and is negotiating with himself about whether to say it.

"Out with it," I tell him.

"There's a posting." He scratches the back of his neck. "At the garrison. They're reforming the watch—not Casimir's people, the new charter, actual oversight, all that. They want veterans who refused orders." His jaw works. "They want me to train them. To teach the difference between following and obeying."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd think about it." He looks at the floor. "Beatrix, I stood at a gate and let the stars go out. What do I know about teaching anyone anything?"

I come around the counter and put flour handprints on his uniform sleeves, deliberately, the way you mark a child who has done something worth celebrating.

"You know exactly what it costs to refuse too late," I say. "There isn't a soul in that garrison who needs a perfect teacher. They need one who knows where the cliff edge is."

He huffs—a laugh trying to escape through guilt—and doesn't wipe off the handprints. He never does. He'll wear them all day, I know, and someone will ask, and he'll say "the baker marked me," and it will mean what it means.

"The bread smells good," he says gruffly, retreating.

"It always does."

"That's the charm."

"That's *mostly* the charm."

* * *

Midmorning, the rush comes. This is my favorite hour, though I'd deny it under oath.

They come for bread, mostly—farmers in from the pale fields, which are pale fields no longer but still bear the name the way old soldiers bear their scars. They come for tea. And they come, sidling up to the counter with the shy look people still haven't unlearned, for charms.

Under Casimir, magic was a thing you whispered about. Now there's a shelf by the register, openly stocked: knots against nightmares, salts for safe roads, tinctures of clear memory for the grieving. I sell them at whatever the buyer can pay, and sometimes for a story, and once—for an entire beehive, which Pip negotiated with the gravity of a trade ambassador—for a promise to plant lavender.

A woman I don't recognize lingers after buying a knot of protection for her daughter's first journey. She turns the little charm over in her hands.

"They say you were there," she says. "At the Doorway. When it opened."

"I was making bread at the time," I say. "Word travels strangely."

"They say someone gave their name to hold the country's memory." Her eyes are careful, kind, probing. "That no record of them remains anywhere. Not in any book, any registry, any mind that wasn't there to hold it."

I slide her change across the counter. My hands are steady. They're always steady; it's the one part of the old training that turned out to be simply *mine*.

"People say a lot of things," I tell her. "Try the rye. It's good today."

She goes. At the door she pauses, looks back at me, and nods—slow, deliberate, the nod you give a monument or a grave. Then she's gone into the bright street, and I stand there with my heart doing something complicated behind my ribs.

Here is the strange arithmetic of it: I cannot find myself anywhere. Not in the census rolls, which rebuilt themselves overnight when Caelmere returned, changed and open. Not in Odile's maps, which are famous now, hung in schools and guildhalls across a country that remembers itself. Not even in my own reflection, entirely—which sounds worse than it is. There's a space where my name should live, a smooth blankness, and when I reach for *my own name*, the word that was mine before the Doorway, I find nothing. Just the shape of it, like a footprint in ash.

But here is the other half of the arithmetic, the half I didn't expect:

Everyone who was there remembers me. Freely, constantly, stubbornly. Dain says my name—I mean, says *the name they gave me*, Beatrix, chosen in a wayshrine kitchen by firelight because I liked how it tasted on the tongue—every time he walks through the door. Maren writes it on the labels of the tonics she leaves for me. Pip has stitched it into a sampler that hangs by the hearth, surrounded by stars that exist nowhere else in the sky.

A name freely given, the ward-ghost said. I thought I understood what I was giving away. I didn't understand that a name given can also be a name *held*—that six pairs of hands could catch it, pass it back and forth, keep it warm.

I am nameless the way a river is nameless: called by everyone who loves it, owned by none.

* * *

The afternoon light comes slantwise through the front window, turning the flour dust to drifting gold, when Pip bursts in with the particular velocity of a child who has news and has been running with it for several streets.

"*She's done it.*"

"Done what? Slow down. Have you eaten?"

"She finished the map!" Pip skids to a halt at the counter, grabs an end piece of yesterday's loaf without asking, which is their right and they know it. "The big one. For the guildhall. She wouldn't let anyone see it until it was done and now it's done and she's bringing it *here* first. She said—" Pip scrunches their face, reproducing Odile's precise dryness: "*'If it must be seen for the first time anywhere, let it be seen among people who know what maps cost.'*"

My chest does the thing it does. I have stopped fighting it.

Odile arrives twenty minutes later carrying a long wooden tube, and behind her, inevitably, half the neighborhood, because nothing happens quietly on Larkspur Row anymore and no one pretends otherwise. She clears my biggest table with a sweep of her arm, unrolls the map, and weights the corners with teacups, a honey pot, and Dain's forearm.

And there it is. Caelmere, whole.

Not the Caelmere of the old maps—those are gone, erased past recovery, and Odile will tell you with a cartographer's flat calm that some erasures are mercies. This is the country as it is now: borders drawn open, drawn *porous*, with roads running out past where the wards used to flare and die, into lands whose names are new-old, half-remembered, negotiated. The mountains have their true names again. The Salt Cathedral is marked with a small arch symbol and the notation, in Odile's hand: *here we knocked*.

Pip leans over it, breath fogging the parchment, hunting for the constellations inset in the corners—the new sky, the slightly wrong sky, the one that came back shaped like us. There's the Baker's Knead, seven stars in the arc of two working arms. The Bell-Ringer. The Healer's Hands. The Deserted Soldier, which Dain objected to and was overruled by unanimous vote. And near the top, brightest, a constellation Pip named themselves and refuses to explain: the Taster, a single star with a scatter of small ones around it like crumbs.

"Look here," Odile says, and taps the lower corner.

There's a space. A deliberate blank, bordered in fine gold line, roughly the size of a hand laid flat. Every other region of the map is labeled, colored, annotated. This space is empty except for one word, inked in Odile's most careful script, the lettering she saves for things that matter:

*Beatrix.*

"It's not a place," she says, to the room, to me. "I couldn't make it a place. Everything else on this map exists whether we remember it or not. That spot exists *only* because we do." She straightens, and her voice does the thing it almost never does, the crack in the professional veneer. "So it stays blank. It stays ours. Anyone who wants to know what goes there has to sit at this table and be told."

The room is quiet. Somewhere outside, the thirteenth moon—no, not the thirteenth anymore, just *the moon*, ordinary and whole—is rising over rooftops that will not fade.

"Well," I manage. "I hope you labeled the blank space accurately. I'm much larger than that."

"It's a *cartographic convention*," Odile snaps, and Maren laughs, and Dain claps me on the shoulder hard enough to stagger, and Pip announces that the blank space should get its own constellation, and just like that the moment dissolves into the beautiful ordinary noise of people who belong to each other arguing about star charts in a bakery.

Later, when they've gone—when the loaves for tomorrow are proofing under cloth and the lamps are low—I stand at the window a long time, looking at the street.

Larkspur Row is lit all the way down. Real lamplight, and charm-light besides, green-gold glimmers in windows where people no longer hide what they are. Two doors down, the widow Sarelle is teaching her granddaughter a cantrip in the open doorway, unashamed. A warden walks the beat unarmed and unhurried and nods to me through the glass, and I nod back.

Once, invisibility was the whole of my craft. Useful and unremarkable. I was very good at it. I could walk through Vellamere like smoke, and I told myself the loneliness was the price of survival, and I believed it, mostly, on the days when believing hurt less than hoping.

Now I am the least invisible person in a country that learned what forgetting costs. Strangers point me out in markets. Children ask me to bless their bread. There is no record of me anywhere in the world, and I have never in my life been so thoroughly, so inconveniently, so *wonderfully* known.

* * *

Closing time. I bank the ovens, sweep the floor, turn the sign. Pip is staying the night—they sleep in the loft more often than not now, officially "to help with the morning bake," unofficially because home is wherever the table has a place set for them, and this table always does.

I climb the stairs and find them already there, dragging a stool to the little table by the dormer window, laying it with two cups, two plates, two spoons. Setting my place. They do it every night without fail, with the solemnity of ritual, and every night I pretend I don't notice the exact care of it, and every night it undoes me completely.

"Chamomile?" Pip asks.

"Please."

They pour with great concentration. Outside the dormer, the stars are coming out—the new ones, ours, the Baker's Knead climbing over the chimney—and below them the older ones, the returned ones, almost right, almost remembered, shining down on a country that chose to be known rather than safe and discovered those were never opposites at all.

"To the blank space," Pip says, raising their cup.

I touch my cup to theirs. The clay clicks, small and certain.

"To the blank space," I say. "May it stay beautifully empty."

We drink. Below us the ovens tick as they cool, holding tomorrow's warmth. On the wall hangs a sampler of impossible stars. In the guildhall across town, a map waits with a golden emptiness that six people alone can fill. And somewhere beyond the borders we once couldn't cross, the world goes on without any record of me at all—

except everywhere.

Except in every mouth that says the name they gave me, every morning, like a gift they keep re-giving.

Like a taste that lingers.
