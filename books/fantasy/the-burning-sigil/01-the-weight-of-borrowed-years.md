## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The widow's grief tasted like copper and cold tea.

Felix Yarrow knew that before he ever touched her mind. Grief always did. Joy came out bright and fizzy, like stolen wine. Love was warm bread. But grief — grief was the taste of a mouth held open too long, waiting for someone who wasn't coming home.

He sat across from Maren Ostwick in the back room of her candle shop, two cups of chicory cooling between them, his fingers laced loosely around hers as though they were old friends sharing comfort. In truth, he was elbow-deep in her memory, careful as a pickpocket at a hanging.

*There it is.* The funeral, three years gone. Her husband Tomas in his coffin, rain on the awning, the terrible specific weight of his hand going cold in hers. Felix didn't yank it free. That was how amateurs worked, and amateurs left wounds. He teased at the edges instead, loosened the roots the way you'd loosen a tooth, and when the memory finally let go, he caught it whole and folded it small.

Maren blinked. Something behind her eyes went quiet — not empty, exactly. Smoothed over. She'd remember that Tomas had died. She'd remember the fact the way you remember a number from a ledger. What she wouldn't have was the rain, the cold hand, the three years of waking up reaching for him.

"Better?" Felix asked.

Maren smiled, and it was the worst thing he saw all day, because it was genuine. "It's strange," she said. "I feel lighter. Is that awful? Should I feel worse?"

"You should feel however you paid to feel." He squeezed her hand once and let go. "You'll sleep tonight. Come back in a month if it starts leaking."

She paid him eleven coppers and a stub of beeswax candle, because Vell's widows were poor and Felix's rates were negotiable, and she left through the shop's front door into the gray drizzle of the Tallow District without once looking back at the chair where her husband used to sit.

Felix sat alone with the memory for a moment, feeling it press against the inside of his mind like a moth against glass. This was the part nobody warned you about. Holding someone's grief was like carrying a wet coat — you felt every stitch of it until you could get it bottled.

He reached under the counter of his stall — nominally a charm-seller's stall, all dried lavender and knotted red thread, the kind of harmless folk-magic the witch-finders tolerated because it was fraud — and drew out a stoppered vial of smoked glass. He breathed out slow, and poured.

The memory went down into the glass like ink into water: gray rain, black coffin, a smear of a man's face that only Maren would have known was beautiful. Felix corked it, shook it once, and watched the colors settle. On the shelf behind him were forty others. A child's fear of the dark, sold by a mother who couldn't afford to soothe it. A drunkard's last binge. A young man's shame over a girl named Pell. A soldier's entire recollection of a battle at some ford whose name Felix had never bothered to learn.

None of it was real magic, officially. Officially, memory-work didn't exist in Vell, had never existed, because the Edict of Unmaking said that any soul who touched another mind without sanction would be *unmade* — which wasn't execution, exactly. Execution left a body. Unmaking left a space shaped like a person, and everyone who loved them slowly forgetting why the space hurt.

Officially, Felix sold charms against headache.

Unofficially, half the Tallow District knew that if you couldn't afford to keep a memory, Felix Yarrow could take it off your hands. And somewhere up the chain — through fences Felix never met, through buyers Felix never saw — those memories became coin, and Felix ate on the proceeds, and told himself every night that what he did was mercy.

Most nights he almost believed it.

* * *

The night market opened at dusk along the drowned canal, lanterns strung between leaning houses, vendors selling eel pie and smuggled pepper and charms that actually worked, if you knew which stalls. Felix set up his little table of lavender and thread and waited for the evening's trade.

It came at the wrong time, from the wrong direction, wearing the wrong face.

The stranger came out of the crowd sideways, the way a man moves when his body has stopped taking instructions. He was tall, gray-cloaked, and there was blood on his lips already, which was never a good sign in a city where the witch-finders preferred their work done quietly. He made it three more steps and then his knees quit, and he went down against Felix's stall hard enough to send lavender sachets spinning into the mud.

Felix was moving before he decided to move — catching the man's shoulder, easing him down behind the stall where the lantern light didn't reach. Up close, the stranger's eyes were wrong. Not injured-wrong. *Burned*-wrong, like a hearth raked down to coals.

"They're coming," the man rasped. His hand found Felix's wrist with shocking strength. "Listen. There's no time to explain, so I'll say it once and you'll hate me for it."

"I already hate you for the lavender," Felix said. "Let go, friend. Find a surgeon."

"There are no surgeons for this." The man fumbled inside his cloak and brought out a folded square of something that was neither paper nor cloth. It was warm. Felix felt the warmth through his glove, and then through his skin, and then somewhere deeper than skin. "I'm Corvane. Royal archivist. Was. The map shows the binding — the sigils, the seams, the way into the king's—"

"Stop." Felix's voice dropped flat and fast. "Do you know what you're saying? Do you know what I do to people who say words like that near my stall?"

"I know exactly what you do." Corvane smiled, and there was blood in his teeth. "That's why I found you. Thief-taker, memory-man, nobody. You're the only man in Vell who can carry this without the Crown knowing it exists." He pressed the folded thing into Felix's palm and closed Felix's fingers over it, one by one, deliberate as a priest. "It burns when they come close. It will teach you, if you let it. And Felix Yarrow—" his grip tightened, coal-bright eyes fixing on Felix's with an urgency that had gone past fear into something like pity, "—the crown eats. It eats, and it is still hungry, and fifteen years ago somebody fed it."

The market noise went distant. Felix heard his own heartbeat instead.

"Fifteen years ago," he repeated. "What happened fifteen years ago?"

But Corvane's eyes had rolled back, and his hand slid from Felix's wrist, and when Felix got two fingers against the man's throat there was nothing there but the slowing tick of a body finishing its business.

* * *

Felix did what any sensible criminal does with a corpse: he made it someone else's problem. Three coppers bought Old Nan Tetch and her handcart; the archivist went into the canal weighted with eel-bricks, and by midnight the river owned him and Felix owned a folded square of burning warmth that he very much wanted to throw after it.

He didn't throw it. That was the trouble with him. Curiosity was the one theft he'd never managed to quit.

In his room above the stall, shutters drawn, single candle lit, he unfolded the thing at last. It wasn't paper. It was skin — some tanned hide, fine as vellum, and the joke of that landed sour. Across it ran lines of silvered ink that moved when he wasn't looking directly at them, a labyrinth of sigils branching out from a central mark like veins from a heart.

And the mark was warm. Warm and getting warmer, and when he leaned close he understood why: the map was *listening*. Somewhere in its weave it could feel the minds around it, and it was cataloguing his the way he'd catalogued Maren Ostwick's grief — gently, thoroughly, without asking.

"Absolutely not," Felix told it. "You don't get to browse."

The warmth pulsed, almost amused.

He should burn it. He should drop it in the stove right now and go back to bottling widows' sorrows and forget the words *royal archivist* and *the crown eats* entirely. That was the smart play. That was the play that kept a man unmade-adjacent rather than unmade.

Instead he looked at the first sigil — the outermost one, the gate, the place where the labyrinth began — and his breath stopped in his chest.

He knew it.

Not from books. Felix Yarrow had never been allowed near books. He knew it from *work*, from the inside of a paying customer's head, fifteen years ago, when he'd been nineteen and greedy and hired through a cutout to lift something enormous from a mind he'd never been shown the face of. The buyer had paid in gold, triple rate, no questions. The memory had been vast and cold and full of that same sigil, that exact curling line, and Felix had carried it out of a stranger's skull and handed it over and never once asked what he'd stolen or from whom.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the third kind, the one the witch-finders rang when they wanted the city to know they were hunting.

Felix folded the map small, tucked it against his ribs, and pinched out the candle.

*Somebody fed it,* Corvane had said.

Felix stood in the dark with his heart hammering and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own trap spring, that the hunt was not for the archivist anymore.

It was for whoever came next.
