## 2. What the Witch-Finders Burn

The witch-finders came at the hour when the night market was at its fattest, when the lantern-oil burned sweet and the pickpockets were fat with other people's coin, and they came the way fire comes to a dry field.

Felix heard it before he understood it. A hush rolling down Lantern Row like a wave pulling back from a shore — vendors killing their lamps, shutters slamming, the particular silence of people who had learned that running early meant living long. Then the sound underneath the silence: boots. Dozens of them. Iron-shod, marching in step, which no one in Vell did except the Crown.

He was mid-sale. Widow Maren stood across his stall counter, her hands folded over her apron, waiting while he finished corking the little blue vial of grief he'd lifted from her an hour before. She'd asked for it back softened, diluted, so she could visit her husband's grave without drowning. Honest work, as honest as anything got in Vell.

"Go," Felix said quietly. "Now. Through the fishmonger's arch, don't look back."

Maren's face did something complicated — fear for him, gratitude, the guilt of the spared — and then she was gone, swallowed by the crowd flowing the other way, all of them moving like water finding cracks.

Felix killed his own lamp and reached under the counter for the stranger's satchel.

The dying man's blood had soaked through the canvas overnight where Felix had shoved it beneath a loose floorboard, and when he pulled it out the smell hit him first: copper and something else, something burnt, like paper held too long to a flame. The map inside was still warm. It had been warm since the courier pressed it into his hands and whispered *the crown eats* and died with his fingers curled around Felix's wrist like a man passing on a debt rather than a gift.

Felix had spent a sleepless night telling himself he'd sell it. Some collector would pay a fortune for royal sigil-work, even incomplete, even cursed. That was the sensible thing. That was the Yarrow way: take what shines, sell it fast, never hold what holds you.

Then the witch-finders turned onto Lantern Row, and holding it stopped being a choice.

They came in their grey lacquered masks, six abreast, each carrying a censer that swung from a chain and trailed smoke that didn't rise — smoke that fell, pooling along the ground like fog with intent. Nullsmoke. Felix had seen it used once, years ago, on a hedge-witch who'd been curing fevers in the Tannery Quarter. They'd made the whole street watch while it unmade her. Not killed her. *Unmade* her — unwrote her, until there was a smear of nothing shaped vaguely like a person and then not even that.

The lead finder stopped at the mouth of the row and raised one gauntleted hand. The censers swung wider. Somewhere behind Felix, a vendor screamed as the smoke touched his wares — charms, harmless ones, bottled memories of summer days — and the bottles simply ceased, glass and memory and the shelf they sat on, gone without even ash.

They weren't searching the market. They were erasing it, row by row, working toward him.

Felix ran.

* * *

The undercity of Vell was older than the city above it, older than the Crown, older maybe than the king himself, though Felix doubted anyone alive knew for certain. It was a sprawl of drowned cellars and collapsed aqueducts and smugglers' galleries stitched together by three centuries of thieves, and every thief worth the name knew at least two ways into it and only one way out again if the finders followed.

Felix took the trapdoor behind the tallow-chandler's stall, dropped eight feet into blackness, and landed in water ankle-deep that smelled of rot and old candles. Behind him, muffled through stone, the market died. He could hear the nullsmoke working — a sound like a page being torn very slowly, over and over.

"They'll scent you." The voice came from the dark ahead, low and dry as old rope. "Nullsmoke follows memory, boy. You're carrying enough stolen years to light up like a festival lantern."

A shutter-lamp clicked open. Wren crouched on a broken aqueduct lip above the waterline, ancient and bird-boned, wrapped in three coats despite the season. She'd been lifting keys off nobles' belts since before Felix was born, and she owed Mother Quill debts that kept her stationed at this entrance most nights, keeping watch and charging tolls to anyone less guild-connected than Felix.

"Wren. I need the Long Gallery and I need it now."

"I need a new hip and a barrel of brandywine. We're both disappointed." But she was already moving, scuttling along the aqueduct with surprising speed, and Felix splashed after her. "Finders don't come down here," she said over her shoulder. "Too many memories soaked into the stone. Their own smoke goes mad in these tunnels — starts eating *them*. So they burn the entrances instead and wait for us to starve. Which means, little Yarrow, that whatever you've got in that bag, they intend to seal the whole undercity to get it."

Felix's stomach dropped. "It's just a map."

Wren stopped. Turned. In the lamplight her eyes were wet and bright and terribly old.

"Felix Yarrow. I've known you since you were a gutter-rat small enough to fit in a bread oven. You lie beautifully, but you've never once lied to me." She held out her hand. "Show me what makes the Crown burn its own market."

He should have refused. Every instinct bred into him by fifteen years of the guild said refuse, deflect, survive alone. Instead — because Wren had fed him when Mother Quill was still deciding whether he was worth feeding — he opened the satchel and unrolled the map across a dry stretch of stone.

The sigil-map glowed faintly in the dark, inked lines shifting like something alive beneath the vellum. It showed the palace. Not the palace as it stood — the palace as it was *underneath*, layer after layer, rooms that existed and rooms that had been forgotten, and threaded through all of it, a lattice of sigils converging on the throne room like veins around a heart.

And at the outermost edge, the first sigil — the gate, the entry point, marked in the archivist's cramped hand with a single annotation:

*Unsealed here. Fifteen years past.*

Felix stared at it and felt the water cold around his ankles turn to ice in his veins.

He knew that sigil. Not from study. From *work*.

He'd been seventeen, hungry, ambitious, trying to buy his way into Mother Quill's real confidence. A buyer had come through intermediaries — anonymous, generous, asking for something specific: the oldest memories of a man in high places. Royal-adjacent. Felix hadn't asked questions; asking questions was how thieves died. He'd gone in through a servant's postern, found the sleeping man, and taken. Childhood. First snow. A mother's face. Whole seasons of a life, decanted into vials, sold for more coin than he'd ever held.

The payment had come with a diagram. Instructions for the extraction site. A sigil to mark where the memories had been cut away.

It was the same sigil. To the last curve and crossbar.

"Felix." Wren's voice seemed to come from very far away. "What is it?"

"The man I stole from," Felix heard himself say. His voice sounded strange, flattened, like someone else's. "Fifteen years ago. This is my work. My mark. Whoever drew this map drew it *around* something I did."

Above them, faint through the stone, came a new sound: the grinding roar of stone collapsing. The finders, sealing the entrances. Sealing him in.

Wren looked up at the noise, then back at the map, and her old face went grim. "Long Gallery's still open. Dane keeps a crew at the far end — you can cut through to the river stairs." She pressed her lamp into Felix's hand. "Whatever you stole, boy, somebody wanted it stolen badly enough to burn a city getting it back. Go. And Felix—" she caught his sleeve, "—don't trust the guild with that. Not even Mother Quill. *Especially* not Mother Quill."

He wanted to ask what she meant. The ceiling shuddered, raining grit into the black water, and there was no time for anything but running.

* * *

The Long Gallery stretched half a mile through the bones of old Vell, a vaulted corridor of pre-Crown masonry where the smugglers' lamps burned green and nobody asked anybody's business. Felix ran it with the map against his chest and Wren's warning ringing in his ears, and it wasn't until he saw the torchlight ahead — four figures, guild leathers, familiar silhouettes — that he let himself slow.

Dane stepped out of the torchlight with his arms crossed and his easy smile in place, the smile that had gotten them both out of a hundred scrapes, the smile Felix had trusted since they were boys sharing a stolen loaf in Mother Quill's kitchen.

"There he is," Dane said warmly. "The whole market's ash and the finders are sealing tunnels, and here's our Felix, strolling out of the dark with a royal satchel full of something that smells like blood and burning." His eyes flicked to the satchel. Stayed there. "Mother wants you home, brother. She wants *everything* you're carrying brought home."

Something cold walked up Felix's spine. Wren's voice: *not even Mother Quill. Especially not Mother Quill.*

"Since when does the guild collect for the Crown?" Felix asked lightly.

Dane's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a shutter closing. "Since the pay got better than you could imagine. Come on, Felix. Hand it over, and we'll laugh about this over ale by morning."

Behind Dane, the other three shifted. Not casually. Felix had grown up reading bodies the way scholars read books, and those bodies were saying *we're not letting you walk past*.

The dying courier's whisper rose in Felix's memory: *the crown eats.* And under it, newer, colder, the shape of a thought assembling itself: the anonymous buyer. The generous pay. The guild's sudden comfort, these past years, its reach growing while other crews vanished. What if the guild hadn't been lucky? What if it had been *fed*?

"You first," Felix said, and threw Wren's lamp at the nearest torch.

Glass burst; oil flared; darkness rushed in. Felix dove left, into a side gallery he'd mapped as a child during hide-and-seek games that Mother Quill had secretly designed as escape drills, and ran blind through passages his feet remembered better than his head. Behind him, Dane's voice echoed off the stones, still warm, still smiling, and somehow that was worse than any threat:

"You can't run forever, brother! Everything comes home to Mother eventually!"

Felix climbed out through a coal chute into the rain-slick alleys of the Tannery Quarter, lungs burning, and huddled in the shadow of a rendering shed to catch his breath. The map, he saw with numb clarity, had soaked through its wrapping and was pressed warm against his skin.

By the light of a guttering match, he forced himself to look at it properly for the first time. Really look.

The sigils weren't a defense. He'd assumed a map like this would show wards, protections, ways to lock a king safe. But the annotations told another story entirely — a record of damage. Each sigil marked a wound in the king's mind, a place where memory had been cut away, and beside each wound, in the archivist's increasingly frantic hand, notes on what had flowed in through the gap.

*It entered where the memories were removed,* the final note read. *It has been eating from the throne for fifteen years. The crown eats. God forgive us, we paid for the knife.*

We. *We.*

Felix sat in the dark with the rain coming down and understood, finally, why the witch-finders were burning markets to find a dead man's map. Not because the Crown feared rebellion.

Because the Crown — or the thing wearing it — feared being *unbound*.

And because somewhere in that map's glowing lattice was a sigil drawn in a seventeen-year-old thief's hand, the first cut, the open door, and the archivist had traced it faithfully to the end of his life.

Felix Yarrow had spent fifteen years telling himself he stole small things. Griefs. Summers. Old loves that owners wanted rid of anyway.

He looked at the map, and the map looked back, and for the first time in his life he wondered what else he'd sold without knowing — and who had bought it.

There was one person in Vell who might know. One person whose records went back further than the guild's, further than the Crown allowed anyone's to go. A disgraced inquisitor named Sera Vane, who had burned the only father Felix ever loved, and who, rumor said, had been asking questions about the king for years.

Felix hated her. He intended to keep hating her.

He got up out of the rain and went to find her anyway.
