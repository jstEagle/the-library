## 3. An Inquisitor's Confession

The rain in Vell always smelled of ash. Sera Vane had stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing a scar.

She stood across the street from a shuttered dyehouse in Lowmarsh, watching the door Felix Yarrow had slipped through twenty minutes earlier, and she let herself notice everything else instead: the way the water ran orange off the roofline, the way two of Mother Quill's pickpockets worked the crowd beneath a shared umbrella, the way her own hands would not stop shaking, no matter how many times she told them to.

Twelve years an inquisitor. Three years since they'd taken the title away. Her hands had never shaken during a burning.

She'd found him easily enough — that was the humiliating part. The Crown's witch-finders had torn through the night market with fire and iron and gotten nothing but smoke. Sera had gone to a moneylender, paid triple for one name, and been handed Felix Yarrow's whole geography inside an hour: where he slept, where he drank, where he bottled other people's lives and sold them back as trinkets. The witch-finders didn't fail because they were incompetent. They failed because they were looking for a fugitive, and Sera was looking for something else entirely.

She crossed the street. The pickpockets marked her — of course they did; she still walked like a woman with authority, which was its own kind of confession in this city — but she let them see her coming. A hidden blade would have ended this before it started.

The dyehouse door was unlocked. That was either an invitation or a trap, and after three years of disgrace Sera had learned there was rarely a difference.

Inside, vats of dead dye sat crusted under a skylight the color of dirty wool. Felix Yarrow stood among them with his back to her, and even from behind he looked exactly like what he was: thin, quick, a boy who had grown up learning that stillness was the first thing stolen from you.

"You're not witch-finder," he said, without turning. "They knock doors down."

"Inquisitor," Sera said. "Formerly."

That got him to turn. He looked at her face and she watched him do the arithmetic — the gray braid, the burned-out left eye, the scar tissue climbing her throat like ivy. People who'd lived through the purges knew that face. People who'd lost someone to it knew it better.

"You're Vane," he said. His voice went flat and careful, the way a floor goes flat and careful over a rotten joist. "You burned Master Corvus."

"Yes."

"He taught me everything I know about keeping my hands clean."

"I know," Sera said. "I've read your file."

"My file." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "I have a file."

"Everyone has a file. Yours is thinner than most. You're careful." She took a step into the room, and he shifted his weight, angling toward the far door without seeming to. Good instincts. Wasted on small crimes. "I'm not here to arrest you, Yarrow. If I wanted you in irons, you'd be in irons."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because fifteen years ago, the king changed." She said it plainly, because plainness was all she had left to offer. "And my own records prove it, and no one above me will read them, and the only person alive who might understand how it happened is standing in front of me smelling of dye and fear."

* * *

He made her talk first. She'd expected that.

They ended up sitting on opposite sides of an empty vat, which suited her fine — a barrier between them felt honest. Rain ticked against the skylight. Somewhere below, the river moved through its culverts with a sound like slow swallowing.

"Start at the beginning," Felix said. "Not yours. The king's."

Sera unfolded the leather folio she'd carried for three years, through disgrace and exile and the long gray afterward, and laid it open between them. Reports. Testimonies. Her own handwriting, younger and more certain.

"Aldric IV was crowned at nineteen. For the first six years of his reign, he was — " she searched for the word and hated that she had to search — "ordinary. Kind, occasionally. Petty, often. He argued with his councilors. He remembered his servants' names. He once spent an entire audience listening to a fisherman complain about eel taxes, and then actually did something about it. I have the transcript. It's boring. It's gloriously, humanly boring."

"And then?"

"Fifteen years ago, in the space of about four months, all of that stops. The transcripts change. Not dramatically — that's what took me so long to see it. He doesn't become a monster. He becomes *smooth*. Every answer perfect, every judgment consistent, every memory of every meeting flawless. No king is flawless, Yarrow. Kings misremember. This one never does. He remembers everything, perfectly, forever — except anything before his thirty-first year. Ask him about his childhood and he gives you a beautiful answer with nothing underneath it. I've watched him do it. There's a half-second delay, like a man reciting someone else's letters."

Felix had gone very still. Stillness, Sera thought, could mean many things in a thief. Guilt was one of them.

"The council calls it wisdom," she continued. "The priests call it grace. The witch-finders call it proof that the old king's weakness has been scoured away. And when I brought my analysis to my superiors — transcripts, patterns, dates — I was told I was seeing shadows. Then I was told it again, louder. Then I was given a choice: a posting to the salt marshes or a quiet resignation. I chose resignation. Then I kept reading. That's my real crime. I kept reading."

"Why tell me?" Felix said. "Why not the guilds, the temples, anyone?"

"Because of this." She turned to the last page in the folio — not a report but a ledger fragment, copied in haste from the royal archive before her access was revoked. "Four months before the king changed, the palace's memory-wards were inspected and certified intact. Four months *after*, they were re-inspected, and the inspector noted — his exact words — 'an absence shaped like a theft.' Someone had drawn memories out of the king's mind through the wards without breaking them. Professional work. The kind of work that leaves no scar. The kind of work only one discipline in Vell knows how to do."

The silence stretched. Rain filled it.

"You think I did it," Felix said quietly. "When I was — what, ten years old? Eleven? Walk into the royal palace, slip past the king's wards, and rob Aldric IV blind."

"No," Sera said. "I think you were a child, and children don't do professional work. But whoever bought that theft — whoever commissioned it, whoever paid — used a broker. And brokers use thieves. And you've spent your whole life selling memories, which means somewhere in that clever head of yours is a map of everyone in this city who ever wanted a piece of a king."

"That's not evidence. That's a guess wearing a uniform."

"It's a thread," Sera said. "And I have spent three years pulling threads alone in the dark, and I am telling you, Yarrow, there is something on the other end of this one, and it is *hungry*."

* * *

He didn't believe her. She hadn't expected him to. Belief was a luxury; what she needed was his fear, and fear arrived on schedule when she told him about the courier.

"The man who gave you the map," she said. "Ostrevant. Royal archivist, second rank. He came to me eight days ago."

Felix's head came up sharply. "Came to you."

"At night, through the servant door of my lodgings, like a man who'd learned which doors were safe. He said the archive had been ordered destroyed — the historical wing, the pre-reign records, everything touching the king's early life. He said the order came with the king's seal but not, he thought, the king's hand. He said—" and here Sera paused, because the words still sat wrong in her mouth, heavy as wet sand— "he said 'the crown eats.' Those words. Then he asked me if I knew any thieves."

Felix stared at her. "And you sent him to the night market."

"I sent him to a name. I didn't know the night market would burn two nights later. I didn't know the witch-finders would kill four people looking for him." Her voice stayed level. It cost more than it used to. "If I had known—"

"You'd have done it anyway," Felix said. "You people always do it anyway. That's the whole trade, isn't it? Burn now, grieve later, call it duty."

The words landed where they were meant to. Sera let them.

"You want to hate me," she said. "Go ahead. But do it accurately. So — accurately: yes, I burned Master Corvus. Six years ago. On charges of teaching forbidden craft to minors. The testimony was false. I knew it was false within the year, when the witness recanted and was reassigned so fast it left a wind. I filed an objection. My objection disappeared. Then my career disappeared. Then I understood that the case had never really been about Corvus at all — it was about what Corvus *knew*. He'd been court memory-smith before he fled to the low city. He'd worked the palace wards. Whatever hole was cut in the king's mind fifteen years ago, Corvus may have been one of the few men alive who could see its shape."

Felix had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of that. Sera watched him start again, deliberately, like a man surfacing.

"He never told me," Felix said. "Six years I — he took me in. After my mother. He taught me the craft, the ethics of it, such as they were. Never take what can't be spared. Never sell grief to the grieving. He never once mentioned the palace."

"Because mentioning the palace is how you die," Sera said. "Or worse. Because whatever he saw in those wards, he decided the safest place for it was nowhere — not written, not spoken, not even hinted at to the boy he loved. And I put him on a pyre anyway, on a lie, serving a thing I didn't know I was serving." She closed the folio. Her hands were steady now; they always steadied after confession, like a fever breaking. "So understand what I'm offering. Not forgiveness. There isn't any. An alliance. Two people who owe the same dead man the same debt."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I walk out that door, and within a day the witch-finders find you, because they're better resourced than I am and they have one advantage I lack." She held his gaze. "They don't care whether you're guilty. I need you to be innocent, Yarrow, or at least ignorant, because if a child's theft opened the king's mind to whatever wears it now, then the trail runs through the thieves' guild — through Mother Quill, through your brother Dane, through everyone who ever brokered a memory upward toward the palace. And they will burn you to reach it, and they will burn the truth with you, and in fifteen years some other disgraced fool will sit in the ash trying to read the transcripts."

Felix looked down at the folio between them, at her young handwriting, at fifteen years of a king being slowly eaten in plain sight while everyone called it grace.

"You said Ostrevant asked if you knew thieves," he said at last. "Before the market. Before the map. Which means the map wasn't random. He chose me."

"He chose the student of the man who built the wards," Sera said. "Yes. I think the archivist knew exactly whose hands he was dying into. I think Corvus taught you more than ethics, Felix. I think somewhere in that head of yours is everything he couldn't afford to say out loud."

Something moved behind Felix's eyes — not fear this time, or not only. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man remembering a lesson he'd been taught so long ago he'd mistaken it for common sense.

"The map," he said slowly. "The first sigil. I've seen it before. Years ago. In a memory I—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "In a memory I sold. A long time ago. To a buyer I never saw twice."

The rain thickened against the skylight. Sera felt the thread go taut between them, the one she'd been pulling for three years, and knew — with the cold certainty of a woman who had burned one innocent man already — that it led somewhere neither of them was ready to follow.

"Then we start there," she said. "With the sale. With the buyer. With the night you may have sold the king his ghost."

Felix looked at her — at the scar, the ruined eye, the folio full of her sins — and nodded once, like a man stepping off a ledge.

"There's a problem," he said. "The only person who keeps the guild's brokerage ledger is Mother Quill. And to ask her about a fifteen-year-old sale, I'd have to go home."
