## 5. Fifteen Years of Stolen Winter

The archive beneath the palace had drowned once, Sera knew. Old records said the river Vell had broken through in King Aldric's grandfather's reign, and rather than drain it, the Crown had simply sealed the flooded halls and built upward. Now the water was gone — siphoned away by some forgotten engineering — but the memory of it remained in everything: the warped shelves, the swollen doors that no longer closed, the smell of rot and river-silt baked into stone.

It was, she thought, a fitting place to learn how the kingdom had rotted from inside.

She knelt over the sigil-map by lamplight, her fingers hovering above the parchment without touching it. The courier's blood had dried black along one edge. The map itself was not inked so much as *grown* — the lines of it shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them, sigils rearranging like ants disturbed under a stone. Royal archive work. Pre-Unification craft. She hadn't seen its like since before her fall.

"Talk to me," she murmured to it. "You got a man killed getting out of that palace. The least you can do is be legible."

Behind her, Felix Yarrow sat on an overturned shelf with his elbows on his knees and the particular stillness of a man trying very hard not to think. He'd been quiet since they'd come down through the collapsed service tunnel — quieter than the night market, quieter than the escape, quieter even than after her confession on the rooftop two nights past. She'd told him about Master Corvane then. About the false charges, the pyre, the way she'd signed the order with a steady hand because a steady hand was what the job required. He hadn't forgiven her. She hadn't asked him to. But he'd stayed, which was more than she deserved, and now his silence had changed texture into something she recognized from interrogations: the silence of a man circling a truth he didn't want to land on.

"The third ring," she said, mostly to fill the air. "That's the king's mind. Look." She traced the air above the map's outermost band of sigils — wards, seals, the architecture of a protected memory. "This isn't a map of the palace. It's a map of *Aldric*. Whoever drew it charted him the way you'd chart a fortress. Gates, walls, and here—" her finger stopped over a ragged gap in the third ring, a hole in the sigil-work where the lines simply ended, frayed like torn cloth, "—a breach."

Felix didn't answer.

"Fifteen years ago," Sera continued, keeping her voice level, clinical, because if she let it soften she'd lose him entirely, "the royal physicians recorded that His Majesty suffered a fever. Memory loss. Gaps, they called them. Charming word for it. The archive has the treatment logs — I pulled them before I was exiled. Eleven sessions of extraction therapy, all signed off by a memory-worker whose name was redacted from every page."

Still nothing. But she could feel him listening the way you feel a wire pull taut.

"The thing wearing the crown didn't fight its way in," she said. "There's no battle here, no siege. There's a door, and someone opened it, and something walked through. It's been living in the gap ever since, growing into the space the way water grows into a cracked foundation."

"How big a gap?" Felix asked. His voice came out wrong. Flat.

Sera turned. In the lamplight his face looked younger than it had any right to, all sharp angles and shadows, and there was something in his expression she'd only ever seen on the condemned: the look of a man who already knows the verdict and is waiting for the reading.

"Why do you ask it like that?"

"Because I know what extraction gaps look like." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, just air escaping a wound. "I've been selling memories since I was twelve years old, Sera. Small ones. A first kiss. A grudge somebody wanted gone. Grief, sometimes — widows pay best for grief. You bottle it, you sell it, the buyer wears it for a while and gives it back lighter. Harmless charms, that's what we called them." He rubbed his face with both hands. "But there was one job. When I was fifteen. Big money, anonymous buyer, paid through the guild. They wanted me to go into the palace during the fever outbreak — the sickness was real, half the household had it, nobody was watching anything. I was supposed to lift specific memories. Winter memories. Fifteen years' worth of winters."

The lamp flame guttered in a draft from the collapsed tunnel. Neither of them moved.

"I didn't ask who bought them," Felix said. "I never asked. That was the rule — Mother Quill's rule. Ask nothing, remember nothing, spend the coin. And the coin was *enormous*, Sera. Enough to keep the whole guild fed through the famine year. I told myself it was just work. I told myself the king wouldn't miss a few winters." His hands had started shaking; he watched them shake as though they belonged to someone else. "Eleven sessions. That matches. That's me. That's—"

He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to.

* * *

Sera gave him a moment. Then she did the cruelest kind thing available to her: she made him look at the evidence, because a truth half-seen would fester worse than a truth laid bare.

"Come here," she said. "Look at the breach."

He crossed the ruined hall like a man walking to his own trial and crouched beside her over the map. This close, the sigils seemed to writhe. She pointed to the torn gap in the third ring.

"Extraction leaves scar tissue. Any trained inquisitor can read it — that's how we caught people like you, back when catching people like you was my job." Her voice caught slightly on the last part, and she let it. "This scar is fifteen years old. It's clean work — too clean for a palace physician, honestly. Whoever did this was gifted." She met his eyes. "And the shape of the wound matches the entity's root-structure exactly. See how the dark lines grow outward from the gap? Like mold from a damp spot. It didn't break in, Felix. It moved into an empty room."

"So it's my fault." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than screaming. "Fifteen years of Aldric being eaten alive. Every decree signed by that thing. Every witch-finder raid, every burning, every family unmade — all of it traces back to a fifteen-year-old thief who needed the famine money and didn't ask questions."

"No." Sera heard her own voice harden, and was surprised by it. "No. Listen to me, because I know exactly how this thinking goes — I've *lived* how this thinking goes. Guilt wants to make you the center of the story. It wants every horror to flow from your single sin, because that's a burden you can carry alone in the dark. It flatters you, even as it crushes you." She took his wrist, felt his pulse hammering. "You opened a door. You didn't build the thing on the other side of it. Someone *bought* those memories, Felix. Someone planned this. A boy with stolen magic doesn't architect the possession of a king. There was a hand behind the coin, and that hand knew precisely what it was doing."

"Then who?"

"That," Sera said, turning back to the map, "is what the rest of these sigils are for."

Because she'd been reading while he sat drowning in guilt, and the deeper rings of the map held something else. Not the breach — the *repair*. The archive-master who'd drawn this chart hadn't merely documented the wound. He'd designed a counter-sigil, an unbinding, a way to pull the intruder out through the same hole it had entered. And at the center of the design, rendered in careful archival script, was a requirement that made her stomach drop.

She read it twice to be sure. Then a third time, hoping she'd misread the old notation.

"Sera?" Felix said. "What is it?"

"The rite works in symmetry," she said slowly. "Memory-magic always does. What was taken must be returned by the same channel it left through. Same hands. Same gift. Same—" She stopped. Started again. "The stolen winters have to go back the way they came out. Through the thief who took them."

"Fine. So I give them back. I still have the buyer's payment ledger somewhere, I can find copies of what I lifted—"

"You don't understand." She made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness and because saying it aloud would make her face it too. "Memories don't travel empty-handed. To carry fifteen years of a king's life, the vessel has to hold something of equal weight. The rite doesn't have room for both. The channel fills completely." She looked at him, and hated every word. "When you return his winters, Felix, everything you are goes into the vessel instead. Your memories. All of them. Permanently."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life.

Felix stared at the map. At the breach. At the elegant, patient, monstrous architecture of a trap that had been set fifteen years ago by someone who understood exactly what a desperate boy would do for coin — and what the repair would cost.

"Anonymous buyer," he said at last, very quietly. "Paid through Mother Quill. Which means the guild knows who it was." He stood, and something in his face had changed — the guilt crystallizing, hardening, transmuting into the only form of it that was useful. "And Dane keeps the guild's deep ledgers."

Sera rose beside him, one hand going to the knife at her belt out of pure old habit. Going back meant the guildhouse. It meant Mother Quill, who had raised him and now served the thing in the crown. It meant Dane, who had marked him for death.

It meant walking into the mouth of the enemy to learn the name of the hand behind the coin.

"Felix," she said. "We don't have to do this tonight."

"We have until the coronation anniversary," he answered, already moving toward the collapsed tunnel, toward the surface, toward the city that wanted him dead. "And I want a name, Sera. Before it takes anything else from me — I want to know whose hand opened the door."

Above them, far overhead, the bells of the palace began to toll the hour — and beneath their bronze voices, faint as a whisper through stone, Sera thought she heard something else. Something vast and patient, turning over in its sleep.

It knew they'd found the map.

It knew they were coming.
