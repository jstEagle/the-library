## 6. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The invitation came to us, which was the first wrong thing.

Sera held it up to the lamplight in our safehouse above the tannery row, turning it so the wax seal caught the flame. Gold paper. Heavy stock. A mask pressed into the wax — the crowned sun of House Aldric.

"Nobody invited us," I said. "We're fugitives."

"Exactly." She broke the seal with her thumb. "Someone wants you at the Anniversary Masquerade. Someone who knows you're alive, knows where to reach you, and isn't telling the witch-finders." She read aloud: *'The King requests the pleasure of the bearer's company.'* "It doesn't say bearer of what."

"That's not creepy at all."

"Felix." Sera set the invitation down and looked at me the way she looked at crime scenes — like something had already gone wrong and she was just cataloguing it. "Fifteen years ago, someone paid a boy to steal from the royal mind. Whoever sent this either did that or knows who did. Either way, they've been patient for fifteen years. People that patient don't send invitations for no reason."

I should have said no. I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep of my stupid decisions: *he considered refusing.*

But the map against my chest had been warm all week, warmer each day, like a coal wrapped in silk. And under the warmth there was a pull, faint as a fisherman's line, drawing me toward the palace district every time I closed my eyes. The entity wearing Aldric's crown knew I had the map. It had known since the courier died in my arms.

Sooner or later, we were going to meet.

Better on ground where ten thousand witnesses stood between me and its hands.

* * *

The Vell Grand Palace on coronation anniversary night was a candle lit inside a skull. We went through the servants' gate with forged livery and faces borrowed off two dead men whose memories I'd bought years ago — Sera wore a courtier's smile she'd clearly practiced hating, and I wore a minor merchant's forgettable features and tried not to flinch every time a guard's gaze slid past me.

Inside, the ballroom was a fever dream built by people with too much money and too little conscience. Chandeliers like frozen waterfalls. Musicians playing a waltz that never resolved, note after note, yearning without arriving. Masks everywhere — gilded birds, silver foxes, porcelain faces painted with tears that were actually pearls.

And over it all, the smell. Underneath the perfume and the candlewax, something else. Old air. The smell of a room that has been closed a very long time.

"You feel that?" Sera murmured behind her fan.

"Yeah."

"It's strongest near the throne dais. Whatever it is, it sits close to him." Her hand found my elbow, steering me along the wall. "We find the king, we confirm the map's route through the under-archive, and we leave before midnight. No heroics. You are a thief, Felix Yarrow, not a savior."

"I've been saying that for weeks. Nobody listens."

A herald's voice cut through the waltz: *"His Majesty, King Aldric the Beloved!"*

The crowd parted like water around a prow.

I'd seen the king once before, fifteen years ago, from a rooftop across the plaza — a broad man with a laugh you could hear over crowds, gold hair going grey at the temples, waving to children. The man descending the dais steps now wore that face the way a coat hangs on a hook. Same jaw. Same shoulders. But nothing moved behind the eyes except when he blinked, and even the blinking looked rehearsed.

He stopped in the center of the floor. The music softened. And then Aldric the Beloved turned his head — slowly, precisely, across three hundred masked guests — and looked directly at me.

At me. Through my stolen face. Through everything.

"Ah," said the king, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing I have ever seen, because it was *my* smile. The crooked one, the one I do when I'm about to lie. "There you are."

* * *

He crossed the ballroom toward me and the crowd made way, and nobody — not one guest, not one guard — seemed to find it strange that their king was walking straight at a minor merchant in borrowed livery. Sera melted backward into the crush; I felt her go and hated her a little for it, because she was right to go, because one of us needed to be free if this went badly.

"Your Majesty." I bowed. My mouth had gone dry as ash.

"No titles tonight." He waved a hand, gracious, easy. "Tonight we're only two old friends catching up." His voice shifted as he spoke — sliding down an octave, losing the courtly polish, until it wasn't the king's voice at all.

It was mine.

Not my voice now. My voice at nine years old. High and quick and full of gaps where teeth used to be, the voice I'd had back when Mother Quill first put a memory-bottle in my hand and said, *you've got the touch, boy, don't waste it.*

"Hello, Felix," said my childhood self, out of the king's mouth. "You got tall."

The ballroom went distant. The music thinned to thread. There was just me and the thing wearing Aldric, standing in a bubble of silence while three hundred people danced obliviously around the edge of it.

"What are you?" I managed.

"Hungry." It said it simply, without drama, the way you'd admit to being tired. "But that's not why I asked you here. I asked you here because we should talk about Tobin."

The name hit me like cold water. Master Tobin. My mentor. The man the witch-finders burned on Sera's warrant fifteen years ago — the man whose last words through the smoke had been my name.

"He's alive," the thing said, watching my face with Aldric's dead eyes. "Or rather — he could be. Memory is such a funny material, Felix. Everything anyone ever was is still in it, even after the body stops. I hold his pattern whole. Every lesson, every scolding, every time he snuck you honey-cakes after curfew and told you not to tell Quill." It tilted the king's head, birdlike. "Give me the map, and I'll give him back. Not a copy. *Him.* Walking, talking, complaining about your posture. I can build him a body the way you build a lie — carefully, and to fit."

My hand had drifted to my chest without my permission. To the map's heat through my shirt.

"Don't," I whispered. Mostly to myself.

"Why not?" Gentle now. Reasonable. My own childhood reasonableness, the tone I used on shopkeepers. "Think about what you're actually protecting. A city that outlawed you at birth? A guild that sold you upward the moment you became useful? An inquisitor who burned the only person who ever loved you?" It leaned closer, and up close Aldric smelled of that old closed-room air, of dust and stopped clocks. "I'm not your enemy, Felix. I'm the only one offering you a fair trade. Everyone else took from you for free."

Somewhere under the panic, the terrible thing happened: I thought about it. I actually thought about it. Tobin's laugh. Tobin's hands correcting my grip on a lockpick. Fifteen years of guilt with a door in it, and the door was standing open, and all I had to do was hand over a piece of vellum.

"You killed the archivist," I said hoarsely. "The courier. You hunted him through half the city."

"The witch-finders killed him. I merely wanted what he carried." A pause. "Though yes. I would have taken it from his corpse regardless. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise — you'd catch the lie. You're too good a reader of memories not to."

"And if I say no?"

The music stopped. All of it, mid-phrase, every instrument, and three hundred masked heads turned toward us in perfect unison, and beneath the masks there were no faces yet, just smooth blank porcelain waiting to be painted —

— and then the music resumed, and the faces were faces, and the king stepped back with a courteous nod as though we'd discussed the weather.

"Then I'll take the map," it said pleasantly, "and everything else you own besides. But I hoped you'd choose better than that. I've watched you for fifteen years, Felix. You always choose the thing that costs you least. It's your best quality and your worst."

Sera's hand closed on my arm — real, warm, human — and steered me backward into the crowd. Behind us, the king raised a glass to our retreating backs.

"One night, thief," it called, in my nine-year-old voice, sweet as honey-cakes. "The offer doesn't expire. But the city might."

* * *

We didn't speak until we were three rooftops away, lungs burning, the palace a glittering wound behind us. Sera pulled off her mask and threw it down between us.

"It offered you Tobin." Not a question. She'd read my face in the ballroom; of course she had.

"It said it could bring him back." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "Sera, it sounded like *me*. Like — it's been carrying my voice around for fifteen years like a keepsake. How does it have pieces of me from when I was nine?"

She went very still. "Because it's been inside the king's memory-economy since the gap opened. And the gap opened the day a boy stole from the royal mind." She said it gently, but the words landed like stones. "Felix. It didn't learn your voice from watching you. It learned it from *him*. From the memories that were torn out — the ones you sold. Somewhere in that thing is fifteen years of King Aldric's missing winter, and tangled all through it, fragments of the child who took them."

I sat down hard on the rooftop tiles. Below us, Vell glittered, ten thousand windows, ten thousand minds, all of them on the menu.

"It knew exactly what to offer me," I said. "Down to the honey-cakes."

"Of course it did." Sera crouched beside me, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked afraid — not of the entity, but for me. "That's what makes it worse than any witch-finder, Felix. Witch-finders burn what they fear. That thing *studies* what it wants. And right now—" she hesitated, then finished anyway, "—right now it wants you more than the map. Because a thief who chooses to open the door is worth ten thousand who have it forced on them."

I stared at her. "What do you mean, forced on them?"

Sera's face went pale in the moonlight. "Tomorrow morning," she said, "every bell in Vell rings the anniversary. Ten thousand people in the coronation square. And I just realized what the entity needs them all in one place for."

Far below, deep in the palace, something began — very softly — to hum.
