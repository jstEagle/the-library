## 7. Ashes of the Mentor

Sera had spent eleven years learning to read the architecture of lies, and the entity's offer was a cathedral of them.

*Your mentor, alive. The map, and he walks out of the dark.* It had spoken through the king's mouth at the masquerade with Felix's own childhood voice, sweet as stolen honey, and she had watched Felix's face do something terrible and hopeful all at once. She'd dragged him out before he could answer. Now, in the safehouse above the tannery row, she watched him not sleep.

He sat by the shuttered window with the map unrolled across his knees, tracing sigils he could no longer pretend he didn't recognize. "It said he's alive," Felix said, for the fourth time since midnight.

"It said what you wanted to hear."

"You don't know that."

"I know exactly that." Sera crossed the room and knelt so their eyes were level. She kept her voice flat, inquisitor-flat, the tone she'd used on a hundred witnesses. "I burned Corvus Ash on the pyre steps myself. I stood close enough to smell it. Whatever is offering you his life, it's selling you a memory it manufactured. That's what they do. That's what *it* does. It eats what people were and wears the shape."

Felix's jaw worked. "Then prove it," he said finally. "Prove he's dead. Prove I'm wrong. Because if you can't—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. If she couldn't, then the deal was still on the table, and every hour Felix carried the map was an hour the entity whispered through the walls of him.

So Sera did the one thing she'd sworn never to do again.

She went back inside the guild.

* * *

Mother Quill's vault sat beneath the Drowned Chapel, behind a door that answered only to blood older than its opener. Sera knew about it because fifteen years ago she'd built the case file that mentioned it — a thieves' cache where the guild stored its most dangerous inventory: memories too valuable to sell on any open market. Regicide confessions. Royal indiscretions. And, if the archivist's notes were right, anything the entity's agents wanted preserved rather than consumed.

They went in through the chapel's flooded crypt at low tide, Felix leading, Sera following with a hand-lamp turned low. The water came up to their shins and smelled of centuries. Felix moved through the dark like a man returning to a house he'd grown up in — which, Sera supposed bitterly, he had.

"The door will know me," he murmured.

"Will it also report you?"

"That depends on whether Dane's awake."

Dane was awake.

They heard him before they saw him — the scrape of a boot on wet stone, unhurried, deliberate, a man who wanted to be heard. He stepped out of the shadow of the reliquary arch with a lantern in one hand and a hooked knife in the other, and he looked at Felix the way you look at a meal that has learned to walk.

"Little brother," Dane said. "Mother Quill's been weeping over you. Says you've forgotten whose table fed you."

"The table fed on me," Felix said quietly. "You all did. Fifteen years of my cuts going upward. I've seen the ledgers, Dane. I know where the memories go."

"And where do they go?" Dane smiled, and it was the smile from their childhood, and it was nothing like it. "Up. Everything goes up. You'll learn that again soon enough."

Sera stepped forward, lamp raised. "We're not here for you."

"No," Dane agreed pleasantly. "You're here for the dead man's jar. Go on, then. Open it. Mother Quill keeps it as a lesson." His eyes flicked to Felix, and something almost human crossed them — grief, maybe, or its counterfeit. "She keeps it to remind us what refusal costs."

The vault door drank Felix's blood from a cut thumb and swung open on shelves of glass: hundreds of jars, each glowing faintly with the color of a life. Sera had catalogued confiscated memory-charms for a decade and had never seen anything like this — not bottled grief, not petty sins, but whole *years*, dense and humming.

Felix found the jar on the third shelf. Plain glass. A wax seal stamped with a crow.

Corvus Ash's mark.

"He sealed it himself," Felix whispered. "Before—"

"Open it or don't," Dane said. "But know that whatever's in there, Mother Quill paid blood price to keep it off the thing's table. Which means it's the truest thing you'll ever hold."

Sera took the jar from Felix's shaking hands because someone had to be steady. She broke the seal.

Memory poured out like cold light.

* * *

A workshop. Rain on glass. Corvus Ash, younger than Sera had ever known him, sitting across from a boy of perhaps ten — Felix, small and sharp-eyed, watching his teacher bottle a stranger's sorrow with careful hands.

*"Rules, little thief,"* Corvus said in the memory, and his voice was exactly as Sera remembered it, which made her chest hurt. *"We take what people can spare. We never take what makes them themselves. And we never, ever sell to the tall gentleman who pays in gold that doesn't tarnish."*

The memory jumped — memories always skipped, worn smooth at the folds. Years compressed. Felix grown, sixteen, counting coin. An anonymous buyer, gold that didn't tarnish, a commission written in a fine cold hand: *the king's winter years. All of them.*

And then Corvus alone, at night, sealing this very jar. Speaking to whoever might find it.

*"If you're hearing this, I'm dead, and the boy took the commission anyway, or the boy was made to take it, and either way the door is open now. I went to the buyer's house to take the job back. What I found wearing the buyer's skin had no face worth describing. It offered me everything — my life, my boys, my guild — if I'd keep quiet and keep teaching. I told it no."*

A pause in the memory. Rain. Then, quieter:

*"I told it no because some things are load-bearing. A man's past is the floor he stands on. Take it and he falls into whatever's waiting underneath. I chose the fire. I want it known I chose it. And I want the boy to know — Felix, if it's you — I never blamed you. You were a child with empty pockets and a buyer with a full purse. The sin belongs to the hand that paid, not the hand that carried. Finish it. Unbind him. And when you can't remember why you loved this work, remember the widow who slept better because we took her worst night away. That was real. That was always real."*

The light guttered out.

Sera came back to herself kneeling on wet stone, the empty jar in her lap, tears freezing on her face in the crypt-cold. Across from her, Felix made a sound she'd never heard a grown man make — small, cracked, the sound of a boy finding out his father had died protecting him from his own crime.

"He chose it," Felix said. "He *chose* it. All these years I thought—"

"I know what you thought." She knew, because she'd let him think it. Her case file had said *resisted arrest, died in the burning* — tidy words that hid her own orders, her own doubt, the false charges she'd signed because refusing meant joining Corvus on the pyre. "I'm sorry, Felix. For all of it. For the file. For the fire. For fifteen years of letting you carry a death that wasn't yours to carry."

He looked at her then, and she braced for hatred.

Instead he nodded once, slow, like a man accepting a debt he intended to pay. "Then help me finish it. Both debts. Yours and mine."

* * *

Dane was still waiting when they came out of the vault.

Of course he was. Sera should have planned for it; eleven years of inquisition work and she'd gotten soft, sentimental, standing in a crypt crying over jars. He blocked the flooded passage with the lantern held high, and behind him, out of the dark, came four more shapes with the patient stillness of guild killers.

"Mother Quill says bring the map," Dane said. "She says bring you both. But between us, little brother—" he tilted his head toward Sera, "—she only strictly needs the map-bearer breathing."

"Dane," Felix said. "Corvus refused them. Refused *everything*. Doesn't that mean anything to you?"

"It means he's ash and I'm not." But Dane's knife-hand wavered, just a fraction, just long enough.

Sera moved first.

She'd spent her career teaching witch-finders how to fight memory-thieves in tight quarters, and she put every lesson into the three seconds that followed: lamp into the nearest killer's face, shoulder into Dane's knife-arm, and a shout at Felix — *"Run, you idiot, RUN"* — as steel opened her side below the ribs. Not deep. Deep enough. She went down into the cold water and rolled, came up with her belt-knife out, and put herself between Felix and the passage like a wall made of old sin and stubbornness.

Felix didn't run. Of course he didn't. He threw the hand-lamp — shattering it, killing the light — and in the sudden black she heard bodies stumble, heard Felix grab her arm, heard his voice furious and terrified in her ear: "*Lean on me. Now.*"

They went up through the crypt on memory alone — hers, ironically, the layout of the Drowned Chapel from a raid fifteen years ago. Behind them, Dane's voice echoed off the wet stone, and it wasn't angry anymore. It was almost gentle.

"Run to the palace, then," it called. "Run to the rite. Mother Quill already sent word ahead. Every witch-finder in Vell will be waiting for you at the gates — and so will *he*. You're carrying the last piece home, little brother. He wants to thank you personally."

* * *

Sera woke on the floor of a smuggler's loft with her wound stitched by candlelight and Felix's jacket under her head. Dawn came gray through the slats. Her side throbbed with every heartbeat, a hot metronome counting down to the anniversary ceremony — two days now, maybe less.

Felix sat against the wall with the map spread out and the empty jar beside him, turning the plain glass over and over in his hands.

"She kept it as a lesson," he said. "What refusal costs." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "It cost him everything, and he'd pay it again. So." He set the jar down and pulled the map toward him, smoothing it flat with both palms. "Teach me the rite. All of it. Before they come."

Sera pushed herself upright against the pain, and found, beneath it, something she hadn't felt in fifteen years: purpose without permission. No orders. No crown. Just a dead man's wish and a living boy's resolve and her own name, finally pointed at the right target.

"There's one problem," she said. "The rite requires the thief who made the wound. That's you, Felix. But it also requires a witness bound by oath to the truth — someone the old law recognizes. Someone who once held inquisitor's authority." She met his eyes. "It requires me. And what it asks of a witness at the binding moment..."

She stopped. In the archive beneath the palace, she'd read that line a dozen times and told herself it was metaphor.

It was not metaphor.

"What does it ask?" Felix asked.

Sera looked at the map, at the final sigil — the one shaped like an open hand giving something away — and told him the truth at last.

"The witness remembers for the thief," she said. "Forever. Whatever he gives up, she carries. Both burdens. One mind."
