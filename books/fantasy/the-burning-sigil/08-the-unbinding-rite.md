## 8. The Unbinding Rite

The archive beneath the palace smelled of dust and old fire, and Felix had spent three days in it without once going outside.

He told himself it was caution. The witch-finders were still sweeping the upper city, and Dane's people had eyes at every gate. But the truth was simpler and worse: he was afraid of the sky. Every time he looked up through the broken grating at the pale morning light, he thought about how many mornings he had left to notice.

Three days, and the map had finally given up its secrets.

Sera spread the sigil-map across a reading table scarred by centuries of scholars' knives, weighting the corners with candlesticks. The vellum had stopped burning after the first night — whatever fever had lived in it had spent itself once they'd decoded the outer ring — but the ink still seemed to move when Felix wasn't looking directly at it. Sigils nested inside sigils, a grammar of unmaking written by someone who had understood the entity better than anyone alive.

"The rite has four parts," Sera said. She'd barely slept. There was a tremor in her left hand she kept trying to hide by holding chalk. "The circle, which we draw here, on consecrated ground — the anniversary ceremony is held in the Coronation Square, which was built over the old temple floor, so that's solved for us. The words, which I have." She tapped a sheaf of her own transcription. "The vessel."

"Which is me," Felix said. "I know. You've said it six times."

"And the memories themselves." She looked at him then, and Felix saw her decide not to lie to him. He'd seen that look before, from his mentor, from Mother Quill, from every person who'd ever decided he was old enough for the ugly version. "They have to be carried back by the hand that took them. That's not poetry, Felix, that's mechanics. The gap in the king was made by a thief. Only the same thief can close it. The sigils won't bind any other way."

"So I carry them into the circle. I give them back. Aldric wakes up whole, the thing starves, everyone lives happily ever after." He made himself smile. "What's the catch? There's always a catch. Memory doesn't move for free. I've built a whole life on knowing that."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. In the silence, water dripped somewhere in the dark of the archive, patient as a clock.

"You've been bottling memories for fifteen years," she said. "Tell me what happens when you pour one out."

"It empties the bottle." He said it lightly. Then he heard what he'd said, and stopped smiling.

"A memory isn't a copy," Sera said gently. "When you lift grief from a widow, you don't leave her grief behind with a note saying *had a lovely time*. It's gone from her. What you sell is the thing itself, moved. And when you return something—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it with visible effort, the inquisitor dragging herself back to the facts like a climber hauling up a rope. "When you return something, the vessel has to make room. The channel runs both ways. As the king's memories flow back into him, everything of yours that touches them will be pulled out. Everything that was shaped by them. Everything that *is* them."

Felix stared at the map. The innermost sigil — the one that matched the memory he'd sold at thirteen years old, the winter market, the anonymous buyer with the soft voice — sat at the center of the design like a heart.

"How much?" His voice came out flat. Practical. A thief pricing goods. "How much of me?"

"All of it." Sera didn't flinch, and he hated her a little for that, and loved her a little too, and didn't examine either. "Every memory that passed through your hands. Every job, every mark, every bottle you ever sold. The guild raised you on stolen recollection, Felix — your skills, your trade, half your friendships are built out of other people's pasts. The rite can't sort them. It takes the whole current. When it's done, you'll stand in that square and you won't know your own name."

* * *

He walked the archive for an hour after that, touching things.

It was a stupid ritual and he knew it, but nobody was watching except Sera, who pretended to work and watched anyway. He ran his fingers along the spines of books he couldn't read. He found a child's slate in a collapsed storeroom, some archivist's kid, decades dead, with a half-erased sum still ghosting the surface. He held it and tried to remember being a boy and couldn't isolate it — couldn't find the seam between his childhood and the thousand stolen ones layered over it, the widow's grief, the soldier's terror, the bride's joy, all of it silted into the riverbed of him.

That was the worst part, he realized. Not losing it. Not being able to tell which parts were his.

"You could walk away," Sera said from the table. "We could run. I know routes out of Vell that the Crown doesn't. We could be in Merrow by month's end and this would be someone else's problem."

"And the anniversary?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. Ten days from now, ten thousand people would pack the Coronation Square to celebrate thirty years of King Aldric's reign, and the thing wearing him would open its mouth and drink, and Vell would become a city of blank-faced wanderers who remembered nothing but their hunger. Felix had seen one mind emptied. He'd done it himself, a hundred times, one small theft at a time. He could not imagine it at scale. He could imagine it perfectly. That was the problem with being what he was.

"No," he said. "No running."

"Felix—"

"I stole the door open, Sera. Fifteen years ago, for eleven crowns and a promise of more." He turned the slate over in his hands. "Whatever's left of me when this is done, I want it to be standing in that square because I chose to be. Even if it doesn't remember choosing."

* * *

He had eight days, and he spent them saying goodbye without saying why.

He went up at night, hooded, moving through the undercity on routes only he knew, and he did small things. He paid off Maren's debt at the Drowned Bell, leaving coin with the barkeep and instructions never to say who sent it. He fixed the loose stair in the tenement on Candle Lane where the Kess family lived, the ones whose daughter he'd lifted a fever-memory from two winters back so the girl could sleep again. He stood outside the orphanage on Tanner's Row where Mother Quill had first found him, starving and sharp-eyed, and he didn't go in, because the guild's people were everywhere now and some of them wore Dane's colors. But he stood there a long time.

On the fifth day he found Dane.

Not on purpose. Dane found him — stepped out of an alley off Gull Street with two guild knives flanking him, and for a moment Felix thought *this is it, this is how it ends, beaten to death in the rain three days before I was going to die anyway.* But Dane just looked at him. Dane, who'd shared a blanket with him in the guildhouse loft when they were nine. Dane, who'd taught him to pick locks and lie to witch-finders and never, ever steal from someone poorer than you — a rule the guild had clearly forgotten before Dane had.

"You look terrible," Dane said.

"I've been told."

"They're saying you're going to kill the king." Dane's jaw worked. "They're saying you're going to kill the *god* the king's become. Mother Quill's put a price on you that would buy this street."

"Then why haven't you collected?"

Dane was silent. Rain dripped off the eaves between them. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough. "Because I keep thinking about a kid who split his bread with me when we had one loaf between us. And I can't figure out if that kid is you or if you sold him to somebody too." He stepped aside, opening the alley mouth. "Run, Felix. Whatever you're doing — run."

Felix walked past him. At the corner he stopped, and didn't turn around.

"When it's done," he said, "there'll be a man in Vell who doesn't remember you. Be kind to him anyway."

* * *

On the ninth day, Sera finished transcribing the final ring of sigils, and they packed for war: chalk and candles, the map, her inquisitor's blade, and a small glass bottle, stoppered with wax, that Felix had filled himself.

Inside it swirled a grey-gold light that moved like smoke underwater. His anchor. The one memory he'd kept for himself all these years, the one he'd never sold, never traded, never shown a living soul — his mother's face, or what he could still reconstruct of it, from before the guild, before everything. He'd need it for the rite. The vessel had to carry something true of its own, or the king's memories wouldn't recognize the way home.

He'd told Sera it was nothing. A keepsake. She'd looked at him with those tired, terrible eyes and let him lie.

"Tomorrow," she said, as they banked the archive lamps. "At dawn we take our places. By noon it's done — one way or another."

"One way or another," Felix agreed.

He lay on his bedroll in the dark and inventoried himself like a thief casing his own house. The smell of the night market in autumn. Sera's laugh, which he'd only heard twice, both times by accident. The weight of Dane's arm around his shoulders in the loft, all those years ago. His name. *Felix Yarrow.* He repeated it like a spell, like a prayer, pressing each syllable down into the bottle of himself as if remembering hard enough could survive what was coming.

It couldn't. He knew exactly how memory worked. He'd built a life on knowing.

But he lay there in the dark anyway, saying his own name over and over into the black, a man rehearsing the shape of a door he was about to walk through and never walk back out of — while across the sleeping city, in a palace lit gold for tomorrow's celebration, something ancient and patient turned the king's face toward the square and began, softly, to smile.
