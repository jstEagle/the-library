## 10. The Man Who Forgot Himself

The silence after the unbinding was the loudest thing Sera Vane had ever heard.

Ten thousand minds in the coronation square, all of them gasping back into themselves at once — a sound like a city inhaling. She felt it pass through her own skull, that great exhalation of stolen thought returning to its owners, and then the pressure lifted. The thing that had pressed against the inside of her awareness for weeks, patient and vast and hungry, was simply *gone*.

She found King Aldric on the steps of the throne dais, crumpled like a dropped coat.

For one terrible heartbeat she thought the rite had failed, that it had taken everything and left nothing, not even breath. Then his chest rose. His eyes opened — and they were *his*. Not the flat amber of the entity's gaze, not the wet black hunger she'd seen at the masquerade. Grey. Tired. Confused in the ordinary way of a man waking from a long illness.

"Where—" he said, and stopped, because he didn't know what to ask. Fifteen years of winter were melting back into him all at once; Sera could see it happening, could see him remembering his wife, his wars, his mistakes, his son. He began to weep without sound, an old man crying in front of ten thousand witnesses, and no one moved to help him because no one understood yet what they had just been saved from.

Sera did. She turned away from the king and looked for Felix.

He was standing at the base of the dais, exactly where the sigil-map had burned out of existence beneath his hands. Dane lay unconscious nearby, the guild's last stand broken across Sera's knee and Felix's desperate, clumsy magic. The witch-finders were scattered through the crowd, their warrants ash in their pockets once Sera had held up the entity's true face for everyone to see — but none of them were looking at Felix now. They were looking at their king, and at each other, and at the terrible arithmetic of what they'd spent fifteen years burning people for.

Felix was looking at nothing.

His eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and brown and clear as clean water, and there was nothing behind them. No wariness. No thief's quick calculation. No grief for the mentor whose memories he'd carried out of the guild vault, no guilt for the door he'd opened as a boy, no fear, no hope. Just a man standing in the ruins of his own history, waiting to be told who lived there.

"Felix," Sera said.

He blinked slowly and turned toward her voice with the trust of someone who had never learned a reason not to trust.

"Who's Felix?"

* * *

They took him to her rooms above the old archive — hers, now, since the Crown had struck her name from the rolls and then, three hours ago, implicitly offered to write it back in. A king who remembered being eaten wanted answers, and Sera was the only person alive who had them in order.

But first: Felix.

He sat on her narrow bed with his hands folded in his lap like a well-trained child, and he watched her move around the room with an attention that broke her heart precisely because it was so unlike him. Felix Yarrow had never watched anyone patiently in his life. He watched people the way a pickpocket watches a purse — for openings, for angles, for the lie underneath. This man watched her the way you watch weather.

"You knew me," he said. It wasn't a question. "Before. You said my name like you were angry at it."

"I was." She sat down across from him, on the chair, keeping the small distance honest. "I was angry at you for years. Then I was angry at myself instead. It's a longer story than you have room for."

"I have room," he said. "That's the whole problem, isn't it? I can feel it. All this space." He touched his own sternum, lightly, as if checking whether the hollow went all the way through. "It doesn't hurt. Shouldn't it hurt?"

"It would, if you remembered what filled it."

"And you're not going to give it back."

Sera had spent the walk home composing the argument. She had prepared justifications, precedents, the archive's records of memory-rite reversals, the names of hedge-mages who might force a restoration. She had built the case the way she'd once built cases for the Inquisition — thorough, airtight, damning.

She dismantled it while she looked at him.

"No," she said. "I'm not."

"Why not?" No fear in it. Only curiosity, clean and open, and somehow that was worse.

"Because what you gave up, you gave up *freely*. Everything else in your life was taken or sold or stolen — your childhood, your mentor, your name in the guild's ledger, fifteen years of a king's mind. You spent your whole existence handling other people's pasts like currency." Her voice caught; she let it. "And the one time you got to choose, you chose to give yourself away. I'm not going to rob you of the only thing you ever decided."

He considered this for a long moment. Outside, the bells of Vell were ringing wrong — joyfully, arrhythmically, the way bells ring when the ringers don't care about the pattern anymore.

"Then tell me about him," Felix said. "The man I was. Start anywhere."

* * *

She started with the market stall, because that was where she'd first truly seen him — not the raid, not the warrant, but the moment before, when she'd watched a young thief lift a widow's grief out of her head and bottle it with hands so gentle the woman had smiled at him afterward. She told him about the rules of outlawed magic, the taste of bottled memory, the way he'd undercharged grieving customers and overcharged rich ones.

"That sounds like a flaw in his business model," Felix said.

"It was the point of it."

She told him about Mother Quill, and softened nothing — the woman who raised him and sold him, the guild that fed him and fed on him. She told him about Dane, and saw something flicker across his blank face when she described the betrayal, some ghost of feeling with no memory attached to it, grief with its roots cut off.

"Do I miss him?" Felix asked quietly.

"I think you miss who you thought he was. I think that part survives even when the facts don't."

She told him about the map. About the dying archivist, the words *the crown eats*, the night market burning. She told him how she'd tracked him — not to arrest him, though she'd told herself that was the reason, but because her own fifteen-year-old records proved the king had changed overnight and no one above her would look at the evidence. She told him she'd burned his mentor.

Here she stopped. Here she made herself stop and look at him and say it plainly, because he deserved a confession delivered by someone who wasn't hiding behind the telling.

"His name was Corvus Aldery. I signed the writ. I lit the pyre. The charges were false — I knew it within a year and said nothing for fourteen. Your mentor died because I was a coward in good standing, and the man I served was a hole wearing a crown." She laid her hands flat on her knees. "You have every right to hate me. I'm telling you so you can start from the truth, since you've got nothing else."

Felix was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice was careful, like a man testing ice.

"I don't hate you. I can't — there's nothing to hate *with*. But I think..." He frowned, reaching for something. "I think if I were him, if I were the man who lost Corvus, I'd want you to live with it. Not cruelly. Just — honestly. No absolution you haven't earned."

"That's fair," Sera said, and meant it, and felt something in her chest loosen that hadn't loosened in fifteen years. Justice done outside the law still counted. Maybe it counted most of all.

* * *

She told him the rest in pieces, over days.

She told him about the ruined archive beneath the palace, and the moment he'd read his own signature in a fifteen-year-old transaction ledger and understood that the villain's door had been opened by the hero's hand. She told him he hadn't flinched. She told him he'd said, *"Then I'm the one who closes it,"* and she had believed him, which was a new experience for her — believing someone.

She told him about the masquerade, and the thing in the crown speaking in his childhood voice, offering him his mentor alive. She told him how he'd almost said yes, and how he'd walked away anyway.

"Was I brave?" he asked.

"You were terrified," Sera said. "Brave is just terrified with somewhere to be."

She told him about the vault, and Corvus's preserved memories, and the message the old man had left rather than serve: *Tell the boy the debt was always his to forgive, never mine to collect.* She watched Felix receive a dead man's love secondhand, wrapped in someone else's words, and she watched him understand it anyway, because some things are older than memory.

She told him about the rite. About the price written into the sigil-map's final page in the archivist's dying hand: *the thief must carry back what he stole, and the vessel cannot hold both.* She told him how he'd heard it, gone very quiet, and then started saying goodbye without saying why — extra bread bought for Sera, a joke told to a frightened apprentice, his mother Quill's ledger burned in a basin while she slept.

"He freed the others first," Sera said. "Every memory the guild was holding. Before he came to the square. He made sure the price was only his."

* * *

On the seventh evening, they sat on the roof of the archive with cheap wine and a view of Vell putting itself back together. The witch-finder orders were dissolved; the new writs coming out of the palace bore Aldric's restored signature and a single line Sera suspected she'd remember longer than any law: *Magic is not the crime. What you do with it, and to whom, is.*

Below them, in the rebuilt market, a memory-seller's stall stood open and legal, and an old widow was buying back a bottle of grief she'd finally decided she wanted to keep.

"The king wants to give you a title," Sera said. "Restorer of the Crown. There's talk of a statue."

"Please tell me you refused on my behalf."

"I told them you'd throw the medal in the canal. He laughed. First time in fifteen years, according to his steward."

Felix smiled — and it was a stranger's smile, unburdened, and Sera grieved it and treasured it in the same breath. That was the arrangement now, she understood. She would carry both. His past and hers, the sins and the small kindnesses, the mentor and the betrayal and the boy who'd sold a king's winters for coin. She would hold it all and hand it over piece by piece, honestly, forever, and he would decide each day what to do with a man he was meeting for the first time.

"Tell me something new," Felix said. "Not about him. About today."

Sera looked out over the city — scarred, half-burned, stubbornly alive — and found, to her genuine surprise, that she didn't know the ending either. For the first time since she'd signed her first false writ, the story ahead of her was unwritten.

"Well," she said, and refilled his cup. "It starts like this: there was a man who forgot himself, and a woman who remembered too much, and between them they had exactly enough of a past to build one future on—"

Above the rooftops, the bells of Vell began to ring again — and this time, every note of it was theirs.
