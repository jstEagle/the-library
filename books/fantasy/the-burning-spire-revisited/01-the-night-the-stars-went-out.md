## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to selling charms in Cinderfall, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything in this city.

Rule one: never call it magic. The word *magic* has a price on it these days — a steep one, payable in years or fingers depending on which magistrate catches you saying it. You call it *craft*. You call it *tradition*. You call it "a little something my grandmother used to do," and you let the customer fill in the rest.

Rule two: never promise results. A charm that promises is a charm that can be proven false, and a charm that can be proven false is a charm that can get you arrested for fraud, which is merely annoying, or proven true, which is considerably worse.

Rule three: always charge too much. Not because of greed — though I won't pretend there's none in me; I like eating as much as the next woman — but because belief is a strange currency, and people trust what costs them. A copper button strung on red thread sells better than a silver one given free. I've tested this. Twice. With control groups. I was a cartographer before I was a shopkeeper, and old habits about measurement die hard, even when the thing you're measuring is nonsense.

My stall sat at the mouth of Tallow Row, wedged between a chandler who watered his wax and a pie-shop whose meat content was a matter of theological debate. I'd been there six years. Long enough that the regulars had stopped asking whether the charms worked and started asking which ones they needed, which is the moment any honest merchant should retire, and which is the moment I decided to stay forever.

"Evening, Villalobos," said Old Marta, hauling her basket of eels past my table. She bought a knot-charm every month and swore by it, though her knots came loose anyway and she blamed the rope. That's the beauty of this trade: the product can never fail, because failure is always the customer's fault. It's also the horror of it, if you think about it too long, so I try not to.

"Evening, Marta. Eels look lively."

"They're furious. There's a difference." She paused at the table, squinting at the sky with the particular frown of a woman who has outlived three husbands and expects the world to keep disappointing her on schedule. "Sky's gone strange, hasn't it."

I looked up. The sky over Cinderfall did go strange sometimes — the foundry smoke did things to the light, made sunsets the color of infected wounds, made noon look like dusk through dirty glass. But this evening the strangeness had a texture to it. The clouds were moving wrong, sliding sideways against the wind like a crowd leaving a theater before the play is done. And the light coming off them was faintly, unsettlingly green.

I didn't think much of it. That's important to record honestly: I didn't think much of it. Fifteen years of careful living teaches you to notice everything and conclude nothing. Notice everything, conclude nothing, sell the lady her eel-knot, go home, lock the door twice. That was the whole architecture of my survival, and it had held up beautifully against floods, purges, two riots, and one memorable incident involving a tax collector and a badger.

"Sable!" Tam came skidding around the corner on legs that hadn't yet learned their final length, all elbows and momentum. He was fourteen, an orphan the slums had issued me without my consent — he'd started by stealing from my stall, graduated to guarding it, and somewhere along the way simply moved into the back room, the way cats and debts do. "Sable, someone smashed the ward-stone on Bellows Street!"

"That so." I said it flat, the way you say everything in Cinderfall. Ward-stones got smashed regularly. Drunks smashed them, smugglers smashed them, occasionally the stones smashed themselves out of sheer exhaustion, being older than anyone could remember and maintained by no one at all.

"Not just cracked. *Gone.* Like — not broken, Sable. *Missing.* There's just a hole where it was, and the hole doesn't have a bottom."

Tam had a way of describing things that made ordinary trouble sound like the opening movement of an apocalypse. He'd once described a rat infestation as "an occupation force with tails." So I gave him a copper for his trouble, told him to stop reading the broadsheets, and sold Marta her eel-knot.

The evening went on being an evening for another hour, which I mention because I want it on the record how long the world stayed ordinary while it was already ending. That's the trick of catastrophes, I've come to believe. They don't announce themselves. They arrive the way rot arrives in a wall — quietly, behind the plaster, while you're arguing about the price of tallow.

* * *

By full dark, Tallow Row knew something was wrong, though nobody agreed on what.

The first sign was the dogs. All of them, every dog within earshot, began howling at once — not the ragged neighborhood chorus that usually rose around midnight, but a single sustained note, like an orchestra tuning to a pitch no human could hear. Then the note stopped, all together, mid-breath, and the silence afterward was worse than the howling.

Then the lamps. Every flame in the row leaned east, all at once, against the draft. Candle flames don't do that. Candle flames are cowards; they follow the air. These flames were leaning toward something the way iron leans toward a lodestone, and I stood up behind my stall and felt the hair on my arms rise and thought, with the calm of a person who has spent fifteen years practicing calm: *that's not weather.*

"Close up, Sable?" Tam asked, appearing beside me with the crate. He'd been helping me pack since sundown, chattering about a fight he'd seen at the reservoir, and I realized distantly that he'd stopped chattering. He was looking up.

"What?"

"The stars."

I looked up.

Now. Here is the thing you need to understand about the sky over Cinderfall: you couldn't see many stars on the best night. Foundry smoke saw to that. On a clear winter evening you might get a dozen, the stubborn bright ones, and children grew up thinking the sky was mostly empty, which the priests said was a metaphor for something. So when I looked up that night and saw nothing — not fewer stars, not smoky stars, but *nothing*, a blackness so complete it looked less like absence than like a lid — I might have shrugged. Bad smoke tonight. Go home.

Except the smoke was moving. And the blackness wasn't.

Every star in the sky went out at once. Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Went out* — the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick, deliberately, all of them, everywhere, in the same instant. And in the hole where the sky used to be, where the few stubborn bright ones should have been hanging over Mount Vesper, there was instead a seam. A line of pale light, thin as a knife cut, running north to south across the heavens like the world had been badly stitched and the thread had finally pulled through.

Tallow Row went quiet in the specific way crowds go quiet — everyone still talking, but lower, faster, the words getting smaller as the fear got bigger. Somewhere a shutter banged. Somewhere a baby started crying and its mother hushed it with a voice that shook.

"Sable." Tam's hand found mine without him looking down. His fingers were cold. They were always cold — poor circulation, I'd told myself for years, the boy needs more meat on him — but this cold was different. This cold had direction. It was pulling. "Do you feel that?"

I did. Under my feet, through the cobblestones, through the packed dirt beneath the cobblestones, something vast had begun to hum. Low. Patient. The hum of a machine turning over after a long sleep, or a throat clearing before it speaks.

And then the wards broke.

I want to describe this accurately, because later — in the days after, when everyone in Cinderfall would claim they'd known all along, seen it coming, felt it in their waters — I want the truth on record: none of us knew. We heard it before we understood it. A sound like a bell the size of a cathedral cracking down its middle, except the ringing didn't fade, it *spread*, rolling outward over the rooftops in a visible wave — I swear this, a visible wave, a shimmer in the air itself, like heat-haze in reverse — and where the wave passed, things changed. Colors drained slightly. Sounds arrived a half-beat late. The lamplight bent toward the seam in the sky like grass bending toward a door.

The border wards of Cinderfall had stood for three hundred years. Nobody living had raised them. Nobody living knew how. They were the city's oldest lie and its truest one: a ring of quiet power we all pretended not to believe in, because believing in them meant admitting what they kept out, and admitting that meant admitting the world beyond the walls was not empty.

They shattered in four seconds. I know because I counted, the way you count when you're terrified — not to measure anything, but because counting is a handrail, and a handrail is all you have when the floor turns out to be a ceiling.

And through the breach, walking down out of the seam in the sky as casually as a man stepping off a curb, came the herald.

* * *

It came down Bellows Street at a walk, and the street made way for it the way water makes way for a sinking ship — not because anything pushed it, but because everything else was fleeing.

Black scales. That's the detail everyone would fix on later, in the taverns, in the confessions, in the nightmares: scales like lacquered obsidian, each one drinking the lamplight and giving none of it back, so the creature seemed cut out of the night itself, a silhouette that had learned to move. It wore the shape of a tall man the way a glove wears a hand — close enough to pass at distance, wrong enough up close. Its joints bent a half-second after its limbs suggested they should. Its face was smooth where a face should have features, and where eyes should have been there were two points of steady amber light, patient as banked coals.

It did not hurry. That was the worst part, the part I'd replay for years: it did not hurry. Around it, Cinderfall was coming apart at the seams — bells ringing themselves, wardens shouting, a stampede of bodies down Tallow Row away from the breach — and the herald walked through all of it at the pace of a clerk heading home for supper.

People fled past my stall. I stood behind it. I've asked myself why, a hundred times since, and the honest answer is that my legs wouldn't take the instruction. Fear froze me in the posture of a shopkeeper, hands on the counter, as if the correct response to the end of the world were to keep the till balanced.

Old Marta went by with her eels, screaming prayers to three different gods in rotation. The chandler bolted his door from the inside, which would protect him from exactly nothing. A squad of city wardens rounded the corner, took one look at the thing coming down the street, and discovered with impressive speed that they were needed elsewhere.

Tam didn't run either. He stood beside me, small and cold-handed, watching the herald approach with an expression I couldn't read and would spend a long time failing to understand. Not terror. Something older than terror. Recognition, maybe. Or memory.

The herald stopped at my stall.

Of all the stalls on Tallow Row — the chandler's, the pie-shop, the pawnbroker with his window full of other people's sorrows — it stopped at mine. It tilted its smooth head, and the amber points of its eyes settled on me with the unhurried attention of an appraiser confirming a mark on silver.

The noise of the street fell away. Not gradually. It was switched off, all at once, as if the herald had reached out and closed a door on the world's sound. In the silence I could hear my own heart, fast and stupid, and Tam breathing beside me, and underneath both, rising through the cobbles, that vast patient hum.

When the herald spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once — from the air, from the ground, from inside my own skull, a voice like dry paper folding.

"Sable Villalobos."

Just that. Two words. My name — the name on my stall, the name on my lease, the name I'd worn for fifteen years like a coat chosen specifically to be forgettable.

But then it said the rest.

"*Sable Meridian Villalobos. Third-born of the house of ash. Signatory, thirteenth and final, to the Compact of Vesper. The court acknowledges the debt. The court has come to collect.*"

I did not know the name it said. That's the truth, and it took me years to make peace with how strange that truth is: the herald spoke a name that was mine, bone-deep mine, and some part of me — some part buried deeper than memory, deeper than the fifteen years I'd carefully built over whatever came before — *recognized it*. Recognized it the way a scar recognizes rain. Something behind my ribs turned over. Something that had been sleeping since before I could remember sat up and listened.

Fifteen years ago, I had come to Cinderfall with nothing — no family, no history, no past I could speak of, a cartographer's satchel and a story about being from up-country that nobody believed and everybody politely forgot to question. I had built a life out of trinkets and misdirection and the slow, daily labor of being unremarkable. I had told myself — ten thousand times, until the telling wore grooves in me — that I had *escaped* something. Run far enough, hidden well enough, become small enough, dull enough, harmless enough. That the hiding had worked.

Standing behind my counter in the dark, with the stars snuffed out overhead and a black-scaled thing from outside the world addressing me by a name I didn't know I owned, I understood, in one clean horrifying instant, that I had been wrong about everything.

The hiding had never been escape.

It had been *storage*. I had not run from the debt. I had been kept safe for it — set aside, sealed, preserved like a coin in cloth, waiting for the day the court came to open the drawer.

The herald straightened. The amber eyes banked low, almost warm.

"You will be given three nights," it said, and the paper-fold voice carried no threat in it, which was somehow more frightening than any threat could have been. Threats argue. This simply informed. "Consider what you owe. Consider what you are owed. The court is patient, Sable Meridian Villalobos. The court has waited fifteen years."

It turned. It walked back up Bellows Street the way it had come, unhurried, indifferent to the screaming, and the seam in the sky swallowed it, and the seam closed, and the stars—

The stars did not come back.

The silence lifted. Sound rushed in — bells, voices, the crash of shutters, the first organized panic of a city realizing its oldest protection was gone. Tam grabbed my wrist and pulled, and this time my legs obeyed, and we ran with the flood of bodies away from Tallow Row, away from the breach, into the maze of alleys I knew better than anyone alive, because I had spent fifteen years learning exactly how a person hides in this city.

Behind us, above us, the sky stayed black. No stars. No moon. Just the lid, and under the lid, the hum, and under the hum, three nights.

Three nights.

I ran, and I counted.
