## 2. Charms for a City Without Magic

The morning after the stars went out, I opened my shop the way I always did: badly.

The shutter stuck. It had stuck for eleven years, and every morning I swore I'd oil it and every morning I forgot, because a woman who sells luck for a living learns very quickly that routine is its own kind of ward against disaster. You don't fix the shutter. The shutter fixing itself would mean something had changed, and in Cinderfall, change was the thing we'd all agreed — silently, collectively, the way a city agrees on anything — not to have.

I oiled it anyway. That should have been my first warning.

Outside, the slums of Cinderfall were doing what Cinderfall did after any catastrophe, which was to say: nothing visible, and everything at once. Women drew water with their shoulders hunched like they expected the sky to drop something into the well. Men who hadn't spoken to each other since the tax riots stood in knots at the corner of Tallow Row, talking low and fast. A child cried somewhere behind the tannery, that thin, exhausted crying that means nobody has come yet and everybody has been listening.

And above all of it, where the stars should have been — nothing. Not clouds. Not haze. Nothing, the way the inside of a closed fist is nothing. The sky went all the way up and came back with nothing in its hands.

"Morning, Sable," said Mrs. Okonkwo from two doors down, passing my stall with her basket clutched like a shield. "You got any of those little blue ones left? The ones for safe travel?"

"I've got everything," I said, which was a lie so practiced it had worn smooth as river stone. "What's wrong with your morning?"

She looked up. She couldn't help it; nobody could. "Everything's wrong with everything," she said. "There's no stars."

"There's no stars in the daytime either," I said. "You weren't using them."

"Sable." She set her basket down. She looked at me the way you look at a doctor when you already know what he's going to say and you want him to say it anyway, because saying it makes it real and real things can be fought. "The wards are gone. Everyone knows the wards are gone. The bells rang wrong last night — you heard them, everyone heard them — and this morning there's no stars, and the charm-sellers' guild put out a notice telling us not to panic, which is how you know it's time to panic."

The Charm-Sellers' Guild of Cinderfall was four old men, a shared ledger, and a standing argument about whether dried newt qualified as inventory or lunch. We had never in our history put out a notice about anything. If we had put out a notice, it meant one of the four old men had died of fright, and honestly, that tracked.

"I'll get you the blue ones," I said.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about me, and I'll tell it quickly because I don't like telling it slowly: fifteen years ago, magic was outlawed in the Nine Provinces, and I survived it by becoming very good at selling things that didn't work.

Not fraud. Let's be precise, because precision was the only honest tool I had left. Fraud implies I knew my charms were empty and my customers didn't. The truth was more delicate, the way a spiderweb is delicate — load-bearing, invisible, and destroyed by anyone who looks too hard at the center.

A charm works through belief. This is not a metaphor. This is the entire trade secret, and it was given to me free by an old woman named Hesper Vane, who ran the shop before me and who taught me the craft in the year the Provinces burned the grimoires. *People need something to hold,* she said, turning a knucklebone over in her arthritic fingers like it owed her money. *They need it to be small enough for a pocket and heavy enough for a decision. Give them that, and the belief does the rest.*

I asked her, once, whether the charms actually worked. Whether the bone really turned aside fever, whether the knotted red thread really kept a husband faithful.

Hesper looked at me with the patience of someone who had buried three husbands and outlived two governments. *Sable girl,* she said, *when a woman holds that thread at night instead of a knife, and wakes up in the morning still married, still alive, still herself — you tell me if it worked. You tell me what 'worked' means, in a city where the alternative to belief was burning.*

So that was the trade. I sold knucklebones and knotted thread, iron nails bent at moonrise (by me, with pliers, while thinking about my rent), small clay tablets pressed with spirals that meant nothing in any language living or dead. I sold them cheap, cheaper than guilt, and I sold them with instructions: hold it when you're afraid. Sleep with it under your tongue only if you're desperate. Do not show it to a magistrate, a priest, or your mother-in-law.

And here is the part I have never said out loud, not to Tam, not to Mrs. Okonkwo, not to the ledger: sometimes it worked. Sometimes a fever broke the night a mother clasped a spiral tablet to her son's chest. Sometimes a cart swerved around a pothole that should have snapped an axle, because the driver had my bent nail in his pocket and his hand wrapped around it white-knuckled. Coincidence, probability, the ordinary mercy of large numbers — I told myself all of it, nightly, like a prayer. But I watched. Fifteen years of watching. And the fevers broke *more often* than chance allowed, and the carts missed *more often*, and I never once let myself finish the thought, because the thought ended somewhere I did not want to go.

Because if belief could do that much — if a city's worth of small held hopes could bend even a hair's width of the world — then what had the border wards actually been made of? And what happened to a city that lost them?

I knew the answer by breakfast. Everyone did. The answer was: this. Panic spreading through the lanes like water finding cracks. Prices doubling by noon. The temple full and the taverns fuller. People looking up at a starless sky and seeing, for the first time in their lives, exactly how much dark had been waiting above them all along, patient as rent.

* * *

By midday the line outside my stall was forty people deep, and it wasn't a line anymore, it was a crowd, and crowds in frightened cities have a smell. Sweat and woodsmoke and fear, which smells faintly of copper, though no one will admit that.

"Protection," said a tanner whose forearms were scarred in patterns I recognized from his trade and chose not to think about. "Whatever you've got. Everything you've got."

"For what?" I asked, and immediately regretted asking, because the question hung in the air between us like smoke and neither of us wanted to name what it was smoke *from*.

He looked up at the blank sky, then back down at me, and his voice dropped to the register men use when they don't want the children to hear. "For whatever comes next."

I sold him a knucklebone, a knot of red thread, and a bent nail, and I threw in a clay spiral for free, which I had never done in eleven years of trade, because free things don't work. Belief needs a price. Hesper taught me that too: *a gift can be doubted. Something paid for is defended.* He counted out his coins like a man paying a ransom and walked away clutching his purchases to his chest, and I watched him go and felt the specific, corrosive shame of a woman who has just taken real money for — for what?

For hope. For hope, shaped like a bone. Which is either the oldest con in the world or the oldest sacrament, depending on the day, and yesterday I could not have told you which, and today the sky was empty and I still couldn't, and somehow that was worse.

Mrs. Okonkwo came back for a second blue charm. "One for my sister," she said. "In Lowmarsh. She won't believe it unless it comes from you."

"That's not how these work," I said.

"That's exactly how these work," she said, and paid, and left, and she was right, and I sat with that knowledge like a stone in my shoe for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

Tam found me at dusk.

Tam was fourteen, maybe — orphan age being a fluid quantity in Cinderfall, where the foundlings aged themselves by growth spurts and bad winters. He lived in the crawlspace under the cistern stair, which sounds grim and was, but it was also dry, and warm from the bakery flue, and mine to give, because the building belonged to a landlord who had stopped collecting rent sometime around the second plague scare and possibly around the first. Tam swept my step. Tam fetched my water. Tam knew the names of every cat in the district and the debts of every merchant on Tallow Row, which is a rarer and more useful form of literacy than reading.

He also, I had long suspected, saw things other people didn't. I'd caught him staring at corners where nothing was, tracking movements across walls that cast no shadow, flinching at doorways seconds before anyone knocked. I'd filed it under *orphan boyhood*, the same drawer where I filed his habit of talking to moths and his conviction that the well water tasted different on Thursdays. You learn not to look too closely at the strange children of Cinderfall. Looking too closely is how they get taken away.

But tonight Tam came through my door without knocking, which he never did, and he was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Sable." His voice was steady, which scared me more than shaking would have. Shaking means the fear is moving through you. Steady means you've decided to live inside it. "There's something walking through the walls of this city."

I was packing up the stall. My hands kept working while my brain went cold. "Describe it."

"It doesn't walk like walking. It pours. Like ink in water, except upward." He held his hands out, shaping it, and his hands were trembling now, now that the words were out and real. "It goes through brick like brick isn't there. Through the tannery wall, through the temple, through the bell tower. It's been circling the district all day. Big circles, getting smaller. Like it's narrowing down on something."

"On what?"

He looked at me. Fourteen years old, maybe, with a soot smudge on his cheekbone and eyes that had seen too much infancy to ever be fully childlike again, and he looked at me the way you look at someone when the answer is going to hurt both of you and you're checking whether they can take it.

"It keeps stopping," he said, "right above your shop."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did with that information, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford and tonight I'm spending it all at once.

I did nothing. That's what I did. I finished closing the stall. I ate half a loaf. I told Tam it was probably a trick of the light, which was a lie so lazy it insulted us both, because Tam had just described something moving through solid masonry and there was no light trick in the world that poured upward through brick.

Then I sat on my cot with my knees drawn up and I thought about Hesper Vane, dead nine years now, and about the night before she died, when she'd had too much plum wine and too little sense and told me a story she'd never told me sober.

*There was a contract,* she'd said, staring at the ceiling beams like they might confess something. *When the city was founded. When the wards went up. Thirteen souls, they say, paid to something underneath the mountain, something old enough that the mountain grew around it like a pearl around grit. Twelve names in the registry. The thirteenth burned out, scraped away, paid in some coin they don't write down.* She'd laughed then, a small ugly laugh. *Every city is built on a debt, Sable girl. Ours just had the decency to keep receipts.*

I'd filed it under *drunk*. The same drawer, nearly, where I'd filed Tam's corners. Cinderfall was full of stories about the mountain, the way any poor city is full of stories about rich people: elaborate, resentful, unverifiable, and mostly true in ways that don't matter until suddenly they matter completely.

Last night the herald had said my name. My whole name. The one I'd buried so deep I'd almost succeeded in convincing myself it had never been mine — and I say *almost* because a buried name is like a buried spring; you can build over it, pave it, forget it, but the ground stays damp in one particular spot, and on certain nights, lying on my cot with the shutter rattling, I could feel it. Cold. Rising.

Fifteen years ago I'd run from a name. I'd built a life the size of a shop stall, small enough to defend, ordinary enough to hide in. I'd sold hope shaped like bones to a city that needed it, and I'd told myself that was survival, and it was, and survival was supposed to be the end of the story. You run, you hide, you grow old over a ledger of small dishonest mercies, and the past stays bought.

But the past, it turns out, keeps its own ledger. And last night a black-scaled thing had stepped through a hole in the sky and read my account aloud.

Now its shadow was pouring through the walls of my district, circling tighter, and Tam — sharp-eyed, moth-talking, Thursday-water-tasting Tam — could see it, and it kept stopping above my shop.

"Sable," Tam said, before he left, standing in my doorway with the starless dark behind him like a held breath. "If it's looking for you — and it's looking for you, I'd bet my teeth on it, such as they are — then running's no good. Things that pour through walls don't lose people. They just take longer."

"I know," I said.

"So what are you going to do?"

And I gave him the only honest answer I had, which was: "I'm going to sleep on it. Under the roof. With the shutters barred. And tomorrow I'm going to open the stall, because a city without stars still needs its charms, and I am apparently the only person in Cinderfall who knows how to make belief hold its shape."

Tam nodded slowly, the way children nod at adults they've decided not to argue with, which is the most frightening nod there is, because it means they've already stopped believing you.

At the door he paused. "One more thing," he said. "The shadow. When it stops over the shop — it pauses. Like it's reading something. Like the roof is a page."

Then he was gone, down the stair, into the dark that used to have stars in it, and I lay awake until the small hours doing the arithmetic I'd spent fifteen years refusing to do.

A burned name. A thirteenth soul. A payment made in a coin they don't write down.

My mother had died when I was young — that was the story, the official record, the version I'd carried like a chipped cup because it was the only one I had. Died, and left me nothing but a name I couldn't bear to speak and a face I could no longer picture, no matter how many nights I tried, no matter how hard I pressed my eyes shut. Fifteen years of trying. Fifteen years of a shape where a face should be.

*A coin they don't write down.*

I sat up in the dark, and my hands were shaking, and for the first time since the stars went out I understood that the shaking wasn't fear.

It was recognition.

Somewhere beneath Mount Vesper, in a court that kept what it bought, there was a vial, or a page, or a flame — something holding the memory of my mother's face. Something that had been paid for it. And the shadow pouring through the walls of Cinderfall, circling my shop, pausing over my roof like a reader over a page —

It hadn't come to collect a debt.

It had come to collect *me*.
