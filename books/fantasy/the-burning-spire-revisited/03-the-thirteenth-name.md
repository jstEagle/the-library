## 3. The Thirteenth Name

There are two kinds of drunk, and I have been both.

The first kind drinks to forget. That's the amateur kind. The second kind drinks because forgetting is a skill, and skills dull without practice. Fifteen years of daily drills had made me a master of the craft, and then the stars went out in a single night and every hour of that careful training turned out to be worth exactly nothing, because the thing I'd spent fifteen years not-remembering came walking back through a hole in the sky wearing my name on its tongue.

So: I drank. But I want it noted for the record — which is a joke, since there was no record anymore, the registry office being rubble and the registry itself being the reason I was sitting in my own kitchen at noon with a bottle of ash-wine and a lockpick's patience — that I drank *slowly*. Like a professional.

My kitchen smelled of mildew and old paper, which are the same smell if you've lived with them long enough. The rain came through the roof in three places, and I'd positioned buckets under each one years ago with the precision of a woman who had once aligned ward-stones to a tolerance of a hair's breadth and now aligned crockery to catch drips. There's a lesson in that about ambition, but I've never been fond of lessons.

The bottle was a third gone when I finally pulled the satchel out from under the floorboard.

Here is what you need to know about Odile Ferran, former First Warden of Cinderfall, current resident of a leaking house in Tanners' Row: I did not lose my post because of drink. The drink came after. The post I lost because of a document, and the document was sitting in the satchel under my floorboards like a debt of its own, patient as interest.

I set it on the table. I poured another finger of wine, purely ceremonial. And I opened the founding contract of the city of Cinderfall for the first time in fifteen years.

* * *

You should understand what the wards were, before they weren't.

Every city on the borderlands sits inside a lattice — ward-stones sunk at the corners, threads of working strung between them like a loom, and the whole apparatus humming day and night against what waits outside. Most people never think about it, the way most people never think about their heartbeat. It works, therefore it has always worked, therefore it always will. That arithmetic holds until the night it doesn't, and then everyone stands in the street looking up at a sky with no stars in it, doing the same horrified sum: *it stopped.*

I was twenty-six when I helped build Cinderfall's lattice. Younger than that when I first understood it needed feeding. A ward is not a wall; a wall just stands there, dumb and honest. A ward is a promise, and promises burn fuel. The founders — twelve of them, wealthy, frightened, respectable — had solved the fuel question before I ever arrived, and I was young enough, and ambitious enough, and paid enough, not to ask too precisely what burned.

I asked once. Early. I remember the room: the founders' hall, all dark wood and darker faces, Magistrate Corvane at the head of the table smoothing his gloves. I asked where the spire drew its heat from, and Corvane smiled at me the way a man smiles at a child who has asked where meat comes from, and he said, "From the past, Warden Ferran. From everything we're better off without."

And I — this is the part I come back to, the part I turn over at four in the morning like a stone looking for what's underneath — I said, "Good." I said good, and took the salary, and went home to my neat little house near the registry, and slept soundly for eleven years.

The document on my table now was the answer to the question I'd stopped asking. I'd stolen it the night the wards fell — walked into the collapsing registry office while everyone else ran the other way, because some instincts outlive their usefulness — and I'd told myself I took it to rebuild the lattice. To study the anchor-points. To do my job one last time.

I hadn't opened it until today. Fifteen years of telling myself stories, and the truth is I knew what was in it the way you know what's in a letter you haven't opened from someone who died angry at you.

The contract was written on vellum the color of old teeth. Twelve signatures marched down the page in ink gone brown — Corvane's at the top, proud and sharp; the others trailing after like ducklings. And above them, in a hand none of theirs, the terms. I read them twice. My wine sat untouched, which for me constitutes a medical emergency.

*In consideration of the perpetual maintenance of the border wards of Cinderfall, the undersigned convey unto the Undercourt beneath Mount Vesper the following consideration...*

Souls. Thirteen of them. Not metaphorically — the contract was specific in the way contracts drafted by things that live under mountains are specific. Thirteen souls, held in perpetuity, renewable upon the consent of the signatory or their heirs. Twelve names followed, each one a founder, each one long dead, their debts passing down bloodlines like a chin or a temper.

And then the thirteenth line.

It was burned. Not crossed out, not struck through — *burned*, the vellum scorched in a perfect oval, as though a coin of fire had been pressed to it. Around the edges of the scorch, half-legible, fragments survived. A letter here, a flourish there. Enough to know the name had been longer than the others. Enough to know it had been written by a different hand — a woman's hand, quick and slanted, the writing of someone who wrote fast because she'd already decided.

I knew that hand.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made me put the bottle down, actually put it down, cork it, push it away, because my hands had stopped being mine. I knew that handwriting from requisition forms filed fifteen years ago. From a permit for a charm-shop license, countersigned by a mother on behalf of a daughter too young to sign. From the only document in the Cinderfall archives signed by Maren Villalobos.

Maren Villalobos, who had volunteered. Who had come to the founders' hall in the last days, before the wards were lit, and offered herself — and then, at the final moment, had amended the terms. I remembered the amendment now. I remembered standing in the corner of that room, holding my ledger, watching her lean over the table and speak quietly to Corvane, and I remembered the magistrates' clerk heating a blade, and I remembered thinking it was a formality. A correction of spelling, perhaps. A change of address.

They burned her name off the contract and wrote another in its place. And the new name — the thirteenth soul conveyed in perpetuity to the Undercourt beneath Mount Vesper — was not hers.

Under the scorch, in the margin, in Maren Villalobos's quick slanted hand, she had written the substitution herself. Four words. I read them perhaps forty times, hoping they'd rearrange themselves into something else.

*Take my portion from her.*

Not "take me instead." She could have given herself. The court would have taken her; souls were souls, and hers was offered freely. But a freely-given soul buys less than a taken one — that's the ugly economics of the Undercourt, the arithmetic no one says aloud. What purchases permanence isn't sacrifice. It's surrender. And so Maren Villalobos had done the crueler, cleverer thing: she'd signed over her daughter. Sable Villalobos. A child, unrecorded, unregistered, invisible to every census in the city because Maren had kept her that way deliberately, all those years, keeping her off the rolls the way you keep a candle behind a shutter.

Except the child didn't stay a child. The child grew up and opened a charm shop in the slums and sold trinkets that worked through belief alone, and lived fifteen years in the open air of a city whose founding contract carried her name in its scorched heart, and nobody came. For fifteen years, nobody came.

Because the collection date wasn't written in years. I saw it now, in the terms, in the clause I'd skimmed a dozen times: *renewable upon the consent of the signatory.* Consent. The debt lay dormant as long as the signatory lived unaware — an unsigned contract sleeps, and a sleeping contract cannot be collected. Maren hadn't just sold her daughter. She'd built the sale to fail unless Sable ever learned of it and affirmed it. Her whole plan hung on one thread: that Sable Villalobos would go her whole life never knowing what she was owed.

Fifteen years, that thread held. Then the wards fell, and something stepped through the breach, and spoke a buried name aloud in a street full of witnesses.

Which meant the thread hadn't broken on its own.

* * *

I sat with that for a long time. The buckets filled and I emptied them without getting up, just tipping them out the door and setting them back, an old woman's small liturgy. Somewhere out in Tanners' Row a child was crying, and somewhere farther off the militia was hammering planks over a broken ward-stone as if a plank could hold back the Hollowing, and the sky through my broken roof was the flat starless black of a closed eye.

Here is what guilt does, if you let it age properly. It doesn't stay a knife. Knives are clean; knives cut once. Guilt left long enough goes to work like water, gets into everything, softens the joists, swells the doors shut. I had spent fifteen years telling myself I'd been a minor functionary in other people's crime. I'd held the ledger. I'd heated nothing. I'd said *good* in a meeting, once, and what is a word?

But I had also, six months after the wards were lit, been handed a request from Magistrate Corvane's office: a routine audit of the founding documents, and instruction to certify that the registry contained twelve names, twelve, count them, twelve, and to file accordingly. And I had certified it. I had written, in my own careful warden's hand, *registry verified complete,* knowing — knowing the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked — that completeness was the one thing it did not contain. I had made the lie structural. I had made myself mortar.

And I had done it because Corvane's clerk mentioned, in passing, that the certification would be noted in my file, and files became pensions, and pensions became the neat little life I'd been promised. I was twenty-seven years old and I sold a girl's existence for the price of a comfortable old age, and the punchline — the joke the universe tells only to drunks and wardens — is that I lost the post anyway, five years later, in a purge I never even understood, and never got the pension either. I'd committed the sin and skipped the wages. Even my corruption was incompetent.

The wine stayed corked. I'm not going to pretend that was virtue. It was that my hands needed to be steady for what came next, and I have never once in my life managed a steady hand and a full cup at the same time.

I cleared the table. I got out my tools — the real ones, the warden's kit I'd sworn I'd melted down and hadn't, because I'm a liar in small ways too — and I began the work I'd spent fifteen years not doing.

Decrypting the registry was not difficult for me because I had helped design the cipher. That was the arrangement, back then: the founders wrote the contracts, and I wrote the locks around them, and neither of us talked about what was inside. The cipher keyed to the ward-lattice itself — elegant, I'll admit, even now. You couldn't decrypt the founding documents unless the wards were alive, because the decryption working drew its power from the same lattice it protected. The registry guarded itself with the very walls it recorded.

The wards were dead. Had been dead for two days.

And dead lattices, it turns out, don't guard. They just stop humming, and every lock built on their song hangs open like a mouth.

I laid the registry pages beside the contract and watched the cipher dissolve under my lens — columns of figures resolving into names, dates, payments. The Undercourt's ledger, mirrored in our archive: every soul conveyed, every renewal, every interest payment rendered in memory and years. Twelve founder-lines, all dormant, all awaiting heirs who would never consent because they'd never know.

And the thirteenth line, in the mirror-ledger, was not burned. Of course it wasn't. The burn was for our copy, for the public record, for the version of history a city could live with. The court keeps what it buys, and it keeps immaculate books.

*Sable Villalobos.* Conveyed in perpetuity. Status: dormant, fifteen years. And beside the status, fresh ink, still glossy, dated two nights ago:

*Called for collection.*

Two nights ago. The night the stars went out. The herald hadn't breached the wards and then happened to speak her name — the speaking of the name *was* the breach. The collection call had torn the lattice open from the inside, the way a shout tears a silence. Every ward-stone in Cinderfall had shattered because a fifteen-year-old debt had finally, formally, come due, and the debt was a living woman selling charms in the slums, and the contract could only be collected with her consent, and so the court had done the only thing a creditor can do when the debtor doesn't know she owes.

It had introduced itself.

I read the entry again. *Called for collection.* And beneath it, smaller, in the clerk-hand that made my skin crawl because I recognized its patience: *Signatory unaware. Grace period extended: three nights.*

Three nights. The herald had given her three nights — I'd heard the rumor drifting through the taverns, garbled, secondhand, a black-scaled thing and a charm-seller and a name spoken like a bell. Three nights, and then collection, consent or no consent, because a grace period is not mercy; it's a deadline wearing softer clothes.

I looked at the name a long time. Sable Villalobos. I tried to remember if I'd ever seen her, back then, a child of seven or eight trailing her mother through the registry halls. I couldn't. That was the worst of it — I couldn't even give her the dignity of having noticed her. She'd been kept invisible so well that even the woman who mortared the lie over her had never once looked at her face.

Well. I knew her shop. Everyone in the slums knew the charm-shop, the one that sold luck to people who'd forgotten what luck felt like. And I knew what I was going to do, and I hated myself for how long it took to decide, and I decided it anyway, which is the most I can say for any redemption: late, reluctant, and probably insufficient.

I was going to walk into that shop and tell a stranger she was sold.

I gathered the contract, the registry pages, my tools. At the door I stopped, because the bottle was still sitting there, corked, full almost to the shoulder, and some old ritual demanded acknowledgment. I picked it up. I considered it. Then I poured it out into the street — all of it, a thin hissing ribbon in the rain — because if I was going to do this I was going to do it sober, and sober was going to hurt, and I deserved the hurt the way I deserved the name I was carrying to her: honestly, and far too late.

The rain had stopped by the time I reached the slums. The streets were wrong — quiet in the wrong register, not empty-quiet but *held*-quiet, the whole district listening. And above the charm-shop, as I came around the corner onto Villalobos's street, I saw why.

The shadow was there. Pouring along the rooftops like smoke with intent, circling the chimney, pausing over the shop's door. Waiting. Counting, maybe. Three nights, and it was keeping the tally itself.

And below it, lighted windows, and a silhouette moving inside — a woman bent over a workbench, hands busy, making small protections for people who no longer believed in protection.

I stood in the street with a stolen contract under my arm and fifteen years of mortar in my lungs, and I thought: *she is going to hate you, and she will be right to.*

Then I knocked.
