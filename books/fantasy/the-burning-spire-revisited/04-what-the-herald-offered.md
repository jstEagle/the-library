## 4. What the Herald Offered

The herald did not leave after speaking my name. That was the first insult. You'd think a creature that had just detonated the sky would have the courtesy to depart dramatically, trailing smoke and consequence, leaving the survivors to pick through the wreckage of their assumptions. Instead it stood in the middle of Lamplight Row with its black scales drinking the light of every shuttered lantern, folding its too-long hands like a clerk waiting for a shopkeeper to find the right ledger.

Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was doing.

"Three nights," it said again, in a voice like paper being folded into a bird. "Collection occurs at the turning of the third night. You will be found, Sable Villalobos. The contract does not permit otherwise."

"I don't have a contract," I said, because denial is free and everything else in Cinderfall suddenly wasn't.

The herald's face was difficult to look at directly — not because it was horrible, but because it kept almost resolving into something familiar. A shape like my mother's jawline. A suggestion of the woman who used to weigh flour at the corner market. It wore other people's features the way a coat wears lint.

"The court keeps what it buys," the herald said. "It bought you before you could walk. But—" and here it tilted its head, and for one vertiginous moment it looked almost *amused*, which was somehow worse than menace — "the court honors its own law above all things. And the law says this: contracts may be rewritten by the signatory alone."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," said the herald, stepping backward into the breach in the air that had swallowed the stars, "that you have three nights to decide what kind of payment you are." The breach began closing around it like water healing over a dropped stone. "Run, if you like. Hide, as you hid. The court has been patient for fifteen years. It can be patient for three nights more."

And then it was gone, and the stars were still gone, and I was standing in the street holding a tray of charms that had never worked and never needed to, until now, when nothing worked at all.

* * *

I locked the shop door behind me out of pure habit, which tells you something about how well I process catastrophe. Fifteen years of building an ordinary life, and my hands still went through the motions: bolt, latch, charm of keeping hung on the nail by the frame — a bit of painted glass that worked exactly as well as it ever had, which is to say not at all, except that Tam believed in it, so in the only way that mattered, it worked.

Tam was sitting on my stairs when I got upstairs, knees pulled to his chest, watching the empty sky through the window with the particular stillness of a boy who has seen something he cannot unsee.

"You talked to it," he said.

"I talked to it."

"It knew your name. Your real name." He didn't look at me. "Villalobos. Nobody calls you that. Even the tax man calls you Sable V., like your name's too long for his book."

"Tax men are cowards."

"Sable." He finally turned, and his eyes were wet and furious in the way only fifteen-year-olds can manage, fury and fear braided so tight you couldn't tell which was holding up which. "It came out of a hole where the stars used to be and it said your name like it was reading off a shopping list. Are you going to tell me what's happening, or are you going to make me a cup of tea and change the subject like you do?"

I made him a cup of tea. Old habits. But I sat down across from him while it steeped, and I told him the truth, or as much of it as I owned: that I didn't know what was happening. That the name was mine but felt borrowed. That fifteen years ago I'd come to Cinderfall with no past I could describe and a talent for drawing maps of places I'd never been, and I'd built a life out of the parts that didn't require explaining.

"That's not a life," Tam said. "That's a hiding place with shelves."

"You would know. You live above a fishmonger."

"This isn't funny."

"No," I agreed. "It really isn't." I wrapped my hands around my own cup, mostly for something to hold. "It gave me three nights. Then it comes to collect whatever it thinks I owe."

"So we run." He said it fast, the way you pull a splinter. "Tonight. Now. There are roads east that aren't warded, there never were any wards on the eastern roads because nobody important lives out there—"

"Tamsin."

"—and the thing said it would *find* you but things say that all the time, it's a intimidation tactic, Odile Ferran says half of ward-work is just convincing the dark you're not worth the trouble—"

"*Tam.*"

He stopped. His hands were shaking around the teacup, and I noticed — the way you notice a crack in a wall only when the house is already falling — that he'd lost weight since winter, that there were shadows under his eyes that had nothing to do with the vanished stars.

"They'll find me anywhere," I said gently. "You heard it. The contract doesn't permit otherwise. Running buys us nothing except making you a fugitive too, and I won't have that."

"Then what? You're just going to *wait* here for it? Like a delivery?"

"Something like that." I looked out the window at the starless dark, and something was hardening in me, some stubborn mineral deposit forming around the dread. "It said something strange, right at the end. It said contracts can be rewritten by the signatory alone."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning somewhere there's a piece of paper with my name on it, and apparently I'm allowed to hold the pen." I smiled, and I could feel it come out crooked. "I'm a cartographer, Tam. Paper is rather my specialty."

* * *

He didn't sleep, and neither did I. Around the hour when dawn should have happened but instead produced only a gray, apologetic lightening — no sun I could see, just the sky bruising slowly from black to slate — Tam started packing my satchel without asking. Dried figs. The good ink. My brass compass, the one with the cracked face that always pointed slightly west of north, toward Mount Vesper, though I'd told myself for years that was just a flaw in the needle.

"You're still running," I said.

"I'm *preparing*," he said with tremendous dignity. "Preparing and running are different things."

"They're really not."

"They are if you run *prepared*." He shoved the satchel at me. "Take the eastern road. Take the stupid figs. By the time that scaly filing-clerk finds you, you'll be three provinces away and it'll have to file a complaint with someone, and bureaucracies hate traveling, everyone knows that."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, ugly, frightened laugh, but real. "Where does everyone know that?"

"Odile Ferran. She shouts it at drunks who try to skip their ward-tithe." He hesitated, then, quieter: "She's been asking about you. Since last night. She came by the fishmonger's at dawn smelling like a distillery and asked if I knew where you lived, and I said no, and she said 'good boy, lie better,' and left money for smoked haddock I don't think she wanted."

"Ward-keeper Ferran is asking about me."

"She's not a ward-keeper anymore. Not since the wards fell. She says—" Tam mimicked, with brutal accuracy, a low gravelly rasp: "*'A keeper whose wards fail is a carpenter whose house burned down; you keep the title out of politeness.'*" He dropped the voice. "Sable, she looked scared. Real scared. And she's the person in this city who's least capable of it."

I took the satchel. I didn't put it on. We both watched it sit there on the table between us like a small packed argument.

"If I run," I said, "it follows. And when it catches me — and it will catch me, Tam, I believe that the way I believe in gravity — I'll be alone. No witnesses. No one who knows what was taken or what was traded. Whatever happens to me happens in the dark, unwitnessed, unmapped." I tapped the table. "But if I stay, if I stand in front of this thing and make it state its business in front of people who write things down — then there's a record. There's a trail. There's a *map*, even if I'm not the one who survives to draw it."

"That's a terrible plan. That's a plan made of being brave and dead."

"Some of history's best plans."

"Name one."

"The founding of Cinderfall," I said, and we both went quiet, because none of us knew anymore what the founding of Cinderfall had actually cost, only that the price had apparently included me.

* * *

Near midmorning — our best guess at midmorning, now that the sky had stopped keeping honest time — the knock came at my door. Three slow raps, spaced like a funeral bell learning patience.

Tam reached for the poker. I reached for the door.

Odile Ferran filled the frame the way weather fills a horizon: broad, gray, and promising trouble. She was, as advertised, drunk, or at least recently and extensively so — but her eyes were clear as cut glass, and they went straight past me to the satchel on the table, and then to Tam, and then back to me, reading the whole scene in one sweep like a page she'd already annotated.

"Packing," she said. "Good. Means you've got sense. Bad," she continued, pushing past me into the room without invitation, "means you're about to waste it." She set a leather folio on my table, heavier than any folio had a right to be, and looked at Tam. "Boy. Out."

"He stays," I said.

Her eyebrows climbed. "You're giving orders now."

"Fifteen years of practice. Nobody noticed."

Something flickered across her face — respect, maybe, or indigestion — and she grunted and opened the folio anyway. Inside were pages covered in her cramped, furious hand, and beneath them, older pages: copies, in faded sepia, of documents I had never seen and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own face in a warped mirror.

"What are those?"

"Burned registry fragments. From the founders' vault, which I broke into at roughly four this morning, which is why I smell like this, and if you repeat that I'll deny it under oath." She flattened a page with one scarred hand. "Thirteen names on the founding contract. Twelve recorded clean. The thirteenth was burned out — deliberately, expertly, by someone who knew ward-script. Except fire doesn't eat everything. The indentations survive. I've spent all night reading them." She looked up at me, and her voice, when it came, had lost all its gravel and gone quiet as ash. "Villalobos. Sable. Daughter of Maren Villalobos, who signed as surety. Who volunteered."

The room did something strange. The walls leaned in. The tea went cold in my cup between one heartbeat and the next.

"My mother," I said, and the word was a coin from a country I'd been exiled from. "I don't remember my mother."

"No," Odile said. "You wouldn't. That was part of the payment." She closed the folio. "I helped forge that contract, girl. I was young and I believed the founders when they said the cost would be spread thin enough that no one would feel it. I was wrong, and I've been drinking about it for fifteen years, and last night the bill came due in front of your shop." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "So here's what I know, and then you can decide whether to run, because I suspect you've already decided and I'd like to be home before the shaking starts.

"The court beneath Mount Vesper keeps what it buys. Always. Forever. That's the first law and they cannot break it even if they wanted to. But the second law—" she tapped the burned name, once, gently, the way you'd touch a grave marker— "the second law is that a contract answers to its signatory. Not the court. Not the ward-keepers. The one whose name is on the line. If the signatory demands new terms, the court must hear them. Must. Their whole edifice runs on the sanctity of agreement, and sanctity cuts both ways."

"Rewritten by the signatory alone," I whispered. The herald's riddle, unpacked.

"Whoever holds the pen holds the debt," Odile said. "Question is whether you've got the nerve to hold the pen, or whether you'll take the eastern road and spend the rest of your life looking over your shoulder at a hole in the sky."

Tam was looking at me. Odile was looking at me. The satchel sat on the table, packed with figs and good ink and a compass that pointed at the mountain, and I realized I had decided sometime in the night, probably around the hour when I'd caught myself sketching the Undercourt's breach from memory — corridors I'd never walked, drawn in my own hand, as if some part of me had been mapping that place my entire exile.

"I'm not running," I said.

"Sable—" Tam started.

"I'm not running, and I'm not paying either. Not the way they expect." I picked up the compass. Its needle strained west, toward Vesper, faithful as a dog. "They bought a soul. Fine. But they wrote the purchase badly, and they let the signatory live, and they gave me *three nights* — which means somewhere in that mountain of theirs, there's a piece of paper terrified of what I might write on it."

Odile grinned. It was not a comforting sight. "There's the founder's blood. Knew it was in there somewhere."

"It's not blood," I said. "It's cartography. Every map is a claim, Odile. Every line drawn is a line defended. They think they collected a soul fifteen years ago." I slung the satchel over my shoulder, and it was heavier than figs and ink, heavy as a signature. "What they actually collected was a mapmaker. And I am going to go down that mountain and chart every corridor of their precious court until I find the heart of the contract — and then I'm going to redraw it."

Tam grabbed my sleeve. His hand was shaking, but his grip wasn't. "Then I'm coming. Don't argue. I see things other people can't, and you know it, and you'll need me."

I opened my mouth to argue anyway, because I loved him, and arguing is what love looks like when it's frightened.

That was when the knocking started again — not at my door, but at every door on Lamplight Row at once, slow and patient and evenly spaced, and beneath the floorboards, faint as a pulse, something far below began to count.
