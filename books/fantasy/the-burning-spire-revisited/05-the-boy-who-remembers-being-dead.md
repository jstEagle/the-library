## 5. The Boy Who Remembers Being Dead

There are three things nobody in Cinderfall knows about me, and I've spent my whole life making sure it stays that way.

One: I can see the herald's shadow moving through walls, which sounds like a gift until you realize what it means about the walls, and about the shadow, and about me.

Two: I stole a spoon from Sable's shop three years ago and never gave it back. It's under my floorboard. I don't know why I keep it. Maybe because it's the only crime I've ever committed that didn't come with a price attached.

Three: I died once.

I was eleven months old. I don't remember it the way you remember falling off a roof or eating something rotten — I remember it the way you remember a room you lived in before you could talk. Warm. Quiet. A corridor with no doors, just light coming from nowhere, and a voice that wasn't unkind asking me to wait my turn.

I waited. That's the funny part. An infant, dead less than an hour, sitting patient as a churchmouse in the anteroom of everything, and then somebody made a mistake. Or a bargain. My mother always said the fever broke and the physician wept and called it a miracle, and my mother believed him because believing him was easier than the alternative, which was asking who signed for me.

Somebody signed for me. I'm sure of it now. Nothing comes back from that corridor without paperwork. The Undercourt doesn't do miracles; it does transactions. Somewhere beneath Mount Vesper there is a ledger with my name in it, next to a date, next to a sum, and whatever paid that sum has been accruing interest for sixteen years.

Which is why, on the second night after the stars went out, while Cinderfall barricaded its doors against a darkness that had teeth in it, I climbed through Sable's window instead of using her door, because doors felt too much like invitations.

She was awake. Of course she was awake. She sat at her worktable with every lamp in the shop lit, which told me everything, because Sable Villalobos was the stingiest woman alive with lamp-oil. She'd mapped the whole city by candlelight to save coin, and now she was burning oil like a woman trying to hold back the sea with a bucket.

"You look terrible," I said, folding myself onto her counter among the charm-bracelets.

"You climbed through my window."

"Your door's got a lock. Locks are for people with nothing to hide." I turned a bracelet over. Little tin suns, worthless, beautiful. "You're not running."

"No."

"They gave you three nights. This is night two. Running is what nights are *for*."

"I don't run from debts, Tam." She said it lightly, but her hand went to her collarbone, where she kept a small brass key on a cord — the only thing she owned that predated Cinderfall. "Debt follows. You run, it arrives tired of waiting and takes the interest out of your hide."

"That's very wise," I said. "I learned the same lesson from a moneylender and a broken thumb."

That got half a smile out of her. Then it faded, and she looked at me across the lamplight with those cartographer's eyes that took measurements of everything, including people, and I knew the moment had arrived. The one I'd been walking toward since the herald spoke her buried name and the sky went black.

"Sable," I said. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to not do the thing where you make a joke so you don't have to feel it."

"I never do that."

"You're doing it right now."

Her mouth closed. She set down her pen. And I understood, suddenly, why confessing is supposed to be good for the soul, because mine had been carrying this alone for sixteen years and it weighed exactly as much as a corpse.

"I died," I said. "When I was a baby. Fever. Eleven months old. Dead long enough that they'd started laying out the little clothes."

Sable didn't laugh. She didn't do the kind face either, the one adults use when children say fanciful things. She just watched me, still as a held breath, and waited, because she was the sort of person who understood that some sentences need room.

"There's a place," I went on. "Past the last breath. A corridor. No doors, no end, warm like bathwater, and light that doesn't come from anywhere. And a voice. Not cruel. Patient. It said *wait your turn*, and I did, and then—" I shrugged, hating how small the gesture was for what it held. "Then I was back. In my body, in my cot, screaming loud enough to raise the street. Fever gone. Physician called it providence."

"And you remember it," Sable said slowly. "The corridor."

"All of it. Nobody remembers being a baby, Sable. Nobody remembers their own birth. But I remember dying, which means I remember something that happened *after* me. Something reached in and pulled me out, and it left fingerprints on the inside of my skull." I tapped my temple. "That's why I see the herald's shadow through walls. That's why I saw the wards break before they broke — I felt them go like a tooth coming loose. Whatever brought me back left a crack, and things from the other side leak through it. Into me."

The lamps hissed. Outside, the dark pressed against the windows like a face.

"The Undercourt," Sable said. The word came out flat, careful, like she was testing whether the floor would hold her weight.

"The court beneath Mount Vesper. Where souls get kept like coins in a vault." I swallowed. "I know how you get in."

Her head came up fast. Too fast. For one naked second, all the wry composure fell off her and I saw what was underneath: a woman who had been ready to die alone in her shop rather than owe anyone anything, and who had just heard the word *in*.

"How."

"The old reservoir. Up past the Tanner's Row cisterns, the one they drained twenty years ago when the aqueduct failed. Everyone thinks the bottom's just silt and broken pots." I licked my lips. My mouth had gone dry somewhere around the word *corridor*. "It's not. There's a stair at the bottom. Drowned — well, it was drowned, before the drainage. Now it's just dark and deep and it goes *down*. I found it when I was nine. I followed it three turns and then the dark started tasting me — tasting my fear, I mean, the wards down there, they taste you — and I ran. I've never told anyone. Telling anyone felt like handing them a knife pointed at me."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you're going anyway." I said it simply, because it was simply true. "Tonight or tomorrow, with or without me, you're going to walk into that mountain, and you'll do it with a map in your head and no idea what the dark down there does to people who taste afraid. I died once, Sable. The dark doesn't scare me the way it scares living people. And the wards that taste fear—" I hesitated. This was the part I'd rehearsed and hated. "They know me. Whatever signature's on my return-papers, the wards read it. When I touched the third turn, they hesitated. Like a dog smelling its master's coat on a stranger."

Silence. Sable stood, walked to the window, and looked out at the starless sky the way she probably looked at unfinished maps — measuring, refusing to flinch.

"You were dead," she said at last, "and someone bought you back."

"Or rented me. I try not to think about the terms."

"And you want to walk back toward the creditor." She turned. "Why? Truly. Not the brave answer."

I looked at my hands. Sixteen years old, calloused from hauling crates, and underneath the skin, if you knew how to look, faintly translucent in certain lights — the way paper gets when it's been erased and rewritten. I'd never told anyone that either.

"Because I've been on credit my whole life," I said. "Every day since the fever broke, I've been spending time that somebody else paid for, and I have never once known the price. It sits in my chest like a stone with a bill folded around it. If I go down there, maybe I find the ledger page with my name. Maybe I find out what I cost." My voice cracked, traitor. "Maybe I pay it myself before it comes due at the worst possible moment. Like, say, mid-rescue."

Sable crossed the room and did something she almost never did: she put her hand on the back of my neck, the way my mother used to before the fever, before everything, and steered my forehead down until it nearly touched hers.

"Tam," she said. "If the ledger has your name in it, we burn the page."

"It doesn't burn. It's contract-parchment. It—"

"We'll find a way it burns. I'm a cartographer. My entire profession is making the impossible fit on a page." Her eyes were fierce and close and absolutely certain, and I thought: *this is what it's like, having family that isn't blood.* It hurt more than blood ever had. "We go together. Down the drowned stair. And Tam — the fear-tasting wards. What do they cost you? Don't lie. Everything costs. I've learned that this week if nothing else."

I should have lied. She'd asked me not to joke and I should have answered with a lie, and instead I told her the truth, because she'd earned it with her forehead against mine.

"Warmth," I said. "Each time I calm them, I get colder. Not outside-cold. Inside-cold. The first time, at nine years old, I couldn't feel my feet for a week. I think eventually there's a version of me that calms one ward too many and just... stops. Goes back to the corridor and waits my turn properly this time."

Her hand tightened on my neck. "How many times?"

"Three, maybe four, before it gets dangerous. There are more than four wards between here and the court. I counted once, in a nightmare."

"Then we spend them like coin," Sable said, "and not one more than we must. I'll map the route so precisely you never touch a ward you don't have to. That's the trade. Your warmth for my ink."

"That's a terrible trade. Ink's cheap."

"Ink," said Sable Villalobos, "is the most expensive thing in the world. Ask anyone whose name is written in it."

* * *

We left before dawn, because the third night was coming and the herald had promised collection at its end, and neither of us said the obvious thing: that we might be walking into the mountain to die, and that the city we left behind would keep breathing either way, indifferent as weather.

At the reservoir rim, the old basin opened below us like a cupped grey hand. Silt and broken pots, just as everyone believed. And beneath the silt, invisible, the stair.

Sable knelt at the edge and unrolled her kit — pens, inks, the good paper she saved for commissions — and began, right there on the rim, to draw the descent before we made it. First the basin. Then the mouth of the stair, from my memory, which she extracted from me question by question like a surgeon pulling splinters. How many steps visible. Which direction the third turn bent. Where the dark had tasted me.

"You said it tastes fear," she murmured, inking. "So we go down without any."

"Easy for you. You're not the one it recognizes."

"No," she said. "But I'm the one it's *owed*. Fear's just interest, Tam. We're going down to argue principal." She capped her ink and looked up at me, and the sky behind her was still starless, still wrong, but her face was lit from inside by something stubborn and bright. "Ready?"

I looked at the stair going down into the dark where I had died's front hallway, and I thought about spoons under floorboards, and bills folded around stones, and a voice saying *wait your turn*.

"No," I said honestly.

"Good," said Sable. "Honest maps only. Come on."

And we stepped off the rim together, down into the cold, toward the first ward — and the dark, far below, tasted the air, and knew we were coming.
