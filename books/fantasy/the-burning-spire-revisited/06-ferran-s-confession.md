## 6. Ferran's Confession

I have been drunk in more interesting places than this, but not many.

The cellar beneath the old ward-house smells of mildew and rat piss and the particular staleness of a building that has been abandoned by everyone except the people too ashamed to leave. That last category is, at present, a population of one. Me. Odile Ferran, formerly Keeper of the Cinderfall Border Wards, currently Keeper of an Empty Bottle and a Headache the Size of the Vesper Range.

Three days since the stars went out. Three days since I sat in my chair with a cup of tea going cold and watched every light in the sky die like candles pinched by an unseen hand. Three days since I did what any competent professional would do upon witnessing the total collapse of her life's work.

I checked the registry. Then I drank.

The registry is why Sable Villalobos is standing in my doorway right now, holding a lantern she doesn't need because the boy behind her — Tam, the orphan from the charm-shop row, the one who sees things — is carrying a soul-lantern that glows green as pond scum. Where he got it, I don't want to know yet. I've learned to ration my wanting-to-know. It's how I've survived this long.

"You found something," Sable says. Not a question. She's got her mother's way of stating things, which is funny, because she doesn't know she has her mother's anything.

"Come in," I say, "before the whole street hears you accusing me of things."

"I haven't accused you of anything."

"You were about to. You have an accusing face. Very economical. Saves time." I wave them down the stairs. "Mind the third step. It's been minding itself for years and has developed opinions."

* * *

I clear a space on the table — which is to say I move the bottles from one configuration of mess into another — and lay out the registry page where they can see it. The founding contract. The one every ward-keeper swears on and no ward-keeper reads, because reading it requires decrypting the founder's cipher, and the founder's cipher requires a mind unclouded by drink, and you can see the problem the profession has always had.

"Thirteen names," I say.

Sable leans over the parchment. Tam hangs back, the way he does near anything official, like paperwork might bite him. Fair instinct, in this city.

"The histories say twelve," Sable says. "Twelve founders, twelve souls pledged to anchor the wards. It's in every schoolbook."

"The histories are wrong, or rather, the histories are *tidy*, which is worse. Count the lines."

She counts. Her finger stops at the thirteenth. There's a scorched place on the parchment, a burn the shape of a name, deliberate and precise — someone held a flame to that line until the ink gave up its secret, but parchment remembers what ink forgets. Under certain light, under the green wash of the boy's lantern, you can still read the ghost of the letters pressed into the vellum beneath.

Sable reads them aloud. Her voice comes out strange, flattened, like she's reading someone else's mail.

"Sable Villalobos."

"That's you," Tam says helpfully, because he is fourteen and believes in the power of confirmation.

"Yes, Tam, thank you."

I let her stand there with it for a moment. There's no kind way to do this. There's only the fast way and the slow way, and I've watched enough people take the slow way to know it just means arriving at the same cliff with worse blisters.

"Sit down," I say. "Both of you. This is a long story and I'm going to tell it badly, because I was there, and being there is the worst possible qualification for telling it."

* * *

"Fifteen years ago I wasn't a drunk. I want that on the record. I was a drunk with prospects."

Tam snorts. Sable doesn't smile, but something in her jaw loosens a fraction, and I'll take it.

"I was thirty-one years old and the youngest senior ward-keeper in Cinderfall's history, and I believed — this is important, I want you to understand that I *believed* — that the wards were failing. Because they were. The Hollowing had come within a half-mile of the eastern border stones. We'd lost two survey teams. The spire's fires were burning low and nobody could say why, and the magistrates of the Undercourt sent us an offer, written in that lovely copperplate they use, the handwriting of things that want your money or your soul and don't much care which.

"The founders went down the mountain to negotiate. Twelve of them. Merchants, guildmasters, the Archivist, old Mother Quill from the temple — good people, mostly, or people good enough, which in a crisis is the same coin at a slightly worse exchange rate. They came back with terms. Thirteen souls, pledged to the court beneath Mount Vesper, to feed the spire, to keep the wards alive. Twelve names signed willingly.

"And then there was a problem. The thirteenth soul had to be freely given too — that's the law of contracts down there, their one law, the law they enforce on themselves harder than any other: consent, freely given, or the whole instrument fails. And nobody else would volunteer. Can you blame them? Twelve brave founders and not one of them willing to be the thirteenth. I remember sitting in that room watching them look at each other, these heroes of the city, and every single one of them discovering the exact depth of their own courage, and it was shallower than they'd promised themselves."

I pour myself water. Water, tonight. Someone has to drive this story straight.

"A woman came to the ward-house that night. Alone. No appointment. She walked past my clerk like he was furniture, which, honestly, fair. She said, 'I hear you need a thirteenth name.' Just like that. No trembling. I've seen soldiers ask for leave with less spine.

"Her name was Isolde Villalobos."

Sable makes a sound. Small. Like a door opening in a house thought empty.

"She was a mapmaker," I go on, because if I stop now I never will. "A good one. Better than good — she'd charted the deep tunnels under the reservoir that nobody else would go near. She had a daughter, six years old, who she'd raised alone since the father died. And she sat across that table from me and the Archivist and she laid out her terms like she was haggling over cloth.

"'Take my soul,' she said. 'But here's what I want in exchange. My daughter keeps her freedom. Magic's going to be outlawed — we all know it's coming, the panic's already writing the laws — and when it is, my daughter will be hunted, because she has the talent, you can see it in her already, the mapping-sense, the way she draws places she's never been. So here is my price. Take my soul, and take her memory of me. All of it. Every recollection. Let her grow up believing she's nobody's daughter, nobody's heir, nobody's mark. Let her be free precisely because she doesn't know what she's free *from*.'"

I look at Sable. She's very still. People think stillness is calm. Stillness is what happens when everything inside a person goes quiet so it can listen better.

"The court accepted," I say. "Of course they accepted. A soul plus a memory? That's a bargain to them. They wrote it into the contract — her clause, her words, nearly verbatim. I watched her sign. I watched the ink take the name off the page and put it somewhere else. And then she looked at me and said, 'Keeper Ferran, when she asks about me someday — and she will, children always dig — tell her the truth. Not gently. Gently is just lying with soft hands.'

"So." I spread my hands. "Not gently: your mother sold herself to the Undercourt to buy your life, and the currency she added on top was you. Your childhood. Fifteen years of Sundays. The sound of her voice. Whether she liked honey in her tea. Whether she sang. Whether she—"

"Stop," Sable says.

I stop. Not gently. But I stop.

* * *

For a while the only sound is the lantern hissing its green hiss and the drip in the corner that I've named Gerald, because naming your problems makes them feel manageable and I am nothing if not a woman of coping strategies.

"Why didn't anyone tell me?" Sable says finally. Her voice is level in the way water is level right before it goes over the edge. "Fifteen years. Someone in this city knew. The Archivist knew. *You* knew. You sold me charms, Odile. You bought luck-knots off my stall and paid full price and never once—"

"I paid full price because your charms work and I needed the luck," I say. "And I didn't tell you because I signed a condition of confidentiality into the contract's witness clause, and because—" and here it is, the part I've been circling, the part that tastes worse than the cheap gin, "—because telling you felt like spending her. She paid for your ignorance. Who was I to give you a refund?"

"That's not your choice!"

"No," I agree. "It isn't. It was hers. That's the whole rotten architecture of it, girl. She chose. She chose so completely that she chose away your ability to ever know she'd chosen. That's not sacrifice, exactly. Sacrifice leaves a wound people can see. What she did was closer to theft — she stole your grief before you could feel it, stole it on your behalf, without asking. I've spent fifteen years deciding whether that was love or arrogance, and I'll tell you my conclusion, which is that it was both, because it always is, with mothers."

Tam shifts in his chair. He's been quiet, which for Tam is like a river being quiet — you know there's a lot moving underneath. "The herald called her name," he says. "Out loud. In the street. If the contract's fifteen years old, why collect now?"

And there it is. The question I've been drinking around for three days.

"Because the contract is due," I say. "Thirteen souls pledged to fuel the spire, and the spire burns through them slow, like a furnace burning wet wood. Twelve souls lasted twelve years past their term because the founders built slack into the terms. The thirteenth — Isolde's — was different. It was pledged with a rider. A deferral. Her soul wouldn't be collected until her daughter came of age and the daughter's memory-payment matured. Twenty-one years. It's arithmetic, Tam. The bill came due because the calendar did."

"But the herald said three nights," Sable says. "Why give me three nights? If I'm just... collateral. An inheritance. Why negotiate?"

I should lie to her. Old habit; lying to frightened people is most of what municipal governance is. But she asked me not to be gentle, and I find, to my considerable annoyance, that I want to honor that.

"Because of a clause," I say. "The last clause. Her final line, the one she made the magistrates write in before she'd sign. I memorized it that night, standing witness, and I've tried for fifteen years to forget it, and I have failed comprehensively, because I have a memory like a debt collector."

I close my eyes. I can see the copperplate. I can see her hand steady on the table beside it.

"*Let my daughter forget me,*" I recite, "*so that she may choose freely.*"

I open my eyes. Sable is looking at me like I've handed her something heavy and she hasn't decided whether it's a gift or a verdict.

"Choose freely," she repeats.

"The court honors its clauses, Sable. It's the only thing it honors. Which means that whatever happens in three nights — collection, negotiation, rewriting, ruin — the choice of what happens next was never mine, never the founders', never even hers, not in the end. She structured the whole damned contract so that the final signature would be yours." I lean back. My chair creaks its agreement. "You're not the debt, girl. You're the signatory. And down in that court, the signatory is the one person who can rewrite the terms."

Tam sits forward. Even the green lantern seems to brighten, though I suspect that's my eyes playing tricks, or hope, which is the same thing.

"So we go down there," Sable says.

It isn't a question either. She has her mother's way of stating things. Now she knows it.

"We," I say. "Interesting pronoun. Last I checked, 'we' included a disgraced ward-keeper whose primary skill is decryption and whose secondary skill is falling down stairs."

"You know the mountain's workings. You know the contract law. And you owe me fifteen years of Sundays."

I look at her — this woman who is a stranger and also the exact image of her mother at that age, the same stubborn set of the shoulders, the same cartographer's eyes already drawing the route before the route exists — and I think: Isolde, you magnificent, infuriating ghost, you knew. You knew she'd come asking, and you knew I'd be too guilty to refuse her, and you arranged the whole board fifteen years in advance.

Mothers. Both and arrogant, both and correct.

"Fine," I say, reaching for the bottle and then, with heroic effort, not. "Fine. We go down the mountain. But first someone tells me how the dead boy got a soul-lantern, because if we're walking into the Undercourt I want to know exactly what kind of trouble is carrying our light."

Tam grins at me, and it's not entirely a comforting grin.

"That," he says, "is a longer story than yours."
