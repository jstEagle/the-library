## 7. The Cartographer's Oath

There is a particular kind of quiet that settles over a room after someone tells you the truth about your own life. It isn't peace. It's more like the silence after an avalanche — the sound of everything having already fallen and nothing yet knowing where to land.

Odile Ferran sat across my worktable with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had no intention of drinking, and told me that my mother had sold her soul so I could forget her. That the founders of Cinderfall had bought the city's safety with thirteen lives, and hers was one of them. That my fifteen years of hiding in the charm-shop slums, selling luck to people who needed it, weren't escape at all. They were the purchase.

I should have cried. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is going to matter a great deal in what follows. But what I felt instead was something colder and far more useful: the specific clarity of a cartographer looking at a map that has been lying to her.

Every map I'd ever drawn of my life had Cinderfall at its center and a blank space where my childhood should have been. I'd told myself for years that the blankness was grief, that grief simply erodes some places worse than others. It never occurred to me that the blankness had edges. That it had been *drawn*. That somewhere, in a ledger beneath a mountain, someone had taken a pen to the map of me and inked out the coastline.

Well. I am, whatever else I am, a woman who corrects maps.

* * *

Let me tell you about the old cartographers' covenant, because nobody teaches it anymore and because it is about to become extremely relevant.

Before the outlawing of magic, before the wards, before Cinderfall learned to survive on belief alone, mapmakers were bound by oath. Not to kings, not to guilds — to their routes. When a cartographer accepted a commission into unmapped country, she would mix her ink with three drops of her own blood and write the terms on her own skin: *I will go where this map goes. I will not turn back while the route remains untraveled. What the map costs, I will pay.* The oath wasn't symbolic. The old texts are clear on this, in the dry way old texts are clear about horrifying things. A mapmaker who broke her covenant found her maps unraveling — literally, the lines crawling off the vellum like worms — and worse, found herself beginning to unravel in sympathy, as though the country she'd abandoned had decided to abandon her back.

The covenant worked both ways, though. A mapmaker who kept it, who walked every line she drew no matter what waited at the end of it, was said to be *true-marked* — and true-marked mapmakers could walk places other people couldn't. Wards parted for them. Doors that had no doors opened. The land itself, the old books claimed, recognized a kept promise the way a dog recognizes its master.

I learned all this at fourteen, from a smuggled primer I bought off a peddler with three weeks of charm-shop earnings, and I thought then that it was the most beautiful thing I had ever read. A profession whose central sacrament was *meaning what you say*. I practiced the lettering for months. I never took the oath, because there was nowhere I needed to go badly enough to pay for it in blood.

I need to go somewhere now.

* * *

Tam found me at dawn, sitting at my worktable with the shutters open, watching a city without stars pretend very hard to be morning.

"You didn't sleep," he said. It wasn't a question. Tam doesn't ask questions he can answer by looking at you; questions cost something, and the boy hoards like a dragon.

"I slept," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and we let it stand between us the way old friends let lies stand — as a courtesy, a small flag planted on neutral ground.

He climbed onto the stool across from me, all elbows and watchfulness, and set something on the table between us. A stub of candle, half-burned. "For the dark," he said. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case you decide to be stupid." He said it flatly, without heat. Tam has the rare gift of delivering insults like weather reports. "Ferran told you. About your mother. About the contract. And now you've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look people get right before they do something that gets them killed." He picked at the candle's wick. "I've seen it. On the dead ones."

"Charming."

"I'm not trying to be charming. I'm trying to keep you alive, and apparently flattery doesn't work on you, so." He shrugged. "Run. That's my professional recommendation. Run north, run fast, change your name, grow a beard if you have to. The herald gave you three nights. That's enough time to be a rumor instead of a person."

"And leave you? Leave Ferran? Leave the shop, and Mrs. Halloway next door who thinks my fever-charms keep her rheumatism quiet?" I shook my head. "Tam, the herald didn't come for Sable-the-charmseller. It came for a name. Names can be buried, but they don't stay buried when someone with a black-scaled face starts digging. If I run, it follows. And it knows where everyone I love sleeps."

"So fight it."

"With what?"

"That," he admitted, "is the problem."

I laughed — actually laughed, short and ugly, the laugh of a woman standing at the edge of something enormous. "No, see, that's where you're wrong. That's where everyone's wrong. It's not the problem. It's the *job*."

Tam looked at me the way he looks at customers who try to haggle over charms that cost a copper. Patiently. With a certain pity.

"Ferran told me the contract can be rewritten by the signatory alone," I said. "That's what the herald's riddle meant. *The court keeps what it buys, but what is bought may bargain.* Fifteen years ago, someone signed for me — my mother, or the founders using her hand, I don't even know yet, and I'll get to that. Either way, the signature that binds me is mine by inheritance. Which means the contract is a document. And documents—" I tapped the table, where my inks sat in their neat row like soldiers, "—documents have architecture. They have a heart. Somewhere under Mount Vesper there is a page with thirteen names on it, twelve recorded and one burned away, and that page is the single point at which everything the Undercourt does becomes possible. You don't fight a court. You don't outrun a debt. But a *page*, Tam—"

"A page can be torn," he said slowly.

"A page can be *rewritten*." I heard my own voice go strange, go lit, the way it does when a coastline finally resolves out of fog. "But first I have to find it. First I have to map the unmappable. The caverns under Vesper shift — the herald said so itself, said the ways down are 'kept moving' so no thief can learn them. Fine. Let them move. Rivers move too, and I've mapped rivers. Weather moves, and I've mapped weather. The only thing that can't be mapped is a thing that refuses to hold still long enough to be *loved* by a line, and I refuse to believe the Undercourt qualifies."

Tam stared at me. Then he did something I hadn't seen him do since the stars went out: he smiled. Small, crooked, gone again in a second, but real.

"You're going to draw it," he said. "You're going to *map* hell."

"I'm going to survey it," I corrected. "Mapping comes after. Surveying is just paying attention with a pen in your hand."

"And the part where it kills you?"

"The part where it kills me," I agreed, "is a detail. Details go on the legend."

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I record it precisely because precision is the only prayer I know.

I cleared my worktable. I laid out the good paper — the vellum I'd been saving for seven years for a commission worth dying for, which is either tragic foresight or comedy, depending on how the next few chapters of my life resolve. I ground fresh ink, black oak-gall, the batch I make myself because commercial ink fades and I have a professional horror of fading. I sharpened three pens to points fine enough to draw a hair's breadth.

And then I rolled up my left sleeve, and I took out the needle.

The old covenant requires blood in the ink. Three drops. I won't pretend my hands were steady. There is a difference between deciding to die for something and being calm about it, and I have never once confused the two. But I opened the pad of my thumb with the same lancet I use for pricking parchment registration holes, and I let three drops fall into the well, and I watched them bloom through the black like smoke through water, and I thought: *this is the price made literal.* This is what the whole world runs on, underneath — blood in the ink, memory in the flame, souls in the ledger. Everyone pays. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is *what for*.

My mother paid so I could forget her. Wren — whoever Wren had been, whoever Wren was now — had paid his memory of me for my freedom. Odile had paid fifteen years of disgrace and drink. Even Tam had paid once, an infant's death traded back for a life that could see the seams of the world. Every person I loved was a receipt.

Fine. Then let me be a receipt too. Let me be the most legible one the Undercourt has ever collected.

I wrote the oath on the inside of my forearm, where the skin is thin and the ink takes deep, in the formal script of the old guild — the lettering I'd practiced at fourteen, every stroke coming back to my hand like it had been waiting fifteen years for permission:

*I, Sable Villalobos, called the thirteenth name, take up the covenant of my craft. I will go where this map goes. I will survey the courts beneath Mount Vesper and every shifting way between here and the contract's heart. I will not turn back while the route remains untraveled. What the map costs, I will pay, and I will pay it in full, and I will pay it *last*.*

That last clause was mine. The old covenant says nothing about paying last. But I have spent fifteen years watching other people pay for me — my mother, my lover, my city — and if I am going to add my blood to that economy, I am going to add it at the end of the transaction, where it can do the most good and be spent the most deliberately. Call it a rider. Call it stubbornness. Call it the only term I have ever negotiated from strength.

The ink burned going in. Of course it burned. Blood-ink burns; that's how you know it's taking. I held my arm steady over the vellum and watched the letters rise on my skin, raised and dark, permanent as scar tissue, and I felt something settle in me that I hadn't felt since the stars went out.

Not courage. Courage is for moments. This was orientation. For fifteen years I had been a woman without coordinates — hidden, unrecorded, a blank space walking around in a stolen shape. Now I had a heading. South and down. Toward the mountain. Toward the page. Toward the truth of what I cost and who paid it, and whether any of it could be bought back.

A mapmaker who keeps her covenant is true-marked, the old book said. The land recognizes a kept promise.

Let's find out if hell does.

* * *

By the time I finished binding the wound — wax and linen, the way you'd treat any ink-work, because infection would be an embarrassing way to fail a quest — Odile had appeared in my doorway with the particular expression of a woman who has been standing there long enough to hear everything and is pretending otherwise.

"That was the covenant," she said. Not a question either. Ward-keepers and orphans, I've noticed, share a horror of asking what they can deduce.

"It was."

"Do you understand what you've done? Truly? Because I knew a mapmaker once, before the outlawing, who took that oath for a commission into the Graywolds. She came back in a box. Her maps came back fine. Whatever she found in there, she paid for it and the *maps survived her*." Odile crossed the room and took my wrist, turned my arm over, examined the raised letters with a professional's frown. "This isn't bravery, girl. This is a contract. You've signed yourself."

"Yes," I said. "I know exactly what I've done. I've done the only thing I know how to do. Someone erased me from the world's records, Odile. My own mother's face, my own childhood, my own name — all of it filed away under a mountain, priced and owned. I have spent my whole adult life drawing coastlines for cities that will never visit them, and now the one coastline that matters, the one *I* live on, is in somebody else's vault." I pulled my sleeve down over the oath. "I'm not brave. I'm a professional with a grievance. It's honestly the most dangerous combination there is."

Something moved across Odile's face — guilt, probably, the permanent weather of hers — and then she reached into her coat and set something on my table beside the vellum.

An old brass instrument. A bearing-finder, the kind ward-keepers used to align their towers before the wards fell. Its glass was cracked and its needle spun lazily, drunk, pointing at nothing — but it was the real thing, pre-outlawing, made in an age when instruments were allowed to mean something.

"It doesn't work anymore," she said. "Nothing works anymore. But it worked on the day the wards were built, and it was calibrated to the ley-line that runs under Vesper, and if any part of that line still breathes, this needle will twitch toward it. Even once. Even wrong." She pushed it across the table. "Take it. Consider it my contribution to your grievance."

"Odile—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me implies I'm forgiven, and I'm not, and we both know it." She turned for the door, then stopped. "The reservoir," she said, without turning around. "The boy's been asking about the drowned stair. He asked me if the old service tunnels under the reservoir still connect to the mountain's roots, and I told him I didn't know, and that was a lie. They do. Or they did, twenty years ago, when I still bothered to read the maintenance charts. Whether they *still* do — whether the Undercourt has flooded them or filled them or folded them into one of its moving ways — I can't say. Nobody can. That's rather the point of unmappable."

She looked back at me then, and for one unguarded moment the drunk and the disgraced ward-keeper fell away and I saw what was underneath: a woman who had helped forge a contract with thirteen souls and had been drowning in the arithmetic of it for fifteen years.

"Three nights," she said. "The herald gave you three nights. Spend the first one getting to the water. Spend the second one getting under it. And spend the third—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the arithmetic of it, "—spend the third doing whatever a true-marked mapmaker does at the heart of a thing like that. Because if you're right, if the contract really can be rewritten by the signatory alone, then everything depends on you reaching that page while you're still *you*. While the name on your skin is still the name in their ledger. After collection night, there's no rewriting anything. There's only keeping."

* * *

That night I sat up with my new map — the first sheet of the survey, blank except for two marks. At the top, a small circle: Cinderfall, my home, drawn with more care than I have ever given any city, because I finally understood that I was drawing it the way you draw a portrait of someone you might not see again. At the bottom, far down past the fold of the paper, a question mark where Mount Vesper's roots should be.

Between them, nothing. Unsurveyed country. The honest kind of blank — not erased, not inked out, just *not yet known*, waiting for someone to come and pay attention to it.

Tam slept curled in the chair by my window, the candle stub burning down beside him, on watch against threats neither of us could name. The city outside lay starless and stubborn, ten thousand hearths pretending at light. Somewhere beneath us, beneath the reservoir, beneath the roots of the mountain, a page with thirteen names waited in the dark, and one of those names was a burn-scar where mine used to be.

I dipped my pen. I wrote at the top of the sheet, in the formal hand, the title every true survey carries:

*The Undercourt of Mount Vesper: Being an Honest Map of the Ways Down, Surveyed by Sable Villalobos, True-Marked, Who Means Every Word of This.*

Then I blew out the candle, woke the boy, and told him to get his boots.

"We're going to the reservoir," I said. "Tonight. Before I lose my nerve, or my nerve loses me."

Tam was already lacing. He'd been ready, I think, since the moment I rolled up my sleeve. "The drowned stair," he said quietly. "I know the way to the water. I don't know the way under it."

"No," I said, checking the bearing-finder's cracked glass, watching the dead needle shiver — just once, just barely — toward the south and the dark. "But the map doesn't know either. Yet."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to be *true*," I said. "Everything else is negotiable."

We went out together into the starless streets, toward the water, toward the down.
