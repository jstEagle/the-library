## 8. Down the Drowned Stair

The drowned stair beneath the old reservoir smells like a coin held too long in your mouth.

I know that's a strange thing to lead with, but I've had time to think about it, wading through the dark with water up to my knees and a dead man's memory rattling around in my skull like a pebble in an empty jar. You get particular about smells when you've been dead once. Everything alive gets louder. Bread. Rain. Sable's ink. And this — cold metal and old blood and something underneath it all that I can only describe as *paid for*.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I said, because somebody had to.

Sable didn't answer right away. She was ahead of me, one hand on the wall, the other holding her lantern low so the light wouldn't catch the surface of the water and give away our position to — well. To whatever was down here keeping position on us. She'd inked her oath onto her forearm two nights ago, and the lines of it had gone strange since we entered the tunnel, the letters shifting like they were trying to spell something the tunnel preferred. She kept glancing at it the way you'd glance at a dog that had started growling at an empty corner.

"Because," she said finally, "running didn't work for fifteen years, and I only found that out last week. I'm a fast learner. Slow starter, but fast learner."

"That's not a plan. That's a personality."

"It's the beginning of a plan. Plans are just personalities with a route." She paused, and I heard her foot find the first step down. "Careful. The stair's real, but it's not *reliable*."

That's the thing about Sable Villalobos. She says things like that like they're normal. *The stair's real but it's not reliable.* Like the world is a badly drawn map and she's the only one who's read the legend.

I followed her down.

* * *

Here's what I haven't told her yet, and what I'm telling you instead, because a confession needs a listener and the dark down here is the best listener I've ever had:

I remember dying.

Not the way people remember a fever, all secondhand and smeared. I remember it the way you remember a room you grew up in. I was an infant. I know how that sounds — how can you remember being an infant — but that's the point, isn't it? I wasn't supposed to remember anything. Whatever took me, whatever gave me back, it left the door open on its way out, and I've been looking through that door my whole life.

What I remember is this: a cold that wasn't cold, exactly. It was more like being *unheld*. Everything I'd been, all that new baby warmth and hunger and the sound of my own name being said over a cradle — it all just stopped being *mine*. And then something enormous and patient looked at me the way a clerk looks at a ledger entry, and decided the column didn't balance, and put me back.

I came back wrong in small ways. I don't feel the cold the way other people do. I dream in the dark under the dark. And I can see the Undercourt's workings — the seams in the world where somebody's soul got spent to hold something shut. The wards over Cinderfall used to look like a net of gold thread to me. Now they're a net of gold thread with the middle burned out, and through the hole, the stars are gone, and something on the other side of the hole is looking *in*.

So when the wards in this tunnel woke up and started tasting us, I knew what to do. That's the ugly gift of it. The wards don't taste fear, exactly. They taste *aliveness* — the heat of a soul, the particular flavor of a person who doesn't want to stop being one. And I'm the only person I know who can walk up to that flavor and make it go quiet, because I've already been on the other side of the counter. I know what it's like to be the thing being tasted.

The first ward hit us about forty steps down.

* * *

It came out of the water like a thought you didn't mean to think. One second the tunnel was tunnel — wet stone, dripping dark, Sable's lantern swinging — and the next there was a *presence* in the water around our legs, something old and procedural, something that had been set here to ask one question and keep asking it until the end of the world.

*WHAT COMES DOWN*, it asked, in the way that water asks, which is to say all at once and through your teeth.

I felt Sable go rigid beside me. I felt her fear the way you feel a change in air pressure — she was terrified, and she was trying to be a cartographer about it, cataloguing the ward even as it catalogued her. I heard her whisper, "Ward. Old. Founding-era. It's tasting us," and I thought, *of course it is, everything down here is tasting us, we're the only warm things in a cold cellar and we just walked into the pantry.*

"Tam," she said. "Can you—"

"I know."

I put my hand into the water.

And I did the thing I do, the thing I've never been able to explain to anyone because there's no one to explain it to who hasn't died, which is a very short list with one name on it. I reached for the memory of my own death — the unheldness, the enormous patient clerk, the moment when I stopped being *Tam* and started being *a discrepancy* — and I held it up to the ward like showing a lamp to a moth.

*Nothing to taste here*, I told it, in the language of the unheld. *I'm already yours. I'm just visiting.*

The ward stilled. The water went flat and polite. Somewhere in the dark below us, I felt other wards turn their attention away, reassured, the way dogs lie back down when the master whistles.

And the cost came, the way it always comes.

It's hard to describe what it's like to lose warmth. Not heat — I said that wrong. Warmth. The inner kind. The kind that makes your hands want to hold things and your mouth want to smile at stupid jokes and your chest feel like a house with a fire in it instead of a house with the fire *sold*. Each time I open that door and show the wards my death, a little of it goes out of me and doesn't come back. I've been doing the arithmetic in my head since we started down. I've got maybe a dozen more uses in me before I'm something that walks and talks and remembers being a boy but can't quite remember why being a boy was *good*.

Sable looked at me in the lantern light, and I watched her do the math too. She's good at math. It's one of the things I'd die for, her doing math — which, given where we were, felt less like a figure of speech than I'd have liked.

"That's the second time," she said quietly. "On the way in from the reservoir it was once. Now twice. Tam, how many—"

"Don't."

"Tam."

"Ask me later," I said, "when the answer is a better number."

She didn't push it. That's the other thing about her — she knows when a map has a region on it marked *here be dragons*, and she doesn't drag you into it. She just put her hand on my shoulder, brief and warm — and I mean *warm*, actually warm, the kind of warm I'm spending down here to buy — and said, "Then we make the uses count. Come on. I want to see a lantern before I lose my nerve."

* * *

The soul-lanterns started about an hour down.

At first I thought they were reflections. Little green smudges in the water, far off, the way foxfire looks when it's thinking about whether to exist. But reflections don't breathe, and these breathed. Slow. Patient. In and out, in and out, like the tunnel itself was sleeping with a candle under the blanket.

Sable stopped on a landing where the stair widened into a half-flooded gallery, and she raised the lantern, and we both just — stood there. Looking.

They hung in the flooded dark like fruit on a tree nobody had planted: dozens of them, then hundreds, receding into the black in ranks, each one a soft green flame sealed inside glass that had gone milky with age. And here's the thing I can't unsay, the thing that curdled the wonder right out of the moment: I *knew* what was in them.

Not who. Not names. But what. Each lantern held a soul — or the part of a soul the court could bottle without killing the person, which is a distinction the court would want you to know they're very proud of. And the flames weren't burning fuel. They were burning *memory*. I could see it, the way I see all the court's workings: little dark flecks in the green, rising off the flame like sparks, and each fleck was a piece of somebody's life. A face. A voice. A first snow, maybe, or a wedding dance, or the smell of a specific kitchen on a specific morning. All of it going up as light, all of it going out as exhaust, and somewhere far above us, past all that stone, the wards of Cinderfall drinking the smoke and calling it safety.

"They're people," Sable whispered. She had her sketchbook out — of course she did, the woman would sketch the inside of her own grave — and her hand was shaking so badly the lines came out wrong, and she redrew them, and they came out wrong again, and she kept going. "Tam, these are *people*. Someone is down here keeping people like — like *pantry goods*—"

"Like fuel," I said.

She looked at me. And I watched her arrive at it, the same thought I'd been carrying since the herald said her name: that the wards she'd lived under her whole life weren't a wall. They were a *stove*. And the city she loved had been warm for fifteen years because somebody, somewhere down here, was burning.

One of the lanterns nearest us flickered. Just once. And I swear — I swear on the door I died behind — the flame leaned toward Sable. Toward her, the way a plant leans at a window. And in the green light I saw something in the glass that might have been a reflection of her face, except the reflection was *older*, and it was *smiling*, and Sable's face wasn't.

"Tam," she said, very quietly. "That lantern knows me."

I stepped between her and it. It's stupid — it's glass and a flame — but I put my body there anyway, because that's the whole job of being somebody's family, isn't it? You stand between them and the things that know them too well.

"We keep moving," I said. "We don't touch them. We don't answer them. Whatever they know about you, we find out from the ledger, not from a bottle."

She nodded. But as we waded on, past rank after rank of breathing green, she kept looking back over her shoulder, and I kept watching the cost of my last ward-calm settle into my chest like wet snow, and neither of us said the thing we were both thinking.

The thing we were both thinking was: *one of those lanterns is her mother*.

* * *

We made camp on a dry shelf of stone where the stair bent westward, and I took first watch, because sleeping is when the cold gets loudest in me and I didn't want Sable to see it.

From the shelf, I could look back the way we'd come and see the lantern-field glowing far below, a green constellation in the drowned dark. And I could look forward, down the westward bend, where the water ran shallower and the air tasted different — less like a coin, more like *paper*. Like documents. Like somewhere ahead, the court kept its filing.

Sable slept badly, the way she has all week, murmuring names into her collar. I counted the ones I recognized. Her mother's. Mine. And one she said differently from all the others — softer, the way you say a word you've been chewing for years without knowing its taste. *Wren.* I didn't ask. There are regions on her map marked *here be dragons* too, and I'm learning to read her the way she reads the world.

Somewhere in the middle of my watch, the lantern-field flickered. All of them at once. In and out, in and out, like a held breath.

And then, from far down the westward bend — from the paper-smelling dark where the court keeps its filing — I heard footsteps. Unhurried. Wet. Coming up the stair toward us, one at a time, patient as a clerk.

Sable's eyes opened. She'd heard it too.

"Tam," she whispered. "How many uses have you got left?"

I did the arithmetic. It came out worse than I'd told her.

"Enough," I said, and stood up, and reached for the door I died behind.
