## 9. The Market of Kept Things

The door I died behind, as it turned out, was less a door than a suggestion.

Tam pressed his palm flat against the reservoir wall — the same wall I'd mapped three times in my career and annotated each time with *solid bedrock, no passage* — and the stone remembered being something else. It went soft the way candle wax goes soft, not melting so much as reconsidering. And through it came a smell I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe: old paper, river water, and something sweet underneath it all, like honey left too long in a copper pot.

"That's the smell of kept things," Tam said. He said it flatly, the way you'd say *that's rain*. "I smelled it when I was small. Before."

"Before," I repeated, because we had an agreement: he could use that word without explanation, and I would not flinch at it. We were both very disciplined about our agreements. It's what happens to people who've lost things — they get precise.

Odile went first, because Odile always insisted on going first and because arguing with her cost more energy than any of us had to spare. Her ward-keeper's badge hung crooked on her coat, and she'd stopped pretending she wasn't wearing it. Fifteen years disgraced, and the woman still walked into the dark like the dark owed her an apology.

The tunnel beyond the wall sloped down in long, breathing curves. Not carved — *grown*, if stone can be said to grow, which down there I began to suspect it could. The walls sweated a faint luminescence, pale as skimmed milk, enough light to walk by and no more. My cartographer's hands itched. I wanted to stop every ten paces and sketch, and I couldn't, because the corridor changed behind us.

I know because I checked. Twice. The second time, the curve we'd walked through had become a straight run, and the seam where Tam had opened the wall was gone entirely.

"Don't map it," Tam said quietly, watching me stare. "It doesn't like being measured. It'll move faster if you try."

"Everything moves faster if you try," I said. "That's been my experience of most things worth having."

He almost smiled. Down there, in the milk-light, with the wet footsteps of whatever clerk-thing still echoing somewhere behind us, it was the bravest thing I'd seen all week.

* * *

We heard the market before we saw it.

Not noise, exactly. More like the memory of noise — a murmur with all the words sanded off, the sound a crowd makes heard through several walls and one bad decision. Then the tunnel opened its throat and gave us the bazaar.

I have drawn markets my whole life. The spice souks of Cinderfall's lower terraces, the fish auctions at dawn, the night market that sprang up illegally under the aqueduct every full moon until the wardens gave up and taxed it instead. I thought I understood what a market *was*: goods, greed, gossip, the ancient human machinery of wanting things and paying for them.

The Undercourt's bazaar is that machinery, stripped of everything human except the wanting.

It filled a cavern the size of the city's Grand Basin, tiered in rings descending toward a center I couldn't see. Stalls — but the stalls were made of shelved bone, or petrified wood, or in some cases simply *arranged air*, held in shape by nothing I could name. Lanterns burned green everywhere, the soul-lanterns from the stair, and their light made everyone look drowned. And the merchants —

The merchants were mostly people. That was the worst part. I'd braced for monsters. Instead: a woman with kind eyes selling years out of stoppered jars, each one faintly luminous, labeled in a hand like a schoolteacher's. A man whose stall displayed nothing but names, written on slips and pinned like moths — *Marisol Vane, thrice-used, good condition; Anselm Bright, never spoken aloud, pristine.* A child-sized figure — I chose not to determine whether it was a child — trading in firsts. First steps. First kisses. First time seeing the sea. Each one bottled, each one glowing softly, each one priced.

"Keep your hood up," Odile murmured. "And your face stupid. Curiosity reads as appetite down here, and appetite gets appraised."

"I'm a cartographer," I whispered back. "Curiosity is my entire personality."

"Then be a boring one."

We moved into the crowd, and here is what I learned about the Undercourt's customers: they were also mostly people. Living ones. I watched a gray-bearded man count out coins that gleamed wrong — too bright, too warm, coins that I slowly understood were *days of his life*, minted and spent — to buy a small vial of something amber. He cradled it like a newborn. When he unstopped it and breathed in, he wept, silently, and mouthed a word I couldn't hear, and walked away lighter and poorer and, by the set of his shoulders, content.

Everything lost is purchasable here. That's the whole architecture of the place. Grief, monetized. Longing, with a stall of its own and reasonable hours.

"Villalobos." Odile's hand closed on my arm, hard. "Don't look left."

I looked left immediately. I am who I am.

There was a stall tucked between a dealer in lullabies and a broker of grudges — grudges, priced by vintage — and the merchant there was tall and thin and wrapped in gray cloth that moved like it was thinking. And on the shelf before it, in a row of perhaps forty vials, glowed a collection of lights I knew.

I knew them the way you know your own handwriting.

Small gold lights, each one different. One flickered with the rhythm of someone humming. One held the color of a kitchen at dusk. One was just warmth — plain, ordinary warmth, the kind that comes from standing near a stove while bread bakes, and I felt my chest crack open along a seam I hadn't known was there, because I didn't know why those things hurt, I only knew that they did, deep in the scar tissue where fifteen years ago something had been cut out of me and the wound had healed over smooth and blank.

The label on the row was written in a careful, loving hand:

*Childhood of Sable Villalobos. Forty-one pieces. Held in trust per contract. NOT FOR SALE.*

"Not for sale," I read aloud, and my voice came out strange.

"That's worse," Tam said beside me. He was staring at the vials too, and his breath fogged in air that shouldn't have been cold. "If it were for sale, you could buy it back. *Held in trust* means somebody's still paying on it. Means it's collateral."

Collateral. My mother's bargain, sitting on a shelf in a dead woman's handwriting — no. Not a dead woman's. A handwriting I didn't remember knowing, which was somehow crueler, because my hand reached toward it anyway, the way a hand reaches toward a stove it has been told about but never touched.

The gray-wrapped merchant turned. Where a face should have been there was a smooth expanse of parchment, and across it, ink bloomed letter by letter, patient as the footsteps on the stair:

*You are looking at your inheritance.*

"I'm looking at stolen goods," I said.

*Stolen?* The ink rippled, almost amused. *Every piece freely given. Read your mother's line sometime, little signatory. She wrote beautifully.*

My knees nearly went. Tam caught my elbow. And the merchant leaned closer, and the parchment face tilted, and the next line of ink wrote itself slow, savoring it:

*Of course — held things can be released. Contracts can be rewritten. By the signatory alone. If you find the ledger room, and if you can pay the reading fee.*

"What's the fee?" Odile demanded, stepping between us with her broken badge held up like a torch.

The ink paused. Then, gently, terribly:

*You already know. Everything here costs memory, ward-keeper. Even answers. Especially answers.*

* * *

We pulled back into a side alcove formed of stacked ledgers taller than houses, bindings cracked, titles worn away. Tam slid down the stack until he was sitting, arms around his knees, shivering though the cavern was warm.

"It offered," he said. "Did you hear it? It *offered* to tell you how to get your childhood back."

"It also told me the price is memory," I said. "Pay memory to buy memories. It's a snake eating its own tail, Tam. That's not commerce, that's a trap with a shopfront."

"Everything down here is a trap with a shopfront," Odile said grimly. She was scanning the crowd over my shoulder, cataloguing exits the way only an ex-ward-keeper can. "But note what it called you. *Signatory.* It wants you to go to the ledger room. The court doesn't leave doors open by accident."

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe a mother's handwriting on a shelf made me stupid for a moment, and the court knows exactly how stupid grief makes people."

I looked back out at the bazaar. At the year-seller lighting a lamp for a customer. At the name-broker pinning up a fresh slip. At the whole vast ringed engine of keeping, humming away beneath the mountain, feeding something above us that I was beginning to guess the shape of.

Forty-one pieces of me, glowing gold on a shelf. A kitchen at dusk. Bread baking. Someone humming.

I didn't know who hummed. That was the thing that finally made me turn away — not fear, not sense, but the simple unbearable fact that I could stand in front of my own childhood and not know who used to hum.

"We keep moving," I said. "Toward the center. Toward the spire."

"Sable—" Tam started.

"And when this is over," I said, "when the contract's settled one way or another — I'm coming back for those vials. Every one. Even if I have to buy them with the memory of buying them."

Somewhere behind us, soft as a page turning, the gray merchant's parchment face wrote one last line, and whether it was meant for me or merely overheard, I have carried it since:

*They all say that. The ones who come back are rarer than the ones who pay.*

We went deeper. And at the mouth of the next gallery, past a stall where a merchant sold nothing but the sound of doors closing, a man looked up from a table covered in sketches — hundreds of sketches, all of the same face, drawn over and over in increasingly desperate detail —

and the face in the drawings was mine.
