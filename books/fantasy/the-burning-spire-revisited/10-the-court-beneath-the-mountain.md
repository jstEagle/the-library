## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I had been drunk for eleven days when the summons came, and I want it noted for the record that I answered sober. Mostly sober. Sober enough that my hands only shook from the cold and not the drink, which in Cinderfall counts as a moral victory.

The summons arrived the way everything from below arrives — through the floor. I was sleeping off a bottle of gutter-rot brandy in the back room of what used to be the ward-house, on a cot I'd stolen from my own office because apparently even disgrace has a bureaucracy, when the stones beneath me began to hum. Not the pleasant hum of working wards. The low, wet hum of something clearing its throat.

A seam opened in the flagstones like a mouth deciding to exist.

Out of it slid a scroll of grey parchment, tied with a cord of braided hair that I recognized, with a lurch, as my own. Grey hair. Mine had been going grey since I was thirty; the wards took their toll in pigment before they took anything else.

The scroll read, in script so precise it looked printed by grief:

*ODILE FERRAN, WARD-KEEPER (STRUCK FROM ROLLS), is summoned to appear before the Undercourt of Mount Vesper to answer for the condition of the border. Come down or be brought down.*

Charming. Even the paperwork down there had a sense of theater.

I sat on the edge of the cot for a long while, holding the thing, feeling the parchment's warmth against my palms. Parchment shouldn't be warm. Parchment especially shouldn't be warm like skin, like something alive and annoyed at being handled.

"Right," I told the empty room. "Right."

There was a part of me — a large part, if I'm honest, most of me — that wanted to write back *bring me down then, see if I care.* But I'd spent eleven days drinking myself stupid over one line in a registry I should never have decrypted, one burned-away name that matched a charm-seller with her mother's stubborn jaw, and guilt is a worse companion than brandy. It doesn't let you sleep. It just sits on your chest and reads over your shoulder.

Besides. If the Undercourt wanted me, it wanted me for a reason, and reasons could be bargained with. I'd spent thirty years bargaining with forces that considered human beings a rounding error. I was out of practice, but not out of nerve.

I put on my old coat. The one with the ward-signs stitched into the lining, dead now, useless now, but they were mine and I wore them the way you wear a wedding ring after the marriage ends.

Then I went down.

* * *

The drowned stair was exactly as cheerful as its name suggested.

I won't bore you with the descent. There are things in those tunnels that feed on fear, and I've found the best way to starve them is to be too tired and too bitter to be afraid. They got nothing off me but a mild headache and a memory of my mother's funeral that I could have done without revisiting anyway.

What I will tell you is this: the deeper you go beneath Vesper, the more the mountain stops being stone and starts being *document*. That's the truth nobody writes down about the Undercourt, mostly because everyone who learns it stops being in a position to write anything down. The walls became vellum. The stalactites hung like ribbons of scrollwork. Water dripped in ink-black threads, and where it struck the ground it dried into clauses.

By the time I reached the court itself, I was walking on paper. My boots made no sound at all, which unsettled me more than the dark had.

The chamber was vast and round and wrong — a courtroom built by something that had heard of courtrooms secondhand. Benches of pressed pulp rose in tiers toward a ceiling that rippled faintly, like the surface of a pond seen from underneath. And there, on a dais of layered contracts fused into something like marble, stood the magistrates.

Three of them. Woven, not born.

They were made of parchment — thousands upon thousands of sheets, folded and pleated and bound into shapes that suggested bodies the way a shadow suggests a man. The tallest had a face like an open ledger, lines running down it in columns. The second was broader, its surface dense with small print, amendments stacked on amendments until it bulged at the shoulders. The third was thin almost to transparency, and I could see older documents showing through it, faded signatures like scars.

When they moved, they rustled. When they spoke, it sounded like a page turning in an empty house.

"Odile Ferran." The tall one's voice came from everywhere, which is a trick I associate with liars and gods. "You are struck from the rolls."

"I am," I said. "You have excellent records."

"We have *all* records." A pause, the sound of many pages settling. "You decrypted the founding registry."

"I did."

"You showed it to Sable Villalobos."

Ah. So they knew about her. Of course they knew about her. She was theirs — or would be, in three nights' time, and the thought landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"I showed her what she had a right to know," I said. "Her own name."

"The name was burned," said the thin magistrate, and its voice was softer than the others, which somehow made it worse. "Burning is a form of knowing, keeper. We find it very thorough."

"Then why summon me? You've got your collection scheduled. You've got your herald doing rounds up top. What do you need a disgraced drunk for?"

The three of them rustled together, a sound like a forest deciding something, and I understood I'd asked the right question. Good. Thirty years of dealing with the Wardens' Assembly hadn't been entirely wasted. Bureaucracies are the same everywhere: they call you in when they need something they can't put in writing.

"Walk with us," said the broad one.

"We don't walk," said the tall one, correcting it. "We *convene*. Walk with us while we convene."

And the dais split, and the chamber unfolded, and I learned that the Undercourt can rearrange itself around a conversation the way a dream rearranges itself around a fear. We walked, all four of us, through corridors of shelved souls.

That's what they were. I'd heard the word before — soul-lanterns, soul-vaults, the usual slum-talk that every city invents about whatever's under its feet — but hearing and seeing are different animals. Rows upon rows of glass vessels, each one holding a light. Some burned white and steady. Some guttered. Some were nearly dark, and looking at those felt like looking at a face you almost recognize at a funeral.

"Fuel," I said, because I'm a professional, and professionals name the horror quickly so it can't sneak up on them.

"Currency," corrected the tall magistrate. "Fuel is what currency becomes when a system is hungry."

"And yours is starving."

The rustling stopped. All three of them went still in a way that confirmed everything, because guilty institutions always go still when you say the true thing out loud. I'd watched the Assembly do it for decades.

"Show her," said the thin one.

* * *

They took me to the spire.

Not up it — I suspect they don't let anyone up it, including themselves — but into its root, the vaulted hollow where the mountain's heart should have been and wasn't. Instead there was fire. A column of flame rising through a shaft so deep and straight that I couldn't see its top, burning with a light that had no heat in it at all. Cold fire. Memory-fire.

And feeding into it, on endless slow conveyances of chain and pulley, came the lanterns.

I watched them go in. White lights, steady ones, whole and bright. And I watched what came out the other side of the flame, down a second chain into the dark: ash. Fine grey ash, sifting down onto mountains of it, drifts and dunes of ash that must have taken centuries to accumulate.

Every soul-light that went in came out as nothing. That was the arithmetic of the place. That had always been the arithmetic of the place.

"The border wards draw from this," the tall magistrate said. It wasn't a question and didn't need to be; I'd helped build the system, in the way that a mason helps build a gallows. "The wards are woven from harvested memory. A city forgets a little; the city survives. This was the founding bargain. Thirteen souls to seed the flame, and thereafter a tithe of recollection, freely rendered, to keep it fed."

"Freely rendered," I repeated.

"There were forms," said the broad one, in a tone that suggested the forms had been extensive.

"And now?"

"Now," said the thin one, and here its voice dropped to something almost like embarrassment, if parchment can be embarrassed, and I believe it can, "the tithe has thinned."

I looked at the chains. Looked closer. And once I looked properly, I saw it — saw what I'd been too busy being horrified to notice. The conveyance coming in was carrying fewer lanterns than the one going out was carrying ash. The intake had slowed to a trickle. Half the lanterns arriving were already dim, half-spent memories, the dregs skimmed from dreams and old letters and the fading of the very old.

"How long?" I asked.

"Decades ago, the flame consumed a thousand kept memories a night." The tall magistrate's ledger-face rippled, columns shifting. "This season, it consumes fewer than two hundred. The wards thin accordingly. The stars—" 

"—went out," I finished. "Because the stars aren't stars. They're exhaust."

"They are the visible portion of combustion," it agreed. "Your city looked up at its own protection and called it beautiful. Humans do this. We have it in several thousand contracts. It is a consistent clause."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Eleven days of drink and guilt and terror, standing in the bowels of the machine that ate my city's past, and I laughed until my ribs hurt, and none of the magistrates objected, because I suspect they'd heard every human sound there is and filed them all.

"You're starving," I said finally. "The great Undercourt of Mount Vesper, the terror under the world, and you're *starving*. Your fuel is running dry because people don't forget the way they used to. Is that it? The city got smaller, poorer, more careful with its memories? Fifteen years of outlawed magic, fifteen years of people clinging to every scrap of joy they had because there was so little of it — and now you can't get enough of them to keep the lights on."

The thin magistrate turned toward me. All of it turned, the way a page turns, and behind its translucent surface I saw the old faded signatures shift.

"You understand our difficulty," it said.

"Oh, I understand it intimately. I've spent my life keeping systems alive past the point they deserved. It's called governance." I folded my arms. "So here's my difficulty. In three nights you come for Sable Villalobos. One soul. The last uncollected name on your founding contract. And one soul, from what I'm looking at, buys you what — a month? A season? You'll burn her and be hungry again by midwinter, and there'll be no thirteenth name left to come for. You're not collecting a debt. You're eating your own furniture."

Silence. The particular silence of a courtroom when someone has said the unsayable and everyone is checking whether the judge heard.

"You presume much, keeper."

"I presume arithmetic. It's the one presumption that never gets a man hanged." I stepped closer to the cold fire, close enough that its lightless glow washed my hands white. "So let's talk about what I came to offer, since we both know you didn't drag me down here for a tour."

"What could you offer?" the broad one rumbled. "You are struck from the rolls. You hold no office, no ward, no title."

"I hold years," I said. "Fifty-one of them, if the actuaries are honest, and they've never been honest, so call it forty. Forty years of memory, keeper-grade, and I'll tell you what that means: I remember every ward-signature ever set on the Cinderfall border. I remember the founding. I remember the thirteen names — all of them, including the one you lot burned, because I'm the one who wrote it down before it burned. My recollection isn't common stock. It's primary source."

The rustling again. Louder now. Interested.

"You propose to sell yourself."

"I propose to sell *time*. Installments. A decade now, against a delay of the collection. Another decade against another delay. You get fed; she gets time; and in the intervals, maybe — *maybe* — someone finds a better answer than feeding people to a furnace." I looked up the shaft, toward a ceiling I couldn't see, toward a sky I couldn't see, toward stars that weren't there. "Because that's the other thing I know, gentlemen, that your contracts don't. Systems that run out of fuel don't die quietly. They fail catastrophically, all at once, and take everything down with them. Your wards go, the Hollowing comes through, and there goes your city, your tithe, your entire balance sheet. You need this delay as badly as she does. More. She only loses her soul. You lose your *income*."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire, consuming, and the ash, falling.

Then the tall magistrate extended a fold of itself, and a fresh sheet of parchment drifted down between us, blank except for a single line near the bottom awaiting signature.

"One decade," it said. "Against one year's stay on the Villalobos collection. The terms are non-negotiable. The rate is fixed. The Undercourt thanks you for your custom."

One year. Ten years of my life for twelve months. It was robbery dressed as bookkeeping, and we both knew it, and I signed anyway, because a year is a year, and a year is time enough for a stubborn cartographer and a boy who remembers being dead to do something stupid and brilliant.

I pricked my thumb on a stylus the color of dried blood and pressed it to the page. The parchment drank. Somewhere inside me, I felt a door close on a decade — I could still see the room beyond it, the memories sitting there in their chairs, but the door wouldn't open again, and I knew that by tomorrow morning I wouldn't miss what I couldn't reach.

"There," I said. "Now. Since I'm a paying customer — one more item."

"The Undercourt does not—"

"Item two," I said, louder. "The spire's fuel is failing. That's not a secret anymore; I've seen the ledgers, or enough of them. Which means every soul down here knows it too, and desperate courts do desperate things. So here's my question, and think carefully before your little clerk-hearts answer it, because I *will* check the fine print later, I always do: when the tithe runs dry completely — truly dry, not thin, dry — what happens to the collected souls? The ones in the vaults. The ones already paid. Do they burn with the rest of the system? Or does the Undercourt have a contingency?"

The three magistrates rustled. And rustled. And did not answer.

Which was, of course, an answer.

I smiled at them, the smile I used to give the Assembly when I'd caught them in a lie they couldn't afford to defend. "That's what I thought. No contingency. You've been running the same engine for three hundred years and never once planned for the well going dry, because planning requires imagining an end, and bureaucracies can't imagine endings. They can only defer them."

"The audience is concluded," said the tall magistrate, in the voice of something that very much wanted this conversation to be over.

"One last thing," I said, walking backward toward the exit seam, refusing to turn my back on them, because I'm disgraced, not stupid. "The girl. Sable Villalobos. When you collect — if you collect — remember that her mother's contract had terms. Original terms. Signed terms. And original terms, gentlemen, supersede amendments. Check your own precedence. It's the one law even you can't rewrite."

The thin magistrate went very still, and behind its translucent skin, I swear I saw an old document shift — saw it move forward, toward the surface, like something drowned rising.

I climbed back up toward the dying stars with ten years lighter in my chest and a plan heavier in my hands.

Three nights. One burned name. And somewhere above me, a charm-seller who had no idea yet that the whole rotten edifice above her head was running on fumes — and that fumes, in my long experience, are exactly what clever people use to start fires.

Let them come for her. Let them bring their parchments and their precedents and their hunger.

I'd spent thirty years keeping Cinderfall's wards alive on nothing but stubbornness and spite.

I could teach the girl to do the same.
