## 12. The Spire That Burns Memory

I want it on the record, whoever ends up reading this — and someone always ends up reading things, that's how the Undercourt works, everything written down, everything filed — that I did not want to follow the dead man's drawings.

Not because they were bad drawings. They were good drawings. Uncomfortably good. Wren drew the tunnels of Mount Vesper from memory he didn't know he had, in a hand that shook like a man trying to remember a dream while drowning in it, and every sketch was right. That was the problem. You don't draw the third turning past the flooded gallery correctly unless you've walked it, and Wren hadn't walked it, not with his feet, not in this life. He'd walked it some other way, and his hands remembered what his head had been made to forget.

So when he held up a charcoal sketch of a corridor and said, "It bends left after the lanterns," and Sable said, "How do you know?" and he said, "I don't," and then we went anyway and it bent left — well. I stopped sleeping much after that.

* * *

The market was behind us by then, thank all the small mercies. I'd hated the Market of Kept Things in a way I couldn't fully explain, the way you hate a room you've been in before. Jars of years on shelves. Names sold by the syllable. A vial of somebody's childhood sitting under glass with a little paper tag, priced like pickled herring. Sable had stood in front of one particular vial for a long time — her own, though she didn't say so, and nobody had to — and her face had done something I'd never seen it do. It had gone still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"We're leaving," Odile had announced, in her ward-keeper voice, the one that expected obedience from a city that had fired her. "Before any of you buys something you can't afford."

"Everything here is something you can't afford," Wren said quietly. "That's the whole architecture of the place."

And now we were past it, deeper than the market, following sketches down corridors where the walls were warm to the touch. Not hot. Warm. Like the flank of an animal.

"I don't like this part," I said, for maybe the sixth time that day.

"You haven't liked any part," Odile said.

"That's called consistency. Ferran. It's a virtue where I come from."

"Where you come from is above a charm shop."

"Exactly. Very high standards."

Sable laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed — and I counted it as a win, because counting wins was how I kept myself together down there. That's the thing nobody tells you about being dead once: you learn exactly how thin the membrane is between having a self and not having one, and you spend the rest of your life doing little accounting exercises to prove the ledger still balances. Here is Tam. Tam likes jokes. Tam is cold all the time but pretending otherwise. Ledger balanced. Moving on.

Except down there, under the mountain, the ledger didn't balance. The wards tasted me every hundred paces or so — I could feel them, a wet gray attention sliding over my skin like a tongue checking for rot — and each time, I had to open up the death-memory and show them. *See? Been through already. Paid my toll.* And each time they let me pass, they took a little warmth with them, a little more color out of my interior weather. By the second day I was wearing every stitch I owned and still shivering, and Sable had started walking pressed against my left side without commenting on it, which was its own kind of ledger entry.

The corridor bent left after the lanterns. Of course it did.

* * *

We heard the Spire before we saw it.

That sounds dramatic, so let me be precise, because precision was Sable's religion and it was starting to infect the rest of us. We heard a sound like wind, except we were three hundred feet underground, and wind has no business existing there. It came through the rock itself, a low continuous exhale, rising and falling, and underneath it — fainter, almost subliminal — voices. Not words. Just the shape of voices. The way a house sounds when people you love are talking in another room and you can't make out what they're saying but you know, bone-deep, that it matters.

"Do you hear that?" Sable whispered.

Wren had gone white around the mouth. "I've heard it before." He said it like a confession. "In dreams. Every night since I can remember dreaming. I thought it was the sea."

"It's not the sea," Odile said grimly. She had her hand flat against the wall, reading it the way she read wardwork, and whatever she read made her jaw tighten. "Keep moving. And whatever you see when we get there — " she looked at each of us in turn, and her eyes lingered on me longest, because Odile Ferran had figured out early on that I was the one most likely to do something stupid out of empathy — "remember it's already been happening for fifteen years. Your horror doesn't help anyone. Save it. Spend it later."

"Spoken like a true bureaucrat," I said.

"Spoken like someone who signed off on this city's founding documents and has spent fifteen years drinking about it," she said. "Move."

* * *

The tunnel opened into a cavern that had no right to exist.

I've thought about how to describe the Burning Spire for a long time now, and I keep coming back to the fact that my first instinct was disappointment. That's the honest truth. When Sable said *spire*, I'd pictured something grand — black glass, green fire, the whole nightmare cathedral treatment. What we actually saw was municipal. That's the only word for it. The Spire rose out of the cavern floor like a chimney rises out of a bakery: functional, sooted, utterly without ceremony. It was maybe two hundred feet tall, built of dark stone banded with iron, and it was *breathing*. Expanding and contracting, slow as sleep. Green light pulsed in the seams between the stones, in rhythm with that exhale we'd heard through the rock.

Around its base, arranged in concentric rings like a market square, were the furnaces.

Hundreds of them. Small iron-bellied things, each one tended by a figure in gray — clerks, I realized, the same soft-footed parchment-people from the court, moving between the fires with trays. And on those trays —

"On those trays," Sable said, very quietly, "are memories."

She'd gone ahead of us, close enough to see, crouched behind a ridge of broken stone with her map-case open and her pencil moving. Sable mapped when she was frightened. She mapped when she was angry. She mapped when she didn't have any other response available, and looking at the Spire, I understood that she had no other response available. Some things are too big for anything except making them smaller on paper.

Each tray held a flame. Not fire the way we knew fire — these burned colors I don't have names for, and each one flickered in a different pattern, and if you watched long enough — I watched, because I'm an idiot, I watched — you could see shapes in them. A kitchen. A wedding. Someone's hands learning a lute. A woman laughing in a doorway with flour on her cheek. Whole lives, burning in miniature, and the clerks fed them one by one into slots at the Spire's base, and the Spire breathed them in, and the green light in its seams brightened, and somewhere far above us —

"The stars," Wren said.

He was staring up. I followed his gaze, and my stomach dropped out from under me, because high above the cavern, set into the mountain's crown like a skylight, was a lattice of iron and crystal, and through it we could see — impossibly, distantly, the way you see the bottom of a lake from a boat — the night sky. Cinderfall's sky. And threaded among the empty black where stars should have been were thin ribbons of green exhaust, rising off the Spire's crown, dispersing into the dark.

"They weren't stolen," Sable said. Her pencil had stopped moving. "They were *spent*. The stars aren't lights, they're — they're the smoke. Fifteen years ago the founders lit this thing, and it's been burning ever since, and what comes out the top looks like a sky with holes in it."

"The wards run on it," Odile said. She'd come up beside us, and her face in the green light looked ten years older than it had that morning, and that morning it had looked old enough. "The border wards. The ones that fell. I maintained them for twenty years and I never asked what fed them. There was a conduit, deep under the registry, a vein of power I was told not to touch. I told myself it was mountain-magic. Old magic. Clean." She laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "I was the ward-keeper of Cinderfall and I never once asked where the fire came from."

"You couldn't have known," Sable said.

"I could have *asked*." Odile's voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, and then reassembled itself. "There's a difference, girl. There's always a difference."

* * *

Here's what I did next, and I'm recording it because Sable says honest maps matter more than flattering ones.

I crept closer to the furnaces.

Not far. Twenty feet, maybe thirty, using the broken stone for cover, with Odile hissing my name behind me in a whisper that could have stripped paint. But there was a tray sitting unattended on a clerk's cart near the outermost ring, and on it burned a single small flame, gold-white, guttering, and I wanted — I needed — to know what a memory looked like up close. Whether it had a face. Whether it had a name.

It did.

Etched around the rim of the little iron dish, in script that moved like ants when I wasn't looking directly at it, was a line of Undercourt text. I can't read Undercourt. But I died once, and the dead apparently get a rudimentary education in the language of ledgers, because the meaning arrived in my head the way meanings arrive in dreams: complete, unbidden, terrible.

*A father teaching his daughter to whistle. Third recollection. Owner: the city of Cinderfall, in trust.*

"In trust," I said aloud, and my breath fogged even though the cavern was warm. "They put it *in trust*."

"What?" Sable was beside me — she'd followed, of course she'd followed, we were both idiots, we matched.

"This one." I pointed at the flame without touching it; touching felt like the kind of mistake you don't come back from. "It says owner: the city of Cinderfall, in trust. Sable. This isn't theft. Or — it is, but it's *legalized*, it's — " I groped for the word, and Odile supplied it from behind us, in a voice like ash:

"Municipal."

The three of us stood there, crouched in the green light of a machine that ate childhoods, and the word settled over us like snowfall.

"That's the horror of it," Sable said slowly. I watched her arrive at it in real time, watch her cartographer's mind lay the revelation out and survey it, take its measurements. "It's not a monster. Monsters you can kill. It's not even a conspiracy, really — conspiracies end, conspirators die. It's *infrastructure*. Somebody, fifteen years ago, decided the city needed wards, and the wards needed fuel, and the fuel was memories, and then they built the pipes and hired the clerks and wrote the trust documents, and then they *died*, probably, comfortably, and the thing just kept running. Nobody has to be evil. Everybody just has to keep showing up for work."

"The magistrates renew the contracts," Odile said. "Every year. I've seen the renewal seals on the registry copies — I thought they were tax stamps."

"Tax stamps." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "My whole life, the taxes I complained about — "

"Somebody's mother is in those flames, Tam." Sable's voice was gentle, but it landed like a slap, and I think she meant it to, because gentleness that lets you off the hook isn't kindness, and Sable Villalobos was many things but never unkind in that cheap way. "Maybe mine literally. Maybe yours. Everyone we've ever smiled at in the market. The baker. The woman who sells ribbon. They walk around missing pieces and call it getting old."

Wren hadn't come with us. He was still back at the ridge, and when we returned to him, he was holding his sketchbook open to a page I hadn't seen before — a drawing of the Spire, done in his shaking hand, dated in the corner. Not recently dated. Dated fifteen years ago, in a hand identical to his current one.

"I drew this the night before I forgot you," he said to Sable. No preamble. He'd clearly been assembling the sentence for a while. "I found it folded inside my old kit last year and assumed I'd copied it from somewhere. From a book. From a dream." He turned the page. Another drawing: the furnace rings, the clerk-trays, rendered in obsessive detail. And in the margin, written small, in the same hand: *Do not let them take the whistling one. Do not let them take the whistling one. Do not let them —*

"The whistling one," Sable repeated.

"There's a memory in that tray," I said. "A father teaching his daughter to whistle."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Above us, through the iron lattice, the green exhaust curled up into the starless dark, patient as smoke, and the Spire breathed in, and breathed in, and breathed in.

Then Sable closed her map-case with a snap that echoed off the cavern walls, and when she turned to look at the Spire, her face had thawed from that frozen stillness into something else entirely. Something with edges.

"Ferran," she said. "You said the magistrates renew the contracts. Renewal requires the signatory. Yes?"

Odile's eyes narrowed. "Yes. Why?"

"Because I've been thinking about this all wrong." Sable pulled her pencil from behind her ear and held it like a blade, which, for Sable, was the same as holding an actual blade. "I came down here to find a way to *pay*. To settle the debt, buy us out, escape. But you can't escape from infrastructure. You can only shut it down." She looked up at the breathing tower, at the hungry green seams, at the distant hole-punched sky. "The spire needs fuel. The court needs contracts. The contracts need a signatory who keeps agreeing. And the signatory—" she tapped her own chest, twice, "—is standing right here, and she has *not* agreed to anything in fifteen years except to stay hidden."

"Sable," Wren said carefully, "what are you going to do?"

She smiled at him, and it was the scariest smile I have ever seen on a human face, because it was warm.

"I'm going to renegotiate," she said. "But first — " she turned to Odile, and the green light caught the ink-stains on her fingers, the oath she'd written into her own skin back in Cinderfall, the covenant that bound her to this route whether it killed her or not — "first I need to see the master ledger. And Ferran? I think you're going to have to introduce me to your magistrates."

Somewhere in the furnace rings, a clerk's bell rang, soft and administrative, calling the shift to feed the fires. And high above, through the lattice, the sky went on being empty, waiting for someone to give it back its stars.
