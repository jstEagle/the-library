## 13. The Ledger Room

The door to the Ledger Room was exactly what you'd expect from a court that kept souls as loose change: enormous, black, and locked.

What you might not expect is that it was also *polite*. The lock didn't growl or hiss like the wards on the drowned stair. It simply asked, in a voice like dry paper turning over, "State your business."

"Cartography," I said.

A pause. Somewhere inside the lock, something considered this.

"I'm serious," I told it. "I'm here to make a map."

Another pause, longer. Then the lock said, "There is no map of this place."

"There wasn't," I agreed, "until about four minutes ago."

I don't know why that worked. Maybe contracts respect audacity. Maybe the Undercourt had simply never been lied to with a straight face before, and needed a moment to process the novelty. Either way, the bolt drew back with a sound like a sigh, and Tam — pressed against the wall beside me, arms crossed, radiating the particular skepticism of a boy who has already been dead once and found it overrated — muttered, "You can't just *talk* your way into places."

"I literally just did."

"That was luck."

"Luck is just cartography you haven't charted yet," I said, which sounded wise and meant nothing, which is the definition of wisdom in my experience.

Wren said nothing at all. He'd said very little since the market, since he'd shown us the sketchbook full of my face — my face, drawn two hundred times by hands that shouldn't have known it. He walked half a step behind me now, close enough that I could hear him breathing, far enough that neither of us had to acknowledge what we were to each other, or weren't, or were in some form the Undercourt had filed under *paid in full*.

The door opened onto darkness that smelled of ink and dust and something older underneath, like the smell of a book so old the words have started dreaming.

* * *

Tam went first, because Tam always went first when there was a chance of dying again — I think part of him wanted to check whether it still worked the same way. He carried one of the green soul-lanterns we'd taken from the market stalls, and its light did strange things in that room. It didn't cast shadows so much as *suggestions* of shadows, shapes that moved a half-second after their owners did.

The lantern light found shelves.

Not shelves of books. Shelves of ledgers — thousands of them, tens of thousands, rising into a dark the lantern couldn't reach, each spine labeled in script that hurt slightly to read, the way bright snow hurts. Names. Dates. Terms. Every bargain the Undercourt had ever struck, every soul sold, every memory harvested, every year traded for a favor, filed and cross-referenced and *kept*.

"It's all here," Tam whispered. His breath fogged, though the air was warm. "Everything they ever bought."

"Then somewhere in here," I said, "is me."

We split up, because the room demanded it — the aisles arranged themselves around us as we moved, I swear it, shelving shifting like water deciding where a stone should sit. Tam took the low rows, the recent acquisitions. Wren drifted toward the middle, running his fingers along spines without reading them, the way he must have done in whatever life he'd lived before he sold the best parts of it.

And I followed the oldest aisle, because I've spent fifteen years learning to read cities, and cities and ledgers are the same animal: both keep their history in their foundations, and both will show you exactly what they want you to see unless you go looking for the load-bearing walls.

The oldest ledgers were bound in something that wasn't leather. The first one I touched was warm.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about looking for your own name in an archive of the damned: you spend the whole search hoping not to find it, and hating yourself for hoping, because if it isn't there, then everything — the herald, the riddle, the three nights ticking down like water through a cracked hourglass — means nothing, and the dread was for nothing, and worse, the *hope* was for nothing too.

I found the founding contract on the thirteenth shelf. Of course it was the thirteenth shelf. This place had a sense of humor the way a landslide has a sense of humor.

It was larger than the others, bound in pale hide that I chose not to speculate about, and its clasp bore twelve seals — one for each founder whose name Odile had decrypted in her registry, back in the world above, back when the stars still existed. Twelve names I now knew: the merchants and ward-keepers and one reluctant priestess who had built Cinderfall's safety on a foundation of purchased souls.

Thirteen signatures, though. That was the thing Odile had found. Thirteen lines, twelve recorded, one burned away — scorched so thoroughly that even the parchment remembered being fire.

I opened the cover.

The ledger didn't resist me. That was the worst part. It opened like it had been waiting, pages falling open to the final signature as though the book itself had been holding a bookmark there for fifteen years.

Twelve terms, in twelve hands. I skimmed them — safety for the city, borders warded, the Hollowing held back beyond the mountains, in exchange for thirteen souls payable to the Undercourt beneath Mount Vesper, in perpetuity, renewable upon consent of the signatories. Standard apocalyptic fine print.

Then the thirteenth line.

It wasn't burned anymore. Or rather — the burn was still there, a scorch mark across the parchment like a wound — but beneath it, in handwriting I did not know and knew instantly, someone had written a new term. Small letters. Careful ones. The handwriting of a woman who had learned to write small so her words would take up less of the world.

*Let my daughter forget me, so she may choose freely.*

I sat down on the floor of the Ledger Room, because my legs had resigned without notice, and I read it again. And again. The way you press a bruise to confirm it's real.

My mother's handwriting. My mother's *voice*, in ink, in a room beneath a mountain, waiting for me the way she must have waited for fifteen years — no. Not waiting. She was dead; her soul was currency in a vault somewhere in this labyrinth. But the words had waited. Words are patient that way. They outlast the mouth that spoke them.

*Let my daughter forget me.*

So that was the shape of it. All those years I'd thought I was running — from the outlawing of magic, from the heralds, from a name I couldn't remember why I'd buried. Fifteen years of believing myself a fugitive, a survivor, a woman who'd gotten lucky in a city that ate the unlucky. And the truth was that my exile had been *purchased*. Gift-wrapped. My mother had stood in front of whatever passed for magistrates down here and negotiated not for her own soul's comfort but for my ignorance — because a Sable who didn't know she was owed could live free, could sell belief-charms in the slums, could build a ragged little family out of an orphan boy and a disgraced drunk and a man who didn't know her face.

A Sable who *knew* would have come down here years ago. Would have tried to buy herself back and failed, because you can't outbid a court with charm-shop coin. Would have died owing.

So she'd made me forget, so that I could *choose*. Not freedom — she couldn't afford freedom, freedom was priced beyond thirteen souls. She bought me the next best thing: a clean slate and an unclouded mind, so that when the bill finally came due, I'd decide what to do about it with my own eyes open.

The choice was always mine. That was the inheritance. Not the debt — the debt was never mine, it was the founders', twelve cowards and one brave woman — but the *choosing*. She'd paid everything she had, including my memory of her, to make sure that when I stood in this room, I'd be standing here as myself. Uncoerced. Unbought.

Free to say yes. Free to say no. Free, gods help me, to do something stupid and magnificent that she never could have predicted.

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet and cracked, the laugh of a woman sitting on a cold floor reading her dead mother's last sentence by the light of a stolen soul. Tam appeared at the end of the aisle, lantern raised, and I watched him decide not to ask. He sat down next to me instead, shoulder against mine, warm the way he was still warm — less warm than last week, I noticed, and hated the noticing. The wards down here took their toll in temperature, and Tam kept paying it.

"Well?" he said.

"She gave me a choice," I said. "Fifteen years ago. Before I could walk. She gave me a choice I haven't made yet."

"That's very her, I bet," Tam said. "Even though you don't remember her." He said it gently, the way you hand someone something breakable. "You've got her handwriting, probably. People do."

I looked down at my ink-stained fingers, at the oath I'd written across my own forearm two nights ago — *I, Sable Villalobos, cartographer, bind myself to the route* — and realized my hand was shaking too hard to tell.

* * *

Wren found us there. He didn't sit, but he crouched, which for Wren was practically a declaration of emotional availability. In the lantern light I could see the sketchbook tucked under his arm, and I could see — because I was starting to learn how to see him, memory or no memory — that he'd already looked at the ledger page. Already read it. Already understood what it meant, faster than I had, the way he seemed to understand everything about me except why.

"The renewal clause," he said quietly. He turned the ledger toward me and put his finger under a line near the top, in the dense legal script that governed everything down here. "*Renewable upon consent of the living signatory.*"

"So?"

"So there are twelve dead signatories and one living one." His eyes came up to mine. Dark eyes. Eyes that had drawn my face two hundred times without knowing why. "Sable. You're not just named in this contract. As far as the law of this place is concerned, you're the only person alive who can *renew* it. Which means you're the only person alive who can refuse to."

The words went through me like cold water through a cracked wall.

Refuse. Default. I didn't know yet what default meant — whether the wards would fail, whether Cinderfall would stand naked against whatever the borders were built to hold back, whether refusing to pay would save me and damn everyone I loved. That answer was waiting somewhere ahead of me, in a captured clerk's mouth, in Odile's interrogation, in the terrible arithmetic of the next few hours.

But standing there, on the floor of the Ledger Room, with my mother's last words open in my lap and a boy who remembered being dead warm against my side and a man who'd paid everything to forget me crouched in front of me like a question —

— I understood, for the first time, that the magistrates hadn't summoned me to collect.

They'd summoned me to *ask*.

And somewhere deep in the mountain, something heard me understand it. The lantern flames guttered green. Every ledger on every shelf rustled at once, ten thousand books turning their pages like a crowd drawing breath, and from the dark at the end of the oldest aisle came the dry-paper voice of the lock, or something older than the lock:

"*The signatory is present. The Court will hear her.*"

Footsteps followed. Not the herald's — smaller, quicker, the steps of someone who scurried for a living. A clerk, robed in contract-parchment, clutching a satchel of fresh ink, bowing so low his spine crackled.

"If it please the signatory," he said, in a voice like a receipt, "the magistrates request the honor of your refusal — or your consent. Whichever you prefer. We're flexible. Within the terms."

Tam stood up. Wren stood up. I stayed on the floor one more second, long enough to press my thumb against my mother's handwriting, the way you touch a grave.

Then I rose, closed the ledger, and tucked it under my arm like the most dangerous book in the world — which, as of that moment, it was.

"Take me to them," I said. "I've read the terms. Now let's talk about rewriting them."
