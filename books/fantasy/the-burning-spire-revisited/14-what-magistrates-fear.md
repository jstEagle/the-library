## 14. What Magistrates Fear

The clerk weighed less than a coat.

That was my first observation, and I want it on the record that I made it before the fear set in. Odile Ferran, disgraced ward-keeper of Cinderfall, drunk for most of a decade and sober now only because sobriety had been forced upon her by circumstance — namely, that there was nothing left in this mountain worth drinking and everything left worth staying sharp for — noticed that the creature we'd pulled from the archive galleries was roughly the mass of a winter overcoat with buttons, and thought: *well, at least the interrogation won't take long.*

I was wrong about that, too. I'd been wrong about a great many things for fifteen years; one more hardly registered.

We had him trussed in the antechamber off the Ledger Room — Tam's word for it, though "room" was generous. It was more a throat of stone, ribbed with shelves that grew out of the rock like teeth, every shelf crowded with ledgers whose spines whispered when you looked away from them. The clerk sat slumped against a pillar of contract-parchment, his wrists bound with ward-cord I'd braided myself from memory, back when memory was something I trusted. He was grey as old vellum, thin as a folded page, and he had the particular stillness of a thing that has been filed so long it has forgotten how to be alive.

Sable stood across from me with her arms crossed, ink-stained fingers drumming her elbow. The cartographer's covenant crawled up her forearm where she'd inked it two nights ago — I could see it moving faintly under the skin, the way a river moves under ice. She hadn't slept. Neither had I. Sleep, down here, felt like an invitation.

Tam crouched near the door, keeping watch on the corridor, which was pointless because the corridors moved, but you let the boy have his dignity.

"All right," Sable said. "Ask him."

"I was going to ask him," I said. "You don't have to supervise my interrogations."

"You've been talking to yourself for three days, Ferran. Someone has to supervise."

She had me there. I turned to the clerk, cleared my throat, and deployed the opening move of every interrogation I'd ever run in twenty years of ward-keeping, which is to say: I told the truth, because lies slide off creatures like this like grease off slate.

"Here's where we stand," I said. "Your court has a claim on her." I jerked a thumb at Sable. "Thirteenth name on the founding contract. Collection night is tomorrow. She intends to contest it. You intend to stop her. One of us is going to be disappointed, and I've had a very bad fortnight, so I'm hoping it's you."

The clerk's eyes opened. They were the color of unmarked paper.

"Contest," he said. The word came out dry, like a stamp coming down. "There is no provision for contest. There is collection, and there is default."

"Default," Sable said sharply. "You mentioned default before. What happens if she defaults?"

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Then — and this is the part I'll be turning over in my head until I die — he *smiled*. It was a small smile, a filing-clerk's smile, the kind of smile you give someone who has asked a question so naive it circles all the way around to profound.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing happens. That is what default *is*."

* * *

Let me back up, because I'm getting ahead of myself, and a good mapmaker — or a good interrogator, which is the same trade with worse handwriting — knows you start with the terrain.

We'd taken the clerk six hours earlier. He'd been re-shelving contracts in the western galleries, humming — actually humming, a little dry rustle of a tune — when Tam spotted him through a wall. The boy sees things no one else can see; he died once as an infant and came back with the Undercourt's workings printed on the inside of his eyelids, and I have stopped trying to decide whether that's a gift or a scar. Probably both. Most true things are both.

Anyway. Tam pointed, Wren — who had joined us by then, sketchbook under his arm, still not knowing why he couldn't stop drawing Sable's face — created a distraction by dropping an armful of soul-lanterns, and I put the clerk in a headlock that would have been embarrassing if anyone from my old garrison had seen it. He didn't struggle. That should have been our first warning. Nothing down here struggles, because nothing down here believes anything can change. Why fight the shelving?

Now he sat among us, patient as a receipt, and explained default to us the way you'd explain rain to a child.

"The contract requires renewal," he said. "All contracts do. A claim on a soul is not a stone, keeper. It is a fire. It must be fed, or it goes out. Every fifty years, the signatory — the living signatory, the one whose name burns in the ledger — must come before the magistrates and reaffirm. Consent, freely given, witnessed and sealed. That is the law beneath the law."

"Sable never signed anything," I said.

"She signed fifteen years ago." His paper eyes slid to her. "Through her mother's hand. A substitution clause. The mother gave her own soul and purchased, with it, the daughter's freedom — but freedom is not exemption. Freedom is deferral. The debt sleeps until the signatory comes of age, and then it wakes, and asks its question."

"And if the answer is no?" Sable asked. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles weren't.

"If the answer is no," the clerk said, "then the contract defaults. And default means—" he paused, and I realized with a lurch that he was choosing his words carefully, which meant the truth was dangerous even to *him*, "—that the Undercourt's claim extinguishes itself. All thirteen names release. Every kept soul returns. Every held memory flies home. The ledgers empty. The market closes. The magistrates unravel into blank parchment, because parchment is all they ever were, and the mountain—" he spread his thin grey hands, "—the mountain forgets us."

Silence. Somewhere above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the Burning Spire roared softly to itself, burning other people's yesterdays to keep the border wards alive.

"That sounds wonderful," Tam said from the doorway. "That sounds like the best news I've heard since I died."

"It is not news," the clerk said. "It is the trap."

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, and I did it standing up because sitting down felt too much like surrender.

If Sable refuses to reaffirm, the contract defaults. Default kills the Undercourt. Wonderful. Magnificent. Except —

Except the wards are fed by the spire, and the spire is fed by harvested memory, and the harvested memory is kept, sorted, and burned by the Undercourt. Kill the court and you kill the machinery. Kill the machinery and the flames go out within days — the clerk gave us the figure without being asked, because clerks love figures: *nine days, perhaps eleven, depending on how much fuel remains in the reserve vaults.* And when the flames go out, the border wards die. And when the border wards die, Cinderfall stands open to the Hollowing, the slow grey tide of unbeing that waits beyond the borders like water behind a cracked dam.

Save everyone by destroying the prison, and the prison's walls turn out to be holding back the flood.

"They built it that way on purpose," I said. I was pacing, which Sable says I do when I'm thinking and which I say I do when I'm annoyed. "The founders. Your magistrates. They wired the whole city to their own heart so nobody could ever cut it out. Fifteen years ago they took thirteen souls and told themselves it was mercy — safety for Cinderfall, bought at a price someone else paid — and then they built the price into the architecture so it could never be refused again."

"Yes," the clerk said, simply.

"Don't agree with me," I snapped. "It makes me feel insane."

"I am agreeing because you are correct," he said. "And because you are the first people in four hundred years to ask the correct question."

That stopped me mid-pace. Sable had gone very still. Even Tam lifted his head from the doorway.

"And what question is that?" Sable asked quietly.

The clerk leaned forward as far as his bonds allowed. Up close, I could see that his skin wasn't skin at all but layers of fine script, written and overwritten until the original text was illegible — a creature composed entirely of amendments.

"Not *how do we escape the contract*," he said. "Everyone asks that. Escapees are recaptured; escapers are a rounding error. Not *how do we pay the debt*; payment is the system working as intended. The correct question is: *what does the contract require that the magistrates cannot provide?*"

"And what's that?" Tam asked.

"Consent," the clerk said. "Living, willing, freely-given consent. It is the one ingredient the court cannot manufacture. We can hold souls. We can hold memories, names, years, first snows and wedding dances. We can burn ten thousand yesterdays to keep nine stars lit. But we cannot *want* on anyone's behalf. Every renewal for four centuries has been coerced, tricked, or inherited — a signature extracted, never offered. Do you understand what that means?"

"No," I said honestly. "Explain it slowly. I'm old and I drink."

"It means the edifice is starving," the clerk said, "and it has been starving since the day it was built. The spire runs low — your ward-keeper has seen the reserves, the girl has counted the lanterns. The magistrates grow desperate because desperation is the honest state of any machine that runs on stolen willingness. And it means—" his voice dropped, and the whispering ledgers around us seemed to lean in to listen, "—that there is exactly one person in all the world whose refusal can bring the whole court down. And exactly one act whose *giving* can make the whole court unnecessary. And they are the same act, performed by the same person, on the same night. Tomorrow night."

He looked at Sable.

"The signatory," he said, "holds the pen."

* * *

After that, the clerk went quiet. Not refusing to talk — clerks don't refuse, refusal implies preference — but emptied, like a drawer after inventory. Tam sat beside him and, in that unnerving way of his, patted the creature's papery knee and said, "It's all right. I remember what it's like to be kept," and the clerk's shoulders did something that might have been shivering.

Which left Sable and me alone at the edge of the Ledger Room, looking at the master ledger itself, chained to its lectern of black stone, thirteen contracts glowing faintly through the vellum like veins in a leaf.

"So," I said. "Refuse, and the court dies, and the wards die with it, and the Hollowing walks into Cinderfall and unmakes everything you love. Reaffirm, and you're theirs, body and soul, forever, and the city gets another fifty years on the same rotten terms." I rubbed my face. My hands smelled of ink and ward-cord and, faintly, of the taverns I'd sworn off. "Some choice. They always give you some choice. That's how you know it was designed by committee."

"Ferran." Sable was looking at the ledger, not at me. The covenant on her arm had gone bright, the ink-lines moving faster now, as if her skin knew something her mouth hadn't said yet. "He said the signatory holds the pen."

"I heard."

"He said consent freely given is the one thing they can't manufacture." She turned, and there was something in her face I recognized from the old registry, from the founding documents, from the moment in chapter whatever-it-was when a woman named Villalobos signed away herself for her daughter — the look of someone reading the last line of a contract and understanding it for the first time. "What if the answer isn't yes and isn't no? What if the answer is *new terms*?"

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out rusty and half-hysterical. "Kid, you're proposing to renegotiate with three magistrates woven out of contract-parchment, on their own ground, the night before collection, while the wards fail overhead and the Hollowing presses the borders. That's not audacity. That's cartography with a death wish."

"You just described my entire career," Sable said.

And that's the trouble with found family, if you're wondering. You spend fifteen years telling yourself you're alone so it won't hurt when it ends, and then some ink-stained cartographer looks at you with her mother's stubbornness and her own impossible courage, and you realize you'd follow her into the dark whether she asked or not.

"One problem," I said, holding up a finger, because if I didn't structure my objections I'd just start shouting. "Even if you could rewrite it — rewrite it into *what*? The spire needs fuel. Memory, willingly given. Where does a city get that much willing memory by tomorrow night?"

Sable smiled. It was not entirely a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who has already drawn the map and is merely waiting for the rest of us to catch up to where she's standing.

"That," she said, "is the part I need to show Wren."

Behind us, in the antechamber, the captured clerk began — very softly, very dryly — to hum again. And this time, I swear on what's left of my reputation, the sound was almost hopeful.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I sat with the clerk and asked him the questions I hadn't wanted to ask in front of Sable, because some truths are load-bearing and you check them privately before you let anyone walk on them.

"If she fails," I said, "if the magistrates reject her terms — what happens to her?"

"Collection," the clerk said.

"And if she succeeds?"

The paper eyes regarded me for a long time. Somewhere far above, a star that wasn't there flickered anyway, out of habit.

"Then the price will be exact," he said. "It is always exact. The court cannot lie about prices, keeper. It can only hope you don't read them."

I thanked him, which is a strange thing to say to a bundle of amendments wearing a man's shape, and went to find Sable.

Because I had read them. Four hundred years of renewals, coerced and inherited and tricked — and every single renewal clause carried the same final line, the one the founders wrote in and never once believed anyone would invoke:

*Terms may be rewritten by the signatory alone.*

Alone. The word had been sitting in the ledger the whole time, patient as a trap, waiting for a girl who spent her life mapping places that didn't want to be mapped.

Tomorrow night, Sable Villalobos would stand before the magistrates and pick up the pen.

God help us all — and I mean that literally, because as of tonight, I was fairly sure nothing else would.
