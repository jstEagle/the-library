## 15. The Things Beyond the Border

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head because paper burns and memories are apparently currency down here — that I did not want to go toward the fissure.

Nobody wanted to go toward the fissure. That's the thing about fissures. They don't invite you. They just sit there in the dark being a hole in reality, radiating a kind of wrongness that makes your back teeth hum, and eventually some fool says *we should look*, and some other fool says *yes, we should*, and then you're all walking toward it like moths with a death wish.

The fools in question were Sable Villalobos, Odile Ferran, and me. Wren had stayed behind at the market of kept things, sketching, which is what Wren does instead of sleeping or eating or acknowledging that anything unusual is happening. He'd pressed one of his sketches into Sable's hand before we left — her own face, drawn forty times from forty angles, none of them quite right, all of them unmistakably her. She'd looked at it for a long moment with an expression I couldn't map, and then folded it into her coat like it was made of glass.

"Stay close," she told me now, for the fourth time since we'd left the market stalls behind.

"I'm not the one who wanders," I said. "You're the one who wanders. You have a whole profession built on wandering."

"Cartography is *systematic* wandering."

"It's wandering with homework."

Odile snorted from the rear, where she insisted on walking because, she said, she trusted nothing behind her, including herself. The disgraced ward-keeper carried a lantern that burned with a flame the color of old bruises, and every few steps she'd mutter something under her breath — ward-cant, mostly dead language, mostly curses. She'd been doing it since we passed the last of the soul-lanterns, the green ones, the ones that used to be people before they were fuel. Her muttering had gotten worse the deeper we went, and by now it had the rhythm of a prayer that had given up on being answered.

The tunnel we followed was not on any map, including Sable's, which bothered her more than the tunnel itself did. It was smooth in a way stone shouldn't be, worn not by water but by *absence*, as if something had eaten the roughness off it over centuries. My bare feet knew the way even when my eyes didn't. That's the part of being dead-once that nobody warns you about: your body remembers things your mind was never invited to. The soles of my feet had opinions. Right now they said: *left at the next fork, and also, run.*

We didn't run. We went left at the fork.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about me, if you're new to this story, and everyone is new to this story because I've never told it straight through before: I died when I was eleven days old.

Fever. The kind that takes infants in Cinderfall's low quarters the way tide takes sandcastles. My mother — whoever she was; I was found, not born into any family I can name — had already gone, and the woman who found me, Maren of the charm-row, sat up three nights with me getting colder and colder in her arms, until on the third night I stopped breathing altogether.

And then, by Maren's account, I started again.

She never talked about it much. When she drank, which was often, she'd say things like *you came back wrong, boy, but wrong in useful ways,* and ruffle my hair with a hand that shook. Useful ways. I see the Undercourt's workings through walls. I can taste wards on the air like weather coming. And when the green lanterns flicker, I feel them flicker in my chest, like distant heartbeats that aren't mine.

Down here, in the deep places where the court keeps its ledger of souls, those useful ways have been working overtime. Every ward we've crossed has tasted me before letting me pass — fear, mostly, they taste fear — and each time I've had to open myself up and show them mine, calm them with the memory of dying, which is the one thing I own outright. It works. But it costs. Each time, I come away a little colder. A little less warm. Maren would say I'm spending myself. I've started keeping count.

Eleven crossings. Eleven little withdrawals from an account I can't refill.

Sable knows. She watches me rub feeling back into my fingers and doesn't say anything, which is its own kind of kindness. Odile suspects. Odile suspects everything; it's why she's disgraced and not dead.

But neither of them knows what I know now, standing at the mouth of the fissure, looking out at the thing the wards exist to stop.

Neither of them knows, because I'm the only one who can look at it directly without his mind sliding off.

* * *

The fissure opened in the tunnel wall like a wound that had been there longer than the wall. Not wide — maybe two armspans across, ragged at the edges, and beyond it, no more tunnel. No more mountain. Just a vast gray nothing that wasn't darkness, because darkness is something, darkness is the absence of light and you can name it and stand in it. This was the absence of *absence*. The absence of naming.

"The Hollowing," Odile said, and her voice came out flat, stripped of all its usual vinegar. "Saints preserve us. It's real. I thought — thirty years I told myself the old keepers invented it to justify their budgets."

"What is it?" Sable asked. She'd moved to the fissure's edge and was doing the thing she does — the thing that makes her insufferable and magnificent in equal measure — which is looking at the unmappable and reaching for her ink.

"It's what's past the border," Odile said. "Past all borders. It's the reason Cinderfall pays thirteen souls a generation to keep its wards lit. It comes slow. That's the horror of it. It doesn't rush. It erodes. Whatever it touches stops having ever been."

"That's not a sentence that means anything."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. Because meaning is one of the first things it takes."

I could hear them talking the way you hear rain on a roof when you're underwater. Because I was already looking. Already *seeing*.

To me — and this is the useful-wrong part, the part of me that died and got handed back with the veil left off — the Hollowing wasn't gray nothing. It was a tide. A slow, patient tide of unbeing, rolling against the far side of the world's skin, pressing at the wards the way water presses at a dam. And here's the thing that made my stomach drop through the floor of the world: the wards were thin here. Paper-thin. I could see the light of the Burning Spire far above and behind us, feeding them, and I could see how little was coming down the long channels of fire to this far edge. The spire was starving. Odile had learned that in the court's audience chamber, and I'd felt it in my teeth ever since.

The tide didn't hate us. That's what I needed them to understand and couldn't find words for. Fire hates. Floods rage. The Hollowing didn't do any of that. It simply continued, the way a debt continues, the way forgetting continues once it starts. Somewhere out in that gray, there had been other cities. Other mountains. Whole nations of people who had names and songs and arguments about whose turn it was to cook. The Hollowing hadn't destroyed them. It had *unwritten* them, gently, completely, until even the idea of them was gone, and the scariest thing my dead-boy mind could tell me was that I couldn't grieve them properly, because grief needs a shape and they had none.

"Tam." Sable's hand on my shoulder. "You've gone white. Whiter. You're normally a sort of cheerful candle-wax color and right now you're fresh paper."

"There were cities out there," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, like someone else borrowing it. "Plural. There were *lots* of cities out there."

Silence. Then Odile, very quietly: "How do you know?"

"Because I can see where they aren't." I pointed, and my finger shook, and I let it. "There. And there. The Hollowing goes around things sometimes — around bedrock, around old deep magic — and it leaves holes in itself, gaps shaped like whatever it finished eating. Look. That gap's got streets in it. That one's got a harbor."

Sable looked. Of course she looked. She's Sable. And I watched her try to see it, watched her cartographer's mind strain against the gray, and I watched her fail, and something in her face cracked a little — the face of a woman who has spent fifteen years believing that everything can be mapped if you're stubborn enough.

"I can't see it," she whispered.

"No. Only me. Being dead has perks."

"Don't joke."

"Joking is load-bearing, Sable. Take out my jokes and the whole structure collapses."

But she wasn't listening anymore. She'd turned back to the fissure, and she'd pulled out her journal, and she was staring at the blank page the way you stare at a locked door.

* * *

I should explain what happened next, and I want to be honest about it, because I've noticed that down here honesty is the only coin that doesn't devalue.

I touched it on purpose.

Not bravado. Not heroics — I'm eleven years dead and twelve years alive and I've never once managed heroics, I trip over furniture. It was worse than either. It was *professional curiosity*, which is the deadliest sin available to people like us, and I caught it from Sable like you catch a cold.

Because here's what I'd realized, standing at the fissure while the others argued in whispers: the Hollowing and the Undercourt are the same animal. Oh, not literally. But the magistrates keep souls as currency, hoard memory, erase names — and the Hollowing does all of that too, just without the paperwork. The court is the Hollowing wearing a suit and charging rent. And if that's true, then somewhere in the difference between them — in the gap between a magistrate's ledger and the raw gray tide — there might be a seam. A weakness. Something Sable could use when collection night came and she stood in front of three figures woven from contract-parchment and tried to rewrite the deal.

I wanted to bring her back intelligence. That's what I told myself. I wanted to be useful, because useful is the only way I know how to be loved, and Maren taught me that and never meant to.

So while Sable bent over her journal and Odile muttered her dead-language curses, I stepped closer to the fissure. Close enough that the gray light — unlight, nonlight, whatever you call a color that refuses to commit — fell across my hands. And I reached out, and I put two fingers into the Hollowing.

For half a heartbeat, nothing. That was the trap. It feels like nothing because it *is* nothing, and your senses report nothing honestly, and then it starts reporting on *you*.

Cold. Not cold like winter — cold like subtraction. My fingertips stopped existing, then my knuckles, and the terrible part wasn't pain, because there wasn't any. The terrible part was that I couldn't remember what my fingertips had felt like. The information was just — gone. Lifted. Like a page torn cleanly out.

I yanked my arm back and fell on my backside, and both of them were shouting, and I was shouting too, though I don't remember deciding to. We sat there in the tunnel breathing hard, the three of us, while I held my hand against my chest like a wounded bird.

My fingers were fine. Whole. Warm, even, warmer than the rest of me, which I understood dimly was the Hollowing's version of a receipt: *we took our payment elsewhere.*

"TAM." Sable grabbed my face in both hands. "What did you lose? What did it take? Tell me."

And I ran through myself the way you check pockets after a pickpocket, and everything seemed to be there — my name, Maren, the fever, the dying, the coming back, the drowned stair, the lanterns, the eleven cold withdrawals — and I almost said *nothing, it got nothing, I won* —

Except.

"Sable," I said. "What color is the sky?"

She blinked. "What?"

"When it's clear. Midday. What color is it?"

"Blue," she said slowly. "Tam, it's blue, why—"

Blue.

The word arrived in my head like a coin from a foreign country. I knew it was a word. I knew other people used it. I could see Sable's eyes, and some ancient, exhausted part of me reported that they were the same color as the sky at midday, and the knowledge landed in me with all the warmth of a ledger entry. *Eyes: category four. Sky: category four.* I knew blue the way you know a city you've only read about.

I couldn't *see* it anymore. Not the color — my eyes worked fine, the light worked fine, the world still arrived in full and various shades. But the *blueness* of it, the thing that made blue blue, the cold-bright singing of it — that had been torn out, cleanly, the way my fingertips' feeling had been torn out. Somewhere in the gray tide outside the fissure, a piece of me was dissolving into the general unbeing, and it was small, it was one color out of thousands, and it was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to me, because now I knew exactly how it worked.

It doesn't take everything at once. It takes one thing. And then it waits to see if you'll come back for more.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small. "Oh, that's what it's like."

"What's what like?" Odile demanded. "Boy, talk."

"It took blue." I laughed, because joking is load-bearing and the structure was swaying badly. "It took — I can't — there's a hole where blue used to be and the hole doesn't feel like a hole, that's the trick of it, it feels like blue was never a thing, like everyone who talks about blue is telling a pretty lie. I'll spend the rest of my life knowing the sky is blue and never once knowing what that means."

Nobody spoke. Down the tunnel, faint and green, a soul-lantern guttered.

Then Sable did the Sable thing. She sat down next to me in the filth of the unmapped tunnel, took my robbed hand in hers, and said: "Describe it anyway. While you still remember describing it. Everything. Every word you've got. I'm writing it down."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, and her jaw did the thing it does when she's decided something irreversible, "if the Undercourt falls tomorrow and the wards die with it, and that tide comes for Cinderfall — I want to know what we're fighting. I want it in ink. And because someday, somehow, I'm going to find a way to give it back to you. I keep accounts too, Tam. That's what ledgers are *for*."

So I described blue to her, word after word after word, watching her write them down, hoarding my own description against the day the words went hollow too. And Odile stood watch at the fissure with her bruise-colored lantern, and I saw her look out at the gray tide for a long, long time, and I saw her reach the same conclusion I had, the one that curdles everything:

The Undercourt is monstrous. The magistrates are thieves, the market is obscene, the spire burns the dead's memories for fuel. All true. All unforgivable.

But the alternative to the court is *that*. And that doesn't bargain. That doesn't take payment. That doesn't leave receipts you can argue with later.

We hadn't come down here to save the Undercourt. But somewhere between the fissure and the loss of one small color, I understood that we couldn't destroy it either — not wholly, not carelessly. Whatever Sable did on collection night, she'd have to do it like a surgeon, not an arsonist. One wrong cut and the whole border comes down, and the tide comes in, and the last thing any of us lose will be the words to describe what we lost.

We turned back toward the market, toward Wren and his impossible sketches, toward the spire and the ledger room and the three nights that were bleeding away faster than any of us wanted to count.

I was halfway back when I stopped, because a thought had finally surfaced through the cold.

"Sable," I said. "The contract. Your mother's contract. It's paid in *memory*, right? Kept memories, harvested ones — that's the spire's fuel?"

"Yes..."

"And the Hollowing eats memory too. Eats *everything*, but memory first — I could feel it going through me looking for the good stuff." I swallowed. "Sable, what happens to a starving court when the thing it's made of walks up to its door?"

She stared at me. Behind her, Odile had gone very still, in the particular way of a woman hearing a plan form that she's going to have to help carry out and probably die for.

And from somewhere deeper in the mountain — from the direction of the market, of the spire, of the burning heart of the Undercourt — came a sound none of us had heard yet in all our days below: the magistrates' parchment voices, raised in something that was almost, almost fear.

They'd felt the tide press the wards too.

And they knew their time was running out faster than ours.
