## 16. Wren's Choice

I have drawn her face four hundred and eleven times.

I know the number because I count them every morning, the way other men count coins or sins. They are pinned to the walls of my room above the dye-works, stacked in drawers, tucked into the linings of coats I no longer wear. Four hundred and eleven sketches of a woman whose name I do not know, rendered in charcoal, in ink, in berry-stain and candle soot. Some are careful portraits. Some are nothing but an ear, or the particular way her hair curls where it meets her neck, or the exact angle at which she squints when she doesn't believe something — which, judging by how often I've drawn that expression, was often.

The dyers downstairs think I'm haunted. They're not wrong.

Here is what I know about myself: I woke up eleven years ago in a rented room with no memory before a certain Tuesday, a pouch of coins on the table, and a note in my own handwriting that said *You agreed to this. Don't go looking.* The handwriting was mine. The instruction was mine. And like any man given a sensible instruction by his past self, I ignored it within the week.

What I found instead of answers was drawing. Or rather, drawing found me. My hand knew things my head didn't. It knew the curl of hair at the nape of a neck. It knew a laugh I had never heard — I drew open mouths with laughter in them for two years before I understood that's what they were. My hands remembered a woman my mind had been emptied of, and every sketch was a letter my fingers kept writing to someone who never received it.

So when the stars went out and the city lost its mind, and a cartographer named Sable Villalobos walked into the reservoir tunnels with a ward-keeper and a dead boy, I did not recognize her.

But my hands did.

* * *

We were three days into the descent when I caught myself doing it again. We'd stopped to rest in a side chamber off the drowned stair, one of those pockets of dry stone where the Undercourt's architecture couldn't quite decide what shape to be. Tam was asleep, curled against Odile like a cat that had decided, provisionally, not to hate her. Sable sat across from me, mapping by lantern-light, her tongue caught between her teeth in concentration.

And I was drawing her.

Not from life — I'd stopped looking at her directly days ago; it made my chest hurt in a way I had no vocabulary for. I was drawing her from memory. From the memory I didn't have. My pencil moved and produced the squint, the skeptical mouth, the small scar above her left eyebrow that I had somehow known about since before I ever met her.

"Sable," I said, because saying her name still felt like handling something hot. "When did you get the scar?"

She didn't look up. "Fell out of a pear tree when I was nine. Why?"

"Show me."

She showed me. Left eyebrow, thin white line, exactly where my pencil had already put it. She looked at the sketch, then at me, and I watched her arrive at the same wrong conclusion everyone arrives at: that I was mocking her, or studying her, or that cartographers simply collected faces the way some men collect stamps.

"That's unsettling," she said mildly, and went back to her map.

That night I laid out all the sketches I'd brought — I travel with a leather folio of the best ones now, forty or fifty faces, the same face — and I spread them on the stone floor and looked at them properly for the first time in eleven years. Not as a compulsion. As evidence.

Because here is the thing about evidence: it accumulates. And here is the thing about the Undercourt: everything there is a transaction, and transactions leave receipts.

The coins in my pouch the day I woke. Enough to live modestly for years, if you were careful. Paid, I realized now, in the old minting — Cinderfall hadn't struck coin like that since the Founding contracts. Someone had paid me. Or rather, someone had paid *for* me, and the change had been left in my pocket.

The note. *Don't go looking.* Written by a man who knew what he'd lose if he looked, which meant written by a man who had already lost it once and chosen the loss. You don't write that kind of note to yourself unless the alternative was worse than forgetting.

And the sketches themselves. Four hundred and eleven of them, and not one — I checked, I checked until dawn — not one dated after the year the wards were forged. My hand had been drawing her since before I could have met her. Which meant I hadn't lost a recent memory. I'd lost something old. Something foundational. Something someone had gone to enormous expense to make sure I would never—

Oh.

Oh, you absolute fool, Wren.

I sat in the dark of that tunnel with fifty versions of her face around me, and I understood what I was. I wasn't a haunted man. I was a receipt. I was the change left in the pocket of a bargain, walking around wearing boots, drawing the face of the thing that had been bought.

She'd said her mother sold herself so Sable could live free. She'd said the price included memories — hers of her mother, and others'. Others'. Plural. A contract like that wouldn't stop at mothers. A contract thorough enough to hollow a daughter's heart would sweep the whole house. Every person who loved her. Every memory of her, purchased and shelved in the market below like jars of spice.

Except mine hadn't been shelved. Mine had been *spent*. Spent on the purchase itself — my remembering of her was part of the currency, the down payment on her freedom. That's why the court let me keep the sketches. That's why the note said don't look. Because a man who forgets why he draws can be trusted to keep drawing, but a man who remembers might start asking what he paid.

I had paid her. Eleven years ago, without knowing it, I had handed over the only version of her I would ever get to keep, and the court had spent it, and here she was, real and breathing and squinting skeptically at maps ten feet away from me, and I didn't know her at all.

My hands knew her. That was all I had left. Four hundred and eleven drawings and a scar above the left eyebrow.

I think I laughed. I'm told I did. Odile woke up and told me I sounded like a hinge.

* * *

I found her alone at the edge of the lantern-light, later, checking her survey marks. Her oath-scarred forearm was bare where she'd pushed back her sleeve, the covenant inked into her skin like a promise the world could read over her shoulder.

"I need to tell you something," I said, "and I need you to not be kind about it. Kindness will ruin me right now."

She looked up. "Wren."

"I figured it out. What I am down here. Why I draw you." I held up the folio, let it fall open. Fifty faces fanned across the stone between us, fifty versions of her, and she went very still in the way people go still around wounds. "Eleven years ago somebody bought your freedom. Your mother started the payment. But contracts like this run deep, and mine was part of it. My memory of you. I sold it — or it was taken as tender, I honestly don't know which, and it doesn't matter. I'm the receipt, Sable. I've been drawing you for eleven years because my hands wouldn't accept what the rest of me signed away."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the dark with the patience of the Undercourt itself.

"You don't remember me," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Not even slightly. But I know you fell out of a pear tree at nine. I know you squint when you lie and go quiet when you're furious. I know you hum when you map and stop humming when you're about to disagree with someone." My voice was doing something embarrassing. I let it. "I know you the way a wall knows a painting hung on it. That's not knowing a person. That's just being shaped by them."

"Wren—"

"Let me finish, because I've rehearsed this and if I stop I won't restart." I took a breath that shook more than I wanted it to. "There's a way to fix this. The magistrates deal in substitution — Odile confirmed it, the clerk confirmed it, everything down here confirms it. Debts can be transferred. Collateral can be exchanged. If the contract needs a soul, it can have mine instead of yours. If it needs a memory, it can take every one I have left. I'll sign whatever they put in front of me. I'll pay in full, with interest, and you walk out of this mountain and go home and live, and somewhere in Cinderfall there'll be a man who draws strangers' faces and doesn't know why, and that's fine. That's *fine*, Sable. It's a fair trade. It's better than fair. One of us should get to remember."

She was crying. I hadn't expected that. I'd braced for anger, for the squint, for the skeptical mouth. Not tears, silent and furious, the way she cried like someone doing arithmetic.

"No," she said.

"Sable—"

"*No.*" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, like the tears were an insult. "Do you know what you're proposing? Do you actually hear it? You're proposing that I stand here and watch another person erase himself for me. Again. My mother did it. Whoever else was on that contract did it. Half my life is built out of other people's disappearances, Wren, and I am *done*. I am done being the thing everyone pays with."

"It's not erasure if I choose it—"

"It's *exactly* erasure if you choose it! That's the whole horror of this place!" She jabbed a finger downward, toward the market, toward the spire, toward the whole hungry machinery beneath our feet. "This court runs on love that volunteers. That's its fuel. That's the entire trick — it finds people who love each other and lets one of them pay, because paying feels noble and refusing feels cruel, and either way the court wins. You want to hand it the most beautiful thing you have? Go ahead. It'll burn your memory in the spire to keep the wards lit and call it commerce, and in a hundred years some clerk will sell vials of you in the bazaar, labeled and priced, and nobody will remember that you were a person. You'd be making their argument for them. You'd be *funding* them."

"So we do nothing? We let them take you?"

"We let them take *nothing*." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were wet and absolutely unyielding, and I thought: my hands drew this. My hands knew this exact expression before I ever saw it. "Listen to me. Whatever happens in that ledger room, whatever the magistrates demand — the answer is not you. It is not Odile. It is not Tam, gods help us, especially not Tam. If the price has to be paid, I will find a way to pay it that doesn't require anyone to vanish. That's the whole point of what I'm trying to build. A contract rewritten so the debt spreads out instead of cutting deep. So many people carry it that it crushes no one."

"And if it can't be done?"

"Then it can't be done with you as the payment." She said it flatly, finally, the way you state a law of nature. "I refuse. I refuse you, Wren. Get used to it."

And there it was — the strangest thing anyone has ever said to me, the sentence I will keep until I die however I die: *I refuse you.* Refusal as devotion. Everyone else in my life had shown love by giving things up for me, or taking things for my sake, and here was this woman telling me that loving me meant declining my sacrifice, rejecting my gift, standing on my offering like it was a document she refused to sign.

I laughed again. This time it came out wet.

"You're crying," I said.

"You're crying."

"We're both crying. This is undignified. There are wards nearby that taste fear, and we're marinating in it."

She laughed too, horribly, hiccupping, and then she did something I will be turning over for the rest of my life. She reached out and touched the folio — not me, the drawings, laying her palm flat over fifty versions of her own face.

"Keep these," she said. "Whatever happens. If I come out of this forgotten, or changed, or not coming out at all — keep drawing. Somebody in this mountain should remember me accurately, even if it's only your hands."

"That's a terrible thing to ask a man."

"I know." She picked up her lantern. "I'm asking anyway."

* * *

Tam found me an hour later, sitting where she'd left me, surrounded by my own evidence. He didn't say anything at first. Dead-boy tact, I suppose — he stood in the entrance with his hands in his pockets and let me be ridiculous in private.

"She said no," I told him.

"Yeah. She says no a lot. It's mostly how she loves people." He shuffled in and sat down across from me, cross-legged, his eyes catching the lantern-light with that faint wrongness they have, like windows into a room where someone died. "For what it's worth, I think you'd have been terrible collateral. You'd have bored the magistrates to death with sketching."

"There's that."

"But also—" He hesitated, and for once the boy who remembers being dead looked genuinely young, genuinely scared. "Also, I heard the wards shifting tonight. Down past the market. They're moving faster, and the Hollowing's pressing harder, and the magistrates called an early session. Collection's been moved up, Wren. It's tomorrow night. Maybe sooner."

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Then I guess," I said, gathering my folio, closing it over fifty faces of the woman I loved and could not remember, "we'd better go tell her the clock just got shorter."

Tam nodded slowly. "And Wren? When we get there — when she stands in front of them and does whatever mad thing she's planning—" He looked at me with those dead-lit eyes. "Don't try to save her. Help her. There's a difference. Took me dying once to learn it."

Down the tunnel, faint and getting fainter, the spire's fire flickered green, and somewhere above us the empty sky waited for its stars.
