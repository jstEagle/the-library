## 17. Three Nights, One Contract

The herald came for me at dusk on the third night, which I thought was a courtesy until I realized it was theater. Everything the Undercourt did was theater. They wanted an audience.

"Villalobos," it said from the doorway of my shop, black scales drinking the last of the light. "The court will receive you now."

"Now?" I said, tying my apron strings with hands that only shook a little. "I haven't even closed out my ledger. You'd think an institution obsessed with accounts would appreciate bookkeeping."

The herald's eyes were the color of deep water where nothing lives. It did not laugh. I hadn't expected it to. Fifteen years of selling belief to desperate people had taught me that the ones who hold all the power never find anything funny — humor is what the powerless use as currency when they can't pay in anything else.

Tam was waiting by the door with his coat already buttoned wrong, two buttons off, and I fixed them because if I didn't I was going to start crying and there wasn't time for that. His hand was cold. It had been getting colder since the drowned stair, since he kept soothing wards that tasted fear, spending warmth like a boy spending his last coins on sweets.

"You could still run," he said. For the hundredth time. He was eleven years old and had died once already, and he still believed running was a thing people could do.

"Where?" I asked, and kissed the top of his head. "I'm a cartographer, Tam. I don't run from places. I map them."

"That's not brave, that's just stubborn."

"They're frequently the same thing. Ask anyone."

Odile Ferran stood at the corner where the lamplight couldn't quite reach her, the way she'd stood for three days now, drunk-looking without being drunk, her ward-keeper's badge hanging off her coat like something she'd forgotten to throw away. She'd offered her remaining years to the magistrates twice already. Both times I'd told her no. She was saving up arguments for a third attempt; I could see them stacked behind her eyes like cordwood.

"Wren?" I asked.

"Sketching," Odile said. "He's been sketching you all day. Says if he's going to forget why he's doing this, he wants to remember the shape of it."

That was Wren. The lover I didn't remember, who didn't remember me, who kept drawing my face anyway because some part of him had refused to sell all of himself. There was a whole history between us written in a language neither of us spoke anymore, and tonight one of us might have to burn the dictionary.

"Right," I said, and picked up my satchel. Inside it: ink, three pens, a knife for cutting quills, and the oath I'd cut into my own forearm eleven days ago, the cartographer's covenant, the promise that bound me to finish whatever map I started. My arm ached the way old scars ache before weather. Or before endings.

We walked down through Cinderfall while the city pretended not to watch us. That's what Cinderfall does best — pretends. Pretends the charms work, pretends the magic isn't outlawed, pretends the stars went out because of weather. But tonight the pretending had cracks in it. People stood in their doorways. An old woman pressed a dried flower into Tam's hand. A man I'd sold a luck-charm to four years ago — a charm that worked exactly as well as any other, which is to say entirely through faith — touched his hat to me like I was going to a funeral.

Maybe I was. Maybe we all were. That was rather the point of what I intended to propose.

* * *

The way down was different this time. No drowned stair, no flooded tunnels. The Undercourt wanted me, so the Undercourt opened itself like a throat. Doors appeared in cellar walls and alley ends and once, absurdly, inside a chimney. We walked down through the mountain the way water walks down — finding every low place, filling every hollow.

The caverns had shifted again. Of course they had. But I had spent eleven days mapping them, and my maps — drawn in ink mixed with my own blood, per the old covenant — stayed true even when the stone moved. That was the secret nobody in the Undercourt seemed to know about cartographers: we don't record what places are. We record what places *owe*. And the mountain owed us a path.

The soul-lanterns burned green along the route, each one a kept memory doing its eternal small suffering. I'd stopped looking directly at them on day six. Tam still looked. I think he felt obligated to, the way you feel obligated to look at graves.

Wren walked beside me without touching me. He'd been doing that for days — orbiting at a distance of exactly one pace, close enough to catch me if I fell, far enough to pretend he wasn't. Tonight, at the last turning before the court, he finally spoke.

"I drew something new," he said, and handed me a folded page.

It was me. Not the me of the past week — tired, ink-stained, terrified — but a me I didn't recognize. Younger. Laughing at something outside the frame. Drawn with the kind of obsessive precision that comes from loving a face, from knowing it better than your own hands.

"When did you see me like this?" I asked.

"I don't know." His voice was very quiet. "That's the worst part. I know I saw it. I know I loved whatever I was looking at. I just can't—" His jaw tightened. "Keep it. If it helps."

"It helps," I said, and put it in my satchel next to the ink, because I am a sentimental fool and always have been, apparently, even about memories I don't own.

Then the doors opened, and the court received us.

* * *

The magistrates were waiting in the Ledger Room, which I had mapped on day nine and which had obligingly not moved since, as though even the shifting caverns understood that some rooms are load-bearing. Three figures woven from contract-parchment, tall as doorways, their faces blank vellum that rippled when they spoke. Behind them, shelves rose into darkness — the master ledgers, thirteen contracts' worth of bought souls, and somewhere among those pages my mother's handwriting, though I'd read it only once and had been carrying it ever since like a coal I couldn't put down or hold.

*Let my daughter forget me, so she may choose freely.*

She'd given me the choice. That was the thing I kept coming back to. Not safety — choice. And so here I was, choosing, in front of three creatures made of paperwork.

"Sable Villalobos," said the first magistrate, in a voice like a page turning. "The contract of Vesper's Founding comes due. Thirteen souls were purchased. Twelve remain recorded. One name was burned away and has lived uncollected for fifteen years."

"One name," I said, "which is mine."

"The court claims its property."

"Do you?" I stepped forward, and I'll admit my knees were doing something unsoldierly, but my voice held. Fifteen years of hawking trinkets to skeptics teaches you how to sound certain. "Because I've read your founding contract. All of it. Including the clauses you probably hoped I wouldn't reach."

The middle magistrate's vellum face rippled. "Speak."

"Clause nine. *Amendments to this instrument may be made by the signatory alone.*" I let that sit in the green lantern-light. "My mother signed. Her blood is in the ink. And her final line — the one your clerks filed under 'sentimental addendum' instead of 'binding testament' — names me as her inheritor. Which makes me, by your own law, a signatory."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Somewhere far above, I imagined I could feel the wards flickering, the Hollowing pressing its slow tide against the border of the world.

"The burned name cannot amend what it was burned from," said the third magistrate.

"Then unburn it," I said. "Restore my name to the ledger, in full, as signatory. Because here's what happens otherwise." I took out my maps — eleven days of work, the unmappable made mappable — and spread them on the great table. "You're starving. Your spire is burning memory faster than your markets can harvest it. The stars went out because the fuel ran low. The wards are failing because the spire is failing. And when the wards fail, the Hollowing comes through, and then there's no city, no court, no debtors, no creditors. Just unbeing, eating everything, including your precious ledgers. You can't collect from a hole in the world."

"We know our arithmetic," said the first magistrate, and for the first time I heard something under the parchment voice. Something dry and thin. Fear, maybe. Or hunger.

"Then you know the current arrangement ends in default either way. One soul — mine — buys you what, another decade? Less? And then you're back here, hungrier, with fewer names left to burn." I pulled out the pen. My hand was steady now. Steadier than it had any right to be. "So I'm invoking clause nine. I'm rewriting the term."

The magistrates leaned in — all three at once, a movement like a book closing.

"The debt will be paid," I said, "not by one soul. By shared memory. Every person in Cinderfall gives one beloved recollection — freely chosen, freely given — to feed the spire. One memory each. A wedding dance. A father's voice. A first snow. The city keeps everything else. Keeps *themselves*. And the spire burns on willing fuel, which I'm told—" I glanced at Odile, who gave me the smallest nod— "burns brighter than stolen fuel ever did. Willing fire lasts. Your own ledgers say so. Page four hundred and twelve of the founding registry, marginal note in the second founder's hand: *that which is given outlasts that which is taken.*"

"You would ask a city to forget," said the middle magistrate.

"No," I said. "I'd ask a city to *give*. There's a difference, and it's the entire difference. Your whole system runs on taking — taking souls, taking names, taking memory from people who never agreed. Mine runs on consent. Every contributor signs. Every gift is chosen. Nobody loses themselves. They lose one piece, one beloved piece, and they choose which." I looked down at my own arm, at the covenant scarred there. "I'll give the first. I know exactly which memory I'd give. I've known for eleven days."

"And if the city refuses?" asked the third magistrate.

"Then you get my soul after all, the wards die within the year, and we all find out together what the Hollowing leaves behind." I smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "But they won't refuse. I've sold charms in that city for fifteen years. I know what they believe. They believe in each other, even when they pretend not to. It's the one thing they've never stopped buying."

The magistrates withdrew into themselves — literally, their parchment faces folding inward, consulting, three documents arguing in a language older than speech. Tam's cold hand found mine. Odile stood at my shoulder like a wall that had decided, after long consideration, to be on my side.

And Wren — Wren was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read, except that it made my chest hurt, except that some traitor part of me whispered that I *used* to be able to read it, once, before the price of my survival was paid out of his eyes.

"If they agree," he said quietly, "you understand what authoring means. The contract's final clause binds to its writer. Everyone whose memory feeds the fire forgets the one who wrote it."

"I know," I said.

"You'd be a stranger in your own city."

"I'd be a *free* stranger in a living city." I squeezed Tam's hand. "There are worse trades. I've seen the alternatives. I've mapped them."

The magistrates unfolded.

"The amendment is heard," said the first magistrate. "The court deliberates at dawn. If the signatory's terms are accepted, collection proceeds as rewritten. If refused—" the vellum face rippled into something almost like a smile— "the original claim stands. One soul. Yours. Tonight's mercy expires with the dawn."

"Dawn," I agreed. "I've never liked negotiating after breakfast anyway."

They sank into the floor like signatures sinking into wet paper, and the Ledger Room exhaled, and we were alone with thirteen contracts' worth of dark.

* * *

We climbed back toward the surface in silence, because what do you say on the night before the world gets rewritten? Odile went ahead to rally the ward-keepers — she had one more argument to make, and this time I wasn't going to stop her, because tomorrow she might need to spend her years buying us time while the city chose. Tam walked between us, humming something tuneless, the way he did when he was frightened and refusing to admit it.

At the mouth of the last tunnel, where the first gray hint of dawn was bleeding into the sky above Cinderfall, Wren stopped.

"For the record," he said, "if I forget you — whoever you end up being to me — I want it noted that I'd do this part again. The choosing. Even without remembering why."

"That's the stupidest, bravest thing anyone's ever said to me," I told him, "and I include my mother in that count."

He almost smiled. "Go write your contract, cartographer."

So I sat down on the cold stone with my ink and my blood and my pen, and above me the starless sky waited, and below me the court deliberated, and around me a city slept that didn't yet know it was about to be asked to give away its most precious things — and to become, in the giving, more itself than it had ever been.

I dipped the pen. I began to write.

And far beneath the mountain, something vast turned a page.
