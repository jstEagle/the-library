## 18. The Bargaining

I have been drunk in some truly impressive places in my fifty-three years — a flooded cistern in Cinderfall's low quarter, the vestry of a god nobody worships anymore, once, memorably, the evidence locker of my own ward-house. But I had never been drunk in front of a magistrate of the Undercourt until tonight, and I want it noted, for the record, that I was sober. Deliberately, painfully, heroically sober. The flask stayed in my coat the whole time. If you knew what that flask had seen me through, you'd understand that leaving it sealed was the single greatest act of courage in this entire sorry affair, and I include Sable Villalobos inking an oath onto her own arm.

The deliberation chamber sat at the heart of the Undercourt like a tumor made of paperwork. Three magistrates stood (or rather *hung*, or rather *were suspended in the manner of things that have never needed legs*) before us: three figures woven from contract-parchment, their bodies a lattice of clauses and signatures, their faces blank where faces should be except for the occasional word surfacing across them like a fish rising in a pond. *Considered.* *Precedent.* *Default.* Words swam across their skin and sank again.

Sable stood beside me with her oath-scarred arm held out like a woman presenting credentials at a border crossing, which, in a sense, she was. Tam hovered behind her, radiating the particular anxious energy of a boy who has died once and does not care to make a habit of it. And Wren — quiet Wren, sketching Wren, who had followed us down into hell because his hands wouldn't let him do anything else — stood at her shoulder drawing the magistrates themselves, which I suspect was either very brave or very stupid, and having met him, I'd say both.

Above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of mountain, Cinderfall was holding its breath. We could feel it down here — or I could, anyway, old ward-keeper's habit, feeling for the wards the way you feel for a pulse. And the pulse was stuttering.

Because the Hollowing was pressing.

* *

You should understand something about the Hollowing, since I'll be talking about it as though it were a weather system, which is how we in the trade always talked about it, because the alternative was screaming. It is not an army. Armies can be fought. It is not a beast. Beasts can be starved. It is a tide of unbeing — a slow gray pressure at the edge of the world that erases whatever it touches, not violently, not even cruelly, but thoroughly. A village doesn't burn when the Hollowing takes it. It simply stops having ever been. The hearthstones forget they were quarried. The graves forget they hold anyone. Somewhere in my files there was a town called Merrow's Ford that isn't anywhere now, and the file itself is blank, and I only know it was ever full because I remember reading it, and memory, as this whole cursed business proves, is negotiable.

The border wards of Cinderfall were the dam against that tide. And the fuel of those wards was memory — harvested, hoarded, burned. That was the spire's purpose. That was the horror at the bottom of everything: the city's safety was bought with souls, maintained with stolen recollections, and the stars overhead were nothing but exhaust from a furnace eating the past.

And the furnace was running dry.

We could feel that too. Every few minutes the chamber shuddered, faintly, like a house settling — except houses don't settle upward, and this tremor came from above, from the spire, coughing on thin fuel while outside the walls the gray tide leaned its weight against thirteen generations of other people's memories.

"Your proposal," said the middle magistrate, and its voice was the sound of a page turning, "is irregular."

Everything about me is irregular, I wanted to say. My ward-keeping license was revoked eleven years ago for drinking on duty, which was true, and for asking questions about the founding contract, which was truer. Instead I said, "Irregularity is rather the point."

"The contract," said the left magistrate, "specifies thirteen souls. One has been tendered." Its parchment face rippled, and I saw the word *Villalobos* surface and sink. "Collection proceeds."

"One soul," Sable said, and her voice didn't shake, which I will never stop being impressed by, because mine would have, "for a city of forty thousand. You're getting a bargain and you know it. You also know your furnace is starving. Thirteen souls bought you fifteen years. What happens when the fuel runs out tomorrow? You default. Everything defaults. The wards die, the Hollowing comes through, and then—" she gestured upward, at the mountain, at the court, at the whole elegant edifice of kept things— "*nothing*. Not even you. You can't collect a debt from a place that never existed."

Silence. Well — silence plus the distant arrhythmic shudder of a dying spire, which is its own kind of commentary.

The right magistrate stirred. Its parchment skin rustled like wind through autumn fields, if autumn fields were made of legal instruments. "The signatory proposes amendment."

"The signatory proposes," Sable said, "a new term."

* *

Here is what I offered, and I want you to appreciate the arithmetic, because I did, bitterly, all the way down the drowned stair, doing sums in my head like a merchant at her own funeral.

Odile Ferran, former First Ward-keeper of Cinderfall, age fifty-three, remaining years: uncertain, but actuarially speaking somewhere north of twenty and south of thirty, depending on how much longer I intended to keep drinking, which, see above, was apparently no longer much. Twenty-some years. Decades of a hard, useful, mostly regretful life.

I put them on the table.

"Not as payment," I said, before the magistrates could get any ideas. "As surety. Collateral. You hold my years in escrow while the amended contract runs. If the city honors its term — if the people of Cinderfall give their memories willingly, one apiece, to feed the spire — you release me. If they renege, if the offering fails, you take my decades and the original claim stands."

"That is not how surety works," said the left magistrate.

"I'm aware. I'm proposing we invent how surety works. You've spent fifteen years running a furnace on stolen goods; surely you can stretch to a novel financial instrument."

Tam made a sound behind me that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Good boy. Know when to be quiet.

The magistrates' words churned. *Surety.* *Escrow.* *Precedent absent.* They consulted each other without speaking, three sheets of parchment leaning together like conspirators, and I used the time to look at Sable.

She was watching them with the expression I'd learned to recognize over these long terrible days — the cartographer's expression, the one that treats even catastrophe as terrain to be surveyed. She wasn't afraid of them. She was *measuring* them. And I thought, not for the first time: her mother knew exactly what she was buying when she paid with herself. Not safety. *This.* A daughter who would walk into the Undercourt and start redrawing it.

Which brings me to the argument. Because the magistrates eventually turned back to us, and the middle one said, in its page-turning voice, "State your justification. Why should the court accept a collective, voluntary, renewable offering in place of a fixed, secured, completed claim?"

And I — Odile Ferran, disgraced, sober, terrified — stepped forward and told them the truth, because it was the only currency I had left that they couldn't devalue.

* *

"A soul hoarded is worth what it's worth," I said. "One soul. Thirteen souls. A fixed sum, spent down, gone. Your ledger knows this better than anyone — you've been burning through your reserves for fifteen years, and now the fire's guttering, and you know it, and that's why you haven't simply taken Sable Villalobos and been done with it. A single soul won't save you. You need more than you took. You need *more than theft can get you.*

"And here's why. Stolen memory burns fast. Ask any thief — no, ask any *fire*. What's taken unwillingly resists. It burns hot and quick and leaves ash that won't kindle. That's why your spire eats and eats and is always hungry. But memory given freely—" I heard my own voice go strange, go quiet, and I let it, because the moment deserved it— "memory given freely is *willing fuel*. A wedding dance surrendered by the couple who danced it. A father's voice, given up by the child who loved it. A first snow, released by someone who remembers being small. Those don't flash and die. They burn slow. They burn *clean*. One freely-given memory is worth ten stolen ones, and you have forty thousand people up there, and every last one of them loves something enough to pay for the sky."

"You cannot know that," said the right magistrate.

"No," I agreed. "I can't. Neither can you. That's the wager. But consider the alternative positions. Yours: take the girl's soul, gain one unit of fuel, watch the wards fail within the year regardless, lose the city, lose the spire, lose yourselves to the Hollowing, recover nothing. Mine: accept the amendment, gain forty thousand units of willing fuel annually — *annually*, gentlemen, this is a renewable resource, the first one your entire economy has ever produced — keep the wards alive, keep the city alive, keep your court alive, and hold my miserable decades as insurance against failure. One of these positions is arithmetic. The other is suicide with extra steps."

"They are not gentlemen," Tam whispered behind me. "They're contracts."

"Contracts can be gentlemen," I murmured. "It's called good faith. Look it up. Actually, don't — you're probably written on it somewhere."

The words churned faster across the magistrates' skins now. *Voluntary contribution.* *Renewal cycle.* *Consent of the governed.* And underneath, faint but legible, the word they'd been circling all night, the word that had brought us here: *default*.

"You argue," said the middle magistrate slowly, "that a city which chooses to remember together is worth more than thirteen souls hoarded apart."

"I argue it because it's *true*," I said. "It's the truest thing I've said in thirty years of ward-keeping, and I once gave a eulogy that made a funeral crowd laugh, so I know true things when I say them. Your whole court is built on the premise that memory is property — that it can be owned, sold, seized, burned. Fine. I won't dispute your premise; I'd be here till the mountains erode. But even under your own law, property freely exchanged holds value that seized property never does. You've known this. It's why you bother with contracts at all instead of just taking. Consent isn't sentiment. Consent is *collateral structure*. A debt acknowledged is a debt that gets paid. You've been running a protection racket on a city that, it turns out, is willing to pay — not because you frightened it into it, but because it loves its own sky. Take the deal. Be the first court in history to be saved by the thing it forgot existed: goodwill."

Somewhere in the middle of that speech I realized I was crying, which was inconvenient, and that I hadn't reached for the flask, which was miraculous, and that both facts were about the same thing, which was this: I helped forge that founding contract fifteen years ago. I watched thirteen names go onto parchment, twelve recorded and one burned away, and I drank myself blind afterward and told myself for fifteen years that I'd been small and powerless and had no choice. Standing here, offering the only thing I had left to a court of living paperwork, I finally understood that I'd had a choice all along, and this was it, arriving late, arriving *finally*, and that late was infinitely better than never.

* *

The deliberation lasted — I am not exaggerating — four hours.

They withdrew into themselves, literally, the three magistrates folding inward like letters being closed, and the chamber filled with the sound of a thousand quills scratching, which I gathered was them thinking. Sable sat on the floor with her back against a pillar of fused contracts and reviewed her maps by lantern-light, because the girl genuinely cannot stop working, God love her. Tam slept, curled against Wren, who sketched the sleeping boy with the tenderness of a man practicing on someone he couldn't hurt yet. And I stood at the edge of the chamber, where the wall of the cavern opened onto a fissure, and looked out at the dark beyond the wards.

The Hollowing was close. Closer than it had ever been in my lifetime. I couldn't see it — you can't see unbeing, you can only see where it *isn't*, a gray absence pressing against the last green shimmer of the failing wards like a thumb testing fruit. Every few minutes the wards flickered and the absence seemed to lean closer, patient as arithmetic, and I thought: this is what waits if we fail. Not death. Death is something. This is the subtraction of everything, including the fact that there was ever anything to subtract.

Four hours. Then the magistrates unfolded.

"The court finds the proposal," said the middle magistrate, and paused, and I swear on my revoked license the pause was theatrical, "not without merit."

High praise, from a contract.

"Conditions," said the left magistrate. "First: the offering must be voluntary. Coerced memory burns as stolen memory burns. The court will taste the difference."

"It will be voluntary," Sable said. "That's the whole point."

"Second: the signatory remains bound. The rewritten contract names her as author. Authors are clauses. If the offering fails, the original claim revives, and the surety—" its parchment face rippled toward me— "is forfeit."

"My decades," I said. "Yes. I heard myself offer them. I was there."

"Third." The right magistrate drifted closer, and its blank face filled, briefly, with a single word I will remember until I die, which might be soon: *tonight*. "Collection night arrives at moonrise. The Hollowing presses the outer wards now. The spire fails within days regardless. There is no fourth night. The city gathers, or the city ends."

Moonrise. I looked at Sable. She was already rolling up her maps, already moving, already three steps ahead of the conversation, because of course she was.

"We'll need the herald," she said. "To carry the summons. Every soul in Cinderfall, at the spire, by moonrise. And Odile—" she looked at me, and her eyes were bright and scared and certain, all three at once, which is the exact recipe for a person about to change the world— "they'll need someone to tell them why. Someone they trust. Someone whose name means something to the old guard."

"Ferran," I said. "Former First Ward-keeper. Currently famous for disgrace."

"Currently famous for coming down here to fight for them," she said. "That travels further than you think."

Behind us, the magistrates began to fold shut again, sealing the terms into themselves, and the last thing I heard before the chamber went quiet was the scratch of a fresh clause being written — our clause, the city's clause, the mad beautiful clause — and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the sound of the Hollowing pressing, pressing, pressing against a sky that had one night left.

I touched the flask in my coat. Left it sealed.

"Come on, then," I said to Sable, to Tam, to Wren, to all of us. "We've got a city to convince. And moonrise doesn't wait for speeches."
