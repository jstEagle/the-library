## 22. Mapping What Remains

**Sable**

The mountain had stopped humming.

I noticed it the way you notice a toothache ending — not all at once, but as an absence you keep prodding to be sure. For fifteen years, Mount Vesper had murmured under Cinderfall like a sleeping animal, and every cartographer in the city had learned to draw around it: *here be the Undercourt, here be the wards, here be everything we agreed not to look at.* Now it was just rock. Cold, ordinary, slightly embarrassing rock.

"It's rude to stare," Wren said beside me. "It just lost its job."

We were sitting on the reservoir wall with our boots dangling over the drowned stair — the same stair Tam had led us down three weeks and one apocalypse ago — and between us lay the first sheet of what I was already thinking of, privately and with some embarrassment, as the honest map.

Every map I'd ever made had been a lie by omission. That's the trade secret nobody tells you about cartography: you don't record the world, you record what your patron can afford to know exists. The guild maps of Cinderfall showed tidy streets and a proud spire and no slums at all. My own maps — the ones I'd sold from the charm-shop for coppers — showed safe roads and friendly borders and a world that made sense. I'd spent fifteen years drawing comfort and calling it truth.

This new sheet had none of that. It had the drowned stair marked in blue ink with a note in my handwriting: *flooded to the ninth step; smells like regret.* It had the fissure where we'd seen the Hollowing, circled twice, annotated: *do not touch, do not think about touching, Tam touched it anyway.* It had the market of kept things drawn from memory, its stalls now empty, its lanterns dark, labeled simply: *was.*

"Was," Wren read over my shoulder. "That's a bold word for a cartographer."

"It's the most accurate word I have."

He was sketching again — he never really stopped, his fingers always moving across paper like they were looking for something they'd misplaced, which, to be fair, they were. But the sketches had changed. Before, they'd all been of me: my face over and over, hundreds of attempts to recover something he'd sold away without knowing what it was. Now he drew the city. The reservoir. The spire, standing quiet and white-scarred, its fire gone out because it no longer needed to burn anything but itself.

"You're staring at me," he said.

"I'm surveying you. There's a difference. Surveying is professional."

"Is that what this is." He turned his hand over on the stone between us, palm up, an offer rather than a demand. I took it. His grip was warm, and I thought — not for the first time, and probably not for the last — about the coin he'd spent. His last coin. A single memory bought back from a collapsing court while the magistrates unraveled into blank parchment around him, and he'd chosen, out of everything the Undercourt had ever kept, to buy *me*.

"You could have bought anything," I said. "You could have bought your childhood back. Your mother's voice. Everyone buys their mother's voice."

"I don't remember having a mother worth the price."

"That's exactly my point—"

"Sable." He said my name the way people say a word they've only recently been allowed to use. "I didn't buy a memory. I bought the person who was standing next to me when everything ended. There's a difference there too."

I looked at the honest map instead of at him, because if I looked at him I was going to do something unprofessional, and the map needed finishing. The border, for instance. The old border — the one that had held for two hundred years, the one whose collapse had started everything — was gone. Not broken. *Gone*, the way a scar goes when the wound finally heals properly. Beyond it, the land resumed: hills, rivers, forests that no guild map had ever recorded because no guild cartographer had ever been allowed past the wards.

An entire unmapped world, sitting there, waiting.

"Tam's late," I said, because my voice was doing something embarrassing.

"He's always late. He says it builds anticipation."

"He says it because half of him doesn't experience time anymore and he forgets which direction it goes."

Wren laughed, and I let myself laugh too, and neither of us said the other thing, the thing we both knew: that Tam's lateness wasn't entirely a joke. That the boy who remembered being dead had given a piece of himself to the Hollowing so the rest of us wouldn't have to give more, and the piece it took — the color blue, and whatever else lived near the color blue — wasn't coming back.

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what nobody warned me about getting your memory back: it doesn't arrive all at once. It comes in weather.

Some mornings I wake up and she's everywhere — Sable in the smell of ink, Sable in the way light hits the reservoir at dawn, Sable in a hundred small moments I apparently lived through and paid to forget and am now collecting again like a man retrieving scattered coins. Other mornings the memory sits behind glass, and I know the facts — her name, her face, the shape of her laugh — the way you know a city from a map, accurately and from very far away.

Today was a map day. Which is why I was grateful for the work. Work doesn't require you to feel anything; it only requires you to hold the pencil steady.

She'd changed the legend again. She kept changing the legend. This morning it read:

*Solid line: confirmed.*
*Dashed line: suspected.*
*Dotted line: hoped for.*
*Squiggle: ask Tam.*

"What," I said, "is a squiggle."

"Terrain that refuses to commit to existing. There's a valley east of the old border that does it. Odile swears it moved overnight."

"Odile also swears she's given up drinking, and yet."

"She's *moderating*."

Odile Ferran arrived at that exact moment, which either proved her innocence or her uncanny instinct for being discussed. She came down the reservoir path with a surveyor's kit over one shoulder and a flask she did not open, and behind her, eventually, predictably, came Tam — running, grinning, half a heartbeat out of phase with the rest of the world.

"Sorry," Tam announced. "I got interested in a cloud."

"A cloud," Odile said.

"It was doing something. Structurally. You wouldn't understand, you're not a cartographer." He dropped onto the wall beside Sable and peered at the map. "You've still got the fissure on here."

"It's part of the record."

"It's part of *my* head, is what it is. You could label it something nicer."

I watched Sable consider this — really consider it, the way she considered everything, turning it over like a coin to check which side was false. Then she dipped her pen and added beneath the double-circled mark: *the place where Tam gave away the color blue, and got laughed at by the Hollowing, which has no sense of humor, which makes it funnier.*

"Better?" she asked.

Tam read it. Something moved through his face — grief, maybe, or its cousin — and then he grinned, wide and crooked, and said, "You spelled 'Hollowing' wrong."

"I absolutely did not."

"There's a letter missing. Or extra. One of those." He tapped the page. "Half of me reads it fine. Half of me thinks it's gibberish. Between the two of me, we've decided spelling is a scam."

And that was Tam these days: laughing at the hole in himself, because the alternative was falling into it. He saw things the rest of us couldn't — the wards healing like skin under the city, the faint green afterimage of soul-lanterns that no longer burned, the seams of the world where the Undercourt had been unpicked thread by thread. He'd become, whether he liked it or not, our instrument. Our compass that pointed at absences.

"Right," Odile said, all business, spreading her charts on the wall. "Today's route. East road to the old border-stone, then past it — *past it*, Ferran, say it with me, we're actually doing this — to the river nobody's named."

"The river nobody's named," Sable repeated, and I saw her hands go still in that way they did when the magnitude of something caught up with her. Fifteen years of hiding inside a mapped cage, and now the door stood open onto country no one had ever drawn. Every hill out there was a blank space. Every blank space was hers.

Ours.

"Before we go," Tam said, suddenly serious, which on Tam was alarming as a fire alarm, "there's something you should see at the border-stone. I saw it on my way here. Well — half of me saw it. The other half refused, but it wrote a note."

* * *

**Sable**

The border-stone stood where it had always stood, except that "always" had turned out to be a lie, and the stone knew it. The old ward-marks carved into its face — the ones that had glowed for two centuries and then shattered the night the stars went out — were fading. Not eroding. *Fading*, like ink left in sun, like something admitting it had never been real in the first place.

But that wasn't what Tam wanted us to see.

Someone had left flowers. A small, untidy heap of them at the stone's base — wildflowers, the kind that grew in the cracks of Cinderfall's slums, the kind that cost nothing and therefore meant everything. And tucked among them, weighted down with a pebble, a strip of parchment.

I picked it up. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be.

It was a list. Names, dozens of them, in a dozen different hands — some careful, some scrawled, one written in what appeared to be charcoal held by someone crying. And beside each name, a single memory. *The wedding dance, 14th of Embermonth. My father singing off-key in the bath. First snow, age six, my sister's mitten.* The memories the whole city had fed to the spire on the last night, willingly, one apiece, to relight the stars.

Except these weren't offerings anymore. Someone had written at the top, in letters that shook: *So we don't forget what we gave. Add yours below.*

Below the original names, new ones had begun to appear. People were coming to the border-stone on their own, leaving their lists, reading each other's losses. A ledger of willing sacrifice, growing by increments, tended by nobody and everybody.

"They're keeping their own records," Wren said quietly beside me. "No court. No contract. Just... remembering together."

"Fifteen years ago," I said, "my mother signed away my memory of her so I could choose freely. That was the whole point. The choice." I looked at the growing list, at the strangers' handwriting, at the wildflowers going soft in the morning air. "They're choosing too. Every name on this page. Nobody made them give those memories. They looked at the dark and decided what it was worth."

Odile took off her hat, which for Odile was roughly equivalent to kneeling. "Your mother would have liked this," she said. "She'd have hated the walking, but she'd have liked this."

My mother's voice had come back to me once, in the last moment of the Undercourt's collapse — released from the ledger along with every other kept soul, saying goodbye, saying *you chose well*. I had built fifteen years of identity on not remembering her, and then I'd been given thirty seconds of her, and then she was gone again, properly gone, at peace, and I had discovered that grief and gratitude could occupy the same chest cavity without exploding. Mostly.

"Add it to the map," I told Wren.

"Which part?"

"All of it. The stone. The flowers. The list." I handed him the parchment, careful, the way you handle a document that is also a heart. "Mark it as a landmark. Future travelers should know this is where the stars came back."

"And what do we call it? Landmarks need names."

I looked at the stone, at the faded wards, at the open country beyond it that no one had ever been permitted to see. I thought about every map I'd ever lied with, and every lie I'd been told, and the single true line my mother had left me: *so she may choose freely.*

"Call it the Signatory Stone," I said. "And put a dotted line leading out of it. Dotted means hoped for."

"Dotted lines are provisional," Odile objected. "Professional standards—"

"Odile." I smiled, and it didn't hurt, which was still new enough to be remarkable. "I'm the last signatory of the founding contract of Cinderfall, the woman who bankrupted the Undercourt, and the only person alive whom the entire city has collectively forgotten. I think I've earned the right to draw a hopeful line."

* * *

**Wren**

She talks about being forgotten like it's a scar. It isn't. I've seen scars; I have several, most of them earned in the last month. What Sable has is stranger: she walks through a city that owes her everything and knows her not at all, and she does it cheerfully, mostly, drawing as she goes.

The forgetting had been thorough. The innkeeper who owed his rebuilt life to her contract serves her ale and calls her "traveler." Old Maren from the charm-shop row, who bought Sable's belief-charms for years, looks straight through her in the market. Even Tam — even Tam remembers her only the way you remember a story you were once in, the details gone soft, though he covers it with jokes, because covering things with jokes is Tam's native language.

Only I remember. One man, one coin, one memory, held alone.

People ask sometimes — the ones who notice the two of us always traveling together, always bent over the same maps — how we met. And Sable has developed an answer she delivers deadpan: "He sketched me before he knew me. It's a long story involving a mountain, a court, and a fire."

It usually ends the conversation.

Tonight we camped past the border-stone, first night truly outside, in country no map had ever claimed. Tam built a fire with the efficiency of someone who has died before and found it dull. Odile drank one measured cup of wine and narrated the constellations — the real ones, the returned ones, burning overhead in patterns the old ward-charts had never bothered recording because the wards made the sky irrelevant.

Sable sat apart with the honest map spread across her knees, working by starlight, and I went and sat with her, because that is apparently what I do now, in every version of my memory, in every weather.

"Show me," I said.

She turned the sheet toward me. The border. The stone. The dotted line running east into blank paper. And there, in the corner, in her smallest, most careful hand, a new entry in the legend:

*Blank space: not empty. Just not written yet.*

"When I was hiding," she said, watching the fire, "I thought a finished map was the goal. Complete knowledge. Nothing unknown, nothing dangerous. I thought that was safety." Her pen hovered over the vast whiteness east of the line. "But a finished map is a closed door, isn't it? Everything my mother wanted for me was blank space. Room to choose."

"So we leave room."

"We leave *lots* of room." She glanced at me, and the starlight did something unfair to her expression. "There will be gaps in this map forever, Wren. Places we don't reach. Things I get wrong. Whole valleys that refuse to commit to existing. Are you — is that —"

"Tolerable?"

"I was going to say acceptable."

"I know what you were going to say." I took the pen from her hand, and on the blank eastern edge of the honest map, in my own terrible handwriting, beneath one of my sketches — her face, of course, because some habits are load-bearing — I wrote: *to be continued.*

She read it. She laughed, and then she didn't quite, and then she kissed me, and the map got a crease in it that she later declared structural.

Across the fire, Tam raised an imaginary toast. "To the thirteenth name!" he called. "Who bankrupted hell and still can't spell!"

"To incomplete maps!" Odile countered.

"To blank spaces," Sable said softly, and we drank to that — water, wine, and in Tam's case a cup of nothing he insisted tasted excellent — while above us the returned stars burned in their unmapped arrangements, and somewhere east, in country no one had ever drawn, the dark waited patiently to be named.

In the morning we would walk toward it. Tonight, the four of us sat at the edge of the known world, and for the first time in my life — in any of my lives, counted or bought back — the edge felt less like a wall than a threshold.

Sable opened a fresh sheet, and began.
