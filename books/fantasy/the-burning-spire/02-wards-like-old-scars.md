## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

The wards over Vespergate flickered at dusk.

Jude noticed it the way a sailor notices a change in wind—not with alarm at first, but with the small animal attention of a man whose livelihood depends on reading invisible weather. He was standing in his shop doorway with a cup of chicory going cold in his hand, watching the light die over the rooftops of the Lantern District, when the air above the street did something it was not supposed to do.

It stuttered.

Most people never saw the wards. They were laid down three centuries ago, woven into the city's oldest stones by covenant-masons whose names had been scrubbed from every ledger and gravestone, and they hung over Ashfall like a second sky—grey, seamless, absolute. To the ordinary eye there was nothing there at all: just evening, just lamplighters beginning their rounds with their long brass poles, just the smell of coal smoke and frying onions drifting up from Marrow Street. But Jude had spent eleven years learning to see the seams of the world, because a thief who cannot see locks is only a burglar, and Jude Kavanagh had never once been arrested.

So he saw it. The ward-line that ran from the bell tower of Saint Ossian's down along the eaves of the tannery row—a thread of pale silver, steady as a plumb line for as long as he'd lived beneath it—that thread *twitched*. Flickered like a candle in a draft. Went dark for the length of one heartbeat, one full heartbeat, in which the whole street stood naked under the open sky, and then came back.

Then came back wrong. Thinner. The way an old scar goes shiny and pale when the body has stopped bothering to maintain it.

Jude stood very still and drank his chicory.

*Old magic,* he told himself. *Tired magic. Three hundred years is a long time for anything to hold.* The city had been built on the assumption that the wards were eternal, the way a house is built on the assumption that the ground beneath it is honest. Nobody maintained them because nobody remembered how. The last ward-mason had died before Jude's grandmother was born, and the Crown had spent the intervening generations doing what the Crown always did with things it couldn't understand: outlawing them, taxing their absence, and building monuments to its own ignorance.

There were seventeen registered mages in Ashfall. There had been forty-one when Jude was born. Every year the number went down—arrests, flights across the border, quiet deaths recorded as accidents—and every year the wards held anyway, indifferent, because they had never been the work of registered mages at all. They were older than registration. Older than the Crown. Older, if you believed Mother Quill's drunker sermons, than the city itself.

They were not supposed to flicker.

* * *

He closed the shop early, which he never did, and pretended to himself that it was because of his head.

His head did hurt. That was the thing he kept circling around and refusing to land on: all through the afternoon, while he'd sat behind his counter grinding dried mothwing and wrapping charms in wax paper for customers who thought they were buying love-luck or safe-travel or a good night's sleep, his own gift had been behaving strangely. Restlessly. Like a dog that hears a whistle no one else can hear.

Jude's magic was small, and he had made a religion of keeping it small. Memory-work—the taking and holding and giving-back of other people's pasts—was a gutter art, a pickpocket's art, beneath the notice of the great thaumaturges who had all been burned or exiled decades ago. It required almost nothing. A touch, a breath, a moment of opening, and a life would pour into you like water into a cupped hand. The trick was not the taking. Anyone with the knack could take. The trick was the *stopping*—knowing how much a soul could lose before it began to fray, knowing how much a thief could hold before the stolen years started bleeding into his own.

He had rules. He'd carved them into the underside of the counter where no customer would ever read them, in letters so small they were more habit than script:

*Never take much. Never take grief whole. Never be seen.*

And underneath those, older, in different ink: *Never wonder why you can.*

That last rule existed because of afternoons like this one. Because today, when Mrs. Halloway had come in for her fortnightly charm—a widow's charm, a forgetting-charm, the gentlest work he did—he had reached for her sorrow the way he always did, two fingers to her wrist, a breath, an opening—

—and she had poured into him like a *flood*.

Not the thin trickle he was used to. Not the careful sip he took and paid for. Her grief had come at him with weight behind it, a whole river of it, and for one vertiginous moment Jude had felt himself filling far past the line, felt memories that weren't his pressing against the walls of him like floodwater against a door—her wedding day, her son's first steps, the sound of the knock on her door last spring, the smell of the hospital corridor, the terrible arithmetic of a half-empty bed—

He had wrenched his hand back. She had blinked at him, mild and unbothered, and asked if everything was quite alright, and he had said yes, ma'am, just a headache, and sold her the charm at half price out of guilt.

But the fact remained. The fact remained that his gift had pulled at her like a lung pulling at air, greedy beyond anything he had ever trained into it or out of it, and the fact remained that since that moment he could feel every memory he currently held sitting inside him like coals—forty-odd little thefts, small ones, sipped and paid for, harmless ones—and each of them was *warm*. Warmer than they should be. As though something in the world had changed the temperature around them.

As though something had opened a door somewhere, and all the locked rooms in his head had felt the draft.

* * *

He walked the walls that evening, which was also not his habit, and which he also declined to examine.

Ashfall at dusk was a city pretending to be simple. Lamplighters moved along their routes like slow golden stitches. In the market square, a fishmonger argued cheerfully with a boy about the honesty of his scales. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned, badly, and someone was laughing at it. The smoke of ten thousand suppers rose straight up into windless air. It was, by any reasonable measure, an ordinary evening in the greatest city of the mortal realm, and Jude walked through it with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and counted ward-lines the way other men counted money.

Saint Ossian's: flickering. He watched it go dark twice in the space of a minute, that silver thread stuttering like a dying moth, and each time it came back a shade fainter, a shade more translucent, until he honestly could not say whether he was seeing it or remembering it.

The Gate of Nine Bells, where the border ward met the city proper: worse. Much worse. The great ward that ringed Ashfall itself—the one that kept the Otherworld on its own side of the world, the one every child learned about in school alongside the names of kings—the ward that had not so much as trembled in living memory—that ward was *breathing*. He stood under the arch of the gate with the customs guards stamping ledgers twenty feet away, and he watched the ancient barrier swell and contract, swell and contract, like the flank of a sleeping animal that had begun, very slowly, to dream.

And here was the thing he would not think about directly, the thing he carried the way you carry a hot coal wrapped in many layers of cloth:

When the ward swelled, his gift swelled with it.

When it contracted, he felt the stolen memories inside him settle and dim. When it swelled again, they flared—all of them, every charm in his stockroom, every memory in his own careful skull, the widow's grief and the dockhand's first love and the soldier's homecoming he'd bought off a deserter years ago—they all brightened together, in time, in rhythm, like lamps answering some vast distant pulse.

Like the city's magic and his own had been two ends of the same thread all along, and something had begun to pull the thread taut.

"That's not possible," he said aloud, quietly, to no one. A customs guard glanced over. Jude smiled his shopkeeper's smile—apologetic, forgettable, a face designed to leave no impression—and walked on.

Because it wasn't possible. That was the comfort he kept reaching for and kept failing to hold. His magic was *small*. That was the entire architecture of his survival. The Crown's witch-hunters found mages by resonance—every working left a ripple in the ambient field, and the hunters' instruments read ripples the way fishermen read water. Eleven years, and Jude had never rippled, because he had taught himself to work in whispers, to take memories in increments so fine that even he sometimes couldn't feel the seam. Small was safe. Small was alive. Small was the whole point.

And now his smallness was gone, or suspended, or drowned—now he could feel his own power lying in him like a flooded cellar, deep and cold and much, much larger than the room it was supposed to fit in—and if he could feel it, then so could they. So could whatever passed for the Crown's instruments these days. Serah Vane's people. He didn't know her name yet; wouldn't for another week. But he knew the Order of the Grey Lantern kept a register, and he knew that somewhere in a vault beneath the Chancellery there was a page with his assumed name on it, annotated in a hunter's precise hand: *memory-worker, minor, low priority.*

Low priority. He clung to those words like a railing over a drop.

* * *

He went home the long way, through the Lantern District, because the lanterns were lighting and because he wanted—one last time, though he didn't know it was the last time—to walk through a city that still had stars coming.

The Lantern District got its name from the old festival, the Feast of Lamps, when every window in Ashfall was lit to guide something home across the veil. No one remembered what the something was. The Church said it was souls. The Crown said it was nothing at all, and had banned the feast forty years ago as superstition, but the district kept its name the way old soldiers keep their scars: without pride, without explanation, simply because removing them would hurt too much.

Jude climbed the outside stair to his rooms above the shop, lit his single lamp, and ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window like a man too restless to sit. Below him the street emptied by degrees. The fishmonger packed his stall. The bad fiddler gave up to applause. A cat conducted its nightly inspection of the gutters with the gravity of a magistrate.

Above the rooftops, the first stars were coming out.

Jude looked at them the way he looked at everything—as inventory, as material, as the raw stuff of other people's longing. Sailors navigated by them. Lovers wished on them. Half the charms in his stockroom were, at bottom, bottled starlight: the wish-memory of a girl who'd seen a falling star at fourteen and built a marriage on the wish. People needed the stars the way they needed the wards—without knowing it, without ever once looking up on purpose—and Jude had built a comfortable living in the gap between what people needed and what they admitted to needing.

He counted them, idly, the way he did most nights. Not all of them—he wasn't a scholar—but the anchor-stars, the seven bright ones the old songs named, the ones Mother Quill swore were set in the sky like nails in a door. The Shepherd. The Pale Sister. The Wandering King, low in the west, red as an ember.

All present. All burning. All exactly where three centuries of unbroken nights had put them.

He told himself the flickering wards were decay. He told himself the surge in his gift was weather, or fatigue, or the chicory. He told himself that tomorrow he would close early again, and take stock, and maybe—*maybe*—pay a visit to the gutter cathedral, where blind Mother Quill held court among the unregistered and the uncounted, and ask her, casually, over cheap wine, whether the old woman had felt anything strange in the field lately. Just to ask. Just to know whether it was only him.

He told himself all of this while he banked his fire and washed his cup and blew out his lamp, performing the small liturgy of an ordinary evening with the thoroughness of a man rehearsing innocence.

And then he lay in the dark in his narrow bed above the dark shop, in the dark heart of the greatest city of the mortal realm, and listened.

The wards hummed. They had always hummed—you couldn't hear it with your ears, but a mage could feel it through the soles of his feet, a vast subterranean note like a cathedral organ heard through miles of stone, constant, patient, the sound of a promise being kept. Jude had slept under that hum his whole life. It was the sound of safety. It was the sound of *no*.

Tonight, the hum had changed key.

It was very slightly flat. Very slightly strained. And underneath it, so faint he could pretend for hours at a stretch that he hadn't heard it, there was a second sound—a counter-note, thin and cold and sweet, threading up through the floorboards of the world like water through a cracked foundation. It sounded the way the Otherworld was described in the forbidden books he'd never admit to owning: beautiful, and wrong, and *waiting*.

Something on the other side of the wall of the world had noticed the wall was thin.

Jude Kavanagh, who had survived eleven years by never taking much, never taking grief whole, and never being seen, lay in the dark and made the decision that would define the rest of his life, though he would not understand until much later that it was a decision at all, nor that it had already been made for him three years ago by a hooded stranger in a rain-soaked alley.

He decided to ignore it.

He decided that the wards were old and tired and would hold, because they had always held; that his gift was small and would shrink again, because it had always been small; that tomorrow he would open his shop at eight and grind his mothwing and sell his gentle lies to gentle people, and the strange electric ache in his blood would fade like any fever fades, and the stars would come out tomorrow night the same as tonight, the same as always, the same forever.

He fell asleep telling himself this. It was, he would think later, the deepest and most restful sleep he'd had in months—the sleep of a man who has chosen blindness and been granted it—and he dreamed of nothing at all.

Which was itself unusual. Jude always dreamed. His dreams were crowded with other people's yesterdays, the overflow of his trade, the tax he paid on every theft.

Nothing. Black, starless, silent nothing.

Outside his window, over the rooftops of Ashfall, the Shepherd star flickered—once, softly, like a candle in a draft—and steadied, and burned on, and no one saw.

No one but the things waiting beyond the veil, who had begun, very patiently, to count the hours until dusk.
