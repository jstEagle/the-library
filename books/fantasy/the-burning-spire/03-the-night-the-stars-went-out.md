## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm sold itself that evening, which was unusual. Most nights I had to talk a little, to lean across the counter of my stall in the Lantern District and let the customer believe they were choosing. But the cooper's daughter came straight to me with coins already sweating in her fist, and she said, "Give me the one with the sea in it," and there was nothing to do but hand it over.

It was a good charm. A fisherman's memory, bought fair three winters past from a man who wanted to forget drowning—not his own, but his brother's—and inside the glass the sea moved the way it only moves in memory: too blue, too kind, gulls crying in a language older than grief. The girl held it up to the lamplight and her face did what faces always did when they looked into a stolen thing. It went soft. It went somewhere else.

"That's my whole week's wages," she said.

"Then you're buying cheap," I told her. "Most people pay more for less sky."

She laughed, and I let myself enjoy it for exactly as long as it took her to walk away, and then I put the enjoyment away too, back in its box with all the other small warm things I allowed myself and no one else. That was the trade. That had always been the trade. I dealt in other people's yesterdays so that I would never have to look too closely at my own.

I closed the stall early. There was no reason to. Dusk had come on strange—that much I'd noticed even through the ordinary machinery of selling, the counting, the pretending. Strange how? I'd asked myself, and answered myself the way I answered everything: *not your problem, Kavanagh.* The wards along the city wall flickered at sundown like old scars remembering they were wounds, and my own magic had sat under my skin all day like a second heartbeat, too loud, too near the surface. When I reached for a thread of power to snuff the lamp—something I could have done asleep, something I had done ten thousand times—the flame didn't gutter. It *leapt*, eager, and burned green for half a breath before settling.

Green. My fire had never been green.

I told myself it was the lamp oil. I told myself a great many things while I walked home through streets that smelled of tallow and river-mud and the particular sourness of a city holding its breath without knowing why. The Lantern District at night was supposed to be a spill of light, hundreds of paper globes strung between the eaves, each one a little captive sun. Tonight maybe a third of them were lit, and those burned wrong—steadier than flame should burn, like eyes that had decided not to blink.

My rooms were above a chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane, two floors of leaning timber that groaned when the wind spoke to them. I climbed the stairs with my key already out, and halfway up I stopped, because the hair on my arms was standing, and not from cold.

Cold, though, was coming. I could feel it arriving the way you feel a storm arrive—not the weather itself but the *pressure* of it, a weight behind the ears, a hush. The city had gone quiet in a way cities never manage. No dogs. No drunks singing outside the Saltcellar. Even the river, which muttered constantly to itself like an old man reading, seemed to be listening.

I unlocked my door and went in and lit no lamps, because some instinct older than sense told me darkness was safer tonight, and I stood at my window with my hands flat against the sill and waited for whatever I was waiting for.

* * *

It began with the stars going out.

Not fading. Not hiding behind cloud—there was no cloud; the air was clear as glass and cold as a blade. One moment the sky over Vaelmoor held its usual scatter of stars, the Shepherd's Knot low in the west, the pale smear of the Queen's Veil arcing overhead, and the next—

Black. Not dark. *Black.* A black with texture to it, a black like ink poured into water, spreading outward from a point above the Spire as if someone had pierced the night and everything behind it was draining through. The stars didn't fall. They were *taken*, each one plucked out of heaven with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard—a high thin note, a plucked string, one after another after another, faster, until the sound became a shriek and the shriek became silence and the silence was worse than either.

I gripped the windowsill hard enough to hurt. Below me in the street, a man dropped a bundle of candles and didn't stoop for them. He just stood there with his head tipped back and his mouth open, and around him others were doing the same, emerging from doorways like puppets pulled upright by their own terror, all of them staring up into a sky that had been emptied.

And then the wards broke.

I want to say I saw it, but what I saw was light—the border-wards that ringed Vaelmoor, the great woven rampart of enchantment that every child learned about in the same breath they learned the city's name, the wards we were all taught had been raised a thousand years ago to keep the fae *out*—what I saw was the whole eastern horizon bloom white, then violet, then a color I had no name for and still don't, a color like the memory of a color, seen once, half-forgotten. The light stood up off the ground. It stood up like a curtain drawn aside, and beyond it—

Beyond it there was nothing I could name, and everything I had spent three years stealing fragments of without knowing it.

The wind came first. It rolled down Ropewalk Lane ahead of everything else, and it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with winter—cold like a cellar, like deep water, like the space between heartbeats. It smelled of loam and honey and something faintly rotten underneath, sweetness going to decay, and it carried voices.

That is the part I will never manage to explain properly, not to Serah, not to anyone, not even now when I understand more of it than I wish to. The voices weren't words. They were *meanings*. They arrived in the ear and bypassed language entirely and planted themselves directly in the mind, the way a stolen memory plants itself, and I knew—I knew with the sick certainty of a man recognizing his own handwriting—that whatever was speaking through that wind was speaking in the same tongue my magic spoke. The tongue of taking. The tongue of giving-over.

They were singing. Or mourning. Or calling roll. I couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Jude." My own name, from the street, and I flinched so hard I cracked my knee on the sill. It was the chandler below, old Brammel, a man who had never once spoken to me except to complain about my stairs creaking. "Jude, are you seeing this? Are you *seeing*—" His voice broke apart. In the black sky, where the Shepherd's Knot had hung, something was moving. Something vast and slow and jointed wrong, a shadow against shadow, visible only when it crossed the place where the moon should have been. The moon was gone too. I hadn't noticed until that moment. The moon was simply not there anymore, and its absence was a wound-shaped hole in the world.

Brammel started to pray. I heard him fumble through the opening words of the Litany of the Warded City, the one they taught us in school, *the walls stand, the walls hold, the walls remember us*—and then he stopped, because everyone within a mile had felt it at the same instant:

The last ward did not break. It *turned*.

I felt it happen in my own body, in the marrow of me. The border-wards were covenant-work, Mother Quill would tell me later, though that night I had no word for it—all I knew was that the magic in the walls and the magic in my blood were made of the same stuff, cut from the same original cloth, and when the outer weave failed, the residue of it came looking for anywhere else it might live. It came looking for me.

Power surged up through me like floodwater through a culvert. I cried out—I'm not ashamed to admit it; alone in the dark, I cried out loud—and grabbed the bedpost as the room tilted. Every charm in my stock, the unsold ones wrapped in their drawer downstairs in my stall, every scrap of borrowed memory I carried in the lead-lined box under my floorboards, *lit up*. I could feel them the way you feel your teeth. They were singing back at the sky. Three years of careful thefts, a hundred small sins, and every one of them suddenly resonating like struck bells, harmonizing with the voices on the wind, answering them.

And beneath the terror—God forgive me, beneath all of it—there was exhilaration.

I have turned that over many times since, the way you turn a stone to see what lives under it. Here is what I found: I had spent my whole adult life being small on purpose. Small thefts, small charms, small rooms, a life folded down so fine it could pass through the cracks of a city that hanged mages. I had rules instead of ambitions. I had invisibility instead of a name. And in that moment, with the sky torn open and the oldest magic in the world pouring through me like I was made to hold it—like I had been *made* for this, though I could not have said by whom or why—the smallness tore. For one vertiginous heartbeat I was not a criminal in a garret. I was enormous. I was a vessel, and something vast was considering filling me.

It was the most frightening feeling of my life, and part of me loved it, and I have never entirely forgiven myself for the loving.

* * *

The voices resolved—or partly resolved—as the night went on. I stayed at the window. I don't think I could have moved if the building caught fire. Down in the lane, Brammel had stopped praying and started weeping, quietly, the way men weep when they've decided no one is left to hear them. Somewhere east, toward the river, a bell began to ring the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, and kept ringing long after whoever rang it must have understood that no one was coming.

The wind brought fragments now, actual words among the meanings, syllables that bent strangely in human air:

*...the spire is cold...*

*...who holds the flame? Who holds—*

*...bearer. Bearer. Where is the bearer...*

I pressed my back against the wall beside the window, out of the direct line of the sky, breathing like a man who has run a long way. *Bearer.* The word snagged in me like a hook. And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing that shames me most from that night: when the voices asked *where is the bearer*, some part of me—some part that lived deeper than fear, deeper than sense—leaned toward them. Answered, almost. Like a dog hearing its name called by a voice it hasn't heard since it was a puppy.

The shards. I didn't know yet what they were, wouldn't know for days, but I knew *this*: the memories I had stolen were not random. They had never been random. The hooded stranger on the bridge three years ago, pressing his yesterdays into my hands like a man handing off a burden at the top of a hill he can no longer climb—*take it, take all of it, quickly, before I change my mind*—and the sight inside that memory, the Burning Spire whole and burning, crowned in starlight, alive. I had sold pieces of that memory over the years because it was beautiful and beauty sells, and every piece I sold had gone out into the city like a seed.

The wind knew. However it knew, however the things beyond the veil had marked those seeds, they were asking after them. Asking after *me*.

Toward midnight the pressure eased. Not ended—eased, the way a predator eases when it has finished the killing and begins the eating. The black sky remained. The stars stayed gone. The moon stayed gone. But the voices thinned to a whispering at the edge of hearing, and the vast wrong-jointed shapes above the rooftops withdrew eastward, toward the river, toward the broken border, and the cold settled in to stay like an occupier.

I lit a candle. My hand shook so badly it took three tries, and when the flame caught it burned green again, and this time I watched it and did not pretend otherwise.

Below, Brammel's sobs had quieted. The alarm bell had fallen silent—no, not silent; I realized after a moment that it hadn't stopped ringing. It had been *silenced*, mid-swing, the sound cut off as cleanly as a throat. The silence that followed had edges.

I went to the floorboards and pried up the loose one and took out the lead-lined box, and opened it, and looked at what I had spent three years gathering. Charms in their wrappings. Threads of memory knotted in hair and wire. And at the bottom, wrapped twice in oilcloth because it frightened me even before tonight, the stranger's memory entire—or nearly entire; minus the pieces I'd sold, minus the piece of it that had come back to me tonight pressed into my palm by a dying emissary from a court I'd believed was legend.

Wait. No. That comes later. That comes four nights later, though it feels in memory as if it happened the same hour, time folding the way it does around catastrophes. That night the box held only what it had always held, and I held it, kneeling on my cold floor in a room with no stars outside its window, and I understood exactly three things.

First: the world I had carefully hidden from had just ended, and whatever replaced it would have no cracks small enough for me.

Second: the magic in my blood was not mine alone, was perhaps not mine at all, and had been waiting a long time for tonight.

Third: I was not safe. I had never been safe. Safety had been the thing I sold to myself every morning along with the charms, and the market for it had just collapsed.

I sat with the box in my lap until the candle burned down to a puddle, and the green flame leaned east, toward the river, toward the veil, like a plant toward sun.

At grey dawn—which was all the dawn there was now, a colorless seeping of light into a starless sky—I heard boots in the lane. Many boots. Armored. Moving in formation, which no one in the Lantern District did, ever. I lifted the corner of the curtain and looked down at witch-hunters in the Crown's black and silver, a dozen of them, moving door to door, and at their head a woman with a sword at her hip and a slate in her hand, checking names against a list.

She paused below my window. She looked up—straight up, directly at the gap in the curtain, as if she could feel me looking—and our eyes met for half a heartbeat through the fabric.

Then she knocked on Brammel's door, and asked him, in a voice pitched to carry, whether anyone on this lane was registered with the Office of Magical Containment.

I set the box down. I packed in five minutes what had taken three years to gather, and I went out the back way over the chandler's roof, into a city whose sky had been hollowed out, carrying everything I had ever stolen—and beginning, without knowing it yet, to run toward the first person who would ever matter.

Her name, I would learn soon enough, was Serah Vane.
