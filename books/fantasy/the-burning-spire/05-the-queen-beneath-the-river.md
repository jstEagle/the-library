## 5. The Queen Beneath the River

Jude Kavanagh had spent three years learning the exact weight of a normal day, and so he knew by the second hour after dawn that this one was wrong.

It wasn't anything he could have named to a magistrate. The bread from the corner oven tasted of nothing at all—flour and water and heat, but no memory in it, no ghost of the wheat field or the miller's hands. The water from his pump ran clear and cold and utterly silent, as if it had forgotten how to sing against the iron. Even his own footsteps on the stairs sounded foreign, like someone else's boots borrowed for the morning.

The city had lost its echoes.

He stood in his doorway with the shop's sign still turned to CLOSED and watched the street where he had lived invisible for three years. Ash drifted down in slow spirals, grey on grey, settling on the awnings and the gutters and the shoulders of people who walked without speaking. Nobody swept it. Nobody spoke about it. In Lowmarket, you learned early that the fastest way to die was to be the first one to say aloud what everyone was already thinking.

*The stars are gone.*

He thought it anyway, because he was alone, and because thinking it in the privacy of his own skull felt like the last small theft available to him—a crime with no victim but himself.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them, these clever hands, these hands that had lifted grief from widows and first loves from soldiers and made them into little glass charms that sold for enough to eat, and he thought: *you did this.* Not with intent, never with intent—but the wards had flickered at dusk two nights ago, and he had felt his own power surge through him like a river suddenly unbanked, and he had ignored it, because ignoring things was the whole architecture of his survival.

He closed the door. He turned the lock. He sat down at his workbench among the charms—the little stoppered vials of colored glass, each holding an hour or an afternoon of somebody else's life—and he told himself that whatever was happening to the sky was too large to be his fault, and almost believed it.

That was when he heard the singing.

***

It came up through the floor.

Not through the street door—through the *floor*, through the old stone beneath the flagstones, through whatever lay under Lowmarket that the city had been built on top of and preferred not to discuss. It was a woman's voice, or something shaped like a woman's voice, singing a melody Jude had never heard and yet knew completely, the way you know your own name, the way you know the shape of your own mouth. Each note landed somewhere behind his sternum and rang there.

He knew the song. He had never heard it. Both things were true, and the space between them hurt.

"Go away," he said to the floor, because talking to floors was less mad than the alternative. "I don't want any part of whatever this is."

The singing stopped.

In its place: footsteps. Wet ones. Coming up his stairs.

He was across the room before he'd decided to move, back to the wall, hand finding the poker by the cold hearth. Three years of rules, and rule one was *never be seen*, and rule two was *never let them come to you*, and both rules were currently being broken by something climbing toward his door leaving prints on the boards—prints that glistened, that steamed faintly, that smelled of river-mud and crushed watermint and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church.

The knock, when it came, was gentle. Almost apologetic.

"I know you're awake," said a voice like wind through reeds. "The river told me which window. They always tell me which window."

Jude said nothing. His heart was going like a trapped bird.

"The door," the voice said, patient as tide. "Or I can come through the wall, but the wall will remember it, and walls hold grudges."

He opened the door because the alternative was worse.

She was standing in the frame of it, and she was drowning. That was the only word. She stood upright in dry air and she was drowning—her hair moved as though underwater, dark green-black strands drifting around a face that might have been beautiful once and was now mostly gone, features eroding like a clay bank in spring flood. Her skin was the grey-white of a fish's belly. Her eyes were the color of deep water over black stones, and they were fixed on him with an intensity that had nothing human in it at all.

Water dripped from her constantly, though there was nothing above her to drip from. Where each drop struck the floorboards, tiny white flowers opened, bloomed, and died in the space of a breath.

"You're the thief," she said. Not accusing. Delighted, almost. "You're the little thief who steals what people can't carry."

"I'm a charm-seller," Jude said. "Registered nowhere. Wanted by no one. You've got the wrong—"

"The river doesn't make mistakes." She swayed, caught herself on the doorframe, and her fingers left wet prints that glowed faintly, then faded. "The river remembers everything, little thief. That is its whole nature. And the river has been screaming since the sky went out." She smiled, and her teeth were small and sharp and too many. "It sent me to find the one man in this city whose pockets are full of stolen light."

"I don't have—" He stopped. Because he did have something. Last night, after the sky went black, he had dreamed—or half-dreamed—of a shard of light pressing itself into his palm, and woken with his right hand clenched so hard the nails had left crescents in the skin, and when he opened it there had been nothing there at all except a warmth that hadn't left yet.

Her eyes went to his right hand. Of course they did.

"May I come in?" she asked. "I would prefer to die sitting down. Dying standing is so undignified, and I have been dignified for four hundred years."

***

He let her in because her legs were giving out, and because some animal instinct older than his rules understood that she was not a threat—she was a *message*, and messages deserve to be delivered.

She sat in his only chair like a queen granting it to herself, spine straight despite the ruin of her, hands folded in her lap. Water pooled beneath her and the flowers kept opening and dying in it, a small silent garden of moments. Up close he could see that her erosion was worse than he'd thought: her left ear was simply absent now, smooth skin where it should have been, and the edge of her jaw was softening, blurring, like ink in rain.

"What are you?" he asked.

"Emissary," she said. "Of Queen Maelis, of the Court of Embers, fourth of her name, may the last ember keep her." Something moved through her face—grief, maybe, or its fae equivalent, which might be the same thing worn differently. "I crossed the border when it broke. Do you understand what that means, little thief? For four hundred years the veil has been sealed. Sealed by covenant. Sealed by *song*. And two nights ago the song stopped, and every door between the worlds blew open at once, and everything on either side came pouring through."

"Including you."

"Including me." She laughed, and it cost her; her outline flickered like a candle in draft. "Including the things that hunt. Including the hunger. The river carried me as far as it could and then the river forgot me—it is forgetting everything now, that is what the breaking *is*, a great forgetting—and so I walked. Crawled. Asked directions of dogs." Another smile, weaker. "Dogs are very honest creatures. They told me where the light-thief lived."

Jude crouched down slowly, keeping the poker near his knee. "Why me? There are mages in this city. Registered ones, real ones, the kind the Crown keeps in towers. Why does a fae emissary crawl through ash to a back-alley charm-seller?"

"Because the registered mages are empty," she said simply. "Licensed. Leashed. Their magic runs in channels cut for it, shallow and safe. But you—" her deep-water eyes found his and held them, and he felt something turn over in his chest, some long-buried thing responding to her voice like a bell rung underwater—"*you* steal memories. Do you know what a memory is, Jude Kavanagh?"

"A thing people miss."

"A memory," she said, "is a *piece of the song*. Every mind that holds one is a verse of the covenant. The covenant was not written on stone, fool child. It was written in *minds*. Scattered, hidden, sung into thousands of mortal heads so that no fire could burn it and no king could seize it. And most verses are just verses. But some minds—" she leaned forward, and the flowers in the water at her feet bloomed faster, desperately— "*some minds caught more than others. Some minds, touched by the old magic in the old days, held fragments of the covenant itself. Shards of the original binding. And three years ago, a hooded stranger walked into a charm-shop in Lowmarket and begged a young thief to take a memory he could no longer afford to carry.*"

The room went very quiet. The ash fell past the window. Somewhere far off, a bell began tolling—not the hour, something arrhythmic and frightened, the sound of a city losing its mind.

"That memory," the emissary whispered, "was not a memory. It was a shard. And you have been carrying it in your vaults for three years, Covenant-Bearer, without knowing you were carrying the last hope of two worlds in a stoppered vial next to a soldier's first kiss."

"Don't call me that."

"It is what you are."

"I'm a *criminal*. I steal small things from sad people and sell them back to them later. I have rules. Rule one is never be seen. Rule two is never take much. There is no rule that says save the world, because rule zero is *stay small enough that nobody ever looks at you*—"

"And yet," she said softly, "the sky went out, and the river screamed, and of all the minds in all the city, mine crawled to yours. Do you believe in coincidence, Jude Kavanagh?"

"No," he said, before he could stop himself. "Coincidence is a story people tell when they haven't done the research."

"Good." She nodded, satisfied, and then the nod became a shudder, and the shudder became a collapse of her edges, her form thinning like smoke. "Then research *this*. The covenant is breaking. When it breaks fully, the veil dies, and the Otherworld pours into yours—the hungry parts, the exiled parts, the parts your Chancellor has already begun bargaining with—and your world dims and ours drowns and there is no more boundary anywhere, no more *inside*, forever. Unless the shards are gathered. Unless the spire is refed. Unless the Covenant-Bearer—"

"I said don't call me that."

"—does what bearers do." She was fading faster now, and she seemed to be spending what remained of herself deliberately, like a miser finally opening his coffers in a burning house. "Six shards. Six minds. Some of them you robbed yourself, little thief. Your crimes are a map. Follow your own sins home and gather what you scattered." Her hand rose—grey, translucent at the fingertips now—and beckoned. "Come here. Give me your hand. The river gave me something to give you, and I am nearly out of time to give it."

Every rule he had said *no*. Stay back. Stay small. Never touch the magic when you don't know its shape.

He gave her his hand.

Her fingers closed around his right palm—cold as riverbed stones—and pressed something into it. Pressed *light* into it. He felt it sink through the skin, through muscle, through whatever part of a man holds the things he refuses to think about, and settle there, warm and specific and *familiar*, the way a key feels familiar to the lock it was cut for.

"What is it?" he breathed.

"Yours," she said. "Returned. One of the things you stole—you'll know it when you look at it. The river caught it falling from the sky the night the stranger died, and kept it safe, because rivers keep things." Her eyes were dimming now, the deep-water color draining out of them, leaving pale shallows. "Listen. Listen, because I am almost gone and the words must land. The Queen is dying. The Court is fracturing. There are those in the Otherworld who want war—want to pour through and drown your cities and salt your fields and end the forgetting by ending the forgetters. The Queen wants the covenant remade instead. She bet everything on a bearer. On *you*. Find the shards. Refeed the flame. And trust—" her voice frayed to a thread— "trust the hunter. The law's woman. She will hate you, and then she won't. Trust her anyway. Promise the river you will."

"Why the hunter? Why would I possibly—"

"Because the covenant was not broken by accident," the emissary said, and the last of her spent itself in the saying, her body coming apart into droplets, into mist, into a hundred small white flowers that bloomed and died on his floorboards. "Someone *unbound* it. Someone inside your walls. Follow the—"

The rest was gone. Just water on the boards, and flowers closing like eyes, and the smell of watermint slowly fading into the ordinary smell of his shop, of dust and glass and cold hearth-smoke.

Jude knelt alone in his room amid the dying blossoms, his right hand burning like a coal, and understood that his life as he had built it—small, careful, unseen—had just ended on his own floor.

***

He didn't sleep. Sleep would have meant dreams, and dreams would have meant the song, and he wasn't ready for the song again.

Instead, near midnight, he lit one candle, sat at his workbench, and opened the cabinet where he kept the oldest charms—the ones he'd never sold, couldn't sell, the ones that had felt wrong in his pocket from the day he took them. His fingers went straight to the third shelf, to a plain wooden case he hadn't opened in three years, and his hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all day, because this at least was craft, this was the work he knew.

Inside lay a single vial of glass the color of deep twilight, and inside the vial, coiled like a sleeping ember, a thread of light that had not been there yesterday.

No—that wasn't true. It had always been there. He had just never known how to see it. Three years the charm had sat in the dark, and the shard inside it had waited with the patience of a thing that had chosen its hiding place.

The hooded stranger. Three years ago. The man who had wept while Jude worked, who had gripped the chair arms and said *take all of it, take the spire, take the singing, I can't carry it anymore, hide it somewhere no one would ever think to look*—and Jude, twenty-two and arrogant and hungry for the strange fat purse the man offered, had taken it all without asking a single question he should have asked.

He uncorked the vial. He didn't drink it—he wasn't sure drinking was even the mechanism—but he held it to the candlelight, and the shard within rose to meet the flame, and for one moment the room filled with a vision:

A tower. A tower of black stone wreathed in white fire, burning from crown to foundation and unconsumed, and around it a wheel of stars so dense and bright it was less a sky than a *chorus*, and the fire sang, and the stars answered, and the whole of it was a single sustained note of *keeping*—

The vision snapped shut. The candle guttered. Jude sat in the dark with his heart slamming and the taste of starlight in his mouth.

The Burning Spire. Whole. Alight. Before.

And pressed into his flesh, warming against the shard in the vial like a hand finding its twin, the fragment the river had returned to him pulsed once, twice, and settled into a rhythm that was almost—almost—a heartbeat.

Two shards' worth of resonance, calling to each other across his own body. And somewhere out in the ash-falling dark, five more minds—five more sins—waiting to be visited.

He thought of the widow Marta, whose grief he had lifted a year ago, whose charm still sat unsold in his cabinet because taking it had felt like theft even by his standards. He thought of the soldier, the chandler's boy, the others. A map of his own crimes, the emissary had said. Follow your sins home.

Outside, the arrhythmic bell was still tolling. Below it, faintly, from the direction of the Lantern District, came another sound: boots. Many boots. Moving in formation. And threaded through the drumming of them, high and cold, a whistle-call he recognized from three years of careful listening—the hunters' signal for *mage sighted*.

They weren't looking for him yet. They were looking for *a* mage, any mage, the way a drowning man grabs at the water.

But they would find the name soon. Serah Vane was thorough; he'd seen her work the markets, seen the way witnesses remembered her without knowing why. And when she found him—

*Trust the hunter,* the dead woman had said. *She will hate you, and then she won't.*

"Madness," Jude said aloud to the empty room, to the flowers on his floor, to the shard burning in his palm. "Absolute madness."

But he was already moving—pulling his coat on, filling his satchel with charms, snuffing the candle—because rule zero had just changed. Stay small had failed. The sky had noticed him, the river had noticed him, the dead had noticed him, and the only direction left was through.

At the door he paused, looked back once at the small white blossoms already browning on his floorboards, and bowed to them—awkwardly, sincerely, the first courtesy he had paid anything larger than himself in three years.

Then Jude Kavanagh stepped out into the starless night, a thief carrying the last light of two worlds, and heard, distantly, the hunters' whistles closing on the Lantern District like a hand closing over a coal.
