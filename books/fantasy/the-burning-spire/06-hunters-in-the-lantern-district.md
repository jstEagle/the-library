## 6. Hunters in the Lantern District

### Serah

The file on Jude Kavanagh was thinner than a hymnal and twice as full of lies.

Serah Vane read it for the fourth time as the carriage rattled toward the Lantern District, lamplight strobing gold across the page through the slats of the shutter. *Registered mage, minor classification. Memory-charms, non-invasive. No prior offenses.* The word *non-invasive* had been written by a clerk who had never felt someone reach into his skull with fingers made of other people's summers.

She closed the folder. Outside, the city was wrong in a way she couldn't yet name.

The stars were still gone — three nights now, a sky like a lid screwed down over the world — but it was smaller things that unsettled her. The way dogs wouldn't look up anymore. The way the river smelled faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room. The way her own ward-sigils, inked into her skin at the Academy, itched at dusk as though they were letters being rewritten.

"You'll want the back streets," said Captain Dree from the opposite bench. He was a broad, grey man who had hunted mages since before Serah could hold a blade, and he spoke of the Lantern District the way sailors spoke of reefs. "Kavanagh's been selling there for years. They'll hide him."

"They won't have to," Serah said. "He'll run. They always run."

"And when he does?"

"Then I follow." She turned the silver hunter's badge on its chain, watching light slide across it. "A registered mage using magic after the Collapse is a traitor under the Edict. That's not complicated."

Dree grunted. "Nothing about the Collapse is simple, Vane. You saw the residue at the border station. Tell me what you concluded."

She had seen it. She had knelt in the ash of the shattered wards with her instruments out and her hands steady, and she had found traces of a working so old that the Academy's own texts called it legend. Covenant-magic. The binding of mortal realm to Otherworld, sealed away a thousand years ago, taught now only as a cautionary fable about why all enchantment was outlawed. Except it wasn't a fable. It had soaked into the broken stones like old blood into floorboards, and it had answered to no working she knew — and beneath it, threaded through it like a single dark hair in pale wool, she had found something else. Something small. Recent. Human.

Memory-magic. The crude, despised craft of charm-sellers and gutter thieves.

"It's nothing conclusive," she said.

"It's everything conclusive," Dree said. "The Chancellor thinks so too. Why else send his best hound after a charm-peddler instead of an army after the sky?"

The carriage stopped. Through the shutter-slats Serah saw the Lantern District unfold itself: narrow lanes strung with paper lamps, ten thousand of them, glowing amber against the starless dark because the people here refused to let the missing stars take their light too. Market stalls crowded the gutters. Somewhere a fiddle played, defiantly off-key. It smelled of frying onions, tallow smoke, wet stone — and under all of it, faint as a heartbeat, of magic. Old magic, worn smooth by daily use, the way a stair hollows under feet.

Serah stepped down and felt every ward-sigil on her body wake at once.

"He's close," she said quietly.

Dree signaled the squad — six hunters in grey, faces masked, iron chains coiled at their belts. Iron didn't stop memory-magic; nothing did, reliably. But it slowed a thief down, and slowing a thief down was half the work.

They moved into the crowd.

### Jude

Jude Kavanagh knew the Lantern District the way a blind man knows his own hands: by touch, by sound, by the particular warmth of each turning.

He knew that the third lamp past the pie-woman's stall flickered blue when the wind came off the river, and that the alley behind the cooper's shop smelled of vinegar, and that if you pressed your palm flat against the wall of the dye-house you could feel the looms through the brick, a great slow animal breathing. He had spent three years learning this district with his eyes half-closed, because a thief who navigates by sight is a thief who can be followed, and a thief who navigates by memory is a ghost.

Tonight the district was holding its breath. The lamps burned, the fiddle scraped, the pie-woman shouted her prices — but underneath, everyone was looking up at a sky with nothing in it, and everyone was pretending they weren't.

And underneath *that*, Jude felt the hunters.

It started as a pressure behind his eyes, the same sensation he got when someone nearby remembered him fondly. He'd learned to read the city's feelings through the residue of what he'd taken — a thousand small charms sold over three years, each one carrying a thread back to the mind it came from. Grief here. A first kiss there. And tonight, cutting through the market noise like a blade through silk: purpose. Discipline. The cold clean taste of a mind trained to hunt.

*Witch-hunters,* he thought, and did not break stride, and bought a meat pie he had no intention of eating.

The trick, when hunters came, was never to run. Running was a shape the eye could catch. Instead he became part of the evening: a young man strolling with his supper, pausing to listen to the fiddler, laughing at something a friend said — except there was no friend, so he laughed alone, which drew the eye worse, so he stopped laughing and simply walked, unhurried, letting the crowd carry him the way a river carries a leaf.

Behind him, six grey shapes entered the square.

He didn't turn to look. He counted them by the way the crowd's attention bent around them, the way water bends around stones. Six. Moving in a spread pattern, closing on the pie-stall, on the fiddler, on the lane that led — inevitably, because hunters always guessed the obvious route — toward his shop.

His shop. Where the shard of light sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, humming to itself like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Jude took a bite of the pie. It was good. He was almost annoyed about that.

Then the crowd parted ahead of him, politely, the way crowds do for danger, and a woman stepped through.

She wore hunter's grey but no mask, and her hair was dark red, cropped short, and she walked like someone who had never once needed to hurry because everything eventually came to her. Her badge caught the lamplight. Her eyes — grey-green, unimpressed, terribly awake — found him across twenty feet of market crowd as though the crowd were glass.

"There you are," Serah Vane said.

* * *

### Serah

Later, she would try to explain to herself how she'd known. Not the description — the description was easy, a thin-faced young man with careful hands and a seller's smile he hadn't put on yet. It was something else. In a district saturated with stolen feeling, he was the one person whose surface was perfectly, deliberately blank, and a blankness that perfect is its own confession.

"Master Kavanagh," she said, and watched him weigh his options in the space of a blink. "I'd like a word about your inventory."

"Madam, I sell pies." He raised the evidence.

"The pie-woman sells pies. You buy them, because a man followed through a market should have a reason to stand still." She moved left; he drifted right, matching her without seeming to. Around them the crowd had gone quiet in that particular way of districts that know their magicians and love them and will not help the Crown, not even by standing in the right place. "You're registered. Minor classification. Which means you already know what the Edict says about practicing after the Collapse."

"I've practiced nothing," Jude said. "I've been having supper. It's been a strange week for everyone."

"Three nights ago the wards fell and the stars went out," Serah said. "I found covenant-residue at the border station with human memory-work woven through it. Yours, I think. So we can do this politely or we can do it in chains, but either way, you're coming with—"

He threw the pie at her face.

It was such a petty, human, *thief's* move that she lost half a second to sheer indignation, and half a second was a country mile. By the time she'd wiped custard from her eyes he was gone — not down the obvious lane, not into the alley, but *up*, onto the awning of the dyer's stall, a shadow crossing the paper lamps like a hand passing over candles.

"After him!" Dree bellowed somewhere behind her. "Vane, west side!"

Serah ran.

* * *

### Jude

He ran the district blind.

That was the phrase Mother Quill used for it, back when he'd asked her how anyone survived a hunt: *you don't run where you can see, boy, you run where you know.* So Jude went over the dye-house roof on legs that trusted the tiles without asking them, dropped through the gap behind the chimney he'd measured two winters ago, rolled, came up running in the cooper's yard, vaulted the vinegar barrels whose positions lived in his knees rather than his eyes.

Behind him: boots. Two sets, then four. The hunters were good. They'd split at the square exactly as training demanded, and they moved through his labyrinth with lanterns and certainty, and certainty was the one thing his labyrinth was built to punish.

He cut through the tannery — the smell alone would slow any pursuer raised above the gutter — crossed the drying-yard between hanging hides like a man wading through ghosts, and slipped out the far gate into the lane of lamp-makers, where the whole street glowed and every shadow was therefore deeper than night.

Where a woman stood waiting, sword drawn, breathing only slightly hard.

"You went *left*," Serah Vane said, almost accusingly. "Every report says you go right along the lamp-lane."

"I pay people to write reports about myself," Jude said. "It's cheaper than a guard dog."

She came at him then, and he learned in the first three seconds that she had not been sent to arrest him gently. Her blade was flat-edged — capture, not kill — but she used it like a whip, driving him back along the lamp-maker's wall, her footwork eating the ground, her eyes doing the real work: reading his weight, predicting his feints, cataloguing him. He ducked a cut meant for his shoulder, caught a second strike on a discarded lamp-frame, felt the frame shear apart in his hands.

"You're fast," she said. Grudging. Interested.

"You're better," he admitted. "This is very flattering, but—"

"—you're cornered." She smiled, and it transformed her face into something fierce and bright, and Jude thought, with the detached horror of a man noticing the water rising: *oh no.*

Because here was the thing about Jude Kavanagh's rules. *Never take much. Never be seen.* Both rules existed to protect him, but the second rule existed to protect *them* — because taking a memory was not like lifting a purse. It left a hole. And holes in people were debts, and debts were the one currency he'd sworn off spending.

But she had him cornered, and behind her the boots were coming, and the shard under his floorboard was humming a note only he could hear, and some cold practical part of him that had kept him alive for three years said: *an hour. Just an hour. The last hour. She'll reconstruct it from notes; hunters keep notes; she'll hate you and live.*

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, which was the worst part.

"For what?" she said — and reached into her coat, not for iron, but for the whistle that would bring the whole squad down on this lane—

He caught her wrist. Their eyes met, close enough that he could see the lamplight doubled in hers, and he *took*.

It was never violent. People imagined violence, teeth and claws, but theft of memory was more like lifting a knot loose — finding the thread of the last hour wound tight through her, and drawing it out smooth and whole. The market square. The pie to the face (he felt her outrage at it, vivid and absurd). The chase over the roofs, her grudging respect for his speed. The moment she'd smiled at him and thought *oh no* in a register she would never have admitted to.

He took all of it, folded it small, and held it — and into the empty space where it had been, because he could not help himself, because he was weak in precisely one way, he left something kind. A small warm thing. The feeling of a fire in winter. Nothing traceable. Just so the hour wouldn't ache.

Serah Vane swayed. Her sword arm sagged. Her eyes went distant, then confused, then narrowed with a fury that had lost its object.

"What—" she began. "What just—"

"An hour," Jude said, backing away. "You'll find your notes. You're thorough; it's one of the first things I noticed about you. Write down what's missing and you'll get most of it back on paper, if not in the flesh."

"You *thief*—" She lunged; he was already moving, backward, unhurried now, because the hunters' boots had stopped at the mouth of the lane, confounded by the lamp-light and the tannery stink and the absence of any clear trail.

"Registered minor classification," he called softly. "Non-invasive. Read your own file, hunter."

And then he was gone, down the lamp-maker's stairs into the under-ways, where the district kept its oldest secrets, and the shard beneath his floorboards waited with its patient, terrible light.

* * *

### Serah

She stood in the lane of lamp-makers for a long time after he was gone, holding her sword and holding nothing else.

Her notes, when she dug them out with shaking hands, told her the shape of the hour: *subject located, subject fled via dye-house roof, pursuit west, contact at lamp-lane.* The words described events her mind insisted had happened to someone else. There was a gap where the fight should be, smooth-edged, painless — and in the gap, inexplicably, warmth. The sensation of a fire in winter. Of being, briefly, entirely safe.

Captain Dree found her there. "Lost him?"

"Yes," Serah said.

"He took something from you."

She should have lied. Training said lie; the Academy said lie; the badge on her chain said lie. She looked at the paper lamps burning against the starless sky, and at the empty lane, and heard her own voice say: "An hour."

Dree was silent a moment. "Does it hurt?"

"No," she said, and that was the truth, and somehow that was the most frightening thing of all. "That's what worries me. He was *gentle*, Captain. Who teaches a thief to be gentle?"

She looked down at her notes again, at her own handwriting describing a man she could no longer remember fighting — and found, beneath the official record, in the margin, a line she had no memory of writing. Small, quick, written in the field:

*Eyes green. Apologized. Meant it.*

Serah Vane stared at those five words for a long time, in a city with no stars, and understood that she had just begun hunting a man she was already, impossibly, reluctant to catch.

Somewhere beneath the Lantern District, Jude Kavanagh lifted the floorboard and unwrapped the shard, and the light that spilled over his hands showed him — for one impossible instant — a spire of white flame standing whole against a sky full of stars.

He had seen that spire before. Three years ago. In a memory he had stolen from a stranger who had begged him to take it.

And the stranger had been wearing the Chancellor's colors.
