## 8. The Chancellor's Edict

The candles in the Chancellor's study had been lit at dusk, and by midnight Aldric Marrow had not once looked at them.

He did not need light to read. He needed it for the men who came before him, so that they could see his face clearly and understand that nothing about him wavered—not his hands, not his voice, not the patient arithmetic of his gaze. Fear, Marrow had learned across thirty years of service to the Crown, was a currency that devalued quickly when spent in anger. Spent in calm, it appreciated forever.

He sat behind a desk of black oak older than the kingdom itself, and upon that desk lay three things: a report from the Wardens of the Border, sealed with wax still warm; a map of the capital, its lower quarters inked in a grey so pale it seemed the cartographer had wished them away; and a single sheet of paper, blank except for the letterhead, waiting for the edict he had composed in his head four days before the stars went out.

That was the detail no one would ever think to ask about. The edict had been written—every clause, every provision, every elegant cruelty—before the sky broke. Marrow had simply been waiting for the world to provide him with its justification. The world had obliged. It usually did.

* * *

The report lay open beneath his fingers. He read it again, though he knew it by heart, because reading it gave him pleasure, and Marrow permitted himself few pleasures.

*At approximately the eleventh hour of the night past, the border wards sustained catastrophic failure along their entire circumference. The failure was not gradual. It was not decay. Preliminary divination suggests deliberate unbinding—the wards were not broken but released, as one releases a held breath.*

Released. Yes. He had paid a great deal of gold to a great deal of silence to ensure that word *released* never traveled beyond this room. To the Council he would present sabotage. To the people, treachery. To the witch-hunters, a hunt. Only here, alone with his candles he refused to look at, did the truth sit on the desk beside him like a well-fed cat: he had brokered the collapse himself, through intermediaries who were now either dead, exiled beyond the veil, or both, and the stars had gone out because he had asked them to.

Not asked. *Traded.* There was a difference, and the difference was everything. A man who asked for power admitted he lacked it. A man who traded for it understood its price, and understanding a price was the first qualification of owning what was purchased.

Marrow rose and went to the window. Below, the capital lay under a sky like a lid. No stars. No moon worth the name—only a bruised disc, dimmed to the color of an old coin, hanging where something vast had withdrawn its attention. The city had not yet learned how to be afraid in the dark. By morning, he intended to teach it.

He could feel it even now, standing at the glass: a low hum beneath the stones, fainter than hearing, more like pressure than sound. The old covenant, unraveling thread by thread, and with every frayed strand something leaking through into the world—something that made dogs howl in districts he'd never visited and made sensible men wake at night certain they'd heard their names spoken in a voice like wind through reeds.

Most men would have called it terror. Marrow called it inventory. Power was loose in the world again, raw and ownerless, and there was exactly one man in the capital positioned to gather it before anyone else understood what had happened.

* * *

They came at dawn: the Lord Marshal, the Master of Wards, the Provost of the Hunter Corps, and the Archbishop's legate, a thin man whose piety smelled of incense and ambition in equal measure. Marrow received them standing, which was unusual, and offered no chairs, which was not.

"The situation," he began, without preamble, "is simpler than any of you wish it to be."

He let the silence do its work. Around the long table, five powerful men exchanged the small glances of subordinates discovering they are all equally afraid.

"The border wards have fallen. The stars have been extinguished. These are not natural events. Natural events have causes we can study; these have authors." He turned to the legate. "Your Grace will recall the old heresies. The covenant between mortal flesh and the fae courts—the bargain our ancestors struck when they were too weak to stand alone. A bargain," and here his voice cooled by a degree, "that required worship. That required *tribute*. That required the surrender of sovereignty to beings who regard us the way we regard livestock."

No one spoke. The legate's throat moved.

"For two hundred years the Crown has taught that the covenant was myth. That enchantment was superstition dressed in criminality. We burned the last registered mage forty years ago and told ourselves the age of magic ended with her." Marrow placed one hand flat upon the map. "We were wrong. The magic did not end. It waited. And four nights ago, someone opened the door we nailed shut."

"Chancellor," the Lord Marshal said carefully, "the diviners cannot identify—"

"The diviners cannot identify the author because the author is not human." He said it flatly, letting the horror arrive on its own schedule. "The residue in the ward-stones is covenant-work. Old work. Older than the kingdom. Which means the fae have moved against us, gentlemen, or—and I confess I find this likelier—someone within our walls has been courting them."

The word *courting* landed among them like a dropped knife. Marrow watched each man privately recalculate his own innocence, his own exposure, his own need for the Chancellor's protection. Good. Fear distributed evenly was fear he could govern.

"Therefore." He drew the prepared edict from beneath the map, and the sight of it—already written, already sealed, already *waiting*—produced in the room a small, collective intake of breath. Men noticed such things. Men would remember later that the Chancellor had been ready, and readiness in crisis reads as either genius or guilt, and Marrow had spent decades ensuring that in his case it could only ever read as genius.

"First: martial law, effective within the hour. Curfew from dusk bell to dawn bell. Assembly forbidden. The gates of the lower quarters—" his finger traced the pale grey districts on the map, the tanneries, the lantern-works, the river slums where the city kept everyone it preferred not to see, "—will be sealed and garrisoned."

"Second: the Hunter Corps is enlarged and emancipated from civilian courts. Provost Vane's hunters will answer to this office directly. They will conduct systematic searches of the lower quarters for enchantment, grimoires, charms, talismans, and persons touched by the fae."

"Third." Here Marrow paused, and allowed himself the luxury of watching their faces as he arrived at the heart of it. "Every resident of the lower quarters will submit to memory-purge. Total ablation. Performed by Crown purgers under hunter escort."

The Master of Wards found his voice first. "Total? Chancellor, the purge rites as practiced—what little literature survives—are surgical. Selective. Total ablation has not been attempted since—"

"Since it was unnecessary." Marrow folded his hands. "You are thinking of it as punishment. Think of it instead as quarantine. The covenant lives in memory, gentlemen—that much the old texts agree upon. It hides in remembered songs, in grandmother's warnings, in the shape of a lullaby. Every mind in those districts is potentially a door. We are not punishing the poor. We are *closing doors*."

"And if some doors resist?" the legate asked softly.

Marrow smiled. It was, by all accounts, a reassuring smile; he had practiced it against mirrors until it achieved a kind of warmth that never reached further than the cheekbones.

"Then we close them harder. Doors break, Your Grace. People survive broken doors all the time." He swept the edict off the table. "Proclamation at noon. See that the criers learn it properly. And someone find me the registry of licensed mages—I want eyes on every name in that book before sundown."

* * *

The proclamation was read from the steps of the Hall of Justice at noon, and Marrow attended in person, because a ruler who sent others to announce his will was a ruler apologizing for it.

The square was full. Word of the starless nights had emptied the churches and filled the streets, and now the crowd stood shoulder to shoulder beneath a sky the color of unwashed wool, listening to the criers in their Crown-blue coats.

*"By order of the Lord Chancellor, in this hour of treachery and darkness..."*

Marrow stood at the balustrade and watched faces rather than listening to words he had written himself. He watched a fishwife clutch her market basket as though the basket might be confiscated next. He watched a young clerk mouth the phrase *fae sympathizers* with the particular relish of a man who has just been handed permission to hate his neighbor lawfully. He watched a woman near the fountain go very still at the words *memory-purge*, her hand drifting to her temple as though she could feel the purger's thumb already pressing there.

*"...all persons harboring enchantment, consorting with the Otherworld, or bearing marks of fae-touched blood shall be remanded to the Crown's justice. Let no citizen shelter the traitor among us. The darkness came from within our walls. The Crown will burn it out."*

Burn it out. He had chosen the verb deliberately. Fire was honest; fire showed the people exactly what order cost, and let them thank him for sparing them the worse alternative—which was, of course, nothing at all. There was no worse alternative. There was only Marrow, ascending.

Because that was the truth beneath the edict, the truth he would carve into the world if it took him twenty years: the covenant had never protected mankind. It had *farmed* mankind—sown human memory and harvested human wonder, generation after generation, while mortals knelt to powers that fed on their devotion. The fae had called it balance. Marrow called it theft, and he was the better thief.

Let the old powers come back starving. Let them find their anchors ripped out, their stars sold, their covenant in the pocket of a man who understood that divinity was not granted. Divinity was *taken*, consolidated, and administered. He did not intend to worship what came through the veil. He intended to become the thing on the other side of it that was worshipped.

A child in the crowd—a girl, six or seven, with a smudge of soot on her cheek—caught his eye. She was staring up at him with the frank, unafraid curiosity of the very young, and for a moment Marrow felt observed in a way that pricked.

He dismissed the feeling. Children forgot. Soon enough, whole districts would forget, professionally, systematically, and the forgetting would be called healing.

* * *

By evening the first purge-wagons rolled into the Lantern District, and Marrow watched from his study window as the lantern-lighters' quarter—streets he had walked exactly once, years ago, to confirm his contempt—began to glow with the wrong kind of light.

Bonfires. His orders specified that confiscated charms and "fae materials" be destroyed publicly, and his officers, eager men, had interpreted eagerness as license. Figures moved around the flames carrying armfuls of whatever the searches had shaken loose: bundles of dried herbs, painted stones, ribbons knotted in patterns the knot-makers' grandmothers had died teaching them. A puppet of woven straw went into the fire and the crowd around it made a sound Marrow chose to interpret as approval.

It was not entirely approval. He noted that. Somewhere in that crowd, grief was being filed away for future use, and Marrow had governed long enough to know that filed grief compounded like debt. But debt could be collected on by whoever held the ledger, and by spring he would hold every ledger in the city.

On his desk, the registry of licensed mages lay open. Forty-one names. Thirty-eight dead, fled, or untraceable. Three remained: a hedge-healer in the Tanners' Row who would be in custody by tomorrow's dusk; an old woman listed as a "weather-speaker" whose address had burned down twice, suspiciously; and the third name, the one that had made him pause when he first read it that afternoon.

*Kavanagh, Jude. Licensed charm-seller. District: Lantern Quarter. Status: active. Notes: minor talent, memory-work only. Assessed threat: negligible.*

Negligible. Marrow read the word again and felt the faintest itch, the way a gambler feels a card that has been in the deck too long. Negligible men did not survive forty years of purges by being negligible. Negligible men survived by being *invisible*, and invisibility, in Marrow's long experience, was the most expensive commodity in any city—more expensive than gold, more expensive than loyalty, more expensive than the truth.

And there was one other thing. In the diviners' residue report, buried in a footnote he had ordered removed from all copies but his own: traces of covenant-signature detected in residual charm-craft, Lantern Quarter, provenance unknown. Provenance unknown meant provenance *concealed*. Concealment required skill. Skill required motive.

He dipped his pen.

*Provost,* he wrote, in a hand so neat it might have been printed, *the bearer Kavanagh is to be taken alive and intact. His mind especially. Deliver it to me personally, unopened.*

He hesitated over the last two words, then left them. Unopened. Let the provost wonder what a Chancellor wanted with an unopened mind. Wonder was a leash; he had used it on better men than her.

Marrow signed the order, blotted it, and set it aside. Then he stood at the window a final time, looking out over a city going dark street by street as the curfew bells rang, and permitted himself—for precisely the length of one breath—the sensation of the future arriving.

Below, in the Lantern District, a bonfire collapsed in a shower of sparks, and the sparks rose higher than any fire should throw them, climbing toward a sky with no stars to receive them.

Marrow watched them fall back down, and smiled, and closed the shutters.
