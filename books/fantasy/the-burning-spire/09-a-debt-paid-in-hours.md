## 9. A Debt Paid in Hours

The hour was gone.

Serah Vane discovered it the way one discovers a missing tooth—by the absence, by the strange smoothness where something used to be. She was sitting at her desk in the Hunter's Hall, third floor, the window behind her throwing bars of grey morning light across her paperwork, when she reached for the memory of the Lantern District and found nothing there.

Not fog. Not blur. A clean excision.

She had been trained to notice such things. Every hunter was. The Crown's curriculum on memory-magic was thin—officially, because officially there was no memory-magic left to study—but what remained of it in the old texts was unanimous on one point: a stolen memory did not leave a wound. It left a seam. The mind healed over the gap like water closing over a stone, and only a disciplined mind, a suspicious mind, could feel the wrongness of the surface.

Serah's mind had been built for suspicion the way a ship's hull is built for water.

She sat very still, hands flat on the desk, and let herself feel the shape of the missing thing. It was recent—last night, or the small hours of this morning. She could feel its edges the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth: a hollow with a particular contour, an hour-shaped absence somewhere between midnight and dawn. Around it, other memories stood intact like houses after a fire has taken only one.

She remembered the order. Captain Dree's voice through the speaking-tube, gravel and lamp-oil: *Vane. The registered mage. Kavanagh. He surfaced in the Lantern District tonight. Bring him in or bring me his head; I'm no longer particular.* She remembered dressing, buckling the hunter's coat with its lead-lined collar that deadened low-grade charms. She remembered walking out into a night that smelled wrong—smoke without fire, rain without clouds, and beneath it all a faint sweetness like fruit going over, which every text agreed was the smell of the veil thinning.

And then—the seam. Then nothing. Then waking this morning in her own bed, boots still on, coat folded over the chair with a precision she did not remember exercising, and a note in her own handwriting on the nightstand that said, in her own hand:

*You followed him into the lantern market. You lost him. You went home.*

That was all it said. Six words. Her handwriting, but not quite—she wrote with a hard forward slant, letters leaning like runners at a start line, and these leaned too, but softer. As if someone had written them while thinking carefully about how she wrote.

Someone had been inside her head, Serah thought, and had taken the trouble to learn her penmanship first.

She should have reported it immediately. Protocol demanded it: any hunter experiencing memory irregularity was to present for examination within the hour, submit to a truth-reading, and be suspended pending review. The regulation number lived in her bones—she'd recited it as a cadet along with the oaths. *Irregularity is contagion. Contagion is treason.*

Instead she locked her door, drew the blind, and opened her field journal.

* * *

Every hunter kept two journals. The official log went to the archives, sanitized and signed. The field journal went nowhere—which was technically against regulations, and universally practiced anyway, because a hunter who trusted the Crown's archives with her raw observations was a hunter who hadn't been paying attention. Serah's field journal was a fat, grease-stained book bound in grey leather, coded in a shorthand of her own devising, and it had never once left her person except when she slept, and even then it slept under her pillow like a child's talisman.

She turned to last night's entry. She had written it—or she must have written it—in the hour before whatever happened happened. The ink was hers. The shorthand was hers. But reading it now was like reading a letter from a stranger who knew her well.

*2340. Entered lantern market via Cooper's Row. Crowd thinner than usual—fear of the dark sky keeping folk indoors. Subject sighted at the charm-stall of the widow Odile Ferran (see prior file, unregistered grief-charm, tolerated). Subject wearing a fishmonger's apron over a grey coat. He is smaller than his file photograph suggests. His hands are careful.*

*He saw me. Of course he saw me—I walk like what I am. He ran. I pursued west through the tallow-lantern rows. Notes on pursuit follow.*

And then the notes on pursuit, and here Serah felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite fear and not quite admiration. Because the notes were good. They were better than good—they were the kind of notes she prided herself on: precise, sensory, unsentimental. *Turned down the alley behind the chandler's. Knocked over a stack of wick-baskets; noted the sound carries oddly here, doubled, as if the walls are listening twice. He moved through the crowd like water finding cracks—people stepped aside without seeming to notice they'd done it. Three years unregistered and no one marks him. That is not luck. That is craft.*

*Cornered him at the mouth of Tanner's Cut. Drew steel. He didn't run. He looked at my face—actually looked, the way nobody looks at a hunter—and said, "You're tired."*

Serah stopped reading. Her own heartbeat was loud in the little room.

She was tired. She had been tired for months, though she'd told no one—not Dree, not the infirmary, not the pages of any journal, because a hunter who admitted fatigue was a hunter reassigned to desk work until the fatigue became permanent. She carried it the way she carried everything: silently, structurally, like a crack in a load-bearing wall. No one knew. No one could know.

But the note said he'd seen it. In the middle of being cornered at knifepoint, with arrest or worse on either side of him, Jude Kavanagh had looked at her face and named the thing she hid from everyone.

Her eyes went back to the page.

*"You're tired," he said. And then the world went soft at the edges, like breath on glass.*

That was the last line before the seam. After it, in different ink—fresher, later, written perhaps in the grey hour before dawn—her own hand had added:

*I woke at home. I do not remember coming home. My boots are muddy. There is a bruise on my left forearm I don't recall earning. I have lost time. I am writing this before I report it, because if I report it first, they will take the losing of it away from me, and I want to know what I lost before anyone else decides what it means.*

Below that, a third entry, in yet another shade of darkness on the page:

*One more thing. When I woke, there was a charm on my pillow. Unregistered make. I am supposed to confiscate it. I have not. I keep touching it.*

* * *

It lay on the desk before her now, where she'd set it down an hour ago and then spent that hour pretending she wasn't looking at it.

A charm, in the old style—a twist of copper wire around a sliver of something pale, bone or shell or moonstone, no bigger than her thumbnail. It should have read as contraband. It should have gone into an evidence bag with her signature across the seal, down into the vaults below the Hall where confiscated magic went to be catalogued and forgotten. Instead it sat in the open palm of her hand, warm—warm, though charms were not warm, though nothing about it explained the faint pulse of heat against her skin like a held bird.

She knew what it was doing. That was the terrible part. She could feel it working, the way you can feel a fire warming a room without seeing the flames. It was a sleep-charm, or near enough—but not the crude Crown-issue sedative charms that dropped a man like a sack of meal. This was finer. It had found the exhaustion in her, months of it, years of it, the tiredness she'd hidden from everyone including herself, and it had—

She tested the hypothesis the only way she could. She tried to remember when she had last slept truly well, and found, with a small shock, that she could. Last night. In her own bed, boots on, coat over the chair, some part of her aware enough to fold the coat and set the journal by the lamp. She had slept like the dead and dreamed of nothing, and woken this morning feeling—for the first time in longer than she could name—rested.

He had stolen an hour of her memory and given her a full night's sleep.

Serah set the charm down very carefully, as if it might go off, though of course it already had. She stood and walked to the window and stood looking out at the city without seeing it, because her mind was busy committing what felt like treason.

Here was the truth she kept circling: the file on Jude Kavanagh described a predator. Registered mage, class three, specialty unknown, flagged three years ago during the last purge and vanished into the city's cracks like a coin down a drain. The training materials were clear on what memory-thieves were. They took what made people people. They left their victims hollowed, smiling, unable to say why the world tasted of ash. Half the horror stories told to cadets featured them. *The thief doesn't need a knife,* Instructor Corvane used to say, pacing the lecture hall with his scarred hands clasped behind his back. *He takes your wedding day and sells it to a lonely widow. He takes your mother's voice and sells it to a grieving son. And you'll thank him, because you won't know what's missing. That is the monster we hunt, Vane. Remember it.*

She remembered it. She had repeated it to herself for eleven years.

And last night, cornered in Tanner's Cut with her blade drawn, the monster had looked at her, seen the thing she most despised in herself, and instead of taking anything she valued, he had taken an hour of pursuit—the hour in which she'd failed, the hour in which he'd escaped—and paid for the theft with rest. With kindness. With a charm worth, by her professional estimate, more than a month of a laborer's wages, pressed into the hand of the woman sent to drag him to the block.

*You're tired.*

No one had ever said it to her. Not once in eleven years. Hunters were not tired; hunters were instruments, and instruments did not admit to wear. She had not known how badly she wanted someone to say it until a criminal said it in a dark alley while she held steel on him.

She hated him a little for that. She examined the hatred and found it thin, a skin stretched over something else, something warmer and far more dangerous.

* * *

By midday she had reconstructed most of the hour.

Not from memory—from evidence. That was the discipline they'd beaten into her at the Academy: *the record does not lie, Vane, even when the mind does.* She walked the route her notes described, from Cooper's Row into the lantern market, past the widow Ferran's shuttered stall, west along the tallow-rows to the mouth of Tanner's Cut. The city in daylight was ordinary, hungover, scrubbed by a thin rain that couldn't decide whether to fall. But the walls remembered. Walls always remembered; that was why hunters learned to read them.

At the chandler's alley she found the scattered wick-baskets her notes mentioned, still lying where they'd fallen, and heard what her notes had heard: the odd doubling of sound, voices arriving twice, a half-beat apart. Veil-thinning. The whole district rang faintly wrong, like a bell with a hairline crack. She crouched and touched the cobbles and felt, through her gloves, the residue—old magic, deep magic, the kind the textbooks insisted was extinct, threaded through the stones like root-systems under a lawn. Covenant-work. The word rose in her unbidden, from some sealed room in her training, and she stood up fast, heart hammering, because that word was not supposed to exist in her head at all.

In Tanner's Cut itself, the story got stranger. She found the scuff-marks of the confrontation—her own boot-heels, his lighter tread—and then, ten paces further on, something her notes had not recorded, because it had happened inside the stolen hour: a second set of tracks leading not away from the cut but deeper into it, toward the river, and beside them, pressed into the mud of the embankment, a handprint. Small-handed. Careful. And around the print, in a perfect ring, the mud was grey. Not wet-grey. Ash-grey. As if something had burned there without flame, coolly, completely, leaving the print untouched at its center like a saint's footprint in a fire tale.

She had chased him into the cut. Something had happened at the river. And then she had walked home through the sleeping city, folded her coat, and forgotten.

On the way back she stopped at the widow Ferran's stall. It was open again today, the old woman selling grief-charms and luck-knots to a thin trickle of customers who bought them the way people buy candles—less from belief than from the need to hold something while the dark lasted. Serah bought a luck-knot she didn't need, and asked, casually, whether the stall had been open late last night.

"Late enough," the widow said, not looking up. "Folk come when folk come."

"Anyone trouble you?"

The old woman's hands paused over her knots. When she looked up, her eyes were sharp as awls. "A young man came by," she said. "Bought nothing. Stood talking to me a while, kind as you please. Then a hunter came down Cooper's Row, and he slipped out the back before she reached us." A pause. "He's not what the papers say, that one. Whatever the papers say."

"He's a registered mage operating illegally," Serah said. "That's what the papers say, because it's what's true."

"Mm." The widow went back to her knots. "And what's true about you, hunter? You've the look of a woman carrying something heavy and calling it nothing."

Serah left without answering, the luck-knot burning in her pocket like a small accusation.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the Hall's records annex, ostensibly cross-referencing ward-inspection reports, actually pulling everything the Crown held on the border wards' collapse—everything she could reach without tripping Dree's attention. What she found frightened her more than the empty sky had.

The reports contradicted each other. Three separate inspection teams had logged the wards as sound within the week before the collapse; two of those teams had filed their reports on the same day, from districts forty miles apart, in identical language. Identical. Word for word. She laid the pages side by side on the reading desk and felt the floor tilt gently under her certainty. Reports were not written that way unless someone had written them once and copied them many times, and reports were not copied unless someone needed the record to say a thing it did not.

And in the margin of the oldest ward-schematic, in faded iron-gall ink, someone long dead had written a single word she recognized from nowhere and everywhere: *covenant*. It had been struck through, struck so hard the nib had torn the paper.

Covenant. The word again, rising like a body from deep water.

She closed the files and sat in the green annex light with her hands flat on the scarred wood, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since morning: *They lied to us. Or they are lying now. Either way, the law I serve is standing on a floor that isn't there.*

Eleven years. Eleven years of oaths and purges and the clean bright certainty of the hunt, and it had taken one hour—one stolen hour—to show her the crack in the wall. She should feel violated. She did feel violated; that was the honest word, and she made herself hold it. He had been inside her mind. He had rearranged her. However gentle his hands, however kind his payment, the fact remained: a man she was sworn to destroy had opened her skull like a locket and chosen what to leave inside.

And he had chosen to leave her rested. He had chosen to leave her a note in her own handwriting so she would know something was missing rather than wake seamless and unknowing. He had chosen honesty inside the theft, which was a contradiction she had no category for. Monsters did not do that. The training materials were very clear about monsters.

Unless, said a small dry voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like her grandmother, *unless the materials were written by the same people who wrote those reports.*

The bell for evening muster rang through the Hall, brass and merciless. Serah closed her field journal, slid the charm into the inner pocket of her coat—over her heart, though she told herself it was merely the safest pocket—and went down to stand in the ranks.

Dree stood before them with the evening orders, and his voice had a new edge to it, whetted on something harder than routine. "Chancellor's edict comes down tomorrow," he said. "Martial law in the lower quarters. Memory-purges, beginning with the Lantern District." His eyes swept the ranks and snagged, briefly, on hers. "There are sympathizers among us. There always are. Root them out."

Around her, two hundred hunters murmured assent. Serah's mouth made the shape of the words with theirs—*so sworn, so bound*—and in her inner pocket, against her heart, the little charm pulsed warm, like a second conscience, like a debt she was beginning to suspect she would have to pay.

That night, before sleep, she wrote one line in the field journal, in her hard forward-slanting hand:

*He owes me an hour. I intend to collect it personally.*

And beneath it, after a long moment, in smaller letters she almost didn't let herself write:

*Find him before the purges do.*
