## 10. The Gutter Cathedral

The way down to the gutter cathedral began, as all true ways do, with a door that wasn't a door.

Jude found it behind the tannery on Corvis Lane, where the river's smell curdled into something older and meaner, and where the city's new edicts hadn't yet bothered to look. He had been here once before, three years ago, led by a drunk cooper who'd sworn there were people under Ashfall who could do what Jude did without hiding it. Jude had turned back at the threshold then. He'd had a full purse and a full set of rules, and rules were how he stayed alive.

Tonight the purse was empty of everything but a shard of light that burned like a coal against his ribs, and the rules had gone to ash with the stars.

He pressed his palm to the brick and felt the ward answer — not the Crown's grey ironwork wards that lined the boulevards, all cold authority and no mercy, but something woven by hand, stitch by patient stitch. It tasted of tallow and thyme and old grief. A password-ward, but the password was a feeling, not a word. Jude closed his eyes and gave it the only coin he had left: the memory of the stars going out. The horror of it. The strange, guilty lift beneath the horror, like a bird rising in his chest.

The brick sighed and became a doorway.

*They know what happened,* he thought, stepping down into the dark. *They felt it too. And they let me in anyway.*

The stair was narrow and slick with river damp, cut from the city's own bones — Ashfall was built on older Ashfall, built on older still, and the deeper strata remembered things the upper streets had never been told. Jude counted forty-one steps. The air changed at thirty: warmer, lit, alive with the smell of woodsmoke and boiling barley and, beneath it, something that made the shard against his ribs hum in recognition.

Magic. Old, unhidden, communal. It was like walking into a room where everyone was speaking his mother tongue.

The stair opened into a space that had no right to exist beneath a city that had outlawed enchantment for a hundred years.

It had been a cistern once — Jude could see the waterlines scored into the pillars, the great arched vaults sweating river-cold. But hands had softened it. Hundreds of hands, over years. Lanterns of colored glass hung from iron hooks, casting pools of amber and green. Tapestries covered the damp stone, embroidered with stars — hundreds of stars, thousands, sewn in silver thread on midnight cloth, so that looking up at the vaulted dark was almost like looking at the sky as it used to be. Someone had hung them before the stars went out. Someone had been preparing for the dark for a long time.

And there were people. That was the thing that stopped Jude's breath in his throat.

People everywhere. An old man teaching a girl of maybe ten to braid light between her fingers — actual light, pulled from a lantern and worked like wool, and no one hiding, no one flinching at footsteps. A woman with burn-scarred arms stirring a cauldron that murmured to itself in a language Jude's bones understood but his ears didn't. Children asleep in a nest of blankets, and over each small sleeping face a faint glow, like banked coals — wards of protection, laid by someone who loved them, renewed nightly.

*Unregistered,* Jude thought, and the word felt obscene. *Every one of them. Every one of them a criminal by the Chancellor's law. Every one of them just... living.*

He had spent three years believing he was alone in his small crimes. He had built his whole life on the architecture of that loneliness — never take much, never be seen, never belong, because belonging could be taken away. And here, forty-one steps beneath the city, was the proof that he had never been alone at all. He had only been hiding in a crowd of other hiders.

"You're the memory-thief."

The voice came from his left, and Jude turned to find a broad-shouldered woman blocking his path, arms crossed, a knife visible at her belt and a second one, he'd wager, not visible anywhere. Her hair was grey-shot and cropped short, and her eyes had the flat patience of someone who had killed before and would again if the arithmetic required it.

"I'm—" Jude began.

"Jude Kavanagh. Registered charm-seller, Lantern District. You lift memories and sell them back as charms, and you've been careful enough that the hunters took three years to put a name to your face." She said it without heat, the way one reads a ledger. "Mother Quill says you're to come up. The rest of us say you're to be searched first."

Jude lifted his arms. "Search away."

She did, brisk and thorough, and when her hands found the shape of the shard in his inner pocket she went very still.

"That's not yours," she said quietly.

"No," Jude said. "And it is. It's the only thing I own that I can't explain."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she found enough of it. "Wren," she said. "I keep the door. Come on, then. She's been asking for you since the sky went black, and Mother Quill doesn't ask for people twice."

* * *

Mother Quill sat at the far end of the cistern, in a chair that had been built up around her with cushions and blankets the way a tree grows around a fence post. She was small — Jude had expected imposing and got instead a wren of a woman, ninety if she was a day, her hands folded in her lap like sleeping birds. Her eyes were closed, and had the seamed, settled look of eyes that had been closed for a very long time.

But when Jude came within ten feet of her, she turned her face toward him with an accuracy that made his skin prickle.

"Sit, Covenant-Bearer," she said.

The word went through him like cold water. "I don't — that's the second time someone's called me that. The first one died saying it. I don't know what it means."

"You know what it means." Her voice was dry and light, like paper turning. "You've known since the emissary put that shard in your hand. You felt it fit. That's what knowing is, boy. Everything else is just telling yourself stories so you can keep your hands clean." She smiled then, and it softened her whole face. "Sit. You've been standing like a man in a doorway his whole life. Sit down and be in the room."

There was a stool beside her. Jude sat.

Up close, Mother Quill smelled of lavender and lamp oil and something faintly mineral, like the inside of a cave. Her closed eyes tracked him anyway. He had the unsettling sense that she was seeing not his face but something behind it — the shape of his memory, the architecture of his thefts, laid out like a city map.

"Three years ago," she said, "a stranger came to you. Hooded. Desperate. Asked you to take something."

Jude's mouth went dry. "How do you know that?"

"Because I sent her." Mother Quill said it simply, without apology. "Her name was Ilse. She carried the covenant's heart-knowledge, and the hunters were one street behind her, and there was nowhere left to hide it. Except—" her lips curved, "—in the one place in all Ashfall where a memory could be hidden inside a thousand others and never be found. Inside a thief's hoard. Inside the mind of a man so careful, so small and so safe, that no one would ever think to look."

"I'm not—"

"You are exactly that," Mother Quill said, "and it was the highest praise anyone has ever paid you. Ilse chose you the way a general chooses a hill. Not because you were mighty. Because you were *unfindable*." She tilted her head. "Do you remember her? Truly?"

Jude did. He had been turning the memory over for days now, examining it like a coin suspected of being counterfeit. A hooded stranger in his shop at closing, rain still beading on her cloak. *Take it,* she'd said. *All of it. Please. You'll know why someday, and I won't.* And she had wept with what he'd taken for grief, and he'd taken the memory anyway, because it was what he did, and he'd told himself her tears were none of his business.

"She begged me," he said. "And I took it and sold her a charm and never thought of her again."

"No," Mother Quill agreed. "You thought of her every day for three years and told yourself you hadn't. That's what guilt does when it's fed on loneliness. It goes to sleep and pretends to be professionalism." She reached out and found his wrist with uncanny precision, her fingers light and dry as moth wings. "You're not here because the stars went out, Jude Kavanagh. You're here because you want someone to tell you it wasn't your fault. And I will tell you something better: it doesn't matter whose fault it is. The covenant is breaking. The stars are dying. And you are the only vessel in this city that holds what's needed to stop it."

"The shard—"

"Show me."

He drew it out. In the lantern light it was less a shard than a splinter of dawn, and the whole cistern seemed to lean toward it — the sleeping children stirred, the cauldron-woman looked up from her work, the light-braiding girl went still with her fingers full of glow. The shard hummed against Jude's palm, and he felt it humming *back* at the tapestried stars overhead, thread to thread, like instruments tuning to one another.

Mother Quill's blind face turned toward it, and for a moment she looked, impossibly, like a woman looking at the sun.

"Six," she whispered. "There were six anchors, and the covenant was woven through all of them, and when the Chancellor pulled down the border wards he didn't just break a wall. He snapped the thread that ran from the spire through every star in the mortal sky. Each anchor holds a shard of the covenant's making. Each shard was hidden, in the last days, inside a mortal mind — the only vaults the fae could not reach and the Crown could not imagine." Her fingers tightened on his wrist. "Ilse's shard sleeps in you. The other five sleep in the minds of people whose memories you have taken. Every theft you ever made, boy. Every charm you sold. You were gathering them without knowing. A magpie building a hoard for a war it couldn't see coming."

The floor seemed to tilt. Jude thought of his ledger, the one he kept in his head because paper could be confiscated. Three years of small thefts. The widow's grief. The cooper's pride. The night-watchman's first love. The scholar's whole lost decade. Dozens of them. He had thought of them as crimes, and they were, but they had also been — he understood it now with a lurch that made him dizzy — a *collection*. Someone had scattered the pieces of the world's salvation through the city's ordinary hearts, and he had walked through that city like a needle through cloth, gathering thread.

"I can't undo them," he said. His voice came out strange. "The memories. I sold some. I—"

"Sold some, kept some, lost some. And the people you took from have lived with holes in them ever since, and now you must go back to every one of them and ask for the holes to be filled." Mother Quill's grip tightened, almost painful. "Yes. That is the shape of it. That is what a covenant *is*, boy — debts, owed and honored, binding stranger to stranger until the binding becomes a world. You thought your magic was theft. It was always covenant. You just never asked anyone's consent before. Now you will have to."

"And if they say no?"

"Then you'll have to be worth saying yes to." She released his wrist and folded her hands again. "The widow you robbed first. She's on your ledger. She'll be the hardest and she'll be the first. They always are."

* * *

He stayed. He hadn't meant to — he'd meant to take Mother Quill's words and slip back up the stair and be a careful man again, alone with his shard and his dread. But Wren pressed a bowl of barley stew into his hands, and the light-braiding girl — her name was Pip, and she was missing two teeth and had no fear whatsoever — planted herself beside him and demanded to know what color memories were.

"Memories aren't a color," Jude said.

"Everything's a color," Pip said with the authority of a ten-year-old. "Marta says fear is grey-green. What's stealing?"

"Pip." Wren's voice, from across the room, carried the weight of a dropped anchor.

But Jude found, to his own surprise, that he didn't want to be rescued from the question. "Stealing memories is," he said slowly, feeling for the truth of it, "like taking the color out of something and leaving the shape. The person still has the day. They just can't see it anymore. It goes grey."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a judge. "That's the worst thing I ever heard," she announced, and then, cheerfully, "Do it to the Chancellor."

Around them the cathedral went on breathing. A man with a lute played something in a mode Jude didn't recognize, and the cauldron-woman hummed harmony with it, and two young men argued cheerfully over a game of stones while a third cheated with a charm so crude Jude could have spotted it blindfolded. Children woke and were fed. The tapestried stars caught the lantern light and threw it back in tiny silver points, and Jude sat beneath them with a bowl in his hands and felt something he had not felt since before his mother died, something he had spent three years and a thousand careful rules making sure he would never feel again.

He felt like he had come home to a place he'd never been.

It terrified him more than the hunters did.

*Don't,* he told himself, the old voice, the safe voice. *You've seen what happens to things that belong to each other. The Chancellor pulls them apart. The stars went out because something belonged to something. Belonging is just a bigger target—*

"Boy." Mother Quill's voice cut through his spiral like a knife through fog. She had not turned her head, but she was smiling. "You're doing it again. Standing in the doorway."

"I'm sitting down."

"In your head you're not." She beckoned, and he came, and she took his hand in both of hers and turned it palm-up, the way a fortune-teller would, though Jude suspected she needed no palm to read him. Her fingers found the shard-callus, the faint heat that never left his skin now. "I'm going to tell you what I see, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to thank me, and then you're going to leave and do it anyway. That's how prophecy works. It's not a gift. It's a bill."

"Go on, then."

Her blind eyes turned up toward him, and though they saw nothing, Jude felt *seen* down to the rivets.

"I see six debts and a thief who must become a beggar," she said. "I see a hunter whose law is dying faster than her faith, and a road where the two of you walk the same direction whether you like it or not. I see a spire dark at the heart of the city and a flame that wants what you're most afraid to give." Her voice dropped, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean in with it, lute and cauldron and children all gone quiet. "And I see the stars, boy. All of them. Waiting in the dark like lamps with their wicks trimmed, waiting for someone to bring the fire. They are not gone. They are *held*. Every star in your sky is an anchor of the covenant, and every anchor is a memory, and the memory is in you and in the ones you robbed, and the Lord Chancellor knows it now too — oh, he knows it, he didn't pull down the wards out of ignorance — and he has hunters on every street and purges in every quarter, looking for exactly what you're carrying."

The cold that went through Jude then had nothing to do with the river-damp.

"He's looking for the shards."

"He's looking for *you*," Mother Quill said. "The shards are just how he'll find you. So you have a choice, and it's the only choice that was ever really yours. You can run — you're good at running, you've had three years of practice — and you can stay small and safe and unfindable, and you can watch the sky go dark forever from behind a locked door. Or you can go out into a hunted city, knock on the doors of everyone you ever hurt, and ask them to trust you with the last light in the world." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone handing over a bill. "I know which one you'll choose. I've known since Wren said your name at the door and your heartbeat didn't change. Thieves' hearts are steady, Covenant-Bearer. It's the one honest thing about them."

Jude looked down at his palm, at the shard's faint dawn-light staining his lifeline gold.

"The widow," he said. "On Corvis Lane. The one whose son died. I took her grief and sold it back to her as a charm to *carry* the grief, which — saints, I made her pay me to hold her own sorrow at arm's length." His voice cracked on it. "She has a hole where her mourning should be. She's been walking around for three years unable to properly miss her own son, and I did that, and I got paid for it."

"Yes," Mother Quill said. "And tomorrow you'll climb her stairs and knock on her door and say all of that out loud, and it will be the hardest thing you have ever done, harder than the spire, harder than the end of this — and you'll do it because it's the first true payment on a debt that has six parts." She patted his hand once, briskly, and released him. "Now go to sleep. Wren will show you where. You can't save the world on an empty night."

* * *

He slept in a alcove behind the tapestries, on a pallet that smelled of lavender and other people's dreams, and if he slept badly, it was not the pallet's fault.

He woke before the others, in the grey hour when even the underground was quiet, and lay listening to the cathedral breathe — the cauldron's murmur, a child's sigh, the soft tick of the lanterns cooling. Above him, through forty-one steps and a false door and a city's whole lying weight, the sky would be the color of dirty slate, starless, the way it had been for six days now. He thought of the stars as Mother Quill had named them: not gone, but *held*. Lamps with trimmed wicks. Waiting for someone to bring the fire.

*Six debts,* he thought. *And the first one lives on Corvis Lane, and she knows my face, and I'm going to have to knock.*

He got up quietly, so as not to wake the children, and found Wren already awake at the door-ward, feeding it a thread of light from her fingertips the way one feeds a horse an apple.

"Leaving," she said. Not a question.

"Going to Corvis Lane."

Wren studied him, then reached into her belt and pressed something into his hand: a slim knife in a worn leather sheath. "Mother Quill will say you don't need it. She's usually right. But she's blind, and I'm not, and I've seen the hunters' new orders." Her voice dropped. "They're not arresting anymore, charm-seller. The purges started last night in the Weavers' Quarter. Whole streets, minds wiped to the brick. They're not looking for criminals anymore." She met his eyes. "They're looking for a vessel. And the Chancellor doesn't leave vessels standing."

Jude took the knife. It was heavier than it looked, the way all true things were.

"Tell Mother Quill—" he started.

"She knows," Wren said, and almost smiled. "She always knows. That's the curse of her. Go on, then. And charm-seller—" she held the brick door open for him, and grey pre-dawn light leaked down the stair, "—come back. Doors are only doors if the people using them mean to return."

Jude Kavanagh climbed the forty-one steps out of the gutter cathedral and into the starless morning, a knife at his belt and a shard against his ribs and six debts waiting in the grey city like unlit lamps — and on Corvis Lane, in a narrow house that smelled of cold ash, a widow was already awake, already dressed, and had been standing at her window since the stars went out, watching the door.
