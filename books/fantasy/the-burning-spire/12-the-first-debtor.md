## 12. The First Debtor

The rain had been falling on Lantern District for three days, and by the third day the city had stopped pretending it was anything other than grief.

Jude walked through it with his collar up and his hood low, past shuttered stalls and lanterns that no one bothered to light anymore. The edict had gone up on every corner two nights ago—broadsheets nailed over older broadsheets, the Chancellor's seal stamped in black wax like a bruise. *By order of Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow: all unregistered practitioners of enchantment will be purged.* The word *purged* sat there in heavy type, and beneath it someone had scrawled in charcoal, in a child's uneven hand, *run*.

He was not running. Not yet. He was doing something worse.

He was going to see Maren Colm.

Her house stood at the end of Tanner's Row, where the street gave up on cobblestones and surrendered to mud. He remembered it the way he remembered everything—too well, too completely, every stolen hour filed away in his skull like coins in a miser's chest. He remembered the blue door with its peeling paint. He remembered the smell of tallow and wet wool. He remembered the shape of her grief when he'd lifted it from her, three years ago now, or was it four—the way grief has a weight you can feel through your palms when you cup your hands around someone's temple and *draw*, slow as pulling thread from a spool.

He had taken her memory of her son's funeral. All of it. The procession, the shovels, the small coffin, the way she had not wept until the earth hit the wood and then had not stopped for hours. He'd sold it to a collector across the river who paid well for authentic sorrow, and he'd told himself the same lie he always told: *it's a kindness, really. She'll sleep better without it.*

He had never once gone back to check whether she slept better at all.

The blue door looked smaller than he remembered. Everything about the house did—the windows lower, the chimney thinner, as if the building itself had shrunk inside its own skin, the way people do when they are left alone too long with an absence.

He knocked before he could talk himself out of it.

Footsteps. Slow ones. The door opened on a chain, and one eye appeared in the gap—grey, sharp, unimpressed.

"We're not buying," said Maren Colm.

"I'm not selling." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Maren. My name is Jude Kavanagh. I—I made charms. On Candlemaker's Lane. Before."

The eye narrowed. He watched her take inventory of him through the gap: the hood, the hollow cheeks, the hands he kept very still at his sides because they wanted to shake.

"The charm-seller," she said. "They say the hunters took you. They say there's a price on your head worth more than this whole street."

"They say a lot of things."

"They do." She didn't move to open the door. "And yet here you stand on my step in the rain, looking like something the tide refused. What do you want, Jude Kavanagh?"

He had rehearsed this. On the long walk down from the gutter cathedral, through Mother Quill's smoke-wrapped words still ringing in his ears—*six shards, thief, six debts, and the first is a woman who cannot remember burying her boy*—he had rehearsed it a dozen times. Every version had sounded reasonable. Every version had sounded survivable.

Standing here, none of them did.

"I took something from you," he said. "Three years ago. Maybe four. You came to my shop and you couldn't stop crying and I told you I could help you sleep."

The chain did not slide free. But the eye changed. Something behind it went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when it hears a name it hasn't spoken in years.

"My son's name was Tam," she said. "I know that. I know it the way you know a word in a language you used to speak. I can write it. I can say it. But when I try to reach for him—" Her hand rose to her sternum, pressed flat, as if holding a door shut against wind. "There's nothing. There's a hole shaped like a funeral and I can't even remember what I wore. People tell me I screamed. Did I scream? I don't know. I don't know anything about the worst day of my life except that it happened to somebody who wears my face."

"Yes," Jude said. "That was me. I did that."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the awning above them. Somewhere down the row a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Are you confessing," she said at last, "or apologizing? Because one of those gets you turned in and the other gets you nothing, and I'd like to know which coin you're spending before I decide what it buys."

"Both. Neither." He pulled back his hood, let the rain hit his hair, let her see his face plainly—the face that had been on broadsheets now, though hers was the only door in the city where being recognized might work in his favor rather than against it. "I'm asking for it back. Your memory. I still have it. I've carried it three years and I never sold all of it—I told myself I kept a piece because it was good stock, but that's a lie too. I kept it because taking it felt wrong even while I was doing it, and some part of me knew I'd have to bring it home someday."

Something flickered across her face—not softness. He didn't deserve softness. It was more like a blade being tested against a thumb.

"You still *have* it," she repeated. "In your head. With everything else you stole."

"Yes."

"And you can give it back?"

"I can. It won't be gentle. Putting a memory back is harder than taking one—it's like trying to pour spilled water into a cup. Some of it will be lost. Some of it will land wrong. You'll grieve it again, all at once, maybe worse than the first time, because the first time you had years to do it in and this time you'll have minutes." He swallowed. "I'm not offering you relief, Maren. I'm offering you the truth of your own son's burial. That's all I have."

She studied him for a long moment. Then the chain rattled, and the door swung wide, and the smell of the house reached him—tallow, wet wool, and underneath it, faintly, the ghost of pipe smoke. Tam's tobacco, maybe. A habit outliving the man who held it.

"Inside," she said. "Before the neighbors see you and earn the Chancellor's blood money by accident. Half this street would sell their mothers for less."

* * *

Her kitchen was small and fiercely clean. One chair at the table. One cup on the shelf. One of everything, arranged with the terrible precision of a life pared down to a single person. She put water on to boil without asking if he wanted any, because hospitality was a reflex older than suspicion, and then she sat across from him and folded her hands and became, abruptly, a magistrate.

"Tell me why now."

"It's complicated."

"I'm sixty-one years old and I buried my only child. Complicated doesn't frighten me."

So he told her—or the shape of it. The stars going out. The wards breaking. The dying emissary in the reeds beneath the river bridge, pressing a shard of light into his palm and calling him Covenant-Bearer with her last breath. He told her about Mother Quill, about the shards hidden in the minds of the people whose memories he'd stolen, about the covenant that had once held the night sky together and was now unraveling thread by thread while the Chancellor built himself a throne out of the wreckage.

He did not tell her everything. He left out Serah Vane, mostly. He left out the way his own heart had begun behaving like a stranger. Some things were not hers to carry.

"So the sky broke," Maren said when he finished, "and the fix is locked inside the heads of everyone you ever robbed."

"That's about the size of it."

"Then the gods have a sense of humor after all. I'd begun to doubt it." She poured the water over the leaves—two cups, he noticed; she'd decided something already—and set one in front of him. Steam curled between them like a question. "Here's my trouble, charm-seller. If you give me back that day, you give me back the worst thing that ever happened to me. I have spent three years learning how to live around the hole where it used to be. I know its edges. I know which thoughts fall in and which ones I've built bridges over. And you're proposing to drop the whole thing back in, all at once, and walk out and leave me alone with it."

"Yes," Jude said quietly. "That's what I'm proposing."

"And why should I say yes?"

"Because it's yours." He met her eyes. "Because I had no right to it, and no right is repaired by keeping it safe. Because you deserve to remember your son's funeral even if remembering breaks you, because a grief you chose is worth ten comforts you were robbed into. And because—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—because I think Tam would want you to have it. Whoever he was. Whatever he was to you. I took him from you twice, Maren. Once when he died and once when I stole the mourning. I can only give one of them back."

The clock on her mantel ticked. Outside, the grey light thickened toward evening, and somewhere far off a bell rang the hour in a city that no longer trusted its own bells.

Maren wrapped both hands around her cup but did not drink.

"There's a price," she said.

His stomach dropped. He'd expected refusal. He'd expected tears, or fury, or a broom. He had not expected negotiation, and negotiation meant she was considering it, and hope was a much more dangerous thing to hold than fear.

"Name it," he said.

"Not coin. I know what you'd offer and I don't want it. Coin spends; this doesn't." She leaned forward. "My Tam died in the Spire district. Everyone says. A wall collapsed during the Chancellor's construction—his grand aqueduct, his monument to himself—and eleven men were killed and the Crown paid out eleven sums and called it settled. But Tam wrote to me every week. Every week, without fail, since he was fourteen years old and conscripted into the works. And the week he died, no letter came—but the week *after*, a letter came. Late. Smudged. Written in a hurry, half of it illegible, and the last line torn clean away, as if someone had ripped the bottom off the page before sending it."

Jude sat very still.

"I have that letter," Maren said. "I've read it a hundred times, or however many times you read a thing when the ending's been cut off. It's full of nothing—weather, wages, complaints about his foreman. And then it stops mid-sentence, right at the bottom, and whatever he wrote next was taken from me before I ever saw it. The Crown says it was damaged in transit. The Crown says a lot of things." Her voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "You steal memories, Mr. Kavanagh. You move through locked places and take what people hide. So here is my price. Find my son's last words. The ones they tore away. Bring them to me, and then put my funeral back in my head, and we'll call the debt between us paid—with interest, if the words are what I think they might be."

"What do you think they might be?"

She looked at him then with an expression he had seen a hundred times across a hundred counters, in the faces of people deciding whether to buy a charm—to buy a borrowed feeling, a secondhand joy. Except this wasn't a charm. This was a woman betting the last of her heart on a stranger's crime.

"He knew the wall was unsafe," she said. "He'd written about it for months. Cracks in the footing, men sent down anyway, the foreman pocketing the timber money. I think the torn part wasn't goodbye. I think it was a warning, or a name, or proof—and I think someone in the Chancellor's office knew exactly what it was when they cut it out. Eleven men dead isn't an accident, Mr. Kavanagh. Eleven men dead is arithmetic." She pushed back her chair and crossed to the dresser, and her hands trembled only a little as she drew out a tin box, and from the tin box a letter gone soft and brown at its folds. "Read it."

* * *

He read it twice.

The handwriting started strong and deteriorated—Tam Colm had written the first paragraphs in the careful clerk's hand of a man taught his letters by the Works, and by the final page the letters slanted and crowded, written fast, perhaps standing up, perhaps in the dark. *Wages short again. Foreman Vess says the footing cracks are frost, but frost doesn't run uphill. Told the overseer and got docked a day for troublemaking. If anything happens—*

And then the tear. Clean, deliberate, a straight edge where the paper simply ended, mid-phrase, mid-breath.

*If anything happens.*

Jude set the letter down on the table between them with the care owed to something explosive.

"When did it arrive?" he asked.

"Nine days after he died. Long enough that I'd already—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Long enough."

"Who delivered it?"

"Crown courier. Grey coat. I signed a receipt. I still have the receipt." She almost smiled, and it was the most frightening thing he'd seen all day. "I keep everything, Mr. Kavanagh. It's what's left when you can't keep the person."

He turned the letter over. Held it near the lamp. The paper was Works stock—cheap, fibrous, the kind that took ink badly and forgave nothing. Along the torn edge, so faint he might have imagined it, there was a ghost of pressure: the mirror-image impression of writing from a page that had once lain beneath this one. Someone had torn the letter in haste, on top of other papers, and the pen of the page below had bitten through.

Or—his pulse picked up—someone had torn it atop a *copy*. And copies leave ghosts.

"There's writing under the tear," he said. "Impressions. I can't read them here, but there are ways. Charcoal dust, lamplight raked sideways, and—" he hesitated, then decided honesty was the only currency he had left, "—and there are things I can do that aren't entirely lawful. Memory leaves marks on objects, sometimes. Places hold echoes. If Tam wrote those last lines somewhere specific—a barracks desk, a foreman's office—the room may still remember the sound of the pen."

Maren stared at him. "You're telling me the walls might remember my son."

"I'm telling you I'll listen to them if they do."

For a moment something moved behind her face—hope, raw and unwelcome, the thing she'd spent three years teaching herself to live without. He watched her wrestle it down, and lost, and didn't seem to mind losing.

"The Spire district is crawling with hunters," she said. "Since the edict. They're purging the lower quarters starting tomorrow—they posted the schedule outside the tithe office. And the aqueduct site has been sealed since the collapse. Crown seals. No one goes in."

"No one law-abiding," Jude said.

"No," she agreed. "No one law-abiding." She slid the letter back into its tin, but she left the box on the table, within reach, the way a person keeps a hand near a wound. "One more condition, charm-seller. When you go—and you'll go tonight, I can see it itching in you—you come back alive. That's part of the price. I've had enough of people not coming back. If you die chasing my son's words, I'll consider the debt unpaid and I will haunt you, and I'm told the dead are very thorough these days."

Despite everything—the edict, the hunters, the shard burning cold in his pocket like a splinter of the vanished sky—Jude found himself almost smiling.

"That's fair," he said. "Terms accepted."

She stood, and he stood, and for a moment they faced each other across the small fierce cleanliness of her kitchen, thief and victim, debtor and creditor, two people bound by a crime that was about to become something else entirely.

At the door, she stopped him with a hand on his sleeve. Her grip was stronger than it looked.

"Mr. Kavanagh. The memory you're holding. The funeral." Her voice was steady, but her thumb pressed hard into his arm. "When you give it back—don't soften it. Don't tidy it. Give me all of it, the screaming and the mud and the shovels. I earned every minute of that day with my boy. I'll not have you edit my love just because it hurts."

"I wouldn't dare," he said. And found, to his surprise, that he meant it—that somewhere between her doorstep and her kitchen table, the theft he'd carried for three years had changed its weight entirely. It was no longer stock. It was no longer coin. It was a debt with a heartbeat, and he would carry it until it was paid in full.

He stepped out into the rain. Behind him the blue door closed, and above the rooftops the starless dark pressed down like a lid, and in his pocket the fae shard pulsed once, faintly, as if it had heard the terms and approved.

The Spire district. Sealed gates, Crown hunters, a foreman named Vess, and a torn letter whispering its ghost-words to anyone ruthless enough to listen.

Tomorrow the purges began. Which meant he had one night to break into the Chancellor's own construction site—and somewhere out there in the wet dark, he suspected, a witch-hunter with a missing hour and a stubborn conscience was already hunting the same streets he was.
