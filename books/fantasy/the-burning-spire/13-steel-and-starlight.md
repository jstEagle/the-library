## 13. Steel and Starlight

The order came at dawn, written in the Chancellor's own hand, and it was the smallness of it that made Serah Vane's stomach turn.

Not a proclamation. Not a decree read from the Spire's steps with trumpets and banners. A single sheet of grey paper, folded twice, delivered by a courier who wouldn't meet her eyes. *By order of Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow: the child known as Wren of Tanner's Row, suspected of fae-touched blood, is to be executed this day. Hunter Serah Vane to carry out the sentence personally. The Crown requires an example.*

An example.

She stood in the doorway of her quarters at the Hunter's Hall, the paper trembling slightly in her grip, and watched the light come up over Lantern District — a thin, watery gold that caught the soot on every rooftop and turned it briefly beautiful. Somewhere below, a baker was arguing with his apprentice about ovens. Somewhere below, a child called Wren was waking up without knowing she would not see noon.

*Fae-touched.* The words had meant something once, before the stars went out. In her training they had been a historical term, a curiosity — *in the old days, they said, some children were born with starlight in their eyes; we burned them anyway.* Her instructors had laughed when they said it. She had laughed too. That was the part that shamed her now, three years later, standing in cold morning air with a death warrant in her hand: how easily she had laughed.

The residue was still on her gloves. She hadn't washed them since the ward-collapse site — ash that wasn't ash, a fine silver dust that clung to wool and skin and, she was beginning to suspect, to conscience. Under lamplight it shimmered like crushed pearl. Under her fingers, sometimes, it seemed almost warm.

Covenant-magic, the old texts would have called it, if any old texts had survived. But the Crown had burned its archives twice — once under Marrow's predecessor, once under Marrow himself — and what remained was doctrine, and doctrine said there had never been anything behind the veil worth covenanting with. Doctrine said magic was theft, plain and simple. Doctrine said the law was a wall, and hunters were the mortar.

Doctrine had not explained why the sky went black.

Serah folded the warrant along its creases, carefully, as if precision could make it something other than what it was, and tucked it into her coat. Then she buckled on her sword, checked the iron-tipped bolts in her crossbow quiver — iron for fae-work, silver for human mages, though no one had needed either in years until last week — and went down into the city to kill a child.

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of lye and wet hides, the way it always had, but underneath that smell was another one now, newer: fear. It hung in the alleyways like smoke after a fire that hadn't quite finished. Since the edict, since the purges began in the lower quarters, people here had learned to move quickly and look at nothing. Doors closed when boots echoed. Shutters swung shut on their hinges with a sound like held breath.

Wren lived above a tannery with an aunt who took in washing. The aunt opened the door two inches and looked at Serah's coat — the grey-and-silver of the Crown's hunters, the crossed swords at the collar — and her face did something complicated and terrible. It didn't surprise. It resigned.

"You're for the girl," the woman said. Not a question.

"I need to ask her some questions," Serah said, and heard how the lie sat in her mouth, heavy and obvious as a stone in a shoe.

"She's seven." The aunt's hands were red to the wrist, raw from the wash-water. "Whatever you think she is, she's seven. She cries at thunder. She can't even—" The woman stopped herself, jaw tightening, because crying about it in a doorway was a luxury, and she knew the arithmetic of this city better than most. "Come in, then. If you must."

The room above the tannery was one room, low-ceilinged, warm from the vats below. A bed, a stool, a table with half-mended shirts pinned to it. And Wren.

She was small the way all seven-year-olds are small, all elbows and knobby knees, sitting cross-legged on the bed with a wooden horse in her lap, painting it with a brush that had gone stiff with dried paint days ago. She looked up when Serah came in, and her eyes were ordinary brown eyes, wide and wary, and for one absurd moment Serah thought: *they've got it wrong, they've got the wrong house, the wrong child,* and felt relief rise in her like water—

And then Wren blinked, and in the instant between blinks, Serah saw it.

A flicker. Like light through a cracked door. The girl's pupils caught the grey morning from the window and threw it back wrong — not reflected but *refracted*, split into a faint wheel of colors that shouldn't have been there, gone before the eye could swear to it. Starlight in the eyes. The old stories had gotten one thing right.

"There," the aunt whispered, from the corner where she'd retreated, wringing her hands in her apron. "You see it? That's what the neighbor told the wardens. That's all it was. A trick of the light, I said. A trick of the—"

"Has anyone else seen it?" Serah asked quietly. "Anyone official?"

"A warden came yesterday. Took a statement." The aunt's voice flattened. "He wrote down that she'd been seen doing charms. Charms! She can't write her own name. He wrote it down and he smiled while he wrote it, hunter, and I've been sitting up all night knowing what smiles like that mean."

Serah knew too. Smiles like that meant quotas. The purge orders had come with numbers attached — *so many examined per district, so many detained* — and numbers required findings, and findings were easiest to manufacture out of children who couldn't argue. The law, which she had served for nine years, which she had believed was a wall against the dark, had become a machine for grinding the innocent, and it ran on men who smiled while they wrote.

Wren had gone very still on the bed, the way small animals go still. "Are you going to take me away?" she asked. Her voice was steady in the way children's voices are steady right before they aren't.

"I'm going to ask you some questions first," Serah said. She crouched, bringing herself down to the girl's level, and set her crossbow on the floor beside her, deliberately, where the child could see it put down. "Can you show me what your eyes do?"

Wren glanced at her aunt. The aunt nodded, miserably. And the girl screwed up her face in concentration, squeezed her eyes shut, and opened them again — and there it was, unmistakable this time, a soft prismatic shimmer, like looking at sunlight through a soap bubble. It lasted three heartbeats. Four. Then it faded, and she was just a tired child again, blinking.

"It doesn't hurt," Wren said, as if reporting a symptom to a physician. "It happens when I'm happy. Mostly. Mam used to say I had sunny eyes." Her face crumpled slightly around the word *mam*, and Serah understood, then, whose daughter this was — a washerwoman dead of fever last winter, if the records in Serah's head served, one of the hundreds the city swallowed without ceremony. An orphan with strange eyes and no one left to protect her except an aunt with raw hands.

"Mam said it meant the stars liked me," Wren added, smaller.

The stars were gone now. All of them. Had someone decided that a child who remembered them, who carried them faintly in her face, was evidence of something? Evidence needed removing?

Serah rose slowly. Her knees ached — she'd been on her feet since the ward-site, since the ash, since the hour of memory she couldn't find in her own head, that missing hour with Jude Kavanagh's name written all over its absence. She looked at the warrant in her mind's eye. *The Crown requires an example.*

"Hunter." The aunt's voice cracked on the word. "Please. There's nowhere she can go. If you don't—"

"Pack her a bag," Serah said. "Now. Whatever she can carry. Don't speak above a whisper."

The aunt stared. "What?"

"You heard me." Serah was already moving, checking the window — it faced the alley, which faced the tannery yard, which opened onto Cooper's Lane, which was narrow and crooked and, thank every forgotten god, unfamiliar to the wardens who patrolled this district on rotation. "There will be a second visit today. The warden who came yesterday will want to see his finding confirmed, and confirmation means custody, and custody means—" She stopped. Made herself say it plainly, because euphemism was the Crown's disease and she refused to catch it. "It means the block. Pack the bag."

The room erupted into quiet, frantic motion — the aunt pulling a satchel from under the bed, stuffing bread and a spare shift into it with shaking hands, Wren standing frozen with her painted wooden horse clutched to her chest. Serah took the horse, weighed it, gave it back. "That comes too. Things that matter get carried. That's a rule."

"What's your name?" Wren asked suddenly, watching her pack the bag with the fierce attention of a child memorizing a stranger.

"Vane. Serah Vane."

"Are you a bad one or a good one?"

The question landed like a blade between ribs. Serah thought of the warrant in her coat. Thought of the hour missing from her own memory, stolen clean by a thief with kind hands — Kavanagh had taken only what he needed, and left something else in its place, something warm she still hadn't fully mapped, and she had spent three nights telling herself that didn't make him anything but a criminal. Thought of doctrine, and walls, and mortar.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "Ask me again tonight."

* * *

They went out through the tannery yard, past the vats steaming in the cold, Wren's hand locked in Serah's, the aunt staying behind with instructions to know nothing and say less. Cooper's Lane received them in its crooked silence. Serah had dressed Wren in a hooded coat two sizes too large, and the child walked close, matching her steps, asking nothing, which was somehow worse than questions.

They were three streets from the tannery when the bells started.

Not the quarter-bells. The alarm bells — the ones in the Hall tower that Serah herself had trained to ring, the pattern that meant *hunter in distress, hunters assemble*. Except she wasn't in distress, and she understood within four notes that the pattern had been changed, that new signals had been added to the old code while she slept, and the new signal ringing now across the rooftops of the capital meant only one thing:

*A hunter has turned. Hunters, take her.*

"They know," she breathed. Of course they knew. The warden's report would have named her; the aunt's door would be watched; and Marrow — Marrow, who signed death warrants for seven-year-olds in his own hand — would have anticipated exactly this. Perhaps he had wanted it. A rogue hunter was more useful than an obedient one, in certain arithmetic. A rogue hunter proved that mercy was treason, and treason was everywhere, and therefore the purges must widen.

Wren tugged her hand. "What's the bells mean?"

"It means we run properly now." Serah lifted the girl onto her hip — seven years old, light as a bundle of sticks, arms locking around her neck with the unselfconscious trust of the very young — and ran.

Lantern District unfolded around them in its morning maze: awnings and stairways, drying lines strung between buildings like rigging, the canal cutting its green ribbon through the middle of everything. Serah knew these streets the way she knew her sword-grip, and she used every trick of them — cut through the cooperage, over the fence with its splintered top, down the steps that didn't appear on any Crown map because the Crown had never bothered to map where poor people walked. Behind her, distantly, boots. More than one pair. Whistles answering whistles across the rooftops, triangulating.

She ducked into the mouth of an old drainage arch, set Wren down, and pressed her back to the cold stone, breathing hard, listening. The whistles passed. Converged somewhere east. They expected her to run for the gates — a rogue hunter's instinct, flee the walls. They didn't yet understand she had nowhere outside these walls to go, and no intention of leaving. Everything that mattered was inside them now: a gutter cathedral full of unregistered mages she'd once raided and never reported fully; a thief named Kavanagh who owed her an hour of memory and perhaps much more than that; six shards of something ancient scattered through the minds of this city like seeds in winter soil.

"Hunter Vane?" Wren whispered. Her hood had fallen back. In the dim of the arch, her eyes did their small impossible shimmer, and Serah found she was no longer afraid of it. It looked like what it probably was: a candle kept lit from a fire everyone else had let go out.

"Don't call me hunter anymore," Serah said. "I'm not that, as of this morning."

"What are you, then?"

She considered the question while the bells rang on overhead, and the ash-silver dust on her gloves caught the thin light, and somewhere across the city a man with a thief's hands was running from her colleagues just as she was.

"Yours," she said finally. "For now. Come on — I know a place where the bells can't follow."

She took the girl's hand and stepped out of the arch, out of the law, out of the life she had built from doctrine and duty — and turned toward the river, toward the gutter cathedral, toward whatever justice looked like when it had to be built by hand in the dark.

Behind them, on the Hall tower, the bells changed their song again, hunting.

Ahead, beneath the bridges where the city kept its secrets, a blind seer was waiting to tell her exactly how much trouble she had just become.
