## 16. Fugitives' Fire

The rain found us before the cathedral did.

It came down in grey sheets over the Lantern District, washing the soot from the rooftops and turning the cobbles to a river of dull mercury, and Serah Vane ran through it with the vault's stolen light burning a hole against her ribs. Two shards now, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat — one warm as a sleeping bird, one cold as river stone — and every bell in the capital tolling her name behind her.

*Serah Vane. Traitor to the Crown.*

She had heard the proclamation read aloud from a herald's cart not an hour past, in a voice like tin hammered flat. *By order of Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow.* The words had chased her through three markets and across the Weeping Bridge, and still they rang in her ears louder than the bells.

Ten years of service. Ten years of warrants served cleanly, of suspects brought in breathing more often than not, of believing — truly believing — that the law was a wall between the city and the dark. And it had taken one child with fae-light in her eyes and one order she would not carry out to bring the whole wall down on her head.

"Left," Jude said at her shoulder, breathless. "Not the alley — the alley floods."

She obeyed without thinking, which was its own small alarm. She was following a thief through the drowned dark of the city she had sworn to protect, trusting his knowledge of it over her own maps, her own training, her own ten years. The thought should have frightened her. Instead there was only the rain, and his hand briefly catching her elbow to steer her around a collapsed drain-grate, and the strange, stubborn warmth of the shards against her skin.

The entrance to the gutter cathedral was nothing. That was the point of it. A dead-end yard behind a tannery, a wall green with rot, and a door that looked like part of the brickwork until Jude pressed his palm flat against a particular stain and whispered something too soft for the rain to carry away. The wall opened on darkness that smelled of candle-wax, wet stone, and old incense gone sour.

"Mother Quill will want to know we've come," Jude murmured. "She always knows."

"Then let's not keep her waiting," Serah said, and stepped inside.

* * *

The sanctuary below the city was larger than its name suggested and poorer than its legends claimed. Serah had raided places like this before — or places she had *thought* were like this, unregistered mage-holes in the Warrens, shuttered charm-shops, a cellar once where a hedge-witch had been boiling moonwater. Those places had been squalid, furtive, full of people who scattered like roaches when the door came down.

This was different. This was a nave.

Someone had carved it from the bones of an old cistern, vaulted arches dripping mineral tears, and lined it with benches made from shipping crates and coffin lids. Candles burned in niches where saints had never stood — dozens of them, hundreds, wax stalactites growing down the walls like pale roots. Children slept under blankets in the transept. An old man mended nets by lantern-light. A woman with burn-scarred hands stirred something in a cauldron that smelled, impossibly, of bread.

And everywhere, everywhere, the quiet hum of magic that no ward had ever been built to find, because no one had ever imagined this many small magics gathered in one place and choosing gentleness.

A blind woman sat near the fire-pit at the center, hands folded, face turned toward them before they'd made a sound. Her eyes were white as boiled milk.

"You're bleeding on my floor, witch-hunter," Mother Quill said. "Mind the mosaic."

Serah looked down. Her palms were scraped raw from the vault's iron grate; thin lines of red marked the flagstones where she'd knelt. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." The old woman's head tilted. "And you, Jude Kavanagh. You've brought me a storm in a glass bottle. I can hear it humming from here."

"It wouldn't stay in the vault," Jude said.

"No." Something moved across Mother Quill's face that might have been a smile. "They never do. Sit. Both of you. The fire is small but it is ours, and tonight that is worth more than a crown."

* * *

They were given a corner behind the baptistery — a dry alcove walled off with hanging sacking, two pallets of straw and blanket, a privacy the cathedral offered the way poor people offer everything: completely, and expecting nothing back. Someone brought them stew. Someone else brought clean bandages for Serah's hands. A girl of perhaps eight appeared at the sacking curtain, stared at Serah with open, unafraid curiosity, said "Mama says you're the hunter who didn't shoot," and vanished before Serah could find her voice.

Now the cathedral had settled into its night-quiet. Distant coughing, the shuffle of the watch rotation, the eternal drip of the cistern walls. Between them, in a ring of stones, burned a fire no bigger than two cupped hands — real flame, fed by real wood someone had carried down through the rain, because even here, especially here, some things were kept honest.

Serah sat with her back to the wall and her boots off, and discovered she could not remember the last time she had sat with her boots off.

Jude sat across the fire. He had the shard in his hands again — the second one, the cold one, turning it over and over like a man worrying a tooth. The light inside it moved when he moved, a slow silver swirl, and it lit his face from below so that he looked younger and older at once.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well in rooms with other people's hopes in them." He said it lightly, but it landed heavy, and she saw him realize it had. "That came out wrong."

"No," Serah said slowly. "I think it came out exactly right."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the nave, a child cried out in a dream and was hushed.

"I spent today committing treason," she said. It felt important to say it plainly, in words, aloud, to another person. "I broke into the Chancellor's own archive. I stole Crown property. There is a warrant with my face on it, and by tomorrow morning every hunter in the capital will have memorized it, and half of them trained me." She flexed her bandaged hands. "Do you know what the strangest part is?"

"Tell me."

"It doesn't feel like falling. Everyone warns you it'll feel like falling. It feels like *setting something down*." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Ten years I carried that badge like it was made of lead, and I told myself the weight was the honor. Tonight I put it down and I keep reaching for it and it isn't there, and all I feel is lighter. What does that make me?"

Jude was quiet a long moment. The shard's light swirled.

"It makes you someone who finally checked whether the floor was real," he said. "I did the same thing three years ago. Different direction, same drop." He turned the shard in his fingers. "You know what I was doing the night the stars went out? Selling a widow her own grief back as a charm. Small crime. Careful crime. I had rules — never take much, never be seen, never matter. Three years of being nobody on purpose." His mouth twisted. "And then the sky went black, and a dying fae woman put a piece of my own theft in my hand and called me Covenant-Bearer, and every rule I ever made turned out to be a cage I'd built around a life I was afraid to have."

"The hooded stranger," Serah said. "You told me. In the vault, while you picked the lock. The one whose memory started all this."

"He begged me to take it. I've been turning that over for days. Why him? Why a memory-thief with no name and no faction?" He shook his head. "Mother Quill says the covenant chooses its bearers the way rivers choose their courses. Not the strongest. The lowest."

"The lowest," Serah repeated.

"The path of least resistance." He glanced up, and the firelight caught something wry and tired in his eyes. "Charming, isn't it. Chosen for our humility."

Despite everything — the warrant, the hunt, the ache in her hands — Serah laughed. It surprised her coming out, rough and real, and Jude smiled like he'd won something.

* * *

The fire burned lower. She should have slept. Instead she heard herself asking, "What was it? Your first theft. Before the stranger. Before any of this."

Jude went still in the way people go still around wounds.

"My mother," he said at last. "When I was fourteen. She was sick — the lung-rot, from the dye-works. By the end she didn't know me most days. But she had these hours, evenings mostly, when she'd come back to herself, and she'd tell me about her village. Up north. Apple orchards. A river you could drink from. Snow that squeaked." His voice stayed level with terrible care. "She was terrified of losing those memories before she lost everything else. So one night I took them. All of them. Every apple, every snowfall. Put them in a locket so they'd survive her."

"What happened to the locket?"

"Witch-hunters burned it. During the purges, when I was sixteen. I watched it go into the brazier." He looked at the fire. "So you see, Vane, I learned early what your Crown does with things people love. I just chose the coward's war after that. Hide, hoard, survive." He met her eyes across the flames. "Until a hunter with a conscience went rogue and dragged me into the honest kind."

"I didn't drag you."

"You cornered me in a fish market and stole an hour of my afternoon."

"*You* stole an hour of *my* memory first."

"And left you something kind instead," he said. "Which you noticed. Which is why we're here."

Serah looked at him — this thief, this liar by trade, this man who had taken a piece of her mind and replaced it with gentleness — and felt the last argument she'd been having with herself quietly lose.

"When you took that hour," she said. "In the Lantern District. What did you leave me?"

He hesitated. "Why?"

"Because I got it back, Jude. In fragments, from my notes, from the shape of the hole. And there was something in the edges of it. A feeling with no event attached. Like — " she searched — "like the moment after rain stops. Anticipation. As if something good were about to happen and I'd forgotten what." She leaned forward. "Was that you? Did you put that in my head?"

The firelight showed her his ears going red.

"It's not — it doesn't work like planting," he said. "When you take a memory you leave a scar, and the scar fills with whatever's nearest the wound. I was looking at you. That's all. You were furious and brilliant and you had rain in your hair, and the hour I took was an hour of looking at you, so that's what filled the gap." He shrugged, defensive. "It wasn't a trick. It was leakage."

"Leakage," Serah said.

"Professional term."

"Is it still leaking?"

The question hung between them over the small fire, and she watched him decide — watched the thief's reflexes fire and fail, one after another, deflection, joke, retreat — until nothing was left but the truth.

"Yes," he said quietly.

* * *

Later — how much later she couldn't say — the fire had burned to embers and neither of them had moved to feed it. They had talked the way people talk when the talking has finally become easier than the silence: about the purge edicts, about Marrow's grey-faced soldiers sweeping the lower quarters, about the children sleeping thirty feet away who would be ash if the hunters found this place. About the six shards, and the four still missing, and the rite neither of them fully understood.

About the cost. He had told her that much, at least — that the spire would demand everything the bearer carried. She had watched him say it and watched him flinch from what it meant, and she had reached across the embers and taken his hand, and he had held on like a drowning man.

Now her head had found his shoulder, somewhere in the talking, and neither of them had remarked on it, which was its own kind of vow.

"Do you hear that?" Jude murmured.

She listened. Under the drip of the cistern, under the breathing of the hidden city, there was something else — faint, rhythmic, rising from the deep galleries below. A sound like a heartbeat, if a heartbeat could be made of starlight.

"The shards," she said.

"All of them. Ours and the ones still out there. They're calling to each other." He lifted the scrap of glass, and inside it the two lights had drawn together, swirling in a slow double helix, brightening and dimming in time with that buried pulse. "Like they remember being whole."

Serah watched the light turn, and felt the warmth of his shoulder against hers, and understood suddenly what Mother Quill had meant about fires that are ours. Everything she had belonged to the Crown for ten years — her badge, her blade, her days, her name. This was the first thing in a decade that was simply *hers*: a corner behind a baptistery, an ember, a thief's heartbeat under her ear, a sky in a bottle learning to be whole.

"Jude," she said. "The pulse. Is it getting faster?"

He didn't answer at once. Then he sat forward, and she felt him go taut beneath her cheek, and followed his gaze down to the glass.

The lights inside weren't swirling anymore. They were straining — both of them leaning hard toward the cathedral's eastern gallery, toward the deep places, toward that buried rhythm, the way compass needles lean toward a pole. And beneath the pulse, faint but unmistakable, threading up through the stone like smoke through a keyhole, came a sound that froze the blood in Serah's hunter-trained veins.

Singing.

Many voices. Far below. Fae voices, old and hungry and patient, singing in the dark under the city — and getting louder.

"Mother Quill," Jude breathed, already on his feet. "Wake Mother Quill. Something's found us first."
