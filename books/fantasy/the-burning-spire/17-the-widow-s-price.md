## 17. The Widow's Price

The Lantern District smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet—a green, metallic promise hanging over the cobbles—and Jude walked through it with his hood down for once, because hiding had started to feel like a habit he could no longer afford.

Three days since Mother Quill had spoken his fate into the smoke. *Six shards, thief. Six debts. You know where they sleep.* He did. He'd always known where they slept; that was the whole architecture of his crime. A memory-thief keeps a ledger whether he wants one or not, and every name in his was a door he'd once opened without knocking.

Maren Ostler's door came first.

He stood before it longer than he'd stood before any lock in his life. It was a plain door—grey wood, iron hinges gone orange with rust, a horseshoe nailed above the lintel facing the wrong way, which meant she'd hung it herself after the funeral and gotten it backwards, and no one had corrected her because there was no one left to correct her. The paint below the handle was worn pale in an oval. Her hand, touching it every day. Coming home to a house that only held her.

He had taken from this woman on the worst night of her life. That was the arithmetic he couldn't escape. Not the worst night of her life by accident—the way a fever takes a lung—but by design, because grief made memories loose in their sockets, easy to lift, and he'd told himself for three years that taking the *edge* off sorrow was a kindness. That he'd left her the love and removed only the pain. He'd believed it right up until the night the stars went out and he'd learned what else lived inside the things he stole.

He knocked.

* * *

She opened the door like a woman who had been expecting worse. Her eyes went to his face first, not his hands—she remembered him, then. Of course she did. He'd worn a different face that night, a softened one, a borrowed kindness, but grief has better eyes than most.

"You," she said.

"Me," he said. "Mistress Ostler. My name is Jude Kavanagh. I'm the man who robbed you."

Something moved behind her face—not surprise. She'd had three years to suspect, maybe. Grieving people do their own arithmetic too.

"And you've come back because?"

"Because I took something that was never mine to take. And I want to give it back." He kept his hands visible, open, the way you'd approach a spooked animal. "All of it. The night Tamrin died. Every hour of it. What I took and what I left."

Her jaw tightened. "You're going to stand on my step and tell me you can hand me back my son's dying?"

"I can hand you back your son," Jude said quietly. "That's the part I never understood until recently. I thought I was taking pain. I wasn't. I was taking him."

The rain finally arrived, fine as sifted flour. She looked at it, looked at him, and stepped aside.

Inside, the house was a museum of a boy who had grown into a man and then stopped. Carved wooden birds on every shelf—Tamrin had been a whittler, Jude remembered now, remembered with a sick lurch because he hadn't learned that from her, he'd learned it from *inside her*, from the hour he'd siphoned away. A coat on a hook by the stove that no one wore. Bread rising under a cloth, because Maren Ostler still baked for two and gave the second loaf to the temple poor, and if that wasn't grief transmuted into something holy, Jude didn't know what was.

She sat across from him at the table and folded her hands like a woman settling in for a hard bargain, because she was.

"Before you do whatever it is you do," she said, "I want my price."

"I owe you anything."

"No." Her voice didn't waver. "Not anything. One thing. You take memories, yes? You find them, you carry them, you sell them like a fishmonger sells herring." She leaned forward. "Then find me his last words."

Jude went still.

"He was found at the river gate," she said. "They told me he fell. They told me it was quick. But Tamrin wrote me letters his whole life—even when he lived four streets away, even when we saw each other daily, he *wrote*. Words were how he loved. And I have spent three years knowing that somewhere between the river gate and the cold, my son said something—or wanted to say something—and no one heard it, or someone heard it and didn't think it mattered." Her eyes were dry and terrible. "Find his last words, Jude Kavanagh. Bring them to me. Then take back your theft, if you still want it. That's the price."

"Mistress Ostler—" He stopped. Started again. "Memories don't survive in the air. When a person dies, what they held goes with them, unless—"

"Unless someone stole it first." She said it flatly, without cruelty, and it landed like a hammer anyway. "You were working the river district that week. Everyone knew it, even if no one could prove it. If anyone in this city might be carrying a piece of my son's final hour, thief, it's you."

And God help him, he checked. Right there at her table, eyes half-lidded, fingers resting on the scarred wood, he turned inward and walked the long corridors of everything he'd ever lifted, looking for a young man's voice, river water, the taste of fear.

He found nothing. Nothing of Tamrin. He'd never touched the boy.

But he found something else, something he'd carried so long it had worn smooth as a river stone, and when he understood what it was, his breath went out of him all at once.

He knew where Tamrin Ostler's last words were.

They had never been in Tamrin's head at all.

* * *

The hooded stranger had come to him three years ago on just such a wet night, and Jude had stolen from him the sight of the Burning Spire whole—that he knew now, that was the first shard, the revelation that had cracked his small criminal life open like an egg. But the stranger's mind had held more than the Spire. Minds always hold more than what they guard on purpose. And among the stranger's memories, filed away like a letter misdelivered, was an evening on the river wall with a young whittler who talked too fast about birds and boats and his mother's bread.

A courier. That was what the stranger had been doing in the city, in part—carrying messages the ordinary posts wouldn't touch. And one of those messages had been spoken aloud, confidentially, to a stranger on the river wall who had seemed safe, who had seemed kind, and it had lodged in the stranger's memory like a seed in a crevice:

*Tamrin Ostler, telling a hooded man he'd never met that he'd found something at the border stones, something wrong, something the wardens were hiding—and that if anything happened to him, tell my mother it wasn't the fever she thinks, and tell her the last bird I carved isn't finished on purpose. Tell her to look underneath.*

Jude sat in Maren Ostler's kitchen with his heart slamming against his ribs, because the dead boy's last words had been sitting in a thief's skull for three years, one stolen room away from the woman who needed them, and no power in heaven or under the hills had arranged that—it was just the world's particular cruelty, or its particular mercy, and he no longer knew the difference.

But there was more. There was always more, now. Because Tamrin had also shown the stranger the thing he'd found at the border stones—he'd pressed it into the man's gloved hand, a sliver of something that glowed—and the stranger, practical man that he was, had hidden it the only way he knew how to hide anything.

He'd asked a memory-thief to bury it inside a mind too small to matter.

Jude closed his eyes. *Mother Quill's smoke had said six shards, six debtors.* He'd assumed the shard would be in Maren's own memories, in the hours he'd taken from her. He'd braced himself to open her grief like a surgeon. But the ledger had another page. The shard wasn't in what he'd stolen from her.

It was in what he'd stolen *for* her, without ever knowing it.

"Tell me about the birds," he said hoarsely.

Maren blinked. "What?"

"The carvings. The last one. Is it still here?"

"It's—" She rose, puzzled, wary, and lifted a wooden wren from the shelf above the stove, the newest one, the one whose wings were rough-hewn, unfinished. "It was on him when they brought him home. I keep meaning to finish it and I can't. I can't touch his knives."

"May I?"

She put it in his palm like handing over an organ.

He turned it over. The base was hollowed—whittlers do that, weight the feet—and something had been set into the cavity and sealed over with wax and sawdust, invisible unless you knew to look underneath. Tamrin's words, made wood and wax. *Tell her to look underneath.* He'd known. Some part of that bright, letter-writing boy had known exactly how short his road was going to be, and he had built his last message into the last thing his hands would make, and then he'd sent a copy of it into the world through a stranger's mouth, trusting strangers to carry it, because words were how he loved and he'd loved twice over, just in case.

Jude picked the seal away with a pin from his sleeve. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a splinter of light no longer than a fingernail, humming against his skin like a struck bell that refused to fade.

The second shard. Warm. Patient. Waiting three years in a dead boy's unfinished bird for a thief to stop being a coward.

"Oh," Maren said softly. She had seen it. The glow lit her face from below, and in that light she looked suddenly much older and much younger at once. "Oh, Tam. Oh, you clever, careful boy."

* * *

He gave her back the rest first.

It took an hour, kneeling at her table with her hands in his, unwinding the theft thread by thread—the night of the fever, the running through the rain, the ugly mercy he'd performed in believing he was merciful. He felt her take each piece back the way dry ground takes water, and he felt, beneath it, the thing he'd actually done: he had stolen three years of her mourning's first raw season, and mourning skipped like a needle over all that grooved time, and she'd had to grieve without the floor under her.

When it was done she sat very still, tears running freely now, saying nothing, holding her son's whole absence again in both hands.

"I'm sorry," Jude said. "There's no payment that covers it. But here is what I found, and it was always yours."

And he told her. Not the memory—the words. He spoke Tamrin's last message aloud, slowly, in the river-wall cadence the stranger had carried, and Maren Ostler listened to her son's voice arrive three years late, and she did not cry harder. She laughed. Once, brokenly, at *the last bird isn't finished on purpose*, and then she pressed the little wren to her chest and wept the way people weep when something long stuck finally comes free.

"He knew," she whispered. "He knew and he didn't tell me because he didn't want me to worry. That stupid, wonderful boy. He wrote me letters his whole life so I'd never have to miss him early."

"Yes," Jude said, because there was nothing else.

She looked up at him then, and her eyes had changed—not soft, exactly. Cleared. Like glass after hail.

"You'll take the light-thing, then. The shard." She said the word carefully; the city's new terror had names now, and everyone pretended not to know them. "I can feel it humming. It doesn't belong in a house of grief." She pushed it across the table toward him, still cradled in the unfinished wren. "Take it. Take the bird too. Finish it someday, when the world lets you sit still. That's the price. That's all. Someone should carve it who has years ahead of him."

Jude took the shard, and the moment his skin closed around it, it sank home—not into his pocket, but into him, into the deep vault where the first shard already burned, and two lights flared together in the dark of his skull and he *saw*, for half a heartbeat, a pattern: six points, a constellation missing four stars, and threads running from each point outward into the city, into sleeping minds, into debts.

Two down. Four to go. And one of the remaining threads ran, he realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the rain, toward the Lantern District's registered rolls—toward a name the Crown had flagged, a hunter whose hour he'd stolen and whose notes he'd read by candlelight while she slept.

Serah Vane's reconstruction of her missing hour had led her to him once already. Now the third thread was pulling him toward *her*—toward whatever fragment of the covenant slept behind her grey eyes, in whatever corner of her mind his theft had touched.

He was still standing in Maren Ostler's doorway, the wooden wren tucked inside his coat, when he saw the lanterns.

Not the amber ones of the watch. White ones. Hunter-white, moving in formation through the rain-slick street below, six of them, converging—not on this house, not yet, but on the district. On the quarter. And nailed to the temple post across the lane, ink still bleeding in the wet, a broadsheet bearing the Chancellor's seal:

*BY ORDER OF THE LORD CHANCELLOR: ALL UNREGISTERED PERSONS OF THE LOWER QUARTERS TO PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR EXAMINATION AT DAWN. SHELTERING THE TAINED IS TREASON.*

Dawn was four hours away. And Serah Vane was somewhere inside that net of white lanterns, hunting or hunted, he no longer knew which.

Jude pulled his hood up, held the dead boy's unfinished bird against his heart, and went down into the rain.
