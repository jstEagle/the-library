## 18. Marrow's Bargain

The Chancellor's study had no windows.

This was not an accident of architecture. Aldric Marrow had designed it that way, thirty years ago, when he was a young man with a surveyor's compass and a grudge against the sky. Windows admitted weather, and weather admitted mood, and mood was a leak in any vessel of state. A ruler who felt the wind on his face would one day feel doubt there too. So he had bricked them over, plastered them smooth, and hung maps where the glass had been—maps of the capital, of the borderlands, and now, pinned up only last week with silver nails that hummed faintly if you touched them, a map of the heavens with every star crossed out in red ink.

He stood before it tonight in his dressing gown, a cup of cold chicory forgotten in his hand, and counted the crossed-out stars the way other men counted coins.

Four hundred and eleven. He had expected more. The old texts spoke of ten thousand anchors, ten thousand burning nails pinning the veil shut between the mortal realm and the Otherworld, but the texts had been written by priests, and priests rounded upward out of piety. Four hundred and eleven was what the covenant had actually cost. Four hundred and eleven small fires, guttering now, one by one, like candles in a draft.

Three had gone out while he dressed for bed. He had felt each one as a faint pressure behind his eyes, a tooth-ache of the soul, and he had smiled at his reflection in the shaving glass until his cheeks ached worse than his head did.

A knock at the door. Soft. Three beats, then two—the pattern they had agreed upon, because even now, especially now, some courtesies were worth keeping.

"Enter," Marrow said, without turning.

The door opened onto darkness, and darkness entered.

It wore a shape tonight—a tall shape, robed in grey silk that moved like smoke poured over glass, and beneath the cowl its face was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: smooth, depthless, and hiding things that drowned. It carried the cold of the far side of the veil with it the way a traveler carries road dust. The candles nearest the door bent their flames toward it, straining like dogs on leashes.

"My lord Vaelith," Marrow said. "You're punctual."

"I am always punctual." The voice was a low bell rung underwater. "Punctuality is the last courtesy your kind understands. I save my strangeness for matters that deserve it."

Marrow turned at last and gestured to the chair across from his desk. It was a good chair, oak and leather, built for men. Vaelith regarded it the way a heron regards a puddle, then flowed past it and stood instead by the cold hearth, where the shadows gathered thickest around him like courtiers.

"You've come about the spire."

"I've come about the *rate*," Vaelith said. "The stars fall slower than promised. My court grows impatient. There are those among us—" the cowl tilted, an affectation of a glance, "—who say I have traded our birthright for a human's *patience*."

"Your court can wait or your court can starve." Marrow set down his cup and folded his hands. He had learned long ago that the trick to negotiating with the fae was the same as negotiating with dockworkers, generals, and wives: never blink first, and never apologize for arithmetic. "The wards are falling exactly as fast as I can make them fall without the whole city rioting. You want me to tear down four hundred years of protection in a night? They'd burn the lower quarters before dawn, and burned quarters pay no taxes, and panicked kings do stupid things—like reopening negotiations with your queen."

"Maelis is dying," Vaelith said. "Her court is a candle-end. What is there to negotiate?"

"That's precisely why she mustn't be provoked into heroics. A dying queen with nothing to lose is more dangerous than a healthy one with everything." Marrow permitted himself a thin smile. "Let her fade. Let her court bicker over the succession until the succession is ash. By the time anyone over there thinks to act, the covenant will be mine, and there will be nothing left to bargain for."

Vaelith was silent for a long moment. In the silence, Marrow heard the house settle around them—the groan of old beams, the tick of the mantel clock—and beneath both, faintly, a sound like wind moving through a forest that no longer existed. The fae lord's presence leaked. It always did. Marrow found it distasteful, the way he found all leakage distasteful: magic, grief, love, weather. Things that refused to stay where they were put.

"You speak of my realm's death with a clerk's calm," Vaelith said at last.

"I speak of everything with a clerk's calm. That is why you came to me."

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it? That was the joke he kept locked behind his teeth, the one he would tell no confessor, no mistress, no diary. The exiled lord of a dying court had crossed the veil looking for a weapon and found a bookkeeper.

They had met two years ago, in this room, on a night much like this one. Vaelith had come expecting to terrify him. Marrow had offered him tea.

The fae had stared at the teapot as though it might contain a scorpion. "You are not afraid."

"I'm afraid of many things," Marrow had said, pouring. "Debt, chiefly. Dying owing money. Fear of you would be a luxury, my lord, and I don't indulge in luxuries." He had slid the cup across the desk. "Now. You want the veil open. I want the power that made it. I believe we can do business."

He remembered how the cowl had tilted then, too—that same birdlike curiosity. And he remembered what he had said next, because he had rehearsed it for six months before the audience ever came, polishing each clause like a gemstone:

"The covenant binds two realms together so that neither may devour the other. Fine. Noble, even. But a binding requires a *bearer*, my lord—a vessel, a keystone, something that holds the whole arch in place while the mortar sets. Your ancients used a spire and a flame and a line of half-divine custodians, and look where that got you: a rotting court and a queen coughing light into a handkerchief. I propose a modern arrangement. Tear down the old covenant. Let me build the new one. And the new one will have exactly one bearer."

"And that bearer is you."

"That bearer is me."

Vaelith had laughed then—the first and only time. It had sounded like ice calving off a glacier, vast and indifferent and utterly without warmth. "And what do I receive, little clerk, for handing you godhood?"

"A future," Marrow had said. "Your realm is dying because the covenant drains it to feed this one. Every star in your sky is a lamp lit at your expense. Break the covenant, and the drain stops. Reforge it—with me as the conduit rather than the spire—and I will reverse the flow. Not entirely. I'm not a philanthropist. But enough. Enough that your court breathes again. Enough that your exiles can go home."

He had watched the fae lord consider it, and he had known—even then, even in that first hour—that Vaelith would say yes. Not because the terms were generous. Because the terms were *legible*. The fae understood gifts and bargains and riddles, but what they truly knelt to, what their whole glittering treacherous civilization was built upon, was contract. And here, at last, was a human who thought in contracts. Who had read the old treaties—not the sanitized versions in the Crown archive but the true ones, smuggled, banned, bought from a dying collector in the Lantern District at ruinous expense—and understood that the covenant was not divine law at all.

It was terms of service. And terms could be renegotiated by anyone with the nerve to hold the pen.

*

Tonight, Vaelith drifted along the wall of maps, trailing one grey sleeve across the borders of nations as though testing them for dust. "The hunters report a complication," he said.

"There's always a complication. Name it."

"A thief. A memory-thief, registered years ago and forgotten, who has been collecting fragments of the old covenant without knowing what they were. The shards sing when they gather, my Chancellor. Even I can hear them from across the veil. A small bright noise, like a spoon against a glass." The cowl turned. "He has taken one from a dead emissary of Maelis's court. He travels now with a witch-hunter who has gone rogue. Serah Vane. Do you know her?"

"I signed her commission." Marrow crossed to his desk and opened the ledger that lived there under lock and chain—a ledger no clerk but himself was permitted to touch, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling. "Vane. Yes. Best capture record in the Lantern District division. Father hanged for fae trafficking; she overcompensated beautifully. A waste." He made a small notation. "She'll be found. Traitors are easy to find; they're always running toward something."

"And the thief?"

Marrow paused.

The thief. Jude Kavanagh. He had pulled the man's registration file three days ago and read it twice with growing, incredulous delight. A charm-seller. A petty criminal of the smallest possible denomination—stolen hours, borrowed faces, memories sold by the dram to grieving widows and lovesick clerks. The man had spent three years perfecting a talent for *insignificance*. And threaded through that insignificant mind, unknown to its owner, lay pieces of the oldest contract in existence.

It was almost elegant. Almost funny. Somewhere, some custodian of the old order—some desperate priest or fleeing acolyte in the covenant's final days—had hidden the shards inside human minds precisely because human minds were too small, too brief, too *busy* to be searched. A vault disguised as a beggar. And now the vaults were walking around the city buying bread.

"They say he steals memories," Vaelith mused. "That is a fae art, in its cruder form. Perhaps he is one of ours, gone astray."

"He's one of yours in the way a rat is a wolf." Marrow closed the ledger. "But he concerns me, yes. Not for what he is—for what he's carrying. Six shards, my lord. Six keys to the mechanism we spent two years dismantling. If they gather in one place, in one *mind*, they could reforge what we've broken."

"Then kill him."

"Eventually. Not yet." Marrow walked back to the map of heavens and touched one of the red crosses, feeling the faint heat of the dead star beneath his fingertip. "A killed thief is a mystery. A captured thief is an inventory. I want him brought to me alive, and I want the shards extracted intact. I have use for them."

"Use," Vaelith repeated. The word dripped.

"You think I intend to betray you." Marrow said it flatly, without offense, the way one confirms the price of fish. "Of course you do. You'd think less of me if I didn't. So let me be clear about the geometry of our arrangement, my lord, since your court loves clarity above all things." He turned to face the fae fully, and let the mask slip—not into kindness, never that, but into the thing underneath, the thing he showed no living soul. "I do not need to betray you, because I have already won. When the last star falls, your realm goes dark whether I keep my word or not. The flow reverses only if the new covenant is forged, and the new covenant is forged through *me*, which means every year it holds, I grow stronger and you grow more dependent. You have not sold me your birthright, Vaelith. You have sold me a leash with yourself at the end of it. I keep my bargains because my bargains are the most efficient instruments of my own elevation. That is the whole of my honor, and it is worth more than any oath sworn on love or blood, because it does not decay."

For a moment the room grew very cold. Frost feathered up the inside of the bricked-over windows, delicate as lace, and the candle flames shrank to blue points. The shadows around Vaelith thickened until the fae lord seemed less a figure than a hole cut in the world.

"You are a loathsome creature," Vaelith said, and there was something in the underwater bell of his voice that might, in a being capable of it, have been respect.

"Yes," Marrow agreed pleasantly. "And you are a desperate one. We suit each other admirably. Now—the rate. I can give you three more stars this month. The eastern districts first; they're poor, they're purged, and no one with a pen counts their complaints. In exchange, I want your hunters watching the river quarter. The thief will surface there eventually—he's gathering debts, and debts live in poor neighborhoods. When he surfaces, I want him *herded*, not caught. Driven toward the Spire."

"The Spire?" The cowl drew back slightly, and beneath it Marrow glimpsed, just for a heartbeat, the thing that wore the beautiful face: something ancient and starved, all hunger and hollow, eyes like the gaps between constellations. "Why would you want him there?"

"Because the Spire is where the shards want to go," Marrow said, "and I am a great believer in letting other people do the carrying." He smiled, thin and precise as a paper cut. "Let him climb it. Let him assemble every piece. And at the summit, at the moment of forging, I will take the whole mechanism from him at once—shards, flame, covenant, and crown. Why harvest six gems one by one when a fool will deliver them to my door already strung?"

*

Vaelith left as he had come, through the dark, without farewell, taking the cold with him. The frost on the bricked windows melted. The candles stood back up straight, embarrassed, like soldiers after an inspection.

Marrow stood alone in his windowless study and allowed himself, at last, the luxury of thinking about the beginning.

Not the bargain. Before the bargain. Thirty years ago, when he had been a junior archivist with ink-stained cuffs and a head full of forbidden texts, and the Crown had sent him to inventory the sealed annex beneath the old temple. Everyone else who worked that annex had come out changed—haunted, converted, weeping over the beauty of what humanity had lost when it outlawed enchantment. Marrow had gone in a hungry young man and come out *furious*.

Because he had seen the accounting.

The covenant, stripped of its poetry, was a transfer of wealth. Every prayer, every ward, every blessed candle in the mortal realm was paid for out of the Otherworld's substance, and the Otherworld paid willingly, endlessly, because its people were creatures of gift and could conceive of no other economy. For four hundred years, humanity had lived in a house of miracles it neither built nor paid for, maintained by neighbors too generous to send an invoice. And humanity's response—the response of kings, of priests, of the wise and the good—had been to outlaw the neighbors, brick up the doors, and pretend the house stood on its own.

Everyone called the old covenant sacred. Marrow had looked at the ledgers and seen a subsidy. An empire of charity propping up a race of ingrates.

Well. He was not ungrateful. Gratitude was cheap. He was something better: he was *solvent*. He would take the miracle out of the hands of the generous and the undeserving alike, and he would make it earn its keep. One bearer. One account. One hand on the ledger of heaven, and that hand would be his, and it would never, ever write a number it could not collect.

He returned to the desk and drew out the ledger again, opening it to the page marked with a grey ribbon. His own handwriting marched down the columns, neat as scripture: dates, sums, names. Names of warders bribed. Names of priests silenced. Names of the purged, the disappeared, the erased—four hundred souls in the lower quarters already, their memories scraped clean by his hunters' new iron instruments, their minds left smooth as fresh paper. Collateral. Depreciation. The cost of doing business between worlds.

At the bottom of the page, in fresher ink, a single entry waited, underlined twice:

*Kavanagh, J. — assets: 6 (est.). Status: AT LARGE. Disposition: RETRIEVE INTACT.*

Below it, added that evening in his smallest, most careful script, a note to himself, because even a chancellor needed reminders:

*The hooded one's companion. Find what became of her. The sixth shard did not vanish—it was taken, years ago, by someone with steady hands and no conscience. Someone like me.*

He dipped his pen. Outside, beyond the brick and plaster, beyond the grey and starless sky, the third-to-last constellation was going out over the river quarter, and somewhere in the dark beneath it a small bright noise was growing louder—spoon against glass, key against lock, debt coming due.

Marrow wrote one word, blotted it, and closed the book.

"Soon."
