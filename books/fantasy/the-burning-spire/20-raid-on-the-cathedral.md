## 20. Raid on the Cathedral

The first sign was the rats.

Jude had been down in the undercroft, sorting the shards—four of them now, wrapped in linen and candle-wax, each one humming faintly against his palm like a trapped moth—when the rats came pouring up the stairwell in a silent, streaming river. They did not squeal. They did not fight. They simply fled, every whisker flat with terror, and in the Gutter Cathedral Jude had learned that rats were the city's most honest heralds.

He was up the stairs before his mind finished the arithmetic. Through the nave of the old drowned church, where the pews had rotted to velvet softness and the altar was a slab of riverstone worn smooth by two hundred years of secret worship. Candles guttered in their hundreds. Mother Quill sat at the center of it all, sightless eyes turned toward the ceiling, her head tilted the way it did when she listened to something far away.

"They're coming," she said, before he could speak. "Boots. Many. And iron."

Jude's mouth went dry. "How long?"

"Long enough to be afraid. Not long enough to run far." She rose, unhurried, her knotted hands finding her staff by memory. "How many children are sleeping below?"

"Eleven. Twelve with the new one from Tanner's Row."

"Then wake them gently. There is no time for gentle, but do it anyway." She smiled, and it was the same smile she'd given him the night he'd first crawled into her sanctuary, half-starved and full of stolen years. "You were always the fastest thief I ever knew, Jude Kavanagh. Be fast now."

"Come with us. There's a way out through the cistern—"

"I know every way out of my own house, child." Her voice was mild as milk. "But a door only holds if someone stands behind it. Go. Wake them. And tell Serah Vane—" she paused, and something ancient and amused moved across her ruined face, "—tell her the line she's been standing on was never a line at all. It was a bridge. Tell her to cross it."

He wanted to argue. There was no time to argue. He ran.

* * *

Serah heard them before she saw them—the particular cadence of Crown hunters moving through the Warrens, boots in unison, the wet-leather creak of warded harness. She had worn that cadence herself for nine years. She had been the sound in the dark that made mothers pull their children behind doors.

Now she was the mother behind the door.

She stood at the cathedral's flooded entrance, where the old church steps sank into black water and the only approach was a single stone causeway, and she counted lanterns in the dark above: thirty. Forty. More, still spilling down the Warrens' crooked stairs like molten metal finding every low place.

"Serah." Jude's hand on her shoulder, breath ragged. "Quill won't leave. The children are at the cistern gate. We have to go *now*."

"Then go."

"You're not—"

"Someone has to make sure they don't follow you into the dark." She drew her blades, and the sound of steel clearing leather was the most familiar prayer she knew. "I was their best, Jude. Let me be their worst problem."

He caught her wrist. In the candlelight his eyes were wild, and she saw it in him plainly now, the thing she had been refusing to name for weeks—the way looking at him had stopped being a duty and become a hunger. "I just got you," he said. "I'm not losing you to a corridor full of iron."

"Then don't lose me. Hold the thought of me. You're good at holding things." She pulled free, and her voice gentled despite everything. "The cistern gate. If I'm not there in ten minutes, you take the children and the shards and you don't look back. That's the price of the alliance, Kavanagh. Pay it."

The first hunters came around the causeway bend with lanterns high and crossbows leveled, and Serah Vane stepped forward to meet her old life with a new one in each fist.

* * *

The fighting was short and ugly and nothing like the drills.

Serah had been trained in the Warden Halls: forms, counters, the elegant geometry of blade-work on clean floors. What the causeway gave her was three feet of slick stone, water on both sides, and men who came in bunches because bunches felt safe. She gave the first one her elbow and his own momentum, and the river took him. The second she disarmed with a twist that sprained something in her thumb. The third was a boy, sixteen maybe, fae-fear wide in his eyes, and she put the flat of her blade against his throat and said, "Run, and tell them Vane is holding the bridge," and to her lasting surprise, he ran.

"TRAITOR!" The voice cracked down the nave like a whip. Captain Doss—she'd known him when he was a soft-bellied sergeant who cheated at cards—stood at the causeway's far end, and beside him, worse, stood a hunter in a grey hood whose presence made the candle flames bend away as if in disgust. A null. The Crown had fielded a null.

"You're under edict, Vane," Doss called. "Surrender and the Chancellor may let you keep your name."

"My name's the only thing I've got left that's mine," she called back, "so no."

The null raised one hand, and the world went *quiet*. Not silent—quiet, the way a room goes when all the air has been let out. The candles died in a rolling wave. The water stilled. And Serah felt the thing she had carried since the night the stars went out—that faint warm hum beneath her breastbone, the covenant-fragment she hadn't known she was carrying until the gutter cathedral taught her to listen—flicker like a pinched wick.

They had brought a null to smother the sanctuary's magic. Which meant they knew. Which meant Marrow knew the shards were here.

"Jude," she breathed. "*Run*."

* * *

Jude ran.

He ran through the cistern tunnels with twelve children strung out behind him on a rope like beads, the four shards burning against his chest, and behind them—muffled by stone and water and distance—the sounds of the cathedral coming down. Stone on stone. A sound like a great bell struck once and never allowed to finish ringing.

He did not look back. She had made him promise. He hated her a little for making him promise, and loved her so much in the same instant that the two feelings wore grooves in him side by side, like cartwheels on the same road.

The children were silent. That was the worst of it. They had been taught—Quill's last great lesson, drilled into them over candlelit weeks—that silence was the armor of the hunted. So twelve children between six and fourteen years old moved through the black water of the cistern without a whimper, and Jude, who had spent three years perfecting invisibility, understood in his bones that he had never once been as brave as they were being right now.

The smallest one, a girl called Pip with a burn-scarred hand, tugged his sleeve at the second junction. "Mama Quill said you'd know the way to the river gate."

"I know the way."

"She said to tell you—" Pip's face scrunched with the effort of exact memory, the way Quill had taught all of them to hold words. "She said: *the debt comes due to the one who taught you to count*. Do you know what that means?"

Jude's blood went cold. The one who taught him to count. The hooded stranger. The first memory. "I know who it means," he said. "Not what. Not yet."

They came out at the river gate as the first grey suggestion of dawn touched the water, and Serah was not there, and the ten minutes were up, and then they were up, and then fifteen, and Jude stood at the mouth of the tunnel with his promise in one hand and his heart in the other and made the only choice that was left, which was the one she'd told him to make.

"We go," he said, and his voice broke on it. "We go *now*."

* * *

Serah stopped counting hunters after the twentieth.

The null's silence had passed over the cathedral like a lid closing, and in that smothered dark she fought by sound and splash and instinct, and it was almost enough. She got Doss into the water. She got three more onto the stones. She got to the nave's far arch and saw, through the smoke of a hundred murdered candles, Mother Quill standing at the altar with her staff planted and her blind eyes lifted, and around her the grey-hooded null staggering as if the silence he carried had turned in his hands and become a weight.

Because the cathedral itself was refusing.

Serah felt it then, even through the null's smothering: the old drowned church was *full*. Two hundred years of whispered rites and hidden names, of every unregistered mage who had ever lit a candle down here and meant it—the stones had soaked all of it up like the river soaked the rain, and now, with the covenant breaking and the wards of the world gone thin, all of it was rising. The null's silence was a hand pressed over a spring. The spring was winning.

"You should have brought more men," Mother Quill said pleasantly, to the null, to Doss, to the dark. "Or fewer sins."

"Quill," Doss snarled, "step away from the altar. Chancellor's orders. The sanctuary is forfeit."

"The sanctuary," she said, "is not mine to forfeit. Nothing in this house has belonged to me for a long time. I only kept the candles lit." She turned her ruined eyes toward Serah, and though she could not possibly have seen her, Serah felt the look land like a hand on her cheek. "Vane. You're still standing on my bridge."

"Quill, *come with me*—"

"Child, I am one hundred and four years old, I am blind, and my knees sound like the Warrens stairs in winter." The old woman smiled. "The only thing I have left to give is a few minutes, and I intend to spend them extravagantly. Go."

The null screamed—a thin, furious sound, the sound of a man losing his grip on something enormous—and the silence tore. Every dead candle in the cathedral flared at once, blue-white, starlight-colored, and Mother Quill raised her staff and *gave*.

Serah felt it go through her like a tide. Two centuries of hoarded small magics, released in a single breath: a charm against iron, a blessing on doorways, a mother's wish spoken over a fevered child in the dark. The hunters were thrown from the causeway like leaves off a roof. The arch above the altar groaned and shed its stones in a deliberate, falling curtain, sealing the nave, sealing the old woman in with her enemies and her God knew what else.

The last thing Serah saw, through the dust and the dying blue light, was Mother Quill turning back toward the altar as if toward a hearth, staff in hand, unhurried, her voice carrying through the ruin with terrible mildness:

"Shut the door behind you, Vane. And mind the children."

Then the stone came down, and the light went out, and Serah Vane ran.

* * *

They regrouped at the tannery ruins a mile downstream, in a cellar that smelled of lime and old blood, and Jude counted them in like a man counting his own ribs: twelve children. Four shards. One witch-hunter, arriving last, soaked to the bone, with dust in her hair and a look on her face that he had seen before—on soldiers, on widows, on anyone who had walked out of a burning building while the people inside stayed.

"Serah—"

"Don't." She sat down hard against the wall. Her hands, he noticed, would not stop moving—checking blades that were already sheathed, straightening straps that were already straight. "Don't say anything kind yet. I can't—give me a minute where nothing is kind."

So he didn't say anything. He sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and put the shards on the floor between them, and one by one the children crept close and made a ring, and Pip climbed into Serah's lap without asking, because children know things, and Serah's hands finally went still around her.

"She held the door," Pip said, into the silence. "Mama Quill held the whole door."

"Yes," Serah said. Her voice was rough as a whetstone. "She did."

Jude looked down at the four shards, wrapped in their linen, humming their small trapped songs. Four of six. The fifth in the mind of an exiled fae lord across the broken border. The sixth—Mother Quill had told them, that last night, in her mild terrible way—in the head of the Lord Chancellor himself.

"They'll hunt us with everything now," Serah said. "We're not fugitives anymore. We're the story. Marrow will make sure every lantern in the city has our faces under it."

"Then we leave the city."

"Through the border. Into the Otherworld." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "With twelve children. Into the land of the things we were trained to hunt."

"Quill said the line was a bridge," Jude said. "She said to tell you to cross it."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, the city bells began to ring—wrong, arrhythmic, the alarm-pattern of martial law, Marrow's voice already being nailed to every door in the capital. She looked down at Pip, asleep against her chest, and at the shards, and last at Jude, and he watched her make the choice the way he'd watched her make every choice since the lantern district: completely, once, without looking back.

"Get the children ready," she said. "We cross at the old ford, tonight, before the wards regrow. And Jude—" her hand found his in the dark, grip hard enough to hurt, which was how he knew it was real, "—when this is over, we build them a cathedral with a door that *opens*. We owe her that."

Above them the bells rang on, and the stars—what few were left—came out over a city that had forgotten how to look up, and Jude Kavanagh, Covenant-Bearer, thief of small things, held the hand of a woman who had burned her whole life down in a single night, and began to plan a crossing into the dark.
