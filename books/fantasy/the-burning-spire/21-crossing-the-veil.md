## 21. Crossing the Veil

The old ford lay three miles north of the Lantern District, where the river Vane—Serah's river, though she'd never claimed it—thinned to a shin-deep whisper over pale stones. In the years before the Edict, lovers had come here to carve their names into the willow roots. Now the willows stood bare as gallows, and the only names written anywhere were the ones Marrow's criers chalked on doors: *PURGED. PURGED. PURGED.*

We came to the water in the last hour before dawn, eleven of us: Serah and I, Mother Quill's eldest apprentice Tam with a baby strapped to his chest, six children ranging from a girl of twelve who walked like a soldier to twins who had not spoken since the raid, old Fen the hedge-witch whose hands shook too badly now to light a candle without flint, and two gutter mages I knew only by their craft-names—Sparrow, who could still small birds, and Wren, who could not.

I carried the shards.

They rode against my sternum in a leather pouch that should have been warm from my body heat and was instead cold as riverbed stone. Four of them now. The widow's grief made light. The stranger's spire made light. The two we'd pulled screaming from Marrow's vault made light. And all that light pressed against my ribs like a second heart, one that beat out of time with mine—*now, now, now*—while my own heart said *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

"Is it true," whispered the soldier-girl, whose name was Bree, "that there's stars on the other side?"

"There were," I said. "Once."

"And there'll be again?"

I looked at her upturned face, at the soot still ground into the creases of her neck from the night the cathedral burned, and I did what thieves do best. I gave her something that wasn't quite a lie.

"That's the whole plan," I said. "Everything else is just walking."

* * *

You have to understand what crossing the border meant, before. Before the wards fell, before the stars went out, before Marrow taught a city to be afraid of its own shadow's shimmer—the veil between the mortal realm and the Otherworld was not a wall. It was a membrane. A skin. The world's own skin, and we lived inside it the way a child lives inside its mother, hearing through the flesh the muffled music of everything larger.

The ford at Greywillow was one of the thin places. Old-timers said you could stand knee-deep at midsummer midnight and feel the Otherworld breathing against your ankles. Children dared each other to sing there, because songs carried strangely across the water—one voice going in, two voices coming out, the second one always a half-beat behind and always, somehow, sadder.

I had stolen memories of that place. A fisherman's boyhood, once: the taste of elderflower wine, the shock of cold water, and beneath it all a low sweet hum he'd never been able to name, the sound of the other side pressing close. I'd sold that memory for three silver marks and never thought about it again until tonight, wading into the shallows with the hum rising up through my boots to meet me.

Except it wasn't humming anymore.

It was *moaning.*

"Do you hear that?" Tam asked. The baby on his chest had begun to cry—not the red-faced outrage of hunger but the thin, continuous keening of an infant who knows something is wrong in the way that adults have paid sorcerers lifetimes to learn.

"I hear it," Serah said. Her hand had gone to the sword she no longer had a right to carry, found nothing, and closed on empty air. She hated that. I watched her hate it, and loved her for how she turned the hatred into motion instead of paralysis. "Everyone stay together. Jude—"

"I know."

"You don't. You've never crossed."

"No," I admitted. "But I've spent three years learning what broken things look like from the inside. That's nearly the same education."

She almost smiled. In the grey pre-dawn, with the moaning water around our knees, it was enough.

We crossed.

* * *

There is no honest way to describe stepping through the veil when the veil is dying, because every description assumes a border, and borders are things with sides. This was not a line you passed. It was a wound you entered.

One moment the world was the world: river, stones, willow stumps, the smudged orange glow of the capital's watchfires to the south. The next moment the air changed texture—became, I can only say, *older*—and the light went strange. Not dark. The Otherworld was not dark; that would have been merciful. It was *dim*, the way a room is dim when the curtains are drawn against a sun that is still, somewhere, burning. Everything was visible and nothing was lit. Colors existed the way ghosts exist: recognizably, reluctantly, from far away.

And the land itself—

Gods forgive me, the land was beautiful, and that was the first wrongness. Grass grew silver-green and tall as wheat, bending in a wind we couldn't feel. Trees stood in perfect rows that no forester had planted, their bark patterned like script in a language I almost—*almost*—could read, the way you almost remember a dream. Hills rolled away to a horizon where mountains floated slightly above the ground they should have been standing on, their roots trailing mist.

It was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful. Everything correct. Nothing alive.

"It's dimming," Fen said. The old witch had gone very still, her shaking hands pressed flat together as if in prayer or restraint. "When I was a girl, the stories said you could see the Otherworld's sky from the mortal one on clear nights—that the aurora was them looking back at us. Look up."

We looked up.

No stars. Of course no stars; the stars had gone out everywhere, the covenant's anchors pulled down by Marrow's treachery. But here, under a sky the color of deep water, the absence had weight. The darkness overhead didn't sit above the land—it *pressed* on it, and I understood with a thief's instinct for hidden mechanisms that what we were seeing was a realm slowly being crushed by the loss of its own covenant. The stars hadn't merely been lights. They had been *load-bearing*.

"The whole sky is a ceiling," Sparrow whispered, "and someone took out the pillars."

Bree reached up as if she could touch it. Tam caught her wrist gently and pulled her hand down, and she let him, which told me more about her fear than any words would have.

* * *

We walked.

The Otherworld does not measure distance the way ours does—I learned this within the hour, when a hill we'd been approaching steadily for what felt like a mile remained exactly the same distance away, patient as a painting. Then, when we stopped arguing about it and simply sat down to rest, it arrived all at once, looming, as if it had been waiting for us to stop watching.

The children noticed first. Children always notice first.

"The ground is listening," said one of the twins—the girl, I thought, though they were identical in their silence. It was the first word either of them had spoken since the raid. Tam's head came up like a hound's.

"What do you mean, love?"

"When we walk loud, the grass lies flat after. Like it's tired." She demonstrated, pointing at a swath of silver-green stalks bent in the exact shape of our passage, though the wind we couldn't feel had touched nothing else. "When we walk quiet, it stands up. It doesn't like noise."

Wren, who could not speak at all, signed rapidly: *the land is grieving.* And then, after a moment, hands hesitant: *or starving.*

Both, I thought. Both at once.

Because here was the second wrongness, the one that settled into my bones and stayed: the dimness wasn't uniform. As we walked, I began to feel it the way I felt memories—in patches, in textures. Near a stream that ran clear as glass but made no sound, the light was a little stronger, the colors a little truer. Around a grove of trees whose branches held what might have been lanterns, or fruit, or sleeping birds—something that glowed faintly gold—the dimness receded entirely, and for thirty paces we walked through something like the world as it was meant to be, and all of us unconsciously straightened, breathed deeper, moved faster, the way flowers turn.

Then we passed beyond the grove, and the grey came down again, and Bree started crying without seeming to know she was doing it.

"It's not dying evenly," I said to Serah, low. "It's dying in pieces. Whatever fed this place—" I touched the pouch at my chest, and the shards pulsed against my palm like small trapped birds, "—it's been cut off for weeks. Everything is running on what it stored."

"Like a city under siege," Serah said. She would think in those terms. She'd been raised by the Crown's war-college before the Crown taught her to hunt witches instead. "Rationing. Falling back to strongpoints."

"Or like a body," I said, "shutting down limb by limb to keep the heart beating."

She looked at me. "Which parts are the limbs?"

Ahead of us, half-lost in the silvered haze, stood a village. Or what had been a village. Houses of white stone and living wood, graceful even in ruin—but empty, doors open, tables set with meals grown grey and stiff as wax. Whatever had lived there had left, or faded, or—

Or been called away, I thought, remembering the voices the cold wind had carried on the night the stars went out. Remembering the emissary who had died in my shop pressing a shard of light into my palm with the last of her strength, saying *Covenant-Bearer* like a title and *find them* like a prayer.

The fae were gathering. Somewhere. To their queen. To their court. And they were leaving the land behind them to starve.

* * *

We sheltered that night—or what the Otherworld offered instead of night, a slow thickening of the dimness that felt less like darkness than like held breath—in the shell of a house whose walls were woven from something between timber and bone, warm to the touch even now.

Fen built a fire from wood that burned blue-green and gave off heat without smoke, and the children crowded to it, and for a little while the room held something like peace. Tam sang—very quietly, because of the grass—and the baby slept, and Serah sat beside me with her shoulder against mine and her eyes on the door, because she was Serah, and would keep watch over a room full of ghosts in a dying world because someone had to and because she had decided, apparently, that the someone was her.

"Your hands are shaking," she said.

"They've been shaking since the vault."

"Liar. They've been shaking since the raid. Since Quill." She didn't look at me. We had not yet found words for Mother Quill, for the sound the cathedral door had made when it came down, for the shape of the seer's body holding the line so that children could run. "Talk to me instead. What did you feel out there? At the crossing."

I considered lying to her, briefly, out of habit. Three years of invisibility builds habits like scar tissue. But she had burned her life down in a single night, and the least I owed her was the truth.

"I felt guilty," I said. "Crossing felt like walking into a sickroom wearing muddy boots. Every step, I kept thinking: we did this. Not you and me. Humans. Mortals. Marrow and everyone who looked away while he built his edicts, and everyone who ever sold a charm or broke a ward or—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Everyone like me. Small crimes. I told myself they were small crimes. Never take much, never be seen. And the whole time the membrane between worlds was getting thinner, and I was one of ten thousand needles making pinholes."

"Jude."

"Don't. I know the arithmetic. Marrow broke it deliberately. One man's treason outweighs ten thousand petty thefts. I *know*." I turned the shard-pouch over in my hands, watching the light bleed through the leather seams. "But knowing isn't the same as feeling, and what I feel is that this place was singing before we ruined it, and I never even listened."

Serah was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she used for the hardest truths, the ones she'd learned to deliver to families of the condemned.

"When I was nine," she said, "my grandmother told me about the veil. Just a story—old women weren't supposed to tell those anymore, but she did. She said the Otherworld wasn't another country. It was the memory of the world before we decided we were alone in it. And she said—" Serah's jaw tightened. "She said the day humans forgot the fae entirely would be the day both worlds died, because a thing remembered is a thing anchored. I reported her, Jude. Twelve years old, model student, and I reported my own grandmother for telling a forbidden story. They purged her memory of it. They purged her memory of *me telling on her*, because the procedure doesn't distinguish. She died two winters later not knowing why her granddaughter never visited."

The fire burned blue-green between us. Somewhere outside, the silent stream went on running without a sound.

"You're telling me this why?" I asked softly.

"Because you're doing the arithmetic wrong." She finally looked at me, and her eyes in the strange firelight were the color of the sky here, deep water over deep water. "You're counting your sins and forgetting the machine that taught you to sin quietly and taught me to inform and taught everyone to look down. Marrow didn't break the covenant in one night. He spent twenty years teaching a whole city to stop looking up. Your pinholes, my reports, the neighbor who didn't report—we're all just the wear. He's the blade." Her hand found mine, grip hard enough to hurt, which was how I knew it was real. "So carry the guilt if you must, but carry it *forward*. Use it. Guilt pointed backward is just another way of standing still."

I held her hand and watched the fire, and did not tell her that I was memorizing the exact pressure of her fingers, because some part of me already knew—I had known since the queen's emissary died, since the shards began to sing against my ribs—what the rite at the end of this road would cost, and who specifically I would forget paying it.

Some prices you don't announce. You just start saving.

* * *

Near what passed for morning, Bree woke me by shaking my arm, and her face told me everything before her voice did.

"There's lights," she whispered. "On the hill. Lots of them. And they're not fires."

I came out into the dim haze with the others stirring behind me, and I saw them: a procession descending the far hillside, dozens of points of soft gold light moving in formation—not torches, not lanterns, but carried things, borne aloft on poles and pikes and the antlers of stags that walked upright. And beneath the lights, silhouettes. Tall ones. Slender ones. Shapes that moved the way water moves, all at once and without effort.

The fae. The gathered remnant of a court, coming toward five humans and six children crouched in the bones of an abandoned house.

Every story I had ever stolen, every fragment of old fear, rose in my throat at once. The fair folk did not forgive trespass. The fair folk did not forget debt. The fair folk—

The fair folk were *dimming*. Even at this distance I could see it: the light they carried flickered like candles in a draft, and the figures beneath it moved carefully, conserving, the way starving men move. This was not a host riding to war. This was a funeral procession that had run out of road.

Serah stepped up beside me, swordless, lawless, hers. "Is that the court? The queen?"

"I think," I said, and the shards against my chest had begun to pulse—*now, now, now*—faster, brighter, answering something in the approaching lights like a struck bell answering a bell across a valley, "I think the queen has come to meet us halfway."

The foremost figure raised its light, and across the dim miles, across the silent grass, I felt eyes older than the city find me, and find the shards, and *know*.

"Jude," Serah said quietly. "Whatever happens next—"

"I know," I said. "Stay behind me."

She laughed once, short and sharp. "You know I won't."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of you."

And we went out to meet the court of embers.
