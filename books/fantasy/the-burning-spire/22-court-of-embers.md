## 22. Court of Embers

The Otherworld did not want us.

I felt it before I understood it—a pressure against the skin like walking into a river that flowed upward, the whole dim country leaning its weight against our passage as if we were splinters in a wound. The road from the broken border had been a road only by courtesy: a ribbon of pale ground stitched through forests of grey-barked trees whose leaves turned to watch us go. Not metaphorically. They turned. Jude had stopped flinching at such things somewhere around the second hour; I was still learning how.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I said.

"Because the queen sent for me." Jude walked with his hands jammed in his pockets, which was his posture for pretending he wasn't afraid. "Because she's dying, and dying queens get honest. And because we need her court's blessing to reach the Ember Vaults, and there is no version of this where we take them without one."

"There's a version where we take them without asking."

"There's a version where we die of hubris too," he said. "Lots of versions. The multiverse is generous that way."

I let it go because my feet hurt and because he was right, and because the truth underneath both our jokes was this: four covenant-shards rode against my ribs in a leather pouch, warm as sleeping birds, and every mile into this fading land they grew warmer, as if they knew they were going home.

The forest ended without warning. One step we were among the watching trees; the next, the land opened like a held breath released—a valley drowned in twilight, and rising from its center, a city built of ember-light and ash.

That was the first thing I understood about the Court of Embers: it was not burning. It was *remembering* burning. Towers of black glass veined with slow orange light, streets paved in cooled lava gone smooth as river stone, windows glowing like coals banked for a night that had lasted three hundred years. Above it all, where a sky should have been, there was only a vast grey ceiling of cloud—no stars, no moon, nothing but the dim reflection of the city's own dying fire looking down at itself.

"It's beautiful," I said, and hated how much I meant it.

"It's starving," Jude said quietly. "Look at the edges."

He was right. The outer districts were dark. Whole quarters of the city stood cold, their ember-veins gone grey, their windows empty—not ruined, just *off*, like a house where everyone has died but the furniture remains. The light retreated toward the center the way warmth retreats from a corpse.

At the valley's mouth, a bridge crossed a river that did not reflect the city above it. The water ran black and slow, and things moved in it—long shapes, patient shapes—and on the far bank stood the honor guard.

They were not what the stories promised. No gossamer wings, no dew-bright beauty. They were tall and lean and wrong in the way that a heron is wrong when you see it up close—all elegant angles hiding something that eats. Their armor was charcoal and bone, and their faces were beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, and every single pair of eyes found me first.

Not Jude. Me. The human. The hunter.

"They can smell the Order on you," Jude murmured. "Iron. Oath-smoke. Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Stand straighter."

* * *

They took our weapons at the bridge, which I had expected, and took our names, which I had not.

A herald—sexless, silver-eyed, its voice like wind through a keyhole—stood before us with a scroll of grey vellum and asked, in the Old Tongue and then, seeing our blank faces, in accented Common: "Your true names, bearers. The court will have them or it will not have you."

Jude glanced at me. We had discussed this. Names were currency here; names were hooks. But lying at the threshold of a fae court was like lying under oath with your hand on a live wire.

"Jude Kavanagh," he said. "Son of Marta Kavanagh, of the Lantern District, city of Vael, mortal realm."

The herald's quill scratched. Its silver eyes slid to me.

"Serah Vane," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Daughter of Corin Vane. Witch-hunter of the Crown of Vael. Third rank, Order of the Pale Flame."

The silence after I said it had texture. The honor guard shifted. Somewhere behind them, in the dark of the bridge, something laughed—a sound like ice cracking in deep water.

"You name yourself *hunter*," the herald said, "in the court of Maelis Ember-forged?"

"I name myself honestly," I said. "You asked for truth. Here it is, ugly side out."

The herald looked at me for a long moment, and then—it was so small I almost missed it—the corner of its mouth moved. Approval, or appetite. With the fae I would learn the two were often indistinguishable.

"The court will decide what your honesty is worth," it said. "Walk. Do not stop on the bridge. Do not look down at the water. And whatever speaks to you from the river—" the silver eyes flicked, briefly, downward, "—do not answer."

We walked. Halfway across, something in the black water said my name in my mother's voice, and I kept my eyes forward and my teeth together, and Jude's hand found mine in the dark between us and stayed there until the far bank rose to meet us.

* * *

The throne room of the Court of Embers had been a volcano once—I could feel that in the bones of it, in the way the air tasted of sulfur and old heat. Now it was a cavern of tiered galleries carved from obsidian, descending in rings toward a floor of cracked basalt, and at the center, on a throne of fused glass that still glowed faintly at its heart, sat the queen.

Maelis was dying the way a candle dies: not diminished, exactly, but *thinning*, her edges translucent, her skin the color of paper held over flame. She wore a crown of iron—actual iron, deliberately, the way a penitent wears a hair shirt—and her eyes were the amber of banked coals, and they were the oldest thing I had ever seen. Older than the Order. Older than the wards. Older, perhaps, than the idea of borders at all.

The court filled the galleries: hundreds of them, thousands, a rustling sea of tall pale figures in colors gone muted—greys, ash-blues, the faded rose of embers cooling. As we walked the long basalt aisle, the whispering began. I didn't need to speak the Old Tongue to understand it. Hatred translates itself.

"*Hunter.*"

"*Iron-blood.*"

"*They burned the groves at Sorrowmere.*"

"*My sister's children died in the Purge of—*"

"*Why does she bring it HERE—*"

Jude's shoulder pressed against mine. "Breathe," he said, barely audible. "You're the bravest person in this room. Act like it."

"I'm the most heavily armed person who *was* in this room," I said. "It's different."

"Serah."

"I know." I breathed. "Courage isn't the absence of fear, it's—"

"—the memory of worse nights. Yes. You've said."

We reached the foot of the throne dais, and the herald struck the floor three times with a staff of white bone, and the whispering died so completely that I could hear the queen breathing—slow, labored, each breath an act of will.

"Mortal bearer," Maelis said. Her voice was quiet and it carried anyway, the way a blade carries. "You stand in the last court of the Ember Line, beneath a sky we blinded ourselves to save, and you bring four shards of the covenant in a pouch of tanned calfskin." A pause. "Calfskin. From my realm's cattle, or yours?"

"Mine," Jude said. "I bought it in a market. I'll—apologize to the cow."

Someone in the third gallery laughed and was silenced by a look.

"Bearer." The queen's coal-dark eyes did not smile. "You are known to us. The emissary we sent—Ashen-Vow, who carried the shard to the mortal realm—she reached you. She spent her last strength finding you. Tell the court what she said."

"She called me Covenant-Bearer." Jude's voice was steady, but I saw his hand tremble at his side, and I moved half a step closer so that my shadow covered it. "She said the covenant was breaking. She gave me the first shard. Then she—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She went out like a lamp. Politely. Like everything else here."

"She chose her death well," Maelis said, "which is more than most of us will be permitted." She leaned forward on her glass throne, and the gallery rustled like a field in wind. "But you have not come alone, bearer. You have brought a hunter of the Pale Flame into the heart of the last free court of my people. So before we speak of shards and vaults and the reforging of worlds, the court will hear an accounting." Her gaze slid from Jude to me, and settled, and the weight of it was physical, a hand flat against my sternum. "Three hundred years ago, your ancestors broke faith with ours. Two hundred years ago, your Crown outlawed enchantment and burned my kin in squares while crowds sang hymns. Fifty years ago, the Order of the Pale Flame hunted the last unregistered mages of Vael into the gutters, and the gutters ran—" she paused, delicately, "—well. You were there, Serah Vane. Daughter of Corin. You were there for some of it. Speak, if you can, for mankind. The court is listening."

And the court *was*. Every face in those tiered rings of black glass turned toward me, and I felt their attention the way you feel a change in weather—in the teeth, in old scars, in the animal brain that knows a predator has marked you.

Jude's hand brushed mine. *You don't have to,* the touch said.

*I do,* I touched back, and stepped forward alone onto the basalt floor.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about courage: it feels exactly like making a mistake. Every time. Your body cannot tell the difference. Standing before that court, my heart hammering the same frantic rhythm it hammered the day I'd been ordered to burn a charm-seller's stall in the Lantern District and had done it, gladly, righteously, believing—I spoke, and every word felt like stepping off a cliff, and I stepped anyway.

"My name is Serah Vane," I said. "Third rank, Order of the Pale Flame. Everything the queen said is true, and worse than true, because the worst of it never made the histories. I was trained from the age of nine to believe magic was a disease and mages were carriers. I was taught that the fae were a fairy story we'd grown out of—that the border wards were flood defenses against nothing, maintained out of superstition. When the stars went out and the wards fell, my first thought was not *wonder*. It was *target acquisition*." I let that sit. Let them see I would not soften it. "I tracked Jude Kavanagh through the Lantern District to arrest him. I would have done it, too, if he hadn't stolen an hour of my memory—which, for the record, was the kindest violence anyone has ever done me, because in that hour I learned what my own Order does to children suspected of fae blood."

The gallery had gone very still. Even the whispering had stopped. In the silence I heard the queen breathe, twice, slow as tide.

"I refused an execution order eleven days ago," I continued. "A child. Nine years old. Fae-touched, they said—as if that were a verdict rather than a description. I refused, and now the Crown calls me traitor, and I am standing in your court because there is nowhere left in my realm where I am not hunted." I drew a breath that shook, and I let them see it shake, because I had decided something on the bridge, in the dark, with my mother's dead voice saying my name out of black water: I would not perform fearlessness for these people. They had earned better than performance. "So no, I cannot speak for mankind. Mankind has spokesmen. They wear crowns and vestments and they are liars. What I can speak for is smaller and it is mine: I speak for the ones who refused. There are more of us than your histories record. There always were. Mothers who hid their touched children. Soldiers who lost their prisoners. Hunters—" my voice caught on it, and I pushed through, "—hunters who filed false reports for years because the reports were the only mercy left to give. You have counted our cruelties, and you should—they are real, and they are yours to count. But count this too: every cruelty required someone to carry it out, and not everyone carried it willingly, and some of us broke, and the breaking is happening faster now. Marrow made a mistake when he purged the lower quarters. He taught the invisible people what he thinks of them. Invisible people remember."

Silence. The long, ringing kind.

Then, from the second gallery, a voice like dry leaves: "Pretty words. My brother died in the Iron Years. Where were the refusers then?"

"Dead," I said, turning to find the speaker—an ancient fae, gaunt as winter, eyes like frost on a window. "Or silent. Or complicit. Mostly complicit. I won't insult your grief by claiming otherwise. All I can tell you is what I know: the man beside me spent three years stealing memories to survive in a city that outlawed his existence, and when the world began to end, he didn't run past the people who needed him. He went back for a widow's grief. He went back for strangers' sins. If that's what mankind is, then mankind is also this, and you should weigh it before you weigh the rest."

"And what are *you*, hunter?" the old fae rasped. "What are you, when the refusing is done? What do you want from us?"

The question went through me like a spear, because I hadn't let myself answer it yet, even in the dark, even with Jude asleep against my shoulder.

"To matter," I said. "To be on the right side of this, finally, at whatever cost. To help reforge the covenant so that no child in either realm is ever again born guilty of a border they didn't draw." I looked back at the queen. "That's what I want. Judge it how you like."

* * *

Maelis Ember-forged was silent for a long time. The court waited. The banked fires in the walls pulsed, very slowly, like the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping under the mountain.

"Daughter of Corin," she said at last. "When I was young—before your realm had a name for iron—there was a human woman who came to my grandmother's court under much the same conditions. Hunted, friendless, honest about her own people's crimes. My grandmother set her riddles and tests, and she passed them all, and in the end my grandmother asked her the same question you were asked: *what do you want?* And the woman said, 'A world where my daughter can love whoever she loves.' And my grandmother granted it, and that woman's daughter became the first of the Wardens, and the covenant held for six hundred years because of a human who answered honestly in a hostile court." The queen's coal eyes closed, opened. "I am not my grandmother. I am tired, and my court is hungry, and half of them want your blood and the other half want your realm, and I have perhaps a season left to hold them to any path at all. So I will not grant you anything, Serah Vane. I will simply tell you what I told the bearer, and let you both carry it."

She rose. It cost her visibly—one hand pressing the arm of the glass throne, the crown of iron dipping forward—and the entire court rose with her, a thousand tall shadows unfolding in the ember-light.

"The Ember Vaults lie beneath the Ashen Stair, three days' ride into the Dimming, where the exiled lord Vesperane keeps his seat. He holds the fifth shard. He is old, and cruel, and he was my consort once, and he brokered the fall of your wards with your Chancellor—and he will know you are coming before you arrive, because the Dimming whispers, and he listens." She descended one step, two, until she stood level with us, close enough that I could see the fine cracks spreading through her skin like glaze on old porcelain. "Vesperane will offer you bargains. He will offer you truths, which are worse. And when he offers you the shard, bearer, he will ask a price shaped exactly like your heart's desire, because that is the only coin he has ever accepted."

"And if we refuse?" Jude asked.

"Then refuse quickly," the queen said, "and run well." Something moved through her face that might, in a younger face, have been a smile. "You have done the impossible thing already, either of you—you made my court listen. That is rarer than courage. Courage I could buy from any soldier. Attention—" her gaze swept the galleries, the rustling, resentful, magnificent ruin of her people, "—attention must be given freely."

She raised one translucent hand, and a servant came forward bearing a lantern of black glass in which a single coal burned white-hot.

"Light for the Dimming," Maelis said. "It will not keep you warm. Nothing will, out there. But it will keep you *lit*, and in the Dimming, that is the difference between travelers and ghosts." She placed the lantern in Jude's hands, and then her coal-dark eyes found mine one last time. "Warden's daughter, if the old tales run true in you—and I think they do—then guard the bearer. Not because he is fragile. Because he is the kind of fool who gives things away, and the world is full of takers."

"Yes, Majesty," I said.

"And Serah Vane." Her voice dropped, and though the whole court strained to hear, I had the sudden certainty that the words were meant for me alone. "The child you refused to kill. The Order will try again, and next time they will send someone who does not refuse. When you return to your realm—if you return—go to her first. Before the vaults. Before the war. Some debts are larger than quests."

The cold went through me like the river's voice on the bridge. *Tamsin.* The girl with the fae-touched eyes, locked in the Order's holding cells below the Citadel, and me on this side of the veil with a war between realms balanced on my shoulders.

"How did you—" I started.

"Dying queens see a great deal," Maelis said, "and explain almost nothing. Go. Both of you. The Dimming is worst at dusk, and dusk here lasts a very long time."

* * *

They gave us horses—or what passed for horses here: tall grey creatures with too-intelligent eyes and manes like smoke—and an escort of six guards whom nobody introduced and who watched me the entire way out of the city with expressions I chose, generously, to read as curiosity rather than appetite.

At the valley's rim I turned in the saddle and looked back once. The Court of Embers glowed against the starless dark like the last coal in a grate, beautiful and doomed and stubbornly alight, and I understood suddenly why the queen wore iron: not as penance. As memory. As a promise that she would carry the weight of the human realm's betrayals in her own flesh until someone came to take up the other side of it.

"We're really doing this," Jude said beside me. "Riding into the Dimming to rob the ex-husband of a dying goddess."

"Consort."

"He's going to eat us, Serah."

"Probably," I said, and spurred my smoke-maned horse down into the gathering dark, toward the Ashen Stair, where something old and patient was already listening for the sound of our hooves.
