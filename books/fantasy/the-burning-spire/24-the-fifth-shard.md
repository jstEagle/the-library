## 24. The Fifth Shard

The Otherworld did not have weather so much as moods.

Jude had learned that in the weeks since crossing the veil. The land breathed. It sulked and brightened and brooded, and tonight it brooded with him, a low grey pressure pressing down on the hills like a hand on the back of a neck. No stars here either—the queen's court had none to give—but the dimming was different in this place. In the mortal city the dark felt stolen. Here it felt owed.

He stood at the edge of the Hollow Court's last lantern-light, watching the road wind down toward the house of the exiled lord.

"You're doing the thing again," Serah said beside him. "The thing where you stare at danger until it stares back."

"One of us should know what we're walking into."

"I've walked into worse." She said it lightly, but her hand rested on the hilt of her blade the way a person rests a hand on an old wound—without thinking, out of long habit. "A cellar full of Marrow's hunters. A cathedral burning around my ears. Your cooking."

"My cooking is *fine*."

"Your cooking is a war crime the Crown never prosecuted because everyone who ate it forgot the experience voluntarily."

Despite everything—the grey sky, the road below, the shard waiting in a monster's skull—Jude laughed. It came out of him small and startled, like a bird flushed from cover. He was still surprised by laughter these days. He was still surprised by her.

Below them, at the bottom of the valley where two dead rivers met, stood Vael Morath's house. Calling it a house was like calling a wound a scratch. It was a manor grown out of black stone, or perhaps the stone had grown around it; its towers leaned at angles architecture shouldn't permit, and its windows glowed with a light that was not fire and not lamplight but something older and hungrier, the light of a memory being slowly consumed. Brambles crowned the roof—not ivy, not thorn, but brambles of silver-grey wood that moved when you weren't looking, rearranging themselves like a sleeper turning over.

Vael Morath. The Lord of the Sundered Bough. The fae noble who had brokered the ward-collapse with Chancellor Marrow, trading the stars for a promise of dominion—and been cheated, exiled from his own court, left to rot in this dimming valley while his co-conspirator climbed toward godhood in the mortal world.

Queen Maelis had given them the intelligence herself, in her court of embers, her voice thin as ash: *Morath holds the fifth shard. He took it from the covenant's guardians before they fell. He will not surrender it. And he cannot be killed—if he could be killed, I would have done it myself three hundred years ago.*

*Then how?* Serah had asked.

And the dying queen had turned her ember-bright eyes on Jude, and there had been something in them that was almost apology. *You are a thief,* she had said. *Steal it.*

* * *

They went down through the bramble-fields as the light failed, which in the Otherworld meant nothing so simple as sunset—the gloom simply deepened, the way water deepens, without any sun going anywhere. Jude kept his breathing slow and even, the way he did before every theft. Three years of practice. Never take much. Never be seen. Those were the rules, and the rules had kept him alive in a city that burned witches.

But the rules had been written for human minds. Grieving widows. Distracted merchants. One witch-hunter whose hour of memory he'd taken in a market alley and replaced with something kind—a moment of sunlight on her mother's face, freely given back. Human minds were houses with unlocked doors. You slipped in, you took what you needed, you left the furniture roughly where you found it.

Vael Morath's mind would not be a house.

It would be a fortress. A forest. A thing with teeth.

"You could still let me go in first," Serah murmured. They crouched now behind a fallen monolith carved with faces, all of them screaming, all of them worn smooth by centuries of rain into expressions closer to weeping. "Draw his attention. Put steel between him and you."

"And he'll fold you inside-out like a glove. You've seen what the lesser fae can do. Morath is to them what a wolf is to a moth."

"Then we need a better plan than *I'll distract the immortal nightmare while you pick its brain.*"

"This *is* the better plan. The first plan involved me knocking on the front door and asking nicely."

"What happened to the first plan?"

"Maelis laughed until she coughed blood, and then said no."

Serah exhaled through her nose—the sound that was almost a laugh, the sound he'd learned to treasure because she rationed it like wartime bread. Then her face went serious again, and she reached out and touched his jaw, turning his face toward hers. Her eyes in the gloom were the color of wet slate.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "you run. Not fight. Not negotiate. Run. You carry four shards and half the hope of two worlds in your skull. That makes you worth more than both of us together, and I have never once in my life valued anything above my duty, so understand what it costs me to say that."

"It doesn't cost you anything," Jude said softly. "That's the whole point of duty. It's free right up until the moment it isn't."

"Jude."

"I run. I promise. But Serah—" He covered her hand with his own, pressed it flat against his cheek. "If I get lost in there—you come in after me. Whatever you see. Whatever I seem to be. You come in after me, and you say my name, and you keep saying it until I find my way back along the sound of it. Can you do that?"

Her thumb traced his cheekbone once. "I've pulled men out of fires by their collars. I can pull you out of a fae lord's head."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever promised me."

"We're setting the bar very low for romance in this relationship."

"The bar's underground, in a gutter cathedral, next to the wine we stole from the Chancellor's own cellars. It's perfect."

She kissed him then, quick and fierce, tasting of iron and the bitter tea Mother Quill used to brew, and then she was up and moving through the brambles toward the eastern wall, blade drawn, a shadow among shadows, and Jude was alone with the screaming monolith and the road and the house of the thing he had to rob.

He closed his eyes. He let the loneliness come, because the loneliness was part of the craft. Every theft began the same way: alone, in the dark, with nothing but himself to spend.

*Never take much. Never be seen.*

Tonight, he thought, the rules were going to bleed.

* * *

The wards around Vael Morath's estate were not wards as Jude understood them. Human wards were scars—Marrow's anti-magic lattice over the capital had felt like old scar tissue, dull and fibrous, a body's clumsy attempt to heal over a wound. These were not healing. These were *hunting*. The brambles at the estate's edge hummed with attention, each silver-grey tendril a nerve ending, and when Jude eased his magic forward to taste them, the taste came back as a question:

*What are you?*

He gave it nothing. He folded his power down small, down quiet, the trick that had kept him invisible for three years in a city that outlawed enchantment—he made himself a crack in the world, a gap between thoughts, a nothing shaped like a man. The brambles' attention slid across him like a tongue across teeth, paused, and moved on.

He went over the wall in the space between one heartbeat of the estate and the next.

Inside, the grounds were a graveyard of seasons. Trees stood in rows, each one frozen mid-change—one blazing with autumn gold though no wind stirred its leaves, one budding with spring blossom that never fell, one bare and black as burnt bone. A garden where time had been pinned like butterflies. Jude moved through it with his heart in his throat, because he recognized the work. This was covenant-magic. This was the same deep grammar he'd felt in the shards, in the spire's residue, in the widow's returned memory of green fire on the horizon. Morath hadn't just taken a shard from the covenant's guardians.

He'd been *practicing* with it.

The manor's doors stood open. Of course they did. A predator does not hide from prey.

Jude stepped into the entrance hall, and the light that wasn't fire wrapped around him like warm water, and a voice came from everywhere at once—out of the walls, out of the floor, out of the space behind his eyes.

*"A thief walks into my house."*

The voice was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was the most beautiful voice Jude had ever heard, layered like a chord, like a choir singing in unison with itself across centuries, and underneath the beauty ran something dry and patient and old, old, old.

"You knew I was coming," Jude said. His voice sounded very small and very human in that hall, and he decided, on the spot, not to apologize for it.

*"The queen dreams loudly. Her little errand has been whispering across the veil for a month. I wondered which of her pets she'd send."* Something shifted in the shadows at the far end of the hall—tall, taller than a doorframe, unfolding the way a spider unfolds, all elegance and wrong angles. *"And she sent the Covenant-Bearer himself. The human magpie. Come to pick at my hoard."*

"Not your hoard," Jude said. "You stole it from people who died protecting it. I'm just returning property."

Laughter rolled through the hall, and the laughter made the frozen trees outside shudder, made dust fall from rafters that had not known gravity in centuries. *"Property. Oh, you are a delight. Do you know what I am, little thief?"*

"A cautionary tale," said Jude. "The fae lord who trusted Aldric Marrow."

The laughter stopped.

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on Jude's chest like deep water, and in that silence he felt the lord's attention settle on him fully for the first time—a vast, cold regard, the regard of something that had watched empires be born and die the way a man watches mayflies.

*"I am going to enjoy this,"* Vael Morath said softly. *"Come. Sit. Let us talk before you do whatever foolish thing you came to do. I have not spoken with a mortal in ninety years, and you—" the tall shape drifted closer, and now Jude could see it properly, and wished he couldn't: a figure like a man stretched through a lens, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, with eyes like the gaps between stars, the starless gaps, the hungry ones, *"—you interest me. You carry four shards in your skull and walk upright. That should have unmade you by now. What are you, Jude Kavanagh?"*

"Just a thief," Jude said, and smiled, and sat, because the chair was there and refusing it would tell the predator everything. "With excellent manners."

* * *

They talked for three hours.

That was the trap, of course, and Jude knew it was a trap, and he sat in it anyway, because the talking was also the work. Morath poured wine that tasted of every summer Jude had ever lost, and asked questions that seemed idle and were not, and Jude answered with truths that seemed like confessions and were armor. Around them the hall breathed. Somewhere above, Serah waited on the roofline with steel and patience, and Jude held the thought of her like a coal in his cupped hands—*you come in after me, you say my name*—and did not look at the ceiling once.

*"Tell me of the mortal city,"* Morath said, refilling his cup. *"I have not walked under a human sky since before your ancestors learned to write. Is it still loud? Are they still frightened of the dark?"*

"Louder. Frightened of the light, now. Marrow's purges took the lower quarters first. Whole streets that don't remember their own names." Jude turned his cup. "You helped build that."

*"I helped build a bargain. What Marrow built from it is his own architecture."* Something flickered across the stretched face—not quite guilt. Guilt requires a conscience, and whatever Morath had, it was older and stranger than that. Regret, maybe. The regret of a gambler who'd backed the wrong horse. *"You think I wanted this? The dimming? I traded the stars for a throne, thief, and received exile instead. Marrow holds both realms' futures in his fist and I hold a garden of dead seasons and a shard I cannot use."*

"Why can't you use it?"

Morath went very still.

It was the stillness of a forest when the deer lift their heads all at once, and Jude felt the temperature of the conversation change, felt the predator finally turn its full face toward him, and understood that he had just stepped off a cliff and the only way out was through.

*"Because the shards answer to a bearer,"* Morath said, each word laid down like a stone on a coffin lid, *"and the covenant chose a human. A gutter-magpie with sticky fingers. Three thousand years of my service, and the flame of heaven picks a cutpurse."* The starless eyes narrowed. *"Unless. Unless the bearer comes to me, talks to me, sits in my hall drinking my wine, keeping my attention exactly where I want it—"*

The hall changed.

The light that wasn't fire went red. The doors slammed shut with a sound like a tomb closing, and the walls began to move—not physically, not yet, but in the way that mattered more, the way a mind moves when it decides to become a cage. Jude felt the pressure descend, vast and ancient, the full weight of a consciousness older than the mortal realm's oldest stone turning its attention inward upon the small warm thing sitting in its guest chair.

*"You came to steal from me,"* Morath said, and the beauty was gone from his voice now, and what remained was worse. *"In my own house. While drinking my wine."*

"Yeah," Jude said quietly. He set down the cup. His hands were steady, and he was distantly proud of that. "I really am sorry about the wine."

*"I am going to open you,"* the fae lord said, *"like a letter."*

And the mind of Vael Morath came down on him like a collapsing cathedral.

* * *

There was no describing it, afterward, except in pieces.

It was falling upward through a forest of memories that were not his—battles fought with weapons made of dawn, a court of ten thousand years, the smell of the Otherworld when it was young and bright and the stars here burned twice as fierce as the mortal ones. It was drowning in someone else's eternity. It was every theft Jude had ever committed reflected back at him as a single drop of what he now faced: he had lifted hours from grieving women, and this thing had lived ages, and it knew he was there, and it was *hunting him through its own halls of memory*, closing corridors behind him, flooding rooms with fire—

*Never take much. Never be seen.*

He broke the second rule first. He stopped hiding. Hiding was for houses, and this was no house, and the only survival in a predator's territory was to move, fast and strange, along paths the predator didn't guard because nothing had ever dared. Jude seized a memory of Morath's—the coronation, ten thousand years of voices roaring—and instead of taking it, he *ran along it*, sprinted through the coronation crowd of the fae lord's own past, and the pursuit behind him howled with fury and confusion, because the thief was not stealing, the thief was *swimming*.

Somewhere far away, in a body slumped in a chair in a red-lit hall, blood started from Jude's nose. His fingers clawed the armrests. His lips moved without sound.

*You come in after me. You say my name.*

He dove deeper. Past the battles, past the court, past the garden of pinned seasons, down to where the oldest memories lay like bedrock—and there, guarded by walls of thorned light and three millennia of paranoid reinforcement, he felt it.

The shard. A fragment of the original covenant, humming the same deep note he'd carried since the emissary died pressing light into his palm. Buried in the mind of the thing hunting him. He would have to reach into the jaws and take it while the jaws were closing.

Behind him, the flood of Morath's rage came on like winter.

Ahead, the shard sang.

Jude Kavanagh, who had spent three years being no one, who had built a life on never being seen, reached out with both hands into the mind of a titan—

—and the doors of the hall burst open below, and a voice rang up through the red light, human and furious and beloved, saying his name.
