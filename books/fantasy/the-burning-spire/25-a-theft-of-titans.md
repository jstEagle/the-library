## 25. A Theft of Titans

The fae lord's mind was not like a house.

Jude had spent three years thinking of memory as architecture—doors and corridors, rooms with locks, attics where people hid the things they could not bear to look at. He had learned to pick those locks the way a burglar learns a city: by feel, by dark, by the small mercies of habit. Every human mind he had ever entered had been built on the same principle. Walls around a self. A hearth at the center.

Vaelthorne's mind was a forest that ate houses.

He understood this within the first heartbeat of contact, kneeling in the wet bracken beneath the drowned orchard while Serah kept watch above him with her blade drawn and her breath held, and he understood it the way a swimmer understands deep water—not as information but as cold climbing up through the soles of his feet. There were no doors here. No walls. The thoughts of the exiled lord grew wild and rootless in every direction at once, ancient and patient and *hungry*, and somewhere in that tangle was a single shard of covenant-light, and Jude had to find it without being found.

*You are a very small animal,* said a voice that was not a voice, everywhere and nowhere, the way wind is a voice if you want it badly enough. *Come into my wood, little thief.*

Jude did not answer. Answering was a door, and doors opened both ways.

He moved instead—carefully, the way Mother Quill had taught him to move through the cathedral's dark, one hand always on something solid. Around him the memories of Vaelthorne grew like black pines: centuries of them, millennia, trunks so wide that a human lifetime would be a knot of bark on their skin. He saw flashes as he passed them. A court lit by ten thousand candles before candles had names. A queen young and laughing, wearing stars like a crown of thorns. The border wards going up, ring by ring, and Vaelthorne's own hands—elegant, grey-skinned, beautiful in the way frost is beautiful—helping to raise them.

*Helping,* Jude noted, filing the word away like a coin in a hidden pocket. The histories said Vaelthorne had been exiled for opposing the covenant. But these memories remembered otherwise. These memories remembered *building.*

The shard's light pulsed somewhere ahead, faint and gold, like a lamp behind fogged glass. He followed it deeper. The forest thickened. The trees began to lean toward him.

That was when he felt the first root take hold of his ankle.

It wasn't pain. It was worse than pain—it was *attention*. Something in the dark soil of that mind had noticed the texture of him, the particular grain of a mortal soul walking where no mortal soul should walk, and it curled around him gently, almost tenderly, the way a spider handles what it intends to keep.

*Ah,* said the not-voice, delighted. *There you are. I wondered which of them they would send. Not a soldier. Not a queen. A pickpocket.* A pause, long as a season. *How very like Maelis. She always did love her ironies.*

Jude kept walking. One step. Another. Sweat ran down his spine inside the borrowed cloak; his real body, far away under the orchard, was shaking so hard Serah later told him she'd thought he was dying. In here there was no body at all, only will, and will was a currency he had spent his whole life counting out in small denominations. Never take much. Never be seen. His rules. His survival.

Tonight he broke both.

The shard hung ahead of him now, caught in the branches of an enormous tree whose roots went down further than light goes down. It turned slowly, shedding gold, and Jude recognized its song—he had heard it five times now, in the widow's grief, in the vault's quiet boxes, in the queen's dying hand—and recognition steadied him the way a familiar street steadies a man fleeing through the night.

He reached for it the only way he knew how: gently. The way he had lifted the widow's last afternoon with her son. The way he had taken an hour from Serah in the Lantern District and left her, without quite knowing why, the smell of rain on warm stone and the feeling of being forgiven for something.

*No,* said Vaelthorne.

The forest closed.

---

Later—hours later, or minutes; time in the Otherworld was a rumor—Serah would describe what she saw. She would do it in her flat hunter's cadence, the one she used for reports she never intended to file, and her hands would shake while she spoke, though her voice would not.

She saw Jude kneel in the bracken and go still. Then she saw the ground around him begin to glow, faint veins of gold spreading through the mud like lightning frozen mid-strike. Then she saw him arch backward as if struck, and the air above him fill with shapes—shadows of trees that cast shadows where no tree stood, and among them, vast and slow, the outline of something with too many joints and eyes like the gaps between stars.

She had raised her blade against it. She had spoken words she'd been taught were nonsense, ward-signs from a childhood catechism the Crown had outlawed, and to her lasting astonishment they had done *something*—the shadow had flinched, just slightly, just long enough—

And then Jude screamed, and the gold light tore free of the dark all at once, and the orchard was only an orchard again, wet and ordinary and full of falling leaves.

---

What Jude experienced, in the moment of taking, was this:

He got the shard. That much was true, and clean, and cost exactly what Mother Quill had warned it would cost—a piece of himself he would feel the absence of forever and never be able to name. He felt it come loose from the branches like a ripe fruit, and he closed his hand around its warmth, and for one glorious instant he thought: *I have done it. I have robbed a titan and lived.*

Then the root around his ankle *pulled*.

Not to keep him. To keep *something of him.* Vaelthorne could not hold the thief, but the thief had made a mistake every novice makes and no veteran escapes entirely: in the instant of taking, their minds had touched, and touch leaves residue. The fae lord's mind was older than rivers, and it did not let go cleanly. As Jude tore himself free, the wound between them tore *both ways*, and things came flooding back through the cut that had never belonged to him at all.

Memories. Vaelthorne's memories. Pouring into him like blood into water.

He surfaced in his own skull gasping, on his knees in the bracken, Serah's arms around his shoulders, her voice saying his name over and over in the tone people use for the drowning—and none of it reached him, because inside his head a forest was burning down, and in the burning he *saw*.

He saw a meeting in a room that smelled of ink and candle-tallow. A human room. A tower office high above a grey city, with wards of Crown design humming in the walls. And across the desk, in a chair that creaked under his weight, sat Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow—younger then, thinner, his ambition not yet fat with success—and opposite him, folded into a mortal shape that fit him badly, grey-eyed and smiling, sat Vaelthorne.

They were making a bargain.

*I will let the wards fall,* Marrow was saying, in a voice like a ledger closing. *I will give you the border, the crossing-points, the old roads. Your court may have the night back. In exchange—*

*In exchange,* Vaelthorne murmured, *you will have what stands behind the veil when it opens. You believe you can hold it. You believe you can *become* it.*

*I don't believe,* Marrow said. *I have calculated.*

And the memory turned, the way dreams turn, and showed Jude what Marrow had calculated: the six shards gathered not to reforge the spire but to *replace* it. A new covenant with a single signatory. All the binding power of the old accord—the tether between mortal realm and Otherworld, the law that held the stars in the sky and the fae behind the veil—poured into one man's flesh. Marrow, crowned in covenant-light. Marrow, the hinge on which both worlds would swing. Every prayer answered by him. Every enchantment licensed by him. Every dawn permitted by him.

*And the stars?* Vaelthorne had asked, in the memory, with the idle cruelty of one discussing weather.

*Mortals have looked up long enough,* Marrow said. *Let them learn to look at me.*

Jude came back to himself in pieces, over hours, in a hollow beneath the roots of a dead alder where Serah dragged him when his convulsions frightened the local spirits. The Otherworld dimmed around them—starless now even here, the queen's court burning its hoarded light like a family burning furniture in winter—and he lay in the crook of her arm and shook and bled memories that were not his.

They came out of him sideways. He would open his mouth to say *water* and instead say, in a voice not his own, *the third bridge fell in the year of the salt famine, I watched them pull the children out, I watched for four days.* He would close his eyes and see a coronation nine hundred years dead, taste wine pressed from grapes that no longer existed anywhere, grieve for a wife whose name he did not know and mourned anyway, bone-deep, helplessly, because the grief was in him now and grief does not check its ownership papers.

Serah held him through all of it. She did not sleep. When he wept for the fae lord's dead wife, she wiped his face with her sleeve and said, quietly, "That one isn't yours. Give it back tomorrow." And when he laughed—cracked, horrified laughter at the absurdity of it—she said, "Tell me."

So he told her. Between the seizures of foreign memory, in fragments, holding her wrist to anchor himself to a self that kept trying to dissolve: the tower office. The bargain. The calculation.

"The shards," he rasped. "Marrow doesn't want to break the covenant. Breaking it was just—clearing the table. He wants to *hold* it. All of it. One man standing where the spire stood, and everything that lives under either sky owing him its existence." He swallowed; his throat was raw, as if he had been screaming for years inside someone else's century. "We're not racing him to save the world, Serah. We're racing him to keep him from *owning* it."

Her jaw did the thing it did when she was absorbing a blow and converting it to motion. "Then we get the sixth shard first."

"It's in his head." Jude stared up through the alder's bare branches at the lightless sky. "It's been in his head for years. He took it from the stranger's companion before I ever—" He stopped. The circle of it closed around him like a fist. The hooded stranger who had begged a small-time charm-thief to hide a fragment of divine architecture in a mind too poor to search. The companion Marrow had found first, or caught first, or broken first. Two thieves working the same mark from opposite ends, and neither knowing.

"Jude." Serah's hand on his cheek, turning his face back to hers. Her eyes in the dark were steady and furious and afraid, all three at once, and he loved her with a completeness that terrified him more than Vaelthorne's forest had. "Can you do it? Steal from *him*?"

He wanted to lie. He was, by trade and temperament, a good liar. But lying to her had become a thing his hands refused to do, like touching a hot stove twice.

"I don't know," he said. "Marrow's mind will be warded like a fortress, and he'll know I'm coming, and even if I get in—" He flexed his fingers, watching them tremble. They still didn't feel entirely like his. Nothing felt entirely like his. "Even if I get in, I don't know how much more of this I can carry. I'm already full of someone else's ghosts. I dreamed Vaelthorne's wedding last night. I woke up missing a woman I never met."

"Then we find another way."

"There isn't another way. There's me, and there's the rite, and there's the price." He heard the words leave his mouth and understood, with a clarity colder than the Otherworld's failing light, that he had just brushed against the truth he had been circling for days—the truth Mother Quill had written in smoke and he had refused to read closely. The rite required a bearer to surrender *all* held memories. All. Not the stolen ones. Not the convenient ones. Everything the vessel carried, poured into the flame until nothing remained but the flame.

Everything.

His eyes found her face and stayed there, and something in his expression must have changed, because hers changed to match it, wariness blooming into dread.

"What?" she said. "Jude. What aren't you telling me?"

Above them, through the bare branches, the starless dark pressed down like a lid. Somewhere far off, in the direction of the queen's court, a horn sounded—one long note, the summons-note, calling all bearers to council. The fifth shard lay warm against his heart like a coal that refused to die.

"Not here," he said, which was cowardice, and they both knew it, and she let him have it—for now—because the horn was sounding again, closer, and because some truths need firelight and darkness and the whole night ahead of them, and the whole night, God help them, was all they had.
