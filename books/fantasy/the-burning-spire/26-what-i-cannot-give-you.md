## 26. What I Cannot Give You

**Jude**

The queen's council chamber smelled of cold honey and old smoke, and it was there, standing before a throne of woven antlers while the last light of the Otherworld guttered in the windows, that Jude finally understood the shape of what he owed.

Queen Maelis did not look at him when she said it. She looked at the fire—or at whatever the fae saw when they looked at fire—and her voice was the sound of wind moving through a house where no one lived anymore.

"The rite is simple," she said. "That is its cruelty. The spire was lit by a bearer who gave everything. Not his life—the flame does not want death, death is only another kind of keeping. It wants memory. All of it. Every hour you have ever held. The covenant is woven from what a soul remembers; to reforge it, the bearer must unweave himself entirely and feed the whole cloth to the fire."

"And afterward?" Serah asked from beside him. Her hand was on her sword hilt, as it always was in this court, though no blade here had ever been drawn against them. "Afterward he'd be—"

"Empty." Maelis turned her head at last. Her eyes were the grey of river ice, and they were kind, which was somehow worse. "Not dead. The body goes on. It eats, it sleeps, it walks out of the spire into the morning. But nothing stays. A face seen yesterday is gone by noon. Grief, love, the name your mother gave you—all of it runs through the fingers like water. We had such bearers once, long ago. They were honored. They were fed and clothed and sung to. And every one of them woke each day a stranger in their own life, until even honor meant nothing, because honor requires remembering why."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere below, the dimming land breathed—forests going grey at the edges, rivers running thin as thread, the whole vast country of the fae slowly fading like a print left too long in the sun.

Five shards sat in the pouch against Jude's chest. He could feel them the way he felt his own heartbeat: five small suns, five stolen pieces of a promise older than either realm. One more, and then the spire.

He had known, in some cellar of himself, since the night the stars went out. He had known and had not let the knowing climb into the light, because up here in the open it would have a face, and the face would be hers.

"Thank you," he heard himself say, steady as a shopkeeper quoting a price. "For telling me plainly."

Maelis studied him for a long moment. "You are welcome," she said. "And I am sorry. Those two things are not often true together."

* * *

Serah didn't speak on the walk back through the twilight orchards. That was how he knew how badly it was. Serah Vane filled silences the way other people filled rooms—with presence, with the creak of leather and the ring of boot-heels and the low mutter of someone cataloguing exits. When she went quiet, the quiet had weight, like snow before the branch breaks.

They reached the guesthouse the queen had given them—a round stone hut with a hearth and one bed, because the fae did not understand embarrassment and had not bothered with two. Jude built a fire because his hands needed something stupid to do. He arranged the kindling three times. His fingers wouldn't stop shaking, so he made fists of them, and then made himself let go, one finger at a time, the way he'd taught himself years ago to release a memory gently instead of tearing it free.

"Say it," Serah said.

"There's nothing to say. You were in the room."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear which version you tell yourself."

He sat back on his heels and looked at her. Firelight moved on her face—the scar through her eyebrow, the jaw set like a door barred against winter. Three months ago she'd been hunting him through the Lantern District with a writ and a crossbow. Now she was the only thing in two worlds he couldn't imagine the shape of the days without.

"The rite takes everything," he said. "Not just the shards. Everything I hold. Every charm I've ever made. Every memory I ever stole—it all goes into the flame, all of it, or the covenant won't bind. And—" His voice caught on the last of it, snagged like cloth on a nail. "And everything since. You. The road. The vault. The fire in the cathedral. What I feel when you laugh, which is not something I stole, it's something I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It goes too. All of it. I'll walk out of that spire and the stars will be burning and I won't know why I'm crying."

Serah crossed the room. She didn't kneel or soften; she came down to him the way she did everything, directly, and took his face in both hands, calluses rough against his jaw.

"No," she said.

"Serah—"

"No. Listen to me, thief. You don't get to decide this alone. That's the one rule we've had since the vault, since you put my life in your hands and I put yours in mine, and it's the only rule I will break your fingers over. You don't get to spend yourself like a coin and leave me holding the change."

"It isn't a choice." He said it gently, because it was the cruelest thing he'd ever had to say. "There are six shards. Five of them are mine to carry. The sixth is in Marrow's skull, and if we take it and don't light the flame, the wards stay broken, the stars stay dark, and Marrow becomes the covenant himself—both realms, forever, with him at the center of it. You've seen what he does with one city. Imagine him with the sky."

"I've imagined it. I imagine it every time I close my eyes." Her thumbs moved along his cheekbones, an unconscious tenderness she would have denied under torture. "So we light the flame. Fine. But find me another way to pay. There's always another way to pay. You taught me that—you, the great artist of the partial theft, the man whose entire creed was *never take much*. Take my memories. Split it. Half from you, half from me."

"It doesn't work that way. One bearer. One whole soul, unwoven." He covered her hands with his own. "I asked Mother Quill's ghost of a question about it, before the raid. Before she—" He swallowed. "She told me. The flame wants completeness. A half-payment makes a half-covenant, and a half-covenant is how you get Marrow."

Serah closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet and furious and more beautiful than anything he had ever stolen, and he thought: *this is what I cannot give you, and it is the only thing I have ever wanted to keep.*

"Then we have tonight," she said. "And tomorrow, and however many days it takes to get the sixth shard and reach the spire. And I am not going to waste a single hour of them being brave in advance. Do you understand me? I spent ten years being brave in advance for the Crown and it bought me a scar and a lie. Tonight I'm going to be selfish. Tonight I'm going to be greedy. You can be noble tomorrow."

"Serah—"

"Shut up, Jude." And she kissed him.

* * *

**Serah**

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red breathing glow and the strange double-dark of the Otherworld pressed at the little window, Serah lay awake listening to him sleep and conducting an argument with God, or with whatever passed for God in a world whose stars had been sold.

She had never been a praying woman. Prayer required believing someone was listening, and Serah had learned early—in the orphan barracks, in the hunter corps, in the Chancellor's lecture halls where doctrine was delivered like ammunition—that nobody was listening, and that the only reliable mercy in the world was the kind you carried yourself and handed out at gunpoint. But lying there with Jude's heartbeat under her palm, slow and mortal and finite, she found herself bargaining anyway, clumsily, like a child trading pebbles.

*Take anything else,* she offered the dark. *Take my sword arm. Take my eyes. Take every year I have left and give them to him doubled. Just leave him one thing. Leave him the smell of rain on hot stone. Leave him his mother's voice. Leave him me.*

The dark, being the dark, said nothing.

She had lost people before. Her whole profession had been loss, administered formally, with paperwork. She had executed a man in Varrow's Field with a steady hand and slept fine afterward, because the law had weighed him and found him wanting, and she had believed in the law the way other people believed in weather—total, impersonal, beyond argument. Then a child with fae-light in her eyes had changed her mind in the space of a single refused order, and Serah had learned that belief was not weather after all. Belief was a choice, made daily, and it could be unmade in a moment, and what remained afterward was just you, alone, deciding what you loved.

What she loved was currently breathing against her shoulder and pretending, badly, to be asleep.

"You're thinking loudly," he murmured.

"I'm allowed. One of us should use the hours."

He shifted, propped himself up on an elbow. In the ember-light he looked young and wrecked and stubborn, this man who had built a whole philosophy out of taking only a little, who had spent three years being invisible and had thrown it away in a single season for a cause and a woman and a sky full of absent stars.

"I keep trying to find the trick," he said. "The angle. There's always an angle—that's what I told myself my whole life. Every lock has a seam. Every price has a discount. And I've lain here turning it over and over and there's no seam in this one. The flame takes all or nothing." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the worst part is? Part of me is relieved. Isn't that obscene? Some small cowardly piece of me is glad there's finally a theft I can't talk my way out of. Because now I don't have to choose between the safe life and the real one. The choice got made for me."

"It's not obscene." She touched the hollow of his throat, where his pulse lived. "I understand it exactly. For ten years I wanted someone to hand me an order so terrible I'd finally have permission to quit. That's why the child broke me open, Jude. Not because I was good. Because she gave me the order I'd been waiting for." She smiled, and it hurt. "We're both cowards who were waiting for the world to make us brave. Lucky we found each other."

"Lucky," he agreed, and his voice cracked on it, and she pulled him down to her and held on.

Outside, far off across the greying hills, something sang—one note, then another, the way the fae did everything, patiently, in patterns older than language. A mourning song, maybe. Or a counting song. She thought it might be counting: naming things as they faded, keeping a ledger of a dying world. *One star, gone. Two stars, gone.* Somewhere in that ledger there was probably a line for her, and a line for him, and a line for the night itself, and none of it mattered, because the ledger wasn't written yet, and tonight the ink was still wet.

"Tell me what you'll miss," she said against his hair. "All of it. I want to carry it for you. If you won't remember, I will. Someone has to keep the books."

He was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough and unhurried, a man laying out inventory he'd never dared count before.

"Bread. Warm bread, the first bite, when the crust cracks. My mother humming in the kitchen—I don't even remember her face anymore, just the hum, low and off-key. Rain on the awning of my first shop. The sound the city makes at dawn before anyone's awake, that big empty breathing. The widow Marta's laugh, when I gave her son's words back to her—she laughed and cried at the same time and it sounded like bells falling downstairs." His arms tightened around her. "Your handwriting. You write like you're stabbing the page. Your face first thing in the morning, before you remember to be guarded. The way you said my name the first time—not Kavanagh, not *the mage*, just Jude, like it was a place you'd decided to live." A breath. "This. Right now. The fire and the singing and your heart going under my ear. If I could keep one thing, it would be this exact minute, and I can't, and I'm done pretending that doesn't feel like dying."

"Then it feels like dying," Serah said, "and we do it anyway. That's the whole job. That's what hunters know and thieves don't: some prices you pay walking forward, eyes open, because turning around costs more." She tilted his chin up so he had to look at her. "But hear me, Jude Kavanagh, and hear me well, because I'm only going to say this once and I expect you to forget it, which means I'll have to mean it enough for both of us. Whatever the flame takes, it doesn't touch me. I'll remember for both of us. I'll remember the bread and the rain and the bells falling downstairs. I'll come find you every day, in whatever shop, in whatever city, and I'll tell you about a witch-hunter who chased a thief across a starless world and fell in love with him somewhere between the vault and the fire, and you'll think I'm mad, and I'll tell you again the next day. You can steal from everyone else in creation, but you don't get to steal this from me. Remembering you is *mine*."

He was crying. She pretended, graciously, not to notice, and kissed his eyelids, salt and smoke, and outside the fae sang their patient ledger-song, and the fire burned down to nothing, and neither of them slept, and it was enough. It was not enough. It was everything there was.

* * *

Near dawn, when the grey light came seeping through the window like water under a door, Jude rose quietly and dressed and stood a while watching her—the tangled hair, the sword within reach even in sleep, the small frown she wore when dreaming, as if even unconscious she was auditing the world and finding it irregular.

Then he took out the five shards and laid them on the table in a row, and took out the scrap of charcoal he carried, and on the tabletop, beneath the shards' faint light, he wrote:

*Her name is Serah Vane. Find her first.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he added, smaller:

*You loved her. Trust the woman who tells you so.*

He heard her stir behind him and closed his fist over the charcoal, and when she woke fully and crossed the room to stand beside him, looking down at the five small suns and the words already half-faded in the growing light, she read them and said nothing, only leaned her shoulder into his, warm and solid and unbearably alive.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together," he said.

Far to the east, past the orchards and the thinning woods, the border between worlds flickered—and through it, faint as a rumor, came the first grey smudge of the human city on the horizon, and the tower at its heart, dark, waiting, hungry for its final shard.
