## 27. Return to a Starless City

**Jude**

The road into the capital had always smelled of bread and river-mud and horse dung, the honest stink of a living city. Now it smelled of ash.

Jude felt the change before he saw it—felt it in his own skin, in the magic that had grown fat and strange inside him since the Otherworld. The city's wards had been rebuilt, but wrong: not the old scar-tissue lattice that hummed like a struck bell at dusk, but something tighter, meaner, a net drawn so fine it caught even thought. Walking beneath it was like walking under a lid.

"Feel that?" Serah murmured beside him. She wore a grey wool cloak with the hood up, her hunter's leathers left behind in the Otherworld along with everything else they'd been. Her hand rested near the knife at her hip out of habit she probably didn't notice anymore.

"Like being frisked by fog," Jude said.

They crested the last hill as the sun set—or where the sun should have set. There was no colour to it. No gold, no rose, none of the bruised violet he remembered from three years of careful evenings spent watching the sky over the Lantern District while pretending he wasn't lonely. The light simply thinned, grey into darker grey, and the city below emerged from it like a body pulled from deep water: pale, bloated, wrong.

No stars. Of course no stars. But Jude had seen starless skies before—the night the wards fell, the dimming fields of the Otherworld—and this was different. This was a sky that had been *emptied on purpose*. Somewhere above the cloud-smear, he could feel the absence of the spire's flame like a missing tooth, a raw socket in the world's jaw.

"The Lantern District's gone," Serah said quietly.

He followed her gaze. The district had been a sprawl of crooked streets east of the river, all paper lanterns and frying oil and hawkers crying charms and candied plums, the one quarter of the capital where the poor were allowed their small magics so long as they paid the tithes. He'd made his living there for years, invisible among ten thousand small sins.

Now it was a grid. Neat streets, straight as ruled lines, lined with identical stone buildings and identical iron lamp-posts burning a flat white flame that gave off no warmth he could feel from here. Between the blocks, watchtowers rose every hundred paces, each crowned with a brazier of that same white fire.

"They burned it?" His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"No." Serah's jaw tightened. "Worse. They *tidied* it."

They came down through the western gate at dusk, joining the queue of carts and tired labourers, and Jude learned what tidying meant.

The gate guards weren't checking faces. They were checking *eyes*. Each traveller passing through was stopped, made to look into a shallow brass bowl of water held by a clerk in Chancellor-grey, and the clerk would murmur something—a word Jude couldn't catch, though his stolen memories stirred uneasily at its shape, some fragment of old covenant-grammar—and study the reflection.

When the man ahead of them bent to the bowl, Jude saw it happen. The water showed the man's face, yes—but around the face, faint as breath on glass, a halo of images: a woman laughing, a child on shoulders, a debt unpaid, a prayer half-remembered. The clerk touched the water with one finger and the images swirled, sorted, counted.

"Memories intact," the clerk said, bored. "Move along."

"What happens if they're not?" Jude breathed.

Serah didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Their turn came. Serah went first, hood back, and Jude watched the clerk's finger stir the water and watched her surface-images rise: a training yard, a sword hilt worn smooth, an order signed in red ink, a boy with fae-touched eyes she hadn't killed. The clerk frowned. Stirred again. The images rippled and settled into something blander—garrison duty, garrison duty, a tavern, rain.

*She's pushing them down,* Jude realized, awed despite himself. *Burying the surface thoughts under rote memory.* Three years of hunting mages had taught her how hunters read minds; now she used it to slip the net.

The clerk waved her through. Then it was Jude's turn, and his heart went hard against his ribs, because he carried six shards of the covenant in his skull and a head full of other people's lives, and if this bowl of water looked too closely—

He bent. The water was cold. His face floated there, and around it—

Nothing.

The clerk blinked. Stirred the water. Frowned. Around Jude's reflected face there was only grey mist, a blankness so complete it looked less like emptiness than like a hole cut in the world.

"Head injury?" the clerk asked.

"Fell off a cart as a boy," Jude said. "Never been right since."

"Hm." The clerk made a mark on his ledger. "Purged or damaged, same thing. You're no use to anyone either way. Move along."

*No use to anyone.* Jude walked through the gate with Serah's shoulder pressed warm against his and thought: *that is the most useful thing I have ever been.*

Inside the walls, the city had gone quiet in a way cities aren't meant to be. Not sleeping-quiet—*hollow*-quiet. The streets were clean. That was the first horror. He'd grown up in cities that wore their filth like fur, and this one had been scrubbed, swept, scoured, its graffiti sandblasted, its beggars gone, its stray dogs gone, its noise gone. People walked where people were meant to walk, in the marked lanes, at the marked pace, and when two of them met they spoke in low, level voices, exchanging words like coins counted out exactly.

And their eyes. He kept looking at their eyes. In the old city, eyes were windows thrown open—grief, greed, lust, boredom, all of it on display, all of it fuel for a thief like him. Here the windows were shuttered. People looked at each other the way they looked at furniture.

At a corner near the tanneries, a woman stood weeping. Silently. Tears running down a perfectly composed face, her hands folded, her breathing even, as though grief were a chore she'd been assigned and would finish by the appointed hour.

"She remembers losing someone," Serah said softly, "but they've taken the *shape* of it. The name. The reason. Look—she keeps turning toward a house. She doesn't know why."

Jude did look. The woman turned, again and again, toward a narrow door with a black ribbon nailed above the lintel, and each time she turned her brow creased and smoothed, creased and smoothed, like water trying to remember it had once been a wave.

His hands curled into fists. Every theft he'd ever committed had left a wound, yes—he knew that better than anyone, he'd spent weeks learning to close those wounds, to give back what he'd taken. But Marrow's purges weren't theft. Theft took something and left the taking visible. This was amputation without scar. This was a city of people who no longer knew what they were missing, which meant they could never ask for it back.

"We get to Mother Quill's street by midnight," Serah said. "If anything's left of it."

"If anything's left of us to find it," Jude said.

They moved through the grey lanes as the white lamps flickered on, two shadows among many, and Jude kept his head down and his mind smooth as still water, and tried not to weep for home.

* * *

**Serah**

She had trained in this city. She had bled in its yards, buried a mentor in its cemetery, drunk away two bad years in its taverns. Walking through it now was like reading a beloved book with every third page torn out.

The hunter's garrison still stood on Foundry Hill—of course it did; Marrow needed his blades somewhere—but the great bronze doors had been replaced with steel, and the statue of First Hunter Aldous Vane, her grandfather's grandfather, the man whose name she carried like a coin she'd never earned, had been recast. She stopped in the shadow of a grain-cart and stared. The old statue had shown him kneeling, sword point-down, head bowed in respect before the thing he served. The new one showed him standing, sword raised, and the plaque beneath bore a single line:

**MAGIC IS TREASON. MEMORY IS EVIDENCE.**

"You knew him," Jude said. It wasn't a question. He'd read it off her face, or maybe just off the way she stood.

"I grew up on stories of him. The family legend is that he wept after his first execution." She heard her own voice go flat. "I used to think that was the tragedy—that it hurt him. Now I think the tragedy would've been if it stopped hurting."

"And has it? Stopped?"

She considered lying, then didn't see the point. "It hurts more every day. I've just gotten better at standing still while it does."

They cut south along the river, keeping to the tanner's walk where the stench gave them cover, and Serah mapped the changes the way she'd once mapped a fugitive's habits: the checkpoint bridges, now manned in pairs with one watcher always facing upstream; the curfew bells that rang at dusk instead of midnight; the posters pasted on every wall, inked in that same red as the edicts, showing a stylized eye above the words **THE CHANCELLOR SEES WHAT YOU CANNOT REMEMBER.**

That was the genius of it, she admitted. That was the part that made her want to break things. Marrow hadn't built a police state by watching everyone. He'd built one by making everyone *unable to trust themselves*. If your memory could be edited in your sleep, then any doubt you felt might be evidence of treason you'd already committed and forgotten. The only safety was obedience, because obedience required nothing of you but the next hour.

A patrol crossed the bridge ahead—six hunters in grey-and-white, moving with the synchronized economy she knew from the inside, and for one lurching moment she catalogued their formation out of pure habit before remembering she was the quarry now. One of them was young. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, with the too-serious face of a recruit trying to be worthy of the uniform. Serah had been that face once. She wondered what the child suspected, deep under the conditioning, deep in whatever cellar of the self Marrow's clerks hadn't reached yet.

Then the young hunter paused. Turned. Looked directly at the shadowed alcove where she and Jude stood.

Serah's knife was in her hand before she decided to draw it.

But the boy only stared a moment—stared at her with an expression she couldn't parse, something between recognition and hunger and fear—and then shook his head slowly, like a man refusing a drink, and walked on.

"That was close," Jude whispered.

"No," Serah said, watching the boy's back recede. "That was something else. He *saw* me. He chose not to."

"Maybe he didn't recognize you."

"He did. I trained half the garrison's juniors." She slid the knife away, unsettled. "Maybe there are more cracks in this machine than Marrow thinks."

They reached the Lantern District's edge as the false lamps burned their heatless white. From here, Serah could see the whole grid of it, the tidy blocks, the towers, and threaded through the middle—impossibly, wonderfully—one crooked lane that the planners had missed. A seam of old city surviving like a bone unbroken in a crushed limb.

Mother Quill's street.

"It's still there," she breathed.

"Of course it is." Jude's grin flashed, quick and feral, the first she'd seen since the Otherworld. "You can't rule a line through a blind woman's street. It refuses to hold still long enough."

Getting there was the work of an hour—two checkpoints circumvented through a drainage culvert that stank of something Serah chose not to identify, a rooftop crossing that ended with Jude catching her wrist as the tiles gave way beneath her, a frozen minute pressed together in the dark while a patrol's lantern-sweep passed inches from their dangling boots. His pulse was fast under her fingers. So was hers. Neither of them mentioned it.

Then they dropped into the crooked lane, and the city changed.

Not loudly. Nothing about resistance, Serah was learning, happened loudly anymore. But the white lamplight seemed to thin here, the way water thins over a hidden spring, and the air tasted of woodsmoke and dried herbs instead of scrubbed stone, and there—third door on the left, exactly where it had always been, because some things refused to be tidied—was a door painted with a faded eye.

Jude knocked. Once, twice, a pause, once—the rhythm of a man asking a question he already feared the answer to.

Silence. Long enough that Serah's hand found her knife again.

Then the door opened a hand's width, and a voice like dry leaves said: "State what you remember."

Jude exhaled shakily. "I remember a woman who told me my crimes were never random. I remember tea brewed from pine needles. I remember her calling me *little thief* like it was an endearment and a sentence in the same breath."

The door opened fully. An old woman stood there—not Mother Quill; Serah had heard the seer described often enough on the road to know this was someone else—young-old, sharp-faced, with the milky, unfocused eyes of the gutter-born and a kitchen knife held low and ready.

"Quill's dead," the woman said flatly. "Three months past. They came at dawn and she stood in the doorway and wouldn't move, and what they did to her—" She stopped. Swallowed. "We keep her chair empty. We keep her kettle hot. Come in before the tower sees you, and mind the third step, it screams."

Inside was a cellar lit by candles that burned *coloured*—real flame, warm and gold, contraband light—and crowded with people. Dozens of them. Children asleep in blankets. Old men mending nets. A girl no older than ten practicing, with fierce concentration, making a candleflame bend without touching it. On the far wall, chalked in a careful hand, was a list of names. Some had lines through them. Most did not.

Above the list, in larger letters: **WHAT THEY TAKE, WE REMEMBER FOR EACH OTHER.**

Serah stood in the doorway with river-filth on her boots and a traitor's brand healing under her collarbone, and felt something in her chest crack open—not painfully, for once.

"This is what's left," she said quietly.

"This is what's *kept*," the sharp-faced woman corrected. "There's a difference. Left things rot. Kept things wait." She jerked her chin at Jude. "Quill left word for you, thief. Said you'd come back with stars in your pockets and a soldier at your side, and that we'd know the hour because the white lamps would gutter." She nodded upward, toward the ceiling, toward the city above. "They started guttering an hour ago. All across the district. The watchers can't explain it."

Jude's hand drifted to his chest, where the six shards sat behind his ribs like coals. Serah saw him feel them answer—saw the candle flames lean toward him, all at once, like flowers toward sun.

"They're not guttering," he said softly. "They're *bowing*."

The sharp-faced woman's mouth tightened. "Then you'd best hear what Quill left, and quickly. Because there's worse news than dead seers tonight." She lowered her voice, and the coloured flames dipped with it. "Tomorrow at dawn, the Chancellor holds a public rite at the Spire. He's going to bind the covenant to himself in front of the whole city. And every soul in this room"—she gestured at the children, the sleepers, the girl with the bending flame—"is on his list for the purge afterward. All of us. Every unregistered mind in the capital. Starting at sunrise."

Serah looked at Jude. Jude looked at the chalked names on the wall, at the empty chair, at the steaming kettle no one would ever pour.

"Dawn," he said. "Then we've got one night to take back the sky."
