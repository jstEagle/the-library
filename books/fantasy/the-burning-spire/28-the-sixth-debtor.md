## 28. The Sixth Debtor

The memory came back to me in the dark hours before dawn, the way drowned things come back to shore.

I had taken it from Vaelith's mind barely a week past—had torn it loose with hooks I still didn't fully understand, from a being three thousand years old who had nearly peeled my own thoughts like fruit skin in return. Most of what I'd stolen from him had been useless vastness: centuries of weather over the Otherworld, the names of dead courts, the taste of a fruit that no longer grew anywhere. But one fragment had lodged itself in me and refused to dissolve, and now, lying awake in the safehouse above the shuttered dye-works while Serah slept an arm's length away, it finally showed me its face.

A road outside the capital. Rain. A woman walking beside a hooded man—the hooded stranger, my stranger, the one whose memory had started all of this—and the woman was laughing at something he'd said, and Vaelith was watching them both from a distance that rain could not cross, because Vaelith had been sent to watch them. Sent by the Queen. And behind *them*, half-hidden in the grey wash of the storm, another figure. A human figure. Tall, narrow-shouldered, with a way of holding his hands folded behind his back as though the world were a document he was proofreading.

I knew those hands. I had seen them raised on a balcony overlooking Lantern District, granting edicts to a starving city. I had seen them sign death warrants for children with starlight in their blood.

Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow had walked with the covenant-bearers. Had spoken with them. Had—Vaelith's memory shivered here, blurred at the edges where even a fae lord's recollection flinched—

Had taken something from them.

* * *

I sat up in the dark. The dye-works smelled of old mordant and wet wool, and somewhere below, one of the gutter children was talking in her sleep, murmuring numbers, always numbers—Mother Quill had taught them counting charms before the raid, before the fire, before we carried her body out through the ash-chutes while hunters kicked the doors in. Serah lay with her back to me, her sword within reach even in sleep, her breathing shallow in the way of someone who had learned never to sleep deeply again.

"Jude." She said it without turning, without waking fully. Three weeks of running together had taught her the sound of me thinking too loudly. "What is it?"

"I know where the last shard is."

That woke her properly. She rolled toward me, and even in the dark I could see the bruised hollows under her eyes, the new scar along her jaw from the vault ward that had branded us both traitors. "Tell me."

"Marrow has it." My voice came out strange, flat, as though someone else were speaking through my mouth. "He took it years ago—from the stranger's companion. From the woman on the road. He stole it before I ever stole anything. Before I ever—" I stopped. Started again. "The circle closes, Serah. Every piece of this leads back to him."

She lit the stub of candle they allowed themselves—one hour a night, no more, light drew eyes—and its small flame made a gold island around us. She pulled her notebook from beneath her coat, the one she'd kept since her hunter days, pages soft as cloth from handling, and began writing as I talked, because that was how she held the world together: ink first, feeling after.

"Walk me through it," she said. "Slowly. What exactly did you see?"

So I told her. The road. The rain. The companion—I described her as best Vaelith's memory allowed: broad-faced, quick-eyed, a laugh that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than her chest. The stranger had called her Wren, once, in the memory. They had carried the shards between them, six pieces of the original covenant split across two minds so that no single theft, no single torture, no single bargain could ever reassemble the whole. It was elegant. It was the kind of plan you made when you expected to be hunted.

And then Marrow had found them. Or been found by them—or perhaps, some cold voice in me suggested, he had sought them out deliberately, already hungry, already reading the world as a document awaiting his signature.

"He took hers," I said. "The stranger escaped with his half—that's why he came to me three years ago, why he begged a gutter-thief to hide his memories inside a mind too small to matter. But the companion—" I turned my hands over, looked at my palms as if the answer might be written there. "Vaelith's memory doesn't show what happened to her. Only that afterward, Marrow walked differently. Straighter. Like a man carrying something precious and heavy in the center of his skull."

Serah's pen had stopped moving. She stared at the candle flame, and I watched her do the arithmetic I had already done, watched it land on her face like a blow.

"The purges," she said quietly. "When the wards fell, Marrow ordered total memory-purges of the lower quarters. Everyone assumed it was terror tactics. Control." Her hand tightened on the pen until the knuckles went white. "But if he holds a covenant-shard—if it's *inside* him, woven through his own memories—then every purge he orders is also a search. He's not just erasing minds, Jude. He's combing the city for anyone else who might have brushed against the bearers. Anyone who might hold a fragment he doesn't control. He's burning the countryside to make sure he's the only well left standing."

"And when he binds the covenant to himself," I said, "he'll need all six. Five are accounted for—four with us, one in the exiled lord's head, which I've already bled. The sixth is in *him*. Which means the final debtor on my list isn't a widow or a lamplighter or a frightened child." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It's the most protected mind in two worlds. It's the man who built the cage we're all living in."

* * *

We spent the morning doing what fugitives do: nothing that looked like anything. Serah mended a seam in her coat with thread she'd unraveled from a curtain. I ground dried nettle for tea that tasted like punishment. Around us, the safehouse breathed with the quiet industry of the hidden—a boy named Tam copying Mother Quill's surviving notes onto scraps of wallpaper, a dyer's widow boiling bark for ink, everyone moving softly, everyone listening always for boots on stairs.

But underneath the domestic surface, we were planning a thing so absurd that I kept waiting for one of us to say it aloud and burst out laughing.

We were going to steal from Aldric Marrow.

Not pickpocket him—not that any thief alive could lift so much as a button off the Chancellor of the realm, surrounded as he was by wards layered like an onion, guards rotated hourly, and a paranoia refined over years into something approaching art. We were going to reach into his mind, past whatever defenses a man who had trafficked with fae lords might have learned to raise, and take a shard of the original covenant out of the middle of his living memory. The same theft I had performed on Vaelith, except Vaelith had been dying and distracted and, in the end, almost willing. Marrow would be none of these things.

"There has to be another way," Serah said, for the fourth time. We stood at the dye-works' high window, looking out over a city gone the color of dishwater. Since the stars had died, no proper night had fallen anywhere; the sky just dimmed to a bruise and brightened again, and people had begun to forget what darkness was for. "The Spire itself. When he performs the rite—all six shards will be in one place. His head, yes, but the rite requires the others brought to him. We could let him gather us. Let him think he's won."

"And then?" I asked.

"Then we're standing in the heart of his power with every guard in the capital between us and the door."

"Yes," I said. "Whereas breaking into the Chancellery tonight only requires us to defeat the wards, the guards, the wards inside the guards, and a man who may have spent twenty years learning to defend his own mind against exactly the kind of magic I do."

"You say that like it's a reason to prefer it."

"It's a reason to prefer it *tonight*. Because every day we wait, he purges another quarter. Every day, more minds go grey, and somewhere in those greying crowds might be someone else who met the bearers, someone whose scattered fragments could have helped us understand the rite, the spire, the cost—" I stopped myself before the word *cost* could open the door it always tried to open. The door with Serah's face behind it. "Besides. There's something else. Something Vaelith's memory showed me that I haven't told you yet."

She turned from the window. Outside, a patrol crossed the square below, six hunters in grey, herding a line of civilians toward the purge-wagons. We both watched until they were gone. It had become a habit between us, this shared witnessing—we saw the same horrors, and neither of us pretended otherwise, and somehow that had become the truest intimacy I had ever known.

"Tell me," she said.

"The companion. Wren. In the last clear part of Vaelith's memory—the part right before the blurring—she's arguing with the stranger. Begging him, actually. And she says—" I closed my eyes, let the stolen words rise. "*Split it thinner, then. Split it six ways if you must. But promise me that whoever carries the last piece can't be someone who wants it.*"

Serah went very still.

"Marrow wanted it," I said. "More than anything. That's why the theft worked on her and failed on the stranger—no, wait, that's not—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes, sorting. "No. What I mean is: the shards respond to wanting. Vaelith told me that much before I took his memories apart. The covenant-shards aren't objects, they're *obligations*. They settle deepest in minds that hunger for them. Marrow has carried his piece for twenty years, and it hasn't burned him out, hasn't driven him mad, because he *wants* it the way other men want breath. Which means it's woven through everything he is. Every ambition. Every cruelty. Every memory of every person he's ever stepped on to climb." I lowered my hands. "To take it cleanly, I'll have to walk through all of it. Everything he is. And I'm afraid, Serah, not of failing. I'm afraid of what I'll recognize."

"Recognize?"

"Ambition looks the same from the inside everywhere," I said. "Even mine. Even a small thief's ambition. I've spent three years telling myself my rules made me different from men like him. Never take much. Never be seen. But the rules were just smaller walls around the same hunger—to matter, to be more than invisible, to hold power over something in a world that gave me none." The confession came out of me like a splinter leaving flesh, painful and relieving at once. "If I go into his mind carrying that, he'll see it. Things like him always see their own reflection."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. Then she did something I hadn't expected: she took my hands, both of them, and held them in her own scarred grip, and looked at me with the expression she wore only when she was about to say something true enough to hurt.

"When you took my hour from me," she said, "in the Lantern District, the first time you ran from me—you left something in its place. I told you once. A kindness. A moment of my mother's kitchen that I'd lost years before, returned to me like a gift I hadn't earned." Her thumb moved over my knuckles. "You've spent this whole journey telling me you're a thief. But thieves don't leave gifts. Whatever you find in Marrow's mind—whatever hunger you recognize—you should remember that the man I chose to love leaves kitchens where he takes hours. That's the self you bring with you. Not the other one."

I couldn't speak for a moment. Outside, the bruised sky pressed down on the city, and somewhere a purge-wagon rattled over cobblestones, and none of it, for one breath, mattered as much as her thumb on my hand.

"Serah," I started, and stopped, because the sentence I had been building for weeks—the one about what the rite would cost, about what I would have to pour into the flame, about her face going with everything else—rose in my throat and choked there. *Not yet,* I told myself. *One debt at a time.*

"We'll need her credentials," I said instead. "Your hunter's seal. If we're going to get near the Chancellery at all, it has to look routine. An inspection. A report delivered in person—Marrow trusts you, or trusted you, before the vault. Before the branding. Men like him trust their own instruments even after the instruments crack, because believing in betrayal means believing the world is bigger than his control of it."

"That's a lot of faith to put in his arrogance."

"It's the only door he leaves open," I said. "Arrogance is a lock that only opens from the inside. I've picked enough minds to know."

* * *

Night came—the sky's daily bruising, the false dusk that fooled no one anymore. Serah dressed in her old hunter's coat, the grey one with the Crown's sigil half-torn from the shoulder, and I watched her pin the seal back on with fingers that didn't shake, and I thought: *this is what courage looks like when it's tired.* Not blazing. Just persistent. A woman putting on the uniform of everything she'd betrayed, to walk back into the teeth of it, because the alternative was letting a tyrant swallow two worlds.

Tam brought us the last thing we needed: a scrap of wallpaper bearing Mother Quill's handwriting, copied from notes salvaged from the cathedral's ashes. The blind seer had written it in the weeks before the raid, when her sightless eyes had still been reading futures in smoke. Serah read it aloud, her voice low, and the words settled over the room like frost:

*Six debts, six doors. The last door opens inward. Beware the bearer who became the bar.*

"She knew," Serah said. "Before any of us. She knew the sixth shard was in him."

"She knew more than that." I touched the paper, and felt, faintly, under the ink, the residue of a seer's craft—smoke and starlight and something older. "Beware the bearer who became the bar. She wasn't warning me about Marrow." I looked up. "She was warning me about what the shards do to the people who carry them. About what carrying all six might make *me*."

The candle had burned down to its final quarter-hour. Somewhere below, the children were asleep, and the widow was keeping watch, and the city ground on beneath its starless sky, ten thousand purged minds dreaming grey dreams, not knowing what had been taken from them or that anything had ever been brighter.

"One more thing," Serah said, at the door, ready to go out into the dark and lie her way into the Chancellery. "If something goes wrong in there—if you get inside his head and can't get out—"

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

I smiled, and it hurt, the way true things hurt. "You come in after me. Blades first, questions later."

"Damn right, questions later." She pulled her hood up, and for a moment she was the hunter again, the woman who had chased me through markets I knew blind, and I loved her so fiercely and so hopelessly that I had to look away before she saw it in my face—before either of us remembered what the rite would someday ask of me.

The door closed behind her. I stood alone in the candle-dark with five shards humming against my chest like caged birds, and I spoke the name of the sixth debtor into the silence, testing its weight, closing the circle my whole crooked life had been drawing without my knowledge.

"Aldric Marrow," I said. "You stole from a bearer before I ever learned to steal at all. Time, then, for the gutter to collect its due."

Outside, far across the rooftops, the Burning Spire stood black against the bruised sky—dark, cold, and waiting. And in the highest room of the Chancellery beside it, a lamp still burned in a window I could not see, above a desk where a man with folded hands was already beginning, I was certain, to dream of crowns.

Tomorrow we would walk through his front door.

Tonight, I sharpened my hooks, and waited for the false dawn.
