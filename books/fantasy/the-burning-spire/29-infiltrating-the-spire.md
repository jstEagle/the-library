## 29. Infiltrating the Spire

The credentials weighed more than they used to.

Serah Vane held the iron badge up to the grey morning light and turned it slowly between her fingers. It was a small thing—a hexagonal plate stamped with the Crown's sigil, a seven-pointed star within a circle, and beneath it her name in letters that had once meant something: *SERAH VANE, HUNTER, THIRD CIRCLE*. The metal was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. It remembered what she had been. It did not care what she was now.

*Traitor*, said the placards on every corner, her face inked above the word in heavy black strokes. The likeness was good. Whoever had drawn it had captured the set of her jaw, the scar through her left eyebrow, the particular flatness of a hunter's stare. They had not captured the hour she'd stood in a rain-slicked square and refused to put steel to a child's throat, because artists only draw what they can see, and courage looks like nothing at all from the outside.

"You're staring at it like it might bite," Jude said behind her.

"It might." She tucked the badge into the inner pocket of her coat—the hunter's longcoat she'd kept, absurdly, through everything: the flight from the Lantern District, the weeks in the gutter cathedral, the crossing back over a border that no longer existed except as memory and misery. The coat still smelled faintly of the tallow they'd used to waterproof it, and under that, older, of the Spire's incense—frankincense and cold iron. Her whole life had been folded into this garment. "Badges don't bite. But the man who checks them will remember my name."

"Then we make sure he remembers it fondly." Jude came to stand beside her at the narrow window of the safehouse, a cobbler's attic above the Tanner's Row canal. Below, the city moved in its new rhythm—or rather, failed to move. Since Marrow's purges began, the lower quarters walked like people wading through deep water. Faces smoothed of anything troublesome. Eyes that met yours and slid away, afraid to hold a thought long enough for it to be seen. Serah had watched a woman in the fish market laugh at something her daughter said, then stop mid-laugh, confused, as if the laughter had been confiscated too.

The sky above the rooftops was the color of unwashed wool. No stars at night anymore—not since the wards fell, not since the covenant broke—and even the sun came through thin, apologetic, like light filtered through a shroud.

"The Spire's heart is on the ninth landing," Serah said, turning from the window. "You know that part of the plan. Say it back to me."

Jude sighed the sigh of a man who had been drilled. "We enter through the hunters' postern on the north face, because the great doors have been sealed since the Ascension Rite preparations began. My papers say I'm your prisoner—" he lifted his wrists, where the manacles sat loose enough to slip, painted grey to look like iron, "—a fae-touched thief taken alive, which explains why a disgraced hunter would be walking back into the building that wants her head. We go up the western stair because the eastern one has ward-lines fresh enough to taste. And at the ninth landing—" he paused, and some of the flippancy went out of him, "—Marrow is binding himself to the covenant with six shards of the old law, and if we don't reach him before moonrise tomorrow, there won't be a mortal realm left to be traitors in."

"And?"

"And I don't steal anything unless you say so." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. Three years of thieving had taught him that a promise kept small was a promise kept. "Not a memory. Not an hour. Not a breath. Nothing from anyone in that building without your word."

"Because every theft leaves a wake."

"Because every theft leaves a wake," he agreed. "And a spire full of witch-hunters doesn't need ripples. It needs us quiet as dust."

Serah looked at him a moment longer than the plan required. He had lost weight since the Otherworld; the court of embers had fed them honey and ash-cakes and stories, but the road home had taken it all back with interest. There was a new scar along his jaw where a hedge-warden's hook had caught him at the border. His hands, always quick, were quick now with a tremor underneath—he'd bled memories that were never his in the exiled lord's hall, and some mornings she found him sitting very still, trying to sort which griefs belonged to him.

She wanted to say something. She had a whole speech saved up somewhere behind her ribs, the kind of thing you say before a battle, in case battles keep their promises. But speeches were for people who believed in last words, and Serah Vane had spent eleven years writing reports about men whose last words had accomplished nothing except making their killers hesitate.

So instead she said, "Check the manacles again," and went to get her sword.

* * *

They reached the north face of the Burning Spire as the bells rang the fourth hour past noon.

It had been called burning for three hundred years, ever since the first flame was lit in its heart—a fire that needed no wood, that burned without consuming, that the old chronicles said was a piece of the covenant itself made visible. Pilgrims had come from every province to see it. Children were named for it. Serah had been sworn to its protection at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble while a Circle-mother pressed the iron badge into her palms and told her, *This is the wall between the world and the dark, and you are a stone in it.*

She had believed it the way you believe in your own heartbeat: completely, and without ever really looking at it.

Now she stood across the canal from the Spire's northern wall and saw it plainly for the first time. The flame was out. Everyone knew it—Marrow had announced that the fire was *resting*, that it would burn brighter than ever once the new covenant was sealed—but knowing and seeing were different things. Without the flame's glow behind them, the Spire's thousand windows were just holes. The pale stone looked less like a tower than a spine, stripped of everything living, standing upright in a dead body. Scaffolding crawled up its southern face where masons were carving Marrow's new sigil over the old star: a single closed eye inside a crown.

"They've carved an eye over the stars," Jude murmured. "Subtle as a boot to the teeth."

"He wants people to feel watched, not inspired." Serah pulled her hood forward. Under it, her hair was cropped short now—she'd cut it in the cathedral with a fish-gutting knife, one more thing the old Serah would never have done. "Eyes are cheaper than stars."

The postern door was exactly where memory said it would be: a squat arch of blackened oak set into the wall's shadow, wide enough for two men abreast, guarded by one. That was the insult of it. When Serah had served, the posterns had each held four hunters and a ward-singer. Now the Crown's strength was stretched across a city full of emptied minds, and the Spire—certain of its own sanctity—was garrisoned by boys.

The guard was a boy. Eighteen at most, pike held wrong, shoulders already tired of holding it. He had the soft chin and chapped knuckles of someone who'd been pulled off a farm and handed a uniform, and when the two figures came down the towpath toward him—one tall, hooded, walking with the rolling stride of a hunter; the other smaller, wrists chained, head bowed—he straightened so fast his pike butt skidded on wet stone.

"Halt! State—state your business." The voice cracked on *business*.

Serah let the hood fall back.

She watched it happen in his face, the sequence she knew by heart because she'd worn it herself once: recognition, disbelief, fear, and then—the worst one, the one she'd counted on—relief. Because Serah Vane's face was on every wall in the city marked TRAITOR, but faces on walls are flat things, and this boy had probably been told the traitor Vane was eight feet tall and breathed smoke. What he saw instead was a Third Circle hunter, badge displayed in her open palm, expression carved from the same stone as every officer who'd ever made him feel small.

"Hunter Vane," she said, and let the old authority pour through the words like water through a broken dam. "Third Circle, retired under edict nine-twelve. I've spent four months in the field tracking the fae-touched thief you see before you. Four months, and I'll thank you not to make me stand in the wind explaining it."

"I—no, Hunter. Of course, Hunter." He fumbled a logbook off his belt. "I just need to—there's a register—"

"There is." She stepped close enough that he could read the badge, and while his eyes were dutifully on the sigil, her voice dropped into the register she'd used on a hundred green recruits, the one that carried both kindness and threat in equal measure. "And when you write my name in it, son, spell it correctly. Vane. There are officers upstairs who will check, and some of them trained me. If your hand shakes and the entry looks doubtful, whose name do you think gets questioned? Yours or mine?"

His pen scratched. *VANE, S., 3C. Prisoner: one (1), male, fae-touched.*

"Open the door."

The bar scraped. The postern swung inward onto darkness that smelled of stone and old smoke and, beneath it, something sweeter and wronger—the scent of the covenant-shard Marrow wore, leaking through nine floors of rock like rot through a floorboard. Jude made a small sound low in his throat. Serah felt him sway beside her and steadied him with a hand at his elbow, disguised as a shove.

"Walk," she said loudly, for the boy's benefit, and pushed her thief through the door.

Behind them, the postern boomed shut. The lock turned. Somewhere in the boy's chest, she suspected, a small pride was blooming: *I registered the famous traitor Vane.* She hoped, distantly, that when the truth came out—if it came out—he wouldn't hang for it. She'd written his description in her head without meaning to. Young. Soft-chinned. A mole below the left ear. If it came to it, she'd swear he'd been drugged.

The stairwell swallowed them.

* * *

Inside, the Spire was colder than the street.

That was the first wrongness. The second was the silence. Serah remembered a building that hummed—ward-singers chanting on the landings, boots on marble, the great flame's crackle audible even through stone, a sound like a hearth the size of the sky. Now the corridors rang with their own footsteps and nothing else. The sconces burned with ordinary oil-flames, yellow and guttering, and their light made the white marble look like skin drained of blood.

Jude walked half a step behind her, manacled hands loose, eyes moving constantly. She could feel him reading the building the way she never could—tasting the air for magic, feeling for the residue of wards the way a blind man feels for walls. Twice his fingers twitched toward his sleeve, where three charms rode hidden against his wrist, and twice he forced them still.

"Western stair," she breathed, barely a shape of sound.

They crossed the ground-floor hall beneath the eyes of two hunters playing cards at a duty desk, who glanced up, saw a Third Circle escorting a prisoner, and looked back down. The card game mattered more than the prisoner. That was Marrow's city in miniature: everyone so tired, so frightened, so hollowed by the purges that duty had become a thing done with the hands only.

The western stair rose in a spiral, lit every tenth step by a lamp turned down to a coal. Serah climbed with her sword-hand free inside her cloak and counted landings the way she'd counted them for eleven years. Second: the archive, its doors barred with new chains. Third: the interrogation rooms, silent now—the purges had made questioning obsolete; why interrogate a mind you could simply empty? Fourth: the ward-singers' dormitory, and here Jude stopped so abruptly she nearly walked into him.

"What?" she whispered.

He was staring at a door, at the small brass plate beside it. Names had been chalked over most of the plates in the corridor—rooms reassigned, occupants purged—but this one was clean, and engraved: *SISTER MAURELLE, WARD-SINGER, SECOND VOICE.*

"She sang the dusk-wards," Jude said quietly. "Back when they still flickered. I used to feel her work from my shop—like someone humming in another room. Comfortable. She was good." His jaw worked. "Is she—"

"Gone, or emptied, or hiding." Serah touched his arm. "We can't carry everyone, Jude. Not today. Today we carry six shards and one chance."

He looked at the door a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, hard, and climbed.

Fifth landing. Sixth. On the seventh, they heard voices and melted into an alcove behind a statue of the First Flamekeeper, whose marble face had been defaced sometime in the last month—the features sanded smooth, so that the founder of the hunters' order stared out of history as a blank. Two clerks passed below, murmuring.

"—the rite chamber's sealed until moonrise, Chancellor's orders—"

"—and the enforcer? Corvess?"

"On the ninth. He sleeps there now. Like a dog on a grave."

The footsteps faded. Serah let out a slow breath. *Corvess.* Marrow's enforcer, the blade he'd sent after her, the man whose duel waited somewhere in tomorrow whether she sought it or not. He slept on the ninth landing, between them and the spire's heart, like a lock made of muscle and malice.

"Charming building you used to work in," Jude murmured.

"I used to think it was a temple." She pushed off the wall. "Come on. One landing left."

The eighth-to-ninth stair was blocked.

Not barred—blocked by a ward-line, a ribbon of silver script laid across the steps like a tripwire, humming faintly. Serah's stomach dropped. She knew this working. Every hunter learned to lay it and every hunter learned to break it, and the breaking required either a licensed null-stone—which she did not have—or a mage.

She looked at Jude. Jude looked at the line. Something almost like a smile touched his mouth, there and gone.

"You said nothing without my word," he whispered.

"I know what I said." She crouched, studying the script. Fresh-laid, tonight or yesterday, keyed to alarm rather than harm—it would scream the whole Spire awake if crossed wrongly. "Can you unweave it instead of stealing through it? Quietly?"

"Unweaving's slower. Stealing's faster." He flexed his bound hands. "But unweaving leaves no wake. Give me a minute and keep your ears open."

What followed was the strangest minute of Serah's life. Jude knelt on the stairs and did—nothing, visibly. His lips barely moved. But the silver script began to change: letters dimming, lines going slack, the whole ribbon of light relaxing like a fist unclenching. He wasn't taking the ward apart, she realized. He was persuading it. Talking to it in whatever language wards dreamed in, telling it that it had already sounded, that the alarm had rung an hour ago and been answered, that it was tired, that it could rest.

The last glyph faded to grey. Jude swayed. Serah caught his shoulder.

"Cost?" she asked.

"A headache and a lie I'll have to live with." He got up. "Wards are honest things. I hate lying to them."

They stepped over the sleeping script and climbed into the ninth landing.

* * *

The heart of the Burning Spire was a round chamber at the tower's crown, and Serah had seen it lit by glory: the great flame rising thirty feet in its basin of black glass, ward-singers kneeling in a ring, the whole room gold as the inside of a bell. She had expected darkness now. She had not expected *cold*—frost feathered on the inside of the windows, and her breath showed, and the great basin at the center held nothing but a crust of grey ash, like a hearth dead a hundred years.

Around the ash-basin, the rite was being prepared. Six pillars of pale stone had been raised in a ring, and five of them already held their cargo: shards of light, each one caged in crystal, each one pulsing with a slow heartbeat she felt in her teeth. Five. The sixth pillar stood empty, waiting.

Between the fifth pillar and the stair, wrapped in a cloak of storm-grey wool, sat a mountain of a man who was not asleep at all.

Corvess opened his eyes.

For one endless moment, nobody moved. Serah's hand found her sword-hilt. Jude went very still beside her, the stillness of prey that has decided pretending to be stone is its best remaining option. The enforcer's face was a map of old violence—a nose broken crooked, a white seam of scar from ear to chin—and his eyes, pale as frost water, moved from Serah to Jude and back with the unhurried interest of a man pricing meat.

"Well," Corvess said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "The traitor comes home."

"Corvess." She kept her voice level, though her pulse hammered against her collar. "Step aside."

"Marrow said you'd come." He rose, and rising took him a long time, because there was so much of him—six and a half feet of him, chain mail under the wool, a two-handed blade leaning against the pillar within reach of his hand. "He said it the night he branded you. Said, *she'll come back, they always come back to the place that made them.* I told him no. I told him a hunter who breaks is a hunter who runs far." The frost-pale eyes flicked to Jude. "But here you are. And you brought the little thief with the clever hands."

"Jude Kavanagh is under my arrest," Serah said. "By right of capture. You have no claim—"

"I have a claim." Corvess smiled, and the scar-seam whitened. "The Chancellor promised the man who brings him the sixth shard a seat at the Ascension. A place in the new order. Immortality, Vane. Did you know that's what he's selling? Not safety. Not order. *Forever.*" He picked up the great blade as if it were a walking stick. "I mean to collect. So you can walk back down those stairs, or you can be carried down in pieces. Either way, the thief stays."

The frost crept along the windows. The five shards pulsed in their crystal cages, five slow hearts beating in the dark, and on the empty sixth pillar the waiting crystal caught the lamplight and threw it back like an open eye.

Serah drew her sword. The sound of it rang through the dead chamber and up into the cold dark above, where no stars had burned for a year—and somewhere beyond the ceiling, beyond the stone, she thought she heard something answer. Faint. Far. Like a flame remembering how to breathe.

"Jude," she said quietly, without taking her eyes off the enforcer. "Run for the pillars."

Corvess came on like a landslide learning to walk.
