## 32. All That I Have Stolen

The Chancellor's mind was a cathedral built on a swamp.

Jude felt it the moment he slipped past the outer defenses—not walls of thought the way human minds were walled, all brick and mortar and habit, but architecture. Deliberate. Marrow had spent decades organizing his own skull the way other men organized empires: corridors of ambition vaulted high, archives of grievance catalogued and cross-referenced, and beneath it all, in the flooded foundations where no light reached, the thing he had bargained for.

Jude stood in the burning antechamber of the spire's heart with blood in his mouth and six years of theft humming under his skin, and he reached into that architecture like a hand into dark water.

*Steady,* he told himself. *You've done this ten thousand times.*

But he hadn't done this. He had never taken from a mind this vast, this fortified, this *willing to burn itself down around him* rather than yield.

Marrow knelt at the base of the dead flame, both hands pressed to the cold hearthstone, and he was screaming. Not with his mouth—his mouth was reciting the rite in a voice gone smooth and liturgical—but behind his eyes, where the fifth shard Jude had torn from the exiled fae lord was doing its work. The shard did not sit quietly in a mind like Marrow's. It *unmade*. It found every seam in the architecture and pried.

"You cannot have it," Marrow said aloud, between verses. His voice cracked on the words, and for one naked moment he sounded not like the most powerful man in two realms but like an old man kneeling on cold stone, holding the only thing he'd ever loved. "It is mine. I paid for it. Do you know what I paid, boy? Do you know what I *gave*?"

"I know what you took," Jude said.

He walked forward. Every step cost him. The air in the spire's heart was thick with cinders and old divinity, and the wards Marrow had raised around himself—the stolen covenant-shard burning in his skull like a crown of nails—pushed back against Jude's approach with a pressure that made his ears ring and his vision swim in colors that had no names. Serah was somewhere behind him, holding the stair against the enforcer; he could hear steel ringing on steel, hear her breath sawing, and every instinct he owned screamed at him to turn around, to go to her, to—

*No.* He had learned that discipline in the gutter markets, lifting grief from widows and selling it back as comfort. The work came first or not at all. You never took much. You never hesitated mid-theft. Hesitation was how thieves died.

Marrow's mind opened before him like a wound.

Jude stepped in.

* * *

Inside, it was raining ash.

The Chancellor's memory-landscape stretched gray and endless under a sky the color of old teeth, and everything was ordered, everything was *labeled*. Jude moved through corridors of remembered slights—Marrow had kept them all, every sneer from every superior, every door closed, every hand not shaken—and past galleries of triumph rendered in obsessive detail: his first appointment, his first execution order signed without trembling, the night he had sat across from an exiled fae lord whose name had been eaten out of history and traded the stars of heaven for a seat at the table of gods.

And there, at the bottom of everything, in the flooded foundation where the architecture met the swamp:

The sixth shard.

It did not look like the others. The others had been fragments of light, warm, almost shy, curling toward Jude's palm like cats remembering a home. This one was caged. Marrow had wrapped it in layers of ritual and law and sheer bloody-minded will, had built around it a shrine of his own worst memories—Jude could see them through the cage's bars, looping endlessly: a mother's face, disappointed. A brother, better loved. A young Aldric Marrow standing outside a mage's shop in the Lantern District, turned away, turned away, turned away, because his talent had been tested and found wanting, because some people were born to be doors and some to be wind.

"Oh," Jude breathed, inside the man's mind, inside the rain of ash. "Oh, you poor, stupid, magnificent bastard."

That was the horror of it. That was the thing he would carry, if he carried anything out of this place: he understood. He had built three years of careful invisibility out of the same clay. Marrow had simply been given more reach, and had reached.

The cage resisted him. Of course it did. Jude pressed his will against it and the will pressed back, and somewhere far above—in the burning chamber, in the world—he heard Marrow's recitation falter, heard the old man feel the theft beginning, and the whole mindscape shuddered like a ship struck below the waterline.

*You can't take it gently,* Jude realized. *There's no gentle left in him.*

So he stopped being gentle.

He had never taken much—that was his rule, his religion, the line between thief and monster. But rules were for markets, and this was war, and the man whose mind he stood in had purged whole quarters of the city, had hollowed thousands of heads into smiling gray nothing, had broken heaven itself because a door had been closed in his face when he was nineteen years old.

Jude Kavanagh took *everything*.

He tore the cage open and reached past it, past the shard, into the flooded foundations, and he drank. Marrow's first betrayal. His last kindness—there had been one, small, buried so deep even Marrow had forgotten it, and Jude took that too, because the rite would need it, because even a man like this deserved to have his one decent moment put to use. He took the taste of the ink Marrow used for death warrants. He took the sound of his own mother's voice saying his name. He took twenty years of ambition and forty years of resentment and the specific, exquisite memory of the night the stars went out, which Marrow had watched from a tower window with wine in his hand and tears running freely down his face, weeping at the beauty of what he'd done.

The mindscape collapsed around him like a burned building.

Jude rode the collapse out, clutching the sixth shard to his chest, and fell back into his body gasping, on his knees on the hearthstone, with blood running freely from his nose and his ears and, when he touched his face, the corners of his eyes, as if his skull had been the thing bleeding all along.

Across the chamber, Lord Chancellor Aldric Marrow swayed on his knees, mouth still moving, reciting a rite to a flame that was not there, to a god that was now a thief in a ragged coat. Then, slowly, like a puppet with cut strings folding into its own architecture, he toppled sideways and lay still. Breathing. Empty. Alive in the way a house is alive after everyone has left it.

Jude looked at him for a long time.

He should have felt triumphant. He tried to find it in himself, the way you'd check your pockets for a coin you were sure was there. It wasn't. There was only the terrible tenderness he always felt after a theft, the thief's grief, the knowledge of how much of a person lived in their memories and how little remained once you'd lifted them out.

*I know what I am,* he thought. *I've always known. I just never had to do it to someone who mattered before.*

Except Marrow hadn't mattered. That was the trick of it, wasn't it? The hooded stranger had known it three years ago: hide the covenant inside a mind too small to matter. And Jude had spent three years believing himself too small to matter, and the belief had been the armor, and now here he stood, mattering enormously, holding heaven in his hands.

Steel rang behind him. Stopped. A body hit stone—big, final—and then footsteps, fast and uneven, and Serah was there.

She was a ruin. Her coat was shredded along one arm, blood sheeted to the elbow—his stomach dropped before he saw the blood wasn't all hers—and her sword was still drawn, though there was nothing left in the chamber to fight. She looked at Marrow's empty shell, then at Jude kneeling in the spreading dark of his own blood, and something crossed her face that he had no word for.

"Tell me you have it," she said.

He opened his hand.

Six shards rose from his palm.

They had been separate things until now—fragments gathered across months, from a dying queen's emissary, from a widow's grief, from the Crown's quiet vaults, from the mind of an exiled lord, and now from the ruined cathedral of Aldric Marrow. Separate things, each warm and strange, each singing its single note.

In the open air above his bleeding hand, they stopped being separate.

Jude watched it happen and could do nothing but watch. The shards drifted together, slowly, the way iron filings find a magnet, the way lost things find their way home, and where they touched they fused—not with light, at first, but with *recognition*, a soundless chord struck in a register below hearing. Then the light came. It poured up his arm and through him, and for one endless moment Jude Kavanagh stood inside the original covenant entire.

He saw it all.

He saw the world when it was young and the veil between realms was not a wall but a *doorway*, propped open in love, mortals and fae passing through with gifts and songs and children who belonged to both. He saw the Burning Spire as it was meant to be—a hearth, not a tower, its flame fed by consent, by memory freely given, generation after generation laying down what they chose to lay down so the door would hold. He saw the six anchors in the sky that men had called stars and the fae had called *witnesses*, burning over both worlds, keeping the terms honest.

And he saw the price.

The knowledge arrived not as words but as certainty, cold and complete, the way you know your own name. The flame was out. The covenant was broken. To reforge it, the flame required fuel, and the only fuel it had ever accepted—the only fuel it *could* accept—was a bearer's whole held memory. Everything. Not a tithe. Not a charm's worth. Not the dregs of strangers' lives he'd skimmed for coin.

Everything.

Every theft. Every charm he'd ever crafted, all the borrowed joy he'd sold in the Lantern District, gone back into the fire. Three years of careful invisibility—the routes he knew blind, the faces he'd memorized, the small warm stockpile of moments he'd hoarded against loneliness. Mother Quill's voice reading his fate in smoke. The gutter cathedral full of candle stubs and children. The widow's face when she got her son's last words back.

Serah.

The alley in the Lantern District, lantern-light on wet cobblestones, her blade at his throat and her eyes furious and bright above it. The hour he'd stolen from her and the kindness he'd left in its place. The fugitives' fire, and her laugh, low and surprised, like she'd forgotten she owned one. Her hand finding his in the dark of the Otherworld. The night they'd spent being honest, knowing the price, spending it anyway. Her name. Her face. The way she said *Jude* like it was a word in a language only she spoke.

All of it. Into the flame.

"Jude." Serah's voice, very far away, coming closer. "Jude, you're crying. What did you see?"

He realized his hand had closed over the assembled covenant, that the light was leaking between his fingers, that he was already walking toward the hearthstone where the dead flame waited—because of course he was. There had never been a choice. There had been a choice three years ago, when the hooded stranger begged him to steal a memory, and he'd made it then, without knowing: *yes, I'll carry it.* Everything since had been the long road from that yes to this one.

"It wants everything," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "All of it. Whatever the bearer holds."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. He counted them. He wanted to keep them.

"No," Serah said.

"Serah—"

"*No.*" She caught his arm, and her grip was the grip of a woman who had held a stair alone against an enforcer bred for slaughter, and it did not shake at all. "There's another way. There has to be. We didn't come this far—we didn't lose Quill, we didn't cross the veil, we didn't—" Her voice finally cracked, right down the middle, and what came out of the crack was not a hunter's voice at all. "I just got you. Do you understand? I spent my whole life being the law's instrument and I only just became a person, and it happened next to you, and you are not going to pour yourself into that fire and stand up empty."

He looked at her. He made himself look at her properly, the way you look at a thing you intend to keep forever, knowing forever was about to be taken as payment.

"Come here," he said.

She came. She was shaking now, now that the fighting was done, now that the enemy was a dead flame and a price, and he held her the way he'd held nothing in his life—both arms, whole weight, no craft in it, no skill, just a man holding the best thing he'd ever stolen, except she'd never been stolen, she'd come to him freely, that was the whole miracle of her, that was the thing he'd never be able to forget because he was about to forget it—

"The stars are going out," he whispered into her hair. "Did you see? One went while we fought. There'll be none left by morning, and then the veil doesn't just fall, it *dissolves*, and everything her court ever feared comes through, and everything Marrow set loose keeps loosed. There's no other way, love. There's only whether I go to it complaining or whether I go to it having had this." He pulled back enough to see her face. "And I've had this. I've had more than any thief in history. No one has ever been as rich as me."

"Don't," she said. "Don't say goodbye while I'm standing here."

"That's not what I'm doing." He smiled, and found, wonderingly, that it was a real smile. "I'm saying thank you. There's a difference. Goodbye is for things that end."

Below them, faint through the spire's stones, he could hear the city—the gutter mages risen in the streets, the bells, the sound of the invisible becoming visible. Above them, through the shattered roof, the sky showed one less star than it had an hour ago. The witnesses were closing their eyes.

The flame waited in its cold hearth, patient as debt.

Jude took Serah's hand, laced their fingers together, and turned to face it.

"Hold onto me," he said. "For as long as I'm still someone worth holding."

And stepped toward the fire.
