## 35. Strangers Again

The first time she came back, it rained.

Serah remembered that much with a cruel precision—the rain coming down in grey ropes over the Lantern District, the gutters running brown and quick, the awnings of the market stalls drumming like distant war-horses. She stood outside the little shop with the painted sign—CHARMS, the letters faded to the color of old bone—and told herself she was only passing by. She was a liar even then, standing in the rain with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird.

He was behind the counter when she pushed the door open, arranging jars he did not remember filling.

Jude Kavanagh looked up at her with the polite blankness of a stranger, and Serah understood, in one long falling moment, exactly what she had done by surviving him. He had poured three years of himself into the spire's flame—every stolen hour, every charm, every careful trick of invisibility—and at the end, when the fire had asked for everything, it had taken her face too. It had taken the night they met, the vault, the fire in the gutter cathedral, the honest dark before the end. All of it gone into the light that brought the stars home.

"Can I help you?" he said. His voice was the same. That was the worst of it. The same low warmth, the same faint roughness at the edges, as if the voice had been carved from something that survived the burning while everything around it burned away.

"I used to come here," Serah said. "Before."

"Before what?"

She had no answer that would not break something. So she bought a candle she didn't need, paid with a coin that trembled slightly in her fingers, and left.

The rain followed her all the way home.

* * *

The second time, she stayed longer. The third time, she didn't buy anything at all.

By the end of the first week she had stopped pretending there was any errand in the Lantern District worth her daily walk, and Jude had stopped pretending not to notice. He was thinner than she remembered—or perhaps she simply remembered wrong now; memory was a country she could no longer trust the borders of—and there was a new stillness in him, a watchfulness, like a man living quietly inside the ruin of a house whose rooms he'd never seen.

"You look at me like I owe you money," he said one afternoon, half-smiling, wiping down the counter with a rag gone grey with use.

"You don't owe me anything," Serah said. And then, because lying to him had become its own kind of prayer: "You owe me an hour. But I forgave the debt."

His smile faltered. "I steal things?" Not horrified. Curious. She watched the curiosity move through him like water through a reed, and hated how familiar it was, how *him*.

"You stole," she corrected. "Past tense. You gave it all back. More than all."

"To whom?"

"To everyone." She turned the candle jar on the counter so its label faced out, a small useless act of tidying, something to do with her hands. "To the whole sky."

He waited. He was good at waiting; she could see that even without his memories, patience lived in him like grain in wood. When she didn't go on, he said, gently, "You're going to tell me anyway, aren't you? Whatever it is. You've been circling it for days like a cat around a closed door."

Serah laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh she'd had since the spire. "Is that what I'm doing?"

"That's what you're doing."

So she pulled the stool from behind the counter, the one with the wobbly leg she knew he'd never fixed because fixing things was never his talent, and she sat down across from him in the little shop that smelled of wax and dried lavender and the ghost of enchantment, and she began.

"Once," she said, "in a city that had outlawed all magic, there was a thief who stole memories instead of coin..."

* * *

It became a ritual the way water becomes a river—not all at once, but by the stubborn repetition of finding the same low ground every day.

She came in the late afternoon, when the light through the shop's front window went amber and long, and she told him their story in pieces, the way you'd tell a child a tale too large for one sitting. She learned quickly which parts to soften and which parts to give whole. She told him about the widow and the borrowed grief. She told him about the night the stars went out, and made him close his eyes and imagine it—a sky like a lid, a cold wind carrying voices from beyond the veil—and watched the shiver move through him, because some part of him remembered the terror even if his mind had sold it to the flame.

She told him about Mother Quill, and the gutter cathedral, and the children singing in the dark under the city. She told him about the raid, and let herself pause there, because Mother Quill's death still sat in Serah's chest like a stone she had decided to carry rather than set down.

"She held the door," Serah said. "Blind as she was, she saw them coming before any of us. She said—" and here Serah's voice did the thing it always did, caught on the edge of the memory like cloth on a nail— "she said, *the boy with the borrowed eyes must run.* She meant you."

Jude sat very still. "Do I have borrowed eyes?"

"You have eyes that have seen everything twice," Serah said. "Once when you lived it, and once when I tell it to you. That counts for something. Maybe it counts for more."

Some days he asked questions that cut her open sideways. *Was I a good man? Was I afraid? Did people love me?* And once, terribly, casually, turning a jar of honey-gold charmsight in his hands: *Were we lovers, you and I? You look at me like we were.*

"Yes," she had said, because she had promised herself she would not lie to him, not about that, not ever.

"And I gave that up too. For the stars."

"For the stars," she agreed.

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "Was it worth it? To you. Watching me burn it all."

Serah looked at him—at this stranger wearing the face she loved, with the same hands and the same voice and none of the history—and said, "I hate it every day. And yes. Both things are true, and I've stopped trying to make them fit together."

He nodded slowly, as if she'd handed him something heavy and he intended to hold it properly. "Tell me the part again," he said, "where you refused to execute the child."

"You like that part."

"It's the best part," he said. "A woman who decides the law is wrong and says so out loud, knowing what it will cost her." He smiled, small and crooked. "I think I would have liked her. Whoever she was."

"She's sitting right in front of you," Serah said, "drinking your tea."

* * *

On the twelfth day, she found the notebook.

She hadn't meant to snoop. Truly. She had come early—the rain again, the district drowned in soft grey—and the shop was empty, the bell over the door giving its small tin complaint, and there on the counter, beside the ledger and the candle jars, lay a cheap bound book with a cracked spine, its pages furred at the edges from handling.

Her name was written on the first line.

Not *Serah Vane*, hunter, traitor, hero of the reforging—none of the titles the broadsheets had given her in those strange bright weeks after the stars returned. Just *Serah*. In his handwriting, which she recognized instantly, achingly, the narrow slanted letters she had seen on charm-labels and smuggled notes and once, memorized forever, on the back of a receipt he'd pressed into her hand in the Vault of Quiet Things: *if we don't make it out, find the blue jar.*

She should have put it down. She picked it up instead.

*Day 4,* the entry read. *She came again. Told me about the vault. I keep thinking I can feel it, like a word on the tip of the tongue. There's a shape in me where she says things happened. It aches, but it's a good ache. Like a muscle remembering work.*

*Day 6. The widow came in today. Mara. She buys nothing, just sits and talks. Apparently I helped her find her son's last words. I don't remember. She cried and thanked me and I had to pretend I knew what for. I'm getting good at pretending. I hate it.*

*Day 7. She (S.) says I chose this. Says I knew the price and paid it smiling. I want to be the man who did that. I keep checking whether I am. Evidence so far: inconclusive.*

*Day 9. Asked her to tell the meeting again. The lantern district, the chase, the hour I took from her. She describes it better than anyone could have lived it. When she got to the part where I left something kind in place of what I stole, she stopped talking for a while and looked out the window. I didn't ask. Some doors you wait for.*

*Day 10. New theory: maybe memory isn't the only way to know someone. Maybe you can learn a person the way you learn a song. Badly at first. Then all at once.*

*Day 11. She laughs at the wrong moments. I love—I mean. Note to self: finish this sentence later.*

Serah set the book down with hands that were not entirely steady.

The bell rang. Jude came up from the back room with a crate of tallow candles against his hip, and stopped when he saw her standing there with the notebook, and Serah braced for embarrassment, for anger, for the shutters to come down.

Instead he set the crate down and said, "You read it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture so perfectly, painfully *him* that her chest hurt. "It's not finished. None of it's finished. But I figured—if you're going to tell me our story every day like a bedtime tale, then I ought to write it down. So it sticks. So I'm not starting from nothing each morning." A pause. "Also I wrote down the things you say when you're not telling the story. The in-between things. Those might be my favorite parts."

"The in-between things," Serah repeated.

"How you take your tea. How you check the street before you sit near a window—old habit, you said, from being hunted. How you hum when you think I'm not listening. You hum badly, for the record. Like a kettle with regrets."

She laughed, and it came out wet, and she didn't care. "You're keeping notes on *me*."

"Somebody has to," Jude said. "You've been carrying the whole story alone. That's not fair. Stories should be carried by two people, minimum. Otherwise they tip over."

* * *

Winter deepened. The visits became the spine of her days.

She learned the rhythm of the shop: the morning customers who came for candles and gossip, the afternoon lull, the way the light moved across the floorboards like something slow and golden being poured. She learned that without his memories, Jude's magic had not vanished—it had gone quiet, sleeping in him like embers under ash. Sometimes, when she described the Otherworld, the jar of charmsight on the shelf would glow faintly in time with his pulse, and he would stare at it with an expression between wonder and homesickness.

"Will it come back?" he asked her once. "What I could do."

"Do you want it to?"

He considered honestly, which was another thing she was learning about him—that the man beneath the thefts had always been honest at his core, and honesty needed no memory to survive. "I want to be able to choose," he said at last. "That's all I ever wanted, I think. Even before. You said I had rules. Never take much, never be seen. Rules are just choices you make in advance." He looked at his own hands. "I'd like to make some choices in advance again."

There were hard evenings too. Nights when the gaps swallowed him—when he woke shouting a name he couldn't place (*Maelis*, it was always Maelis, the dying queen's name lodged in him like a splinter too deep for the flame to reach), or when he stood in the middle of his own shop unable to say why the sight of the back-room door made his palms sweat. On those nights Serah stayed past closing and told him the story again from the beginning, her voice steady in the dark, an anchor-line thrown across the water between them, and gradually his breathing would slow and he would say, "Again. From the widow. Start with the widow."

And she would start with the widow.

The city was changing around them, slowly, unevenly, the way ice changes on a thawing river. Grey skies had given way to weather again—real weather, indifferent and alive. The Crown's edicts had not been repealed, but they had gone unenforced for weeks, and in the lower quarters people had begun, cautiously, to speak of magic in daylight voices. Serah heard rumors on her walks: that the Queen's emissaries had been seen crossing the restored border under white banners; that certain members of the Assembly were drafting papers; that a witch-hunter who had once refused an unlawful order was being courted by both sides as a symbol.

She paid the rumors no mind. Her days had narrowed to this: the amber light, the wobbly stool, the story told in installments, the notebook growing fatter on the counter.

Until the evening the letter came.

* * *

It arrived at dusk, borne by a courier in the livery of the Assembly itself—silver-grey, star-stitched at the collar—and Serah signed for it with a hand that remembered how to be steady only through long practice.

She didn't open it in the street. She carried it into the shop, where Jude was lighting the lamps one by one, and the smell of hot brass and beeswax filled the little room, and for a moment she simply stood there watching him, committing it to memory the way she used to commit faces for warrants—carefully, completely, against loss.

"Bad news?" he asked, without turning. "You've got your reading-a-warrant face on."

"Am I that transparent?"

"Completely. It's one of the in-between things. Page nine."

She broke the seal. The paper was heavy, official, smelling of ink and ambition. She read it once in silence. Then again aloud, because they had a rule now: no secrets carried alone.

"*To Serah Vane, formerly of the Office of Hunters*"—formerly; the word landed like a key turning—"*the Assembly of the Reconciled City requests your presence and counsel. Her Majesty Queen Maelis of the Ember Court has proposed a formal accord concerning the practice of enchantment within mortal lands. The Assembly finds itself... divided. It is the opinion of this body that no voice carries greater weight on these matters than yours, nor that of the Covenant-Bearer, should he consent to speak. We await you at your convenience. — Undersecretary Oren Hale, by order of the Assembly.*"

Silence. The lamp Jude had just lit flickered, steadied.

"They want us," Serah said slowly. "Both of us. At the table where they decide what people like you are allowed to be."

Jude had gone very still. And then—she saw it happen, saw the thing move through him that she had been waiting weeks to see—he reached across the counter, opened the battered notebook to a fresh page, and wrote, narrating as he went in that dry voice she was learning to love a second time:

"*Day twenty-three. They asked the stranger from the bedtime story to help write the law.*" He looked up. His eyes were catching the lamplight, and behind them, faint as dawn under a door, something old and patient seemed to stir. "Well," Jude Kavanagh said. "I suppose I'd better decide who I am before I get there."
