## 36. A New Covenant

The ink was still wet when Serah Vane realized her hands had stopped shaking.

It had taken eleven months. Eleven months of sessions in a hall that smelled of cedar smoke and old arguments, of translators whispering between two tongues that had not been spoken across a table in three hundred years, of fae lords who considered a handshake a binding oath and human ministers who considered it a pleasantry. Eleven months of Serah standing between them with a quill and a spine, translating not just words but intentions, because she had learned—hunting mages through the Lantern District, then fleeing beside one—that the space between what people said and what they meant was where all the real law lived.

The document before her ran to forty-one pages. She had written most of them herself, in a hand that had once only ever filled out execution orders.

*The Accord of the Rekindled Flame*, someone had titled it. She would have called it something plainer. But titles mattered to courts, and this one had two.

Article One: That enchantment, being neither good nor evil but only old, is hereby recognized as lawful within the mortal realm, subject to consent freely given by all parties touched by its working.

That word—*consent*—had cost her four sleepless nights and one shouting match with a magistrate who thought he was being generous. She had stood in front of the assembled ministers and told them about charms sold in back-alley shops, memories lifted from grieving widows for coin, and she had watched their faces do the arithmetic of every kindness they'd ever accepted from a stranger's magic without asking what it cost. Consent was not generosity. It was the whole of the law, or it was nothing.

She had won. Barely. The way most true things were won.

Serah set down the quill and flexed her fingers. Outside the treaty hall's tall windows, the sky over Vael Corren was doing something it still made her stop and stare at: it was *blue*. Not the bruised grey of the purge years, not the starless black of the broken winter. Blue, with clouds in it, moving at their own pace, indifferent and ordinary and miraculous.

And tonight, if the reports from the observatory were right, there would be stars again. All of them. The astronomers had wept over their charts, finding constellations exactly where the oldest records said they should be, as though the sky had simply been holding its breath.

"Signatory Vane." The voice came from the doorway, low and resonant, like water moving over stones in the dark.

Queen Maelis did not enter rooms so much as arrive in them. Even diminished—even faded to a shadow of the radiance Serah had first seen in the Court of Embers—the fae queen carried a presence that made the candle flames lean toward her like courtiers. Her gown today was grey silk shot through with threads of silver that caught the light and held it, and Serah suspected those threads were actual starlight, spun while the sky was dark, kept against a day like this one.

"Your Majesty." Serah rose and inclined her head—not a curtsy; Maelis had laughed at her first attempt and asked never to see it again—but something older than either gesture. Recognition, perhaps. Between equals who had bled for the same thing from opposite sides.

Maelis crossed the room and looked down at the pages. Her eyes, pale as frost on a windowpane, moved across the script. "You have given my people the right of passage through the border wards," she said. "And given yours the right of refusal."

"They're your wards too now. That was the point."

"Mm." The queen's mouth curved, which on Maelis was the equivalent of another woman's open laughter. "Three hundred years ago, the first covenant was written in blood and sealed in fire, and no one asked the small folk of either realm what they wished. You have written yours in ink, at a table, with witnesses." She touched the edge of a page, delicately, the way one touches a sleeping child. "It is a poorer document for lacking drama. It will be a better one for lasting."

"That's the idea."

"And the school?" Maelis asked. "Your gutter cathedral. The blind seer's house."

Serah's chest tightened, the way it always did at Mother Quill's name—a grief worn smooth now, like a stone carried in a pocket too long. "Opens next week. Jude's already moved in. He sleeps there more than he sleeps at his own shop, I think. Says the walls remember her."

"All walls remember," Maelis said softly. "It is people who forget. That is why we are here, Signatory Vane. Why any of this exists." She drew a slender stylus from her sleeve—bone-white, ancient—and signed the final page in a script that seemed to move slightly after she finished, letters settling like birds onto a branch. Then she looked up, and her frost-pale eyes fixed on Serah with an intensity that had once frightened her and now felt almost like affection.

"You wrote a covenant that begins with an apology," the queen said. "In three hundred years of courts and crowns, I have never seen a law begin by admitting wrong. Whatever else comes of this—" she tapped Article One, the word *consent*, "—that page alone justifies everything you spent."

Serah looked at the signature drying beside hers. Two names, one human hand and one not-quite-human, bound to the same line.

"It wasn't just me," she said. "There was a thief involved."

"Yes," Maelis said, and her smile deepened. "I have heard. The Covenant-Bearer who remembers nothing." Something flickered behind her eyes—old knowledge, old guilt, the confession she had made in the Court of Embers still living in her like a splinter. "Give him my regards. And tell him the stars burn brighter over the Otherworld this season. My court believes it is gratitude. I believe it is a boy who gave away everything he had, still shining somewhere he cannot see."

* * *

Jude Kavanagh had never taught anyone anything in his life, and now there were nineteen children sitting on the cathedral floor looking at him like he knew what he was doing.

He didn't. He'd told them so on the first day, plainly, and somehow that had made them trust him more, which seemed like a lesson about people he ought to write down. Instead he'd started keeping a ledger—Mother Quill's old habit, borrowed like everything worth having—and each evening he recorded what he'd tried, what had failed, and what one of the children had understood faster than he had. Which was most things.

"Now," he said, crouching among them in the dust-and-candlelight smell of the nave, "who can tell me the first rule?"

"Nineteen voices, variously pitched: *"Never take what isn't given."*

"And the second?"

*"Never take more than is offered."*

"And the third?" He pointed at Pip, a sharp-eyed girl of nine with soot permanently ground into her knuckles, who had been caught lifting bread from three different stalls in the same week and whom Serah had dragged to the school personally with the words *if she's going to steal anyway, she may as well learn properly.*

Pip grinned, gap-toothed. "If you must take, take the pain. Nobody misses it."

Jude laughed despite himself. "Close enough. Though we're going to work on *must*." He settled cross-legged on the flagstones, and the children crowded in around him, a circle of small bodies and bigger questions. Above them, the shattered rose window had been partially reglazed with donated glass—mismatched panes, green and amber and one defiant square of deep violet—so that the afternoon light came through fractured and colored, laying pools of stained brightness across the floor. Mother Quill would have hated the imperfection. Mother Quill would have loved the children. He liked to think the second outweighed the first.

"Today," he said, "we're going to talk about what a memory feels like when someone gives it to you. Not takes. Gives." He held up his hands, empty. "Because that's the whole difference. Everything else—the technique, the focus, the quieting of your own thoughts—that's just craft. Any clever person can learn craft. The giving is the covenant."

A boy in the back—Tomas, solemn, whose sister had died in the purge years and who had come to the school because he'd heard rumors that magic could hold the dead a little longer in the world—raised his hand. "Does it hurt? When someone gives you something that big?"

Jude considered lying. He considered the gentler half-truth. Then he remembered a night beside a small fire, a woman who had gone rogue for him, an honest conversation that had cost him everything and been worth it.

"Sometimes," he said. "Grief is heavy even when it's handed to you gently. But here's the thing nobody told me for three years, because I was busy being a criminal." He leaned forward. "When someone gives you a memory freely, it doesn't weigh the same as one you stole. Stolen memories are always cold. Given ones are warm. I don't know why. Maybe because warmth is just what it feels like when somebody trusts you."

The children were quiet, in the way children are quiet when they've actually heard you—which was rarer and more precious than any spell.

"So," Jude said, clapping his hands. "Pair up. We're going to practice passing *small* things. The taste of an orange. The sound of rain on a particular roof. Nothing that hurts, nothing you'd miss. And if your partner says *stop*, you stop instantly, or you'll be scrubbing the crypt steps until midwinter."

They scattered into pairs, giggling, faces scrunching in concentration. Jude sat back on his heels and watched them, and felt the strange double vision that had become his daily companion: seeing the present clearly, and beneath it, faint as a watermark, the shape of a life he'd poured into a flame. He knew the facts of himself the way you know a story someone else told you. *You stole memories. You ran from hunters. You loved a woman named Serah Vane.* He believed them the way you believe scripture—true, important, not quite *his*.

But sometimes, watching a child concentrate until her nose wrinkled, he felt something in his chest lean toward the moment like a plant toward light, and he thought: *whatever I burned, some of the warmth stayed in the wick.*

The cathedral door groaned open. Cold air, street noise, and Serah—still in her travel coat from the treaty hall, hair escaping its braid, carrying a rolled document under one arm and a paper bag of roasted chestnuts in the other hand, because she had learned long ago that the fastest way to nineteen children's hearts ran directly through their stomachs.

"The accord's signed," she announced, and the room went still, and then it went loud, and Jude found himself on his feet with his heart hammering a rhythm he didn't recognize but trusted completely.

Later—after the chestnuts, after Pip had led the smaller ones in a victory chant that was mostly just shouting, after the children had been sent home into a blue evening full of returning stars—she sat beside him on the altar steps, shoulder to shoulder, the signed copy spread across both their knees.

"Read the first article," she said.

He read it aloud. His voice caught, slightly, on *consent freely given*.

"That's yours," Serah said quietly. "Not mine. I put the word in, but the idea—I got it from a thief who left me an hour of kindness in place of an hour he stole. Who spent three years taking only what people could spare. Everyone thinks the new law came from the war, from the spire, from the queen." She shook her head. "It came from the rules you made for yourself in the dark, back when breaking them wouldn't have cost you anything."

Jude stared at the page. Something moved in him—distant, like a fish turning far down in deep water. "I wish I remembered making them," he said.

"I know." She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She'd stopped saying that weeks ago, when he'd asked her to. Instead she said what she always said, the sentence that had become the load-bearing wall of their strange new life: "We'll get there. One memory at a time. I'm very patient. Ask anyone. Ask the entire Crown diplomatic corps."

He huffed a laugh. Then, because the light through the mismatched window had turned gold and he wanted to mark the moment with something better than a laugh: "Show me something? From before?"

It was their ritual, or becoming one. Each evening she offered him a piece of their history—a scene, a detail, a small true thing—and he chose whether to take it. Take, not receive: she insisted on the word, insisted he reach for it himself, because the whole point, she said, was that nothing between them should ever again be taken by accident or given by pity.

"Tonight," Serah said, and there was something new in her voice, something carefully casual that fooled him not at all, "I think I'll show you the lantern market. The first time you saw me. You were running from me, technically."

"Sounds flattering."

"It wasn't. You knocked over a fruit cart." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes were bright and steady and a little afraid. "Ready?"

Jude closed his eyes. Reached out, gently, the way he'd teach the children tomorrow—never take what isn't given, never take more than is offered—

And stopped.

Because beneath her offered memory, warm and waiting, he felt something else. Something in *himself*. A door he had never noticed, standing in a room of himself he had never entered, and through its seams leaked a scent of river-water and cold iron, and a voice like water over stones saying words he almost understood:

*The seventh shard, Covenant-Bearer. There were always seven.*

His eyes opened. Serah was watching him, her offered memory still glowing unspent between them, and outside the reglazed window the first stars of the evening were coming out—one, then three, then dozens, kindling over the city like a promise kept.

"Jude?" she said. "What is it? What did you find?"

He looked at her, and at the sky, and at the ledger lying open on the altar above them, its blank pages waiting.

"I think," he said slowly, "the covenant isn't finished with me yet."
