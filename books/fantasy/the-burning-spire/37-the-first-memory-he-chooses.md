## 37. The First Memory He Chooses

The healers at the gutter cathedral say the mind is a garden after a flood. They say this to me gently, in the voices people use for children and the dying, as though I cannot hear the pity folded inside the metaphor. Everything is still there, they tell me. The soil is good. But nothing grows in its proper place anymore, and some beds lie bare where the water took everything.

I have learned to stop arguing with them.

What I have instead of an argument is a notebook. Serah gave it to me in my second week—cheap paper, a cracked leather cover that smells of lamp oil and rain—and I have filled forty pages with my own handwriting, which looks like a stranger's and yet is mine. *Your name is Jude Kavanagh. You were a memory-thief. You saved the world by emptying yourself out.* The words sit on the page like stones someone else stacked. I can read them. I can even believe them, in the way one believes in the history of a country one has never visited.

But there are gaps in me shaped like specific things. A woman's laugh. The smell of a particular street in autumn. The weight of a hand in mine. I know these gaps exist because everyone around me moves carefully around their edges, the way you step around a hole in the floorboards you've learned by heart.

Today, Mother Quill's successor—a round-faced young seer named Odile who sees very little but pretends beautifully—sits across from me in the old cathedral's vestry and asks the question they've all been waiting for her to ask.

"The flame left you a choice, Jude. It always does. That's the mercy in the rite no one talks about." She folds her hands. "You can't have everything back. The covenant took what it took. But the spire remembers what was poured into it, and once a year—once only—it will give a bearer back one memory. Whole. Chosen. Yours."

My heart does something complicated against my ribs. "One."

"One," she agrees. "So think carefully. Most bearers ask for their mother's face. Or the day they first did magic. Something foundational."

I look down at my hands. They are a thief's hands still—long-fingered, quick, scarred at the knuckle from three years of lifting things that couldn't be lifted. They feel like borrowed tools. Everything feels like borrowed tools.

"I don't remember her," I say. It isn't a question, and Odile doesn't pretend otherwise. "Serah Vane. I know the shape of the absence. I know I loved her because forty pages of my own handwriting insist upon it, and because she comes here every day and tells me our story like it's a bedtime tale, and because when she laughs at the parts that aren't funny, something in my chest pulls toward her like a tide toward a moon I can't see." I turn my palms up. "But I don't *remember* her."

Odile is quiet for a long moment. When she speaks, her voice has lost its professional softness. "Then ask for that."

"No." The word comes out faster than I expect, and firmer. She blinks. I hear myself continue, and I am surprised by the man speaking through my mouth—the man I apparently was, or became. "If the spire gives her back to me, then she's given to me. A gift from a fire. And I'd spend the rest of my life wondering if what I feel is mine or just... returned goods." I shake my head slowly. "There's another way. There has to be. I spent three years taking memories that weren't offered. I'm not going to rebuild the most important thing I ever had on a theft—even a theft the universe commits for me."

"Then what will you ask for?"

"Nothing." I stand. My legs hold, mostly. "I want to ask *her*."

* * *

She comes at dusk, as she always does, when the light through the broken rose window goes amber and lays long gold bars across the nave floor. I hear her boots before I see her—she still walks like a hunter, heel to toe, silent on stone, but she lets me hear her now. That's new. That's a courtesy she invented for my sake, and I love her for it without knowing why, which is somehow worse and better than loving her with reasons.

She carries bread and hard cheese and a bottle of plum wine, because she says I used to complain about cathedral cooking, and she sets them on the vestry table like an offering at a small domestic altar.

"You look like you've been thinking," she says. "That's dangerous. Last time you thought too hard, you poured yourself into a fire."

"Once," I say. "I'm told I only do these things once."

Her smile is careful. She smiles carefully around me now, the way you handle a cup with a hairline crack—not because it will break, but because you want it to last. I hate that smile. I am trying to earn back the careless one.

"Odile told you about the choice," she says. Not a question either. News travels fast in a sanctuary full of seers.

"She did." I take a breath. This is the part I rehearsed all morning, standing at the window watching the spire burn gold and steady on the hill, and now that the moment is here the rehearsal evaporates and I am just a man with holes in him talking to a woman who knows all his pages by heart. "I'm not going to take it."

Something flickers across her face—hurt, quickly mastered, and I realize how it sounds. "Jude—"

"Let me finish. Please." My hands are shaking. I put them flat on the table like a man steadying a map. "I could ask the spire for the night we met. Odile says it would come back whole. Perfect. Every detail restored, factory-fresh, guaranteed authentic." I look up at her. Her eyes are grey-green, storm-colored; I know that much because I've spent weeks learning her face like a text I'll be examined on. "And it would be a lie. Not a false memory—but a false *giving*. The fire would hand you to me, and I would never have chosen you. I'd just be wearing a love someone else picked out."

Serah sets down the wine bottle very slowly. "And what exactly are you proposing instead?"

"That you show me." My voice cracks on the second word, and I let it. "Not the spire. Not magic. You. Tell me about the night we met—the real version, not the bedtime-tale version. The ugly parts. The part where I stole an hour of your memory and ran. The part where you cornered me in the Lantern District with a blade at my throat and I deserved it. All of it. And then—" I swallow. "Then you decide. Whether the man telling you he loves you without remembering why is worth starting over with. Freely. Both directions. No enchantment in it anywhere."

The silence stretches so long I can hear the cathedral settling, wood ticking as the evening cools. Somewhere below us, in the school that used to be a hideout, a child is practicing scales on a cracked flute, badly and with tremendous commitment.

"You absolute idiot," Serah says finally, and her voice is wet, and she is smiling the careless smile, the one with the crack running straight through it. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I include the time you bled out half your soul into a magical furnace."

"So that's a yes to the storytelling?"

"That's a yes." She pulls a chair around to my side of the table, close enough that our knees touch, and pours two cups of plum wine with hands that aren't quite steady either. "But I warn you—I'm a hunter. I give testimony under oath. Every detail. Even the ones that make you look bad."

"*Especially* those," I say. "I want the whole indictment."

She laughs, and the sound lands in some part of me that has no name and no history and recognizes her anyway.

"All right." She wraps her hands around her cup. Outside, the first star of the evening pricks through the darkening blue over the spire—one of the new stars, the ones I bought back, burning in a sky I emptied and refilled. She follows my gaze to it, and something passes over her face like weather. "All right. The night we met. Testimony begins."

* * *

"It was raining," she says. "It was always raining in the Lantern District that autumn—you remember the city better than that, at least, the lanterns bleeding orange into the puddles, the fishmongers' stalls stinking at the end of the market day. I'd been tracking you for six days. Six days of paperwork and dead ends and informants who kept describing a man who wasn't there. 'Ordinary,' they said. 'Forgettable.' Which should have been the first clue, shouldn't it? Nobody's forgettable by accident."

I write that down. I have taken to writing everything down during her visits, my pen scratching along while she talks, building the case file of my own heart. She watches me do it with an expression I've decided means *fond exasperation*.

"'Forgettable by design,'" I murmur. "That's good. Can I steal that?"

"You already did. Page twelve." She nudges my knee with hers. "Where was I. Right. The widow. Maren Ostwick, Candlemaker's Row. I'd been canvassing her street because of a registered charm-sale, and she mentioned a gentleman caller who'd sat with her after her son's funeral. Kind eyes, she said. Never caught his name. And I remember thinking—that's either the gentlest predator in the capital or the strangest thief. So I set a trap. I spread word that a grieving father in the Lantern District wanted a charm made. Grief work. Your specialty, according to every informant who'd ever sold you out."

"And I walked into it."

"You walked into it like a man walking into his own front door. That's what unnerved me later—how *comfortable* you looked. You came down the alley in a grey coat with the collar up, and you had this expression—" she pauses, searching my face as if checking whether the description still fits, "—like you were apologizing in advance. For existing. For the job. For the rain. And I stepped out of the doorway with my blade drawn and said, 'Jude Kavanagh, you're under arrest for unlicensed enchantment,' and you didn't run."

"I didn't?"

"You looked at my warrant seal and said—" she closes her eyes, quoting, and her voice drops into a rough imitation of a voice I don't remember owning, low and tired and wry, "'Is there any chance we could discuss this over a drink? You look like you haven't slept in a week.'"

I stare at her. "I flirted with the witch-hunter arresting me."

"You *sympathized* with the witch-hunter arresting me. That was worse. That was infinitely worse." She takes a sip of wine, and over the rim of the cup her eyes catch the lamplight. "Because I'd been awake for thirty hours and my feet hurt and no one in the Crown's service had asked me if I was tired since I took my oath. And for one stupid second I wanted to say yes. To the drink. To the discussion. To whatever came after." She sets the cup down. "Instead I put the blade closer and told you to keep your hands where I could see them. And you did. And then you stole an hour of my memory anyway, the moment I blinked, and vanished into the crowd like smoke, and I woke up on a rain-slick street holding a warrant for a man I could no longer describe."

"That's monstrous," I say quietly. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I did that to you."

"I know you are. You apologized for eleven consecutive chapters of my bedtime story." But she says it gently, and her hand crosses the table and covers mine, and her palm is warm and calloused from sword-hilt. "Here's the thing I figured out later, reconstructing that hour from my notes. You took exactly sixty minutes. Not a minute more. And you left something in the gap—you left me sitting dry under an awning with a hot chestnut seller's bun in my hand, paid for, wrapped in paper, with a note: *For the hunter who hasn't eaten. Debt acknowledged. —J.K.*"

The pen stops in my fingers.

"I ate the bun," she says, "standing in the rain, furious, and it was the best thing I'd tasted in a year. And I spent the next month telling myself I was hunting you out of duty." She turns my hand over, traces the scars on my knuckles like a cartographer reading a coastline she mapped long ago. "I was hunting you because you were the first person in the Crown's service or out of it who'd looked at me and seen a tired woman instead of a weapon. That's the truth of the night we met. That's the testimony. Take it down verbatim."

I write it. My eyes blur halfway through and I let them, and she pretends not to notice, and the star outside the window burns on, patient, one among thousands, each one a debt I paid and would pay again.

* * *

We talk until the candle gutters. She gives me the Lantern District chase, the vault, the fire in the gutter cathedral where she first called me by no title at all, just my name, softly, like setting something down. She gives me the Otherworld, the court of embers, the queen's confession. She gives me the rite—sparely, watching my face, and I make her tell it anyway, all of it, the pouring-out, the stars coming back one by one like lamps being lit down an endless street.

And then, near the end, she goes quiet, turning her cup in circles.

"There's one more thing," she says. "From that first night. Something I've never told anyone, because it sounded mad even to me."

"Tell me anyway. I'm collecting madnesses. It's becoming a hobby."

"When you vanished—after the theft, after the bun—I stood in that alley and I felt it happen. The hour leaving. And for half a heartbeat, right at the edge of it, I *felt you*. Not your face. Just—what it cost you. Taking from someone. Like a flinch. Like grief." She looks up. "Every other mage I ever hunted, and I hunted dozens, took like it was nothing. You took like it was breaking your heart. That's why I followed you. Not the warrant. The flinch."

I set down the pen. The notebook page dries in front of me, my stranger's-handwriting recording a stranger's kindness, and I understand suddenly that this is the answer to the question I brought to Odile's vestry this morning. The spire could have handed me a perfect memory, seamless, true. But it would have been a memory of a night. What Serah is giving me, hour by hour, freely, across a scarred table—is a memory of *her*. And a thing given can be built on. A thing given becomes yours in a way nothing stolen or restored ever was.

"Ask me something," she says. "Anything from the story. Test me."

I consider. "Did you love me before the spire? Before the rite? Before I was safe to love?"

"Yes," she says, without hesitation, without looking away. "Since the chestnut bun. Possibly since the flinch. Ask a harder one."

"What happens tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I come back at dusk. Same bread, same terrible cheese. And I tell you about the widow Maren, and the son's last words, and the price you paid her. And the day after that, the vault. And the day after that—" she shrugs, and her mouth quirks, "—as many tomorrows as it takes. I'm a hunter, Jude Kavanagh. I don't quit a trail. Especially not one that ends at my own door."

The candle dies. Neither of us moves to light another. In the dark, the cathedral breathes around us, and far off the spire burns its steady gold, and above it the stars wheel in their courses, keeping the covenant I can no longer remember making—but which, I am beginning to understand, was never really mine to remember. It was mine to keep. And I am keeping it, one dusk at a time, one page at a time, one freely given hour at a time.

"Serah," I say into the dark.

"Mm?"

"Tomorrow—start with the part where I was an idiot. I want the complete record."

She laughs in the darkness, close enough that I feel it more than hear it, and her hand finds mine in the black without searching, the way a key finds the lock it was cut for.

"Oh, beloved," she says. "Tomorrow I'm bringing the *notes*."

And outside the broken rose window, unnoticed by either of us until morning, a new star has risen over the capital—one that was never in any sky before, small and stubborn and burning white-gold, in a pattern the astronomers will spend years arguing about, and which Mother Quill, had she lived to see it, would have recognized at once: the exact constellation the old covenant maps called *the Thief's Debt*, which appears, according to texts no library admits to owning, only when a stolen thing is freely returned—

and never, the texts say, only once.
