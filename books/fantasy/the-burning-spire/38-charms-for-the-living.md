## 38. Charms for the Living

The shop on Lantern Row had been dead for three years.

I stood in the doorway of it now, key in hand—the same key, iron gone brown with rust—and let the smell of the place come out to meet me. Dust and old tallow. Dried lavender from the bundles I'd hung in the rafters to mask the scent of magic, back when magic was a thing you hid like a knife under your coat. The little brass bell above the door gave a single tired note when I pushed it open, and I flinched at how loud it seemed, how much it sounded like a beginning.

"You could still change your mind," Serah said behind me. She was carrying a crate of my things—what few things had survived the purge of my old lodgings. A mortar and pestle. A jar of river stones. The notebook in which she had written our story in her own careful hunter's hand, because mine had been poured into a flame along with everything else I'd ever taken or made.

"I could," I agreed. "But then who would I be?"

She set the crate down on the counter that had once belonged to another man, another life, and looked around the shop the way she looked at crime scenes: cataloguing, assessing, finding the shape of what happened here. "Small," she said finally.

"It was always small."

"Good." She touched the counter's edge, where some long-dead carpenter had carved a row of notches. "Small things are harder to burn."

* * *

It took us nine days to make the shop ready.

Serah scrubbed the windows until the gray light came through them honest and clean. I mended the shelves, rehung the lavender—new bundles this time, bought openly from a herbwife in the Gutter Market who looked at my hands for a long moment before naming her price. Word travels fast in the low quarters. Word had traveled about the spire, about the night the stars came back one by one like lamps being lit down an endless corridor, about the man who had walked into the heart of the Burning Spire carrying every memory he owned and given them all away.

They didn't know what to do with me. That was plain enough. In the old days they'd have known exactly what to do: report me, or rob me, or slip through my door after dark with coin in their fists and grief in their eyes. Now they only watched. I'd catch them at it across the market—a woman pausing over her fish stall, a boy on a rooftop, an old soldier by the fountain—all of them looking at me as if I were a door they weren't sure was safe to knock on.

On the tenth day, I swept the step, turned the sign—no name on it yet, just blank wood—and opened the bell to the morning.

The first customer came before noon.

* * *

She was a cooper's wife named Maren Ostler, and I knew her face before she spoke, though I couldn't have said from where. There are gaps in me now, whole neighborhoods of my past razed to the foundations, and sometimes a face walks through one of those empty lots and I feel the ghost of walls that aren't there anymore.

"You're Kavanagh," she said. Not a question. Her hands were knotted together in front of her like rope that had been tied and retied too many times. "They say you used to sell charms. Memories."

"I did."

"And now?"

"Now—" I gestured at the shop, the clean windows, the jars of river stones and dried herbs, the single chair set opposite mine with a small table between. "Now people come here and sit down, and if they want to, they give something away. Something they're tired of carrying."

Her chin came up. There was anger in her, the kind that lives inside grief like a coal inside ash. "You mean memories. You mean you'll take them. Like before."

"If they choose it." I kept my voice level, kept my hands visible on the table. Three years of practice at being harmless, and it had never once been true until now. "That's the difference, Mistress Ostler. Before, I took. Now, people give. And I don't keep anything. I don't sell anything. What someone sets down here goes into the flame, and the flame is the city's, and the stars', and no one's."

She stared at me for a long time. Then she sat down in the chair as though her knees had simply given up arguing, and put her knotted hands flat on the table, and said:

"My daughter died in the purges. The Chancellor's men came to our street and they took minds, whole streets of minds, and my girl—she fought them. Sixteen years old and she fought them, and they—" Her voice snagged and tore. "They said afterward it was quick. They always say that. Quick. As if quick is a mercy you can hand to a mother."

I waited. This was the part I had never done before, I realized—the waiting. In the old trade, I'd have already been inside her grief, lifting the worst of it out like a pickpocket lifting a purse, leaving her lighter and not knowing why, and myself richer by one sorrow I could sell to someone else. It had seemed clean, then. Efficient. Kind, even, if I was honest with myself on the bad nights.

It had been theft. All of it. Every charm, every hour, every stolen smile.

"What do you carry?" I asked gently.

"Everything." She laughed, wet and short. "The sound the door made when they brought her home to me. The way the purge-wagon wheels squeaked. Her name—I can't stop saying her name. Brinna. Brinna. I say it to the wall. I say it to the soup. I woke up last week saying it to the cat and the cat looked at me like I'd gone mad, and maybe I have, Master Kavanagh, maybe that's what this is."

"No," I said. "That's what love is, with nowhere left to go."

Something in her face cracked open at that—not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it becomes water again. "Can you take it? All of it? Can you take her out of my head so I can sleep?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading since the day Serah and I first talked about reopening this place, sitting across the fire in the gutter cathedral with the children asleep around us.

"No," I said.

Maren Ostler's face went hard as slate. "Then what in the starless dark am I paying you for?"

"For the parts that aren't her." I leaned forward. "Listen to me. Your daughter isn't a weight, mistress. She's a person, and she lives in you now, and I will not touch her—not her voice, not her name, not the door-sound or the wagon-wheels if those are how you hold her. But grief is more than the beloved. Grief is also guilt. Fear. The moment you couldn't reach her. The thought that circles and circles like a bird against glass. Those I can help you set down—if you point them out to me yourself. One at a time. You choose. You choose every time, and you can stop whenever you want, and whatever we release goes to the flame and no one ever wears your sorrow as a charm. Not me. Not anyone. Never again."

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart rattled past; the lavender stirred in the draft.

"The bird against the glass," she whispered. "Yes. That one. Take—that one. If I ask you to."

"That one," I agreed. "If you ask me to."

She closed her eyes. "I'm asking."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the years of taking, and what I am learning again, slowly, in the giving back:

A memory is not a thing. It's a living tissue, and it grows into everything around it. When I stole, I was careless about roots—I pulled up the flower and half the garden came with it, and the people I robbed walked away with holes in them shaped like hours, and never knew why certain songs made them weep. I told myself I took only a little. Never much. Never seen. Rules for a thief, not rules for a healer.

So now I work the other way, and slower, so much slower. I don't reach into Maren Ostler's mind and rip. I ask her to show me the bird. I ask her to describe the glass. And when she does—when she gives me the circling, battering panic of the thought that says *you should have run after the wagon, you should have thrown yourself under the wheels*—I hold it the way you hold a splinter you're drawing out, gentle as breath, and I ask her, *are you sure*, and she says *yes*, and only then do I lift.

It comes free easier than anything I ever stole. That's the thing no one tells you. What people cling to hardest is rarely what they most need to be rid of. The guilt came away like a burr off wool, and Maren gasped, and then she sat very still with tears running down her face and her hands open on the table, palms up, empty.

"She's still there," I said quietly. "Brinna. Check."

Maren's lips moved. *Brinna.* Then she laughed—one broken, astonished note. "Still there. Still saying it to the soup." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Gods below. It doesn't hurt less. But it's—it's just hers now. Just missing her. Not all the rest of it screaming on top."

"That's the craft," I said. "What's left is yours to keep. For as long as you want it."

She paid me in eggs and a jar of honey, because coin is still scarce in the low quarters, and at the door she stopped and looked back at me with red-rimmed eyes.

"They said you were the last registered mage in the city," she said. "Back when it was a crime. That you stole from everyone."

"I did."

"And now you sit in a little shop and pull thorns out of people for eggs." Her mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "The world got strange, Master Kavanagh."

"The world got strange," I agreed. "I'm just trying to get strange along with it."

* * *

Word spread, the way word does. By the end of the month there was a line some mornings, and Serah started keeping the ledger because my handwriting had gone strange since the rite—my hand remembered how to form letters but not always which ones went where, and I'd write *bread* when I meant *Thursday*. She sat behind the counter with her hunter's posture softened into something almost domestic, and she took names, and fees in kind—eggs, mending, a fiddle lesson, once a live hen that terrorized the shop for two days before we gave it to the neighbors—and she watched me work with an expression I spent whole evenings trying to read.

One night, closing up, she said it plainly, because Serah Vane was never any good at circling a thing.

"You could take more than they ask for. You know that. Half of them would thank you. Some of them would beg."

"I know."

"You could take the whole wound. Heal them clean. It would be kinder, maybe. Faster."

"Faster," I said. "Maybe." I turned the sign, dropped the bolt. "Do you remember what you told me, in the cathedral? The night after the raid. You said the Crown taught you that mercy was just control wearing softer gloves. You were angry about it, but you were right. If I decide what a stranger should forget—if I reach in and tidy their grief to my own taste—then I'm the Chancellor with gentler hands. Marrow wanted to edit everyone. Whole streets of minds, smooth and quiet and obedient. I won't hold the pen, Serah. Not even for love of them. Especially not for love of them."

She was quiet a while. The lamp guttered; outside, far above the rooftops, I could feel the stars the way you feel warmth from a hearth in the next room—not seeing them, not anymore, not with my old senses burned away, but knowing. The covenant hummed in the stones of the city like a struck bell that would not quite finish ringing. I was part of that hum now. Hollowed out and refilled with something older.

"You've changed," she said at last.

"I've been emptied," I corrected. "It looks similar from the outside."

"No." She crossed the small room and took my face in both hands, the way she did when she wanted to be sure I heard her. "I knew you emptied. I've spent weeks telling you our story like a nursemaid's tale—don't think I haven't noticed you writing it down after, checking my facts, filling your notebook with a life you can't recall. That's not what I mean." Her thumb traced my cheekbone. "I mean the thief I arrested in the Lantern District would never have said no to an easy kindness. He'd have called it efficiency. He'd have slept fine."

"He slept fine," I admitted. "I have notes saying so."

"And you?"

I thought about Maren Ostler walking home with her daughter's name still whole in her mouth, and the bird against the glass gone, and the look on her face—not lightness, exactly. Room. Room where the screaming used to be.

"I sleep like a stone," I said. "It's the strangest thing I own."

* * *

In the second month, the school sent its first students.

Mother Quill's cathedral—her memory of it, rather; the woman herself was ash and a marble marker and a street renamed in defiance of every edict Marrow ever signed—had become, under the new accord, the Gutter Academy of Practical Enchantments, which the children immediately shortened to the Quillhouse, which suited everyone. Serah had drafted the accord herself, three months of argument with the Crown's lawyers and Queen Maelis's emissaries, and the clause she fought hardest for was the smallest one: that no student's memory might be read, taken, or traded without witnessed consent given aloud. Consent, written into law. She framed the first copy and hung it in the Quillhouse hall, and Mother Quill would have laughed herself sick at the sight of law being used as a shield instead of a whip.

The students came to my shop on rotation, two at a time, to learn what the teachers called *ethical craft* and the children called *Kavanagh's class*.

Mostly I taught them to do nothing. That was the first lesson and the hardest. I'd set a bowl of water on the table between us and tell them: put your gift away. Feel it wanting. The water is full of reflections—faces, light, the window—and every instinct you have will scream at you to dip your fingers in, to take, to taste, to know. And you will sit there with your hands in your lap and you will let the water be water.

Tomas, a ferret-thin boy of eleven with fae-green flecks in his irises, lasted four minutes the first day before he burst out, "But what's it FOR, if you don't use it?"

"That," I said, "is the second lesson. Come back tomorrow."

He came back every day. They all did. Children whose gifts had been registered like diseases under Marrow, hidden like shames before him, and who now sat in a warm room with a bowl of water and learned that the power in them was not a hunger to be fed nor a curse to be cut out but a tool, and tools answer to the hand that holds them, and the hand answers to something higher up. Conscience, the teachers called it. I called it the difference between a thief and a craftsman, and I was qualified to speak on the subject from both sides of the fence.

One gray afternoon, Tomas stayed after the others left. He turned the bowl of water slowly with one finger, not dipping, not taking—just turning it, watching the light wheel.

"My mam says you gave everything away," he said. "At the spire. Every memory you had. Is that true?"

"It's true."

"Do you miss them?"

I considered the question honestly, which is the only way I permit myself to consider things these days. "I miss a woman I met three years ago," I said. "I don't know her name. I have notes that say she mattered, and notes that say I loved her, and none of it means anything to me except that when she walks into the room, something in my chest turns over like a key in a lock. So yes. I miss them. The way you'd miss a house you lived in that burned down. You know the shape of it. You can't live in it anymore."

"But you'd do it again." It wasn't quite a question. Children always know.

"The stars were going out, Tomas. Everything was going out. Somebody had to put wood on the fire." I smiled at him. "Besides—I'm not convinced I lost everything. Ask me again in fifty years."

He frowned, sensing a mystery larger than the lesson, which was exactly the right response to me. Then he said, "My mam wants to come see you. About my da. He was conscripted into Marrow's enforcers, near the end, and they made him do things, and now he sits in the dark and won't talk, and she heard you helped the cooper's wife—"

"Send her," I said. "Tell her the chair by the window is the comfortable one."

* * *

Autumn deepened. The lavender in the rafters dried to silver. I learned the rhythms of the new trade: mornings for the line, afternoons for the Quillhouse students, evenings for the ones who could only come after dark, the ashamed ones, the ones who still flinched at the word *mage* spoken aloud in daylight. I pulled thorns. I drew splinters. I sat with a former enforcer for six sessions before he could say his daughter's name without his hands shaking, and when he finally could, he wept like a spring thaw, and I sat with him and did absolutely nothing, and it was the best work I did all season.

Some nights Serah found me standing at the window after close, looking up past the rooftops at the sky.

"You're counting them," she said, coming to stand beside me. Her shoulder fit against mine the way a keystone fits an arch.

"I'm not. I can't see them properly anymore—whatever I am now, it's mostly echo. But I can feel them. Like teeth in a jaw that used to ache." I paused. "There's a gap, though. Low in the east. Do you feel it? Where the seventh star used to sit."

She was quiet a moment. "The records call it Vael. The Shepherd's Eye. It hasn't relit."

"No. It hasn't."

The covenant held—the borders stood, the veil was veiled, the Otherworld kept courteously to itself beyond wards that sang instead of scarred. But that one star stayed dark, and neither the Queen's emissaries nor the Crown's astronomers could say why, and I felt it the way you feel a missing tooth: not pain, exactly. Attention. Something absent, insisting on its absence.

"Probably nothing," Serah said, in the tone of a woman who had chased probably-nothings into burning spires and vaults of quiet things and knew precisely how often probably-nothing turned out to be otherwise.

"Probably," I agreed.

She kissed my temple. "Come to bed, thief."

"Craftsman," I corrected, and blew out the lamp, and followed her.

Behind us the shop settled into its dark, jars and stones and dried lavender, the two chairs facing each other across the little table, the ledger with its columns of eggs and honey and fiddle lessons. On the counter lay the notebook where I wrote down each day what I chose to keep—today's entry read, in my crooked hand: *Taught Tomas to leave the water alone. Pulled a thorn from a soldier. Serah laughed at supper. Keep all of it.*

And low in the east, through the window I had scrubbed clean, the gap where Vael had been seemed, just for a heartbeat, to flicker—like a lamp behind a curtain, like a held breath, like something very old and very patient beginning, at last, to wake.
