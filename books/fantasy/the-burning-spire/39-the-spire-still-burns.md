## 39. The Spire Still Burns

The stairs of the Burning Spire had been rebuilt wider than they had been before, Serah noticed, the way she noticed everything now—habit of a hunter, even a retired one. Wide enough for two to climb abreast. Wide enough that no one climbing them would ever have to go alone.

She said nothing about it. Some things were better left as small mercies.

Jude climbed beside her, his breath coming a little harder than hers, though he'd never admit it. A year since the rite. A year since he'd poured himself into the flame and come back out the other side as a man with hollow places where his life used to be. He was fuller now—he'd been refilling slowly, deliberately, the way one fills a cistern from a spring rather than a flood—but there were still rooms in him with bare walls, and she had learned not to flinch when he wandered into one and stood there, quiet, trying to remember why it mattered.

"Counting steps?" he asked.

"Counting stars," she said. "There are more than last month."

He glanced up through the gaps in the stairwell's open ribs, where the night showed itself in slices. "The astronomers say the sky's still healing. Patches over the eastern sea where nothing shines yet."

"The astronomers can wait." She took his hand without looking at it, the way you take the hand of someone whose grip you know by heart—which, for her, was literal. She knew his grip from her notes, from her own careful handwriting: *His left hand is warmer than his right. He holds on like he's afraid the thing held will be taken back.* "I want to see it whole tonight."

They climbed.

* * *

From the top, the city spread out below like a spilled jewelry box.

Lantern District first, always—the old market quarter where he'd once sold charms made of other people's Tuesdays, where she'd once chased him through stalls and alleys with a warrant in her fist and something worse than a warrant in her chest. The lanterns were lit now in their hundreds, real fire and fae-glow both, sanctioned under the new accord, and the streets between them ran with people who were not afraid of being seen. That was the change no edict could have made. Marrow had ruled by making everyone invisible to each other; the new covenant, the one written in Serah's hand and Maelis's, had done the opposite. It had made looking legal.

Beyond the district, the river caught the light and carried it out toward the dark hills where the border wards now stood restored—not the old scars of the old covenant, but newer marks, kinder ones, drawn jointly by human mages and fae emissaries who still flinched at each other's shadows but were learning, slowly, the shape of each other's hands.

And above it all, the stars.

Full sky. Every anchor burning. The astronomers' charts called three of them *the recovered ones*—stars that had come back dimmer than before, like voices hoarse from shouting—but to anyone standing on the spire's crown, they simply burned, and that was enough.

Jude stood at the parapet with his palms flat on the stone, and Serah watched him do what he did every year on this night: he listened.

Not with his ears. With whatever it was the flame had left in him—a scar tissue of magic, a callus where the covenant had passed through him like a hot wire. Mother Quill would have called it an echo. He called it *the hum*, and he said it sounded like a room full of people remembering at the same time.

"It's louder up here," he said.

"It always is."

"No—" He turned to her, and the starlight did something kind to his face, softened the lines the last year had carved. "It's different. Listen."

She listened. Wind. The far murmur of the city. Her own heart, which had learned to beat calmly in his presence only through considerable practice.

"I don't hear anything," she admitted.

"That's the point." He smiled—not his old thief's smile, all edges and misdirection, but the newer one, the one that arrived slowly and stayed. "A year ago the hum was a wound. Now it's just... a voice. One voice. It says the same thing every time I listen." He looked back at the stars. "It says *held*. Like something being held. Like something deciding, every day, to keep holding."

Serah felt her throat tighten. She was a woman who had built her life on evidence, on notes and warrants and the clean architecture of proof, and here was a man offering her testimony she couldn't verify, couldn't file, couldn't cross-examine. And she believed him anyway. That was the miracle she'd stopped fighting sometime around midwinter: belief without documentation.

"What do you hold?" she asked quietly. It was their ritual question. Every anniversary, same words.

He turned back to her. And this—the answer—was the part he never got right the same way twice, because he didn't remember it the same way twice. What he knew of their story came from her telling, from his own notes in a handwriting he was still learning to call his own, from the way his body remembered things his mind had surrendered.

"You," he said. Simple as that. "I hold you."

"And if you'd known the price beforehand? If someone had stood at the bottom of these stairs and told you exactly what it would cost?"

He considered it honestly, the way he considered everything now—no charm, no deflection. She had learned that this was what she loved most: not the man he'd been, clever and quick and armored in stolen hours, but this one, who answered slowly because he refused to lie even for comfort.

"I'd have paid it," he said. "But I'm glad I didn't know. Not because ignorance is kinder. Because—" he searched, found it, "—because choosing blind is still choosing. Maybe more so. You can't call it love if you know how it ends."

"It doesn't end," she said. "That's rather the point of tonight."

* * *

They sat against the parapet, shoulder to shoulder, and shared the supper she'd packed—bread still warm from the bakery on Candlemaker's Row, hard cheese, a flask of cider that tasted of autumn. Below them the city glowed. Above them the stars performed their ancient, patient work.

"I brought something," she said, when the flask was empty.

He raised an eyebrow. "Is it a warrant?"

"Worse. A gift." From her coat she drew a small book—not her field journal, the famous one, the one the archive had tried to acquire and she'd refused twice. A different book. Newer. Its cover was soft leather gone supple with handling, and its pages, when she opened it, were dense with two kinds of writing: hers, precise and upright, and his, looser, learning itself.

His notebook. The one he'd started keeping after the rite, when Dr. Ellery at the recovery house had suggested that memory lost might be partially rebuilt from the outside in—write down what others tell you, write down what feels true, and eventually the true things start to feel like yours.

Except this wasn't his notebook. His notebook lived in their flat above the charm shop. This one—

"This is your copy," he said slowly. "Of us."

"My copy." She set it between them. "Every version of our story I've told you. Every note. Every night you asked me to repeat something and I wrote down which parts made you laugh, which parts made you go quiet. Three hundred and eleven entries." She touched the page. "I've been keeping it in case—" She stopped. Started again, because she'd promised herself honesty on this night of all nights. "In case you decided one day that the story was too heavy. That living inside a memory you have to be told is worse than living without it. In case you wanted to put it down and walk away and be a man with no past instead of a man with a borrowed one."

"And you'd let me?"

"I'd hate it," she said. "And I'd let me. Because that's what the covenant taught me—what you taught me, actually, though you don't remember teaching it. Nothing given freely stays given if the giver gets to keep a leash on it. Not memories. Not love. Not stories." She closed the book and pressed it into his hands. "So. Tonight, on the anniversary, I'm asking properly. Do you want to keep hearing it? Or do you want to stop?"

The wind moved through the parapet's open arches. Somewhere far below, a bell rang the ninth hour, and the sound climbed the spire like a slow tide.

Jude turned the book over in his hands. She watched him read the spine, where she'd lettered the title herself in hunter's block-caps, the only script she trusted to survive weather and time:

**THE BURNING SPIRE: AS TOLD TO JUDE KAVANAGH**

"When I was a thief," he said at last, "I had a rule. Never take much, never be seen. And underneath that rule there was another one I never said out loud, because saying it would have ruined the professionalism." He looked up at her. "The real rule was: don't get close enough to anyone that losing them would leave a hole. Because I already knew what holes looked like. I saw them every day in my customers. Grief is just a hole shaped like a person."

"Yes."

"So here's what I know, even without the memories." He tapped his own chest. "There's a hole in me. Big one. Everything I gave to the flame went through it. But it doesn't ache like grief does—I know that much, I've spent a year around grieving people in the shop, I know the shape of their hurt. Mine doesn't hurt. It *waits*. Like a room swept clean and kept ready." He met her eyes. "And every day, some part of me understands that the room isn't empty. It's furnished. You furnished it. I just have to be told where everything goes."

Serah laughed—wet-eyed, helpless—and leaned into him, and he put his arm around her, and for a while neither of them spoke, because the stars were doing their work and the city was doing its work and some silences are full to bursting.

Then Jude said, "Tell me the part about the lanterns again. The chase."

"You always ask for that one."

"Because it's the best one. A terrifying witch-hunter cornering a helpless shopkeeper—"

"You stole an hour of my memory and fled across rooftops."

"*Allegedly.*"

"Through a fish stall," she said, settling into the rhythm of it, the well-worn groove of a tale told often enough to become a kind of home. "You knocked an entire barrel of pickled herring onto Constable Bray, who to this day believes it was an assassination attempt by the Herring Guild."

"And then?"

"And then you caught me. That's the part everyone leaves out. You caught me—you, the fugitive—and instead of running, you looked at me like you were seeing the whole of your future, and you stole one hour instead of the ten you needed to escape, and you left something kind in its place." She paused. "Do you want to know what you left?"

He nodded. He always nodded. He never remembered, and he always wanted to.

"You left the feeling of being *believed*. For one hour, I walked through the Lantern District certain—absolutely certain—that somewhere in this city was a person worth breaking every rule I had. When it faded, I went home and wrote in my journal: *Today I met a criminal who made me feel more honest than the law ever has.*" She smiled. "I've kept that entry. It's in the book. Page twelve."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. Then he opened the notebook to page twelve, and read his own history in her handwriting, and when he looked up his eyes were bright.

"One more year," he said. "That's what the healers said. One more year and the echoes might start arriving on their own. Fragments. Not everything—never everything—but pieces. My own pieces, coming home."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I'll spend my life being told the best story ever written." He kissed her forehead. "By its co-author."

* * *

Later—much later, when the city's bells had counted midnight and the cold had driven all but the most stubborn lovers indoors—they stood once more at the parapet for the moment they'd climbed all these stairs to see.

The anniversary hour. The moment, one year past, when the sixth star had reignited and the covenant had sealed, and the sky had begun, star by star, to come home.

Jude took her hand. And then he did the thing he did every year, the thing that had become, without either of them planning it, the truest ritual of their lives:

He chose her.

Out loud. Facing her. Under the whole repaired sky.

"A year ago I didn't know your name," he said. "Now I know it better than my own. Serah Vane. Witch-hunter. Keeper of terrible field notes. Woman who packs too much cheese." Her laugh cracked the cold air. "I don't remember falling in love with you. But I remember—" he pressed her hand over his heart, "—this. Whatever the flame left. Whatever the hum is holding. It knows you. So I figure the man who made that choice knew something worth knowing, and I trust him. I choose what he chose. I choose you. Today, tomorrow, and every day after, until the stars burn out or I learn them all by name—whichever comes first."

Below them the city glittered. Above them the stars burned—every anchor, every light, the whole patient machinery of heaven turning as it should.

And Serah Vane, who had once believed that love was a liability and memory was evidence and the law was enough, rose on her toes and answered the only way that mattered:

"Then choose me again tomorrow," she whispered against his mouth. "And the day after. And I'll keep telling you the story until you can tell it back to me in your sleep."

"I'll need more cheese," he murmured.

"You'll get more cheese."

They kissed beneath the spire's eternal flame, and the flame bent toward them like it remembered them too, and far overhead a star that the astronomers had not yet catalogued flickered—once, twice—and steadied into brightness, as if the sky itself had been listening.

Neither of them saw it.

But the hum did. And deep in the marrow of the world, something old and patient began, very softly, to sing.
