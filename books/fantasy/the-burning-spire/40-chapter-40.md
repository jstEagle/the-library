## 40. Chapter 40

The spire had been burning for a year, and the city had learned to sleep beneath it.

Jude felt the change in the streets before he saw it in the sky. A year ago, the Lantern District had moved like a held breath—shoulders hunched, eyes down, everyone performing the great pantomime of being no one. Now the fishwives argued about prices in voices that carried. Children ran messages between the market stalls with charms braided into their bootlaces, legal charms, licensed charms, little knots of warmth and luck that Mother Quill's students—her students, the phrase still caught in his throat like a fishbone—taught in the old cathedral every morning. The gutters had become a school. The school had become a neighborhood. The neighborhood was becoming, slowly and without anyone decreeing it, a city.

He walked through all of it carrying a satchel of charms for the living, and none of them were stolen.

That was the discipline now, the daily practice of it. A woman on Tanner's Row had come to him three days ago with her grief wrapped around her like a wet coat—her husband, drowned two winters past—and she had asked him, in the old way, to take it from her. Take it all, she'd said. I don't want to carry it anymore. And he had sat with her while the tea went cold and explained, for the hundredth time, the difference between theft and release. *I can't take it. But I can help you set it down. You'll still know he died. You'll still miss him. But it won't be a stone in your chest. It'll be a stone by the road, and you can visit it when you choose.* She had wept. She had chosen. And when she'd left, walking lighter, she'd paid him in bread and a jar of honey and a kiss on the cheek that smelled of woodsmoke, and Jude had stood in his doorway a long time, watching the street, feeling something he had no charm for.

Belonging. That was the word. He had spent three years learning how to be invisible and one terrible, luminous night learning that invisibility was just loneliness with good posture.

Tonight he was climbing the spire.

* * *

Serah was waiting at the base of the stairs, and even after everything—even after the telling and retelling, even after the notes in his own hand that described her so precisely he sometimes felt he'd written her into existence—she still stopped his breath.

She wore her hunter's coat, the old one, the grey one with the Crown's sigil cut away at the shoulder. She'd kept the sigil in a drawer for a year and finally burned it on the anniversary of the raid, and what remained was just a coat, worn soft at the elbows, that smelled of her: steel oil and lavender and the particular cold-air sharpness of a woman who had spent her life walking toward danger so others could walk away from it. Her hair was shorter than it had been in the notes. She'd cut it the week after the rite, he'd learned. He liked to imagine her at the mirror with the shears, deciding who she would be when the law no longer told her.

"You're late," she said, and smiled to take the sting out of it, because she knew—he had told her, she had told him, they had built the whole architecture of their honesty together—that he was never late. He was early, and then he waited where he could watch her arrive.

"I was talking to Widow Merrin's boy. He wants to apprentice."

"Another one?"

"He's the fourth this season." Jude shifted the satchel on his shoulder. "The gutter cathedral's going to need a bigger nave."

"Mother Quill would have hated that."

"She'd have hated it beautifully. Complained the whole time while reorganizing every classroom herself." He looked up. The spire rose above them into the dark, its flame a soft gold at the crown, steady as a heartbeat. A year of steady. "You ready?"

"I've been ready since dawn." Serah took his hand. Her grip was calloused and sure and it fit his the way a key fits a lock it was made for. "One year. You know what Quill's students are calling tonight?"

"Starfall Eve."

"Starfall Eve." She laughed, low. "Children. We nearly died up there."

"We did die up there. Some of us." He said it lightly, but they both went quiet, because the list of the dead was not light: Mother Quill, who had read his fate in smoke and walked into Marrow's hunters with her white eyes open and her hands raised, singing. Tam and the twins. Half the cathedral's defenders. A year did not make that lighter. A year just taught you how to carry it—by the road, where you could visit.

"Then we climb for them too," Serah said.

"Every stair."

* * *

The spire's interior had been rebuilt in the old way, the covenant way, which was to say the masons worked from no plans anyone had written down and somehow every wall came out right. The stairs wound upward through stone that remembered being a chimney for a god. Jude felt it as they climbed—that low thrum in the bedrock, the flame's pulse, the covenant breathing. Before the rite, the spire had been cold. He remembered that from the hooded stranger's memory, the first thing he'd ever stolen: the Burning Spire whole, and how the memory had ached with warmth, and how he'd assumed for three years that the ache was just the beauty of it. It wasn't. The spire was warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a held hand is warm, and the warmth had a source, and for one year now the source had been him.

Every memory he'd ever gathered. Every charm he'd ever sold. Three years of careful invisibility, the widow's grief, the stranger's spire, the hour of Serah he'd stolen in the Lantern District and the hour she'd given back and the thousand hours after that she'd given freely—all of it poured into the flame, and the flame had taken it and said *yes, this will do, this will hold the sky.*

The stars had come back one at a time. First the Anchor, dead center over the spire, blazing so bright the whole city had stopped in the streets and wept. Then the others, night by night, like lamps relit in a house long dark. The fae had withdrawn behind the restored borders—Queen Maelis's court, dimmed but enduring, sending ambassadors now instead of armies, and Serah had spent half the year in rooms with the ambassador drafting the accord that bore both their names. The Vane-Maelis Accord. Magic, lawful. Memory, sovereign. No one's mind taken without consent, ever, under any crown, in any realm. Serah had fought for that clause with her whole scarred heart. Jude had watched her do it and fallen in love with her for the fortieth time, and told her so, and she'd said *only the fortieth?* and he'd said *the rest are in the notes.*

They climbed. The stairs went on. Somewhere below, the city glittered, and above, through the arrow-slits, the sky was salted with stars.

"Stop," Serah said at the half-landing, the one where the wall still bore the scorch-marks from Marrow's rite. She turned him gently to face the marks. "We always rush past this part."

"Do we have to do the part where you make me stand still and feel things?"

"Yes. It's in the accord. Article Nine." She touched the scorched stone. Here Marrow had stood, crown of stolen light burning in his skull, chanting himself into the covenant's place, certain that divinity was a thing a man could seize. Here Serah had dueled his enforcer, blade against blade in the dark, while the gutter mages rose in the streets below and the invisible became visible together. Here Jude had knelt beside the Chancellor's collapsing mind and taken the sixth shard—the last theft of his life, and the only one that had felt like justice.

"He still writes letters," Jude said. "From the prison at Greywatch. Did I tell you?"

"Every month." Serah's voice went flat and careful. "Still claiming the stars were his to give."

"He thinks he almost won. That's the worst of it. Not the cruelty—men are cruel, I've been cruel. It's that he looked at the whole sky and saw a purse." Jude shook his head. "I stole memories for coin and even I never—"

"You were poor and careful. He was rich and hungry. It's not the same crime." She said it the way she said all her absolutions: like a verdict, final, no appeals. "I've told you this."

"You've told me this."

"And you don't believe me yet."

"And I don't believe me yet." He smiled. "Give me another year."

* * *

The crown of the spire opened to the sky like a chalice.

They came out onto the platform where the flame burned—no, where the flame *lived*, because that was the word the fae used and after a year Serah had stopped correcting them. It rose from the old brazier in a column of gold, silent, warm on their faces at ten paces, and it did not flicker. Wind moved around it. Rain, on the nights it rained, fell past it. The flame had opinions about weather.

Jude stepped to the edge and looked out, and the city unrolled beneath him to the river and the hills and the broken border-fells beyond, all of it under stars, real stars, the anchors holding the veil like stitches holding a wound closed. Somewhere out there, past the veil, the Otherworld was slowly remembering its own light. Queen Maelis had written—she wrote to Serah in a spidery antique hand that took three scholars to translate—that the first new star had kindled over her court in a century, and that her people had danced until dawn, and that she, who had let the wards fall and gambled a war on one small thief, had wept in front of her entire court and regretted nothing.

"Do you ever miss them?" Serah asked. She came to stand beside him, shoulder to shoulder. "The stolen ones. The memories."

He considered it honestly, the way she always demanded. "Sometimes. Like missing a room you lived in. I know what was in it. I just can't walk back in." He turned his hand over, looked at his palm, the thief's palm, quick and clever and now just a hand. "But here's the thing I figured out. Everything I gave up was borrowed. Every memory I ever took was somebody else's life. The flame didn't take anything that was mine." He looked at her. "Except you. The night we met. The night at the fire. All of it. Those were the only ones that were mine, and I gave those up too, and it was still right. It was the most right thing I ever did."

"I know." Her voice had gone rough. "I was there."

"You were there. I have it on excellent authority. There are notes."

"There are notes," she agreed, wetly, laughing. "Forty pages. I've read them to you so many times you correct my mistakes."

"You always make the Lantern District chase too short. I was much more impressive."

"You were terrified and you ran into a fish stall."

"I was *strategically* terrified, and the fish stall was a tactical choice—"

She kissed him. That ended the argument, which was, he suspected, why she did it.

* * *

Later—they sat with their backs against the brazier's warm stone, the flame at their shoulders, the city's lights below, the stars wheeling slowly overhead—Serah took out the little book.

His book. The one he'd started keeping in those first empty weeks, when she came every day and told him their story like a bedtime tale and he'd begun writing it down so it couldn't slide away again. Forty pages now. She'd teased him about the number. He'd said it felt right.

"Read me the last one," she said.

"You know the last one."

"Read it anyway. It's tradition. We're a people of traditions now, apparently. Starfall Eve, the accord, the school, the letters to Greywatch. We might as well have this too."

He opened the book to the final page. His own handwriting, steadier now than it had been a year ago, when his hands had shaken for months—not from grief, he'd learned, but from the strange lightness of being a man with no past, like a ship with no ballast rolling in a calm sea.

He read: *"The last memory I choose is this one. Not the night we met—she can tell me that one again tomorrow. This one. The top of the spire, the fire warm at my back, her shoulder against mine, the stars doing their slow old work. I choose it because it isn't a memory of something that happened to me. It's a memory of something I'm still doing. I'm still up here. I'm still choosing. Tomorrow I'll choose again, and the day after, and if the flame wants the whole stack of them someday, it can have them, and they'll all be this: her, and the stars, and me, staying."*

Serah was quiet a long moment. Below them, faintly, the bells of the gutter cathedral rang the hour, and he could hear, threading under the bells, the sound of the Starfall Eve festival in the square—music, voices, the licensed charms of children popping like green sparks.

"Jude," she said.

"Hm?"

"There's something I haven't told you yet. I was going to wait. I've been waiting all week and it's making me terrible company."

He turned to look at her. In the flame's gold light, her face was doing something complicated—trying to be composed and failing at the edges. His heart, which had learned to read her the way he'd once read locks, went quick.

"Serah."

"The ambassador arrived this morning. From Maelis's court. With a letter." She reached into her coat and drew out a fold of paper that shimmered faintly, the way fae paper did, like it was lit from behind. "It's addressed to you. Both of us. It's—Jude, it's an invitation. To the Otherworld. To the court. For the solstice." She was smiling now, wide and unguarded, the smile from page twelve of the notes, the one from the fugitives' fire. "But that's not the part. The part is—Maelis writes that the new star over her court? The one that kindled after the rite?" She took his hand and pressed the letter into it. "It didn't kindle from the covenant. The covenant stars are all accounted for. The scholars have checked three times. This one is new. It appeared the night of the rite, in the place where the old sky-map shows nothing, and it burns gold, Jude. Spire-gold. And the queen writes that her people have a word for a star that kindles from a mortal's given memory, and the word translates—" she laughed, helpless, "—the word translates as *hearth*."

Jude looked down at the letter. The shimmering script moved like it was breathing. And far overhead, past the veil, past the borders, a gold star burned in a place where no star had ever been named.

"Serah," he said slowly, "are you telling me I'm a lighthouse?"

"I'm telling you," she said, taking his face in both her hands, "that you gave away everything you had, and the sky is still counting it. And I'm telling you that the queen wants us there at the solstice, and I've already said yes, and you're going to say yes too, because I know you, Jude Kavanagh. I've read all forty pages."

He looked at her—his hunter, his historian, the woman who had walked into the dark with him and walked back out carrying the light between them—and he felt the future open like a door.

"Yes," he said.

Below, the bells rang on. Above, the new star held. And the spire burned, warm at their backs, keeping the sky, keeping them, all the way home.
