## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The morning the world began to end, Jonah Eriksen woke before the bells and made themselves small.

It was a discipline, smallness. A craft practiced as diligently as any sword-form or prayer. Jonah lay in their narrow bed above the tallow-chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane and performed it in order: first the face, smoothing away sleep until it held nothing but mild attention; then the hands, stilling the small restless movements that betrayed a mind at work; last the name itself, folding *Jonah Eriksen* away into some interior drawer and drawing out instead *the taster* — a function, not a person, useful and unremarkable as a spoon.

Below, the chandler was already stirring his vats. The smell of rendered fat crept up through the floorboards, sweetish and heavy, and Jonah breathed it in with something like affection. Ten years they had lived above that smell. It was the smell of being no one in particular, which was the safest smell in the world.

They dressed in grey. Not mourning-grey, not servant-grey — a grey so precisely between that the eye refused to record it. They braided back their hair in the style worn by half the palace staff. They ate a heel of bread and a boiled egg, chewing slowly, cataloguing without meaning to: the bread was day-old, slightly sour at the crust, harmless; the egg was fresh, laid within two days, harmless. Everything Jonah ate was catalogued. It had been so long since they swallowed anything without reading it first that they had forgotten there was another way to live.

Outside, Thornfell's capital was shaking off its dark. The city had no true name of its own anymore — everyone just called it the Queen's City, or the City Under the Mountain, because the Thornfells rose behind it like a sleeping animal, white-shouldered even in high summer, and everyone knew better than to wake what slept. Snow lay in the high passes year-round now. Had done for a hundred years. The long winter, people called it, though it wasn't winter exactly; it was more like a debt the sky kept collecting, a little cold paid out every season, forever, in exchange for something nobody talked about at dinner.

Jonah walked to the palace by the servants' road, past the fish-stalls and the shrine to the Unnamed Saint, and passed through the postern gate where the guard — a broad woman named Hesk whose breath always smelled of onions — nodded at them without interest.

"Taster," Hesk said.

"Hesk," said the taster.

That was the whole of it. That was the whole of Jonah's public life: one word exchanges, grey clothes, a body that moved through the palace like water through cracks. In ten years of service they had learned the names of four hundred courtiers and made certain that none of them had learned theirs. The chambermaids called them *love* and forgot them by supper. The stewards wrote *taster* in the duty rolls and nothing else. Even the Queen, who saw them daily, looked at Jonah the way one looks at a chair — acknowledging its presence, never its existence.

It was, Jonah told themselves every morning, exactly what they wanted.

Most mornings they almost believed it.

* * *

The kitchens of the Queen's City were hot as a throat, and smelled this morning of roasted quail, honeyed parsnips, and something darker underneath — a bitterness Jonah caught before they were through the door.

They stopped. Closed their eyes. Let the tongue do its slow work against the roof of the mouth, sorting the air the way a jeweler sorts gravel.

Bitterness. Faint. Green. Under the roast, under the smoke of the ovens. Coming from the west wall, where the spice cabinet stood.

"Morning, taster." Cook Brannick, enormous and flour-white, did not look up from his birds. "Her Majesty takes the morning meal in the solar today. Quail, parsnips, the almond cakes she likes."

"Mm." Jonah drifted toward the spice cabinet the way a bored person drifts, hands loose, expression vacant. "New shipment?"

"Came in Tuesday. Why?"

"Just asking."

The cabinet's third drawer held the dried herbs: sage, hyssop, wormwood, and a paper twist of finely ground root that should not have been there at all. Jonah pinched a grain between finger and thumb, touched it to the tongue's tip, and waited.

There it was. A slow bloom of numbness, cold spreading backward from the taste-buds like frost crossing a window. *Crownshade*, or something near kin to it. Not enough to kill — not in this quantity, not outright. Enough, over weeks, over months, to make a queen vague. Forgetful. Malleable.

Someone was seasoning Isolde's food the way you'd train a dog to walk into water: gradually, so she'd never notice the river rising.

Jonah folded the paper twist closed again, exactly as they'd found it, and turned back to Brannick with their face arranged into pleasant blankness.

"Quail and parsnips," they said. "I'll take the full service."

"You always do." Brannick grinned. "You've got a stomach like a stone cistern, taster. Twenty years I've cooked for this palace and I've never seen you so much as hiccup."

"Lucky blood," Jonah said, which was the joke everyone believed, which was also, in its way, the truth.

The meal came to the solar on silver dishes under linen covers, and Jonah tasted each one standing at the Queen's left hand, in the place reserved for furniture. Quail — clean, fat-rendered, no taint but the crownshade already sitting in the cook-pots from days of dosing. Parsnips — honey, butter, salt, safe. Almond cake — and here Jonah paused a half-second longer than protocol allowed, because beneath the almond and the sugar there ran a thread of something else, thin as wire. Bitter. Cold. The same family as the powder in the drawer, but subtler. Refined. Expensive.

Someone had moved from training doses to something with an edge on it.

Queen Isolde watched her taster eat from beneath lids heavy with state business. She was not old — fifty, perhaps, though the long winters aged rulers faster than wars — and she had the kind of face that had been beautiful in the way mountains are beautiful, all distance and weather. Today she looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleep.

"Well?" she said. "Do I live?"

"You live, Majesty." Jonah set down the fork. Their voice was a servant's voice, flat and warm at once. "The quail is excellent. The parsnips are sweeter than yesterday's. The almond cake—" a pause, measured to the width of a hair, "—is very rich. Her Majesty may wish to leave half for the afternoon."

Isolde's eyes narrowed, just slightly. She was sharp, whatever the poison was doing to her; she had been sharp for thirty years of throne-work, and crowns don't dull easily.

"You always say that about the cake."

"The cake is always very rich, Majesty."

A silence. Somewhere below, the kitchen bells rang the third hour. The Queen looked at Jonah — really looked, for perhaps the fourth time in ten years — and Jonah felt the old danger of it, the vertigo of being *seen*, and held their grey blankness like a shield-boss against a spear.

"Strange," Isolde said at last. "Sometimes I think you're the only honest face in this palace, taster. And other times I think I've never seen your face at all." She waved a ringed hand. "Go. Take the rest for the household. And tell Brannick if he sends me almond cake again this week I'll have him promoted to something he hates."

Jonah bowed and withdrew, carrying the tray and the knowledge of the poison out of the solar like a coal cupped in bare hands.

In the corridor, alone, they let themselves breathe.

Here was the arithmetic Jonah lived by, the sum they checked every day the way misers check coin: the crownshade in the spice drawer meant someone at court was moving against the Queen — slowly, patiently, someone with access and money. Reporting it would mean becoming visible. Becoming visible meant becoming a name, and names got written down, and things written down got found by other people, and Jonah had spent twenty-five years making sure that no ledger anywhere contained more than the word *taster* beside their shape.

But not reporting it meant watching a queen be hollowed out one almond cake at a time.

Jonah carried the tray down the back stairs and told themselves, as they did every day, that they would decide by evening. They had decided by evening every day for a week. They had decided nothing.

Some debts, they thought, you pay by pretending they aren't yours.

* * *

The archive of the Royal Survey stood apart from the palace proper, a low stone building hunched against the mountain's flank like a barnacle, and it smelled of dust and lamp-oil and the particular dry sweetness of old vellum. Jonah had a legitimate reason to be there — the household steward wanted a map of the orchard roads for a delivery route, and maps were Wren's trade — but legitimacy was only ever the outer wrapping of the reason. The true reason was waiting inside, as she always was, with ink on her fingers and lamplight caught in the loose brown curls escaping her braid.

Wren Ashvale, cartographer, second-class. The only person alive who could pick Jonah out of a crowd of ten thousand greys.

"You're late," she said, not looking up from her drafting table. Her voice was dry as her paper, and just as warm once you knew how to read it.

"I'm punctual. Everyone else is early."

"That's the same thing said backwards." She did look up then, and the look went through Jonah's grey blankness like a needle through cloth — found the person folded inside it, and smiled at them. Only at them. "You taste anything interesting today?"

"Almond cake."

"Poisoned?"

"Not yet."

Wren laughed — a short, quiet laugh, trained down small by years of working in places where quiet was currency — and pushed aside her drafts to make room on the bench. Jonah sat. Their shoulders came down from around their ears for the first time since dawn. This was the miracle of Wren, the thing Jonah had never managed to say aloud: that in her presence the craft of smallness simply failed. She saw them. There was no performing otherwise.

"There's my commission," Wren said, tapping a folio bound in cracked leather. "Merchant house wants a survey of the eastern passes redrawn. Which means cross-referencing every survey of the Thornfells for the last two centuries, which means the restricted shelves, which means—" she wiggled her ink-stained fingers, "—me, elbow-deep in dead men's handwriting. Listen to this. A surveyor in the reign of Isolde's grandmother claims there's a valley above the treeline that stays green all winter. Green, Jonah. In the long winter. He swears he saw wildflowers in the snow."

"Drunk," Jonah said.

"On brand for surveyors. But here's the thing — three other accounts mention it. Same valley, different centuries. All dismissed as error." Her eyes had gone bright, that cartographer's fever Jonah both loved and feared. "Places don't stay green in the long winter unless something is keeping them green. And things that keep valleys green under a hundred years of snow are exactly the sort of things that get erased from official maps."

"Wren."

"I'm not going to hike up there. I'm saying it's *interesting*."

"It's interesting," Jonah agreed, and reached for her hand, and she let them take it, ink and all. Her fingers were cold from the stone room and Jonah warmed them without thinking, the way you'd bank a fire. Around them the archive ticked and settled, full of the accumulated weight of everything the kingdom had chosen to write down — and, in the gaps between the shelves, everything it had chosen not to.

This was the hour Jonah lived for. Not the tasting, not the grey craft of invisibility, but this: lamplight, dust, Wren's shoulder against theirs, talking about nothing that mattered. Or rather — talking about things that mattered enormously and were allowed to matter because no one else was listening. With Wren, Jonah's voice came out different. Less flat. More like a person's.

"Stay for supper?" Wren asked. "I've bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled things I'm afraid of."

"Afraid of?"

"It's been in my cupboard since spring. I think it's achieved sentience."

"I've eaten worse on purpose."

"I know," Wren said, and kissed them, quick and sure, tasting of lamp-oil and tea. "That's the most romantic thing about you."

And Jonah — who had spent the whole day swallowing poisons and silences, who had held a queen's secret murder in their mouth since morning and told no one — felt the strange, guilty peace of the deeply hidden. Here was the whole architecture of their life, and it was good, it was *good*: useful enough to be safe, invisible enough to be free, loved by exactly one person who knew the whole of them, which was one more than anyone had known in twenty years.

Routine built on quiet self-erasure. But it was warm. It was theirs.

They stayed for supper. The pickled things were, indeed, sentient. Wren talked about contour lines until the lamps burned low.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when Wren went back to her table for one last look at the merchant commission, and Jonah, pulling on their grey coat to go, heard her stop breathing.

Not dramatically. Just — stopped. The specific silence of a person who has seen something and needs the whole world to be quiet so they can be sure.

"What is it?" Jonah asked.

Wren didn't answer. She was standing over an open book — not her commission folio but something older, something from the restricted shelf, its pages soft and foxed as autumn leaves. A book of prophecies, from the look of it; the Survey archives were full of them, the fever-dreams of dead seers, catalogued and ignored. Jonah came around the table and looked over her shoulder, and the lamplight fell across a page of cramped brown ink, a date written in a margin —

Tomorrow's date.

And beside the date, a sketch.

It was small, done quickly in faded sepia, the kind of throwaway drawing a scribe makes while waiting for inspiration. A face, three-quarter view. Short dark hair braided back. Grey clothes suggested in two strokes. Mild attention in the eyes, and beneath the mildness, if you knew how to look — and Jonah knew how to look — a watchfulness like a blade kept sheathed.

It was Jonah's face.

Drawn a hundred years ago.

"Wren," Jonah said, and their voice came out in their servant's voice, flat and calm, because the other voice — the one that wanted to say *that's me, that's my face, that's impossible* — was not safe to use, even here, even with her. "Where did this book come from?"

"I don't know." Wren's hand hovered over the page without touching it, the way you hover over a snake. "Restricted shelf. Uncatalogued. Jonah, I pulled it for the pass surveys, I swear, I wasn't looking for anything, it was just — wedged behind the Alaric folios—"

She stopped. Went pale in the lamplight.

"The Alaric folios," she repeated, very quietly, as though the words were a door she'd just noticed standing open in a wall she'd thought was solid.

Below the sketch, in the same century-old hand, the scribe had written a single line. The ink had gone brown and brittle, but the letters were clear:

*The thorn that burns goes down alone.*

Jonah stared at their own face staring back at them across a hundred years, and felt the grey craft of a lifetime crack straight down the middle — because you can make yourself small, you can make yourself a function, a spoon, a shadow on a duty roll, but you cannot make yourself unwritten. Somewhere in the dark behind them, the mountain loomed over the city with its hundred-year snow, patient as a creditor.

"Jonah," Wren whispered. "Why does this book know you?"
