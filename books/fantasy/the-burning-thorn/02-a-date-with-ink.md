## 2. A Date With Ink

The archive of Vanehold Palace smelled the way all old places smell when no one loves them enough: dust and tallow and the faint, sweet rot of leather that had outlived its readers. Wren had worked there for six years and had come to think of the smell as a kind of honesty. The palace above—the marble halls, the perfumed corridors where Queen Isolde's court drifted like moths in velvet—smelled of nothing at all. It was scrubbed, polished, scented into blankness. But down here, three floors beneath the map room, the past was allowed to have a body.

Wren loved it the way some people loved a lover: unreasonably, possessively, with a willingness to forgive it anything.

Tonight the archive was theirs alone. The lamp on the reading table threw a cone of amber light across a scatter of documents—deeds, survey fragments, a water-stained petition from some long-dead miller—and at the center of it all, open under brass weights, lay the book.

*The Thornfell Oracles*, someone had written on the flyleaf in a hand like frost forming. *Collected by Brother Aldous Merrow, Anno Domini of the Long Count 412.* The date meant nothing to modern reckoning; the archivists had argued about it for a century and settled on "roughly a hundred years ago, give or take a war." The book itself was small—a fat little octavo bound in green leather gone black at the corners, its spine cracked into soft segments like an old man's knuckles. It had been catalogued as *Curiosities, Minor, Shelf 9* and not touched, by the accession record, in forty years.

Wren had pulled it because of a commission, and the commission was the only reason they were still awake at this hour, and the reason they were willing to be awake at this hour was sitting somewhere across the city right now, probably eating something dangerous, probably smiling about it.

Queen Isolde wanted a map. Not just any map—a *commemorative* one, a grand rendering of the Thornfell range to hang in the new council chamber, celebrating (the heralds had chosen this word with great care) *a century of winter's faithful protection*. One hundred years since the Frost Accord, whatever that had been; one hundred years of snow that came early, stayed late, and never once failed to seal the northern passes against invasion. The mountain kingdoms beyond the Thornfells had tried twice in living memory to come over the ice. Twice the weather had simply *refused them*, armies turning back white-lipped and half-frozen, and the crown had built cathedrals' worth of gratitude on that fact.

So: a map. And Wren, being the best cartographer in the palace's employ and too junior to refuse, had been given the task of making the mountains beautiful.

Which required research. Which required, tonight, cross-referencing every text that mentioned the Thornfells from the century before the Accord onward, because Her Majesty's chamberlain wanted period detail—"flourishes," he'd called them, "little ornaments of authenticity"—and Wren had privately decided that if they were going to ornament anything, they would ornament it with truth.

The Oracles were the last item on the list. A book of prophecies collected by a monk who had apparently wandered the foothills interviewing shepherds and hedge-witches. Wren expected nonsense. Delightful nonsense, hopefully—prophetic verse made good marginalia, a decorative border of portents.

They turned the first page and fell in.

Not with the prophecies, at first. With the *hand*. Brother Aldous had drawn as well as he wrote, and his sketches crowded the margins of every page: shepherd huts, a frozen waterfall rendered in strokes so fine they looked woven, a thorn-tree with its roots gripping a hillside like fingers. He drew the way Wren thought—which is to say compulsively, constantly, unable to leave a thing alone until it had been looked at properly. Wren felt an immediate, unreasonable kinship with a dead monk.

The prophecies themselves were the usual soup. A king with two shadows. A river running backward. A bell that rang underground. Aldous had dutifully recorded each one with its source and date, and most of them had the flat, generic texture of things said by people who wanted to be interesting.

Then, near the middle of the book, the handwriting changed.

It tightened. Sped up. The ink went blotchy in places, as though the pen had been moving faster than care allowed, and the neat scholarly entries gave way to something rawer. Wren leaned closer, tilting the page into the lamplight, and read:

*From a woman at the foot of the mountain, the third night of the deep snows. She would not give her name but she held my hand while she spoke it, and her grip was strong as a smith's.*

*A thorn that burns shall walk unseen*

*till the hour comes to be seen whole.*

*What was sold below shall rise above;*

*what sleeps in debt shall wake in soul.*

*Before the long winter finishes falling,*

*it will come down the goat-roads, carrying fire,*

*and the door beneath the root will know its name.*

There was more—a few lines smudged past recovery, a stain that might have been wine or tears—but Wren barely registered them, because beside the verse, filling the margin from top to bottom, Brother Aldous had drawn a face.

And Wren knew that face.

Knew it the way you know your own hands in the dark. Knew the sharp line of the jaw that always looked slightly bruised even in perfect health. Knew the crooked break high on the nose's bridge, badly set long ago, that Jonah refused to explain and that Wren had privately decided was a story worth waiting years for. Knew the eyes—not their color, which shifted between grey and green depending on the light and the lie—but the *set* of them, watchful and level, the eyes of someone standing very still in a room full of movement. Aldous had drawn the mouth with particular attention: closed, unsmiling, but with the ghost of something at one corner, wry and private, the expression Wren saw maybe once a week and hoarded like coins.

It was Jonah. It was Jonah exactly, rendered in iron-gall ink by a man a hundred years dead, and beneath it, in Aldous's hurried hand, a single line:

*She says this one walks already. She says I should write the day it begins.*

And below that, the date.

Wren sat back. The chair creaked. Somewhere overhead, a pipe knocked twice in the walls—the palace settling, breathing, indifferent—and the lamp flame stood perfectly still, and Wren discovered that they had stopped breathing and made themselves start again.

Tomorrow's date. Written in a century-old book. Under Jonah's face.

"That's not possible," Wren said aloud, to the archive, in the reasonable tone they used on apprentices who claimed compasses had lied to them. "That is a coincidence of faces. Faces repeat. There are only so many ways to arrange a jaw."

But even as they said it, their eye went back to the drawing and found the details that could not be coincidental, because Wren had spent months memorizing them without meaning to. The tiny scar through the left eyebrow, which Jonah had gotten—they'd finally admitted—falling out of a smuggler's cache as a teenager. The way the hair was sketched pushed back off the forehead, exactly how it looked after Jonah raked both hands through it, which they did whenever they were thinking hard. Aldous hadn't drawn a portrait from life. He'd drawn a portrait from *description*, from a woman's telling, and somehow he had caught all of it, and the woman had said *this one walks already*, and the date was tomorrow.

Wren did what they always did with a problem too large to hold: they reached for paper and began to work it.

They copied the verse out entire, letter-perfect, including the smudges. They traced the face onto a loose sheet, quickly, their own pencil catching the likeness in a dozen strokes because they knew it too well to miss. They noted the page number, the accession mark, the shelf. Methodical. Calm. Their hands were steady, and they watched them being steady with a strange detachment, the way you watch someone else's hands.

Then they went back through the book, page by page, fast now, hunting context. And the context was worse than the prophecy.

Because Aldous's later entries kept circling back. Three pages on, another note: *Returned to the woman. She spoke of a prince sold beneath the stone, and of a debt the mountain keeps count of. She wept and would not say more.* Five pages on: *The snows began this year earlier than any man remembers. The abbey roof groans under it. I begin to think the woman's words were not madness but arithmetic.* And then, near the end, in a final entry dated—Wren checked it twice, three times, pressing the page flat with trembling fingers—dated within a fortnight of the Frost Accord itself:

*I asked her when the winter would end. She said: when the thorn burns. I asked her what that meant. She looked at me with such pity, brother, such pity, and said: ask the ones who sold him.*

Ask the ones who sold him.

Sold *him*. Sold whom? Alaric? The name surfaced from Wren's schooling unbidden—Prince Alaric Vane, third child of the old king, died young, unremarkable, a footnote in a genealogy chart Wren had drawn twice for commemorative commissions just like this one. Died young. Had he? Wren tried to remember the actual record and found, with a cold prickle at the nape, that there wasn't one. No death date. No burial site. Just a birth, a title, and then nothing, as though the ink itself had been paid to look away.

The lamp guttered. Wren trimmed the wick with fingers that were no longer entirely steady, and made themselves sit, and think, in the manner of their craft.

A mapmaker's first law: when two sources disagree, do not average them. Find the error. So—error hypotheses. One: the date was misread. They checked it again by lamplight, then by candle, holding the page at three angles. Not misread. Two: the book was a forgery, planted recently. But the binding was right, the foxing was right, the iron-gall had burned brown into the fibers the way only real age does, and the accession record confirmed forty years of untouched shelf-time. Three: coincidence of faces. Four—

Four. Four was the hypothesis Wren's mind kept sliding away from, the way a sled slides off a trail, and it was this: that the woman at the foot of the mountain had told the truth, and the truth had been waiting patiently on Shelf 9 for a hundred years for someone to need it, and what it said was that the person Wren loved was named in prophecy, tied to a sold prince and a burning thorn and a door beneath a root, and that it would all begin *tomorrow*.

Wren thought about Jonah's hands. Jonah's hands were beautiful in the way worn tools are beautiful, scarred and capable, and Wren knew those hands better than anyone alive, and it occurred to them now—with a lurch, a vertigo of intimacy—that they did not know why the fingertips were stained faintly silver at the edges, permanent, like someone who handled certain metals or certain poisons for years. They had never asked. There were so many things they had never asked, because Jonah offered nothing unprompted and Wren had learned to receive what was given and call it enough. That was love, they'd thought. Patience. Not prying.

Or it was two people keeping each other's secrets so carefully that neither noticed they were building a wall.

"Walks unseen," Wren murmured. "Till the hour comes to be seen whole."

A food-taster. Wren had known that much almost from the beginning—Jonah was cagey about work, but the palace was small below stairs, and tongues were not. A royal taster, which meant a body deliberately, methodically poisoned over years until it became a ledger of venoms, a walking antidote, a person whose entire profession was *being harmed so that others needn't be*. Walks unseen. Of course Jonah walked unseen. Jonah had made an art of it, a career, a self. Wren had found that reticence romantic once. Now it sounded like the first line of a prophecy.

And tomorrow. What happened tomorrow? Nothing. A routine day. Jonah tasted for the queen in the morning; Wren had survey work on the commemorative map; in the evening they would meet—at the old boathouse on the canal, their place, where the reeds hid the light of a shared candle. Ordinary. Except that a dead monk had written tomorrow's date under Jonah's face a hundred years ago, and ordinary suddenly felt like the thin ice everyone insists will hold.

Wren closed the book. Then opened it again immediately, unable to bear the separation, and looked at the face once more.

Here was the thing they would remember afterward, in all the terrible weeks to come: the drawing did not frighten them. What frightened them was that they understood it. Looking at Aldous's lines, at the watchful stillness of those inked eyes, Wren felt the same thing they felt watching Jonah across a market crowd—pride, tenderness, and a low persistent worry, like a splinter under a nail, that the person they loved was braced, always, for something. That the stillness wasn't calm. It was readiness. You don't learn to stand like that unless you're waiting for a blow.

The woman at the foot of the mountain had seen it too. A hundred years before Jonah was born, someone had described that exact stance to a monk, and the monk had drawn it, and here it was.

Wonder curdled slowly in Wren's chest, thickening, souring. Wonder at first—genuine, breathless wonder, the cartographer's ecstasy of finding a feature on the ground that matched the map, except inverted, horrifyingly inverted: the map had come first. Then dread, arriving underneath the wonder like cold water under warm, the way a lake turns over in autumn. Because if the prophecy was true—if any part of it was true—then Jonah's careful, erasing, invisible life was not a choice. It was a countdown. And Wren had fallen in love with someone standing inside a fuse.

And there was a further thought, the worst one, which Wren tried to set down and could not: *what was sold below shall rise above.* Sold. The monk's notes said sold. Ask the ones who sold him. Somewhere in this kingdom's polite, commemorated, cathedral-grateful history was a sale—a person, sold, beneath a mountain—and the winter everyone thanked the gods for was, perhaps, not protection at all but *payment*, still running, still falling, a hundred years of snow like a debt accruing interest. And tomorrow, by ink and by date, it became Jonah's problem. Or Jonah became its.

Wren blew out the lamp, then relit it, because the dark was suddenly intolerable. They gathered the book, the copies, the tracing. Their movements had gone quick and precise, smuggler-smooth, and they recognized with distant amusement where they'd learned to move like that: from Jonah, absorbed across a hundred stolen evenings.

They signed the archive ledger. *Purpose of consultation: commemorative commission.* The lie looked small and gray on the page. They hesitated, then added nothing else. Some entries you didn't make until you understood them.

Three floors up, the palace slept its perfumed sleep. Through the high slit windows, Wren could see the mountains eastward, black against a sky already sifting down the season's first serious snow—early, the guards had grumbled at supper, earliest in years. Earliest in memory, someone had said. Wren looked at the falling flakes, unhurried, endless, patient as arithmetic, and thought: *before the long winter finishes falling.*

The snow wasn't beginning tonight.

By one measure, it had begun a hundred years ago, and had never once stopped.

* * *

The boathouse, when Wren reached it near midnight, smelled of cold water and tallow and, faintly, of the rosemary oil Jonah rubbed into their forearms after long days of tasting—some counter-habit, some ritual of washing the palate clean. The candle was already lit. Jonah was already there, sitting on the overturned skiff with their elbows on their knees, and when they looked up, everything about them was the familiar architecture of evening: the tired warmth, the guarded ease, the crooked smile arriving late like a guest who'd circled the block first.

"You've got ink on your cheekbone," Jonah said. "Left side. You've been at the archive all night."

"I found something."

Jonah went very still. It was the smallest change—a settling, a lowering of weight onto the balls of the feet—and Wren had never once thought of it as significant, and now, with a dead monk's drawing folded against their heart, they thought: *that is the stance of a person deciding whether to run.*

"What kind of something?" Jonah asked lightly. Too lightly. The wry corner of the mouth was working.

Wren sat down beside them on the skiff's hull. Close. Shoulder to shoulder, the way they always sat, and for a moment they simply breathed together in the dark that smelled of reeds and winter coming on.

"A face," Wren said. "In a hundred-year-old book. Drawn beside tomorrow's date."

Jonah did not laugh. That was the thing Wren would carry out of that night, the thing that turned dread into certainty: Jonah did not laugh, did not tease, did not say *you and your musty books*. Jonah looked at the candle flame for a long moment, and then at the black water, and said, in a voice stripped of all its cleverness:

"Tell me everything. Leave nothing out."

And Wren, unfolding the tracing with hands that finally shook, understood that the person beside them had been waiting for this conversation their entire invisible life.
