## 3. The Thornfell Roads

The mountain did not want to be known. That was the first thing I ever learned about it, back when I was small enough to fit inside a flour sack and my mother carried me over her shoulder along a track so narrow the scree on either side whispered constantly, like the Thornfells murmuring in their sleep.

I had never told anyone that story before. Not the whole of it. But there was something about the way Wren walked—pencil already out, sketching the treeline as we climbed, as if the mountain might change its mind about its own shape if they didn't write it down—that made confession feel less like risk and more like trade. You give me your map-hands, I'll give you my goat-tracks. Fair dealing. The only kind I trusted.

"Stop here," I said, and put a hand flat against the rock.

Wren stopped. They were good at stopping, which sounds like nothing until you've tried to teach someone to be quiet in country where sound travels like water downhill. Most people fidgeted. Most people breathed wrong. Wren simply went still, the way paper goes still, and waited for me to explain what we were listening to.

"Tell me what you hear," I said.

"Wind." A pause. Their head tilted, that cartographer's tilt, cataloguing. "Water somewhere below us. Birds—no. Not birds."

"Marmots. And under them, the mine road. Listen to how the wind comes off it." The air moved against our faces in slow pulses, warm then cool, warm then cool. "That's not wind. That's breath. There's a shaft down there big enough to swallow a house, and every hour or so the mountain exhales through it. The crown's engineers mapped this slope three times and three times they wrote *inaccessible scree*."

"Because?"

"Because when you know the mountain breathes, you learn to walk between the breaths." I stepped off the ledge onto a shelf of stone no wider than a coffin lid and held out my hand. "Come on. Keep your weight on your heels. The rock trusts heels more than toes."

Wren took my hand. Their palm was ink-stained and dry and warm, and I was aware—as I had been increasingly, inconveniently aware for months now—of exactly how much skin contact that was, and how little of my attention remained on the mountain.

"You enjoy this," they said, stepping across. "Being the one who knows."

"I enjoy being alive. Knowing is how."

"That's not what I asked."

I didn't answer. Below us the valley opened up in layers: the terraced orchards going gold at their edges, the river running thin and quick with the first meltwater of autumn, and beyond it all the grey sprawl of the capital, where Queen Isolde's kitchens kept me employed and anonymous and fed. From up here the palace was just a pale smudge, a tooth in the valley's jaw. It was strange, how small it looked from the smuggler roads. Strange, how much of my life had been spent making myself that size.

"This way," I said, and ducked into the treeline.

* * *

The path I chose wasn't a path. That was the point. To anyone standing ten feet away it was a wall of pine and deadfall and shadow, and you would walk past it your whole life without once suspecting that the wall had a door in it. I'd found this particular door when I was eleven, half-starved, running from a guildmaster who'd decided I was more trouble than a novice was worth. I'd run straight through a juniper thicket expecting scratches and instead fallen into a corridor of stone older than any kingdom, walled by roots that had grown around the passage like ribs.

Wren crouched at the entrance and touched the roots with two fingers, gentle, the way they touched everything they meant to record.

"These are thornwood," they said. "It's supposed to grow only above the snowline."

"It grows where it likes. Ask it sometime." I squeezed past them into the dark. "Mind the third rib. It bites."

The passage ran forty feet and came out on the far side of a ridge that would have taken a full day to climb honestly. On the other side: a hanging valley, green and secret, with a stream threading through it and an old shepherd's hut slumped against the hillside like a drunk leaning on a friend.

"This is—" Wren turned a slow circle, taking it in, and I watched their face do the thing it always did when the world exceeded their maps: a kind of hungry delight, eyes moving fast, hands twitching toward the notebook. "Jonah. This valley isn't *anywhere*. I've surveyed these foothills. There's a blank space on the commission charts right here, maybe two fingers wide, and every surveyor before me just drew hatch-marks and wrote *steep*."

"The guild pays surveyors to write *steep*," I said. "Has for a hundred years. Best security money can buy—a blank space everyone agrees not to look at."

"And you look at it."

"I live in it." I said it lightly, but it came out heavier than I meant. I sat down on the stream bank and pulled off a boot to shake out grit, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "Sit. We've got light for another few hours, and if I'm going to show you the roads, I'm going to show you properly. Not the postcard version."

Wren sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. They didn't open the notebook. That was how I knew they understood this part wasn't for the commission.

"Goods come up from the lowlands at night," I said, nodding at the stream. "Salt, iron, dye, medicine when the crown's tariffs get stupid—which is always. They move through valleys like this one, hut to hut, and each keeper gets paid in goods or silence, whichever they prefer. Nothing travels the same route twice in a season. Nothing is written down." I glanced sideways at them. "Until now. So understand what I'm giving you."

They were quiet a moment. "You're giving me a hundred years of other people's survival."

"I'm giving you *my* survival, actually. The rest is inheritance." The word sat strangely in my mouth. I didn't know why yet. I would find out soon enough, though neither of us knew it then. "People move on these roads too. Deserters. Debtors. Girls who didn't want the marriages bought for them. Once, a whole family of six, fleeing a plague quarantine that was really just the local lord clearing land. I carried the youngest on my back for two days. She bit me the entire time."

"Did she make it?"

"All six. They're farming outside Vell's Crossing now, if they're still alive. The girl would be—what, nineteen?" Something in my chest pulled tight, old and unhealed. "I don't check. That's the rule. You deliver people to the far side of the mountain and you don't check. Checking means hoping, and hoping means names, and names mean the crown can pull them out of you."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. "And you? Do you have a name that could be pulled out of you?"

"Jonah Eriksen," I said, and smiled, because it was true and also nothing. "A taster's name. Worthless. That's the design."

"Eriksen isn't worthless." They said it plainly, no drama, and reached into their satchel—not for the notebook but for a folded scrap of paper, a page torn from some genealogical appendix. "I came across it last month doing provenance work on an old estate survey. Eriksen holdings, upper Thornfell, deeded before the winter pact. The line ends abruptly a century ago. Records after that are—" they hesitated, choosing the word carefully, the way they chose everything, "—scrubbed. Not lost. Scrubbed. Someone went through the archives and cut the name out with a knife."

The stream made its small cold noise. Somewhere above us a marmot whistled twice and went silent.

"My parents' name," I said. My voice sounded fine. I'd had practice sounding fine; it was most of what a food-taster's face was for. "They vanished when I was seven. The guild told me they'd been debt-runners who skipped town. I stopped asking questions around age nine, when asking started costing teeth."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Be useful instead. Tell me why a name gets scrubbed from royal records." I laced my boot back on, slower than necessary. "Names get scrubbed for treason. Names get scrubbed for debts to the crown. And names get scrubbed"—and here I heard my own voice drop, go flat, go careful—"when someone very powerful wants a family forgotten so completely that even the family forgets."

Wren folded the scrap away. "There's more. I didn't bring it today because I wanted to be sure before—"

"Wren." I looked at them. The light was starting to go amber in the hanging valley, catching the edges of their hair, the ink on their fingers, and I felt the day tilt slightly, the way the mountain exhales and everything shifts a half-inch. "Whatever you're sure of, say it here. This valley doesn't exist. Whatever's said in it doesn't either."

They opened their mouth—

—and closed it again, because that was when the dog barked.

One bark, high and sharp, from the ridge above the hidden passage. Then silence, which was worse. Sheepdogs barked in runs. That single note had been a question, and whoever held its leash had answered it themselves.

I was on my feet before I'd decided to stand, boot back off, listening. Wren rose with me, pencil frozen mid-air.

"Crown patrol?" they breathed.

"No. Crown patrols don't use dogs on the scree—they lose them. That's a guild hound." My mouth had gone dry in the specific way it did when I tasted something bitter under a sweetness, the way it had warned me about the night the ambassador's wine was dosed with hemlock. "Nobody knows I take this road. Nobody living."

Another bark, closer. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the crunch of boots on stone—more than one pair, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where they were going.

Who had told them?

I caught Wren's sleeve and pulled us both into the shadow of the shepherd's hut, my heart doing the cold, professional arithmetic it did in kitchens: exits, timing, odds. One exit east through the willow-brake, reachable in ninety seconds if the dogs hadn't been given our scent. Two of us, one injured-slow. And behind us, waiting in the dark of the passage like a held breath, the rest of the mountain—and whatever scrubbed name waited in Wren's satchel, in some knife-cut archive, wearing my face.

"They're not here for salt smugglers," I whispered.

"No," Wren said quietly. "I think they're here because of what I found. Jonah—the book, the date, tomorrow's date, and the face—it's yours. It's *you*, and I should have told you the moment—"

The second bark split the air, close enough now to hear the animal's breath in it, and there was no more time for anything except the road.
