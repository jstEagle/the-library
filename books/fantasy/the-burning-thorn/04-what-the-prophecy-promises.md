## 4. What the Prophecy Promises

The archive at Vellamont House kept its own weather. Wren had worked there long enough to know it: the dry cold of the map room, the papery warmth of the reading gallery where the sun came through honey-colored glass, and the deep, breathless stillness of the restricted stacks, where the air itself seemed to have been pressed flat under a thousand years of vellum. Tonight the restricted stacks were theirs alone. The archivist, Master Corvel, had gone home with his cough, leaving Wren the loan of a lamp, a key to the iron gate, and the particular blindness of a man who trusted his apprentices too much.

Wren did not feel like a thief. They felt like a person standing in a room full of sleeping animals, trying not to wake anything.

The book lay open on the reading table where they'd left it an hour ago—*Prognostications and Portents of the Northern Reaches*, compiled by some forgotten hand in the reign of Alaric IV, its spine cracked like old ice. Wren had told themselves all day that there was a rational explanation for what they'd seen. There was always a rational explanation. Faces in old sketches looked like living people the way clouds looked like castles; coincidence was the mortar of every prophecy ever written; and tomorrow's date beside Jonah's face meant nothing more than that some dead scribe had once had business on the ninth of Frostmoon.

But Wren was a cartographer, and cartographers were trained out of coincidence early. A river that bent where no rock bent was not a whim. A coastline that repeated itself across three charts by three different hands was not error. Patterns were testimony. And the sketch on that page—the sharp jaw, the crooked nose broken once and set slightly wrong, the eyes drawn with a level, unimpressed patience that Wren would know anywhere, that Wren had spent whole evenings watching across a shared plate of roasted chestnuts—was not a cloud-castle. It was testimony.

They turned back to the page and made themselves look again.

*The thorn that burns,* the scribe had titled it, in a hand that grew smaller and more cramped as the lines went down, as though the writer were afraid of being overheard even by their own ink.

Wren read it aloud. They didn't decide to; the words simply demanded voice, the way a name demands saying. In the hush of the restricted stacks, their whisper carried like a shout.

*"In the hundredth year of the long winter's keeping, when the crown forgets what it purchased and the seller's line forgets what it sold, a thorn shall grow among the palace stones—unseen, unlooked-for, tasting of every bitterness and dying of none.*

*It shall be known by three signs: a tongue that poison cannot claim, a road known better than its makers knew it, and a scar worn upon the body in the shape of the door it must open.*

*The thorn that burns shall go down beneath the mountain, alone or near-alone, and steal back what was sold before the long winter finishes falling.*

*And if it fails, the winter finishes falling."*

The last line seemed to hang in the air after Wren said it, the way smoke hangs after a candle is pinched out.

They stood very still. Somewhere above them, through floors of stone and timber, the city went on with its evening—cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bell of Vespers at the Chantry of the Thaw, which struck Wren suddenly as the most obscene name for a church imaginable. Here below, in the pressed-flat silence, the words sat on the table between Wren and the lamp like a third person who had come to dinner and refused to leave.

*A tongue that poison cannot claim.* Jonah tasted the queen's food. Every day. Had done for six years. Wren had watched them work once, from the servants' gallery—watched them take a spoonful of everything with that same level, unimpressed patience, swill it against their tongue like a sommelier, and swallow. *Bitter almond in the marzipan, third tray, left side,* they'd reported afterward, casual as a weather note, and two kitchen boys had been dragged out weeping while Jonah asked for more bread.

Wren had thought it was training. Everyone thought it was training. The tasters' guild taught resistance the way smiths taught hammer-work: slowly, painfully, over years. But the prophecy hadn't said *trained*. It had said *tasting of every bitterness and dying of none*. It had said the tongue was a sign, not a skill.

And *a road known better than its makers knew it*—Jonah walked the Thornfell goat-tracks the way other people walked their own hallways in the dark. Wren had seen them do it, two nights ago, guiding them both down a slope of scree in fog so thick Wren couldn't see their own boots, Jonah's hand steady around theirs, saying *loose stone here, mind the third step, the cairn's been moved since autumn* as if reciting a letter they'd memorized in childhood. Those roads predated the kingdom. No one knew who'd cut them. But Jonah knew them the way you know your mother's heartbeat—without having earned it, without being able to explain it.

Wren's hands had gone cold on the edge of the table. They noticed this distantly, the way one notices rain starting.

Which left the third sign. *A scar worn upon the body in the shape of the door it must open.*

Jonah had a scar. Wren knew it intimately—a pale branching mark low on their ribs, forked like a root system, like a river delta, like—

Like nothing. Like a scar. People had scars. Wren drew maps for a living; they knew better than anyone how a shape could mean whatever you needed it to mean. A coastline could be a dragon if you squinted. A birthmark could be a continent. This was the oldest trick in the prophet's book: write three signs vague enough, and the world would obligingly produce someone who fit them. Somewhere in this kingdom tonight there were a dozen poisoned tongues and a hundred scarred bodies and a thousand people who knew the mountains well. The odds did the prophesying. Not fate. Arithmetic.

That was the sensible conclusion. Wren held onto it the way a climber holds a rope.

Then they looked at the date.

Tomorrow. The ninth of Frostmoon. Inked into a book bound before Jonah's grandmother was born, beside a face that was Jonah's face, in a chapter about a thorn that burns going down beneath the mountain.

Arithmetic didn't do that.

* * *

Wren did what Wren always did when the world stopped making sense: they started cross-referencing.

The restricted stacks gave up their neighbors grudgingly. Wren pulled folio after folio—chronicles, court calendars, tax rolls, a water-stained martyrology—and built a little archipelago of evidence on the reading table, island by island, connected by threads of logic the way their maps were connected by roads.

First: the winter. Everyone knew the winter. The Long Winter, the histories called it, though it had never ended—nearly a century now of frost that came earlier each year and left later, harvests thinning, the northern valleys emptying year by year into the capital. The Church called it penance. The Crown called it climate. The schoolmasters called it a cycle and changed the subject. But Wren, pulling the agricultural ledgers, saw the curve plainly: not a cycle. A descent. Each decade colder than the last, smooth as a glacier's profile. Whatever was happening, it wasn't weather behaving badly. It was something running down. Or running *out*.

Second: the date of the prophecy's writing. Wren hunted it through marginalia and colophons until they found it—the compilation was finished in the spring of the year 1123, which meant the prophecy itself, entered as "received tradition," dated from the winter of 1122–23. And the current year was 1223.

One hundred years. To the season. *In the hundredth year of the long winter's keeping.* Whoever wrote this hadn't been guessing at a far horizon. They'd been counting.

Third—and this was the island that sank Wren's rope-hold entirely—a single line in a court calendar from the year 1122, the winter before the prophecy was received. Most of the entries were ordinary: feasts, hunts, ambassadorial visits. But on the page for the month of Deepfrost, one entry had been scraped away. Not faded—*scraped*, deliberately, with a knife, the parchment thinned to translucence, though enough ghost of ink remained that Wren could make out fragments under the lamp, tilting the page to catch the light:

*...the prince... given over... according to the bargain...*

And beneath it, in a different, later hand, small and bitter as a splinter: *Let no record stand. The Crown's memory is mercy.*

Wren sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Their mouth had gone dry as old vellum.

A prince. Given over. According to a bargain. One hundred winters ago—the exact founding of the endless cold.

They thought of the name then without wanting to: *Alaric Vane.* Every child in the kingdom learned the succession the way they learned their letters, and every list of the royal house began with the tragedy: King Aldous Vane and his two sons, the elder lost young, the younger—Alaric—dead of a winter fever at nineteen, poor lamb, the first great grief of the Long Winter. There was a cenotaph in the royal crypt. Wren had sketched it once, for a commission. *Alaric Vane, 1104–1122. Taken by the winter.*

*Taken by the winter.*

Wren read the phrase again in their memory and felt it turn over like a stone showing its underside. Everyone had always read it as *killed by the winter*. But suppose it meant what it said. Suppose it meant *taken*—handed over, given, sold—in exchange for something. In exchange for—

For the winter itself. For protection. For a shield against something worse. The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, fully armed: *what if the cold isn't the punishment? What if the cold is the payment? What if the winter isn't falling on the kingdom—what if it's being kept, like a fire is kept, like a prisoner is kept—and the thing it protects against is still out there, waiting for the fuel to run out?*

And the fuel was a soul. A prince's soul, fed nightly to whatever court kept the bargain, burning down like a candle a hundred years long. And now the candle was guttering. *Before the long winter finishes falling.* Not *before the winter ends*. Before it *finishes falling*—like snow, like night, like an axe.

Wren put both hands flat on the table to stop them from shaking, and found they were standing in a room full of paper that had been lying to an entire kingdom for a hundred years, and that they were possibly the only living person who had just read the truth of it, and that the truth wore Jonah's face.

"No," they said aloud, to the stacks, to the lamp, to the scraped entry. "No, this is—" But the sentence had nowhere to go. Every alternative ending (*this is coincidence, this is fantasy, this is a dead man's poetry*) died on the tongue, because Wren was a professional skeptic of bad geography, and this geography was *good*. Every detail fit every other detail. The dates locked. The curves matched. You could walk this map, and maps you could walk were true.

Reluctance, Wren discovered, was a physical thing. It sat in the chest like a swallowed stone. Because believing this meant believing that the person they loved—quiet, careful, self-erased Jonah, who wanted nothing but their evening bread and Wren's hand in theirs—was named in a century-old doom. That the kingdom's whole frozen order rested on a stolen soul. That *steal back what was sold* was not poetry but an instruction, addressed to Jonah, postmarked a hundred years ago, arriving tomorrow.

And that if Jonah failed—or simply refused—the winter would finish falling. On everyone. On the city, on the valleys, on the Chantry of the Thaw with its obscene sweet name, on the children in it.

Belief came anyway. It came the way dawn comes in winter: grayly, gradually, and then all at once, and there was nothing to be done about it.

* * *

Wren copied the prophecy entire, in their own neat cartographer's hand, and folded the copy into the inner pocket of their coat, over the breast. Then, after a moment's war with themselves, they copied the scraped calendar fragment too, and the ledger curves, and tucked those behind it. Evidence. If this was madness, it would at least be well-documented madness.

The original they hesitated over. Take it? The restricted stacks did not lend. But Master Corvel trusted them, and the key was warm in their pocket, and if the Crown had scraped one entry, what would it do to a whole prophecy that named its founding crime?

In the end Wren left it—slid the folio back into its niche with hands that wanted to do otherwise. Better to know where the truth slept than to have it missed and hunted. They memorized the shelf mark instead: *N-7, third rank, behind the martyrology.* Some things you carried by knowing where they were.

At the iron gate they paused, lamp raised, and looked back down the long aisle of sleeping books. Somewhere in there, a dead scribe who had been afraid of their own ink had pressed a message into vellum like flowers between pages, and it had waited a hundred years for exactly one reader.

"I believe you," Wren whispered, and hated how much they wished they didn't. "God help us both, I believe you."

The gate clanged shut behind them. The sound ran down the stacks and came back changed, and for half a heartbeat Wren could have sworn it came back as words: *before the winter finishes falling.*

Outside, Vellamont's courtyard lay white under the first hard frost of the season, and the sky had the bruised, metallic color that meant snow within days. Real snow. The first true fall of the winter that might, if a taster with a scarred rib and a smuggler's memory did not go down beneath the mountain, be the last one the world ever needed.

Wren pulled their coat tight and walked fast, toward the tanners' quarter, toward the narrow stair, toward the attic room where Jonah would be waiting with bread and chestnuts and the small, ordinary, unbearable peace of their evenings together.

There was no kind way to say any of it. So Wren rehearsed the words on the walk, over and over, like a traveler rehearsing a border crossing:

*Jonah. I found your face in a book of prophecies. It says you have to go beneath the mountain and steal back a dead prince's soul, and it says you have to do it before the winter finishes falling, and I think the winter has been falling for a hundred years, and I think tomorrow is the day it starts to finish.*

They climbed the stair. They stood a moment outside the door, listening to the ordinary sounds within—the scrape of a knife, the small domestic music of Jonah making supper—and understood that they were about to end the only life Jonah had spent six years building: the life of being no one.

Wren knocked.
