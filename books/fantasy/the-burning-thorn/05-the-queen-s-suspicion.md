## 5. The Queen's Suspicion

The summons came at the hour when the palace kitchens went quiet between the noon service and the evening preparations—a dead hour, a held breath, when the only sounds were the settling of copper pots and the slow drip of fat from the roasting spits into their pans.

Jonah was grinding white pepper when the page arrived. They knew before he spoke. Pages sent on ordinary errands ran; this one walked, and his eyes had the particular shine of a boy who has been told to deliver something important and has rehearsed it eleven times in a corridor.

"Taster Eriksen." He said the name carefully, as if checking its weight. "Her Majesty requests your presence in the Amber Gallery. At once."

"Requests," Jonah repeated, setting down the pestle. Their hands were steady. They had trained them to be steady through worse than this—through nightshade honey that stopped the heart for six seconds, through the gray numbness of arsenic taken in measured sips over months so the body would learn to recognize it like an old acquaintance. Steadiness was not courage. It was habit laid over fear like mortar over brick.

"At once," the page confirmed, and then, because he was young and could not help himself: "She doesn't usually— I mean, she sends for you every morning, but never *here*, never in the—"

"I know where the Amber Gallery is," Jonah said, and smiled the small, forgettable smile they kept for servants and guardsmen, the one that made people look away satisfied that they had seen nothing worth remembering.

Inside, something cold turned over.

* * *

They walked the long gallery corridor slowly, because hurrying would mean something, and everything about Jonah was calibrated to mean nothing. The palace of Vanehold in late autumn was a place of amber light and long shadows; the windows on the western side caught the low sun and threw it across the floor in panes of honey-colored gold, so that walking the corridor was like wading through still water. Outside those windows, the Thornfell mountains stood against a sky the color of unpolished pewter, their peaks already wearing the first serious snow of the season. The long winter, the court called it, with affectionate weariness—the endless cold that had shielded the kingdom for a hundred years, that no neighboring army had ever crossed, that fell and fell and never quite became spring.

Jonah had tasted for Queen Isolde for four years now. Before that, three years tasting for her father. Seven years of standing slightly behind and to the left of power, silver spoon in hand, swallowing small deaths so that others would not have to. It was good work for someone who wished to be no one. A taster is furniture that eats. A taster is noticed exactly as much as a chair is noticed, and forgotten faster.

But the summons to the Amber Gallery was not how one summoned furniture.

Two guards flanked the gallery doors, and Jonah knew both by name and by habit—Corvan, who cheated at dice and always lost anyway; Pell, who hummed when he thought no one listened. Knowing guards was part of being invisible. You learned their names, their mothers' illnesses, the debts they owed, and then you were not a person to them but a fixture, a kindness, a thing that had always been there.

"Go on in," Corvan said, and there was something new in his face—not suspicion, but the careful blankness of a man who has been instructed to feel nothing. That was worse. Jonah catalogued it, filed it away in the ledger behind their eyes where they kept everything: Corvan has been spoken to. By whom? About what?

The doors opened onto warmth and light and the smell of beeswax and dried roses, and Queen Isolde of Vanehold, alone.

* * *

She was not a large woman, but rooms arranged themselves around her the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone. She stood at the far end of the gallery beside a tall window, her back half-turned, watching the mountains. She wore mourning grey—not for anyone in particular; Isolde wore grey the way other monarchs wore ermine, a permanent statement that grief and governance were the same labor—and her hair was bound up under a circlet of black iron set with river pearls. On a side table near her hand sat a single cup of wine, untouched, and beside the cup, a book.

Not a large book. Old. Its cover was the color of tanned skin gone leathery with age, and Jonah's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop before their mind caught up and reminded them that hundreds of old books existed in the world, and most of them had nothing to do with anything.

"Taster Eriksen." The queen did not turn. Her voice was low and even, a voice used to being heard the first time. "Come and look at the mountains. I find I cannot look at them alone anymore. They have begun to seem like a debt."

Jonah crossed the gallery, footsteps silent out of long practice, and stopped at a respectful distance from the window. The Thornfells filled the glass: white shoulders, dark throats of pine, the high passes already closing. Somewhere beyond those peaks, Wren was in the archive district with ink on their fingers and tomorrow's date written somewhere it should not have been. Jonah had not told them to burn the page. They had not told them anything except *be careful*, which is what you say when you are too afraid to say what you mean.

"A debt, Your Majesty?"

"Every kingdom owes something to its mountains." Isolde turned at last. Her face was fifty years old and carved for it—fine lines around the mouth, eyes the pale grey of ice over deep water. "Some pay in grain. Some in soldiers. We have paid in cold, and been glad of it." She studied Jonah then, openly, without the polite deflection of court looking. It was like being weighed on a merchant's scale. "You have served my house seven years. Four for me. In all that time you have never once been ill. Never once faltered. Do you know what my physicians say of you?"

"That I have a fortunate constitution, Majesty."

"They say you are a marvel." A thin smile, without much warmth in it. "I say you are careful. There is a difference. A fortunate constitution is luck. Careful is a craft. And craft, Taster Eriksen, always has a teacher. Craft has a *lineage*."

The word landed in the room and stayed there, turning slowly, like a knife left balanced on its point.

"You keep no family name in the household rolls," the queen continued, moving along the window, her reflection ghosting across the glass beside the mountains. "When I asked my chamberlain, he said you had given none. When I asked the guild registry—" and here Jonah's heartbeat did something complicated and quiet, "—he said the Smugglers' Guild certified you, which is itself curious, since the Guild certifies routes and cargo, not persons. And when I asked the heralds' office for the name *Eriksen*—"

She let the silence do its work. Jonah watched the snowfields and kept their face pleasant and empty, the face of a woman listening to a story that did not concern her.

"—the heralds' office returned a most interesting answer. They returned nothing. Not *unknown*. Not *foreign*. Nothing. As though the name had been removed. And names, Taster, are not removed from the rolls of Vanehold by accident or by time. Names are removed by decree. And decrees leave records." She turned fully toward them. "Except this one does not. Someone burned the record so thoroughly that even the burning was erased. That takes either great power or great fear. Often both."

"Majesty," Jonah said, and heard their own voice come out mild and slightly puzzled, pitched just below interest, "my father was a carter. My mother kept goats above the valley towns. They died when I was nine. If there was ever a name worth recording, no one troubled to tell me of it. I took the Guild's name because the Guild fed me. I am what I appear to be: a woman with a strong stomach and no history."

It was a good speech. Every word of it true, which is what made it good. Lies are fragile; truths arranged in the wrong order can hold a door shut for years.

Isolde looked at them for a long moment. Then she gestured to the table, to the untouched cup of wine. "Taste."

Jonah crossed to it. This was familiar ground, holy ground almost—the one place where they were not furniture but function, where their body's terrible education became useful. They lifted the cup, breathed over the wine the way they'd been taught by a dead smuggler named Hesk who had learned it from someone older: first the nose, then the wrist-warmth of the bowl, then a sip held on the tongue like a secret.

Iron and cherry and the faint green bitterness of something that did not belong.

Their pulse did not change. That was the discipline of it—you did not let the pulse change, because men watched your throat when you tasted, and a jumping vein was a confession. Jonah swallowed, set the cup down, and waited three breaths, the ritual count, before speaking.

"Hemlock extract, Majesty. Prepared in oil rather than spirit, which slows it. Whoever brewed it wanted the death to come hours later, well away from the pourer. The vintage is a southern red, the Marches bottling, four or five years old." A pause. "It is a competent poison. Not an inspired one."

Something flickered across the queen's face—satisfaction, or its close cousin. "And you can drink the rest without harm."

"With harm, Majesty. But without dying. There is a difference, and my stomach knows it intimately."

"Drink it, then."

Jonah drank. The hemlock unfolded in them slowly, a greyness climbing the arms, a soft ringing in the ears like a bell heard through water—but their body knew this guest, had entertained it before, and the antidotal bitters they chewed each morning out of habit did their quiet work. They set the empty cup down with a click that sounded very loud in the gallery.

"The poison was not the test," Isolde said. "Or not only. I needed to see your hands." She nodded at them. "Steady. Even now, with enough hemlock in you to stop a horse. Either you are the calmest creature God ever made, Taster Eriksen, or you have been afraid before, often, and have made a home there." She moved away from the window, past Jonah, toward the old book on the table, and her hand came to rest upon its cover lightly, the way one touches a sleeping animal one does not entirely trust. "Someone at my court is trying to poison me. That is the plain fact of the season. Three attempts since midsummer. You caught the last two. And now, in the same weeks, someone begins asking questions about a name that was erased before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath. Coincidence wearies me. I no longer believe in it."

The fire popped in the grate. Out the window, the light was failing; the mountains had gone from gold to violet to a darkness with teeth.

"What name, Majesty?" Jonah asked, and hated how carefully casual it came out, and prayed the hemlock's grey haze would excuse any flaw in the performance.

"You know perfectly well what name." Isolde did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "*Eriksen.*"

* * *

Later—in the years after, in the life that came later—Jonah would remember this next part with terrible clarity: the way the firelight moved on the queen's rings, the smell of the dried roses, the exact weight of the silence before she spoke again. Memory keeps what it wants. But in the moment, everything narrowed to a point, and the point was survival.

"The name appears once in the historical record," Isolde said, "in a fragment of the privy ledger from the reign of Alaric the Fifth. One line. *Payment rendered to the House of Eriksen, brokers, per agreement concluded beneath the Thorn.* And then nothing. No House of Eriksen in the peerage. None in the guild rolls. None anywhere, as if the line had been scoured from the kingdom like a stain." Her pale eyes fixed on Jonah. "And yet here you stand, wearing it like a borrowed coat, telling me your father was a carter."

"My father was a carter," Jonah said. "Whatever the name meant once, it didn't mean it by the time it reached him. Names wash downhill, Majesty. They arrive at the bottom worn smooth."

"And yet." The queen lifted the old book from the table—held it, did not offer it—and Jonah saw now that a ribbon marked a page near the middle. "Three days ago, a scholar of my archive brought it to my attention that certain pages of the prophetic collections have been consulted recently. Consulted, and copied. By a cartographer. Wren Ashvale." The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Jonah heard it fall all the way down. "Ashvale holds a royal commission, so the consultation is not itself a crime. But the pages copied concern the thorn-court beneath the mountain, and the sale of a century past, and a figure the text calls *the thorn that burns*. And the same week, my chamberlain reports that my food-taster—the one with no history, the one whose very name matches a broker erased from record—is seen leaving the archive district after dark, by the garden gate, in company with that same cartographer."

The gallery was very warm and very far away. Jonah stood inside their own body the way one stands inside a house during a flood, watching the water rise over the things they loved.

"Majesty," they began.

"Do not insult us both with denials. I have not survived thirty years of this court by believing convenient stories." Isolde set the book down. "Here is what I know, and what I do not. I know someone poisons at me. I know the name Eriksen was bought and buried a hundred years ago by men who could erase decrees. I know my archive has been read by candlelight by two people who meet in gardens like conspirators in a bad play. What I do not know is whether you are a threat to me, or the only honest thing in a dishonest season. And I am tired, Taster, of not knowing."

"Then ask me plainly, Majesty, and I will answer as plainly as I safely can."

A ghost of respect moved through the queen's expression and was gone. "Safely. Good. At least you don't pretend safety is simple." She folded her hands. "Who are you?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the questions, the one Jonah had spent twenty-six years building walls against—not *what do you know* but *who are you*—and every wall had a crack in it now, and through the cracks came a wind smelling of snow and old paper and Wren's ink-stained fingers laced through theirs in the dark of the garden.

"I am the woman who tastes your food," Jonah said quietly. "That is the whole of what I can swear to tonight. But I will tell you this much more, because you deserve a truth after so many evasions: whatever the House of Eriksen was, I did not choose it. And whatever is coming off those mountains—" they nodded at the window, at the darkening peaks, at the snow that had fallen without ceasing for a hundred years, "—it is coming whether I stand in front of it or not."

For a long moment the queen said nothing. The fire settled. Somewhere below, the kitchens were waking for the evening service, and the smell of roasting meat climbed the stairwells of the palace like a tide.

"You may go," Isolde said at last. "Continue your duties. Taste everything I eat as you always have—and Taster Eriksen: henceforth, taste everything *you* eat as well. Consider it professional courtesy." Her mouth curved, without humor. "And send the cartographer to me. Tomorrow, at this hour. A royal commission carries obligations, and I find I wish to discuss maps."

Jonah bowed—the full court bow, deeper than a taster had any reason to bow—and walked backward three steps before turning, because some grammars of power must be spoken fluently or not at all. At the doors, the queen's voice followed them, soft as ash falling:

"Oh, and one thing more. The scholar who brought me the prophecy book. Serah Dole. You will encounter her, I imagine. She has... questions of her own. Do try to be civil. She lost someone to the crown's forgetting, years ago, and it made her thorough."

The doors closed behind Jonah with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

They did not go back to the kitchens. They walked, instead, the long way—down the service stairs, through the warm labyrinth of corridors that smelled of bread and tallow, out through the herb garden where the rosemary had gone woody with frost, to the garden gate, to the wall, to the spot where the city's rooftops fell away toward the archive district and the single lit window of the map-room where Wren worked.

The hemlock was still in their blood, a grey silkiness at the edges of everything, and perhaps that is why the fear felt beautiful instead of merely terrible: the mountains black against the last violet light, the first flakes of the evening's snow starting down, slow and enormous, each one visible for a moment before it vanished into the dark.

Tomorrow, Wren would walk into the Amber Gallery and sit across from a queen who had just looked through Jonah as though they were glass. Tomorrow, Serah Dole—*she lost someone to the crown's forgetting*—would begin asking her thorough questions. And somewhere in the old ledgers, a line waited like a snare beneath leaves: *payment rendered to the House of Eriksen, brokers.*

Brokers. Not victims. Not servants. Brokers.

Jonah pressed their forehead against the cold stone of the garden wall and breathed, and the snow came down on their shoulders and did not melt, and far below, the lamp in the map-room went out—which meant Wren was coming, which meant Jonah had perhaps twenty minutes to decide what to tell them, and what to hide, and whether love could survive being a liability.

They pushed off the wall and started down the stairs, taking them two at a time, the poison singing softly in their veins, the first crack running clean through the armor of their anonymity like frost through a windowpane.

Behind them, in the Amber Gallery, Queen Isolde opened the old prophecy book to the ribboned page and read, once more, the words she had not shown her taster:

*The thorn that burns shall go down alone, and the mountain shall give up what was sold.*

Alone.

She closed the book, and smiled at the mountains, and rang for her chamberlain.
