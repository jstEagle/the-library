## 6. Alaric Vane, Sold

The archive did not want to give up its dead.

Wren felt that from the moment they descended the spiral stair into the undercroft of the Royal Cartographic Society, where the oldest holdings were kept—not maps at all, in the deepest shelves, but the documents maps were made *from*: surveyor's oaths, border treaties, land grants sealed in wax the color of dried blood. The air down there was cold in a way that had nothing to do with season. It was the cold of paper that had not been touched in a hundred years, holding its breath.

They had come for a commission. That was the story Wren told themselves, and it was even mostly true: the Society wanted a commemorative plate of the Vane Centenary—the hundredth winter since the reign of Alaric Vane began, though no one at court called it a reign anymore, and Wren had never thought to ask why. A decorative map of the kingdom wreathed in stylized frost. An hour's work, maybe two. Ink and gilt and forget it.

But Jonah's face was still inked behind Wren's eyes, sketched in a dead prophet's careful hand beside tomorrow's date, and Wren could not decorate frost while their lover slept upstairs wearing a prophecy like a noose. So instead of pulling the centenary plates, they pulled everything the archive had on the year the long winter began.

* * *

The first thing they learned was that the archives lied by omission.

There should have been a mountain of record. A century ago, the kingdom had been wealthy enough to employ forty cartographers and vain enough to commission portraits of its own weather. Every harvest festival, every royal birth, every bridge raised over every river—all of it documented, indexed, cross-referenced in the beautiful crabbed hand of clerks who had nothing better to do than be thorough.

And then, in the autumn one hundred years past, the record simply—stopped.

Not gradually. Not fading the way records fade when kingdoms decline. It stopped like a heart stops: mid-sentence. Wren found a shipping ledger whose final entry was dated the third of Frostmonth, listing barrels of salt herring bound for the capital, and beneath it, in the same ink, the same hand, three words and a smear:

*Held for inspection—*

And nothing. No inspection recorded. No further entries. The next document in the drawer was dated four months later, midwinter, and it was a proclamation of thanksgiving, thanking the gods—for what, it did not say—for delivering the realm through its "season of trial."

Season of trial. As if winter were an illness you caught. As if it had not arrived all at once, in a single night, and refused, ever after, to leave.

Wren sat back on the little stool and looked at the lamp flame and made themselves think it plainly, because thinking it plainly was the only way to test whether they had gone mad: *the winter began on a specific night, one hundred years ago, and something happened that night that the entire apparatus of the crown conspired never to write down.*

Kingdoms did not conspire. Kingdoms were too clumsy, too full of grudge and gossip. But crowns conspired beautifully. Crowns had done it before.

They reached for the next box.

* * *

It was near midnight when they found the name, and they found it the way one finds most buried things: by accident, in the margin of something else.

A household inventory, the personal effects of Queen Sable Vane—Alaric's mother, though the inventory only said *Her Majesty's effects, second chamber*—compiled by her steward in the years after the winter began. Most of it was ordinary: gowns, jewels, a lute with a cracked soundboard. But item forty-one stopped Wren's pen where it moved across the page.

*Item 41. One portrait, small, of His Highness Prince Alaric, aged nineteen years. Her Majesty requests this be kept facing the wall.*

Facing the wall.

Wren read it five times. Somewhere overhead, boots crossed the archive's upper floor—a night clerk making rounds—and the sound seemed to come from another world, a world where portraits hung face-out and winters ended and names meant what they said.

Because here was the thing Wren had noticed without noticing, the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've known since childhood: everyone knew there had been a King Aldous Vane, and a Queen Sable, and a daughter, Princess Maren, who had married into the southern courts and left descendants that half the nobility claimed. The Vane line was embroidered into every tapestry of ancestry in the palace.

But Alaric. Ask anyone about Alaric Vane and they would say, *the winter king*, meaning the era, meaning the frost itself, meaning nothing. There was no portrait of him in the great gallery. No statue in the Fountain Court. No feast day, no founding, no bridge named for him. A prince of the blood, erased so thoroughly that his own name had become just another word for cold.

Wren pulled the inventory closer and read item forty-one again, and understood, with a slow cold spreading through their chest that had nothing to do with the undercroft, that Queen Sable had not been able to look at her son's face either. Either because she had sold him. Or because she had failed to stop the selling. And she had not known which grief was worse, so she had turned his face to the wall and let a hundred years do the rest.

* * *

The proof, when it finally came, came in a courier's hand.

That was the detail that would keep Wren awake for the rest of their life, they suspected: not a treaty, not a decree, but a *receipt*. Tucked inside a folder of mundane correspondence from the winter of the Centenary's beginning—a folder labeled, with bureaucratic tenderness, *Miscellaneous, Frostmonth–Thawmonth*—there was a single sheet of good rag paper, folded twice, unsealed.

Unsealed. Because whoever wrote it had never expected it to survive. Or because sealing it would have made it real, and the whole point of the thing was that it must never quite be real.

The hand was hurried, elegant, slanting—the hand of someone educated past the need for care. Wren recognized the letterforms from other documents of the period. They had spent enough of the last six hours drowning in a dead century to know its handwriting the way you know a neighbor's walk.

*To the bearer,* it began, *and to the office, not the man, for men die and offices endure—*

*Let it be set down once, in a place where no eye will seek it, what was given and what was taken beneath the Thorn on the night of the first frost.*

*The court below asked a price for their shelter. We offered gold; they do not use it. We offered land; they are beneath all land. What they asked for was a soul, freely yielded, to anchor the pact as a keel anchors a boat. Without it the bargain drifts and breaks, and we are told the breaking would be heard from the peaks to the sea.*

*His Highness Prince Alaric went down of his own feet, God keep him, having drawn lots among the three of us and lost—or won, I cannot anymore say which. He gave his name into the Regent's keeping and his warmth into the stone, and in exchange the winter was promised to hold at mercy: snow enough to seal our passes against the Emperor's legions, cold enough to freeze any fleet in any harbor, hunger enough in the far fields to keep the peasants praying to us instead of to invaders. One hundred years, they said. One hundred years of winter, and no enemy crosses the Thornfells.*

*We have bought our safety with a boy. Let whoever finds this understand that the bread you eat is baked in his stead, that the peace you sleep in is his sleeplessness, and that when the hundred years run out, the debt comes due again—to whom, and in what coin, I pray I am ash before I learn.*

*Burn this.*

*I did not burn it. I find I could not. Let that stand as my confession also.*

*—M., Steward of the Household*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, moving their lips, because some part of their mind refused to accept the words at reading-speed and insisted on hearing them spoken aloud, the way you'd hear a verdict.

*A soul, freely yielded.*

The lamp guttered. Wren trimmed the wick with fingers that did not feel entirely attached to them, and looked around the undercroft—at the orderly shelves, the labeled drawers, the whole patient machinery of a kingdom's memory—and saw it differently now. Saw the gaps. The missing months. The proclamation of thanksgiving with its hollow center. Item forty-one, facing the wall. A prince dissolved into a synonym for cold.

And above all of it, falling and falling and never stopping, the snow.

* * *

They worked until the lamp oil ran low, and what they assembled in those last hours was not a story so much as a skeleton, each bone found separately and fitted together with shaking hands.

The winter-pact was renewed. That was the second horror, the one that crept up on the first: Maret's-era documents spoke of the sale as a single terrible night, but the correspondence of later decades—coded, oblique, deniable—showed the pattern. Every twenty-five years or so, a delegation went up into the Thornfells and did not file a report. Every twenty-five years, the crown's private accounts showed an expenditure entered only as *alpine maintenance.* Every twenty-five years, some minor noble or inconvenient courtier vanished from the record, and within a season, the court's fashion changed in small strange ways—mourning rings worn openly, then suddenly not; a certain hymn dropped from the winter solstice service; a door in the palace's north wing bricked over, quietly, between one census and the next.

Payments. Installments. The deep court, it seemed, accepted interest on its principal.

And the principal—Alaric himself—was never returned. Never ransomed, never rescued, never once mentioned in a hundred years of state papers except as *the original consideration*, a phrase Wren found in a marginal note in a lawyer's hand and had to put the pen down after reading, because *consideration* was a word for the price of a horse, a house, a barrel of salt herring.

The kingdom's comfort had a line item. The line item was a person. And the comfort was still running, still falling out of the sky in white and beautiful flakes, still sealing the passes against enemies and starving the far fields and killing the poor every thaw-flood, and every soul in the capital warmed their hands at it and called the winter *ours*, called it *character*, called it *what makes us strong*—

Wren pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until they saw stars.

They thought of Jonah. Of course they thought of Jonah; they had been thinking of Jonah all night, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Jonah, who tasted poison for a living and called it a good position. Jonah, whose family name had been scraped out of every record Wren had searched tonight—*Eriksen*, gone, not archived but *excised*, the ink lifted so cleanly that only the ghost of pressure in the paper showed anything had ever been written there. Jonah, whose face waited in a prophet's book beside tomorrow's date, drawn a hundred years ago by someone who had seen this coming.

Someone had seen this coming. Someone a century dead had looked down the long corridor of the future and seen a taster with a ledger of poisons for a body, standing at the mouth of the deep court's road, and had written one word beside the sketch:

*Thorn.*

Wren gathered the documents—not all of them, never all, they were not fools—but the receipt, and the inventory page, and the shipping ledger with its unfinished sentence, and wrapped them in oilcloth against the damp, and carried them out of the undercroft like a person carrying embers in cupped hands.

On the stair, halfway up, they stopped.

Because a thought had arrived that was worse than all the others, worse than the sold prince and the renewed payments and the bricked-over doors. It was simple, and it fit everything, and it would not go away.

If the crown had been paying the deep court in souls for a hundred years—if the machinery of renewal still turned, quiet and well-oiled, somewhere in the palace's north wing—then the crown would very much like to know when the hundred years ran out.

Tomorrow.

The date beneath Jonah's face. The date the prophet had circled twice, pressing hard enough to tear the paper.

The debt was coming due, and the crown already knew it, and somewhere in the palace above Wren's head, someone was deciding who would carry the payment down the mountain.

Wren climbed the rest of the stairs two at a time, into the dark and the falling snow, to find Jonah before the crown did.
