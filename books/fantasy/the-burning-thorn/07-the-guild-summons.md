## 7. The Guild Summons

The summons came the way all Guild business came: folded inside a loaf.

Jonah had been back at their rooms above the tannery for less than an hour, still wearing the palace's smell—beeswax and lilies and, beneath it, in the Queen's presence chamber, something older and colder that Jonah had never been able to name and had learned not to try. They had hung up the grey coat. They had eaten half a heel of bread standing at the window, watching the lamplighters work their way down Tanners' Row with their long poles, small flames blooming one by one like a slow, patient fever through the city.

Then they'd noticed the loaf on the table.

They hadn't bought a loaf. The baker on the corner knew them by sight and by coin and by nothing else—that was the arrangement—and he left their rye on the sill outside, wrapped in cloth, never inside. This loaf sat square in the middle of the scarred table, flour-dusted, warm as if it had been set down minutes ago. Which meant someone had been in the room while Jonah stood at the window with their back turned, close enough to touch, and Jonah had heard nothing.

The old fear rose first, the good fear, the one that kept you alive: *you are not careful enough; you have never been careful enough.* Then it settled, the way floodwater settles, into recognition. Only one kind of visitor entered without sound and left bread behind.

They broke the crust. Inside, folded twice, a slip of grey paper gone soft at the creases from handling. No seal. No signature. Just ink in a hand Jonah knew better than their own reflection—the broad, blunt letters of Magister Corvane, who had taught them to pick a lock blindfolded and to lie with their whole body, and who had held them, once, when they were nine and feverish and crying for parents who were never coming back, and had said, gruffly, into their hair: *You're ours, little thorn. Nobody takes what's ours.*

The note said:

*The Salt Cellar. Tonight. Come alone. Come sober. Come knowing that we have been patient longer than patience deserves.*

Below that, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought, which was how Corvane delivered his cruelest sentences:

*We know about the cartographer.*

* * *

The Salt Cellar sat under a chandlery near the river docks, down a stair that smelled of tallow and brine, behind a door that opened for a knock in the rhythm of a shanty no sailor had sung in forty years. It was called the Salt Cellar because it had been one, once—a low stone vault where herring had been packed in barrels—and it had never been anything else since, except now it packed people instead. Smugglers, mostly. Runners, fences, forgers, the occasional discreet assassin. Children, sometimes, when the Guild found them orphaned or abandoned or sold, children with quick hands and no names worth keeping. The Guild collected them the way other men collected coins: not out of love, but because a hoard was useful, and every piece had its weight.

Jonah had been six years old when two Guild runners found them sleeping in a culvert above the Thornfell passes, three days after their parents went up the mountain and did not come down. Six years old, hollow-eyed, gnawing on pine bark. They could remember almost nothing of before—the culvert was the beginning of themselves, as far as memory cared to reach. Everything after belonged to the Guild. The food, the training, the scars, the languages, the poisons taken in measured doses until Jonah's blood became a fortress no venom could storm. The Guild had made them. The Guild had unmade them, too, quietly, year by year, sanding away the child named Eriksen until only Jonah remained: a function, a tool, a face no one remembered seeing.

They had told themselves for twenty years that this was family.

Walking down the stair tonight, they let themselves feel, for the first time in a long while, how much that word had cost them.

* * *

The vault was lit the way it always was—badly, deliberately, so that faces stayed suggestions and hands stayed visible. A dozen men and women sat along the walls on casks and crates, and every one of them watched Jonah cross the floor, and every one of them was someone Jonah had eaten beside, bled beside, pulled out of a river or a jail cell or a bad marriage. Kess with her cropped white hair and her knife-hand ruined by frostbite. Old Tam, who'd taught them cards and cheating at cards. The twins, Pell and Perrin, identical even in their silence.

Family. The word sat in Jonah's chest like a swallowed stone.

Corvane stood at the far end beneath the one lamp that worked, and he had not bothered to sit. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a door, with a beard gone the color of wet ash and hands that had broken things for sixty years and looked proud of every one. He did not greet Jonah. He waited until they stopped, arms loose at their sides, weight balanced, the way he'd trained them to stand in front of authority—and then he picked up something from the barrel beside him and tossed it.

Jonah caught it without thinking. Their body had been trained too well to do otherwise.

It was a sketch. Charcoal on cheap paper, done fast but done well, because Wren did everything well. It was Jonah's own face—not the careful blankness Jonah wore in public, but the other face, the one that existed only in the archive after closing, in the lamplight, in the hour when Jonah forgot to be no one. Laughing, almost. Softened. Recognizable across a room, across a year, across a kingdom.

Someone had copied it. Someone had carried it here. Someone had laid it on this barrel and waited for Jonah to walk into the dark and catch their own heart thrown back at them like change.

"Where," Corvane said, "did you get a face?"

The vault was quiet enough that Jonah could hear the river moving somewhere beyond the stones, a slow dark breathing.

"It's a drawing," Jonah said. "People draw."

"People draw *kings*. People draw saints and whores and prize pigs." Corvane came forward, boots ringing on stone, and took the paper from Jonah's fingers with a strange gentleness, the way a man handles a thing that might go off. "Nobody draws you, boy. That was the entire point of you. I spent twenty years teaching you how to be forgotten. You were the best investment this Guild ever made. And now—" he held the sketch up to the lamp, and the lamplight showed Jonah's smiling face flickering on the paper like something drowning, "—now there's a copy of your face sitting in the desk of a man who sells information to anyone with coin, including, as of last week, a crown inquisitor named Serah Dole."

The cold that went through Jonah then had nothing to do with the cellar.

"Serah Dole has been asking about you," Corvane said. "Not about the taster. About *you*. About a smuggler who knows the Thornfell goat-tracks better than our own runners. She paid for the name Eriksen."

The name landed in the silent vault like a dropped glass. Jonah heard it break. Around the walls, the watchers shifted—Kess looked away, Tam suddenly found his knuckles fascinating. They knew. They had all known, sitting here in the dark, for however many days, and none of them had sent warning. None of them had climbed the tannery stair and left a different loaf.

"You knew my name," Jonah said slowly. "All this time. You knew what I was called before."

"We knew what everyone is called before." Corvane's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the terrifying thing about him; his anger came down instead, flattening, pressing. "Names are inventory. We keep them locked. We kept yours locked because your parents asked us to, and because a dead name is a safe name, and because—" for one moment something moved behind his eyes, some old sediment stirred up, "—because your mother made me swear it on salt and iron, and I have kept worse oaths than hers for worse reasons."

"My parents are dead."

"Your parents are *gone*, which is not the same animal, and you know it, because you've spent twenty years refusing to know it." He set the sketch down on the barrel. He smoothed it flat with one huge palm, absently, the way he used to smooth Jonah's hair after nightmares, and the gesture was so precisely the same that Jonah's throat closed. "And now the name is walking around the capital in the mouth of a crown inquisitor, and there's a prophecy book involved—I'm told there's a prophecy book involved, Jonah, tell me there isn't a prophecy book involved—"

"There's a prophecy book involved."

Corvane closed his eyes. For a moment he looked every one of his seventy years, and then some. When he opened them again, the tenderness was gone, packed away like contraband, and what remained was the Magister, and the Magister was the Guild, and the Guild was a machine for surviving, and machines do not weep.

"Then hear me," he said. "Hear all of us." He turned his head slightly, and around the vault, one by one, the others nodded—Kess, Tam, the twins, faces Jonah loved in ways they had never examined until this exact moment, when love and ownership finally showed themselves to be different animals after all. "Stop asking questions. Stop going to the archive. Stop seeing the cartographer—or send him away, lose him, whatever you have to do, but end it cleanly and end it tonight. Dole is circling. The Queen's people are circling. Whatever's written about you in that book, whoever sold a prince a hundred years ago, whatever debt your family's name is carrying—it is bigger than us. We are smugglers, Jonah. We move silk and brandy and desperate people under a mountain. We do not fight crowns. We do not fight courts beneath mountains. We survive them. That is all we have ever done, and it is why any of us are alive."

"And if I don't stop?"

The question came out quieter than Jonah intended. Corvane looked at them for a long time. Somewhere overhead, the chandlery's dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"Then we give you to whoever asks next," Corvane said. "That's the law, and it's the law because it's the only reason any of us are still breathing. We don't protect our people past the point of protecting ourselves. Your parents understood that. They broke the law anyway—they went up that mountain to undo what the Eriksens did, and they vanished, and we did not go looking, because looking gets finders killed." His voice roughened, just barely, a burr on smooth wood. "I have regretted it every day for twenty years. I will regret giving you up too. But I will do it. Do you understand me? I am telling you to your face so you can't say you weren't warned by family. We will hand you over, Jonah. To Dole. To the Queen. To whatever asks next with enough silver. Because that is what we are."

He said it like a confession. He said it like a sentence being passed. Both at once, and Jonah understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between ribs, that both were true, and that this was the whole of the Guild in a single breath: *we love you, and you are stock, and stock gets sold.*

"I understand," Jonah said.

"Do you?" Corvane stepped closer. Close enough that Jonah could smell the tobacco and the lamp oil on him, could see the fine webbing of broken veins at his temples, could remember being small and feverish and held. "Because I need you to understand it, boy. Not as a threat. As a gift. The last gift I've got. Walk away from this. Be nobody again. Being nobody is the only safety there ever was, and I taught you how, and you were *good* at it, and I am begging you—" and there it was, the crack in the door, one heartbeat of the man beneath the Magister, "—I am begging you not to make me sell you."

The vault waited. The river breathed beyond the stones. Twenty faces Jonah had trusted with their life watched them decide whether to trust them with anything else.

"No," Jonah said.

It came out steady. Jonah was almost proud of that.

"I can't unknow it, Corvane. There's a prince under the mountain who's been eaten alive for a hundred years while we all got fat and cold and comfortable, and my family put him there, and my parents died trying to take it back, and somebody wrote my face in a book a century before I was born. I can't unknow any of it. You taught me too well—you taught me that a job half-done is the dangerous kind. So no. I won't stop asking questions. And I won't give up Wren." They heard their own voice drop, go flat and final, the voice of a person closing a ledger. "So I suppose you should give me to whoever asks next."

For a moment, nothing. Then Corvane nodded, very slowly, as if Jonah had confirmed a figure in an account he'd already balanced.

"Aye," he said softly. "I suppose I should."

He turned away. It was dismissal, and it was surrender, and it was grief, all wearing the same coat. Jonah walked the length of the vault with every eye on their back, and no one spoke, and no one moved to stop them, and no one—this was the part that would keep them awake for years—no one said goodbye.

At the foot of the stair, Kess caught their sleeve. Just for a second. Her ruined hand, the one the frostbite had curled into a claw, pressed something small and hard into Jonah's palm and closed their fingers over it, and she said nothing, and her eyes said everything the vault couldn't: *run, and I'll deny I ever saw you, and God forgive us both.*

Jonah climbed the stairs into the night and opened their hand on the dockside. A key. Small, brass, worn smooth. They knew it instantly—the waystation at Hollowmere, three days into the Thornfells, the inn that existed on no map because the Guild had erased it from every map. A bolt-hole. An inheritance of a different kind.

Kess had chosen, in the only currency the Guild allowed.

* * *

Jonah did not go home. Home was a room above a tannery where someone had already entered once without being heard, and where a copied sketch of their face might already be crossing the city in Serah Dole's gloved hands. Instead they walked—fast, hooded, taking the runner's routes Corvane had drilled into their legs before they were ten—through the tallow-stink streets, over the canal bridge, into the quarter where the University's libraries leaned against the old city wall like drunkards against a fence.

Wren's window was lit. Of course it was lit. It was always lit; Wren treated sleep as a rumor spread by people with insufficient work.

Jonah stood in the street below for a full minute, doing arithmetic. *If I climb those stairs, I bring the Guild's attention with me. If I bring the Guild's attention, I bring Dole's. If I bring Dole's, I bring the Queen's. Every road from this moment leads somewhere worse, and the only question is who walks it beside me.*

But Corvane had said *end it tonight,* and Jonah had already answered him in front of the whole vault, and there was no version of the future, however short, that Jonah wanted to live in a world where they'd climbed these stairs alone.

They knocked the pattern—three, pause, two—that meant *it's me, open quickly, I may have been followed.*

The lock clicked. The door swung. Wren stood in the doorway with ink to the second knuckle and a smudge of charcoal high on one cheekbone, hair escaping its tie, eyes going from Jonah's face to the empty street and back with the quick professional sweep of someone who had learned, in one terrible week, what it meant to be hunted.

"You're pale," Wren said. "Paler than usual, which for you is genuinely impressive."

"The Guild knows." Jonah stepped inside, and Wren shut the door, and shot the bolt, and leaned against it, and waited, because Wren always waited, always let Jonah arrive at words in their own time. "They know about you. They know about the book. They gave me until tonight to end it, and when I wouldn't, they—" Jonah opened their hand again, looked at the small brass key catching the lamplight. "They gave me a head start instead. Three days, maybe less. Then they sell me to whoever's asking. And someone is asking, Wren. A crown inquisitor. Serah Dole. She bought my name off an information broker today, and my name is Eriksen, and apparently that name is worth killing for."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, instead of fear—which Jonah had braced for, had already begun composing apologies for—something else crossed their face. That look. The one from the archive, the night they'd shown Jonah the sketch of their own face inked beside tomorrow's date: wonder and dread in equal measure, braided tight.

"Eriksen," Wren repeated. They crossed to the desk buried under maps and folios, dug beneath a treatise on riverine surveying, and came up with the prophecy book—the old one, the dangerous one, its vellum soft as mothwing. "I've been reading it all day. There's a marginal note, in a later hand, someone trying to work out the lineage of 'the seller's heir.' It stops mid-sentence. The page after it's been torn out." They looked up. Lamplight moved in their eyes. "Jonah. I think someone tore out the answer a hundred years ago. And I think I can redraw it."

"Redraw it how?"

Wren smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile; it was the smile of a person about to do something brilliant and slightly unforgivable. "The same way I redraw everything. From fragments. Trade ledgers, parish rolls, boundary disputes—names leave shadows, Jonah, even erased ones. Especially erased ones." They were already pulling fresh paper toward them, already sharpening a pencil with a small knife, already, unmistakably, beginning. "Sit down. Tell me everything the Guild said. Every word. And then help me find where your family lived—because if the prophecy points at a gate beneath the Thornfells, then somewhere in a hundred years of dusty records, there's a map of exactly where it stands."

Outside, the wind had turned. Jonah felt it against the window glass, a breath from the mountains, cold and patient, carrying the season's first promise of snow.

Three days. Maybe less.

Jonah sat down beside Wren, and began to talk.
