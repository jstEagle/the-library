## 8. Wren Draws a Map to Nowhere

The archive at night was a different country.

By day it belonged to the crown—clerks with ink-stained cuffs, petitioners waiting on benches for certified copies of deeds and boundaries, the dry rustle of a bureaucracy that believed paper could hold the world still. But after the last lamp in the reading room guttered out and Master Corvane locked the outer doors behind him, muttering about his rheumatism and his supper, the archive became mine. My lantern. My dust. My ten thousand drawers of other people's certainties.

I had been coming back to the prophecy book every night since I found Jonah's face in it. I told myself it was diligence. A cartographer verifies her sources; that was all this was. I would sit beneath the cold green lamp in the restricted alcove with *Oracles and Auguries of the Northern Reaches* open before me, and I would look at that small inked face—the sharp jaw, the watchful eyes, the mouth that went crooked when it lied—and I would tell myself: *coincidence*. Faces repeat across centuries. Prophecy is a mirror smeared with breath; anyone can see themselves in it if they lean close enough.

But I had leaned close. And the sketch had a scar at the collarbone, a thin pale line like a stitch, exactly where Jonah's lived under their shirt, exactly where my thumb had traced it a hundred times without ever asking how it got there.

So instead of sleeping, I worked.

* * *

The commission that had started everything sat forgotten on my desk: a surveyor's request to reconcile three conflicting charts of the Thornfell passes for a new military road. The queen wanted a road through the mountains. The queen always wanted roads—roads meant soldiers, soldiers meant control, control meant the winter taxes came in on time. It should have been a week's tedium, overlaying old maps until they confessed their disagreements.

Instead, the disagreements had opened like cracks in ice.

The first anomaly was small enough that any honest clerk would have smoothed it over. On the oldest chart—a vellum so brittle it crackled when I breathed near it, drawn ninety years ago by a royal surveyor named Aldous Finch—the Thornfell range was drawn with its rivers running *outward*, sensible, down into the valleys. On a chart from forty years later, two of those rivers ran the wrong way. Inward. Into the mountain. Someone had corrected them in a later hand, red ink over black, as though the original had been an error.

Cartographers do not make errors like that. Rivers run downhill. If Finch drew them flowing into the mountain, then either Finch was a fool—and his other work suggested he was anything but—or the water had actually gone there, once, and someone had decided afterward that it shouldn't have.

I laid the charts side by side on the long table and weighted their corners with brass weights, and I began, very carefully, to steal.

That is what mapping the unmapped felt like, in those nights. Theft. Every archive has its silences, and silences are data. There were no surveys of the mountain's interior—of course there weren't—but there were mining claims that stopped abruptly mid-lode, with no notation of collapse or flooding. There were herders' boundary disputes referencing a "low road" through the Thornfells that appeared on no chart at all, only in testimony, described always in the same evasive language: *the way that isn't a way*. There was a coroner's inquest from sixty years past, a smuggler found dead in a snowdrift with frostbitten hands and, according to the examining physician, lungs full of air that smelled of "hot stone and something sweet," though he had been found a full day's walk from any cave entrance.

Hot stone and something sweet. I wrote it on a slip of paper and pinned it above my desk, where the slips were beginning to accumulate like snow against a fence.

* * *

On the fourth night, Corvane caught me.

I heard his keys before I saw him—the archive master moved through his kingdom like a slow tide, and the jingle of his ring announced him from three aisles away. I had just enough time to slide the prophecy book under a folio of grazing rights when his lantern swung around the corner of the stacks and found me standing over a table buried in contradictory charts.

"Late again, Vane." He said my surname the way he said everything, flat as a pressed leaf. Wren Vane. No relation to Alaric, though the coincidence had made clerks smile at me for years, back when anyone smiled. "Corvane doesn't pay overtime, and neither does the crown."

"I'm reconciling the Thornfell commissions." True. Technically true. I had learned from Jonah that the best lies are load-bearing truths with one wall knocked out. "The river discrepancies. Someone's redrawn the hydrology twice in a century."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not suspicion—something older and wearier than suspicion. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing weather he remembers.

"The mountain keeps its own accounts," he said. "Best not to audit them."

"That's not really how maps work, Master Corvane."

"No," he agreed, and set his lantern down on my table, and looked—at the charts, at the pinned slips, at the corner of the prophecy book peeking out from under the grazing folio—for one long moment. Then he took a key from his enormous ring, one I'd never seen him use, brass gone dark with handling, and set it beside the lantern.

"Sub-basement. East wall. Everything filed under 'Hydrological Corrections, 712–present.'" He picked up his lantern and turned away, and paused, his back to me. "You didn't get the key from me. And Vane—" the pause stretched, creaking like old vellum, "—whatever you find down there, draw it true. That's all any of us can do. Draw it true and let the truth do what it likes."

Then he was gone, keys jingling, and I stood in the lamplight holding a key to a room I wasn't supposed to know existed, thinking: *so it isn't only me.*

* * *

The sub-basement east wall was colder than the rest of the archive, cold in a way that had nothing to do with season. The door it opened was iron, and beyond it the shelves held perhaps four hundred documents, each one thinner than it should be, each one wearing the particular shabbiness of things handled often and catalogued reluctantly.

I read until my lantern burned low and I had to fetch oil, and then I read until dawn grayed the high slit of a window, and what I assembled, slip by slip, was a map that had never been drawn because no one had ever been permitted to draw it.

Here is what the fragments said, if you held them to the light at the right angle:

There was a road beneath the Thornfells. Not a tunnel—not merely a tunnel—but a road, maintained, lit, older than the kingdom that now pretended not to know about it. The mining claims hadn't collapsed; they had been *closed*, by order, with compensation paid to the claimants and oaths sworn. The rivers had been diverted at terrible expense in the decade after some event whose name had been scraped off every document like a scar picked at until only the smooth place remained. And the "hydrological corrections"—the phrase Corvane used, the filing category itself—were a century of small bureaucratic lies, each one individually defensible, together amounting to a conspiracy of silence so large it required an entire profession's complicity.

My profession. I sat among the shelves with my cold fingers laced behind my head and understood that I had inherited, along with my pens and my brass weights, a hundred years of looking slightly away.

And underneath the silence, visible now that I knew to look for its shape, was the outline of a door.

It appeared eleven times in the fragments, in eleven different disguises. A mine foreman's deposition mentioned "the sealed gallery, the one with the carving." A water-diversion engineer's notes recorded an aquifer that "terminates at the old gate—do not breach, per instruction." A priest's travel journal, misfiled among drainage reports, described a pilgrimage route "to the thorn-door, where the mountain listens," dated eighty years ago, written in a hand shaking with either age or fear. Eleven mentions, eleven angles, and when I triangulated them the way I'd triangulate any unknown feature from known bearings, they converged.

Not on a cave. On a point roughly two-thirds of the way up the Thornfell massif, on its northern face, in a region every map in the kingdom marked with nothing at all—not even the courtesy of blankness, just the mapmaker's convention of hatched lines meaning *here there is nothing worth your time*.

I drew it. That was the whole of my craft, distilled: I drew what the fragments insisted upon, in pencil first, then ink, building the underside of the mountain the way you build any absent thing—from shadows, from testimony, from the shape of the hole it left behind. Rivers plunging inward and going dark. Galleries branching like root systems. The low road winding down and down, past the drowned levels, past the closed mines, to a small chamber drawn at last in careful cross-section, and in that chamber, a door.

I gave it the symbol we use for sealed gates: a rectangle barred with a diagonal. Then, because the fragments kept using a word I couldn't ignore, I added a second mark beside it, unofficial, in the margin where honesty lives: a small thorn, inked freehand, its single barb curved like a question.

* * *

It was nearly morning when I noticed the thing that changed everything, and I almost missed it, because I was crying, a little, from the lamp-oil fumes—or that is what I told myself the wetness on my cheeks was.

I had drawn the gate chamber in cross-section, and cross-sections want detail, so I had given the door its carved lintel based on the priest's description: *"a lintel cut in the likeness of a briar, and upon the door itself a mark like a birthmark, which the brothers say is the mountain's own sign."*

A mark like a birthmark.

My pen stopped.

I thought of Jonah's shoulder. Of course I did; I thought of Jonah's shoulder the way sailors think of harbors. They never spoke of it, the mark—most people wouldn't notice it, a pale whorl below the collarbone, half-hidden, the kind of flaw a body accumulates without explanation. I had kissed it in the dark of the archive loft more times than I could count, tracing it absently, and once, drunk on cheap wine, I had laughed and said it looked like a map of somewhere, and Jonah had gone very still under my mouth, still in that particular way they had, the stillness of a person listening for footsteps, and said, *don't*, and I had let it go, because loving someone means learning which doors stay shut.

A mark like a birthmark. On a door beneath the mountain. On a door positioned, when I checked my bearings a third time and a fourth, directly beneath the hatched nothing-region where every smuggler's rumor placed the Eriksen routes—the family name Jonah dropped like hot coal whenever it surfaced, the name Queen Isolde had asked about, the name erased from every register a century ago.

I pulled the prophecy book out from under the grazing folio with hands that were not quite steady, and turned to the page with tomorrow's date, and looked at the sketched face I had spent four nights telling myself was coincidence, and then at the margin note beneath it in the old prophetic hand, the note I had read a dozen times without letting it mean anything:

*The thorn knows its own door.*

I am a cartographer. I deal in distances, in bearings, in the honest arithmetic of space. And the distance between Jonah's shoulder and that door, measured through every fragment and forgery and scraped-out record I had assembled, was zero. They were the same mark. The mountain wore Jonah's skin on its gate, or Jonah wore the mountain's gate on their skin, and either way the implication landed in my chest like a dropped anchor:

Someone had made Jonah for this. Before Jonah was born, before their parents vanished, before the guild scooped up another orphan—someone had designed a key and then lost track of it, and the key had grown up learning to taste poison and walk unseen, and had fallen in love with a mapmaker, of all the improbable mercies, and now the mapmaker had drawn the lock.

* * *

I finished the map as the sun came up, because finishing was the only prayer I knew.

I drew it true, the way Corvane said. Every river, every closed mine, every scraped-out name left honestly scraped-out, shown as the gap it was. And at the center, the gate, and beside the gate, in my smallest, steadiest hand, I wrote the only dedication I have ever put on a chart: *For J., who walks where I can only draw.* Because that was the truth underneath all the others, the datum from which every bearing in my life was now taken. I could map the whole underworld, but I could not go where Jonah was going. I could only make sure that whatever waited down there, they would not walk into it blind.

I rolled the map, tied it with cord, and sat holding it in the gray light, exhausted and terrified and more certain of my love than I had ever been, because love, I understood finally, was not the kissing in the loft or the stolen evenings. Love was this: sitting alone all night in a cold basement, assembling a weapon out of paper, so that the person you loved might live long enough to be annoyed at you for meddling.

The archive bell rang the hour. Somewhere above me, the palace was waking—servants lighting fires, guards changing shift, and in the royal apartments, I now had no doubt, Serah Dole was also awake, also working, hunting the same scent I'd been following, with warrant and steel where I had only ink.

And below me—below all of us—behind a door marked with a birthmark, something that had been fed for a hundred years turned over in its sleep.

I blew out the lamp, tucked the map inside my coat, and went to find Jonah.

They needed to see what I'd drawn. And they needed to see it before anyone else did—because I had checked the access ledger on my way out, and someone else had signed for the sub-basement key two nights ago, in handwriting I recognized from the inquisitors' office, and the entry had been blotted out with a care that fooled no one who knew how to read gaps.

The hunt had my map's scent now. All that was left was to outrun it.
