## 9. Poison Practice

The vial was no bigger than a sparrow's egg, and it had cost Jonah everything they had left of the guild's goodwill to acquire.

They sat cross-legged on the floor of their rented room above the chandler's shop on Vetch Lane, the kind of lodging that asked no questions and remembered no faces, and turned the glass slowly in the candlelight. Inside, the venom moved like something alive—because it was. *Widow's milk*, the brokers called it, though its proper name was *lachryma serpentina*: the tears of the white-throated asp that lived only in the deep caves beneath the Thornfells, where nothing else grew and everything that survived there learned to be cruel. The tears were not tears at all but a secretion gathered from the glands of hibernating females, milky-pale, faintly luminous, and lethal in quantities smaller than a grain of rice.

Jonah knew this because Jonah had spent eleven years learning everything that could kill a person, and then teaching their body to disagree.

The ledger of their blood was long. Foxglove at fourteen, when Master Corvane had held their head over a basin while their heart stuttered like a moth against glass. Hemlock at fifteen, slow and green-tasting, three days of watching the ceiling breathe. Aconite at sixteen—the worst one, the queen-maker, the plant they called wolfsbane because it killed wolves and queens with equal indifference—and Jonah had lain rigid for two days while their own nerves screamed in languages they hadn't known the body spoke. Arsenic, belladonna, ergot, the gray deathcap that grew in the palace cellars, seven varieties of snake venom from four kingdoms, and once, memorably, a dish of lampreys that someone had packed in lead salt out of spite or stupidity or both.

Each dose had been a small war. Each victory had been written into them permanently, invisibly: the way their pupils now contracted too fast in bright light, the fine tremor in the left hand that only appeared when they were very tired, the scar on the inside of the elbow shaped like a comma where a taster before them had died and Jonah had been given the antidote a breath too late for comfort but early enough for survival. Their body was a ledger, yes. But ledgers were meant to be read by other people. This one Jonah was writing for themselves alone.

Because tomorrow—or soon, soon enough—there would be no gentle dishes from the palace kitchens. There would be whatever the court beneath the mountain chose to offer, and Jonah did not imagine the Pale Regent kept a food-taster on staff. The deep court fed on souls; what would it serve at table?

*You're going down there,* Wren had said, not as a question, three nights ago, their ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the prophecy book as if they could hold the pages shut through sheer stubbornness. *You're actually going to walk into the dark and steal a soul out of a king's reliquary because a book told you to.*

*Because a book drew my face,* Jonah had corrected. And because the alternative—letting the crown send some other expendable body, letting Serah Dole's kind turn the theft into an execution, letting Alaric Vane spend another century being eaten bite by bite in the cold—was not an alternative at all. It was just the old habit wearing a new coat. The habit of being no one. The habit of disappearing so thoroughly that even fate lost track of you.

Well. Fate had found them. It was time to stop pretending otherwise, and time to start preparing for a journey no smuggler's map could chart.

Hence the sparrow's-egg vial.

* * *

The preparation took two hours, and Jonah performed every step of it the way they'd been taught at thirteen, kneeling on cold flagstones in Master Corvane's cellar while the old man recited the catechism of the trade: *A taster who fears the poison dies of fear. A taster who trusts the poison dies of trust. Measure everything. Trust nothing. Keep your wits about you like a knife in your teeth.*

First: the emetic, mixed weak, drunk warm. If the body was going to reject the venom, better it happen early and controlled than later and catastrophic. Jonah drank it standing at the window, watching the lamplighters work their way down Vetch Lane, small flames blooming one after another like a slow thought.

Second: the base. Milk thickened with ground chalk and honey, to line the stomach, to give the venom something to cling to besides living tissue. Old trick. Older than the guild. Jonah drank half and set the rest aside.

Third: the countermeasures laid out in a row on the floorboards, each labeled in their own cramped hand. The antivenin serum bought at ruinous price from a herbalist in Lowmarket who owed the guild a favor and hated owing it. A tourniquet belt, buckled loose around the left arm where the veins ran close. A basin. A bucket. A knife—not for the vein, not yet, but in case the convulsions came and Jonah needed something to bite down on that wasn't their tongue.

And fourth, last, most important: the bell rope, run through the window frame and knotted to the bedpost, its end hanging within reach of where Jonah would fall. Not to call help. There was no help to call. Calling help meant questions, and questions meant Wren, and Wren knowing about this night would either stop it or break him, and Jonah could not afford either thing. The rope was so that if Jonah died here—alone, stupid, in a rented room smelling of tallow and beeswax—they might at least be found before the smell got worse. Some dignity. Even now. Even for no one.

They almost laughed at that. Almost. The laugh came out as a breath, and the breath shook, and Jonah made themselves sit down and stop performing bravery for an audience of furniture.

*You don't have to do this tonight.*

The voice in their head was Wren's voice, because of course it was. Wren, who had spent the last week drawing maps of nowhere with hands that trembled slightly whenever he looked at Jonah, as if he were already memorizing them against loss. Wren, who had kissed them at the door of the archive two nights ago with the desperate thoroughness of a man signing his name to something he couldn't take back. Wren, who would have sat up all night holding the basin if Jonah had asked.

That was exactly why Jonah hadn't asked.

There is a particular loneliness in preparing your body to survive a thing no one else can know about. Jonah had felt it before, in smaller ways—a fever weathered alone in a smuggler's bothy, a broken bone set by their own hands with a stick between their teeth—but never like this. Never with the full weight of intention behind it. Tonight they were not practicing survival. They were forging a weapon, and the weapon was themselves, and the forge-fire was a venom harvested from blind white serpents sleeping under the mountain they would soon descend.

The body as a weapon forged against oneself. Master Corvane would have approved. Master Corvane, wherever his bones lay, had never once said *I'm proud of you*, but he had said, the day Jonah survived the aconite, *Good. Now you're useful.* It was the closest thing to love the guild had ever taught them, and Jonah had carried it like a coin worn smooth from handling.

They unbuckled the tourniquet, tightened it one notch, and picked up the vial.

* * *

The first drop went onto the tongue and tasted of—nothing. That was the horror of widow's milk. Every other poison Jonah had ever taken announced itself: the green bitterness of hemlock, the pepper-burn of aconite, the sweet almond ghost of cyanide that had once nearly ended a visiting ambassador's banquet. Widow's milk tasted of cold water, of clean stone, of the moment before a sneeze. It gave you nothing to fight against except itself.

Jonah counted to sixty. Then a hundred. The candle flame stood perfectly still in the windless room. Down in the street, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a dog began barking at nothing, and Jonah sat very straight on the floorboards with their palms open on their knees like a novice at prayer, waiting for their body to tell them what it thought of the gift.

At a hundred and forty, the cold arrived.

It started in the tongue—that was expected, that was the venom finding the mucosa—and spread backward down the throat like swallowing winter. Not pain. Worse than pain: absence. The tissues simply stopped reporting. Jonah flexed their fingers and watched them move with the strange detachment of watching someone else's hands through glass. The cold reached the chest and settled there, and Jonah's heartbeat, which they had been counting without meaning to, slowed from seventy to sixty to fifty-something, each beat arriving a little later than the last, like footsteps falling further behind on a long road.

*Good,* said the catechism in Corvane's voice. *This is the venom mapping you. Let it map. Learn the map faster than it draws.*

Jonah let it map.

The second phase came at the quarter hour, and it was the phase the herbalist in Lowmarket had warned them about with visible relish: the dreaming. Widow's milk didn't kill cleanly, the way aconite did, stopping the heart like a slammed door. It killed slowly, from the edges inward, and on its way through it opened doors that were better left shut. The deep caves where the white asps slept were old places, older than the kingdom, and the venom carried something of that age in it. Everyone who survived a full dosing reported the same thing afterward, in the fragmented way people report dreams: a sense of descending. Of stairs. Of cold fire burning without heat, and faces that were almost familiar, and a hunger so vast and patient it made the ocean look like a puddle.

Jonah had read those reports in the guild archives years ago and filed them under superstition, the way sensible people file things they'd rather not think about.

Now the room tilted, gently, like a boat, and the candlelight went the color of moonlight on snow, and Jonah felt the first stair under them.

*No,* they told themselves, gripping the floorboards. *No. You are on Vetch Lane. You are on the floor. The floor is real. Count the boards.*

They counted. Fourteen boards across, seven lengthwise, the eighth one warped near the wall where rain had gotten in two winters ago. Real. Solid. But beneath the counting, beneath the catechism, something vast and patient turned over in the dark of the venom's dreaming, and Jonah understood—with the terrible clarity of a mind chemically unmoored—that what they were feeling was not hallucination. It was orientation. The venom came from under the mountain. The mountain's underside was where they were going. Somewhere in the chemistry of lachryma serpentina, the deep court's attention had brushed against theirs, the way a spider feels a thread go tight, and Jonah had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that something far below had noticed a new flavor entering its domain.

*Not yet,* Jonah said aloud, and their voice came out slurred and distant. *Not yet. I'm not yours yet.*

The cold reached the diaphragm.

Later—much later, reconstructing it from the evidence of the room—Jonah would piece together what happened next. The breathing stopped being voluntary somewhere around the twentieth minute. The body simply forgot the instruction, the way a drowning man forgets which way is up, and Jonah's chest went still, and the candle flame stood in its stillness, and the dog outside had stopped barking, and the whole world narrowed to the space between one heartbeat and the next, which was stretching, stretching, becoming a corridor, becoming a road, becoming—

The tourniquet. The belt. Left arm. Veins close to the surface.

Jonah's hands were not their hands anymore; they were tools operating at the end of a very long rope, and the rope was fraying. But the guild trained for this too. *Your last hour belongs to your habits,* Corvane used to say. *Build the habits well enough and they'll carry you when you can't carry yourself.* So the hands moved because they had been drilled to move: buckle off, strap between the teeth, needle of antivenin found by touch among the row of labeled glasses, pressed to the inside of the elbow, plunged, depressed, slow, slow, slow.

Then nothing for a while. Then the return.

It came back the way tide comes back into a tidal pool—all at once and everywhere, agonizing, every nerve reawakening at the same instant and screaming its location. Jonah convulsed, bit through the leather strap, vomited the milk and chalk and honey into the basin, missed twice, and lay afterward on the cold floorboards shaking like a wet dog, weeping without any emotion attached to it, the way bodies weep when they've been wrung out like laundry.

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Through the window, Vetch Lane lay silent under a sky going gray at its eastern edge. Dawn. They had been on the floor most of the night.

Jonah lay still and took inventory, the way they took inventory of every dish they'd ever tasted. Heart: present, laboring, steady. Lungs: burning, working. Hands: trembling, functional. Mind: present. Mostly. Something had been left behind in the descent—some small thing, some trinket of the self, gone the way the venom's toll always went—and Jonah probed the gap carefully and couldn't find its edges, which frightened them more than the convulsions had. You could survive a poison. Surviving the payment was another matter.

But underneath the fear, colder and harder, sat the thing they had come for: the knowledge. Their body knew widow's milk now. Knew its taste, its pace, its dreaming weight. If the deep court served it at table—if the Regent's hospitality came laced with the mountain's own venom—Jonah would not die of the first cup. Maybe not the second. The ledger of their blood had a new entry, written in a hand that shook.

*One more weapon,* they thought, staring at the ceiling. *Forged on myself. As always.*

They laughed then, really laughed, a cracked and ugly sound in the empty room, because the alternative was crying with feeling attached, and they had used up all their allowance of that for the night.

* * *

Three days later, scrubbed, rested, and steadier than they had any right to be, Jonah went to meet Wren at the fountain in Tanner's Square, where the water ran brownish and the pigeons owned the ledges and nobody looked at anybody, which was why they'd chosen it.

Wren was late. Wren was never late. He treated punctuality the way cartographers treated scale—as a matter of professional honor—and the few times he'd been delayed he'd sent word ahead through a chain of apprentices and errand boys that could have delivered a message to the moon. Jonah waited ten minutes. Fifteen. Watched the square empty of market traffic as the afternoon aged, watched the light go long and amber across the cobbles, and felt the new-cold place in their chest where the venom had mapped them begin, quietly, to ache.

Twenty minutes. Then a boy darted out of the crowd—a printer's devil Jonah half recognized from the archive district—and shoved a folded scrap of paper into Jonah's hand and vanished before thanks could catch him.

The paper was archive stock, heavy rag paper, and the handwriting was Wren's, hurried, the letters leaning like trees in wind:

*Something has changed. Someone has been asking about you at the archive—by description, not name. She knew my face before I gave it. Her badge said DOLE. I'm going to lose her in the stacks and come to you the long way. Don't wait at the fountain. Don't go home. Go to the third chimney on Grocer's Row and wait inside until midnight. Burn this.*

*—W*

Jonah read it twice. Around them the square went on with its evening business: pigeons, water, a knife-grinder's wheel singing against a cleaver. Nobody looked at anybody. That was the whole architecture of the city, the whole covenant of anonymity that Jonah had spent a lifetime upholding—and now a woman with a badge had walked into Wren's world of dust and maps and started asking questions by description, which meant she had seen the face, which meant the face was no longer safely buried in a century-old book, which meant—

Which meant the crown had begun to hunt.

Jonah burned the note in the fountain's lee, watching the rag paper curl and blacken and scatter, and pulled their collar up, and became, with the ease of long practice, no one: a figure in a gray coat walking at an unremarkable pace toward Grocer's Row, unmemorable, unremarked, invisible.

For the first time in eleven years, the invisibility felt less like armor than like a shroud. And somewhere behind them, in the labyrinth of the archive, Wren was running.
