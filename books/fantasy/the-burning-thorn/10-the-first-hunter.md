## 10. The First Hunter

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum, which Wren had always thought was what knowledge smelled like when it was dying. They had been there since the third bell of the morning, hunched over the commission for the Duke of Callowmere—a surveyor's map of his eastern holdings, tedious as a sermon—when the light in the long room changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* As though something had stepped between the high windows and the sun, something with edges.

Wren did not look up. That was the first lesson of the archive: you learned to read a room by its shadows before you ever lifted your eyes. The other clerks had gone quiet in that particular way clerks went quiet—not the silence of concentration but the silence of animals deciding whether to run. Quills stopped scratching. Someone's inkwell lid clicked shut with exaggerated care.

The footsteps came anyway. Measured. Unhurried. The boots of someone who had never once needed to hurry because everything in the world would eventually come to them.

"You are the cartographer called Wren."

It was not a question, but Wren answered it anyway, setting down their pen with the deliberate calm they'd practiced for years—calm borrowed from Jonah, who wore calm like armor worn so long it had grown into the skin. "I am, my lord. Or lady. Forgive me—I don't know the convention."

"Serah Dole." A pause that invited correction and offered none. "Inquisitor of the Crown. There is no convention for me."

Wren looked up then, because not looking up had become its own kind of answer.

Serah Dole was not what the stories promised. Inquisitors in the tavern songs were gaunt vultures, all black wool and hungry eyes. Dole was broad-shouldered and plain-faced, forty perhaps, with grey shot through her brown hair and the weathered look of someone who had ridden hard country for years. Her coat was good wool dyed the deep blue of the crown service, cut practically, no braid, no ornament except the small silver thorn pinned at her collar—the sigil of the Queen's inquiry, a joke or a threat, given what else thorns meant these days.

Her eyes were the dangerous part. Not cruel. *Patient.* The eyes of a woman who had learned that people told you everything if you simply waited beside them long enough.

"I'm told you have been consulting the Vellum Codex," she said. "The prophecy book. Three visits this fortnight."

"The Duke of Callowmere's commission required—" Wren began.

"Sit down, cartographer." She pulled out the chair across from Wren's desk herself, turned it, and sat astride it with her arms folded on the backrest, close enough that Wren could smell road dust and horse and, beneath it, something faintly chemical—ink, but not any ink used above ground. "I'm going to tell you a story. You're going to listen. And at the end, you're going to decide how much of a fool you want to be. I've found most people prefer to be fools. It's less work."

Wren's hands, resting on the desk, had gone very still. Stillness was another thing learned from Jonah. *When you can't run, be furniture.*

"I'm listening," they said.

* * *

"Two hundred years ago," Serah said, "the crown established the Office of Inquiry with a single mandate: find anything written, spoken, dreamed, or scrawled on a privy wall that might threaten the succession. Do you know what we found, in two hundred years? Almost nothing. A few pretenders. A few madmen. Prophecy is like weather in this kingdom—all talk, no rain." She tilted her head. "Until last month."

Wren said nothing. Their heart was doing something embarrassing against their ribs.

"A taster in the royal household," Serah continued, conversationally, terribly, "whose name appears nowhere before six years ago. No birth record. No family. A person assembled out of nothing, which is itself interesting, because people are never assembled out of nothing—they're *disassembled* into nothing, usually by professionals. And now that same person's face has been found sketched in a hundred-year-old book, beside tomorrow's date, by a cartographer who also shouldn't exist in any record older than four years." She smiled without warmth. "You see my problem. Two ghosts, holding hands."

"We're not holding hands," Wren said, and immediately hated themselves for it.

"No," Serah agreed pleasantly. "You're worse than that. Holding hands is innocent. What you two are doing is *researching*."

She let that sit. Somewhere across the archive, a clerk turned a page too loudly and flinched at the sound.

"Here is the story I promised you," she went on. "A hundred years ago, Prince Alaric Vane walked into the Thornfells with an escort of twelve and came back as twelve coffins and one empty litter. The official record says avalanche. The official record says a great many things; I've spent eleven years learning where the official record keeps its lies, and they keep them in the margins, cartographer. In the margins of the oldest ledgers, in the queen's grandmother's hand, there is a word repeated every winter when the accounts are balanced: *stipend*. Paid to whom? To what? The winter never breaks. The harvests fail just often enough to keep us grateful. And every year, the crown pays a stipend into the dark and calls it protection."

"You think the prophecy is real," Wren said slowly.

"I think the crown thinks it's real, and the crown has thought so for a hundred years, which amounts to the same thing." Serah's voice dropped, and though the archive was nearly empty, she spoke as though the walls themselves might inform on her. "Queen Isolde does not want the thorn-that-burns stopped, cartographer. She wants it *used*. A weapon pointed down the mountain instead of up at the throne. Someone goes below, steals back whatever was sold, returns it to the crown, and the crown controls the pact forever after. No more stipends. No more dependence. The kingdom holds its own leash."

"And Jonah—"

"—is the thorn," Serah finished. "Or will do until something better comes along. Her Majesty requires the prophecy fulfilled on terms favorable to the throne. Which means your taster goes down into whatever waits under that mountain carrying the crown's authority and none of its protection. Officially, they'll be a traitor the moment they cross the threshold. Unofficially, they'll be bait. Bait, cartographer, is defined by what happens to it."

The word landed in Wren's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They heard it fall and fall and never heard it land.

"And me?" Their voice came out smaller than they wanted. "What am I, in her Majesty's design?"

Serah studied them for a long moment, and something flickered across her plain face—not kindness exactly, but its rough cousin, honesty. "You're the map. Maps get copied. Then the original gets burned." She unfolded herself from the chair and stood, straightening her coat. "I'm telling you this because I was ordered not to, and because eleven years in the margins has taught me that the crown's plans have a way of eating everyone who draws them, including the people who draw them. I liked being an instrument once. Instruments don't have to choose." She set something on the desk between them: a small lead seal, broken, stamped with the thorn. "That's your summons. The queen requests the cartographer's presence at court. Requests. In the language of my office, that means the door will be locked behind you."

Wren stared at the seal. Lead, heavy as a sin. "And if I don't come?"

"Then I come back with fewer polite words and more colleagues." Serah moved toward the door, then paused, her back to them. "One more thing, since you've earned it. The guild sent word to the palace yesterday. About your taster. Someone sold them, cartographer. Someone very close to home." She glanced over her shoulder, and for one unguarded instant she looked tired, old, human. "Hunted together means endangered together. Whatever you two are to each other—decide quickly what that's worth. Everything in this kingdom is negotiable except time."

Then she was gone, and the light came back through the windows ordinary and indifferent, and the archive resumed its scratching, dusty breathing around Wren as though nothing had happened at all.

* * *

They did not go home. Home was the first place anyone would look, and besides, home was full of Jonah's things—Jonah's spare shirt, Jonah's whetstone, Jonah's half-mended cloak—and Wren could not bear to sit among those small evidences while the words *bait* and *burned* rang in their skull like struck bells.

Instead they walked. Out past the tanneries, where the smell drove off casual followers, up the switchback path to the old watchtower ruin on Gullwing Hill, the place where they had first kissed Jonah three summers ago, with map ink still wet on their fingers and Jonah laughing at how badly they'd drawn the hillside they were sitting on.

The city spread below, smoking and murmuring, crowned by the palace where Jonah was, at this hour, tasting the queen's evening meal. Swallowing poison for strangers. Had always swallowed poison for strangers. Wren had found it noble once. Now it looked like practice. Like a body being slowly forged into a tool, and tools got used up.

*Bait,* Serah had said. *Bait is defined by what happens to it.*

Wren sat on the fallen lintel and took out their field kit with shaking hands. Not to draw the city. To draw the mountain.

Thornfell rose in the western distance, white-capped even in autumn, beautiful the way deep water is beautiful. They began sketching it from memory—the southern face, the passes, the goat-tracks Jonah had walked them through in the long golden evenings of the last month, naming each hidden road like a parent naming children. *The Widow's Stair. The Hollow Way. The road that isn't a road unless it's snowing.*

And beneath it all, somewhere in the dark under the roots of the world, a prince who had been sold, and a debt paid annually in winter, and a lover marked in century-old ink to walk down and steal him back.

The queen wanted Jonah to go. The guild had sold Jonah. The prophecy wanted Jonah to go. Every power in the world, above and below, had converged on one unremarkable person whose entire life had been built on never being noticed—and Wren understood, sitting there with cold fingers and a page full of hidden roads, that the only thing keeping Jonah alive was the one thing the crown could not control: that Jonah knew ways down the mountain that appeared on no map but the one being drawn right now, in Wren's own hand, by Wren's own love.

Which made the map the most dangerous object in the kingdom.

They looked at the sketch for a long time. Then, carefully, they tore it out, folded it small, and fed it to the watchtower's old fire-ring, watching the hidden roads curl and blacken and rise as smoke.

Not destroyed. Just kept where only Wren could reach it. Some maps you carried in your blood.

The sun was going down. Jonah would be leaving the palace soon, slipping through the servants' gate, walking the long way to avoid the guild quarter, coming to find Wren because that was what evenings were. And Wren would have to say the words aloud: *she knows. They know. We are already caught, we just haven't felt the teeth yet.*

Below, at the foot of the hill, a figure in a food-taster's drab cloak came up the switchback path, walking fast, head down, moving with that particular economy that Wren had learned meant fear dressed as purpose.

Jonah had heard already. Of course they had. The guild's message would have reached them before it reached the palace.

They met halfway up the path, breath steaming in the cooling air, and Jonah grabbed Wren's wrist—not tenderly, urgently, thumb pressing the pulse point like checking a wound—and said, before hello, before anything:

"We leave tonight. Before the first snow. Pack nothing you'd mourn."

Behind them, far below in the gathering dusk, the palace bells began to ring the evening hour—and beneath their bronze voices, faint but unmistakable, came another sound. Hooves. Many horses. Coming up through the city toward Gullwing Hill.

Someone had followed Wren after all.

Or someone had followed Jonah.

There was no time to learn which.
