## 11. Flight Through the Passes

The first true snow came down out of the north like a debt collector.

Jonah felt it before they saw it—a pressure behind the eyes, a mineral taste on the tongue that had nothing to do with poison and everything to do with the mountain exhaling its long-held breath. They stood in the doorway of Wren's garret with a pack already slung over one shoulder, watching the sky turn the color of pewter, and thought: *three days early.* The passes would close three days early, and Serah Dole knew it too, which meant she was already riding.

"Wren," they said quietly. "It's time."

Wren looked up from the map spread across the drafting table—the map to nowhere, the map that matched the scar on Jonah's ribs—and Jonah watched them understand. Watched the color leave their face in stages, like ink washed from a brush. Watched their hands go still above the paper, fingers splayed, as if they could hold the whole future flat beneath their palms.

"Already?"

"The snow doesn't wait for prophecies." Jonah crossed the room and began gathering what mattered: the archive copies of the Alaric pages, folded small; the vial of mallowroot antidote; a knife with a blade thin enough to slip between armor plates. "We have maybe six hours of light. The Hollow Inn is two days' walk if we take the shepherd roads. Three if we're followed."

"And we'll be followed."

"Oh, certainly." Jonah said it lightly, because terror spoken plainly becomes a stone you must carry forever, but terror dressed as a joke can be set down at the next milestone. "Serah Dole strikes me as the persistent sort. She'll have dogs by morning."

* * *

They left through the tannery district, where the smell of lye and rotting hides covered all other scents and made tracking a misery even for hounds bred to it. Jonah moved through the streets the way water moves through cracks—never hurrying, never hesitating, always appearing to be exactly where a person might reasonably be going. It was the oldest trick they knew: be unremarkable. Be furniture. Be weather.

But Wren walked beside them now, and Wren was not furniture, and every glance Jonah spared them—checking their stride, their breathing, the set of their jaw—felt like a lantern held up in a dark room full of hunters.

"You keep looking at me," Wren murmured as they slipped between two warehouses.

"I'm checking you're still real."

"I'm real." A ghost of a smile. "You're the one who's supposed to be a prophecy."

"Don't say that word on an open street."

They cut south along the drainage channel, waded the shallow stink of it, and came up through a gap in the city wall that Jonah had known since they were fourteen years old—a crack in the foundation stones wide enough for a slim body and a smuggler's pack, hidden behind a curtain of ivy that died back every winter and regrew every spring like something patient and forgiving. Jonah went first, shoulders scraping stone, and emerged into the grey afternoon with the Thornfells rising ahead of them like the backs of sleeping animals.

Behind them, the city bells rang the hour. Ahead, the first flakes came down, fat and slow, melting on Jonah's sleeves.

"There," Wren said softly, pointing back the way they'd come.

On the wall's parapet, small with distance, a figure in crown-grey sat a horse and did not move. Watching. Not following. *Marking.*

"She's letting us run," Wren said.

"She's letting us think she's letting us run." Jonah shouldered the pack higher. "Or she genuinely wants us to lead her somewhere. Either way, the answer's the same. We run, and we run badly, and then we stop running entirely and disappear. Come on. The road I'm going to show you isn't on any map, including yours."

"That hurts my feelings."

"I know. That's why I said it."

* * *

The smuggler roads of the Thornfells were not roads. That was the first lesson, and Jonah gave it to Wren while the light still held: no path worth keeping is ever a path. It is a dry streambed that runs only in August. It is the underside of a rockfall, a corridor of leaning slabs where a person walks bent double with the mountain's weight overhead. It is a sheep-track that exists only in the memory of shepherds dead thirty years, passed mouth to mouth among people who needed it, never written, never worn.

"You could map this," Jonah said, helping Wren up a shelf of wet granite. "That's the thing. You could, and it would be the end of everything the guild owns. Every toll, every secret, every life saved or ruined by knowing the way. One map and the whole economy of hiding comes apart."

"Is that a warning or a request?"

"It's me thinking out loud, which I don't do, so consider yourself honored." Jonah glanced back. Far below, the valley floor was vanishing under white, and the road out of the city—a real road, a crown road—was a pale ribbon with dark specks moving along it. Riders. Four, perhaps five. "Also, we're being paced. See the treeline there? They're keeping level with us on the low road. Someone who knows these mountains is advising them."

"Another guild hand?"

"Guild hands don't work for the crown." Jonah frowned at the specks. "Which means either the guild sold us anyway, or someone else has been reading the same old books we have."

The snow thickened. It came in gusts now, horizontal, needling, and the temperature fell so fast that the wet rock began to glaze. Jonah made a decision they'd been circling for an hour: the high route, the one called the Kestrel's Stair, a climb that would cost them the last of the daylight and most of their strength but would put them above anything a horse could do and into country only a fool or a native would enter in falling snow.

"We go up," they said.

"In *this*?"

"In this, yes. Down there we're rabbits on a hedge-line. Up here we're weather. Even Serah Dole can't follow weather."

* * *

The Kestrel's Stair earned its name twice over: once because kestrels nested in its crags, hovering on the updrafts with terrible patience, and once because climbing it felt like being picked apart by small sharp things. Jonah went first, testing each hold, calling warnings down in a low voice—*loose stone left, ice on the slab, trust the root not the branch*—and Wren followed with a cartographer's stubbornness that surprised no one more than it surprised Wren themselves.

Halfway up, in the blue hour between day and dark, the wind dropped all at once, the way wind does in the high passes, and the world went silent and enormous. Snow drifted down through the stillness like ash from a fire no one had seen. Below them the valley was a white sea. Above them the peaks burned faintly rose-gold, the last light caught in the ice-glaze, and for a moment Jonah forgot to be afraid.

"This is what they never tell you," Wren breathed beside them. "In the archives. In the maps. All those contour lines and elevation figures, and nobody writes down that it's *beautiful*."

"They don't write it down because beauty doesn't help you survive it." But Jonah's voice came out rougher than intended. "Look again in an hour, when your fingers are black, and tell me about beautiful."

"My fingers are already black."

"Then you're learning the mountains properly."

They climbed the last pitch in the dark, by feel and by Jonah's memory, and came over onto a ledge wide enough for two bodies pressed against the rock, sheltered from the wind by an overhang furred with icicles. Jonah got the oilcloth down, the little spirit-stove lit inside a ring of stones they'd built on their first crossing of this route at seventeen years old and rebuilt on every crossing since. The flame was the size of a thumbnail. It was the warmest thing in the world.

They ate hard bread and dried plums and drank snow melted over the stove, and Wren shook so violently for the first quarter hour that Jonah finally pulled them close, back to chest, wrapping both arms around them and sharing heat the way climbers do—not tenderly, at first, but with the blunt pragmatism of survival. Then, gradually, less bluntly. Wren's shivering slowed. Their breathing deepened. Jonah rested their chin on the top of Wren's head and watched their breath rise and vanish and rise again.

"I used to do this alone," Jonah said. "Every winter. Every run. You get so you prefer it. Cold is honest company—it tells you exactly what it thinks of you and asks nothing back."

"And now?"

"Now I'm furious at how much better this is with you in it. Do you understand what that costs me? I spent twenty years building a self that didn't need anything, and you've gone and put a door in it."

Wren turned in their arms, awkward in the cramped space, and found Jonah's face in the stove-light. "I could close it again," they said. "If you asked."

"Don't you dare."

"I wasn't going to. I wanted to hear you say it."

Jonah laughed—a short, startled sound, swallowed quickly by the wind—and kissed them, tasting of smoke and dried plums and the iron tang of altitude. Above them the stars came out between the clouds, hard and cold and numberless, and below them, far down on the crown road, torches moved like a slow procession of fireflies.

"They're camping," Jonah murmured against Wren's mouth. "Horses won't do the night climb. We've bought until dawn."

"Then dawn is when the terrifying part starts."

"No, love. Dawn is when the *fun* part starts. The terrifying part started the day you opened that book."

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless, and with it came the sound.

Jonah woke to it before they understood it—a far-off belling, rhythmic, wrong. Not wolves. Wolves sang. This was the disciplined chorus of hounds on a line, and it was coming from *above* them.

They were on their feet with the knife out before their mind caught up to their body, scanning the crags. The overhang hid them from the slope above, but the sound grew, and grew, and then the first dog came over the rim of the ledge-approach—a lean grey brute with frost in its whiskers—and stopped dead at the sight of them, hackles rising, breath steaming.

Behind it, boots. Two men in hunting leathers, and between them, wrapped in a fur cloak with frost melting on her shoulders, a woman Jonah had hoped never to see outside a courtroom.

Not Serah Dole. Worse, in its way: a guild face. Master Corvane, who had taught Jonah the lock-picks and the ledger-books and the difference between loyalty and ownership.

"Hello, little thorn," Corvane said.

Jonah did not move. Behind them, he heard Wren wake, go still, understand. The stove-flame had died to nothing. The drop below the ledge was four hundred feet of ice-slick rock. The dogs—there were three now, fanning out along the rim with the patience of animals that had done this many times—blocked the only upward path.

"You gave us to the crown," Jonah said.

"I gave you to *whoever pays*," Corvane said mildly. "That's the trade, child. You knew it when you were eight years old and grateful for soup. The queen wants you found. The queen's inquisitor wants you found *alive*, which is fortunate for you, and found *soon*, which is unfortunate, because the passes close tonight and nobody—guild, crown, or god—wants to dig corpses out of the Thornfells in spring." He extended a gloved hand. "Come down the easy way."

Jonah's mind ran the arithmetic of the ledge: three dogs, two men, one cliff, one stove, one coil of rope, one bag of chalk dust, and one body that had been drinking poisons for fifteen years and had learned, among other things, that the sweat of a poisoned man will sicken a hound that bites him.

"Corvane," Jonah said pleasantly, "do you remember the summer you taught me nightshade identification? You said, *the plant doesn't hate you, it just is what it is.*"

"I remember."

"Well." Jonah smiled, and let some of the old guild-cold come up through their face like frost through soil. "I've been thinking about that a lot lately."

Their boot found the stove-stone. Their hand found the chalk bag. And the mountain, which had been holding its breath all morning, finally exhaled.
