## 12. The Hollow Inn

The inn appeared the way a thought appears—suddenly, and as if it had always been there.

Wren had been watching Jonah's back for three hours by then, watching the set of their shoulders change with every switchback, reading the mountain through them the way Wren read it through ink. Snow had begun in earnest an hour past, fat flakes that fell without wind, straight down, like the sky was emptying itself of something. The pass had narrowed to a shelf of stone with darkness on one side and a wall of black pine on the other, and Wren had been thinking—not for the first time—that they would die on this road, that fleeing into the Thornfells ahead of winter was not escape but simply a slower, colder way to be caught.

Then Jonah stopped, tilted their head like a bird considering something, and said, "There."

"There what? There's nothing there."

"There's never anything there. That's how you know." Jonah turned, and even in the failing light Wren could see the exhaustion under their skin, the fine tremor in their hands that they were pretending wasn't happening. "Smoke. Smell it?"

Wren breathed. Woodsmoke, faint and strange, threaded through the cold—woodsmoke with something underneath it, resinous and dark, like the smell of a fire burning something other than wood. "That's not pine."

"No," Jonah said, and did not elaborate, which was its own kind of answer. "Stay behind me. And whatever you see when we come around this bend—don't say you saw it. Not out loud. Names are how things get found here."

They came around the bend, and there it was: a low stone building crouched in a fold of the mountain like a animal sheltering from weather, its roof shingled in slate gone green with age, its single window glowing amber against the blue-dark. No road led to it. No track, no cart-rut, nothing but the goat-path they'd been following, which seemed to arrive at its door as if the door had grown there overnight to meet it. Above the lintel hung no sign at all—just a bracket, empty, polished smooth as if a sign had once hung there and been deliberately taken down.

"It's not on any map," Wren said. They'd drawn maps of these mountains. They had *drawn* these mountains, every ridge and valley the crown's surveyors had reached, and this fold of stone had been blank on their work. Blank, or worse—blank with a little conventional squiggle meaning *unreliable terrain*, which was cartographer's language for *the mountain lies here*.

"Maps are arguments," Jonah said. "This inn is a fact that lost the argument." They pushed open the door without knocking.

* * *

Inside was warmth so sudden and total that Wren's eyes stung. The common room was low-ceilinged and round-walled, built into the rock itself, lit by a fire that burned in a hearth of black stone—and now Wren understood the smell, because the fire was not burning wood alone. Among the logs lay chunks of some dense, dark material that flamed without crackling, giving off a light that was warm-colored but somehow cold to look at, the way certain deep water looks warm and is not.

The room held perhaps a dozen people, and every one of them looked up when Jonah entered, and every one of them looked away again within a heartbeat, in the practiced way of people who have agreed together not to see each other. A woman mending a net by the fire. Two men playing dice with counters instead of coin. An old man asleep sitting up, his tankard still full, balanced perfectly on his knee.

No one asked who they were. No one looked twice. Wren felt the whole room perform, in unison, the same act of forgetting Jonah performed daily in the palace—and understood, with a small cold shock, that this was a house built for people like Jonah. A house for the unrecorded.

Behind the bar stood a woman so old that her age had stopped being a number and become a texture. Her skin was like paper that had been folded and refolded along the same lines for decades; her eyes were pale as skimmed milk, and they fixed on Jonah with an attention that made Wren's neck prickle.

"You'll be wanting the back room," the old woman said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Two beds, one lamp, supper at the second bell. I don't take coin."

"What do you take?" Jonah asked, and their voice had changed—had gone careful, formal, the voice Wren had heard them use only with the queen.

"Stories," the old woman said. "Good ones. True ones. Pay me before you leave, not after. Debts of that kind go bad if you let them sit." She jerked her chin at Wren. "And your friend there keeps looking at my walls like they're going to confess something. Tell your friend the walls have heard everything already. It's the guests that still have surprises in them."

"I'm Wren," Wren said, because apparently they were constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone.

"Mm." The old woman's milk-pale eyes moved over Wren's face, their ink-stained fingers, the map case slung across their back, and something flickered in them—recognition, or its shadow. "I knew a man who drew maps. Long time back. He drew one of this valley once, and the valley didn't speak to him for a year after." She smiled, showing teeth worn smooth as river stones. "I'm Maret. I keep the Hollow. Supper at second bell. Don't ask the fire questions after midnight—it answers."

She turned away to draw ale, and that was that, apparently. Jonah steered Wren to a corner table by a pressure on their elbow, and they sat, and the room closed over them like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Supper was barley stew with dark greens and a bread so dense it was nearly a cake, and it was the best thing Wren had eaten in a week, and they ate it in silence while watching Maret move through her room.

Because there was something wrong with her—or rather, something right, something too right. She moved like a woman much younger than her body. When she bent to feed the fire, her spine curved easily; when she reached a high shelf, her arm extended fully, without the hitching caution of the very old. Only her face and hands carried the weight of her years, and they carried it unevenly, Wren thought, like a garment worn thin in odd places. As if she had spent a very long time being old, or as if oldness had been applied to her rather than arrived naturally, the way frost forms on glass.

Jonah followed Wren's gaze and shook their head, minutely. *Not now.*

But then Maret herself settled the question, because she came to their table with a third tankard nobody had ordered, set it down in front of Jonah, and said:

"Eriksen."

Not a question. A name, laid on the table between them like a knife.

Wren felt Jonah go still beside them—the particular stillness of a person whose body has just become a ledger again, recording everything, revealing nothing. "I don't know that name," Jonah said.

"Mm." Maret sat down across from them, uninvited, folding her ancient hands. "No more do the records. That was the point of it, wasn't it. Scrubbed clean, a hundred years back. Every ledger, every parish roll, every deed. There was a family in these mountains that could walk any road unseen because every road remembered them, and then one winter there was no such family, and the roads forgot, and the mountains kept their mouths shut about why." She leaned forward. The firelight moved in her milk-pale eyes. "But I'm older than the mountains' discretion, child. I remember the Eriksens. I remember *you*."

"I think you're mistaking me for someone else."

"I don't mistake." Maret's voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "I watched you come through that door and I watched my floor learn your feet. This house has stood on that spot since before the palace above had its name, and it knows the weight of everyone who crosses it. You walk like Halda Eriksen walked. You stand like Tam Eriksen stood. And you've got her way of going quiet when a name gets too close—as if quiet were armor." She tapped the table once, softly. "Halda's girl. Or boy. Or neither. Doesn't matter to me. What matters is that Halda and Tam Eriksen sat where you're sitting, eleven years ago, on a night with weather like tonight's weather, and paid me in stories, and one of those stories has been sitting in my chest ever since like a swallowed coal. And now their child walks in out of the snow, and I find I can't hold it anymore."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the room, the dice game had stopped. Wren realized the whole common room had gone silent—not listening, exactly. *Attending.* The way a forest attends.

"Maret," Jonah said quietly. "Whatever story you're carrying, it isn't mine. My parents died when I was six. Nobody told me how. Nobody told me much of anything."

"They died trying to unmake a sale," Maret said. "A sale their own blood had brokered, a hundred years before them. That's the shape of it, child. Your family sold something that couldn't rightly be sold, and every Eriksen since has been born owing, and your mother and father went into the high passes to pay it back and the mountain kept them." Her voice gentled, which somehow made it heavier. "You want to know how they died. I'll tell you. But not for free, and not all at once, because truth poured out all at once scalds the vessel. First you'll hear the beginning, and the beginning isn't yours. The beginning belongs to a prince."

Wren's heart was suddenly loud in their ears. "Alaric Vane," they said.

Maret turned those pale eyes on Wren for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rustling sound, like wind through winter reeds.

"A cartographer," she said. "Of course. Of course it's a mapmaker who says the name first. You people are the worst kind of dangerous—you write things down." She rose, joints moving smoothly, wrongly, and took up the lamp from their table. "Come, then. Both of you. Back room's warmer, and this story wants telling in the dark, where it's been living."

* * *

The back room was small and round, windowless, its walls of raw stone hung with objects Wren couldn't catalogue: bundles of dried herbs, a child's shoe, a birdcage with no door, a mirror turned to face the wall. Maret set the lamp on a table and lowered herself into a chair by the fire that burned in yet another black-stone hearth, and gestured them onto a bench opposite, and for a moment she simply looked at them both, and the looking had weight, like snow accumulating.

"A hundred years ago," she began, "this valley had a different name, and I had a different face, and the winter was coming down the mountain like it always comes, and nobody thought anything of it. Winters end. That's what winters do. But that year the winter didn't end. It sat on us through spring like a hand pressing down, and the rivers stayed locked, and the seed-corn died in the granaries, and folk started saying the old words again—the words about the court beneath, the ones we'd gotten too comfortable to say." She poked the fire with an iron rod, and the strange dark fuel flared cold-bright. "And the king—Alaric's father, Vane the Fourth—sent riders down into the deep places with an offer. And the offer came back up wearing a prince's face."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "He went himself?"

"He was *taken* himself, and glad to go, by all accounts—he was young and solemn and believed everything his priests told him about duty. The pact was struck at midwinter. His soul, freely given, to anchor the bargain; the deep court's protection in return; an endless winter's *mercy*, they called it—cold enough to kill the wars, warm enough inside the wards to keep the kingdom fed. And the Eriksens—" Maret paused, and looked at Jonah, and her ancient face did something complicated, pity and accusation braided together, "—the Eriksens brokered it. They were road-people, door-people. The only family whose paths ran both above and below. It was their tongue the treaty was spoken in. Their hands that carried the terms down and the seal back up. For that service they were granted what they'd always had—freedom of every road—and something new besides: erasure. No debts, no taxes, no records. The crown wiped them from every book as payment, and they thought it a gift."

Jonah made a sound Wren had never heard from them—small, involuntary, wounded. "They erased themselves."

"They were erased, and called it freedom, and taught their children to call it that too. To be no one. To be unwritten." Maret's voice dropped. "And for eighty years it worked, and the winter held gentle, and everyone forgot there'd ever been another arrangement. Until a pair of Eriksens got curious about what exactly their family's freedom had cost, and went looking, and found out, and couldn't live with it. Halda and Tam. Your mother and father, child. They spent nine years preparing to go down and steal the soul back—to break the pact, pay the debt, end the feeding. And eleven years ago they climbed into the high passes on a night like this one, and the mountain closed over them, and no bone of them was ever found."

The silence afterward was enormous. In it, Wren reached without thinking and found Jonah's hand, and Jonah let them take it, and Jonah's hand was cold as the stone of the mountain itself.

"The prophecy book," Wren heard themself say. Their voice sounded far away. "The thorn that burns. It's not the first time someone tried."

"No," Maret said. "It's the first time someone was *named* for it before they tried. That's the difference. That's what frightens the crown, if you want to know—they can bear a thief, but not a foretold one." She leaned forward, and the firelight carved her ancient face into ridges and hollows, and for one disorienting moment Wren thought they saw, beneath the age, the outline of a much younger woman—someone who might have stood in this valley a hundred years ago, watching riders descend with an offer. "One thing more, and then you'll sleep, because you're half-dead on your feet and the dead make poor listeners. The night your parents left, Halda sat where you're sitting and gave me a story as payment, and the story was this: *if our child ever comes to you, tell them the debt runs through their blood, but so does the key.* Those were her words. The debt runs through the blood, but so does the key." Maret's milk-pale eyes held Jonah's. "I never knew what key she meant. But I know this house, and I know these mountains, and I know that doors below don't open for crowns or guilds or prophecy books. They open for bloodlines. Yours."

Jonah's hand tightened around Wren's until it hurt.

"And tomorrow," Maret added, rising, taking up her lamp, "I'll tell you how they died. That part you'll need. Sleep now. The house will watch the door."

She left them in the lamplight with the story settling over them like the snow outside—soft, relentless, burying.

Wren meant to say something comforting. What came out instead, barely a whisper, was: "Eleven years, Jonah. They tried eleven years ago. And the book has your face next to tomorrow's date."

"I know," Jonah said. Their voice was flat and steady, the taster's voice, the voice that reported poison calmly to a queen. But their hand hadn't let go of Wren's, and it was shaking now, finely, continuously, like a wire in wind. "I know what date it is. Tomorrow's the anniversary. The night they went into the mountain." A breath. "The book isn't predicting, Wren. It's remembering. Something down there has been waiting for an Eriksen to come back—and it's tired of waiting."

Outside, the snow thickened against the shuttered window, and somewhere far below them—farther than any map Wren had ever drawn—something turned over in its sleep, and began, slowly, to dream of the door opening.
