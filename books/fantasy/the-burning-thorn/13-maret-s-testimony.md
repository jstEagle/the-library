## 13. Maret's Testimony

The Hollow Inn had a back room the way a body has secrets: everyone knew it was there, and no one spoke of it.

Maret led us through it after the dishes were cleared, past a curtain of stitched-together flour sacks gone soft as skin with age, into a low room where the ceiling beams curved like ribs and a fire burned in a hearth built of river stones so old they'd gone glassy. The light did strange things in there. It pooled instead of spread, gathered in the corners like something shy, and left the middle of the room in a permanent amber dusk that smelled of tallow, dried yarrow, and beneath it all—faint but unmistakable to my tongue—the mineral bite of deep-mountain air, the same cold sweetness I'd caught once through a crack in a cellar in Thornfell's lowest town and never forgotten.

Wren sat close enough that our shoulders touched on the bench. Neither of us said anything about it. We hadn't spoken much since Maret had looked at me across the common room and said my name—not Jonah, not *taster*, but *Eriksen*—in a voice like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known my body carried.

Maret lowered herself into a chair by the hearth with the care of someone whose joints had been negotiating with winter for eighty years. She was small. She seemed smaller in the amber dusk, folded into herself like a letter. But her eyes—her eyes were young in a way that had nothing to do with age, bright and appalled and awake, the eyes of someone who had seen a thing once and never been permitted to stop seeing it.

"I knew your mother," she said.

The words went through me like a fishhook. I kept my face still. Twenty years of tasting for queens teaches you to keep your face still while your throat closes.

"You didn't," I said. "My parents died before I could walk."

"They died before you could *remember*, which is a different thing." She leaned forward. Her hands, knotted as roots, opened on her knees. "I held you once. You screamed the whole time. Your mother laughed and said you had your father's opinions about strangers." A pause. "You still make that face. When Wren there first walked in with you, you made it. Just for a breath."

Wren made a small sound beside me—half laugh, half sob—and pressed their knee against mine under the table.

"Maret," I said carefully. "You said you remembered the night Alaric Vane was taken. That's why we came. But if you're going to stand there and tell me you knew my parents, then you know why I'm asking what I'm about to ask, and you're going to answer it straight, because I have spent my whole life being nobody from nowhere, and I would rather be gutted than spend one more hour being lied to by kindness."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the inn groaned under the weight of the snow that wasn't falling yet but was thinking about it.

Maret nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something for her.

"Straight, then." She drew a breath that seemed to come up from her boots. "Your family brokered the sale."

I had expected it. Some part of me—a part I'd fed scraps of suspicion for years without ever letting it eat—had already known. You don't survive in my trade by ignoring the taste of a thing underneath the taste. And yet hearing it aloud was different. Hearing it aloud made it real in the way ink makes a debt real.

"Say the rest," I said.

"The Eriksens were intermediaries. Not smugglers, not then—*brokers*. There's a difference, though the guild has spent a century muddying it. A broker stands between two parties who cannot speak to each other directly and carries the terms back and forth until both sides agree. The crown above couldn't treat with the court below—kings and queens can't be seen kneeling—and the deep court wouldn't treat with anyone who hadn't been vouched for by blood. So there had to be a family in between. A family whose word was collateral. Whose name meant the deal would hold." Her eyes fixed on mine. "Eriksen meant *the one who binds*. Did you know that? It's old speech. Most folk think it just means cooper, barrel-maker. It doesn't. It means the one who ties the knot."

"And the knot," I said, "was Alaric Vane."

"The knot was Alaric Vane." Maret's voice didn't waver, but her hands closed slowly over each other, knuckle over knuckle, like a man making a fist very gently. "One hundred years ago this coming midwinter. I was seven years old. My father kept this inn even then, and the night it happened, there were riders on the road all hours—crown men, hooded men, and one woman on a grey mare who stopped here at dawn and drank nothing and asked for nothing except hot water, and stood at the window looking up at the mountain with a face I have spent seventy years trying to forget." She swallowed. "That was your grandmother. Sable Eriksen. I've never known whether she was riding away from it or toward undoing it. I think she didn't know either."

"Tell me how it was done," I said. "All of it. Don't spare me the shape of the knife."

So she told me.

She told it plainly, the way old people tell terrible things when they've had a lifetime to sand the horror down to something carryable, and somehow that plainness was worse than any telling dressed in thunder. The kingdom had been dying—not by war, not by plague, but by the slow arithmetic of a bad century: failed harvests stacked on failed harvests, granaries emptying, wolves coming down into the villages bold as bailiffs. And the deep court had offered terms. Protection. An endless winter, yes—but an endless winter that would hold at the edge of survivability, snow enough to seal the passes against every army and raiding band in the world, cold enough to kill the locusts and the blight and the brigands, a fortress of season with the kingdom safe inside it like a jewel in ice.

The price was not gold. Gold meant nothing below. The price was a soul, freely given, anchored in the deep court, fed upon nightly—for the court beneath the mountain did not eat bread or meat. They ate what souls put out. Warmth. Memory. Will. The slow distillation of a person, drawn off drop by drop across years, the way maple sap is drawn from a tree that stays standing and looks, to every casual eye, perfectly alive.

And it had to be given freely. That was the law below, Maret said—the one law, older than the Regent, older than the mountain. Nothing could be taken. Everything must be traded. Which was why they needed a broker. Because no prince gives his soul freely, and no king asks him to. But a broker could arrange the circumstances. Could build the room where a desperate young man, third son, unloved and unneeded, might be persuaded that giving himself was the one grand thing he would ever get to do.

"They didn't force him," Maret said, and her voice cracked on it at last, the sanded horror showing its raw grain. "That's the filth of it, Eriksen. They *persuaded* him. Your grandmother sat with him eleven nights in a room in the old summer palace—I've heard it from three sources and they all agree on the number—and she laid out the famine and the dead children and the wolves at the doors, and she wept, by all accounts, genuinely wept, and in the end Alaric Vane signed the bargain with his own hand and walked up the goat-track to the thorn-door in his best coat, waving to the servants like he was going hawking." She stopped. Drew breath. "He was twenty-two years old. He thought he was saving us. God help us all, maybe he was."

The silence after that was long. In it I became aware of my own heartbeat, steady and traitorous, and of Wren's hand finding mine on the bench, cold fingers lacing through cold fingers, and of the fact that I could not remember deciding to let them.

"What happened to the family after?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's. "After the signing."

"That," Maret said, "is the part that got people killed."

She told me that too. How the pact had held for thirty years, and the kingdom had grown fat and strange inside its shell of winter, forgetting hunger so thoroughly that it began to mistake comfort for virtue. How the brokers' line had continued, generation to generation, each Eriksen sworn to maintain the terms, carry the yearly renewal tokens down to the door, and keep the ledger—the true ledger, the one that named the price and the payer and the term of years. A hundred years, the contract read. One hundred years of winter for one soul, renewable at the crown's pleasure.

"And then your parents," Maret said.

"My parents," I repeated. Two words I had said ten thousand times in my life, always flat, always finished, a closed door in a sentence. They had died. It had been an accident, the guild told me later—a rockslide on a pass, bad luck, the mountains take whom they take. I had built my whole self on top of that flat little grave-marker of a story.

"Your parents read the ledger," Maret said. "All the way through. Properly, with a scholar's eye—your mother had one; she used to sit where you're sitting and argue contract law with me till dawn, and I want you to know that losing an argument to her remains among the great intellectual experiences of my life." Something moved through her face, grief and love tangled past separating. "And they found the clause. The renewal clause. The pact was written to renew automatically unless formally contested by the brokering line. For sixty years, no Eriksen had contested it. Why would they? Contesting meant the winter ended, and the winter ending meant the kingdom lay naked to the world. Every broker before them had chosen the kingdom's safety over one man's soul, year after year, and called it duty, and slept soundly, more or less."

"But my parents contested it."

"Your parents tried to *renegotiate* it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. They went down to the door—they went down twice, that I know of—and they offered the deep court a trade: end the feeding on Alaric, accept payment in memories instead. Freely given memories, from willing donors, renewed each year. The court below was starving even then—the soul of a prince runs thin after thirty years, like a field farmed too long—and your parents thought they'd found the crack in the wall. A way to feed the hunger without eating a man forever." Maret's hands had gone still in her lap, entirely still, the stillness of held breath. "The Regent refused. Or rather—" and here her voice dropped, went almost gentle, which was the worst thing she'd done yet, "—the Regent accepted the principle and demanded a guarantor. A soul of the brokering line, pledged in earnest, to prove the memories would keep coming. And your parents refused to pledge themselves, because pledging themselves meant leaving you orphaned by choice rather than chance, and they would not do it. And they went home to find another way. And on the road home, between here and the low towns, there was a rockslide."

The fire had burned low. I watched it and felt nothing, and then felt everything, all of it arriving at once like water through a breached dam—the rockslide that was not a rockslide, the scholar mother arguing contract law in this very room, the father with opinions about strangers, the two of them climbing toward the thorn-door twice with a plan to save a man they'd never met because the ledger sat in their keeping and the ledger was conscience—and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in cool and coming out burning, that my parents had not died *of* anything. They had died *for* refusing. The guild's story, the flat little grave-marker, the accident—all of it a lid set over a murder, set there by whoever benefited from the brokering line going quiet. The crown, perhaps. The guild, certainly. Both, likely, in the comfortable way institutions collaborate without ever having to say so aloud.

"They had a child," I said. "Me. They went anyway."

"They went anyway," Maret said, "because your mother used to say that a debt you inherit is still yours, and pretending otherwise just means paying it late, with interest." She looked at me then, straight on, and her ancient young eyes held no pity at all, only a fierce and terrible respect. "They hid you well, Jonah. The guild took you in because your mother paid them to, years in advance, in the only coin the guild respects—which was favors, and routes, and the locations of three roads I will die without learning. She bought you a life of being no one. And you built it beautifully. I mean that. No one I've ever known has disappeared so thoroughly while walking about in broad daylight." A dry pause. "But the debt didn't disappear with the name. Debts of that kind don't. They wait. And now the prophecy's found you, and the queen's found you, and the guild's found you, and the door will find you, because the door knows its broker's blood when it tastes it."

Outside, the wind rose against the inn's north wall with a sound like a vast slow exhale, and somewhere in it, or in my imagination, which had stopped distinguishing between the two some hours ago, I heard the mountain breathing.

Wren spoke for the first time in what felt like an hour. Their voice was rough. "The clause. If Jonah contests the pact—if Jonah goes down and takes Alaric back—what happens to the winter?"

"What happens," Maret said, "is what the prophecy says happens. The long winter finishes falling. All of it, all at once, every flake the pact has been holding back for a hundred years, and it will fall on a kingdom that has forgotten how to survive a real winter, and the crown will need someone to blame." She turned back to me. "Which is why, child, you should understand exactly what you've inherited tonight. Not a curse. Worse than a curse. A *choice*. Your parents died holding the door of that choice open, and every Eriksen for a hundred years chose the kingdom over the man, and the kingdom grew fat on it, and the man wore thin to nothing, and now the choice has come round again, the way choices do, wearing your own face."

I sat with it. I sat with it the way I'd learned to sit with poison on my tongue—still, attentive, letting it show me what it was before I decided what it would do to me.

Grief, I found, was not what I'd expected. I had grieved my parents all my life, the way you grieve a photograph: two faces, a rockslide, a flat little story. But this was another grief entirely, and it was worse and better at once, because it had *shape*. My mother's voice arguing law in this room. My father's opinions about strangers living on in my own face. Two people who had climbed toward a door twice, carrying mercy in their hands, and died for the carrying. I had spent twenty years being no one to stay safe, and all the while I had been the child of the only two people in a hundred years who had tried to be *someone* about it—and they had paid for it with everything, including the truth reaching me, until a cartographer and a prophecy book and an old woman with a memory like a blade had dug it back out into the light.

"I'm not my grandmother," I said at last. "I'm not my parents. I don't owe the deep court a broker. I don't owe the crown a sacrifice."

"No," Maret agreed. "You owe them nothing. That's the whole point, and it's the hardest lesson in the ledger. You don't owe anyone. But you're the only one alive who *can*." She rose, slowly, joint by joint, and crossed to a chest beneath the window, and from beneath false bottoms and old wool she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table between us.

A book. Small, bound in grey leather gone soft as chamois, its edges worn round as river stones. On its cover, tooled in a script I recognized from a certain archive shelf a hundred miles south, was a single word: *Ledger*.

"Your mother left it with me the night before the last time they went up," Maret said. "In case. She said, 'If we don't come back, give it to our child when our child starts asking questions.' And I have waited forty years, Jonah Eriksen, and you started asking questions the moment you walked into my common room and pretended not to know your own name."

I didn't touch it. Not yet. My hands stayed flat on the table on either side of it, the way you keep your hands flat when something is too charged to handle barehanded.

"Why give it now? Why not let it burn with the rest of it?"

"Because tomorrow," Maret said, "the snow comes early, and heavy, and wrong—I've felt it in my knees for a week, and my knees have never lied about the mountain yet. And because there are crown riders on the north road as of dusk, asking after a taster and a mapmaker, and my knees say those aren't coincidence either." She pushed the ledger an inch toward me. "Whatever you choose, choose it moving. That was your mother's rule. Grieve on the road."

Wren's hand tightened around mine. Outside, the wind changed its note—dropped lower, gathered itself, began to push at the shutters with the patient insistence of something that had all night and intended to use it.

I picked up the ledger. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

It fell open, as old books do, to the page most often read—and there, in a hand I had no way of knowing and knew instantly, small and precise and furious, was my mother's writing, filling the margin of the renewal clause with a single line, underlined twice:

*There is always a third option. Find it.*

I looked up at Wren. Wren looked back at me, and in the amber dark of Maret's hidden room, with the storm gathering its breath outside and crown riders somewhere on the north road and a hundred years of inherited guilt sitting in my palms no heavier than a grey leather book, I saw the same thing arrive in their face that had arrived in mine.

Not fate. Not duty. A question, finally asked honestly, with time running out to answer it.

"We leave before dawn," I said. "Before the snow. Before the riders." I closed the ledger gently, the way you close a hand around another hand. "And Maret—tell me about the second gate. The one the maps don't show. Because if my parents went down twice, they went down some way, and I'd wager my tongue it wasn't the front door."

Old Maret smiled then, for the first time all night—a small, sharp, sorrowful smile, the smile of a woman watching a debt come due at last and finding, against all odds, that she was glad.

"Sit down, child," she said. "And pour us all something warm. This is the part where I tell you why the Hollow Inn exists on no map—and why your mother made me swear that if ever an Eriksen came asking, I'd tell them the truth about the root-door, even if it cost me the inn."

She glanced at the shuttered window, where the wind had begun, very softly, to sing.

"And it may yet."
