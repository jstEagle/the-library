## 14. What Was Sold

The archive at Hollowmere kept its own weather.

Wren had learned this in their first year as a cartographer's apprentice, when they'd mistaken the chill that pooled between the eastern shelves for a draft and spent a week hunting a window that did not exist. There was no draft. The cold simply lived there, the way dust lived there, the way silence lived there—a resident thing, patient as stone. The archivists said the building remembered every document it had ever swallowed, and that some documents were colder than others.

Tonight, Wren understood what they'd meant.

They sat at the long reading table beneath a lamp whose oil was running low, surrounded by the scattered anatomy of a century-old crime. Ledgers. Correspondence. A treaty draft with three of its pages razored out—razored carefully, by someone who had wanted the removal to look like damage rather than decision. Wren had spent four days pulling threads, following the smell of ink the way Jonah followed the smell of snow before a storm, and now the threads lay all around them, cut and sorted, and the shape they made when Wren finally stepped back and looked at the whole of it was so much worse than anything they had imagined.

*Alaric Vane was not taken,* Wren wrote in the margin of their notes, because writing it made it real in the way thinking it refused to. *He was sold.*

The word sat on the page like a dead bird.

They had found the first thread three days ago, in a shipping manifest from the royal household—the kind of dry document no one reads twice, listing provisions moved out of the palace in the winter of the hundredth year of Isolde's line. Among the candles and salt fish and bolts of grey wool: *one (1) soul, princely, whole; one (1) contract of winter, perpetual.* The clerk who had entered it had used the same hand, the same ink, the same bored economy for both lines. A soul and a shipment of wool. That was the detail that had kept Wren awake the first night—not the horror of the sale itself, but the boredom of the man who recorded it. He had probably gone to supper afterward. He had probably complained about his boots.

And tonight, in the coldest corner of the archive, behind a false spine labeled *Hydrological Surveys, Eastern Reaches*, Wren had found the rest: the contract itself. Or most of it. Enough.

They read it again now, though they no longer needed to. The words had begun to live behind their eyes.

*Let it be known that the Crown of Vane, in its wisdom and necessity, does convey unto the Court Beneath one soul entire—that of Alaric Vane, second son, aged nineteen—to be held in perpetuity as anchor and surety, that the winter-pact may bind and hold. In exchange, the Court Beneath shall turn back the killing frost from the borders of the kingdom, season upon season, world without end, so long as the anchor holds.*

So long as the anchor holds.

Wren set down the page with hands that were not quite steady and looked at the lamp flame, which burned small and blue-edged in the archive cold, and tried to make their mind do the thing it was built to do: take scattered points and draw the lines between them. A map. Everything was a map if you looked at it rightly. Terrain and consequence. Here is the mountain; here is the river; here is where the water goes when the snow melts.

Here is a boy. Here is a kingdom. Here is what the kingdom paid to keep from freezing.

* * *

The mechanics of it, once seen, could not be unseen.

Wren spread their notes across the table and began, methodically, to build the diagram—not a map of land but a map of flow, arrows and counter-arrows, the way they'd once charted the trade winds off the southern coast for a merchant who paid in pepper. The soul of Alaric Vane sat at the center of it, drawn as a small circle, because Wren could not bring themselves to draw a person. Around the circle, the arrows: out from the deep court, nightly, feeding. Back from the crown above, through the pact, continuous, the winter turned aside at the borders like water diverted around a millwheel. And anchoring everything—the word was the contract's own, *anchor*, and anchors were things sunk deliberately, things meant never to rise—the soul held beneath the mountain, burning, presumably, the way a wick burns. Slowly. For a century. For as long as it lasted.

That was the part the old texts had been circling without saying. Wren had pulled six volumes on deep-court lore from the restricted shelves, each one more hedged than the last, the authors hiding behind *it is said* and *some scholars hold*, and pieced together what none of them would state plainly: the court beneath did not keep Alaric Vane the way a jailer keeps a prisoner. It kept him the way a larder keeps meat. The pact was not powered by his presence. It was powered by his consumption. Every night, something fed. Every night, the feeding bought another day of thawed roads and planted fields and children who did not starve in the dark of midwinter.

A hundred years of springs, purchased one night at a time, from a nineteen-year-old who had never stopped paying.

Wren put their head down on the table, briefly, among the papers, and let the cold of the archive press against their cheek like a hand.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not resolve. They had come into this—into all of it, the prophecy book, the face in the margin, the thorn that burns—believing they were uncovering a rescue. A theft, yes, but a righteous one: steal back what was stolen, return the stolen thing to itself, and let the wronged party walk free. Jonah would go down under the mountain and come back up with a soul in their hands, and the story would have the clean geometry of all good stories, the thief and the treasure and the door closing behind them.

But the contract did not describe a theft. It described an engine. And you did not rescue the fuel from the engine. You took the fuel out, and the engine stopped, and everything the engine had been doing stopped with it.

If Jonah stole back Alaric's soul, the winter-pact ended.

Not weakened. Not frayed at the edges, giving them time, giving the kingdom time. Ended. The frost that had been turned aside for a hundred years—the frost that had been *waiting*, perhaps, all this time, piling itself up against the borders of the spell like floodwater against a dam—would come through all at once. Wren thought of the maps on their studio wall. The northern passes. The river valleys where the grain grew. The city itself, its ten thousand chimneys, its markets, its children. All of it sitting in the path of a century of withheld winter, and the prophecy's own words rising in memory with new and terrible clarity:

*a thorn that burns will steal back what was sold before the long winter finishes falling.*

Before the long winter finishes falling. They had read those words a dozen times since the night in the archive when they'd found Jonah's face beside tomorrow's date, and every reading until now had assumed the long winter was the one already outside the windows—the ordinary, grinding, endless-seeming cold of the pact years, the winter that never quite ended even in summer. But that wasn't it. That wasn't it at all. The long winter hadn't fallen yet. The long winter was what happened *after*. The long winter was the bill coming due.

The prophecy wasn't promising a rescue. It was promising a race.

* * *

Somewhere above them, the palace clock struck the third hour, and the sound came down through the archive's stone bones muffled and strange, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Wren should sleep. They knew they should sleep. But sleep was where the diagrams waited, and so instead they lit a second lamp and did the thing they had been avoiding all evening: they went looking for Alaric himself. Not the clause, not the anchor, not the fuel. The boy.

The portrait gallery records were easier to find than Wren expected, because no one had bothered to hide them. Why hide them? The official history—Wren had read it now, the sanitized version taught in every schoolroom—held that Prince Alaric Vane had died young, of a fever, in the hard winter of his nineteenth year, and had been mourned beautifully and buried quietly, and that his elder brother's line had ruled ever since. There was even a statue. Wren had walked past it a thousand times in the palace gardens without looking: a slender youth with a book under one arm, gazing north, his bronze face worn featureless by a century of weather.

But the gallery records held the original commission notes, and painters, Wren had learned, were terrible gossips in ink. Master Corvin, court painter, had written of his sitting with the prince in the autumn before the sale: *His Highness sits well, though he is melancholy past his years and asks odd questions—whether I believe a man can be painted against his will, whether a likeness can be made to serve a purpose its subject never chose. I told him a portrait serves only memory. He laughed at that, or something near a laugh, and said he hoped I was wrong, because he did not much care to be remembered as he was about to be.*

About to be.

He had known. That was the thing that broke Wren open, there alone in the cold with the lamps burning low. Nineteen years old, melancholy past his years, asking a painter odd questions—and he had known. Second sons knew their place in the arithmetic of crowns. Somewhere in that last autumn, someone had sat him down and explained the famine winter, the dying herds, the borders going white, and then explained the offer, and then explained that he was the offer. And he had gone. The record showed no struggle, no escape attempt, no desperate letters smuggled to allies. Just a boy who sat for his portrait, asked whether a likeness could be made to serve a purpose its subject never chose, and then walked down under the mountain so that everyone else could go on living.

And the kingdom had thanked him with a fever story and a featureless statue.

Wren thought of Jonah then—thought of them with an ache that had become, over these weeks, less like a wound and more like a pulse, always there, always keeping time. Jonah, who had made themselves unremarkable so thoroughly that Wren sometimes caught them practicing it, smoothing their face into nothing in a window's reflection. Jonah, whose body was a ledger of poisons, who carried the crown's dangers in their blood so the queen need never feel them. Jonah, who had been raised by a guild that owned them and employed by a crown that would spend them, and who had chosen invisibility because visibility, in this kingdom, was what got you sold.

Alaric Vane had been visible. That had been his whole misfortune. He had been the second son, the spare, the one whose life had a price tag hanging from it, and when the crown needed coin it had spent him.

And now the crown wanted Jonah to go down and take him back—but Wren had read enough of the court by now, had tasted enough of its machinery in the days since Serah Dole's shadow had first fallen across them, to understand what the crown actually wanted. It wanted the prophecy fulfilled *safely*. It wanted the thorn that burns to descend, retrieve the anchor, and restore the pact—or better, to find some way to renew it, some new arrangement, some new arrangement *with someone else's soul in it*. Serah Dole had called Jonah expendable bait, and Wren had flinched at the word, but the word was precise. Bait was what you used when you wanted something to come to you. The crown was fishing. And whatever the crown hoped to catch at the bottom of that mountain, it was not a rescued prince.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Wren trimmed the wick and, in the small ceremony of it, caught sight of their own reflection in the darkened window beside the reading table—a pale smudge of a face floating over the scattered papers, eyes too bright, ink on the side of one hand. They looked, they thought, like a person standing at the edge of something.

Because there was one more line in the contract. They had saved it for last, the way you save the worst news, and now they made themselves read it aloud, quietly, just to hear how it sounded in a human voice.

*"Should the anchor be removed by force or fraud, the surety shall lapse entire, and the Crown of Vane shall owe the Court Beneath the full arrears of a hundred winters, to be collected as the Court sees fit."*

Collected as the Court sees fit.

Wren sat back. Outside the archive's high, slit windows, the sky was doing the thing it had been doing more and more these past weeks—bruising early, thickening, gathering itself. Snow was coming. Real snow, the first true fall of the season, and every year of their life the first snow had been a small festival, a reason for wine and firesides, because the first snow meant the pact was holding, meant the frost beyond it had been turned aside for another year, meant the kingdom would see spring.

This year the first snow felt like a countdown.

They began, slowly, to gather the papers, squaring the edges, stacking the ledgers, returning the false-spined volume to its place among the hydrological surveys where it had slept for a hundred years. Their hands moved automatically while their mind ran the map one final time, the whole terrible flow of it: a soul at the center, burning; a court beneath, feeding; a crown above, complicit for five generations, renewing by silence what it had purchased by sacrifice; and now, descending through the storm toward all of it, Jonah—Jonah with their poison ledger of a body and their smuggler's knowledge of the goat-tracks and their stubborn, furious, unremarkable face, carrying a prophecy they had never asked for and a debt they had not yet learned was theirs.

Steal back what was sold. Everyone wanted it done. The prophecy demanded it; the crown schemed for it; Wren's own heart, helplessly, ached for it—for the boy under the mountain, for the man worn thin by a century of teeth, for the justice of returning the stolen to itself.

But no one, Wren realized, stacking the last ledger, no one had yet said aloud the sentence that mattered most. Not the archivists with their hedged volumes. Not the crown with its schemes. Not even the prophecy, which spoke in riddles because prophecies were cowards.

No one had said: *when we take him back, we take back the shield, and everything the shield was holding off comes through the gap at once.*

We are not planning a rescue, Wren thought, dousing the first lamp. We are planning to break a dam. And the only question—the question that would follow them up the mountain and down beneath it, into the mirrored halls and the cold firelight—was whether a hundred years of withheld winter was a price worth paying for one soul.

For Alaric's soul, Wren corrected themselves, shrugging into their coat, feeling the cold of the archive cling to them like a second skin. For him, yes. For him, a thousand times, yes. But it would not be only his price to pay. It would be the miller's price, and the farmer's, and the child's in the northern valleys, all of them frozen in a debt they had never signed, all of them paying for a bargain struck before their grandparents were born.

The archive door boomed shut behind them, and the corridor beyond was dark, and somewhere far above, through fathoms of stone, the wind had changed its voice.

It had started to snow.
