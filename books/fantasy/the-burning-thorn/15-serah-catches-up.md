## 15. Serah Catches Up

The bridge had no name, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Jonah knew every crossing in the Thornfells the way other people knew their own hands—by touch, by habit, by the small betrayals of wear. This one was a smuggler's bridge, old as the guild and twice as crooked: three fallen pines laid across a gorge where the river Vane ran white and loud, its span furred with moss that never froze even in the cruelest weeks of winter. No map showed it. No crown road came within six miles of it. It existed only because people needed to cross here and could not afford to be seen doing so.

And yet Serah Dole stood at the far end of it, arms folded, snow gathering on her shoulders like she'd been carved there.

Jonah stopped mid-span, boots finding the one dry plank without looking down. The river roared below, loud enough to swallow voices, which meant Serah would have to come close to speak—and close was where Jonah lived.

*Don't panic,* they told themselves, and found, to their mild surprise, that they didn't. Nine years of tasting for Queen Isolde had taught the body a different grammar than fear. Fear said *run*. Training said *wait, watch, weigh*. Somewhere between those two instructions Jonah had learned to stand very still while everything in them screamed, and to let the screaming be quiet, the way you let a kettle go off the boil by taking it off the fire.

Wren was thirty feet behind, coming up the switchback with the map-roll slung across their back, humming—actually humming, some half-remembered tune from the Hollow Inn, from Maret's fire, from the last warm hour of their lives before everything became running. Jonah couldn't call out without giving away exactly how much Wren mattered. So instead they did what they did best.

They became unremarkable.

It was a strange thing, being invisible on purpose. Most people thought it meant shrinking, hunching, apologizing with your whole posture. But Jonah had learned long ago that invisibility was a kind of abundance—you gave the eye nothing to hold, so the eye moved on. They loosened their shoulders. Let their face go soft and stupid. Became a hired guide, some nobody the cartographer had picked up in a low-town tavern, standing on a bridge because bridges were where guides stood.

Serah wasn't fooled. Of course she wasn't. She'd been hunting them since the archive, and hunters who were good at their work learned to see through exactly this trick—the trick Jonah had spent a lifetime perfecting, turned inside out and held up to the light.

"You walk well," Serah called across the span. Her voice carried oddly over the water, flattened by the roar into something almost gentle. "For someone who's supposed to be dead."

"I get that a lot," Jonah called back.

"Do you?" She took a step onto the bridge. The pines flexed under her weight—a big woman, broad through the shoulders, moving with the deliberate economy of someone who had killed often enough to stop performing it. "I read the reports. The taster who vanished the same night the prophecy book went missing. The guild filed your death papers within the day. Very tidy. Very expensive." Another step. The moss compressed under her boot, darkening with meltwater. "Tidy things bother me. Tidy things mean someone wanted the story closed before I opened it."

"Maybe the story's just boring," Jonah said. "Maybe I stole silverware."

"Maybe." Serah stopped at the bridge's midpoint, ten feet away, close enough now that Jonah could see the frost in her eyelashes, the thin white scar bisecting her left eyebrow. Close enough to smell oiled steel under wool. "But then why does the queen's own spymaster keep asking me about a family name? Eriksen." She said it lightly, the way you'd toss a stone into a well to hear how deep it went. "Ring any bells?"

The name landed in Jonah's chest like a swallowed coal. They kept their face still. They had kept their face still through hemlock and nightshade and the slow gray creep of thallium, and they could keep it still through this.

"Should it?"

"It should ring bells for *you*, I think." Serah tilted her head, studying them with the patience of a woman who had all morning and knew it. "Here's what I know, taster. A hundred years ago, an Eriksen brokered a sale. Not goods. Not information. Something bigger. And a hundred years later, an Eriksen shows up in a prophecy book with tomorrow's date inked beside their face, and then runs for the mountains with the only person alive who can read the deep-court script." She spread her hands. "I'm not a superstitious woman. I don't believe in fate. But I believe in patterns, and this pattern has your face on it."

Behind Jonah, faint beneath the river's roar, the humming had stopped.

*Keep walking,* Jonah willed. *Take the high track. Don't.*

But Wren had stopped, because Wren had seen them—two figures on the bridge, talking—and Wren was not a person who walked past a conversation involving Jonah. That was the trouble with love. It made you legible. Every secret Jonah had ever kept had been safe precisely because no one cared enough to look, and now there was someone who looked always, who read Jonah the way they read contour lines, and Serah Dole could use that the way she'd use a crowbar.

"The queen wants the prophecy fulfilled," Jonah said. Buying time. Moving the conversation the way you moved a blade—slowly, edge angled away from yourself. "You said so yourself, in the archive district. So why kill us? We're doing her work for her."

"The queen wants the prophecy fulfilled *on her terms*." Serah's voice hardened. "A thorn that burns goes down alone, steals back a soul, comes up dead or broken, and the crown gets its hero-story and its shield both. Clean. Contained. What the queen does *not* want is a taster and a cartographer going down together, learning things, coming back with testimony." Her eyes flicked past Jonah's shoulder, toward Wren, and something cold moved through them. "Dead witnesses make the cleanest prophecies."

"And you? What do you want?"

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered behind Serah's expression. Not doubt. Something older and more tired than doubt.

"I want to know what my queen is protecting," she said quietly. "I've served Isolde for nineteen years. Nineteen years of winters that never end, and every year the granaries run shorter and every year the court tells us it's normal, it's the mountain, it's God's weather. And then I find a prophecy book and a sold prince and a family name that's been scraped out of every record in the kingdom like a stain—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice was flat as slate. "I want answers, taster. You're going to give them to me. One way or another."

She lunged.

Later, Jonah would try to reconstruct those seconds and find them mostly gone, worn smooth by speed. What remained was sensation: the flash of steel leaving her coat; the bridge flexing; their own body answering before thought, because that was what nine years of poison had built—not strength, not skill, but *speed of consequence*. A taster who hesitated died. A taster who flinched died. The body that survived was the body that moved first and understood after.

They dropped. Not a dodge—a collapse, knees buckling like a man fainting, and Serah's blade passed through the space where their throat had been and buried itself in the pine trunk with a sound like a struck bell. Jonah rolled, came up inside her reach—inside, where the sword was useless and elbows and thumbs did the work—and drove the heel of their hand up under her jaw. Her teeth cracked together. She staggered. Jonah hit the wrist holding the knife, the same strike they'd used a thousand times on practice dummies soaked in contact toxins, and felt something in it give.

Serah made a sound—not a scream, a grunt of pure professional annoyance—and headbutted them.

The world went white and rang. Jonah's back hit the bridge. Moss and meltwater soaked through their coat, cold as a hand around the spine. Above them Serah loomed, blood threading from her lip, reaching down—

—and then Wren hit her sideways.

It was not graceful. Wren was a cartographer, built of ink and long walks, and they took Serah around the waist like a man tackling a door, and the two of them went down hard on the wet planks. The knife skittered free. Jonah grabbed it—of course they grabbed it, the hand did it on its own—and came up on one knee with the point leveled at Serah's throat as she pinned Wren beneath her, forearm across their collarbone.

Nobody moved. The river roared. Snow fell into all three of their open mouths, so it seemed, melting on Jonah's lips like a taste of something unfinished.

"Off them," Jonah said. "Slowly."

Serah looked at the knife. At Jonah. Something calculating moved behind her eyes, and Jonah recognized it with a lurch, because it was the same arithmetic they performed at every royal meal: *what will this body survive, and what won't it.* She weighed the blade's reach against the distance to her sword, the slope of the bridge against the drop to the river, and Jonah watched her decide—not to surrender, but to trade.

"You need me alive more than I need you," she said. "You're going down there. Into the mountain. And you'll want someone above ground who isn't trying to bury you." She rose off Wren slowly, hands open, and stepped back along the span. Blood dripped from her chin and spotted the snow like scattered berries. "So here's my offer, Eriksen. Take me with you, or take me as an enemy. Those are the choices. Everything else is weather."

"Weather can kill you too," Jonah said.

"Yes," Serah agreed, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand. "It usually does."

Then she moved—not toward them, but sideways, a sudden lunge off the bridge's edge that Jonah thought for one insane heartbeat was a leap into the gorge, until they saw her catch the rope strung along the pines' underside, the smugglers' hand-line, the route Jonah had shown Wren not four days ago with the easy intimacy of shared secrets. She swung down and out, boots finding footholds in the gorge wall, and pulled herself onto the eastern bank with the economy of a woman who had climbed worse things than rivers.

And she took the map-roll with her.

It happened so simply. Wren had come up coughing, and the roll had been knocked loose in the struggle, lying on the planks between them like a dropped loaf, and Serah's hand had closed on it in passing the way a farmer takes an apple off a cart. One motion. No flourish. By the time Jonah's shout tore loose it was already done, already hers, already swinging at her hip as she gained the treeline.

"No—" Wren was on their feet, swaying, reaching for empty air. "That's—that's the working copy, that's the *gate*, I marked the approach, I marked the *root-door*—"

"I know," Jonah said. The fury arrived late, the way it always did, flooding in behind the calm like water behind a dam. Their hands shook around the stolen knife. "I know exactly what she has."

They stood on the bridge in the falling snow, the two of them, breathing hard, the river shouting underneath about how nothing lasts. Wren's lip was split. Jonah's head still rang like the bell of a drowned church. Between them lay the place where the map had been, a rectangle of pressed-down moss already filling with white.

"She'll follow our route," Wren said. Their voice was shaking—not with fear, Jonah realized, but with the specific rage of a maker whose work has been taken and misread. "She'll think the marked gate is real. She'll go to the false door with the crown's keys and the crown's confidence, and she'll open it, and—"

"And wake whatever's behind it." Jonah closed their fist around the knife until the edge bit their palm. The pain helped. Pain always helped; it was the one taste that never lied. "How long do we have?"

Wren stared east, where the trees had already swallowed Serah Dole, where their own careful ink was traveling toward a door that should never be opened by anyone in a hurry.

"If she pushes through the night," they said quietly, "less than a day."

* * *

They moved fast after that, faster than sense allowed, because sense said rest and fear said run and fear had the louder voice. Jonah led them off the bridge and up the western track, the true route, the one that existed nowhere but in Jonah's feet and Wren's memory, and they climbed through the failing light with the storm building behind the peaks like a debt coming due.

Somewhere above the tree line, Wren finally spoke.

"You could have let her take me." It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was a question, asked gently, into the wind. "When she had me down. You could have traded. Given her what she wanted and walked clean."

Jonah kept climbing. The snow thickened. When they answered, they didn't turn around, because some truths were easier said to a mountain than to a face.

"I've spent my whole life being worth nothing to anybody," they said. "It kept me safe. It kept me *alive*. And then you looked at me like I was a map worth reading, and I—" The words snagged. They pushed through. "I'm not trading you. Not to her. Not to the queen. Not to whatever's under the mountain. If that makes me slow, then I'm slow."

The wind took their words and shredded them, but Wren caught the shape of them anyway. A hand found Jonah's shoulder in the dark—cold fingers, steady grip—and squeezed once, and that was answer enough.

Below them, far behind, a thread of torchlight moved along the eastern ridge. Small. Determined. Following the ink.

Jonah watched it for a moment, then turned uphill into the storm, toward the hollow root of the burning thorn, and began to run.
