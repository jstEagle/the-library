## 17. Down the Goat-Track

The storm found them an hour past the ridge, the way storms always did in the Thornfells—not arriving so much as *remembering* itself, as if the mountain had been holding this weather in reserve for a hundred years and had finally decided to spend it all at once.

Jonah felt it first in their teeth. That was the old smuggler's warning, the one no guildmaster ever wrote down: when your fillings ache and the fine hairs on your arms lift, the sky above you is already turning traitor. They stopped on the narrow shelf of rock, Wren a half-step behind with the map case strapped across their chest like a breastplate, and listened.

Wind, low and building. The creak of a stunted pine below. And underneath it all, faint as a whispered secret, the sound of snow beginning to fall somewhere very high up and very far away—falling toward them with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"Jonah." Wren's voice was tight. "Tell me that's not what I think it is."

"It's exactly what you think it is."

"How long do we have?"

Jonah looked up. The clouds had come down to meet them, gray and boiling, swallowing the peaks above like ink poured into water. An hour, maybe two, before the pass became a white throat that swallowed everything else.

"Long enough," they said, "if we're off this ridge before it closes."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we learn to enjoy being buried alive together. I hear it builds character."

Wren laughed—a short, sharp sound with fear inside it—and Jonah loved them for it. That was the thing about Wren that four years of careful anonymity had never managed to sand away: they laughed at the edge of cliffs. They laughed at poison, at pursuit, at prophecy. Not because they weren't afraid, but because laughing was how they refused to let fear have the last word.

"Goat-track?" Wren asked.

"Goat-track."

* * *

There were three ways down from the shoulder of the Thornfells where they stood, and Jonah knew all of them the way you know the lines of your own palm.

The first was the shepherd's road: wide enough for mules, switchbacked and cairned, safe in fair weather and a killing ground in foul. The second was the old quarry cut, straighter but exposed to the full fury of the wind, its ledges glazed with ice by now. Any sensible traveler would take one of those.

Any sensible traveler would die on either of them tonight.

The third way was the goat-track. It wasn't really a track at all—it was a rumor of a path, a seam in the mountainside that the goats had found and the smugglers had stolen and the maps had never once admitted existed. It dropped through a chimney of split rock, traversed a face that looked, from a distance, like sheer cliff, and threaded between outcrops in a series of shelves no wider than a coffin laid sideways. It was sheltered from the wind by the very walls that made it terrifying. It was invisible from above. It was, in short, the only road down the mountain that would still be a road when the storm finished falling.

It was also, Jonah knew, the kind of path that killed people who hesitated.

"Wren." They turned, and took their lover's cold hands in theirs, and made themselves say the next part plainly, because Wren deserved plainness even if the world had never given them any. "I need you to listen to me, and I need you to believe me, because there won't be time to explain twice. This descent is going to be the worst hour of your life. You will be afraid the whole way down, and you will be right to be afraid. But I have taken this path forty times, in weather better than this and weather worse, and I know every handhold on it the way I know your heartbeat. If you stay exactly where I tell you to stay, and move exactly when I tell you to move, we will reach the bottom. Do you understand me?"

Wren's eyes were very wide and very dark in the failing light. Snow had begun to fall now, fat flakes that came down sideways and melted against Jonah's cheeks like small cold kisses.

"I understand," Wren said. Then, because they were Wren: "You've counted my heartbeats?"

"Four years' worth. It's a lot of counting."

"You absolute—" Wren started to laugh again, and then the wind hit them both, a solid shove of frozen air that staggered them into the rock, and the laughter died into something more serious. "All right. All right. Tell me where to put my feet."

* * *

The chimney came first.

It was a crack in the mountain's face, maybe three feet wide, dropping sixty feet through darkness into a pocket of scree. In summer it was a ladder. Tonight, with the first snow already slicking the stone, it was a throat.

Jonah went down it facing outward, back pressed to one wall, boots braced against the other, walking themselves downward in slow, deliberate steps while the storm screamed overhead and the snow fell past them into the dark like ash from a burning sky. Below them, Wren did the same, following Jonah's instructions in a voice pitched to carry over the wind without carrying any further.

"Left foot on the ledge. The one shaped like a kneecap. Yes. Now slide your back down six inches. Don't drop—slide. Good. Again."

They descended like that, inch by inch, two creatures moving down the mountain's gullet while the world above turned white and furious. Jonah counted Wren's breaths instead of their own—counted them the way they'd learned to count everything, precisely, obsessively, because precision was the only prayer a smuggler got to say. Twenty-two breaths to the bottom of the chimney. When Wren's boots finally crunched onto the scree below, Jonah dropped the last four feet after them and caught them before they could stumble.

For one moment, in the pocket of relative calm beneath the chimney's mouth, they simply held each other. Wren was shaking—from cold or fear or both—and their breath came in ragged white plumes, and snow was matting their dark hair flat against their skull, and Jonah thought, with a clarity that felt almost like grief: *I would carry this person down every mountain in the world.*

"We're a third of the way," they said. "The hard part's behind us."

"That's a lie," Wren said into their collar. "Cartographers can smell a lie about terrain."

"The hard part is mostly behind us. The rest is just... differently hard."

"Wonderful."

* * *

The traverse was next, and the traverse was where the mountain demanded its toll.

The face of rock they had to cross was not vertical—that would have been simpler, honestly, because vertical is honest. It leaned out over a drop of several hundred feet, a bulge of granite like the brow of some sleeping giant, and the path across it was a shelf barely wider than Jonah's boot, sloping outward, dusted now with an inch of new snow that the wind kept trying to strip away and keep trying to replace.

In clear weather, Jonah crossed it without thinking. In this, they crossed it like a man crossing a frozen river over black water—one deliberate step at a time, weight always over the uphill foot, three points of contact maintained at all times, eyes on the stone and never on the void.

And behind them, Wren followed.

This was the part Jonah had dreaded—not for themselves, but for the moment when Wren's nerve would break, because on a shelf like this, nerve breaking was not a metaphor. Nerve breaking meant freezing. Freezing meant the person behind couldn't pass. Freezing meant death by inches while the storm laughed.

Halfway across, it happened.

"I can't—" Wren's voice, very small. Jonah turned their head carefully, carefully, and saw their lover pressed flat against the rock face, fingers hooked into a crack, one boot on the shelf and the other hovering, unable to commit. Snow whirled around them. Below, the drop fell away into gray nothing. "Jonah, I can't feel my hands, I can't—"

"Yes, you can." Jonah kept their voice level. Low. The voice they used on spooked animals and nervous customs men. "Look at me. Not down. At me."

Wren's eyes found theirs. Wide. Terrified. Trusting.

"There's a crack at your waist, on your left. Put your left hand in it. Now. Good. Now bring your right foot forward six inches, onto the darker stone—the dark stone has grip, the pale stone lies. Six inches, Wren. That's all the world is asking of you. Six inches."

Step by step, six inches at a time, Jonah talked Wren across the face of the mountain. It took twenty minutes that felt like twenty years. The storm built around them until the world was nothing but white noise and white blindness, and the only things that existed were the shelf, the rock, the two of them, and Jonah's voice—steady, patient, relentless—describing the next six inches, and the next, and the next.

When Wren's boots finally touched the broad ledge at the far end, their legs gave out and Jonah caught them, and for a moment they knelt there together on the stone while Wren shook and breathed and shook some more.

"I'm sorry," Wren gasped. "I'm sorry, I froze, I—"

"Don't you dare apologize." Jonah pulled back far enough to look at them. Snow had gotten under Wren's hood; their lips were going blue-gray; there was a raw patch on their cheek where the rock had scraped them. "You crossed a face that kills shepherds in good weather. You froze, and then you unfroze, and then you crossed it. That's not failure. That's the whole job."

"My hands—"

"Give them here." Jonah took Wren's hands and worked them, chafing them, pressing them inside their own coat against their chest, where the blood-warm hollow between ribs and arm held heat like a banked fire. "We'll rest here five minutes. Not six. Then the last stretch."

"Jonah." Wren's voice had gone strange. "My ankle. When I slipped just now—I didn't want to say it up there. I think I've done something to it."

* * *

The word *carrying* is a simple word. Two syllables. Everyone knows what it means.

Nobody knows what it means until they've done it down a mountain in a blizzard.

Jonah rigged the carry from everything they had: Wren's belt looped through their own, Wren's arms over their shoulders, Wren's good leg taking what little weight it could on the gentler stretches while Jonah bore the rest. Wren was not heavy—they were all cartographer's lean muscle and ink-stained fingers—but weight means nothing on a mountain. On a mountain, ten pounds becomes fifty, fifty becomes a hundred, and gravity stops being a law and becomes an adversary with opinions.

The last stretch of the goat-track dropped through a forest of wind-twisted pines, and here at least the trees broke the wind, and here at least the snow fell straight down instead of sideways, and here Jonah put their head down and simply *worked*.

Step. Breathe. Step. Shift Wren's weight. Step. Find the next hold in the dark. Breathe. The storm roared above the treeline like a river in flood. Somewhere behind and above them, the pass they'd abandoned was closing like a wound. Step.

"Put me down," Wren said, for the fourth time.

"No."

"Jonah. Your legs are shaking."

"My legs always shake. It's how they work."

"That's not—you're lying to a cartographer about terrain again."

"Then let me lie. It's the only map I've got."

He felt rather than saw Wren smile against their shoulder. They went on. The pines thickened, and the snow underfoot gave way to needle-fall, and the grade eased, and Jonah began—slowly, cautiously, the way you test thin ice—to believe in the valley.

It was full dark when they heard the water.

A stream, running loud and open beneath its skin of new ice—the surest sign of lower altitude, because streams that run under the ice are streams fed by springs, and springs live in valleys. Ten minutes later the trees opened onto the floor of a side-canyon, and there, hunched against the base of an overhang, was the thing Jonah had been praying toward for six hours without letting themselves name it: a smuggler's cache, a dry-stone hut no bigger than a grave, roofed with slabs of turf, invisible from thirty feet away in any direction.

Home. Or near enough.

Jonah got them both inside, got Wren onto the pile of cached blankets, got a fire going from the sealed tinderbox and the split pine stacked by some nameless predecessor whose debt they would honor someday. They bound Wren's ankle with strips of linen and snowmelt and the particular tenderness of someone who has set broken fingers on themselves more than once. They fed Wren dried meat and hot water and watched color come slowly back into their face.

Outside, the storm howled on, burying the goat-track, erasing them, keeping them safe.

"Jonah." Wren's voice was drowsy, firelit, fraying at the edges toward sleep. "That face we crossed. Has anyone else ever done it? In a storm like this?"

"No."

"So the map of tonight—there's only one copy."

Jonah lay down beside them, pulling the blankets over them both, fitting their body along Wren's the way a key fits a lock.

"There's only one copy," they agreed. "And I'm looking at it."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and burrowed closer. "When we get out of this, I'm drawing it anyway. Every ledge. Every six inches. So the next person doesn't have to be you."

"The next person shouldn't be on that mountain at all."

"The next person might be someone like you. Someone who needs a way down that nobody knows." A pause. The fire ticked. Wind worried the turf roof. "Someone like us."

Jonah didn't answer. They lay listening to Wren's breathing slow toward sleep, counting the beats of it out of habit, out of love, out of the same stubborn precision that had carried them both down the mountain—and it was only as sleep finally took them too, heavy and total as snowfall, that they registered the other sound.

Faint. Distant. Wrong.

Down the canyon, in the direction of the valley and the low country and everything they'd fled—someone was ringing a bell. Over and over. Patient as winter.

A hunter's bell.
