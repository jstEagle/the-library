## 18. The Door Beneath the Root

I had never been carried before.

It was a strange thing to learn about oneself at the bottom of a mountain, in the blue hour between storm and silence, with one's leg folded wrong beneath one and the world reduced to the rhythm of Jonah's breathing against my ear. They moved through the snow like something born to it—shoulders low, weight rolling from hip to hip, each step placed rather than taken. I had spent my life believing that maps were the highest form of knowledge a person could hold of a place. Lying in Jonah's arms while they read the mountain by feel, I understood I had been worshipping ink.

"Talk to me," Jonah said. Their voice came out scraped, as if the wind had gotten inside it. "If you stop talking, I'll think you've fainted, and then I'll have to stop, and we'll freeze."

"I'm weighing our options," I said.

"We don't have any."

"Then I'm weighing them slowly."

A huff that might have been laughter, quickly swallowed. The storm had spent itself against the upper crags and come down here only as a fine, needling sleet, the kind that didn't fall so much as arrive sideways, patient as an archivist. Above us the sky was the color of old pewter. Below us—the way we'd come—a slope of scree and ice that would have killed anyone who tried it without Jonah's particular education in the Thornfells' cruelty.

My ankle was broken. I knew this the way I knew a mislabeled border: not because anyone had told me but because everything on that side of my body now disagreed with everything else. Jonah had bound it with a strip torn from my own coat lining and two straight branches of blackthorn, their hands quick and unsentimental, and when they'd finished they'd looked at me with an expression I hadn't seen on them before. Not pity. Something more like apology—as though my pain were a debt they had personally incurred.

"You should leave me," I had said, because it seemed honest to say it.

"You should be quiet," they had said, "before I remember all the reasons I like you," and then they'd picked me up as though I weighed no more than a roll of vellum, and we had gone down into the last of the light.

* * *

The valley, when we reached it, was not on any map I had ever drawn or stolen or bought.

That deserves saying plainly, because I have made maps of places no crown official has ever seen. I have charted smuggler roads that exist only in the memories of people who would kill to keep them unrecorded. I thought I knew the shape of the gaps in the kingdom's knowledge the way a priest knows the shape of his god's silences. But this valley—this narrow throat of stone wedged between two shoulders of the Thornfells, floored in snow that lay undrifted and smooth as fresh paper—was not a gap. It was an absence. As if the cartographers who had come before me had all, without coordinating, agreed to look away.

"There," Jonah said, and set me down against a boulder with a gentleness that hurt worse than roughness would have. "Do you see it?"

At first I saw nothing but trees. Blackthorn, hundreds of them, crowding the valley floor in a dense and vicious thicket, their branches bare and hooked, their bark the gray of drowned wood. The sleet had glazed every thorn in ice so that the whole grove glittered faintly, a field of frozen needles, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful when you're looking down its length.

Then I saw the tree.

It stood at the center of the thicket the way a king stands at the center of a court—not because it was largest, though it was, enormously, but because everything else oriented toward it. The other blackthorns grew in rings around it, younger generations leaning inward like courtiers straining to hear. And the tree itself—I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only prayer I have left, and I am writing this by firelight knowing what waits below—but the tree itself was hollow.

Not broken. Not dead. Hollow, the way a bell is hollow: deliberately, resonantly, *purposefully*. Its trunk was wide enough that six people joining hands could not have circled it, and somewhere in the long centuries of its life its heartwood had gone, whether rotted or removed or given away, leaving a chamber within the living bark. The opening faced us across the snow. It was tall as a doorway and arched at the top, and the edges of it were smooth as furniture, worn—or polished—by touch.

And it was warm. I could see that from thirty paces. No snow fell inside the arch. No ice glazed the wood around the opening. The air above it shimmered very slightly, the way air shimmers over a forge, and where the tree's shadow should have lain across the snow there was instead a circle of bare, dark earth, steaming gently, green things sprouting at its rim in the middle of winter as though the ground beneath had forgotten what season it was.

"The burning thorn," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, small in all that white silence. "Jonah. The prophecy. *Where the thorn burns cold, the thief goes down.*"

"I know." Jonah crouched beside me, and I watched them look at the tree with an expression I couldn't parse—hunger and horror in equal measure, the face of a starving person shown a feast they already know will cost them. "I know what it says. I've had a hundred years of prophecy recited at me since Wren found my face in that book. I can recite it in my sleep. That doesn't make it any easier to walk toward."

* * *

We rested first. Jonah insisted, and for once I didn't argue, because I had learned in the last three days that arguing with Jonah Eriksen about survival was like arguing with water about which way downhill lies. They built a fire in the lee of the boulder—small, smokeless, fed on resin-rich blackthorn twigs they stripped from the nearest trees—and melted snow for us to drink, and forced me to eat the last of the dried plums from their pack even though they were bruised and half-frozen and tasted of smoke.

While I ate, they worked. That was the thing about Jonah that I think outsiders never understood, the thing the guild had built into them so deep it had become indistinguishable from the bone: they could not stop preparing. Even now, at the edge of the thing the prophecy had promised, with a broken-legged lover and a stolen map in Serah Dole's hands and a kingdom's worth of hunters behind us, Jonah checked the edges of their knives, tested the seals on their poison vials, counted and recounted the small tools of their trade with the calm devotion of a monk at office. Their body was a ledger, they'd told me once. Every scar an entry. Every immunity a line of credit against a future withdrawal.

"What are you thinking?" I asked.

They didn't look up from the vial they were stoppering. "I'm thinking that Serah has my map. Or your map. Whichever. She'll take it to the false gate—the one under the glacier, the one your sources described—and she'll open it, because Serah Dole has never in her life met a door she didn't believe she was entitled to open." A pause. The stopper went in with a soft click. "And whatever's behind the false gate is going to wake up angry."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it isn't." They did look up then, and the firelight moved in their eyes, and for a moment all the careful blankness they wore like armor slipped, and underneath it was just a tired person, mid-thirties, frostbitten at the ears, terrified. "I'm thinking that when I was nine years old the guild taught me that the safest place in the world is the middle of nowhere, and I have spent twenty years making myself into the middle of nowhere, and tomorrow—" They stopped. Started again. "Tomorrow I stop being nowhere. Whatever happens after that door, I stop being nobody. And I find that I'm grieving it. Isn't that obscene? I'm grieving my own invisibility like it was a friend."

"It was a friend," I said. "It kept you alive."

"Yes." They fed a twig to the fire. "And friends ask terrible prices too."

* * *

We went to the tree at first light, or what passes for first light under a storm sky—which is to say the darkness loosened its grip just enough to let us see how much trouble we were in.

Jonah carried me the last hundred yards. I had wanted to hop, to lean, to preserve some dignity of self-locomotion, but the snow between the outer thicket and the great tree lay over hidden roots and sinkholes, and after my second fall Jonah simply gathered me up without comment and carried me the rest of the way, and I put my arm around their neck and let myself be freight. There is a particular intimacy in being carried by someone who has spent their life ensuring they cannot be caught carrying anything. Every step said *you*. Nothing else needed to.

Up close, the tree was worse. Better. Worse and better.

The bark of it was not gray up close but a deep, blood-dark brown, almost black, and it was warm to the touch—I pressed my palm flat against it and felt heat radiating out of the wood itself, steady as a sleeping animal's flank. The warmth had a pulse to it. Very slow. Very deep. Once every dozen breaths, the trunk seemed to swell fractionally and subside, and I told myself it was imagination, and then I made Jonah press their hand to the same spot, and I watched their face change, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

The carved arch of the doorway rose above us, and here at last was evidence of hands—ancient hands, human ones, I was nearly sure. Someone had shaped this opening. Someone had cut away the heartwood of a living tree and left it standing, and they had done it with such care that the inner walls of the hollow were smooth as the inside of a violin. Faint marks still showed in the wood, spiraling down into the dark below the threshold: script, or near-script, characters worn almost to suggestion, older than any alphabet I could name. I traced one with my eyes and felt the vertigo of the scholar confronted with the thing scholarship was invented to avoid: knowledge that predates the instruments of knowing.

"It's warm all the way down," Jonah said quietly. They had leaned into the hollow, one hand braced on the doorframe, peering into the dark. "There are steps. Cut into the root-wood. They go down further than the light reaches."

"How far?"

"Far." They drew back. Their face had gone carefully neutral again, which I had learned to read as *very bad indeed.* "Wren. Before we go in. If this goes wrong—if the door does something, if the toll is what the stories say—"

"Don't."

"—I need you to know that the summer at Lake Vessel, the one with the ferryman who sang off-key, that was the best month of my life, and I don't regret a single day of what it cost me to be there—"

"*Don't,*" I said again, sharper than I meant to, and grabbed their collar with both hands, and pulled them down until our foreheads touched. Their skin was cold from the wind and hot from the tree-warmth at once, and their breath came fast. "You are not allowed to give me a eulogy on a threshold, Jonah Eriksen. You are not allowed to say goodbye in past tense. We go in together, we come out together, and you can eulogize me later, at length, in iambic pentameter, if you must, but you will do it to my face while I argue with every line."

A breath. Another. Then, muffled against my mouth, half laugh: "I don't write in meter."

"No," I said. "You really don't."

* * *

The threshold itself was a hand's width of exposed root-wood at the base of the arch, and crossing it was like crossing the surface of a lake.

I felt it the instant Jonah's boot came down—a shudder running up through my own body where I hung against their shoulder, a deep subsonic tremor, as though somewhere far below a vast instrument had been struck a single soft note. The warmth surged. The spiral script on the inner walls flickered—there is no other word for it; the worn carvings brightened with a light that came from inside the wood itself, a dull ember-glow, red-gold, rising and falling in time with that slow pulse we had felt in the bark. The burning thorn. Burning from within. Cold fire, Old Maret had called it, the night she told us about Alaric Vane, and I had thought it a figure of speech, and here it was, lighting our way down.

Steps cut into root-wood, spiraling into the earth. The air below smelled of loam and hot iron and, faintly, unbearably, of summer—of cut grass and ripening fruit, the whole buried memory of the growing season, exhaled from the dark like a secret kept too long.

Jonah stopped on the third step and turned back to look at me, and the ember-light moved over their face and made them, for a moment, a stranger: someone ancient and appointed, marked, named in books a century before their birth. Then their eyes found mine and they were only Jonah again, my smuggler, my taster, my impossible love, and they held out their hand.

"Stay close," they said. "And Wren—if you hear your own name coming out of the dark, don't answer it."

"Why? What answers?"

"I don't know," Jonah said. "But doors like this one always keep something on the landing."

Below us, far down in the warm dark, something shifted—a sound like stone settling against stone, like a lock turning over after a hundred years of sleep. The ember-light along the walls brightened, steadied, and began, slowly, patiently, to move downward ahead of us, lighting the steps one by one, the way a servant lights a guest along a corridor.

The door had opened. The door had known we were coming.

And from the depths rose a voice—not heard so much as felt, rising through the soles of my boots and the bones of my broken leg and the marrow of everything I was:

***What do you bring to pay?***
