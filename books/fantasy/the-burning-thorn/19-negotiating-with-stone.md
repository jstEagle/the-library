## 19. Negotiating With Stone

The thorn-tree breathed.

Jonah had no better word for it. The hollowed trunk rose out of the mountainside like a ribcage picked clean by centuries of wind, its black wood veined with something that glowed faintly when the storm-light passed through the gap above—a slow pulse, in and out, in and out, as though the mountain itself were sleeping and this was the place where its heart came closest to the skin. Snow hissed against the bark and did not land. It turned aside, the way water turns at the lip of a waterfall, and fell elsewhere. Inside the hollow, the air was still and warm and smelled of struck flint and old honey.

Wren leaned against Jonah's shoulder, weight more than they should have carried after the descent—the ankle bound tight with strips of Jonah's own shirt, the fever-bright look in their eyes that Jonah kept pretending not to see because seeing it would mean stopping, and stopping was death on this slope. Behind them, somewhere up in the white roar, Serah Dole was picking her way down toward the false gate with a stolen map and a crown's certainty. They had hours, perhaps. Perhaps less.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Wren whispered. Their breath fogged and hung in the still air far longer than breath should.

"I don't know what it's supposed to do." Jonah stepped closer to the inner wall of the hollow, where the grain of the wood swirled into patterns that were almost—almost—writing. "I know what Maret said. *The door beneath the root keeps its own accounts.*"

"Maret also said the Eriksens were the ones who built the debt," Wren said, gently, the way you set down something sharp. "You don't have to be the one who pays it."

"Yes, I do." Jonah said it without heat, the way they'd say *the queen takes supper at seven* or *the third stair creaks*. Fact. Ledger-line. "That's the whole shape of it, Wren. My family sold a man's soul to buy a kingdom a hundred years of winter. The guild raised me like a tool and threw me away the moment I became interesting. The queen wants me dead or useful, whichever's cheaper. Everything I am was decided before I was born." They pressed their palm flat against the warm black wood. "This is the first door I've ever walked up to on purpose."

The wood answered.

It was not sound, exactly. It was pressure—a low thrum that Jonah felt in their teeth, in the healed-over scars inside their elbows where a hundred venoms had been folded into their blood like grim embroidery, in the socket of the eye. And beneath the thrum, words assembled themselves out of it the way frost assembles ferns on glass: not heard so much as *recognized*, arriving whole in the mind as if they had always been there and had only just been noticed.

**WHO KNOCKS.**

Jonah glanced at Wren. Wren's face had gone the color of the snow beyond the hollow, but they nodded once: *I hear it too.*

"Jonah," Jonah said aloud. Then, because lying to a door seemed unwise, "Jonah Eriksen."

The silence that followed had texture. It had *weight*. Somewhere far below them—farther than the mountain had any right to be deep—something vast shifted in its sleep, and the pulse of light in the wood stuttered like a candle in a draft.

**ERIKSEN,** said the door, and the word was cold all the way through, colder than the storm outside, colder than the winter that had fallen on the kingdom a century ago and never finished falling. **THE SELLERS' BLOOD RETURNS TO THE ACCOUNT.**

"I've come to settle it," Jonah said. "Not to add to it."

**ALL WHO PASS ADD.** The thrum deepened. In the swirling grain of the wood, patterns began to move—not writing now but images, faint as breath on glass. A bridge over a river gone silver with ice. A kitchen with bread rising under a cloth. A woman laughing, her face turned away, unknowable. **THE TOLL OF THE THORN-DOOR IS MEMORY. FREELY GIVEN. FREELY TAKEN. NAME WHAT YOU WILL PART WITH, AND IT IS NAMED FOREVER.**

Beside Jonah, Wren made a small sound—not quite a word. Jonah reached back without looking and found their hand. Cold fingers. Steadier than they had any right to be.

"How much?" Jonah asked. "For two to pass?"

The door considered. Jonah felt it considering, the way you feel a taster's pause at the table—the held breath, the weighing tongue. When the answer came, it came not in words but in sensation: a hollowing, a subtraction, an arithmetic performed directly on the soul. Enough to make the knees soften. Not enough to empty. A purse opened, not a life spent.

"One summer," Jonah translated aloud, because saying it made it real and real things could be bargained with. "One season's worth of remembering. From each of us."

"No." Wren's grip tightened hard enough to hurt. "No, Jonah, listen—you don't know which summer it'll take. You can't choose. What if it takes—" They stopped. They didn't have to finish. *What if it takes the archive. What if it takes the night we met. What if it takes me.*

"It doesn't work like that." Jonah was surprised by how certain their own voice sounded. "Listen to it. It's not a thief, it's a *toll-keeper*. It wants fair payment, honestly weighed. That's why my family used this door in the first place, isn't it? Because the deep court cheats and the crown cheats, but stone only counts." They turned to face Wren fully, and the glow of the wood lit them both from below, making ghosts of their faces. "Wren. If we turn back, Serah opens the false gate with your map, and whatever she wakes comes up behind us angry. If we go forward, we find Alaric Vane before the winter finishes falling. Those are the roads. There are no smuggler paths past this point."

"You always say that," Wren whispered. "'There are no other roads.' You say it like it comforts you."

"It does." Jonah smiled, and meant it, and hated how thin it must have looked. "It means I stopped having to pretend there were choices I wasn't brave enough to make."

* * *

They argued for another quarter hour, there in the breathing dark of the tree, while the storm outside buried the world and the door waited with the patience of geology. Jonah let Wren argue. It was a kindness, they understood—letting someone exhaust every road that didn't exist before walking the one that did. Wren raged quietly about maps, about how a person was not a ledger, about how memory was the only country anyone truly owned. Jonah agreed with all of it. Agreement changed nothing. The mountain did not care what they believed about ownership; the mountain had been keeping accounts since before there were kingdoms to cheat it.

In the end Wren went first, because Wren insisted, because Wren had spent their whole life drawing doors other people walked through and wanted, just once, to be the one who opened one.

They pressed both palms to the wood and closed their eyes. "Take what's fair," they said. "And be *gentle*, damn you."

The door took.

Jonah watched it happen and would spend years afterward trying to describe it. Wren did not cry out. They simply... dimmed. Like a lamp turned down a hand's width. Their mouth moved around some word that never arrived; their eyes tracked something across the inside of their own darkness—a summer, a season, a room, a face—and then released it. When they swayed backward, Jonah caught them, and Wren blinked up with the same eyes, the same fierce cartographer's squint, and said, hoarsely:

"There's a hole. Right there. I can feel the edges of it. Like a tooth gone from my mouth." A shaky laugh. "I don't know what it was. That's the worst part. I miss something and I'll never know what."

"The door knows," Jonah said. "Fair payment, honestly weighed. It'll keep it safe. That's what toll-keepers do—they hold what's given, they don't destroy it."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe it's the best price we're going to get."

Wren gripped Jonah's collar and pulled them close, forehead to forehead, breath mingling in the warm dark. "Whatever it takes from you," Wren said, "it doesn't get everything. Whatever's left, I'll carry. That's the arrangement. That's the map."

Then it was Jonah's turn.

* * *

They faced the wood alone—or as alone as one can be with the person they love holding their shoulders from behind, anchoring them like a rope-anchor on a cliff face. The thrum rose to meet them. The images in the grain sharpened, circling, patient: the ice-silvered bridge again, the kitchen again, the laughing woman with her face turned away.

**NAME IT NOT,** said the door. **IT IS ALREADY WEIGHED.**

And Jonah understood, with the particular horror of the expert recognizing mastery in another's hands, that the door had already chosen—that it had read them the way Jonah read a dish, tongue to the poison, knowing in an instant what was harmless and what was lethal and what was merely bitter. It had sifted a lifetime of memories and found the one whose loss would cost exactly what the passage cost, no more, no less. Fair payment. Honestly weighed.

The laughing woman turned her head.

And Jonah remembered.

Not the memory itself—those were already going, sliding away like snowmelt finding a crack in the stone—but the *shape* of the remembering, the last instant of possession before the purse closed. A summer. Of course it was a summer; the body knew summers, had hoarded them against all the winters of a poisoned childhood. A farmstead in the foothills, before the guild, before the palace, before the name Eriksen became a thing to whisper instead of say. A grandmother—no, an aunt, someone's hands in someone's hair—cutting early apples under a tree that hummed with bees. The smell of crushed grass and cider vinegar. A dog, yellow, enormous, gentle as weather. Long gold evenings when the light lay across the valley like spilled honey and nobody asked Jonah to taste anything, to be anything, to carry anything except the apples from the basket to the basin, which was a good and sufficient purpose for a child to have.

A whole season of being loved without being *used*.

That was what the door had found. Not the most precious memory—Jonah had those, guarded deeper, the nights with Wren, the first true map drawn in the dust of a smuggler's track—but the most *irreplaceable*. The one that could never be remade, because everyone in it was dead and the orchard was surely gone under a hundred winters of snow. The one whose absence would leave a tooth-shaped hole Jonah would feel with their tongue for the rest of their life and never be able to name.

The door was honest. That was the terrible thing. It had priced fairly.

"Wait," Jonah heard themselves say, and their voice sounded very young. "Wait, I—"

But their hands were already on the wood, and the wood was already drinking.

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest mercy of it. It felt like falling asleep in summer—like the long gold light thickening, sweetening, settling over the eyes. The bees' hum faded first, then the dog's weight against their leg, then the apple's green snap between the teeth, then the voice calling them in from the grass, and last of all the light itself, the spilled-honey evening, draining away down some crack in the world that Jonah would spend the rest of their days running their fingers along without ever finding.

Then it was done, and Jonah was kneeling on the warm floor of a hollow tree with Wren's arms around them, and there was a hole in them shaped like a summer, and they could not remember what color the light had been.

"Gone?" Wren asked softly.

"Gone." Jonah tested the edges of it, the way Wren had. Tooth-shaped. Clean. "I know I lost something. I know it mattered. I can feel how much it mattered, like heat off a stove after the fire's out." A breath that shook more than they wanted it to. "I don't know what it was."

"That's the arrangement," Wren murmured into their hair. "Whatever's left, I carry. Tell me what you feel."

"Warm. Angry. Grateful. Like I paid a debt I owed someone whose face I can't recall." Jonah laughed, wet and short. "Which is fitting, I suppose. My whole family's fortune was built on debts other people couldn't remember signing."

Before Wren could answer, the door spoke again—and this time it was not a demand but a sound like a great lock turning, like a mountain clearing its throat, like the first note of a song a hundred years silent.

**THE ACCOUNT STANDS OPENED,** said the stone-thorn. **TWO SOULS PAID. TWO SOULS PASS. AND HEAR THIS, CHILDREN OF THE SELLERS:** the thrum dropped low, grave as a funeral bell, **WHAT WAS SOLD BELOW WAS SOLD THROUGH YOU, AND WHAT YOU SEEK BELOW REMEMBERS YOUR NAME BETTER THAN YOU NOW REMEMBER YOUR SUMMER. GO DOWN. GO QUIETLY. AND WHEN THE PALE REGENT OFFERS YOU COMFORT—**

The light in the wood pulsed once, twice, and stilled.

**—REMEMBER THAT COMFORT IS ALSO A PRICE.**

The inner wall of the hollow split along a seam no eye had ever seen, and the heartwood swung inward on hinges of living grain, exhaling a breath of air from far below: cold, mineral, ancient, threaded with that same faint sweetness of old honey. Stairs descended into the dark, cut from the tree's own roots, each step worn smooth in the centers by feet that had passed this way a hundred years ago and never come back.

Jonah stood. Their legs held. The hole where the summer had been ached like weather coming, and they found, to their surprise, that they could stand inside the ache and look at it squarely, the way they'd learned to stand inside pain at the tasting table—cataloguing it, naming it, refusing to let it decide anything for them.

*So that's the cost,* they thought. *Now I know the coin. Now I know what the rest of the journey spends.*

Wren took their hand. Below, the stairs went down farther than the lamplight of the glowing veins could follow, down toward the mirrored halls and the cold fire and the worn-thin prince who barely remembered his own name. Down toward the Pale Regent, and comfort offered at a price.

"Ready?" Wren asked.

"No," Jonah said honestly. And stepped onto the first stair, into the dark that remembered their family's name, carrying nothing but what remained.
