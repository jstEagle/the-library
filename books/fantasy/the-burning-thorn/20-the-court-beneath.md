## 20. The Court Beneath

The gate closed behind them like a held breath finally released, and Wren's first thought was that they had stepped inside a drawing.

Not a map—Wren knew maps the way other people knew their own hands, and this was not cartography. It was a *drawing*, the kind an apprentice makes when asked to render the palace from memory and gets everything almost right: the proportions subtly wrong, the shadows falling in directions no sun could command. The corridor stretched ahead of them in pale stone veined with something dark—not marble's grey threading but black filaments that branched and forked like frost on glass, or like roots, or like the inked lines of veins in an anatomist's plate.

"It's the palace," Jonah said quietly. Their voice came back wrong, flattened, as though the air itself were wool. "The throne hall corridor. The one I walk every morning to reach the queen's table."

It was. Wren had never seen the palace above except in commissioned plans and stolen glimpses over garden walls, but even they recognized it—the double colonnade, the ribbed vaulting, the long sweep of floor polished to mirror-brightness. Except here the columns were carved inside-out, ornamentation sinking *into* the stone rather than rising from it, so that acanthus leaves bloomed in negative, hollowed into the rock like wounds. And the floor did not reflect them.

Wren looked down and saw, instead of their own face, a faint suggestion of the mountain's underside—a vast concavity of stone hung with what might have been stalactites or might have been teeth, receding into darkness below the darkness they stood in. A map of where they were going, drawn by no human hand.

"Cartography as threat," they murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing." Wren touched Jonah's sleeve. "Keep talking. I want to hear if the echoes stay strange."

They walked. The cold fire began at the second turning.

Wren had no better name for it. Lamps burned in niches along the walls—or things shaped like lamps, or things shaped like the *idea* of lamps—and each held a flame that gave light without warmth, a blue-white radiance like moonlight compressed until it hurt. The flames did not flicker. They did not move at all. Each one was a still portrait of fire, and standing before one felt like being watched by a painting of an eye.

Jonah reached toward the nearest flame and stopped, fingers curling back. "Cold," they said. "I can feel it from here. Cold like—" They hesitated, and Wren watched them search for the word with the careful expression Jonah wore when a poison's name sat on the tip of the tongue. "Like the winter above. Like it's the same cold."

"The pact," Wren said. "A hundred years of it. Maybe the winter up there is just... runoff. What leaks through the seal."

They descended by degrees, the corridor spiraling down in great slow turns, and with each turn the architecture grew more beautiful and more wrong. Wren had spent their life among drawings of beautiful things and had learned to read beauty the way Jonah read flavor—for what lay beneath it, for the bitterness under the honey. This place was beautiful the way a lure is beautiful. Every proportion invited. Every line drew the eye deeper, further down, the way a flower draws the bee without any part of it caring what happens to the bee after.

"I hate how much I like it," Jonah admitted at the fourth turn. Their voice had gone rough. "That's the trap, isn't it? You're supposed to want to stay."

"You've been drugged before," Wren said. "You know this feeling."

"I know this feeling exactly. It's hemlock with good manners." Jonah flexed their hands, rolled their shoulders, the small ritual Wren had watched them perform a hundred times in the palace kitchens—grounding the body against the mind's betrayals. "Stay close. If I start saying I want to live here, hit me."

"If you start saying you want to live here," Wren said, "I'll say it louder, and we'll hit each other."

Something that was almost a laugh passed between them, and the corridor seemed to tighten around the sound, displeased.

* * *

The court announced itself first by sound.

Music, far below—strings, or something like strings, notes drawn out long and slow as sap. Then voices, many voices, layered in a murmur like wind through wheat. Then a smell: not decay, nothing so honest. It was the smell of a feast laid out in an empty house, food going elegant and strange in the absence of anyone to eat it. Roast meat and candied peel and wine gone to vinegar, all of it sweetened over with something floral that made the back of Wren's throat ache.

Then the corridor opened, and there it was.

The deep court spread beneath a vaulted ceiling so high its details dissolved into gloom, hung with chandeliers of ice or crystal or frozen water-light, each blazing with the motionless cold flame. A hall mirrored to the throne room above—but inverted, Wren realized, in more than architecture. Where the queen's hall ran east to west with the dawn behind the throne, this hall ran north to south, its dais at the deep end, facing *up*, as though the throne here were oriented toward the world it had been cut off from. As though someone down here had spent a century looking at the ceiling of the world.

And the courtiers—

Wren stopped walking. Jonah's hand found theirs, grip tight, warning: *don't stare*. But it was impossible not to stare, and worse, it was impossible to say precisely what was wrong.

The courtiers were beautiful. That was the first thing, the overwhelming thing: faces like coins struck in silver, hair in colors that belonged to autumn and midnight, garments that moved like poured ink and frozen smoke. They danced without music matching their steps, or perhaps the music matched them and Wren simply couldn't hear the seam. They conversed in clusters, laughing at intervals so perfectly timed that the laughter sounded rehearsed—which it would have been, Wren thought wildly, if these people had been performing courtliness for a hundred years with no one new to perform it for.

And every face was borrowed.

Wren understood it gradually, the way one understands a trick of perspective. First one courtier turned, smiling, and Wren thought: *I know that face.* A merchant who kept a stall near the archive. Then another: a guard captain from the palace roster. Then a third, a fourth, a dozen—faces Wren had catalogued in passing, faces of the living city above, worn here like masks worn slightly out of true. The smiles lasted a half-second too long. The eyes didn't crinkle where the mouths curved. One woman laughed and her laughter was a perfect copy of a sound Wren had heard in a market square years ago, note for note, and seeing it now on this cold beautiful face was like hearing a beloved song played on an instrument made of teeth.

"They're wearing us," Wren breathed. "Jonah. They're wearing *us*."

"I see it." Jonah's voice had gone flat and careful, the taster's voice, the voice they used when a dish had to be pronounced safe and wasn't. "Don't react. Whatever you recognize, don't react. If they know what we know about them, we lose the only advantage we have—we look like we belong."

"We don't belong."

"No," Jonah agreed. "But we can look like we're expected."

* * *

They were expected. That was the horror of it.

Before they had crossed a quarter of the hall, three courtiers detached from their clusters and converged with the smooth inevitability of water finding a drain. The foremost wore the face of a young man Wren had once seen hanged for smuggling—had seen, because the guild had required all its runners to attend, as instruction. The face smiled with the hanged man's mouth and none of his terror.

"Guests," it said. The voice was lovely and toneless, like a bell rung under water. "Above-guests. Warm guests." It inclined its head, and the gesture was flawless, copied from some courtier above, some ambassador, some lord. "You are come to the court. How long has it been since the court had guests."

Not a question. The sentences arrived without their grammar quite attached, as though language itself were something these people had learned secondhand, from listening through stone.

"We're passing through," Jonah said evenly. "On business."

"Business." The courtier tasted the word. Its companions leaned in, and Wren saw that all three were breathing—of course they were breathing, chests rising and falling—but the breaths were not synchronized with anything. Not with speech, not with movement. Breathing as decoration. "The last ones who came on business," it said, "were a hundred years ago. They sold. We bought. Business concluded." The smile widened by a precise fraction. "Is your business concluded also?"

Wren's heart slammed against their ribs. Beside them, Jonah had gone very still in the way that meant danger-assessed, options-weighed. "Our business is our own," Jonah said. "As yours is yours."

"Oh, ours is everyone's," the courtier said pleasantly. "Ours is the oldest business. Hunger." It said the word without menace, which made it infinitely worse—said it the way a man says *Tuesday*. "But you are guests, and guests are fed before they are eaten. Come. The Regent holds audience at the turn of the hour, and you must be presented. And first—" the silver face glowed in the cold-fire light, "—first you will dine. You must be starving. Everyone who comes down here is starving. It is the mountain. It takes the warmth out slowly, and the body begins to miss it, and the body begins to think—" the borrowed face tilted, studying them with eyes that reflected nothing, "—the body begins to think the hunger is homesickness. And then the body thinks the court is home."

"That won't happen to us," Jonah said.

"No," the courtier agreed, still pleasant, still smiling. "It happened to the last ones too. At first."

* * *

They were swept along between the dancers, and Wren made themselves do the thing they did best: observe, record, remember. If they survived this, every detail would go into the map. If they didn't survive, well. Someone should at least have drawn it.

The feast tables ran the length of the hall, laden with dishes that gleamed like wet jewels. Fruits Wren didn't recognize, dark as bruises. Meats glazed to a mirror shine. Bread pale as bone marrow. Courtiers ate delicately, beautifully, lifting morsels to borrowed mouths—and Wren watched closely, taster's training lending them the eye for it, and saw that the eating was wrong too. The chewing was correct. The swallowing was correct. But nothing diminished. Platters stayed full. Mouths opened and closed around food that seemed to pass through them like light through glass.

They weren't eating the food. Wren understood, with a lurch, that the food wasn't the point—that the courtiers performed dining the way they performed breathing and laughing and dancing, all of it mimicry, all of it a century-deep rehearsal of a life they could no longer touch. What they actually fed on was elsewhere. Was *above*. Was a soul in a reliquary, drained nightly, keeping a hundred beautiful hungry things alive on a prince's stolen self.

And the worst of it, the thing that made Wren's hand find Jonah's under the table's edge: it was gorgeous. All of it. The cold fire turned every surface to moonstone. The music wound through the hall like smoke given melody. The courtiers moved with a grace no living court could match, because living courts were made of people who got tired, and these things never tired, only repeated, only refined, only *hungered* more beautifully each decade. Beauty with nothing behind it but appetite. A lantern lit inside a corpse.

"Wren." Jonah's voice, low, at their ear. "Your hand is shaking."

"I know."

"Eat something. Not the food—I mean, actually eat. We brought provisions. Chew. Swallow. Keep your body remembering whose side it's on."

Wren fumbled a strip of dried apple from their coat and ate it beneath the table, and the small human sweetness of it nearly undid them. Around them the feast glittered and the music played and a courtier wearing the face of Wren's old geography tutor raised a glass of dark wine in their direction—an acknowledgment? a toast? a promise?—and did not drink.

"The Regent," Wren whispered. "When we're presented. What do we ask for?"

"Time," Jonah said. "We ask for time, and we watch everything, and we find Alaric Vane before anyone remembers to wonder why two warm things walked in and haven't started starving yet." A pause. Their thumb moved once across Wren's knuckles, deliberate, grounding them both. "Whatever happens in there—whatever it offers, whatever it shows us—you remember it lies. Beautifully. It has had a hundred years of practice lying beautifully."

"And you?"

"I'm a taster," Jonah said. "I assume everything is poisoned until proven otherwise. Even the truth. *Especially* the truth."

The music changed then—a single low note, sustained, like the mountain clearing its throat—and the entire court turned as one body toward the deep end of the hall, toward the dais, toward the doors that were opening there with the slow grandeur of something that had waited a hundred years to open them.

The courtiers began to kneel, wave upon wave, silver faces bowing, and the three who had escorted them pressed gently but irresistibly at Jonah's shoulders until they too bent, and Wren bent beside them, heart hammering, eyes down on the mirror-dark floor where the map of the mountain's underside stared back up at them.

Through the opening doors came a light unlike the cold fire—older, slower, the color of the winter sky an hour before dusk.

And a voice, neither male nor female, neither young nor old, a voice like snowfall given patience:

"Rise, warm things. Let me look at what the mountain has finally sent me."

Wren rose beside Jonah, and across the hall, on a throne of black root and white stone, the Pale Regent gazed at them with eyes like the last light before a long dark—and smiled, and its smile was the most beautiful and most terrible thing Wren had ever seen, because it was the first expression in the whole uncanny court that was not borrowed from anyone at all.
