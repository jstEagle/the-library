## 21. Audience With the Pale Regent

They took my knives at the door of the antechamber, which told me two things: that the deep court knew what I was, and that they were afraid enough of it to bother.

The door itself had been a mouth of black wood set into living stone, guarded by two figures who might once have been men. Their faces were smooth as river-worn pebbles, features worn down by centuries of something—water, or grief—and when they reached for my weapons they did it with a courtesy so old-fashioned it felt like an apology. Wren's hand found mine in the dark behind them, squeezed once, and let go. That was the agreement. I went forward alone; Wren would wait with the guides in the gallery above the throne room, close enough to see, too far to intervene. *If things go wrong,* Wren had whispered against my hair before we entered, *I will find you. Even here. Especially here.* A cartographer's promise. Every place can be mapped. Even this one.

So I walked alone down a corridor lit by cold fire—blue-white flames that gave light but no warmth, burning in sconces of what looked like bone but was probably only very old ivory, and probably wasn't. The flames did not flicker. Nothing here flickered. In the palace above, a thousand candles breathed with the draft from the windows, and you learned to read the room by how the light moved. Down here the light was still as a held breath, and every shadow lay exactly where it had been placed.

My body kept its own ledger, as it always did. Pulse steady. Mouth dry—not fear, just the mineral taste of the air, which was like licking slate. The faint ache in my left shoulder where the gate's toll had taken the summer: the long grass at Aunt Sefa's farm, the smell of cut hay, the sound of my mother laughing at something I could no longer remember her laughing at. I kept reaching for it the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. Gone, gone, gone, and in its place a smooth-edged absence that hurt more than any poison I'd ever swallowed, because poisons announce themselves. This just quietly wasn't anymore.

*You paid a childhood summer for a door,* I reminded myself. *Remember what the rest costs.*

The antechamber opened without a door opening. One moment I stood before a wall of veined grey stone; the next, the stone had decided to be elsewhere, and I was being gestured through by a chamberlain whose face was borrowed from someone I almost recognized—a courtier from Isolde's hall, maybe, or his brother, or the brother of a man dead a hundred years. He wore it the way a man wears a coat that fits well but belongs to someone else. His eyes, at least, were his own: pale, patient, and hungry in the way of a thing that has learned hunger is not shameful but simply seasonal.

"The Regent will receive you," he said. His voice was beautiful and slightly wrong, like a bell struck underwater.

"I'm honored," I said, because in two courts now I had learned that the words cost nothing and buy much.

"Not yet," he said, and smiled with someone else's mouth.

* * *

The throne room of the deep court was the throne room of Vanehold turned inside out.

I had walked beneath the queen's dais ten thousand mornings, head bowed, tasting spoon in hand, and I knew that hall the way I knew my own hands—the honey-colored pillars, the banners of the winter stag, the long window of colored glass through which the morning came down like a blessing. This place was its mirror. The pillars were black and veined with silver, like frost seen from underneath ice. Where the banners would hang, there were tapestries woven of something that moved faintly, thread by thread, as though the images on them were dreaming. And where the great window should have been there was a wall of clear stone, and beyond it—not sky, not rock, but a slow drift of white shapes, vast and unhurried, turning in a darkness that had no floor and no ceiling.

Snow, I thought. Snow the size of cathedrals, falling upward or sideways or in no direction at all. The long winter's true face, kept down here like a portrait of an ancestor.

And before that wall, on a throne of grey stone that seemed less built than grown, sat the Pale Regent.

I had expected—gods, I don't know what I'd expected. Something vast. Something with too many joints, too many mouths, the kind of thing smugglers describe after too much rotgut and not enough sleep. Instead there was a figure that might have been carved from the same stone as the throne and then taught, patiently, over centuries, how to sit. Tall. Thin as a winter branch. Its skin was the white of the first snowfall, unmarked, unlined, and its face was—

Its face was unfinished. Not blank—unfinished. The features were suggested rather than committed: the idea of cheekbones, the memory of a mouth, eyes that were two depths of grey in which the cold fire of the sconces reflected without warming. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen and it was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful, which is to say: it was beautiful about the fact that it would kill you if you trusted it.

"Jonah Eriksen," said the Regent.

My name in its voice sounded like snow landing on snow. Soft, and final, and burying.

"That's not my name," I said. "Not anymore. The records burned."

"Records burn." The Regent rose, and the movement was wrong in a way I couldn't parse—too smooth, as though it glided along a rail invisible to me. "Names do not. We keep names down here, taster. Yours has been waiting a hundred years in our ledger, filed under debt. You are the last Eriksen. Did you know that?"

"I know my parents died."

"I know what your parents *did*." It descended from the dais—not stairs, the dais simply became level ground beneath its feet—and stopped six paces away, which I understood instinctively was a courtesy. Close enough for conversation. Far enough that my reflexes, honed on a hundred poisons and a thousand bad alleys, would not be insulted by proximity. "Your grandmother brokered the sale of a prince. Your grandfather witnessed it. Your parents tried to unmake it, and the mountain ate them for their trouble—or rather," and here the unfinished face tilted, and I felt the weight of its attention like a hand on the back of my neck, "the guild above sold them to us for the price of silence. You were raised by the people who killed them. They fed you, clothed you, trained your tongue, and never once told you why your family name tastes of ash. Does that surprise you?"

"No," I said, and it was mostly true. Maret's testimony had cracked the door; the Regent was only walking through it. "The guild owns people. That's what ownership means. I've known since I was nine years old."

Something happened around the Regent's mouth that was not quite a smile but was stored in the same warehouse. "Good. I dislike negotiating with liars. It wastes the century." It turned, gliding, and gestured toward the wall of drifting snow-cathedrals. "Walk with me. There is something I want you to see before we discuss terms."

I walked. My legs obeyed me, which I noted with professional approval. Whatever the air of this place carried—and I could taste it now, faintly, at the back of my throat: something sweet and numbing, like violets cut with laudanum—it was not affecting my balance. Either the Regent wanted me steady, or my body's long education had finally made me proof against even this court's gentle chemistry. Both possibilities were useful. Neither was comforting.

We stopped before the clear stone. Beyond it, the vast snowflakes turned, and among them—I saw it then, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world—there were faces. Small, distant, folded into the crystal structures like insects in amber. Some were screaming. Most had forgotten how.

"What is that?" I asked, though I knew. I had read the prophecy aloud with Wren by lamplight. *Before the long winter finishes falling.* I had thought it a metaphor. Cartographers' ink and poets' habit. But metaphors do not have faces.

"The pact," the Regent said. "One hundred years ago, your kingdom faced a winter that would have ended it. True famine. True death, in numbers your historians flinch from writing. Queen Maerin Vane came to us—we did not seek her out, whatever her priests say—and she offered a trade. Her son's soul, given nightly to feed my court, in exchange for a winter that protects instead of devours. Mild snows. Full granaries. A shield against the killing cold that waits—" it gestured at the wall, at the faces, "—*there*. Your kingdom has eaten well for a century off a boy's suffering. Your queen signs the renewal every twenty years. She signed it again four years ago, in the crypt beneath her own chapel, with her own hand. She knows exactly what she buys and exactly what it costs. Do you imagine she does not?"

I thought of Isolde's hands, small and precise, lifting the tasting fork. I thought of her probing questions about my family name, the way her eyes had narrowed like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. I thought of Serah Dole in the archive stairwell saying *the crown wants the prophecy fulfilled on the crown's terms*, and I had thought she meant the crown wanted a hero. 

It meant the crown wanted a scapegoat.

"You're telling me this," I said slowly, "because you want me to go home angry."

"I am telling you this because it is true, and because truth is the only currency I accept." The Regent turned to face me fully, and up close its unfinished face was worse, or better—I couldn't decide. There was a weariness in it that no sculpture could fake. "Now. Terms."

* * *

"Here is what I offer," the Regent said, and the whole throne room seemed to lean in—the chamberlain, the pebble-faced guards, the dreaming tapestries. "Leave. Tonight. Take your cartographer, take the living road upward, and walk out of this mountain into the snow, alive, whole, and free. No hunt. No curse. My court will forget your name within the hour; we are very good at forgetting, it is our native art."

"And in exchange?"

"You forget too."

The word landed in my chest like a coin dropped down a well.

"There is a craft below," the Regent continued, in the tone of a merchant displaying flawless silk, "older than your kingdom, older than mine. We call it the unmaking. A single memory, cleanly lifted: tonight, the gate, the prophecy, the thorn-tree, the toll you already paid. Everything from the moment Wren put that book in front of you. You would wake in your bed in the servants' wing with a mild headache and a sense of having dreamed of snow. You would go on tasting the queen's food. You would go on loving your cartographer—you may even love them better, unburdened. The winter continues. The pact holds. The kingdom eats. The boy in the wall keeps feeding us, as he has for a hundred years, and no one new suffers for it. Everyone keeps what they had." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Including you."

I stood very still, the way I stand when a dish arrives and I don't yet know if it's poisoned.

Because here is the thing no one tells you about making yourself invisible for fifteen years: part of you starts to want it. Not the anonymity—God, no, I'd have traded that in a heartbeat for my mother's laugh, which I no longer owned. What I mean is that some starving corner of me had spent a lifetime believing that safety was a place you could reach if you were only careful enough, quiet enough, unremarkable enough. And the Regent was offering it. Not the false version, not the smuggler's version where safety lasts until the guild decides otherwise. The real version. Forget the prophecy. Forget the debt. Forget the name that tasted of ash. Wake up tomorrow as no one, loved by Wren, fed and warm under a shielded sky, and never know that somewhere below the world a prince was being eaten alive to pay for my breakfast.

It would work. That was the horror of it. It would work perfectly. I had spent years building a self designed to survive erasure; the Regent was offering to complete the architecture.

"You've made this offer before," I said.

"Twice." No hesitation. "A guild courier, sixty years ago, who found the gate by accident. A priestess, thirty years ago, who came looking for her god and found us instead. Both accepted. Both lived long, comfortable lives above. The priestess died in her bed at ninety-one, beloved by her parish." The grey eyes held mine. "You are wondering whether I tell you this to reassure you or to warn you. The honest answer is both. I am not your enemy, taster. I am a landlord collecting rent on a lease your kingdom signed gladly and has renewed four times. If you steal the soul, the shield falls, and the winter outside that wall comes down on ten thousand people who never signed anything. Children. Farmers. The innkeeper who fed you on the road. Is your righteousness worth their lives? Is a stranger's century of pain worth a kingdom's?"

And there it was. The trap wasn't in the offer. The trap was that the offer was *fair*.

I thought of Wren, three galleries up, watching through cold firelight, holding a map to nowhere and a promise. I thought of Alaric Vane, whom I hadn't met yet—only a name in a ledger and a sketch in a prophecy book, a young man's face drawn beside tomorrow's date a hundred years before tomorrow. I thought of the summer I'd paid at the gate, the hay-smell and the laughter, gone, gone, and how the absence had teeth.

Then I thought about what forgetting actually meant, and I finally understood the shape of the thing being offered to me. Not peace. Erasure with good lighting. The Regent wasn't offering to spare me suffering. It was offering to make me the kind of person who had never been burdened with knowing—which is to say, the kind of person this entire arrangement depends on. Ten thousand comfortable people above, each one innocent because each one ignorant. The priestess had died beloved. She had also died having done nothing, having been gently returned to the machinery, another gear polished and reinstalled.

"You're right," I said.

The Regent's unfinished face went very still.

"It's fair," I said. "That's the problem. Fair bargains are how we got here. My grandmother made a fair bargain. Queen Maerin made a fair bargain. Isolde makes a fair bargain every twenty years, and every time, the boy in the wall pays the difference between fair and right." I took a breath, and the violet-and-laudanum air went down cold. "I'm a taster, Regent. Do you know what that's taught me? That the deadliest dishes aren't the ones laced with obvious venom. They're the ones that taste wonderful. That's how kings get killed—by the meal they trust. You've seasoned this offer beautifully. I can taste everything in it except the part where anyone down here stops being hungry."

For a long moment the throne room made no sound at all. The cold fire did not flicker. The snow-cathedrals turned their buried faces.

"You would refuse comfort," the Regent said at last, and there was something in its voice I hadn't heard before—not anger. Wonder, maybe, or its fossil. "You, whose whole life has been the practice of disappearing."

"My whole life," I said, "has been the practice of surviving disappearance. It's different." And then, because honesty was apparently the only currency here and I was tired of counterfeit: "Ask me again in a year and I might say yes. Ask me tonight, after the gate took my mother's laugh, and I'll tell you what I've learned about paying memory for passage. It doesn't come back. Comfort does. Comfort comes back every morning with breakfast. I won't trade the permanent for the renewable."

The Regent regarded me for the length of three heartbeats. Then it inclined its head, a gesture of such grave, ancient courtesy that I understood suddenly how old it must be, and how tired, and how very long it had been since anyone below had refused it anything.

"Then we are concluded, taster, and I grieve it. You will find the road upward closed until you reconsider—consider it hospitality, not imprisonment. My court is generous to guests." It began to turn back toward the throne, and paused. "One courtesy, since you paid honestly at the gate. You came looking for the Vane boy. He is kept in the west reliquary garden, where the feeding happens. Go and look at him before you decide what your righteousness is worth. Look at what a century of being *enough* does to a man. Perhaps it will teach you what I cannot."

It glided away toward its throne of grown stone, and the chamberlain appeared at my elbow wearing his borrowed face, gesturing with antique grace toward a side arch hung with moving tapestry.

"This way," he said softly. "The garden is through here. Mind the roots—they remember being trees."

And as I followed him into the dark, my heart hammering its steady, trained, poison-proof rhythm, I heard—faint, from far above, through stone and cold fire and a hundred feet of dreaming mountain—Wren's voice calling my name, and under it, rising like water through cracks, the first thin note of an alarm.
