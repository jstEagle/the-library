## 22. Finding Alaric

The deep court did not sleep, but it dreamed.

Wren understood this by the third hour of their descent through the mirrored halls, when the corridors began to change in ways that had nothing to do with walking. A gallery of cold-fire lanterns would stretch ahead of them, and by the time they reached its end the portraits on the walls would be different—faces Wren had almost recognized replaced by faces they had never seen at all, or perhaps had seen only a moment ago, wearing different expressions. The court was dreaming them, Wren thought. Or they were dreaming the court, and neither dream was entirely under their own control.

Jonah walked ahead, one hand trailing along the wall as though reading it by touch. They had not spoken in an hour. Speaking here felt like dropping stones into still water; the ripples came back wrong.

*He is kept below,* Maret had said, a lifetime ago, in an inn that existed on no map. *Below the below. Where even the Regent's own people don't like to look.*

Wren had spent the intervening days—and were they days? Time moved strangely beneath the mountain, measured in meals and ceremonies rather than suns—learning to read the deep court the way they read any landscape. Every map was a portrait of power. Show me where the roads bend, and I will tell you who is afraid of whom. And everything in this inverted palace bent away from one direction: down, and further down, past the banquet halls where Jonah had eaten poison with a straight spine and a courtier's smile, past the reliquary with its guards of woven shadow, into the oldest part of the mountain, where the walls stopped pretending to be architecture and became simply stone, ancient and patient as a held breath.

That was where the roads bent from. That was what everyone was afraid of.

"It's colder here," Wren whispered, and immediately regretted speaking, because the whisper came back to them a half-second later in their own voice but slightly older, slightly wearier, as if someone else had said it first and they were only echoing.

Jonah glanced back. In the cold firelight their face was all planes and shadows, the scar at their collarbone—the birthmark-shaped one, the gate-shaped one—catching light like a seam of silver. "Colder means closer," they said. "Everything down here radiates from him. Even the temperature." A pause. "Maret said the winter above is his soul, stretched thin over a hundred years. Maybe we're walking inside the coldest part of it."

Wren nodded and said nothing more. But they marked the cold as they went, noting it in the way they noted everything—a cartographer's habit so deep it had become a form of prayer. *Cold increases leftward. Cold pools where the mirrors are cracked.* Somewhere in their satchel was a sheaf of sketches that no honest mapmaker would ever publish: the floor plan of a nightmare, drawn in charcoal on paper that had begun to sweat.

They descended a stair that had not been there when they looked up.

This was the thing about the deep court that frightened Wren most—not the courtiers with their borrowed faces, not the Regent's voice like frost forming on glass, but the casual dishonesty of the geometry. Stairs appeared where stairs had not been. Corridors agreed to exist only while observed. Wren had learned to fight it the only way a mapmaker could: by drawing. Each time the halls shifted, they sketched what they had seen before it changed, and slowly, across dozens of sketches, a true shape emerged from the lies—one consistent chamber at the heart of every version, a room that never moved because it could not, because everything else in the mountain was arranged around it the way a wound is arranged around the blade.

They found the door to it behind a waterfall of frozen mercury.

It was not locked. That was the detail Wren would remember longest, turning it over in the years after like a stone found on a beach. It was not locked, and it was not guarded, and it was not hidden—only forgotten, which is a stronger lock than any of those. The deep court had fed on Alaric Vane for a hundred years, and somewhere in that century the feeding had become so ordinary that no one thought to watch the pantry. You do not guard the well. You simply drink.

The door was black wood gone soft with age, carved with thorn-branches worn nearly smooth. Jonah put their hand against it and stopped.

"I can hear something," they said quietly.

Wren listened. At first there was nothing—only the mountain's deep silence, which was not silence at all but a sound below hearing, like the inside of a bell. Then, beneath it, fainter than breath: a heartbeat. Slow. Enormously slow. One beat, then a pause long enough to doubt it, then another, steady as a lighthouse in fog.

"That's not possible," Wren said. "A hundred years. No heart beats for a hundred years."

"Not a hundred years," Jonah said. Their hand was still flat against the door, and Wren saw that it trembled. "One year, a hundred times. He's been dying since he was nineteen. They just—" Jonah's jaw tightened. "They just keep letting the year finish."

They pushed the door open together.

* * *

The room beyond was small. That was the first cruelty of it. After the vastness of the mirrored halls, after the cathedral spaces where the court held its ceremonies of cold fire, Wren had expected the kingdom's most precious prisoner to be kept in something grand. Instead: a chamber scarcely larger than the roadhouse rooms they had shared above ground, with low ceilings of raw stone and a single window that showed not sky but a slab of mountain, close enough to touch, as if the room had been buried alive along with its occupant.

There was a bed. There was a chair. There was a table bearing a cup of water that had evaporated to a ring of mineral stain, refilled and evaporated again so many times that the rings overlapped like tree growth—centuries of small attentions, none of them kind.

And there was a man.

He sat on the edge of the bed with his hands open on his knees, palms up, in the posture of someone waiting to receive something that has been promised for a very long time. He was thin the way winter branches are thin—not starved exactly, but pared down, reduced to what could survive the season. His hair had gone white at thirty shades once removed from age, a pale gold bleached toward transparency. He wore clothes of fine cut gone gray with washing, a prince's wardrobe laundered down to a servant's color.

When the door opened, he looked up, and Wren felt their whole body flinch.

Because his eyes were wrong—or rather, they were right in a way nothing down here should be. Everything in the deep court performed its beauty; the courtiers' borrowed faces gleamed with effort. But this man's eyes were simply tired. Humanly, ordinarily, devastatingly tired. They were eyes Wren had seen in border towns after bad harvests, in old soldiers telling stories they didn't believe anymore, in their own reflection some mornings before the work of the day gave them a reason to be otherwise.

"Visitors," the man said. His voice was rust and honey. "How strange. I'd almost—" He stopped. Started again, slower, feeling for each word like a man crossing ice. "I used to know what visitors meant. Before."

"Before what?" Jonah asked softly.

The man frowned, and the frown itself seemed to cost him something, like lifting a weight. "I've been trying to remember that all morning," he said. "Or all century. It's difficult to tell the difference, down here. The days come around again the way water comes around in a well." He looked at his open palms. "You'll think me mad. Everyone thinks me mad. But I can't recall my name. I had one. I'm nearly sure I had one. It began with—" His mouth worked. "With a sound. Like a struck bell."

Wren's throat closed. Beside them, Jonah made a small sound—not quite a word—and stepped forward into the cold-fire dimness, into the range of the man's tired eyes.

"Alaric," Jonah said. "Your name is Alaric. Alaric Vane. You were a prince."

Something happened to the man's face then that Wren would carry for the rest of their life. It was not recognition, not yet. It was the moment before recognition—the way a frozen river looks just before the ice gives, all of it still surface, all of it already moving underneath. His lips shaped the name without sound. *Alaric.* Then again, aloud, testing: "Alaric." A third time, and his voice broke on it like a boy's. "Is that—is that mine? Truly? It feels—" He pressed both hands flat over his sternum, as though the name might be kept there, as though it might beat. "It fits. Like a key fits. How do you know my name?"

"From a book," Wren said, and their own voice came out unsteady. "A hundred years old. Your face is drawn in it beside tomorrow's date."

"Tomorrow." The man—Alaric—laughed, and it was a terrible laugh, gentle and broken. "Tomorrow. I remember tomorrow. There have been so many of them." He looked up at them both, and now the fear came into his face, the fear of a man who has learned that hope is the cruelest thing the dark can send him. "Are you real? Forgive me. They send me dreams sometimes. Beautiful ones. My mother, my brother, a garden with bees in it. They let me hold them until I'm happy, and then they feed." He touched his chest again, lower this time, over the hollow beneath the ribs. "I feel it most when I'm happy. That's why I try not to be. It's rude to complain about being eaten when you've been given a garden."

Wren crossed the room without deciding to. They knelt in front of him, the way you kneel to a wounded stranger on a road, and took his hands—his cold, papery, unbearably light hands—and held them the way you'd hold a bird you were afraid of crushing.

"You're real," Wren said. "We're real. We came to take you out of here."

"Don't say that." His grip tightened with sudden shocking strength, a strength that must have been living on nothing for a century, hoarded like a coin under a floorboard. "Don't say it unless it's true. Do you understand? They will take the words and make a garden out of them. They will let me walk out of here in a dream and wake to the ceiling, and it will be worse than never saying it. If you are lying to me, lie smaller."

"We're not lying." Jonah crouched beside Wren, and their voice had the flat steadiness Wren knew from the smuggling stories, from the passes, from every moment when the truth was the only tool left. "Listen to me. My name is Jonah Eriksen. My family sold you. A hundred years ago, the Eriksens brokered the trade that put you in this room, and I have spent my whole life not knowing that, and now I know it, and I am here to undo it. Not because of prophecy. Not because anyone ordered me. Because it's owed to you. Do you hear me? You are owed."

Alaric stared at them. And slowly, joint by joint, like a machine remembering its purpose, he began to shake.

Not weeping, exactly. Something older than weeping—the shaking of a body that has held still too long, that has practiced stillness until stillness became the whole of it, now trying to remember motion. Tears came, but they seemed incidental, almost surprised, sliding down a face that had clearly forgotten how to arrange itself around grief.

"A hundred years," he whispered. "I knew—I always knew there was a number. I could feel it. Like a debt. Like the cold." He looked around the little room as if seeing it for the first time, the stained cup, the buried window, the bed he had died in ten thousand times. "My brother. Did my brother—"

"He's dust," Jonah said gently. "Everyone who did this to you is dust, except the ones below, and the crown above, which renewed your sale every twenty-five years and told no one. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. There's no one left to punish. There's only you to take home."

"Home." Alaric said the word the way a starving man says bread. Then his face clouded, and Wren watched—with a compassion that felt like a physical ache—as he tried to reach for the memory of home and found the shelf empty. "I can't see it. I know I should be able to see it. There were towers. There was a river that froze in—no. Was there a river?" Panic rose in his voice, thin and high. "What good is taking me home if I can't remember it? What will be left of me to bring?"

Wren did the only thing they knew how to do. They pulled the sketchbook from their satchel, tore out a page, and pressed it into his hands along with a stick of charcoal.

"Then we'll build it back," Wren said. "Piece by piece. Whatever you've lost, we'll redraw it. I'm a mapmaker—that's what I do. I take what people half-remember and I make it solid enough to stand on. Start anywhere. A tower. A garden. The sound of a bell. Draw it badly. I'll fix the rest."

Alaric looked down at the blank page trembling in his fingers. And then, haltingly, like a child, like a ghost, like a man digging himself out of his own grave with a teaspoon, he began to draw.

* * *

Later—hours later, or minutes; the mountain kept its own time—they sat in the small cold room among a scatter of pages, and Wren understood that they were looking at the truest map they would ever make.

Alaric drew the way drowning men swim. His lines wavered and doubled back. But out of the ruin of his memory, things surfaced: a tower with a crooked finial he insisted was real, *the masons argued about it for a year*; a dog named something he couldn't retrieve but whose ears he could draw perfectly, one up and one folded; a garden with bees, and when Wren said gently, *the court sends you that garden as a dream*, Alaric shook his head with sudden fierce clarity and said, *No. They stole it. It was mine first. The dream is a copy. Look—the bee stung me here, on this thumb, the summer I was nine. Dreams don't leave scars.*

And the scar was there. Faint, ancient, human. Proof that he had been a boy in a garden before he was a meal in a cellar.

Jonah sat apart through most of it, near the door, keeping watch in the way of their profession—but Wren caught them watching Alaric's hands, and caught the expression on their face, and understood that Jonah was looking at the physical fact of what their family had done. Not an abstraction. Not a chapter in a forbidden book. A man. Thin as winter. Drawing a dog's ears with a shaking hand because it was the last thing he owned.

At some point Alaric set down the charcoal and looked at them both for a long moment.

"You're afraid," he said. "Both of you. You keep glancing at the door." He smiled, and it was a wry smile, an old soul's smile in a worn-out face, and for a moment Wren glimpsed the prince he must have been at nineteen—quick, kind, probably insufferable in the way beloved princes are allowed to be. "Whatever you're planning, it's worse than you're letting me believe. I know that look. I wore it once, the night before—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. "The night before the well. I remember the well now. Isn't that funny. I remember the well."

"The reliquary," Jonah said quietly. "His soul is kept in the Regent's reliquary. We've seen it. A seed of burning light, no bigger than a hazelnut."

"And you mean to steal it." Alaric said it without drama, the way one confirms the weather. "During the ceremony. Yes. I've watched them carry it past my door for a hundred years. It's the only warm thing in this mountain." He looked down at his own hands, at the drawing of the dog. "Take it. Take it and run, and don't look back, whatever you hear behind you. Because I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear it while you can still choose."

The cold in the room deepened. Wren felt it settle on their shoulders like a hand.

"When you lift my soul out of that reliquary," Alaric said, "the pact breaks. All of it. The winter above isn't weather—it's the payment, running. And payments that stop running get collected." His tired eyes moved from Jonah to Wren and back. "I have heard them talk outside my door, all these years, the way servants talk when they think the furniture isn't listening. The Regent doesn't want my soul because it's beautiful. The Regent wants it because it's collateral. And there is a clause, written in the original contract, in my family's blood—" his voice dropped, and the mountain seemed to lean in to listen— "that says if the collateral is taken by force, the debt falls due *in full*. On the kingdom. Immediately. Winter such as the world has never seen. Famine. The rivers freezing to their beds."

Silence. Somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, a bell began to toll—the summons to the ceremony, the one Jonah had been invited to attend as an honored guest, the one where the reliquary would be carried in procession past ten thousand hungry eyes.

"So that's the choice," Alaric finished softly. "Leave me here, and the kingdom stays comfortable on my suffering for another hundred years. Or take me, and save me, and maybe kill everyone I ever—" He laughed again, that broken gentle laugh. "Except I can't remember them. So perhaps it's easy. Perhaps that's why they ate my memories first."

Jonah stood. Wren watched them stand—watched the taster's stillness come over them, the stillness of a person who has decided something in the place behind the eyes where decisions are made before the mouth knows.

"No," Jonah said. "It's not either-or. It was never either-or. That's what they want everyone below and everyone above to believe—that the only choices are the ones written in the contract." They turned to Wren, and their eyes in the cold firelight were absolutely certain. "Contracts can be renegotiated. Debts can be paid differently. My family wrote the original terms, Wren. Which means the Eriksen claim is still in the ink. And I am the last Eriksen."

"What are you going to do?" Wren asked.

Jonah picked up the torn sketch of the dog with the mismatched ears, folded it with great care, and tucked it into their coat over their heart.

"First," they said, as the bell tolled a second time, "we get him out of this room. Then I'm going to commit the largest theft in the history of two kingdoms—and then, if we live, I'm going to sit down with the Pale Regent and renegotiate a hundred-year-old contract over tea." A ghost of the smuggler's grin. "But if that fails—"

"If that fails," Wren said, "we run."

"If that fails," Jonah said, "we run, and I pay whatever the door asks, and you carry him into the light. Promise me that part. Whatever happens to me—you carry him into the light."

The bell tolled a third time, and far off through the stone, Wren heard the deep court begin to stir toward its ceremony—hundreds of borrowed footsteps, all moving toward the reliquary, all moving toward the theft that would break the winter or end the world, and no one among them yet knowing that the thorn had already entered the garden.
