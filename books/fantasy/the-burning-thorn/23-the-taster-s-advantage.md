## 23. The Taster's Advantage

The banquet hall of the deep court was a mirror held up to the palace above and left out in the rain.

Jonah saw it at once—the way the architecture repeated the throne room of Queen Isolde in every particular and got every particular slightly wrong. The columns were the same fluted marble, but they curved inward like throats. The banners that hung from the vaulted ceiling bore the same stag-and-star of the Vane dynasty, but the stag's antlers had grown long and rootlike, branching downward instead of up, as if the beast were trying to burrow into the stone. Even the light was an inversion: not the honeyed gold of a thousand candles but a cold blue-white fire that burned without heat, floating in iron cages along the walls, casting shadows that moved a half-second behind the bodies that made them.

And the courtiers. The courtiers wore borrowed faces.

Jonah had counted eleven of them since being escorted in—eleven faces that belonged, unmistakably, to people who had lived and died in the kingdom above. A merchant whose portrait hung in the customs house at Greyharbor. A knight whose name was carved on the cenotaph in the Palace Square. A girl of perhaps sixteen with a milkmaid's sunburned cheeks, though no sun had ever reached this far beneath the mountain. The faces fit their wearers imperfectly, like gloves a size too large, and when the courtiers smiled, the smiles arrived a moment after the eyes had already finished with it.

*Don't stare,* Wren had told them, back in the corridor outside Alaric's cell, gripping Jonah's hand hard enough to leave marks. *Don't stare, don't count them where they can see you counting.*

But counting was what Jonah did. Counting was how a taster survived: count the dishes, count the servers, count the seconds between the lord lifting his fork and the first swallow going down. Counting had kept them alive through nine years of Queen Isolde's table, through the arsenic in the wedding wine and the henbane some ambitious cousin had dusted over the sugared plums. Counting was the one prayer Jonah had never abandoned.

So they counted. And what they counted told them this: the Pale Regent had seated them at the high table, three places from the empty throne, and every dish that came up from the kitchens passed under Jonah's nose before it went anywhere near the Regent's silvered lips.

The invitation had come that morning, delivered by a page whose face was fresh—young, unremarkable, *unborrowed*, which somehow frightened Jonah more than the rest. *The Regent requests the taster's service at tonight's feast.* No threat attached. No alternative offered. In the deep court, Jonah was learning, threats were considered vulgar; everything here was framed as courtesy, and the courtesy itself was the cage.

Now Jonah stood behind the Regent's chair with a small gold spoon in hand, wearing borrowed finery—a doublet of grey silk that fit too well, as if cut for someone who had worn it before—and performed the oldest ritual of their life in the newest place it had ever been demanded.

The first course arrived on a platter of black glass: eels from a river that did not exist above, glazed in something dark and glistening, arranged in a spiral like a coiled rope.

Jonah took the spoon, carved a sliver from the tail, and put it on their tongue.

The taste bloomed slow and strange—smoke, then iron, then a sweetness underneath like rotting apples, and beneath *that*, faint as a whisper through a wall, the unmistakable bitter signature of something meant to kill.

*Wolfsbane,* Jonah thought, cataloguing it the way other people said their own names. *Aconitum, prepared in oil so the bitterness comes late. Clever. Above ground, this would stop a heart in four minutes.*

They swallowed. They waited. They let the poison find nothing in them to burn.

Nine years of deliberate self-poisoning had built Jonah's blood into a fortress. Nightshade, arsenic, mercury, the sap of the thorn-fig, the venom of the Thornfell adder—each one taken in measured doses, each one survived, each one leaving its small scar on the inside where no one could see. Their body was a ledger, as Wren once said, laughing and horrified in equal measure, and every entry had been written in pain. There had been nights, especially early on, when Jonah had curled around a chamber pot in the taster's quarters, sweating and shaking, whispering *again tomorrow, again tomorrow* like a rosary, because immunity was the only currency a person with no name and no family could mint.

Every scar becoming useful at last. They had not known, when they were twenty and retching alone by candlelight, that the ledger would someday be read aloud in a court beneath the world.

The Regent watched. Of course the Regent watched. It sat enthroned in its pallor—tall, thin, crowned in antlered bone that might have been the mirror-image of the stag on the banners, its skin the white of things that live in caves—and its eyes, pale as frost on a window, never left Jonah's throat.

"You do not die," the Regent observed. Its voice was quiet, courteous, and carried to every corner of the hall anyway, the way cold carries.

"No, my lord." Jonah inclined their head. "The eel is excellent. The glaze is seasoned with wolfsbane, which I imagine is traditional."

A murmur ran down the high table. Somewhere to Jonah's left, a courtier wearing a dead admiral's face laughed—a sound like water dripping in an empty cistern.

"It is traditional," the Regent agreed. "The last taster who served this table lasted two courses." It gestured, and the next platter came forward. "Continue."

* * *

They continued.

Course after course, Jonah ate what would have killed any rescuer the crown above might have dreamed of sending. That was the point; Jonah understood that now, understood it with the cold clarity of someone watching a trap assemble itself around their feet. The Regent was not testing the food. The Regent was testing *them*—and advertising the results. Every dish Jonah survived was a message broadcast to the whole court: *this one cannot be poisoned, cannot be quietly removed, cannot be fed to the dark between the mirrors.* Whatever the taster intended, they would have to be stopped some other way, and the Regent wanted its courtiers to start thinking about what that other way might be.

It was also, Jonah admitted, swallowing a broth laced with enough hemlock to drop a horse, buying exactly what Jonah needed most: time.

Because time was the whole shape of the plan now, thin and fragile as spun sugar. Alaric's soul—the burning seed, the small gold flame the Regent kept in its reliquary and fed upon nightly—was guarded by ceremony, not soldiers. The deep court did not fear invasion; who could invade a place like this? It feared *impropriety*. And so the soul was brought out at the high feasts, displayed in its reliquary before the assembled courtiers, honored with rites older than the pact itself—and the next great feast, the Feast of the Long Falling, was three days hence. Three days to learn the rite. Three days to learn which hands touched the reliquary, in what order, at what hour. Three days of banquets, of tasting, of standing close enough to the throne to study the lock on the reliquary's cage.

Three days of eating poison in front of monsters and smiling about it.

"The broth," Jonah announced, setting down the spoon. "Hemlock in the stock, water-hemlock in the garnish. Whoever your cook is, they're wasting good talent on murder."

This time the murmur was different. Not laughter now—something closer to appetite. The courtiers had begun to lean toward Jonah the way flowers lean toward light, or the way things lean toward warmth in a cold room. A lady with the face of a woman who had drowned in the Lake of Sighs two hundred years ago pressed a ringed hand to her bodice and sighed, "It has been so *long* since anyone interesting sat at this table."

"Madam," Jonah said, bowing, "I promise you I am the least interesting thing in this hall."

"Oh, no," she said, and her borrowed smile finally caught up with her eyes, and Jonah wished it hadn't. "You are the most interesting thing here. You are *proof*."

*Proof of what?* Jonah didn't ask. Some questions were better saved for daylight, even daylight as false as this.

* * *

Between the fourth and fifth courses, the feast paused for the rite of the salt.

Jonah had read about it—or rather, Wren had read about it, in fragments, and recited it back to Jonah in the dark of Alaric's cell while the prince slept his century-thin sleep: *the deep court salts its tables with grave-dirt, and each guest must taste it and speak a true thing, because lies curdle salt.* Wren had thought it superstition, a garbled detail from a secondhand account. Wren had been wrong. The salt was real, heaped in a bowl of black crystal and passed down the high table spoon by spoon, and each courtier took their measure and spoke.

"I regret the winter of 1213," said the drowned lady. True; the salt stayed bright.

"I remember my own name," said the courtier with the milkmaid's face, and the salt dimmed grey, and the other courtiers turned away from it as if from something indecent, and the page refilled the bowl without comment.

Then the bowl came to Jonah.

The hall went quiet in a way that had weight, like snowfall. The Regent's frost-pale eyes fixed on them, patient and bottomless. *Speak a true thing.* And Jonah understood, with the instinct of nine years spent reading dangerous rooms, that this was the real test—not the wolfsbane, not the hemlock, but this. The deep court did not care what Jonah's body could survive. It cared what Jonah's mouth would confess.

A lie would curdle the salt. And then—

Jonah took the spoon. The salt was cold and gritty on the tongue, tasting of earth and old stone and, very faintly, of the sea, though no sea had ever run beneath this mountain.

"I am afraid," Jonah said.

The salt stayed bright. Brighter than bright—it seemed almost to glitter, to catch the cold firelight and throw it back doubled, and a ripple went through the courtiers like wind through wheat. The drowned lady pressed her hand to her heart. The Regent tilted its antlered head.

"Yes," it said softly. "That is true. That is why you are still alive. The brave ones we eat quickly." It lifted its goblet of black glass. "To fear. May it keep you honest a little longer."

Jonah raised their own cup and drank, and the wine was laced with belladonna, and they tasted it, and named it aloud—"belladonna, and something else, something I don't know"—and the Regent smiled for the first time all evening, a thin white crack in the pallor.

"*Something I don't know,*" it repeated, delighted. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone at my table said those words? We are all so old here, taster. We know everything, and it has rotted us knowing it." It leaned forward, and the cold-fire light slid across its face like water over ice. "Tell me. When you were a child above—did you ever eat something you shouldn't have? Something that should have killed you?"

The question landed soft as a moth and heavy as a stone.

Because there *had* been such a night. Jonah had never told anyone. A summer evening, seven years old, in the garden behind a house whose location they had long ago trained themselves to forget; berries from a hedge, glossy and black, eaten by handfuls in the last of the light. Their mother finding them, going white, carrying them inside—her hands shaking, her voice low and fast, brewing something bitter over the stove, forcing it down Jonah's throat hour after hour through a long screaming night. *You'll be all right, little ledger, you'll be all right, your body learns, our bodies learn—*

*Our bodies learn.* As if it were inheritance. As if it were a birthright handed down like a ring or a name.

Jonah had filed the memory away for thirty years as a child's accident. Now, sitting at a mirrored banquet beneath the roots of the world, they felt it turn over like a card and show its other face. A child of the Eriksen line, dosed deliberately, taught to survive poison before they could write their own name—because a hundred years ago, their family had sold a prince's soul to this court, and perhaps the Eriksens had always known that someday a debt collector might come calling, and had bred their children to survive the collection.

"I ate nightshade berries when I was seven," Jonah heard themselves say. "I lived. My mother wasn't surprised."

The salt in the bowl, refilled by the silent page, gleamed bright and true.

The Regent studied them for a long moment, and something moved behind the frost of its eyes—something old, and hungry, and possibly, horribly, *wistful*.

"Eriksen," it said, tasting the name like a rare vintage. "We remember the Eriksens. They dined at this table once, you know. Your blood has sat in that chair." It gestured, gently, at the seat three places down—the empty one, the one set with silver no one used. "They also feared us. But they were useful, and polite, and they paid what they owed. I wonder which of these things you will turn out to be."

"My lord," Jonah said, "I only came for the food."

The drowned lady laughed her cistern laugh. The Regent did not laugh, but the crack of its smile widened by a hair.

"More eels," it said.

* * *

Later—much later, when the feast had wound down to its final sweet course and the courtiers drifted away in pairs toward whatever mirrored chambers awaited them—Jonah stood at a balustrade overlooking the lower hall, letting the last traces of aconite and hemlock and belladonna settle in their blood like sediment in a riverbed, and allowed themselves, briefly, to feel it.

Not triumph. Nothing so clean. Something stranger: the sense of a life's worth of damage suddenly *arranged*, like tools hung on a workshop wall in order of use. The arsenic years, the nightshade nights, the adder venom taken at nineteen because the guild masters said a smuggler of the Thornfell passes ought to survive a snakebite—all of it, every wretched deliberate dose, had been leading here, to a banquet beneath the mountain where survival itself was a language and Jonah was more fluent than anyone alive.

Wren would have made a joke about it. *You're the only person in history who could enjoy a dinner party in hell,* Wren would say, and then go quiet, and then say, *come home, come home, come home.* Jonah pressed the thought of Wren's voice against their ribs like a coal carried in cupped hands, and looked down at the hall below.

That was when they saw the commotion at the far doors.

Two courtiers had risen from their seats, heads turning in unison, the borrowed faces slack with something that on living features might have been alarm. The cold-fire in its iron cages flickered—all of it at once, every flame in the hall guttering like candles in a draft. And through the tall doors at the end of the hall, flanked by the unblinking guards with their antlered helms, the page with the fresh young face was hurrying toward the throne, leaning close to the Regent, speaking words Jonah was too far away to hear.

The Regent listened. Then it turned its head—slowly, precisely, like a weathervane finding the wind—and looked directly at the balustrade where Jonah stood.

Across the length of the hall, through air gone suddenly colder, the frost-pale eyes found Jonah's and held them.

"Taster," the Regent called, and its courteous voice filled the hall the way frost fills a window, corner to corner. "It seems we have another guest. Arrived by the *wrong door*." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "She is asking for you by name."

The cold sank through Jonah's silk doublet, through the ledger of scars, all the way to the bone.

*Serah Dole.* Half-consumed by whatever she'd woken behind the false gate, if the map's logic had done what Jonah feared—and walking into the deep court with Wren's stolen routes folded in her coat, and the queen's warrant folded beside them.

"She claims," the Regent continued, rising from its throne, antlers scraping a sound like ice cracking off a roof, "to know what you intend to steal."
