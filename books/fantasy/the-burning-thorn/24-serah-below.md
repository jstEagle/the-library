## 24. Serah Below

The deep court did not sleep, and so neither did we.

I learned this in the first hours after our descent, when I tried to mark the passage of time on the scrap of paper I'd begun keeping as a journal and found that my ink froze at the nib and thawed again without ever quite drying. The cold fire that lit the halls burned without warmth, and it never dimmed. There was no dusk to measure by, no bell, no change in the quality of the light—only the slow procession of courtiers through the mirrored corridors, wearing faces that were almost right and never quite, their footsteps making no sound on floors of black glass.

Jonah slept when they could, curled against a pillar carved with thorn-vines that had long since grown into the stone, their body a ledger even here, cataloguing every new poison in the air. I kept watch and drew. It was what I knew how to do: put the world down on paper until it made sense.

The world beneath the Thornfells refused to make sense.

The palace above was built to be seen from a distance—a statement of power legible from the valley floor. This place was its negative image. Where the queen's halls soared, these sank. Where the palace above was full of windows, this one had none; every surface was mirror or obsidian, and the light came from nowhere, cold fire crawling along the seams of the walls like frost given ambition. I mapped as much of it as I could from what we saw, though mapping a place with no fixed geometry was like trying to chart a dream. Corridors that took fifty steps going in took eighty coming back. A stair we descended twice delivered us to different floors. I began to suspect the deep court rearranged itself the way a sleeper shifts in bed—not maliciously, merely because stillness was not in its nature.

"They're watching us," Jonah murmured once, waking without transition from sleep to speech, the way they always did. "Don't look up."

I looked up. At the far end of the gallery, three figures stood among the pillars, dressed in the fashion of a century past, their faces smooth as eggs. When I turned fully toward them, they were gone—or rather, they had simply stopped being where they were, which in this place amounted to the same courtesy.

"Why do they let us wander?"

"Because we're already inside the trap," Jonah said. "A taster learns this early. The poison doesn't chase you. It waits for you to swallow."

* * *

It was on the third day—or what I decided to call the third day, since I needed the word for something—that we heard the screaming.

We had found a chamber I privately named the reliquary antechamber: a round room hung with veils of grey silk, each veil embroidered with names in a script that hurt to read too long. Alaric's name appeared on more than forty of them. We had been searching them for hours, looking for some thread that might lead us to the man himself, when the sound came echoing up through the walls—not from the corridors but from *below*, from whatever lay deeper than the deep court, a raw animal shrieking that went on and on and then stopped all at once, the way a snapped string stops.

Jonah was on their feet before the echo died. "That's a person."

"That's a person who woke something," I said, because I had spent two days studying the maps Serah Dole had stolen from me, reconstructing in my head the route she would take, and there was only one door her stolen logic could have led her to. "That's the false gate."

"You're certain."

"I drew the map," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out. "She'll have followed the fault-line the way it reads on paper. Down through the hollow seam, to the door under the glacier tongue. It opens. That's the cruelty of it—it opens beautifully. And then—" I gestured vaguely downward, at everything.

Jonah was already moving. "Then she's dying down there, and she knows things about the crown above that we need."

"She tried to have you killed."

"Yes." Jonah said it the way they announced the presence of nightshade in a soup: flatly, informatively, without personal grievance. "And now she's a source. Come on."

* * *

We found her at the edge of the Hollow Seam, an hour's careful descent along ledges of glassy stone, following the screaming's aftermath the way one follows smoke to fire.

Serah Dole, crown inquisitor, the woman who had cornered me in the archive with soft threats and harder eyes, was kneeling at the mouth of a shattered doorway, and she was coming apart.

There is no gentler way to say it. Whatever she had woken beyond the false gate had gotten into her the way frost gets into mortar—not violently, but thoroughly. Her left arm was still an arm. Her right had become something else: a lattice of pale crystal grown through the flesh, branching like frost-ferns, catching the cold firelight and throwing it back in colors that don't exist above ground. The crystallization had reached her jaw. When she spoke—and she was speaking, muttering, reciting—I could see the glint of it between her teeth.

She was reciting the oath of the crown inquisitors. Over and over. *I serve the realm unseen. I serve the realm unseen.* A prayer against the dark, worn smooth as a rosary bead.

"Serah." Jonah crouched at the edge of her awareness, hands open and empty, the way you approach a wounded animal. "Inquisitor Dole. Look at me."

Her eyes found Jonah and focused with a snap, like a lock turning. For a moment the fanatic was gone and what looked out of her face was just a woman in terrible pain, alone in the dark, a hundred miles under a mountain.

"Eriksen," she rasped. Crystal ground against crystal. "You're supposed to be dead. The queen's orders were—"

"I know what the orders were. You wrote them."

"The queen's orders were that you reach the gate alive and that nothing else come back up." She laughed, and the laugh broke into a cough, and the cough brought flecks of glittering blood. "You were bait, taster. Bait for the door. The crown has known the gate's location for a hundred years—they just needed someone the door would want. Someone with the old blood. You were never meant to be the hero of anything. You were meant to be the toll."

I felt Jonah go very still beside me—the particular stillness of a person absorbing a new poison, testing its weight.

"And yet," Jonah said quietly, "here you are, dying at the wrong door, and here I am, alive at the right one. So perhaps the crown's cartography is as flawed as yours."

Something moved in the darkness behind her. Through the shattered gate I could see a corridor of ice, and in the corridor, far off, a shape that was less a shape than an absence—a place where the cold firelight bent wrong, folded, swallowed. It was not approaching. It didn't need to. It had already taken what it wanted from her and was content to let the rest cure slowly, like meat.

"We can't fight that," I whispered.

"We're not going to fight it. We're going to bargain." Jonah turned back to Serah. "Inquisitor. You know how this ends otherwise. I've seen it before—the venom that takes a week to kill while it eats the mind first. You'll recite your oath until there's nothing left to recite it with. Or." They let the word hang.

"Or," Serah repeated. Her voice had gone sly, the inquisitor reassembling herself out of spare parts. "Or I give you something worth carrying. Is that the trade? Knowledge for passage? You can't carry me, Eriksen. Look at me. Whatever you do, I'm staying in this mountain."

"Then we trade for something else. Your knowledge of the court above. What the queen knows, when she knew it, who else is coming down behind you."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange we make sure the thing in that corridor stays bored," Jonah said. "And when we go up—we say you died at your post. A clean death. Your name comes home whole."

I understood then, watching her face, that this was the offer that mattered. Not passage. Not mercy. *Name.* Serah Dole had spent her life serving a realm that dealt in erasure—she had told me so herself in the archive, with something like pride. Now the erasure was being done to her, from the inside, and Jonah was offering the one currency the deep court could not mint.

"How do I know you won't just leave me?" she said.

"You don't," Jonah said. "But I'm a taster. My entire life is knowing exactly what a thing will do before I commit it to my body. Judge me accordingly."

* * *

We carried what we could of her—her pack, her dispatch case, the sealed orders she'd been given by the crown, which she surrendered with a bitterness like swallowing glass. She could not walk. The crystallization had reached her spine. So we made her as comfortable as the Hollow Seam allowed, propped against the wall where the cold firelight couldn't reach, and Jonah did something I didn't expect: they opened their own kit and measured out a dose of poppy-and-ash, the compound they used to dull pain without dulling wit.

"This will hurt going in," Jonah said. "Everything here does."

"Do it."

While the drug worked its slow grace, Serah talked. She talked the way drowning people grab at rope—fast, greedy, spilling everything.

The crown had known about the pact for a century. Not suspected—*known*. There was a ledger, kept in the queen's private vault, recording the annual renewal: a tithe of memories collected from the kingdom's own subjects, gathered under cover of the census, fed to the deep court through an intermediary the ledger called only *the Gardener*. Queen Isolde's grandmother had formalized it. Isolde herself renewed it every midwinter, in a ceremony the court believed was a blessing of the granaries.

"They've been feeding it anyway," I said. "Even with Alaric down here."

"Alaric is the anchor," Serah said. Her voice was slurring now, the crystal reaching the roots of her tongue. "The tithe is maintenance. But the pact itself—the original contract—it needs the heir's soul to stay valid. That's why they need you, Eriksen. Not to steal the soul back. To *renew the claim*. The Eriksen blood holds the seller's signature. If an Eriksen stands before the Regent and speaks the old words, the contract extends another hundred years, and the crown buys another century of winter-protection cheap." Her eyes, clouding, found Jonah's. "They don't want you to rescue anyone. They want you to sell someone else."

The silence after that was longer than the screaming had been.

Jonah's face did what it always did with bad news: nothing. But I had learned to read the nothing—to see the way their breathing shortened, the way their thumb pressed hard against the pad of their forefinger, counting, counting, the way they counted when the world handed them another weight to catalogue.

"My parents," Jonah said finally. "They died trying to undo it. Maret told us. And the crown let them die, because an undone contract was worth nothing, and a renewal was worth everything."

"The crown lets everyone die," Serah said. "That's what service is. I've known that since I was nine years old. I just thought—" The crystal had reached her throat; the words came out slower, ringing faintly, like a struck cup. "I thought if I served well enough, I'd be the exception."

Behind her, in the ice corridor, the absence shifted. Not approaching. Just reminding us it was there.

"We should go," I said.

"One more thing." Serah's hand—her left one, still flesh—caught Jonah's sleeve. "The Regent will offer you a bargain. It offers everyone a bargain; it's how it feeds. Whatever it offers you—and it will know exactly what you want most, that's the horror of it, it's *precise*—remember that the price is never written on the same page as the gift. Read both pages, Eriksen. Promise me you'll read both pages."

"Why?" Jonah asked. "Why warn us? You came down here to use us."

"Because you're the first people in twenty years who offered me a trade instead of an order." Her mouth twisted—the last expression she would make that was entirely her own. "Turns out I'd forgotten what that felt like. Go. Before I stop meaning any of this."

Jonah pressed a second vial into her flesh hand—the good one, the kind one, the one that let a body sleep instead of cure. "When you need it. Not before. While you still choose."

Serah Dole laughed once, softly, and the laugh rang like a small clear bell in all that dark. "A taster giving poison advice to an inquisitor. The realm unseen indeed."

* * *

We climbed back up in silence, and the silence held all the way to the gallery of veils, where Jonah finally stopped and stood with their back to a pillar, looking at nothing.

"Wren." Their voice was careful. Overly careful, the way it got when they were handling something fragile inside themselves. "What she said. About the renewal. About why the crown sent me down."

"I heard it."

"If I speak the old words—if I stand in front of the Regent and claim the Eriksen inheritance, even as a lie, even as a trick—the contract doesn't care whether I mean it. Contracts like this one run on blood and presence, not intention. Maret said my parents learned that the hard way." Jonah's thumb pressed their forefinger. Counting. "So every plan I've made since we came down here—every version of the heist I've rehearsed—now runs through the exact ritual the crown wants performed. To steal the soul, I may have to hold it. To hold it, I may have to claim it. And claiming it might be the sale, all over again, with my voice doing the selling."

"Then we find the difference," I said. "Between speaking the words and meaning them. Between holding the contract and signing it. There's always a difference, Jonah. That's what maps taught me—there's always a second reading of the terrain. The first one is just the one the powerful paid to have drawn."

Jonah looked at me then, and in the cold firelight their face was the one I had fallen in love with on an ordinary evening over ordinary wine: tired, guarded, and underneath it all, ferociously awake.

"Read both pages," they murmured. "All right. Then we'd better find out what's written on the other side of the Regent's generosity—because I think it's about to make us an offer."

As if the mountain had been listening—as if it had only been waiting for the words—as if the whole architecture of the deep court were one vast ear pressed to our conversation—the cold firelight in the gallery seams brightened, all at once, from white to a color like the moment before frostbite takes the finger.

And down the long mirrored corridor, walking without sound, came the Pale Regent's herald, bearing a tray on which two cups steamed gently in a place where nothing had ever been warm.

"The Regent," the herald said, in a voice like snow sliding off a roof, "invites the thorn that burns to tea."
