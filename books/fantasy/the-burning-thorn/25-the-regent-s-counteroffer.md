## 25. The Regent's Counteroffer

The Pale Regent did not keep a throne room. That was the first thing Jonah understood, standing in the antechamber of cold fire while a courtier with a borrowed face—Wren's face, or something very like it, worn slightly wrong at the mouth—gestured them forward through doors of black glass.

The Regent kept a kitchen.

It was absurd, after everything: the mirrored halls, the courtiers whose smiles sat an inch too high on their faces, the ceiling that rippled like the underside of a frozen lake. And here, at the heart of the deep court, a room of copper and stone and low blue flame, where something simmered in a pot no larger than a soup kettle, and the smell of it was so ordinary that Jonah's eyes stung. Rosemary. Bone broth. Salt.

"You are the taster," the Regent said, without turning. Their voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way sound travels under ice. "I have watched you eat tonight. Eleven courses. Three of them would have stopped the heart of anyone you love." They lifted the lid from the pot, and steam curled up pale as breath in winter. "You did not flinch once. Not even at the seventh course. I confess I admired that."

Jonah kept their hands loose at their sides. In the halls above, they had learned long ago that a taster's stillness was armor; here, it might be the only thing they owned. "Admiration from a soul-thief," they said. "I'll treasure it."

The Regent laughed. It was not an unkind sound, which made it worse. They turned then, and Jonah saw them properly for the first time: tall, narrow, dressed in robes the color of old snow, with a face like a face seen through frosted glass—features present, correct, and somehow always half-obscured. Their eyes were the blue of deep ice, the blue that means the water beneath is black.

"Thief," the Regent repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. You have come to steal. I knew it before your feet touched my stone. The door told me what you paid to pass." Something moved behind their frost-glass face—not quite a smile. "A summer. A small one. You were six, perhaps seven. There was a river."

Jonah's throat tightened. They had not spoken of the summer to anyone; had barely been able to hold its shape since the gate took it. But the Regent spoke of it the way one reads a ledger entry. *Paid in full: one summer, riverine, early childhood.* "You keep accounts," Jonah said.

"I keep *everything*," the Regent said softly. "That is the difference between me and the crown above. Sit. Eat. What is in this pot will not harm you—I know your tolerances better now than you do yourself, I think. Eleven courses, taster. Your body is a cathedral of poisons, and every stone of it was laid by someone else's hand."

Jonah did not sit. But they did not leave either, and the Regent seemed satisfied with that, ladling something into a bowl for themself and carrying it to a plain wooden table as though this were any kitchen in any inn above, as though the walls were not breathing faintly, in and out, like the flanks of a sleeping animal.

"You want Alaric Vane's soul," the Regent said. "You want to carry it up through the thorn-door and give it back to a kingdom that sold it. And you believe, because Wren Halvorsen believes, and because the prophecy says it in its pretty riddling way, that this will break the winter and free everyone." They blew across the surface of their bowl. Steam scattered. "Let me tell you what actually happens. Let me tell you who taught me to keep accounts."

* * *

"The first winter after the sale," the Regent said, "the crown sent soldiers. Two hundred of them, down through passes that no longer exist, led by a captain named Aldous Vane—the prince's uncle, if you care for the genealogy. They carried swords blessed by three temples and a warrant signed in the old king's hand. Do you know what happened to them?"

"They died," Jonah said.

"They starved," the Regent corrected. "For eleven days they marched in circles inside my halls, because I folded the corridors, and I fed them nothing, and on the twelfth day the captain knelt in this very kitchen—" they touched the table with one long finger, "—and asked to negotiate. And do you know what he offered me? Not his own soul. He offered me *peasants*. Criminals. The names of people the crown wanted gone anyway. He had a list, taster. He had brought it with him, folded in his breastplate, in case the war went badly. The crown above has always known exactly what I eat, and has always known exactly where to find it."

The pot murmured. The blue flames leaned and straightened. Jonah found they had sat down after all, though they did not remember choosing to.

"And the pact was renewed," they said. Their voice sounded far away to themselves.

"Every twenty-five years," the Regent said. "By Isolde's grandfather. By Isolde's father. By Isolde herself, nine years ago, in her third month of reign, when she came down through the root-door wearing a traveling cloak and no crown, because she did not wish to be recognized." The Regent's frost-glass face tilted. "She sat where you are sitting. She ate from this pot. She was charming. She renewed the terms, adjusted the delivery schedule—your guild handles the transport, did you know that? Every solstice, certain carts go into the mountain and do not come out. Your masters call it the salt trade. The salt is real. It is simply not all there is in the carts."

The floor seemed to tilt. Jonah thought of the guild hall, of Master Corvane's heavy ring tapping the map-table, of the solstice runs that were always given to orphans and nobodies, the ones whose absence would be noticed by no one. *The family that owns you isn't family.* They had written that truth in their own heart years ago and thought they had finished learning it.

"I don't believe you," they said, because it was required.

"No," the Regent agreed pleasantly. "But you will check. That is what makes you dangerous, taster—you verify everything. It is why you have survived eleven courses tonight." They rose, drifting toward a shelf of stone jars, each sealed with wax and stamped with a sigil Jonah recognized with a sick lurch: the royal seal of Vane, unchanged for a century. "Here. Proof, since you insist."

They set a scroll on the table. The wax seal was broken and resealed many times over, layers upon layers like the rings of a tree. Jonah unrolled it with hands that did not shake—they had trained even that out of themselves—and read.

*Terms of continuation, being the third renewal of the Accord of Root and Crown...*

The language was old but not archaic. The signatures were unmistakable. Edvard II. Maren the Regent-Queen. And at the bottom, in a hand Jonah knew intimately—had watched sign death warrants and feast invitations and the quarterly allotments for the taster's office, a hand with a distinctive hard crossbar on every T—

*Isolde, by grace, Queen of the Thornfell Reach.*

Nine years ago. Nine years ago, while Jonah had stood behind her chair tasting her food, guarding her body with their poisoned blood, she had walked down into the dark and signed away another generation of souls to keep her winter gentle and her throne secure.

"She knows about the prophecy," Jonah said. It was not a question. The realization arrived whole, cold, complete. "She's known since Wren found the book. Serah Dole. The archive seals. The way the guards started watching the gates. She wasn't trying to stop us."

"She was trying to *schedule* you," the Regent said. "A prophecy is a delivery window, taster. The thorn that burns descends, steals back what was sold, and the winter finishes falling—yes, she has read it too, she has had scholars reading it aloud to her for months. And when the winter breaks catastrophically, as it must, the crown needs someone to blame. Someone invisible enough to be guilty of anything. Someone whose family name is already written in the oldest pages of the crime." The Regent leaned close, and their breath smelled of snowmelt and old iron. "She does not want you to fail, Jonah Eriksen. She wants you to *succeed*, loudly, and die in the doing. Your corpse and your stolen name will buy her another century of innocent grief. The kingdom will weep for the traitor who broke the winter, renew the pact in their sorrow, and never once ask what their queen signed in the dark."

* * *

Jonah stood in the doorway of the kitchen for a long time after the Regent dismissed them—or perhaps the Regent did not dismiss them; perhaps Jonah simply left, walking because walking was the only thing their body remembered how to do.

The mirrored halls passed around them. Courtiers turned to watch with faces they had borrowed from the living—a young man wearing Jonah's own face, badly, smiled at them from a niche, and Jonah did not break stride. Above, through a hundred fathoms of stone, was the palace where they had spent ten years making themselves nothing. Ten years of tasting. Ten years of swallowing ruin so that a woman could eat safely while she sold other people's souls by the cartload.

*Your body is a cathedral of poisons, and every stone of it was laid by someone else's hand.*

They thought of the first day, aged fourteen, in the taster's cellars, when the old taster—a gray man named Pell, who had died of nothing at all, simply worn through—had held out a cup of laced wine and said, *This is how you matter, little ghost. This is how a nobody becomes necessary.* Necessary. Not loved. Not trusted. Necessary, the way a lock is necessary, the way a wall is necessary. Useful until replaced.

And Queen Isolde had been kind to them. That was the poison that worked slowest. She had remembered their name—no. She had *given* them their name, the clean anonymous one, Jonah Eriksen, the name that fit so neatly over the erased one. She had praised their discretion. She had once, memorably, set aside the sweetest pear from her plate and sent it down to the taster's quarters, and Jonah had eaten it like a sacrament, like proof that service was a kind of belonging.

The pear had probably cost her nothing. Everything had cost someone else.

They found Wren in the guest chamber the deep court had assigned them—a room of velvet and shadow, beautiful the way a trap is beautiful, all soft edges arranged around a hard center. Wren looked up from their maps, ink on their fingers, and whatever was in Jonah's face made them stand at once.

"What happened?" Wren asked. "What did it offer you?"

"It didn't offer me anything yet." Jonah's voice came out level, which surprised them. Underneath the levelness was something vast and shaking, like a lake before the ice cracks. "It showed me a signature instead. Wren. The queen signed the renewal. Nine years ago. Her own hand. And the guild carries the deliveries—the solstice carts, the ones they give to the ones nobody misses. We were never smugglers, some of those runs. We were *couriers*. I've been carrying souls into this mountain since I was sixteen years old."

Wren crossed the room and took their hands. Ink-stained fingers, warm, human, real. "Jonah—"

"She'll let us die here." Saying it aloud made it solid, made it have weight and edges. "That's the plan. That's been the plan since the archive. I'm the bait and the scapegoat and the delivery mechanism, all in one convenient body. She probably chose me *because* of the name. Because when they dig through the records afterward, they'll find the Eriksens sold Alaric, and the story will write itself, and no one will ever look higher than a dead smuggler's orphan."

Wren's grip tightened. "Then we finish it our way," they said. "Not hers. Not the Regent's. Ours. We take Alaric's soul back, and we put it in front of the whole court, alive and testifying, and we make her look at what she signed."

"You can't force a queen to be ashamed," Jonah said. "Believe me. I've watched her eat for ten years. Nothing shames her."

"Then we make her *afraid*," Wren said quietly. "There's a difference, and you of all people know it."

Jonah laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. Through the velvet dark of the chamber, somewhere far below, they could feel it: the reliquary, the burning seed, the small imprisoned sun that had been fed upon nightly for a hundred years while kings and queens slept warm. Alaric Vane, worn thin as winter paper, barely remembering his own name. Sold by Jonah's blood. Renewed by Isolde's hand. Fed upon by the hungry court. Everyone complicit except the stolen and the dead.

"The Regent said something else," Jonah said. "Before I left. They said the reason they've kept the pact so carefully, the reason they fold the corridors and starve armies rather than hunt, is that willing gifts taste different. Better. That stolen souls are gruel and given ones are feasts, and the crown has never once, in a hundred years, given anything willingly." They stared at their hands—taster's hands, poison-honed, steady as stone. "I think they're going to offer me a bargain. And I think I need to hear it before we move against the reliquary. Because if there's a door out of this that doesn't end with us dying for Isolde's sins—"

"There isn't," Wren said. "There's only the door we choose."

A knock at the chamber door—soft, precise, wrong. Neither of them had heard footsteps in the corridor, because there were never footsteps in the corridor unless the court wanted you to hear them.

Wren's hand went to the knife at their belt. Jonah raised the other hand slowly, *wait*, and crossed to the door, and opened it.

A courtier stood there in robes of gray silk, wearing a borrowed face Jonah almost recognized—an old woman's face, kind at the eyes—with a silver tray held out before it. On the tray lay a single card of black glass, and etched into its surface in letters that gleamed like frost:

*The Pale Regent requests the pleasure of the taster's company. Alone. Now.*

And beneath it, smaller, in a different hand, scratched hastily as if by someone who should not have been writing at all:

*Come quickly. Before the banquet. —A.V.*

Jonah looked at the card for a long moment. Then they looked back at Wren, whose face was already asking the question.

"He's awake," Jonah said. "Alaric. And he wants to talk to me before the Regent gets what it's waiting for."
