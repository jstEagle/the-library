## 26. Wren's Choice

They came for Wren in the hour the deep court called morning, though no sun had ever reached this far beneath the mountain to prove it.

The summons arrived not as a messenger but as a change in the light. The cold fire that ran along the corridors of the mirrored palace—the pale blue-green flame that burned without heat and cast shadows pointing the wrong way—shifted its hue toward silver, and where it touched the walls, a door appeared that had not been there before. It stood open. Beyond it, stairs descended in a spiral so long that Wren could see only the first few steps before the dark took them.

A courtier waited beside the door. Wren had learned, in the days since their descent, to read these creatures the way one reads weather: by what they borrowed. This one wore the face of a young woman with a milkmaid's freckles and a scholar's hands, ink-stained at the nails, and the combination was wrong in a way that made Wren's teeth ache. No one was born with both those things. Faces down here were assembled like arguments.

"The Pale Regent requests the cartographer," the courtier said. Its voice was pleasant and slightly too slow, as if each word were being unwrapped from paper. "Alone."

"And if I decline?"

The courtier smiled with the milkmaid's mouth, and behind the smile something patient moved, vast and old and unhurried, the way a glacier is patient. "You are already alone," it said. "That is rather the point of the invitation."

Wren looked back once, down the corridor toward the guest chambers where Jonah slept—or did not sleep; Jonah slept the way a knife sleeps, all edges folded away—and then stepped through the door.

* * *

The Regent's map room sat at the very root of the palace, deeper even than the throne hall, and it was the most beautiful room Wren had ever wanted to burn.

It was vast as a cathedral and lit from below. The floor was a single slab of black glass, and upon it, rendered in veins of cold fire, lay the world: every range, every river, every coastline of the kingdom above and the country beneath, drawn in lines of light that pulsed faintly, like the circulatory system of something alive. Mountains rose from it in sculpted glass. The Thornfells stood at its center, and beneath them—this was the part that stopped Wren's breath—a second Thornfells, inverted, its peaks driving downward into darkness, mirror-perfect, so that the two ranges met somewhere in the middle of the earth like hands pressed palm to palm in prayer or in arrest.

Wren had spent eleven years making maps. They knew what a map was: an argument about reality, a claim staked in ink, always partial, always lying a little, because the world refused to be flattened and something always got left off. But this—this was not a map. This was the territory dreaming of itself.

"You are admiring my work," said the Pale Regent.

It stood at the far edge of the floor-globe, and up close, for the first time, Wren saw that it was not wearing a face at all. Every courtier below had worn a borrowed face, but the Regent's features were its own, and they were terrible in their plainness: a long grey countenance like weathered limestone, eyes the color of the space between stars, hair fine as frost-fern. It was beautiful the way a winter sky is beautiful—from a distance, and never for long.

"It is the finest cartography I have ever seen," Wren said, because flattery cost nothing here, and because it was true, and truth was the only coin they had left that hadn't been debited.

"I drew it myself." The Regent drifted along the edge of the glowing world, and where its hem passed, the little rivers brightened. "Over four hundred years. I have had time. Time is the one thing this place gives in abundance, and the one thing your kind spends like drunkards." It turned those starless eyes on Wren. "Which is why you interest me. You have spent eleven years learning to write the world down. Imagine having forever to get it right."

Wren kept still. Below their feet, a glass sea glittered.

"I know why I've been brought here," they said. "I'd rather hear it plainly. I find I trust plainness more than gifts."

"Good." Something that might have been approval crossed the limestone face. "Plainness, then. Your queen's ancestor sold me a soul, and your lover has stolen it back, and when they fail—and they will fail—I will be owed a century of interest besides. That is the shape of the next few days as I see them." The Regent gestured, and the cold fire in the floor rearranged itself: the Thornfells blazed, and within them a thread of light descended, the smuggler roads, the goat-tracks, the hidden ways, all of it laid bare. Wren felt sick. Every secret Jonah had ever whispered against their ear in the dark, every path traded like a kiss, was burning here in light for anyone to read.

"But you," the Regent continued, "I would spare. You especially."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the first honest thing to walk into my court in a hundred years." The Regent said it without warmth, as a fact of geology. "Your taster lies beautifully—they must, it is their trade—but you, cartographer, you look at my inverted mountains and you *want to correct the survey*. I can see it. You noticed that my southern ridge is drawn three furlongs too far east, and it is taking everything in you not to say so."

Wren's jaw tightened. It was three furlongs. It had been driving them mad since the moment they'd seen it.

"I am offering you a position," the Regent said. "Court cartographer. Mine, not the crown's. You will have four hundred years to finish what I began—to map not merely the world but the *deep* world, the roots of things, the places no living hand has charted. You will have my library, which contains surveys of lands that drowned before your kingdom had a name. You will be remembered—not as a footnote in some guild registry, but as the hand that drew the under-earth. And in exchange—" the starless eyes held steady, almost gentle, "—you will step aside. Leave the taster to their fate. Walk out through the thorn-door tonight, alone, and live a long life knowing exactly what you bought with your silence. Immortality, prestige, and the work you were made for. All of it yours. All it costs is one person."

The words settled over the map-room like snowfall.

Wren thought of many things at once, the way one does at the bottom of a fall. They thought of the archive above, the dust and the slanted afternoon light, the smell of vellum and iron-gall ink. They thought of the day they had found Jonah's face sketched in a book a century old, and how wonder had curdled in their chest like milk, and how they had walked home through streets that suddenly seemed made of glass. They thought of the hollow inn, and Old Maret's cracked voice saying *Eriksen* like a curse, and Jonah's face when the name landed—Jonah, who had spent a whole life sanding themselves smooth so no one would ever look too closely, standing there finally *seen*, and hating it.

And they thought—this was the thought that decided everything—of the night on the passes, storm screaming, Wren's ankle broken and useless, riding on Jonah's back down a slope no sane creature would descend. Jonah had carried them for six hours. Had not complained once. Had, at the worst of it, when Wren had sobbed from pain into the frozen collar of their coat, said, quietly, against the wind: *Talk to me. Tell me about a map you love. Any map.* And Wren had talked, delirious, about a survey of the Saltmarsh coast done in 1311, inaccurate and gorgeous, and Jonah had listened as though it were a song, and had kept walking, and had gotten them down alive.

Love, Wren had learned, was not a feeling. It was labor. It was carrying someone down a mountain.

"No," Wren said.

The Regent tilted its head, limestone and patience. "Consider the arithmetic. One mortal life, already forfeit by prophecy, against four centuries of yours. Even your kind's poets would call that a fair trade."

"You misunderstand the offer you're actually making." Wren's voice shook, and they let it. There was no armor here worth wearing. "You're not offering me forever. You're offering me forever *without them*. A library full of maps and no one to show them to. Four hundred years of getting the world right and not one evening of sitting across a table from someone while they eat, complaining about the wine, arguing about whether north should go at the top." They laughed, and it came out cracked. "I've seen what a century of being fed on does to a person. Alaric Vane can barely remember his own name. What do you imagine four hundred years of being fed on does to a *cartographer*? You wouldn't be buying my skill. You'd be buying a hollowed thing that still holds a pen, drawing your mountains until it forgets there was ever anything south of the ink."

The Regent was silent for a long moment. In the floor below, the glass sea lapped soundlessly at its glass shores.

"They will die here," it said at last, and now there was something else in the voice, something almost like weariness, ancient and cold. "Or worse. I have been patient with them because patience costs me nothing. But the debt is owed, cartographer, and debts in this place are not metaphors. When I collect, I collect *all*. You cannot carry them out of the dark on your back. There is no slope gentle enough."

"I know," Wren said. "I've mapped the way out. I know exactly how impossible it is." They lifted their chin. "But here is what you don't understand about people who make maps. We don't draw the world as it is. We draw it as a set of instructions: *here is how you cross*. Here is how you get from where you are to where you're going, past the marshes, past the cliffs, past everything that says you can't. Every map I've ever loved was a promise that somewhere was reachable." They stepped closer to the Regent, close enough to feel the cold coming off it like the breath off a glacier, and did not flinch. "So no. I won't take your immortality. I won't take your library. I choose the mortal one with the poisoned blood and the smuggler's scars and the terrible habit of volunteering for suicide missions. I choose them broke, hunted, doomed, and *specific*. Forever is just duration. They are a direction."

Something flickered across the Regent's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. Grief, perhaps. Or recognition. Four hundred years is a long time to sit alone in a beautiful room.

"So be it," the Regent said quietly. "Then hear the rest, since you insist on plainness. Tomorrow night I hold the Rite of Continuance, the ceremony by which the pact is renewed. Your taster will attend. So will you. And at that ceremony, before the whole court, I will offer them the same bargain I offered you—life, freedom, forgetting—and they will take it, because everyone takes it eventually. Everyone chooses the warm room over the cold road. I have watched a hundred years of brave souls learn this about themselves." The starless eyes narrowed, and for the first time Wren saw hunger in them, vast and patient and very old. "Come and watch them learn it too. Perhaps then you'll want the position after all."

"Perhaps," Wren said, and did not mean it, and saw that the Regent knew they did not mean it, and understood, in that moment, that the Regent had known all along—that this entire audience had been theater, a move in a game whose board was the floor beneath their feet.

The Regent turned back to its map. "You may go. The courtier will return you to your chambers." A pause. "Cartographer. For what it is worth—you are the better artist. My southern ridge has been wrong for two hundred years, and no one before you ever had the courage to notice."

Wren walked to the door. At the threshold they stopped, because the map below had changed again: the thread of light marking the smuggler roads had shifted, and now a new line glowed there, faint, freshly drawn, running from the guest chambers down through the mirrored halls to the Regent's reliquary—the vault where a small burning seed hung in its cage of glass and cold fire.

Someone had added a route to the map. Someone was planning a theft.

Wren kept their face perfectly still, the way Jonah had taught them—*be no one, be nothing, be furniture*—and let the courtier lead them away, heart hammering a smuggler's rhythm against their ribs.

Tomorrow night, then. The Rite of Continuance. The reliquary. The seed.

Back in their chamber, Jonah was awake, sitting up in the dark, and the look on their face told Wren everything: they had heard. Of course they had heard; the walls down here were thin as parchment and twice as full of eavesdroppers. Jonah's eyes were wet and furious and shining.

"You turned down forever," Jonah said hoarsely. "For me. You absolute idiot."

Wren crossed the room, took Jonah's scarred hands in their ink-stained ones, and kissed each knuckle like sealing a document. "I turned down forever," they said, "because I'd rather have one true map than a thousand blank ones. Now listen carefully, love, and keep your voice low—"

Outside the door, the cold fire burned silver, and somewhere above them both, the long winter finished gathering itself to fall.

"—because tomorrow night, we're going to need a route out of this place, and I happen to know one that isn't on any map yet. But it will be by dawn. I'm going to draw us one."
