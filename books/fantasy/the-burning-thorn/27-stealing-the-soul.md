## 27. Stealing the Soul

The reliquary was carried into the hall on the shoulders of six courtiers whose faces had been borrowed from dead men.

Jonah watched them come from their place at the long table's end—a seat they had earned three nights ago by eating, without flinching or dying, a dish of pale mushrooms that had turned two of the Regent's own servants gray-lipped and silent before the second course was cleared. The deep court did not applaud. It *inclined*. A hundred cold-fire eyes had tilted toward Jonah like flowers toward a sun that did not exist here, and something in the tilt had said: *this one is useful*, and usefulness, Jonah knew from both courts above and below, was the only currency that never devalued.

Now the courtiers set the reliquary upon the dais with the tenderness of midwives, and Jonah understood why Wren had spent a week mapping nothing but this room.

It was a thing of black glass and silver root-work, tall as a child, shaped—Jonah's stomach tightened at the recognition—like a thorn tree in winter. Bare branches of etched metal spread from its crown, and from those branches hung small glass vessels, each one dim, each one holding a curl of gray smoke that might once have been color. Trophies. Souls taken across centuries by whatever bargain the deep court had struck with whoever sat on the throne above. And at the center of it all, cradled where a heart would sit if the tree had one, burned a seed.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. An actual seed, no larger than a hazelnut, suspended in a lens of clear crystal, and it burned. Its light was the first warm light Jonah had seen since passing beneath the root-door—the color of hearth-coal, of candleflame, of the lamp Wren trimmed each night over their maps—and looking at it made Jonah's chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

*Alaric Vane,* Jonah thought. *A hundred years, and still you burn.*

The ceremony was called the Tithing. Old Maret's testimony had not covered it; nothing in the archives above had; but three nights of sitting at this table, tasting what was set before the Regent, had taught Jonah its rhythm. Once each season—or what passed for a season in a place without sky—the Pale Regent fed.

The court assembled in tiers of borrowed faces. Musicians played instruments made of ice and gut, producing notes like water dripping upward. And the Regent rose from a throne of woven roots to begin the slow, ritual walk down the length of the hall, pausing at each vessel on the reliquary's branches, pressing one long white finger to the glass, and drinking.

That was the word for it, though the mouth barely moved. The gray smoke would stir, thin itself through the crystal, and slide into the Regent's fingertip as though the finger were a wick. With each soul the Regent drank, the cold fire in the sconces brightened. The music swelled. The courtiers' borrowed faces grew briefly more convincing—color rising in cheeks that had none of their own blood in them—and Jonah understood, finally and completely, what the winter above was.

It was a pantry door held shut.

Every soul in that tree was a storehouse. Every Tithing was a meal. And the endless winter that shielded Thornfell's kingdom from its enemies was not protection at all—it was appetite, stretched taut across a century, paid for nightly out of a prince who had been sold at nineteen years old and had burned faithfully ever since so that strangers he'd never met could sleep safely behind walls of frost.

Jonah tasted nothing at the banquet. They were past hunger. Their body was a ledger, and tonight every line in it pointed forward to a single entry.

* * *

They had rehearsed it the way Wren rehearsed a map: obsessively, in fragments, in the dark hours when the deep court slept—or did whatever passed for sleeping among things that wore other people's faces.

"The reliquary is never unguarded," Wren had whispered, drawing the hall from memory on the floor of the cell-like chamber the court had granted them—"guest quarters," though the walls sweated cold and the door had no lock, which meant it was not a gift but a display case. "But it moves. Procession days, it travels the hall. That's your window."

"And the crystal?"

Wren had been quiet a long moment, charcoal hovering. "I've been thinking about that. About you." They had looked up, and in the cold firelight their face had been so dear to Jonah that it hurt worse than any poison ever had. "You told me once—your first week as a taster—you told me the trick isn't the tongue. Anyone can train a tongue. The trick is the hands. You can lift a poisoned fig off a king's plate and replace it with a clean one while the whole court watches, because everyone watches the wrong thing. Everyone watches the food."

"Everyone watches the miracle," Jonah had said.

"Exactly." Wren's smile had been thin and fierce. "So give them a miracle."

* * *

The miracle began with a mushroom.

Jonah had spent two days cultivating it—not growing it, that was beyond even a smuggler's craft, but *selecting* it, learning which of the pale fungal growths in the lower galleries carried the toxin the deep court prized, and more importantly, which ones carried it in a form that bloomed slowly. A fast poison was an insult here. A slow one was theater.

On the night of the Tithing, Jonah requested—through the fawning, faceless steward who had appointed himself Jonah's keeper—an audience favor. A taster's tribute. Let the guest from above offer the Regent a delicacy of the surface world, prepared in the surface fashion, as thanks for the court's hospitality.

The Regent, amused—it showed only in the way the cold around it seemed to sharpen, like a smile made of weather—assented.

Jonah carried the dish themselves. That was part of the theater too; a servant would have spoiled it. They walked the length of the hall with the plate balanced on one palm, and every step was a lesson they had learned at fourteen years old in a palace kitchen above: spine loose, gaze soft, attention everywhere and fixed nowhere. Let them look at the mushroom. Let them wonder if the surface guest was brave or mad. Let them watch the food.

The dish was simple, as all impressive dishes are. The mushroom, sliced thin as paper, arranged in a spiral, dressed with oil pressed from pine kernels and a salt scraped from the roots of the thorn-tree itself—which Jonah had gathered openly, reverently, letting three separate courtiers see them do it, so that the gathering would be remembered and the rest forgotten.

They knelt at the dais steps and offered the plate up.

The Regent descended. Up close, it smelled like the inside of a glacier, like stone that had never been warm, and its face—if face it was—was smooth as an egg, with features that arrived only when you looked directly at them and dissolved when you looked away. It regarded the mushroom. It regarded Jonah.

"You taste for the queen of frost and iron," it said. Its voice came from somewhere behind the sound of it, the way thunder comes from behind lightning.

"I taste," Jonah said. "What I taste for has changed."

A ripple went through the court. Truth, offered plainly, was apparently rarer below than above.

The Regent took the slice between two fingers that did not bend so much as *arrange* themselves, and ate.

And Jonah—who had dosed that single slice with enough slow-blooming toxin to fell a horse, who had eaten three slices of the same themselves that morning to prove the harmlessness and to prime their own ledgered blood—waited.

The bloom took eleven minutes. Jonah counted them against their heartbeat, which was doing its best to hammer its way out of their ribs and had to be disciplined back down, breath by breath, the way Serah Dole had once taught them to steady a hand over a suspect cup. Eleven minutes during which the Regent finished the Tithing's opening rites, drifting down the reliquary's row of vessels, drinking gray soul after gray soul, the cold fires climbing brighter, the music swelling, the whole court leaning into its feeding like a congregation into prayer.

At minute nine, the first tremor.

It was subtle—a hesitation in the Regent's drift, a pause at the seventh vessel a half-breath too long. But the court saw it. The court *felt* it, the way a household feels a stumble in the master's step, and a murmur ran the tiers like wind through wheat. The Regent, who had not paused in a century of Tithings, had paused.

At minute ten, the cold fires guttered.

Only for a moment. Only a flicker, a brownout in the blue-white blaze, but in a court lit entirely by cold fire, a flicker is an eclipse. The murmur became a rustle became a low, rising hiss of alarm, and every eye—every borrowed, hungry, terrified eye—fixed on the Regent on its dais, on the sovereign who might be faltering, might be dying, might be—

Everyone watched the miracle.

No one watched the taster, who had risen from the dais steps with their empty plate, who walked with a servant's unhurried gait along the reliquary's flank, who stood for one moment beside the black-glass thorn tree with their head bowed as if in awe, as if in prayer, as if merely a guest overcome by the splendor of the court beneath the world.

Their hand went up. It looked like devotion. It looked like a gesture of homage, palm open toward the burning heart of the tree.

The sleight was one Wren had unknowingly named weeks ago, in a sunlit archive above, talking about maps: *the eye goes where the meaning is.* The meaning, right now, was a faltering god. The meaning was a court's collective gasp. The meaning was anywhere but the steady, poison-calloused fingers of a royal food-taster finding the seam in a crystal lens that Wren had sworn existed—finding it by touch alone, in the dark of their own bowed head, the way Jonah had found a hundred seams in a hundred kitchens: the gap in the larder grate, the false bottom of the spice chest, the hairline flaw in the vial that meant someone had already opened it.

The lens parted along its seam with a whisper no louder than snowfall.

And Jonah Eriksen lifted Alaric Vane's soul out of a hundred years of captivity the way they had lifted a thousand poisoned morsels from a thousand plates: gently, completely, and without anyone seeing.

It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. Warmth flooded their closed fist like a captured coal, and through the skin of their palm Jonah felt—not heard, not saw, *felt*—a flicker of something ancient and exhausted and astonished. A presence turning over in its sleep. A voice worn down to an ember:

*...who...?*

"Hold on," Jonah breathed, so softly the words were less than air. "I'm getting you out."

They let the empty lens swing shut behind their retreating hand. The seed vanished into the lining of their sleeve, into a pocket Wren had sewn there against their skin—insulated with wool and waxed silk, because Wren thought of everything, because Wren had lain awake drawing diagrams of soul-containment based on nothing but intuition and love, and Jonah could have wept with gratitude for it if there had been time.

There was no time.

* * *

Because the Regent recovered.

Later—much later, in the running and the screaming and the years after—Jonah would replay those seconds endlessly, hunting the mistake. Perhaps the toxin had bloomed faster than intended in a body that was not a body. Perhaps the Regent had felt the lens part; the deep court was old, and old things feel drafts. Perhaps it had simply turned at the wrong moment, the way fate turns, the way a card falls.

But turn it did, at the eleventh minute, just as Jonah's hand left the reliquary, just as the warmth of the stolen soul was still sinking through cloth into skin—and the cold fires came back all at once, blazing, furious, brighter than they had been in a century, and the Regent's smooth face found the center of the hall and found the taster standing beside its tree.

Its finger rose. It pointed. And the temperature of the entire cavern dropped so suddenly that frost bloomed across the musicians' instruments mid-note, across the wine in the cups, across the borrowed faces of a hundred courtiers, cracking several of them like eggshells and revealing—briefly, horribly—what was underneath.

"*Thief.*"

The word went through the hall like a blade through canvas.

Jonah did not run. Running was the instinct, and instinct was what got tasters killed; the training, the whole long brutal apprenticeship of the poisoned body, came down to one commandment: *when the moment breaks, be boring.* So Jonah bowed. Deeply. Gracefully. The bow of a guest who has been misaccused, baffled, wounded—and beneath the fall of their own hair, their eyes swept the hall and found the exits Wren had mapped and mapped again: the service gallery behind the musicians, the root-stair down to the flooded vaults, the long dark throat of the corridor that led, eventually, impossibly, home.

"The guest does not understand," Jonah said, and their voice did not shake, and they were proud of that forever after.

"The guest," the Regent said, gliding down from the dais, and the frost came with it, spreading from its feet in feathering sheets, "*will open its hand.*"

Slowly—slowly, the way innocence moves—Jonah raised both palms. Empty. Of course empty; the seed was sewn into their sleeve, wrapped in wool, warm against the pulse point of their wrist, and the Regent's terrible gaze slid over the sleeves because sleeves were clothing and clothing was beneath its notice, because even gods have blind spots, because Wren had thought of everything.

For one suspended moment, the hall hung still. The Regent stared at Jonah's empty hands. The court stared at the Regent. Somewhere in the tiers, a courtier whose borrowed face had cracked was quietly, wetly rearranging itself.

And then the reliquary spoke.

Not in words. In absence. Every gray soul in every hanging vessel surged against its glass at once—smoke pressing outward, swirling, straining toward the taster with the empty sleeve—and the vessels rang like struck bells, and the Regent whirled toward its tree and understood in the same instant Jonah understood:

The souls knew. The stolen things always know when one of them is being carried free.

"*SEIZE THEM.*"

Jonah ran.

Not the boring walk anymore—the moment for boring was spent, spent gloriously, bought and paid for, and now the smuggler's legs took over, the goat-track legs, the legs that had flown down scree slopes in hail with a quarter-tonne of contraband brandy on their back. They went over the banquet table rather than around it, plates scattering, and behind them the cold rose like a tide with a voice, and ahead of them the service gallery gaped open and dark, and somewhere far above—through a mile of stone and root and frozen earth—somewhere under the blizzard that was even now beginning to finish falling, Wren was waiting at the thorn-door with a lamp lit and a map clutched to their chest, watching the passage for a shape that had better arrive.

"They'll blame us for the storm," Wren had said, weeks ago, in another life. "When the pact breaks, the whole sky comes down, and the kingdom will need someone to hate."

"Then we'd better bring them something worth the hating," Jonah had answered. "Bring them their prince."

The seed pulsed against Jonah's wrist like a second heart as they plunged into the dark, alarms howling behind them, frost chasing their heels, the whole hungry court rising from its feast.

*Hold on,* Jonah told the ember in their sleeve, and ran.
