## 28. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The first sign was the light.

Wren had spent three days underground, and their eyes had learned the deep court's palette—the cold-fire blue of the lanterns, the silvered skin of courtiers wearing borrowed faces, the way shadows fell *upward* in certain corridors, as though the mountain's heart pulled at them like a second sun buried in stone. So when the mirrored hall ahead began to glow a different color entirely, Wren stopped walking and knew, before they could name it, that something above had broken.

It was white. Not the white of moonlight or frost-glass. It was the white of a sky gone blind.

"Jonah." Their voice came out smaller than they intended. "Look at the walls."

The mirrored surfaces—polished obsidian, each pane reflecting some inverted memory of the palace above—had begun to flicker. And in them Wren could see snow. Not falling snow: *driving* snow, horizontal, violent, the kind that erased roads and swallowed travelers whole. The reflections showed a kingdom being scoured clean. Rooftops vanishing under drifts. The Thornfell passes closing one by one like eyes.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound like a great bell struck underwater—a single tolling note that traveled up through the soles of their boots and settled in their teeth.

Jonah reached back without looking, found Wren's hand, and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "That's the reliquary alarm," they said. "We have minutes. Maybe less."

"The winter—"

"I know." Jonah's face, lit by the flickering white, looked carved from something older than fear. "I know what I did. What we did. Walk now. Grieve later."

Wren walked.

* * *

They ran through halls that were coming apart at the seams—or rather, coming *together*, which was worse. The deep court's architecture had always felt provisional to Wren, like a drawing an artist kept revising; corridors rearranged themselves when unobserved, staircases breathed. Now the whole place shuddered with every tolling of that drowned bell, and the borrowed-faced courtiers stood frozen in their alcoves and doorways, all of them turned toward the direction of the reliquary, all of them utterly silent.

One of them caught Wren's eye as they passed. It wore the face of a young woman, or the memory of one—smooth, symmetrical, slightly too perfect, like a portrait restored by someone who had never seen the original. Its mouth opened. No sound came out. But its expression was one Wren recognized from archives and old engravings, from statues of saints and martyrs:

Hunger interrupted.

"They can feel it," Wren panted. "The soul. They feed tonight, don't they? That's what Maret said—the deep court feeds on Alaric nightly."

"Then they're about to be very hungry," Jonah said, "and very angry, and we are going to be exactly between the two. Left here. Trust me on the left—the right corridor loops back to the banquet hall and I never want to see that table again."

Wren trusted them. That was the strange, stubborn truth at the center of everything: amid all this unraveling, they trusted Jonah Eriksen's sense of these passages the way they trusted their own hands to hold a pen. Jonah moved through the deep court's chaos like water finding its level, reading the mountain's tremors in the dust on the floor, in the way cold fire guttered, in instincts no map could record. Wren followed, clutching the satchel against their chest where Alaric's testimony lay inked in their own careful hand—and where, wrapped in oilcloth against their ribs, the small burning weight of the soul-seed's empty reliquary case rode like a coal that refused to cool.

Because the seed itself was not with them. That was the arrangement they'd made at the gate of the reliquary vault, in the frantic minute after the theft: Jonah carried it, because Jonah's body was a ledger of poisons and protections, hardened against things that would kill anyone else. If the deep court's guardians touched Wren, they would find only paper and ink. Everything that mattered rode against Jonah's skin.

Everything that mattered was also, Wren thought grimly as another bell-toll shivered the mirrors, currently the most wanted object beneath a hundred miles of stone.

* * *

They surfaced into the antechamber below the thorn-door gasping, and there Wren saw the first true evidence of what they had done.

The gate stood open behind them—the passage Jonah had paid a childhood summer to open, the hollowed thorn-tree whose heartwood doors still glowed faintly with the toll already taken. But around the doorframe, the ancient wood was *moving*. Not swaying. Moving the way a sleeper moves in pain. The great roots that anchored the tree to the mountainside flexed and strained, and sap the color of dark amber welled from splits in the bark like blood from split knuckles.

"It's the pact collapsing," Wren said. They hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words simply arrived, the way all the worst realizations arrived, fully formed and terrible. "The tree is part of it. The thorn-door, the winter, the sale—it's all one mechanism. We pulled out the keystone."

Jonah set down the satchel and pressed both palms flat against the nearest root. For a long moment they said nothing. Then, quietly: "It's warm. The whole tree's warm, like a fever. A hundred years of holding something in, and now—" They withdrew their hands. "We climb fast. Whatever happens up there, we do not want to be inside the mountain when it finishes deciding what it's becoming."

They climbed.

The goat-track Jonah had chosen for the ascent was one Wren had mapped in happier weeks—narrow ledges stitched along the mountain's southern face, hidden from crown patrols by overhangs of granite, navigable only by someone who had learned it smuggling untaxed brandy and fugitive cousins through the high passes. In autumn it had been exhilarating. In autumn, Wren had laughed on this path, wind in their hair, Jonah's hand steady at their elbow, the world below a quilt of gold and green.

Now the world below was white.

Not the gentle white of early snow. The storm above had become a living thing. Wren felt it before they saw it—a pressure change, a ringing in the ears—and then they rounded a shoulder of rock and the full sky opened above them and they stopped breathing.

The clouds had come down to meet them. There was no horizon anymore, no distinction between mountain and weather; the blizzard filled everything, a churning gray-white ocean with the two of them clinging to an island of stone. Snow struck sideways with the force of thrown gravel. The temperature had dropped so far, so fast, that Wren's exhalations froze into crystals that rattled against their hood like flung sand.

And through it all, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, the prophecy's words rang in Wren's memory with the clarity of a struck tuning fork. They had read them aloud once, in the archive, by lamplight—had read them to Jonah with dread gathering in their chest like storm-water:

*A thorn that burns shall steal again*
*what was sold before the long winter finishes falling.*

"Before the winter finishes falling," Wren shouted into the wind. "Jonah! The prophecy didn't mean the winter would *end*. It meant the winter would *complete itself*. Finish. Like a sentence being finished. Like a debt being called in!"

Jonah, twenty feet above on the ledge, turned. Even through the blur of driven snow, Wren saw their face change—the moment the arithmetic landed. A hundred years of purchased protection, ending not gradually but catastrophically, all the withheld winter arriving at once, every storm the pact had deferred for a century demanding payment together.

The kingdom below them—Isolde's kingdom, the villages in the valleys, the farms, the herds, the people who had never known they were living on credit—was about to receive a hundred winters in one night.

"We did this," Wren said. The wind took half the words, but Jonah read the rest off their lips, the way lovers learn to.

"No." Jonah climbed down to them, moving with that unnerving surety, and took Wren's face in both mittened hands. Their eyes were fierce and close. "The crown did this. Isolde's grandmother did this. Every monarch who renewed the pact in secret for a hundred years did this—they just planned to keep doing it forever, feeding a man to the dark one night at a time and calling it peace. We broke a machine built out of a person. Yes, the machine is crashing. But Wren—" their voice cracked, and Wren realized how much it was costing them, "—we got him *out*. Whatever falls tonight, Alaric Vane is not in that reliquary tonight. That's not nothing. That has to be worth something."

Wren wanted to believe it. They held onto Jonah's certainty like a handhold on a cliff. But even as they nodded, even as they turned to climb, some cartographer's part of their mind—the part that measured coastlines and understood that every line drawn on a map was a choice about what to include and what to leave out—kept calculating.

How many hours since the theft? Six. Perhaps seven. How fast did a catastrophic front descend the Thornfells? Faster than two climbers. Which meant the lowland villages were already in it. Which meant somewhere below, right now, a miller was looking out his window at snow past his knees in late autumn, and a shepherd was losing her flock, and someone in the capital was standing before Queen Isolde with a report, and the queen—who had sent Serah Dole to ensure the prophecy was fulfilled *on the crown's terms*, with Jonah as expendable bait—was learning that her expendable bait had stolen the prize and broken the shield.

"They'll blame us," Wren said. Not a question.

"They'll be right to, partly," Jonah called back. "That's what makes it dangerous. A lie you can't refute is stronger than a lie nobody believes. Climb!"

* * *

The storm worsened with altitude, which should not have been possible, and yet the mountain seemed determined to demonstrate that possibility personally. By the time they reached the hollow where Jonah had planned their first camp—a fold of rock beneath a leaning slab of granite, sheltered enough to risk a fire if the wind allowed—Wren's fingers had stopped registering cold and started registering only pain, and then not even pain, just a distant wrongness, like hearing about your own injury secondhand.

Jonah built the fire with the economy of long practice, shielding the flame with their body, feeding it slivers of resin-fat pine until it caught and held. They worked in silence, and Wren watched them, and thought: *this is what my love looks like when it's terrified.* Jonah's competence had always been a kind of language. Tonight it said what their mouth wouldn't: *I will get us through this. I refuse to consider otherwise.*

"Drink." Jonah pressed a flask into Wren's hands. It was the good brandy, smuggler's stock, saved for emergencies. "Small sips. Don't let yourself sleep until you've stopped shivering."

Wren drank. Fire bloomed in their chest. Around them the storm screamed against the granite slab overhead with a sound like a thousand voices trying to speak at once, and in the firelight Jonah finally sat still, and Wren saw the cost written on them plainly for the first time.

"You're blaming yourself too," Wren said.

"I'm *counting*," Jonah corrected quietly. "There's a difference. Blaming is useless. Counting tells you what to do next." They stared into the flames. "I counted the villages in the southern valleys on the way up. Forty-one. I tasted for that court for six years, Wren. I stood behind Isolde's chair and watched her eat bread made from grain those farmers grew, and I never once asked where the endless winter came from. I was so busy being no one that I never wondered what being no one *cost* other people." A pause. The fire popped. "So yes. Some of it's mine. I'm keeping that part. It's useful. Guilt that teaches you something is just tuition."

Wren reached across the fire and took their hand. "Then we pay the tuition together. And then we go back down and we tell them the truth. All of it. The pact, the sale, the Eriksens, the crown's complicity—everything. Let them blame us for the storm. We'll make them blame the *right* people for the century."

Jonah looked up. In the firelight their eyes were wet, though their voice stayed level. "You'd stand in front of that court and testify? After everything? You know what they do to witnesses."

"I'm a cartographer," Wren said. "My entire profession is insisting that things are where they are, whether or not anyone wants them to be. It's the family trade." They squeezed Jonah's fingers. "Besides. You heard Maret. Your parents died trying to undo this. Finishing their work isn't the same as causing the storm. We have to hold onto that distinction or it'll eat us alive."

For a moment neither spoke. Above them, muffled by stone and storm, the mountain groaned—a long, tectonic sound, like a giant turning over in sleep. Snow sifted down through a crack in the granite and hissed in the fire.

Then Jonah stiffened. Their head tilted, the way it did when their taster's senses caught something the rest of the body hadn't reported yet.

"What?" Wren whispered.

"Listen. Under the wind."

Wren listened. At first there was nothing but the storm's endless white roar. Then, threading through it, faint and rhythmic: a sound Wren had read about in smugglers' accounts but never heard. A horn. Deep, brazen, blown in the specific triple pattern that every guild-child learned before they learned their letters.

Crown signal. Patrols mobilized. *Fugitives sighted.*

"In this?" Wren hissed. "No one climbs in this."

"No one sane climbs in this." Jonah was already kicking dirt over the fire, movements quick and precise. "But Serah Dole has the map I drew. My map, with my routes, my campsites marked in my own shorthand. She doesn't need to climb well. She needs to climb *predictably*—and I'm the one who taught this mountain how to be predictable." They shouldered the satchel and pulled Wren to their feet. "She'll reach the upper ledges by dawn. We need to be at the summit cave before she does, and then down the far side toward the thorn-tree before the storm seals the pass for good."

"And after that?"

Jonah paused at the edge of the hollow, a silhouette against the roaring white, and when they turned back there was something new in their face—not the flat calm of the taster, not the wariness of the smuggler, but something rawer and more decided.

"After that, we stop running and start talking. To the queen, to the court, to whoever will listen. We put Alaric in front of them breathing and testifying, and we dare them to call him a lie." A ghost of the old crooked smile. "I spent six years making myself invisible, Wren. Turns out the cure for that is doing something so loud no one can ever look away again."

They climbed out into the storm together.

Above them, unseen but unfelt-no-longer, the last of the long winter finished falling—and far below, in the burning halls of a palace built on a secret, the first fires of blame were already being lit.
