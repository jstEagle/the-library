## 29. Chase Through the Mirrored Halls

The alarms of the deep court were not bells. They were hunger.

I felt it before I heard it—a shiver in the cold fire that lined the mirrored halls, a dimming, as if a thousand candles had all turned their faces toward us at once. Then came the sound: a low, resonant thrumming through the stone beneath our feet, like a heart the size of the mountain remembering how to beat.

"Run," I said.

Wren ran. Alaric ran—thin as a winter branch, his stolen soul-seed cupped against Wren's chest inside its wrapping of map-cloth, glowing faintly through the fabric like an ember breathing. Behind us, down the long gallery of silvered stone, the courtiers were rising from their banquet seats with the slow, terrible grace of things that had never once needed to hurry.

We fled through halls that hated us for running.

That was the thing about the deep court no one had thought to warn me about, because no one had ever run from it and lived: the mirrors were not decoration. Every wall was polished stone that held your reflection a half-second too long, a half-second wrong, and as we passed, our reflections kept moving after we'd gone—lagging ghosts of ourselves, reaching, mouths open in warnings we couldn't hear. The hall remembered us. The hall would show them where we'd been.

"The gate," Alaric gasped. His voice was rust. A hundred years of being fed upon had left him with just enough of himself to know what he'd lost. "The thorn-door. It opens from this side only when—" He stumbled; I caught his arm; his skin was cold as riverbed stone. "When it's asked properly. I've forgotten how it's asked."

"Then we ask it rudely," I said, and pulled him on.

* * *

The mirrored halls of the deep court were laid out like the palace above, because of course they were—the Pale Regent had built its kingdom as an inversion, a reflection pressed face-to-face with the world of light. Which meant I knew these corridors. Not from maps. From memory.

Every taster learns the palace above by heart: which stair turns left where it seems to turn right, which door is painted shut, which passage behind the tapestry runs from the queen's solar to the kitchens in forty paces. I had spent nine years memorizing Isolde's palace so thoroughly that I could walk it blindfolded, poisoned, or drunk. And here it all was, flipped like a glove turned inside out—the same bones, reversed.

"Left here," I said, and took it, and the corridor bent the way I knew it would, past a fountain that ran upward instead of down, its water climbing into the dark like smoke made liquid.

"You're sure?" Wren's breath came ragged. There was blood on their temple from the fall at the reliquary steps, and they were still carrying the soul-seed like it was the only child in the world.

"I'm sure of the shape," I said. "Not the flesh. In the palace above, this hall ends at the queen's portrait gallery. Here—" I skidded around the corner and stopped so fast Wren walked into my back.

Here, the portrait gallery was full of faces.

They hung in niches along the walls, dozens of them, scores—faces without bodies, preserved under glass like specimens, each one lit by its own small flame of cold fire. Old faces. Young faces. Some wore expressions of peace. Most did not. And every single one of them, as we stood there panting, turned its eyes toward us.

"They're not portraits," Alaric whispered. He had gone greyer than grey. "They're what's left. When the court feeds too long, when there's nothing left but the last look a person ever wore—they keep those." His hand rose, trembling, toward one niche—a young man with a crooked smile—and fell again. "I knew him. Before. I think I knew him."

"We don't have time to grieve the walls," I said, and hated myself for saying it, and said it anyway. "Which way out?"

"The servants' way." Alaric's eyes had gone distant, dredging a century of silt. "There's always a servants' way. Even here. Especially here—the court doesn't carry its own trays." He pointed, hand shaking, down a narrow arch half-hidden behind a hanging curtain of woven frost. "There. But it goes up through the feeding rooms."

"Then we go quiet," I said.

* * *

We did not go quiet.

The feeding rooms announced themselves by smell—or rather by the absence of smell, a clean, sweet nothingness like the air after lightning, and then by sound: a wet, patient rhythm, like tide against a dock, coming from behind doors of black wood banded in silver. We pressed ourselves into an alcove and watched a procession pass—not courtiers now but something older, taller, their borrowed faces slipping at the seams as they hurried, showing underneath the pale suggestion of features not yet decided. They carried empty chairs. Chairs, on their shoulders, like litters, and I understood with a lurch that the chairs were for us, that the deep court did not chase its food, it *collected* it, and something in me that had spent nine years being useful and invisible looked at those empty chairs and felt a rage so pure it tasted like iron.

"No," I said aloud.

Wren grabbed my sleeve. "Jonah—"

"They put out chairs." My voice came out low and steady, which surprised me. "A hundred years they've set the table with him. With all of them." I nodded at the niched faces we'd passed. "And they bring *chairs*."

"Jonah, love, I know. But rage is a luxury with a bill attached. Move."

So we moved. Through the servants' way—narrow, unlit, the walls rough-hewn where the public halls were mirror-smooth, as if the deep court preferred its labor unseen even here, even underground. Alaric knew it better than he knew his own name, which was not saying much; his feet remembered what his mind had lost, and he took turns with the confidence of a man walking through a dream he'd had ten thousand times. Twice we heard pursuit behind us—the thrumming pulse of the alarm, closer now, and beneath it a new sound, a skittering, like cutlery dropped on marble, multiplied by hundreds.

And once, ahead of us, we heard Serah Dole.

* * *

She came around a corner with her sword drawn and her left arm hanging wrong, and for one stupid heartbeat I raised the little knife I'd taken from the banquet table—the one I'd used to lift a soul out of a god's reliquary, the finest piece of sleight-of-hand of my life—and then I saw her face.

Serah Dole, crown inquisitor, the woman who had cornered Wren in an archive and hunted us across three mountain passes, looked like a woman who had opened the wrong door and met what was behind it. Half her body moved correctly. The other half lagged, a half-second behind, like the reflections in the mirrored halls—as if whatever she'd woken on her way through the false gate had gotten its teeth into her timeline and was slowly pulling her apart from the present moment. Her right eye saw me. Her left eye saw somewhere else entirely.

"Eriksen." Her voice was gravel and blood. She leaned against the wall and did not raise her sword. "You're alive. Good. That's—good."

"You followed my map," Wren said, and there was no triumph in it, only grief. "You opened the false gate."

"I opened the false gate." She laughed, and the laugh cost her something visible. "Your map was honest. I just read it the way the crown reads everything—looking for the door that locks others out instead of the door that lets you in." She pushed off the wall, swayed, steadied. "It's behind me. What I woke. It eats time, do you understand? Not flesh. Time. Everything I touch is getting further away from me. I shook its hand three hours ago and I remember it like a dream from childhood." Her lagging arm rose, and she pressed something into my hand: a signet ring, black iron, stamped with the crown's seal. "Evidence. For your queen. If she'll take it. The old guard renewed the pact eleven times. Eleven signatures. I found the ledger in her own archive, under her own seal. She's known since before she was crowned."

"Why give it to me?" I asked. "You came to drag us back. You told Wren the queen wanted us as bait."

"I came to drag you back." Her lagging half smiled, and it was the most terrible thing I had seen all day, and I had seen the feeding rooms. "And then I opened a door I shouldn't have, and I had a long walk to think about what I'd been dragging people back *to*." She looked at Wren. "You drew a true map. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else I've ever known draws the map that keeps them safe." Her gaze came back to me. "You ate the poison rather than let them use you. Both of you keep choosing the expensive option. I'm tired of choosing the cheap one."

Behind her, far down the corridor, the skittering had become a roar, and with it came a cold that was colder than the deep court's cold—a cold with appetite in it, the thing she'd woken, wearing her stolen hours like a coat.

"It's coming," she said simply. "And I can't outrun it anymore. But I can hold a doorway. I've been holding doors for the crown my whole life. Might as well hold one worth holding." She turned toward the junction ahead—the last turning before the thorn-door, a broad archway where the corridor narrowed, perfect ground for a stand. "Go. Ask the door properly. Alaric will remember when he hears his own name spoken by someone who means it. That's how it works. That's how it always worked. The gates here answer to truth, not keys."

"How do you know that?" Wren asked.

"Because I loved someone the crown erased." Serah's voice went very quiet, and for a moment both her eyes—both her timelines—looked at me and saw me whole. "Her name was Ilse. She was a taster too, thirty years ago. She died proving a dish was poisoned, and the crown called it an accident, and struck her from the rolls, and I have served them for twenty years knowing exactly what they do to people who are useful and then inconvenient." She smiled, and this time it reached both halves of her face. "You're not the first person they tried to make invisible, Eriksen. You're just the first who refused."

The thing she'd woken rounded the corner behind her.

I will not describe it. Some things are a mercy not to describe. It was shaped like the space where a person used to be, and it moved the way regret moves, and Serah Dole planted her feet in the narrowing archway, raised her sword in both hands—one hand in the present, one hand three hours gone—and said, "Close the door behind you."

We ran.

* * *

The thorn-door stood at the end of the last hall, roots of the great tree above piercing down through living rock, and the gate in their midst was already stirring, sensing us, sensing the soul-seed burning through Wren's shirt like a second heartbeat. Behind us the sounds of Serah's stand echoed—steel, and that terrible tide-sound, and once, briefly, a woman's voice singing something in a language I didn't know, clear and defiant and completely unafraid.

Alaric stood before the gate and could not speak. A century of being eaten leaves a man with nothing left to ask with.

So I spoke for him. I put my hand flat against the ancient wood, beside the scar-shaped mark that matched the one on my own ribs, and I said, loudly, in the voice I never used in the palace above, the voice with no anonymity in it at all:

"My name is Jonah Eriksen. My family sold this man. I am here to unsell him. Open."

The door remembered everything.

And behind us, in the dark, Serah Dole's song ended on a note that sounded, impossibly, like thanks.
