## 31. What Jonah Chose to Keep

The door was still hungry.

I could feel it the way I felt poison in a cup—not a taste exactly, but a pressure behind the tongue, a knowing. The thorn-tree's gate had opened for me once on a coin of memory: one summer, one childhood afternoon of sun-warm river stones and my mother laughing somewhere out of frame. It had taken that and let us through, and now it wanted the rest of what it judged we owed.

And Wren—Wren was standing in front of it with their hands open and their chin up, offering me to it like a plate set before a king.

"Take mine," they said again, louder, as if the door were deaf instead of ancient. "My memories of Jonah. All of them. That's what you want, isn't it? What's most precious. Take those and let us pass."

"No." My voice came out flat and strange, stripped of everything soft. The reliquary was strapped against my chest under my coat, Alaric's soul-seed burning faintly through the leather like a coal wrapped in wool. Above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone and root and frozen earth, the deep court was screaming. "Wren. Step away from the door."

"You don't get to decide this alone."

"I decide everything alone. That's the whole shape of my life." I moved between them and the gate. The carved bark-face regarded me with eyes of amber sap, patient as winter. "You've already given enough. You gave your maps, your name, your safety, your—" I stopped. My throat closed around the word *heart* like a fishbone. "The door doesn't get you. Not one more piece of you."

Behind us, down the mirrored corridor, something vast was coming. Serah Dole's last stand would buy minutes, not hours. She'd held the turning of the hall with her body and her blade and whatever she carried that was heavier than both, and when the cold fire finally washed over her she had looked at me—not with hatred, not even with forgiveness, just with recognition—and mouthed something I will carry longer than any memory the gate could take: *someone too.*

So no. I was not going to stand here and watch the door eat Wren's love for me out of their head while the Regent's courtiers came to eat the rest.

"Wren," I said, gentler now, because fury is just fear wearing armor, "look at me."

They did. Their face was streaked with soot and someone else's blood and the particular ruin of having watched an enemy die for them. Their hands were shaking. Cartographer's hands, ink-stained, precise, made for holding pens and my jaw and nothing sharper than either.

"The door wants payment," I said. "It told us that. A toll of memory, freely given. But doors are only mouths, Wren. They don't choose what they're fed. Someone built this one. Someone bound it. And debts—" I pressed my palm flat against the amber eye, and the tree shivered through its whole impossible height, "—debts have roots."

* * *

Here is what I understood in that moment, standing in the hollow heart of the burning thorn with the deep court howling at my back:

The Eriksens sold a soul. A hundred years ago, my family stood where I stood—or somewhere very like it, in some older season of this same mountain—and they brokered the trade. Prince Alaric Vane, living boy, warm blood, whole soul, handed down into the dark in exchange for an endless winter's protection. And the crown signed it, and the guild witnessed it, and the snows fell gentle ever after, and everyone agreed to forget.

But a bargain like that needs an anchor. A claim. Something written into the world's ledger that says *this debt belongs here.* The gate hadn't asked for random memories when it stopped us. It had asked for *mine*—had known my face, my scar shaped like its own seal, my blood. Because the debt ran in my veins. Because I wasn't just a thief come to steal back what was sold.

I was the heir of the ones who sold it.

Old Maret's voice, from the Hollow Inn, from a hundred years ago by way of a bottle of plum brandy: *Your people didn't just carry the papers, child. They made the papers. The Eriksen name was the wax on the seal.*

That was why the guild owned me so easily. Why the queen could erase my family from every record and still have someone at court whisper my surname like a curse. Why the prophecy chose a taster, a smuggler, a nobody—a person who had spent their whole life sanding themselves down to nothing. Fate has a sense of humor, and it is crueler than any queen.

The inheritance wasn't gold. There was no Eriksen land, no title, no crest. The inheritance was the debt itself. The claim. The right—and the obligation—to be the seller's hand, generation after generation, until the account was settled or the world ended.

And if the debt lived in me, then I could pay it out of myself. Root and branch.

* * *

"Wren," I said, "when you mapped the underside, what did you call the sealed gate?"

They blinked. Grief and terror and cartographer's habit all fighting for control of their face; habit won, the way it always does with people who love their work. "The Root Door. In the fragments—the ones from before the erasure—it's called the Root Door. I thought it was just a name. Doors at the bottom of things."

"It's not a name. It's a function." I turned back to the amber eyes. "A debt is a root. Cut the stem and it grows back. Burn the leaves and it sleeps till spring. But pull the root—" I drew a breath that tasted of cold sap and old stone, "—and the whole thing comes out of the ground."

"Don't." Wren grabbed my sleeve. "Jonah. Whatever you're about to do—don't. We don't know what it takes. Your name, your history, your—"

"My history is a bill of sale." I said it quietly, and found, to my surprise, that it didn't hurt the way I expected. It hurt the way setting down something heavy hurts—afterward, in the new lightness, in the ache of muscles unclenching. "Every story I ever pieced together about the Eriksens ends at that table in the deep places. Before that there's almost nothing. A name on shipping manifests. A signature. They were brokers, Wren. Middlemen. They made themselves useful to power and power made them invisible, and then they made themselves *more* than useful—they made themselves necessary—and the mountain swallowed them whole. My parents died trying to unmake that signature. Maret said so. Died trying to undo it, and couldn't, because you can't undo a thing you're still holding."

"And you think you can let go of it?"

"I think I can give it away." I smiled, and it felt like the first honest smile in days. "Freely given. That's the toll, isn't it? That's always been the toll. The deep court feeds on what's stolen—Alaric proves that, a century of nightly feeding on a stolen soul—but the doors, the old doors, the honest doors, they only take gifts."

The amber eyes flared. The tree knew I had understood it. Somewhere far above, wind screamed across the Thornfell passes, and somewhere far below, the Regent's hunters found Serah's body and paused, briefly, to consider it.

"Jonah." Wren's voice cracked clean through. "If you give it your name—if you give it *all of it*—what's left? Who comes out the other side?"

I took their hands. Ink and blood and trembling.

"Someone new," I said. "Someone chosen. Isn't that what we keep telling each other we want? A life assigned versus a life picked?" I laughed, short and wet. "I've been nobody on purpose for fifteen years. Let me be nobody on purpose one more time—but this time as a gift, not a hiding place. This time it buys something."

"What does it buy?"

"A spring."

* * *

I turned to the gate.

The bark-face waited, and now that I looked properly, now that I let myself see it without dread, it didn't look hungry. It looked *tired*. A door is a servant too, when you come down to it. Bound to its threshold, fed on scraps of other people's pasts, holding shut—or holding open—whatever its makers commanded. This one had been holding the line between two courts for a hundred years, swallowing memories drop by drop, keeping the ledger balanced. It deserved better than scraps.

"Eriksen," I said aloud, and the name rang in the hollow of the tree like a bell struck underground. "That's the name. That's the claim. Four generations of brokers, and before them whatever we were, and all of it bent toward one act: the selling of Alaric Vane. The debt is anchored in my blood. So take the anchor."

The amber eyes brightened. *Speak it whole,* said the tree, in a voice like sap moving through winter branches, slow and sweet and inexorable. *Names given in part remain. Names given whole are spent.*

"I Jonah Eriksen"—my mother's stubbornness in my jaw, my father's silence in my hands, none of it mine to keep if keeping it meant the wheel kept turning—"heir of the sellers, holder of the claim, keeper of the debt of Alaric Vane, do freely surrender the inheritance entire. The name. The claim. The right of the sellers' hand. The obligation of the sellers' blood. Let the root be pulled. Let the ledger close. Let no one after me owe what I owed, and let no one before me be remembered for it either. Spend it all. Leave nothing growing."

Wren made a sound behind me—my name, half-spoken, *Jo—*—and I raised one hand: *wait, wait, almost done.*

"And in exchange," I said, "take also the memory of the summer. The one you already hold. Keep it. It was the truest thing I owned, and true things should belong to doors that keep faith." My voice shook on the next part, but I said it anyway. "What remains of me walks out of here owing nothing—to the crown above, to the court below, to the dead, to the living, to fate herself. Free and clear. That is the payment. That is the whole payment. Witness it."

The tree breathed.

There is no other word for it. The whole vast hollowed trunk inhaled, and every vein of amber sap lit at once, and the carved face opened its eyes fully for the first time since we'd come down to it, and I felt the taking begin.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. Poison hurts; venom burns; every resistance I'd ever built in my body was built on pain, on surviving the worst thing and letting it leave a scar. But this was like being *edited*. Like a careful hand going through the pages of me and lifting out a chapter whole.

I felt the name go first. *Eriksen* simply—stopped meaning anything. It became a sound, then less than a sound, a shape my mouth could make with no more weight than a cough. Then the claim went, the deep structural thing underneath, the sense I'd never known I carried of being *bound*, of walking through the world with a chain running from my sternum down through the mountain's roots. It slid out of me like a splinter leaving flesh after twenty years, and the relief was so violent I nearly went to my knees.

Then the summer. River stones warm as bread. My mother's laugh, out of frame, out of reach, gone—I reached for it and found smooth blank paper where it had been, and grieved it in the same instant I gave it, because that was the price of true things, and I had said *witness it*, and the tree was witnessing.

And then—this I hadn't asked for, this was the door's own grace or its own cruelty—the erasure spread backward through the generations. I felt my parents' faces soften into strangers. I felt the guild's ownership loosen its grip on something it had never realized it held by a thread of my blood rather than a thread of law. I felt a hundred years of inherited guilt, the weight I'd been carrying since Maret's kitchen without ever knowing its name, drain out through the soles of my feet into the roots of the tree.

The last thing to go was small. A habit, maybe. The way I flinched, always, at the sound of my own surname—even false ones, even the safe ones, some buried part of me braced for the bill to come due. The flinch left. What stayed was just a person standing in front of a door.

The amber dimmed to embers. The bark-face settled into something like sleep, or peace.

*Paid,* said the tree, in a voice already fading. *Paid in full, and the root with it. Walk through, unclaimed one. The winter above is not yours anymore.*

* * *

The gate opened onto cold air and grey light and the smell of snow, and I stood swaying on the threshold with Wren's arm suddenly hard around my ribs, holding me up.

"Talk to me," Wren was saying, urgent, searching my face like a map they'd drawn and feared had burned. "Jonah. Talk to me. What's left? Do you know me? Do you know *you*?"

I took inventory, the way I'd once inventoried poisons, methodically, tongue against palate.

"I know you," I said. "Cartographer. Terrible liar. Kisses like they're trying to memorize terrain." Wren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "I know the passes. I know every poison I ever survived—that's in the body, apparently, not the name. I know I paid something enormous and I know it was worth it, even though—" I frowned, probing the blank spot where the summer had been, where my parents had been. It didn't ache. It was just quiet. Quiet the way a house is quiet after the arguing stops. "Even though I'll spend years finding the edges of what's missing. Tell me about them sometime. My parents. You heard Maret's testimony too. When we're somewhere safe, tell me everything, and I'll decide which parts to keep."

"They were brave," Wren said thickly. "That's the part to keep. They died trying to fix what their family broke. Same as you just did."

"Same as I just did," I agreed. And then, because the seed against my chest had begun to pulse faster, warmer, answering some call from the storm-white world above: "Help me up. We have a prince to deliver."

We climbed. The passage narrowed, whitened, filled with the groan of wind through stone, and behind us the thorn-tree's light sank to nothing, and below us the deep court's rage rose like floodwater against a door that no longer answered to anyone's claim—including theirs.

Alaric's soul-seed burned steady against my heart. Unowned now. Unanchored. Free as I was, and just as frightened, and just as determined to see the sky.

The last of the passage opened, and snow came in sideways, and I stepped out of the mountain a person with no name, no debt, no title, and no master—

—and straight into the arms of the Queen's soldiers, who had been waiting at the treeline, it seemed, for a very long time.

Wren shouted. The seed flared. Somewhere above us, thunder rolled across a winter that was finishing its fall.

And I smiled, bloodied and blank-paged and lighter than I had ever been, because they had come to arrest a thief and an heir and a prophesied thorn—

—and none of those people existed anymore.
