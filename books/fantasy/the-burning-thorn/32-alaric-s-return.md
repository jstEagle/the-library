## 32. Alaric's Return

The last thing the deep court gave them was its cold.

Wren felt it first as a change in the air against their face—the close, warm-animal breath of the thorn-tree's hollow giving way to something vast and moving, and then they were through, and the mountain spat them out into a white screaming world.

The blizzard took them like a wave takes a swimmer.

They had come up through the root-door at the base of the hollowed thorn, and Wren had expected—had braced for—the winter above, but expectation was nothing against this. The snow did not fall so much as *arrive*, sideways, in sheets thick as wool, erasing the sky and the ground and the distinction between them. Jonah had Alaric over one shoulder before Wren could even find their feet, hauling the prince up the slope away from the door, and Wren scrambled after, one hand fisted in the back of Jonah's coat because if they let go the storm would take them apart from each other and that—

That was not something Wren's mind would hold. Not after everything below.

*Jonah,* they tried to say, and the wind ate it.

They climbed. That was all there was. The goat-track Jonah had promised was somewhere under their boots or it wasn't; there was only white, and the pull of Jonah's grip, and the weight of the map case strapped across Wren's chest like a second heart. Somewhere in that case, wrapped in oilcloth, was the drawing of the true gate. Somewhere behind them, sealed now by roots and snow and whatever old magic Maret's grandmother's grandmother had whispered of, was the false door Serah Dole had opened—and what had come out of it, or what she had become to close it again. Wren did not let themselves think about her. There would be time for grief later, or there wouldn't, but not here, not in this white roar where thinking cost warmth and warmth was life.

They found shelter as dusk came down—or what passed for dusk, the light failing to a bruised grey even at midday. A shelf of stone beneath an overhang, half-choked with drifted snow, and Jonah dug them into it with their hands, bare hands, blue to the wrist, until there was a hollow big enough for three bodies and a small fire that fought the wind and mostly lost.

Alaric Vane lay between them, still as carved wood.

The soul-seed sat on his chest. Wren had carried it the whole way up—Jonah had lifted it, but Wren had insisted on carrying it out, cupped in both palms inside their coat, against their skin, because it was warm. That was the thing no one had warned them about. In all the archive accounts, in every fragmentary record of the sale a century ago, the soul of a prince had been described as a thing taken, a thing owed, a thing lost. No one had said it would be *warm*. It sat in Wren's hands like a sparrow's egg made of ember-light, pulsing faintly, patiently, the way a heart pulses when the body around it has forgotten how.

Now it lay on Alaric's sternum, and it pulsed, and he did not.

"Is he—" Wren started.

"He's breathing." Jonah's voice was scraped raw. They had talked to the gate; they had paid it; Wren had watched them do it and would carry the memory of that transaction in their chest for the rest of their life, however long the rest of it turned out to be. "Barely. But he is."

The seed pulsed again. And this time, when it dimmed, Alaric's chest rose.

When it brightened, his fingers twitched against the stone.

"It's going back into him," Wren breathed. "Look—it's not just sitting there. It's *sewing itself back*."

Jonah leaned close, snowmelt dripping from their hair, and watched with the expression Wren had learned to read over these terrible weeks: the taster's face, cataloguing. Poison and antidote. Symptom and response. "A hundred years apart," they murmured. "I'd have thought a soul would forget its shape."

"Would you?" Wren asked softly. "Forget yours?"

Jonah didn't answer. They looked at their own hands instead, the ones that had paid the gate, and Wren reached over and covered one of them, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

He woke near midnight, and the first thing he did was scream.

It was not a warrior's sound. It was not even, really, a human sound at first—more like something long-compressed finally splitting, ice groaning off a roof, a tree giving way. He screamed with his eyes open and his hands clawing at his own chest, at the place where the seed had sunk into him and vanished, and Jonah held him down with the practiced strength of someone who had wrestled poison-fevers before, and Wren did the only thing they could think of: they talked.

"You're Alaric," they said, over and over, into the storm-noise and his noise. "You're Alaric Vane. You were born in the year the winter began. You liked—your mother kept bees, we found the record, there were hives in the south garden, they had to burn them when the frost came. Your tutor was called Master Ellery. You were nine years old. You were *nine*, and none of it was your fault—"

The screaming wound down. Not because the words calmed him, Wren thought, but because he was listening, hungry for them, a man in a desert hearing water described.

"Nine," he rasped at last. His voice was appalling—a century of disuse and feeding-upon had left it like paper burning slowly. "I was nine. I remember—I remember being told it was an honor. I remember my father couldn't look at me. I thought—I thought for a hundred years I thought I'd done something wrong. That I'd been bad, and this was—" He stopped. His eyes, pale as the Regent's court, fixed on Wren's face. "Who are you?"

"Wren Hale. Cartographer." A mad little laugh escaped them. "Officially, I'm dead. Unofficially, I'm the person who mapped the way in."

"And the other one?"

"This is Jonah."

Alaric looked at Jonah for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the shadow of it, the shape recognition leaves behind when the memory itself has been eaten.

"Eriksen," he said.

The word dropped into the shelter like a stone into a well.

Jonah went very still. "You know that name."

"I know everything now." Alaric pushed himself up against the stone, and the movement had a terrible wrongness to it, a marionette quality, as though his body was remembering motion the way a drowned man remembers swimming. "That's what coming back means, apparently. Everything they fed on, everything they took, all of it pouring home. I can feel the pact like a scar. I can feel the *signing*." His gaze stayed on Jonah. "Your family stood at the table when it was signed. Not the crown. The crown sent chancellors and lawyers. But the ones who knew how to speak to the deep court—the brokers, the intermediaries—that was the Eriksens. Your people carried the terms down the mountain. Your people taught the Regent's heralds which words bound and which words merely decorated." He paused. "And your parents, in the end, tried to un-teach it."

"My parents," Jonah said carefully, "died in a rockslide."

"Your parents died trying to reach this door," Alaric said, "and the rockslide was arranged, and you know it, or you've always suspected it, and I'm sorry to be the one who confirms it. I heard it happen. From below. I hear everything that happens on this mountain. I've been listening to a century of your kingdom through a wall of hunger." He laughed, and it was the worst sound yet, because there was genuine humor in it, gallows-bright. "Do you know what I know? Do you understand what they've made me? A hundred years of witness, and no one to witness *to*. Until now."

* * *

The blizzard held them prisoner for two days.

Two days in a stone hollow with a dying fire, a dwindling scrap of smuggled food, and a resurrected prince who could not stop talking—which was, Wren came to understand, both his healing and his torment. The memories came back in floods, and he had learned, somewhere in a century of captivity, that a memory spoken aloud was a memory anchored. So he spoke. He spoke while the wind screamed and the snow sealed them in like a tomb with better lighting.

He told them about the signing.

"I was there," he said, the second night, staring into the fire. "Not as a participant. As the *collateral*. They dressed me in white and sat me on a chair between the two tables, and everyone pretended I wasn't frightened, which was the cruelest part. My mother had taught me that fear was a thing gentlemen managed privately. So I sat there managing it privately while the Eriksen broker—your great-grandmother, I think, though she looked young, they always look young, don't they, the ones who deal with the deep court—read out the terms." His hands had stopped shaking by then; Wren wondered if that was recovery or emptiness. "Endless winter's protection. The deep court would hold the passes closed to invasion, hold the plagues in the lowlands, hold the famine-winds off the harvest. In exchange: one soul, freely given, renewable by consent of the crown. Renewable, Wren. Did you hear that word? Every twenty-five years, the pact comes due, and every twenty-five years, some chancellor climbs the mountain and renews it. With a signature. With a *fee*." He looked up. "There's a ledger. There's always a ledger. The Eriksens kept it, and when your parents ran, the crown took it, and I would stake my returned soul that it sits in the royal vault to this day, in Isolde's own keeping, with a hundred years of signatures in it."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "She knows. The queen knows."

"The queen's grandmother knew. The queen's grandmother *renewed* it. Isolde renewed it herself, eleven years ago—winter-marks, they call the payments, buried in the treasury accounts as border garrison funding." Alaric smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing, that smile, because it was not broken. It was *aimed*. "I have spent a hundred years with nothing to do but listen and remember. I am, I believe, the most complete witness in the history of this kingdom. Every secret whispered within a mile of the thorn-line. Every renewal. Every name. Every payment." He spread his hands, pale and steady. "They fed on me nightly and never once thought to wonder what a soul does with all that it sees. A soul *keeps*. That's what a soul is for."

Jonah sat with their back to the stone, arms around their knees, and said nothing for a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice was flat in the way Wren had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"They'll kill him. The moment we surface properly, the moment anyone official learns what he is—they can't have a living heir walking around with a century of the crown's crimes in his head. He's not a rescue. He's a threat with a heartbeat."

"Yes," Alaric agreed pleasantly. "So we should make certain that by the time anyone official learns what I am, there are too many *unofficial* people who already know. Witnesses are only dangerous alone." He nodded at Wren's map case. "You're the one who draws things so people can find them. Draw this. Draw me. Draw the truth so widely that killing it becomes a war, not an errand."

* * *

On the third day the storm broke, abruptly, the way mountain storms do—one hour a white hell, the next a silence so total it rang. The sun came out over a world remade: the Thornfells wearing snow like borrowed ermine, the valleys below drowned in cloud, the air so clear and cold it hurt to breathe.

They descended.

Jonah led, reading slopes Wren would have sworn were featureless, finding the smuggler cuts and switchbacks under two feet of fresh powder as if the mountain had shown them personally. Wren walked behind Alaric and watched him see the world.

That was the thing that undid them, more than the blizzard, more than the deep court, more than the gate and its toll. Watching Alaric Vane see the sky. He stopped four times in the first mile just to stand and look upward, face tipped back, eyes streaming in the cold, saying nothing. Once he knelt and put his bare hand through the crust of the snow to touch the earth beneath, and when he lifted it, he held a single dried stalk of some summer weed, brown and brittle, and he looked at it the way scholars look at scripture.

"I forgot green," he said quietly. "You can't miss what you can't remember. For a hundred years I didn't know there was anything to miss." He tucked the stalk inside his ruined coat, over his heart, absurdly careful. "I'm going to make them tell me everything I missed. All of it. Year by year."

By late afternoon they were down among the pines, and by full dark they had reached the edge of the high pasturelands where Old Maret's kin kept their winter steadings, and Wren saw lamplight in a window and nearly wept.

Maret met them in the yard with a lantern and a loaded crossbow, lowered the crossbow when the lantern-light found Jonah's face, and then raised the lantern higher, and higher still, and went absolutely still.

"Well," Old Maret said at last, in the voice of a woman watching a story she'd been told in childhood walk out of the snow and ask for supper. "The prophecy book never said he'd be *polite* about it. Come in, all of you. Come in before the cold takes what the mountain spared." Her eyes lingered on Alaric's face—older than it should be, worn thin, but unmistakably the face from the old portraits, the face on the coins that had stopped being minted a century ago. "Saints preserve us. We're going to need more than supper. We're going to need every honest soul in this valley, and we're going to need them *tonight*."

Inside, in the low warm room, with soup in front of them and the door barred, Alaric set down his spoon and folded his hands like a man preparing to give evidence in court—which, Wren realized, was precisely what he was doing.

"Before I eat another bite," he said, "I want ink. Paper. And I want you to send for the valley headmen, the freeholders, anyone whose grandfather remembered my father's reign. Because I have a hundred years of testimony in me, and I intend to spend every word of it before the crown can decide I'm a rumor." He looked around the table, at Maret, at Jonah, at Wren, and his pale eyes burned with something that a century of feeding had somehow failed to starve. "They sold me and called it protection. They renewed it and called it policy. Let's see what they call it when the whole kingdom hears my voice."

Wren was already unrolling fresh paper, already sharpening a pen, hands steady, heart hammering. Outside, the wind had died entirely, and in the enormous silence the snow began to fall straight down, soft as ash, soft as a page turning.

Three days later, when the first riders in crown colors crested the pass toward Maret's steading, they would find not a fugitive and a dead cartographer and a madman, but a hall full of witnesses, a written testimony already copied seven times and carried by seven different roads toward seven different towns—

—and Queen Isolde's summons waiting, delivered by a herald whose face, when he read the room, went the color of the snow outside.

The queen commanded them to the capital.

Alaric Vane read the summons twice, smiled his aimed smile, and said, "Good. I've waited a hundred years for an audience."
