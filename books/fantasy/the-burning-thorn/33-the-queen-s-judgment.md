## 33. The Queen's Judgment

They brought me up through the mountain the way ore comes out of a mine—slowly, in a cage, in the dark.

The chains were ceremonial, which was its own kind of insult. Silver links, cold enough to burn, clasped at my wrists and throat with a weight that said *we know what you are capable of* without ever saying it aloud. Four guards walked me. Two ahead, two behind, their breath steaming in the torchlight of the ascent shafts, and not one of them would meet my eyes. I had spent eleven years making myself a piece of furniture in this palace—a chair that tasted soup, a rug that walked the walls—and now that I was finally visible, they could not bear to look.

I understood it. I had spent my whole life unable to look at myself either.

Wren walked beside me. They had refused to be separated—had planted themselves at my elbow at the mouth of the thorn-door and simply *stayed there*, arms crossed, chin set, until Serah Dole's surviving soldiers gave up arguing with a person who could not be moved by force or reason. The guards had chained Wren too, more from confusion than necessity. My cartographer wore iron like an accessory, like something borrowed for a costume ball, and kept up a low running commentary meant only for me.

"Your hair has frost in it still," Wren murmured as we climbed. "It suits you. Very tragic. Very prophetic."

"You're enjoying this."

"I am terrified out of my mind," Wren said, "and if I stop talking I will start screaming, so yes. I'm enjoying this." A pause. The cage groaned upward past a seam of raw stone veined with something pale and glimmering—deep-court light, leaking through a hundred feet of mountain like moonlight through cloth. "Jonah. When we get up there. Whatever happens."

"Don't."

"Let me say it once. I've earned one sentence." Their voice dropped to almost nothing, and the chains clinked as they leaned close. "I would do all of it again. Every map, every mile, every door. You were never invisible to me. Not for one single day."

I had no answer. I had spent years perfecting silence, and in that moment every silent thing I knew failed me at once. So I did the only honest thing left: I pressed my chained wrist against theirs, silver to silver, and let the cold speak.

Above us, the palace was screaming.

I heard it before I saw it—the sound coming down the shafts like water finding cracks. Bells, but wrong bells, rung out of sequence, the alarm-pattern reserved for invasion and regicide. Voices layered over voices. And underneath it all, a sound I recognized with a taster's instinct for the wrongness of things: the particular hush of a court trying very hard not to panic, which is louder than any panic itself.

The cage opened onto the service galleries behind the great hall, and the world was white.

Frost on the inside of windows three fingers thick. Ice climbing the pillars like ivy given permission. The pact was dead—I had killed it, or freed it, depending on who was telling the story by suppertime—and the long winter, unanchored, unfinished, was falling on the kingdom with the full weight of a century of withheld appetite. Servants hurried past with braziers and blankets and faces like paper. Nobody looked at the prisoners. Everybody looked at the prisoners. It is possible to do both at once; I have seen courts manage it.

They took us in through the side door of the great hall, and the doors closed behind us, and the sound cut off like a held breath released into a pillow.

* * *

Queen Isolde sat her throne beneath the antlered chandelier, and she was not looking at me.

She was looking at Alaric Vane.

He stood between two guards who did not dare touch him, wrapped in Wren's spare cloak because his own clothes had been eaten to lace by a hundred years of deep-court weather. He was thin the way winter branches are thin—not broken, but pared down to what could survive. His hands would not stop moving, turning over and over, touching his own sleeves as though confirming they existed. He had not stopped doing that since we surfaced. I suspected he might never stop.

But he was standing. He was breathing. He was *here*, in the hall where his portrait hung above the eastern arch, painted at nineteen, golden and certain, the prince who had "died of a wasting fever" in the official record while his soul went below to feed a hunger the crown had agreed to ignore.

Isolde looked at him the way I had once seen her look at a dish that smelled faintly of almonds when no almonds had been ordered.

"Chancellor Vess," she said, and her voice was level, and I heard the crack running through the level-ness like a flaw through ice. "Explain to me why a dead man is standing in my hall."

Vess came forward. Old Vess, silver chain of office, the face of a man who had signed renewal documents for forty years without once reading them aloud. He bowed precisely. "Your Majesty, the prisoner has produced an impostor. Some resemblance can be purchased. Some—"

"I was purchased," Alaric said.

His voice filled the hall. It was not loud. It did not need to be. A hundred years underground had burned everything out of it except the truth, and truth carries better than volume.

"I was purchased," he said again, "by Chancellor Aldous Vane, my uncle, who found my signature too easy to forge and my mother too newly dead to object. I was sold to the Pale Regent for a winter that would never end, so that the harvests would hold and the borders would freeze solid and no enemy would march on Thornfell in the cold. I have been fed upon nightly since. I remember perhaps four hundred days of the last thirty-six thousand. The rest belongs to them." He turned, slowly, and looked up at the portrait of himself. "That boy died of nothing. He was *spent*. Like coin. And every one of you—" his hand swept the assembled court, the lords and ladies in their furs, the officers, the priests, "—every one of you has lived warm inside what he paid."

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

Then Lord Cassel, old guard, old money, older guilt, said the fatal thing—the thing people say when they are guilty and frightened and stupid all at once:

"This is sedition. The boy is clearly deranged. Your Majesty, the winter is failing, the crops are dying under snow, and these two criminals—" he pointed at Wren and me, chains and all, "—have brought us a madman and a story. Hang them and pray for snowmelt."

And Queen Isolde, who had ruled for twenty-two years by knowing exactly which voice in a room was lying, turned her head and looked at me for the first time.

"Taster," she said. "You were in my kitchens eleven years. You tasted my food. You stood at my shoulder while I dined, and you never once spoke unless spoken to." She rose from the throne, one slow degree at a time, furs shifting like storm clouds. "So speak now. Speak, and be spoken to. Tell me what you know of the pact, and tell me why I should believe a smuggler and a poison-eater over my own chancellor."

I had rehearsed nothing. Rehearsal is for people who expect to survive the performance. But I had carried this moment up out of the mountain in my chest like a second soul-seed, and it came out of me whole.

* * *

I told them about the ledger.

Not metaphorically—an actual ledger, pulled from the archive beneath the Hollow Inn, copied in Maret's careful ancient hand from documents older than the dynasty. Wren had carried it the whole way down and back, wrapped in oilcloth against their heart like a second map. The guards brought it forward when I nodded, and it lay open on the table before the throne, and I watched the queen read her own kingdom's confession.

"The Eriksens brokered the sale," I said. "My family. We wrote the contract. We carried the payment. For six generations we were the crown's hands below the mountain, and when my parents tried to end it, they vanished, and the guild that raised me was paid to make sure no Eriksen asked questions again." I let that sit. Let them look at my face and try to find the invisible servant they'd never bothered to see. "I am the last Eriksen. I am the seller's heir. And I am also, apparently, the thorn that burns, which means the prophecy book in your own archive drew my face beside this date a hundred years ago, and nobody thought to wonder why."

"Renewals," Chancellor Vess said quietly. He had gone the color of candle wax. "Every reign renews the pact. It is in the succession oath. Your father swore it. Your grandfather. Your Majesty—you swore it at your coronation. The clause is sealed. You never—"

"I never *read* it," Isolde said. "Because none of you told me it existed." Her eyes moved across the assembled court, and I watched twenty noblemen discover simultaneously that a queen who has just learned she was lied to for her entire reign is more dangerous than any winter. "Who else knew?"

No one answered. Everyone answered. The silence named them all.

Alaric stepped forward then, and unpinned his cloak, and turned his back to the hall.

Beneath his shoulder blades, where the deep court had fed, the skin bore marks—pale, branching, cold-fire scars in the shape of roots, or veins, or a crown turned upside down. The gasp that went through the hall was one voice with a hundred throats.

"Feed-marks," said Old Maret, who had insisted on coming, who stood at the back among the servants where she had always stood, watching everything. "I saw them once before, the night they took him. On the ones who carried him down." She spat, delicately, onto the marble. "Your grandfathers' grandfathers did that. And called it protection."

The fracture, when it came, was not loud. That is the thing about courts—they shatter along seams polished smooth by centuries, and the break sounds almost like order. Lord Cassel moved first, toward the doors, murmuring to his cluster of old-guard allies, and found the doors already held by Serah Dole's soldiers. Serah herself stood beside them, half her face still shadowed by what the false gate had done to her, and she did not move aside.

"Open the doors, Captain," Cassel said.

"No, my lord." Serah's voice was rough as gravel underwater. "I served the crown's secret for nine years. I hunted children for it. I am done serving secrets." She looked at me across the hall, and something passed between us that had no name—two people who had both been used as tools by the same machine, one of whom had put out her own eye to see it clearly. "The captain's oath is to the throne. The throne has been stolen. I'm giving it back."

Cassel's faction made their mistake then, the same mistake every frightened old power makes: they reached for violence in a room full of witnesses. Steel cleared somewhere near the eastern arch. A lord shouted about treason. And the hall fractured fully, loudly, lords against lords, guards uncertain, priests praying in three different directions—

—and Queen Isolde descended from her dais, walked through the middle of it untouched the way only queens and cats can walk through fights, and stopped in front of the open ledger.

"Enough," she said, and the word fell like an executioner's blade, and the steel went back into its sheaths one piece at a time.

She looked down at the contract. At the renewal clauses. At the line where her own coronation oath was quoted back at her, verbatim, with the sealed clause appended. Then she looked at Alaric—at the living proof that her kingdom's comfort had a heartbeat and it had been starving for a century.

"Chancellor Vess," she said. "You are relieved of office. Lord Cassel—you and your allies are confined to your estates pending judgment. Captain Dole—you will draw up the list of everyone who knew. Everyone. I want names by morning, and I want them accurate, because I will be checking them against this ledger, and I have just learned what happens to those who lie to me in writing."

The old guard fell the way old guard always falls: not fighting, but *accounting*—already calculating which way to lean when the new wind came. Vess was escorted out past the portrait of the boy he had helped sell, and I watched him flinch as he passed beneath it. Small justice. But I have learned to take small justices; they are the only kind that don't cost someone else their soul.

Then Isolde turned to me, and the chains at my wrists suddenly felt very heavy and very deserved.

"And you," she said. "Taster. Smuggler. Eriksen. Thorn." Each word landed separately, stripping away a layer of the anonymity I had built my life inside. "You broke the pact. You stole back a prince. You have doomed this kingdom to a winter with no shield, and you did it all without my leave." She lifted my chin with one finger, the same gesture she'd used a thousand times to tilt a tasting-cup toward the light. "Give me one reason I shouldn't hang you."

Behind her, Wren inhaled sharply. Alaric went very still.

I looked at my queen—my poison-callused, frost-rimed, terrifying queen—and I gave her the only true thing I had.

"Because hanging me changes nothing about the winter," I said. "And because everything I did, I did with the truth your court buried. I'm not your traitor, Majesty. I'm your receipt."

A long moment. Somewhere in the hall, a log shifted in a brazier and sent up a fountain of sparks.

And Queen Isolde began, very quietly, to laugh—a hard, cold, genuine laugh, the first I had ever heard from her. "A receipt," she said. "Eleven years at my shoulder, and it turns out I employed the most dangerous document in the kingdom." She turned to the hall, to the fractured remnant of her court, to the frozen windows and the failing winter beyond them. "Hear me. The prisoner Jonah Eriksen stands accused of nothing but honesty, and honesty is in short supply this season. The chains come off. And then—" her jaw tightened, and I saw the weight of the next words land on her like the snow still falling outside, "—then we discuss how a kingdom survives a winter with no pact. Because I am told," and here her eyes flicked to me, sharp and terrible and almost hopeful, "that you have ideas, Taster. Ideas involving going *back down*."

The guards unlocked my wrists. The silver fell away, and my hands ached with the sudden lightness, and Wren's shoulder came to rest against mine—warm, solid, real.

Outside the high windows, the long winter finished falling, fat and relentless, burying the kingdom to its eaves.

And I caught Wren's eye, and saw my own dread reflected there, and knew that the hardest road was still the one leading down.
