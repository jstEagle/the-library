## 34. Winter Without a Pact

The cold came for the kingdom like a debt collector who had finally found the door.

Wren felt it first in the ink. In the weeks after they surfaced from beneath the Thornfells—Alaric's soul-seed cradled against Jonah's chest like a coal carried through a storm—the wells in the palace scriptorium had begun to freeze overnight. Not the water in them; the water *under* them, rising up black and glittering through the stone channels until the whole room smelled of iron and deep places. Wren would come down at dawn to find their inkwells crowned with ice so clear it magnified whatever lay beneath it, and once, looking too long, they had seen something looking back: a pale suggestion of a face, patient as geology.

They had stopped using that well after that.

The winter itself was worse than any in living memory, and everyone said so, and everyone was wrong, because no one alive remembered what winter had been before the pact. The old records Wren pulled from the archive—records the crown had kept locked behind three seals and a lie about damp—told of snows that came in the eleventh month and left by the third. What fell now began in the ninth month and did not leave at all. It simply accumulated, month over month, like interest.

By the time the granaries were counted, the number was bad enough that the queen's steward burned the tally sheets and ordered a second count, as though arithmetic were the problem.

* * *

Wren saw the famine coming the way they saw everything: in lines on a page.

It was a map that did it. They had been drafting supply routes for the relief caravans—honest work, urgent work, the kind of cartography that decided whether villages lived or died—and they had laid the grain stores of the lowland provinces over the population rolls, and the two patterns refused to overlap. The food was where the people were not. The people were where the roads were buried under eleven feet of snow. And between the stores and the starving lay the Thornfell passes, which Jonah knew better than any living soul but which even Jonah could not keep open when the avalanches came down like the mountain shaking out its hair.

Wren sat with the map spread across their knees in the small rooms they still kept near the archive, though they slept now in Jonah's chamber in the tasters' wing, because after everything, being apart at night had become unthinkable. Candlelight moved across the parchment. Somewhere below, the city coughed and muttered. The frost on the window had grown ferns and feathers, white forests in miniature, and beyond it the sky was the color of a blade held near a fire—not warm, only lit.

Jonah came in smelling of woodsmoke and the bitter leaf they chewed against the cold, and stood over Wren's shoulder the way they always did, reading maps with a smuggler's eye rather than a scholar's.

"That route's dead," they said, tapping the pass above Hollowmere. "I could take a goat down it in June. In this? You'd be feeding the crows."

"Then we have no route," Wren said. "That's the point I'm trying not to make."

Jonah was quiet a moment. When they spoke again, their voice had changed—flattened into the careful tone they used for hard truths, the one Wren had learned meant *sit down.*

"The rationing edict went out today. Quarter-loaf in the city by month's end. Half that in the villages." They turned Wren's chair gently with one hand, so they were facing each other. "There are granaries under the palace that haven't been opened since Isolde's grandmother. There's grain rotting in sealed vaults because opening them means admitting how bad it is. And there are—" Jonah stopped. Started again. "There are people in the lower quarters burning their furniture to stay warm. Burning the floors they sleep on."

"I know."

"You know what you don't know?" Jonah crouched, bringing their face level with Wren's, and Wren saw the exhaustion in them plainly, the bruised skin under the eyes, the hands that never quite stopped trembling since the deep court—a tremor Jonah insisted was nothing, was just cold, was just the aftermath. "You don't know what the crown is planning. Because I taste for them, love. I stand behind the queen's shoulder while she eats, and I listen, and lately what I hear is ministers saying the word *cull* in the soft voices they use for things they've already decided."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

"They're talking about the passes," Jonah said. "Sealing the northern villages off entirely. Declaring them lost to winter. Writing off—" their jaw worked— "eleven thousand people. On paper. With wax seals. So the rest of the kingdom survives on what those eleven thousand would have eaten."

Wren looked down at their own map, at the neat little symbols they'd drawn for granaries and garrisons, and felt suddenly sick at how clean the lines were, how innocent a map could look while describing murder by paperwork.

"And Alaric?" they asked quietly. "What does he say?"

Alaric Vane had been given rooms in the east tower—gilded, guarded, and officially nonexistent, because a prince returned from the dead after a hundred years was not a gift to a fragile court but a bomb with a heartbeat. He spent his days giving testimony to anyone the queen permitted to hear it, which was fewer people every week, and his nights sitting with Wren and Jonah over the fire, learning the names of things that hadn't existed when he was taken: the tea, the lamp-oil, the little clock on the mantel whose ticking he still flinched at, because in the deep court nothing ticked, nothing marked time, and time had been the thing they fed on.

"He says what he always says," Jonah answered. "He says the crown made this bargain and the crown must unmake it. He says it louder every day, and quieter every day is the part where anyone listens."

* * *

Three nights later, the queen summoned them both.

It was not a friendly summons. It arrived in the form of four guards and a captain who would not meet Wren's eyes, and it took them through corridors Wren had mapped a dozen times and still found disorienting at night, when the torches made the tapestries move. They were brought to the council chamber, where Queen Isolde sat alone at the long table with a single document before her and her hands folded on top of it like a woman keeping something pinned down.

She looked older than she had in autumn. Wren realized, with a strange distant clarity, that the endless winter had been the queen's inheritance too—that Isolde had grown up inside the pact's protection the way Jonah had grown up inside the guild, shaped by a safety she'd never been allowed to question.

"You stole from the deep court," the queen said, without preamble. Her voice was level. Her eyes were not. "You have cost this kingdom its shield. Eleven thousand people will die in the north this season who would have lived. Perhaps thirty thousand by spring. And you sit here—" her gaze flicked to Jonah, then to Wren, and something in it was almost, terribly, personal— "you sit here having known, the pair of you, what the price would be. The prophecy told you. The archives told you. Maret told you. And still you climbed down and broke it."

"We broke a pact that fed a man to monsters for a century," Jonah said.

"Yes," Isolde said. "And it worked. That is the thing none of you will say aloud. It *worked*. A hundred years of mild winters. A hundred years of harvests. My grandfather's peace, my father's peace, my peace. Purchased at a price I did not set—but paid all the same, and paid reliably, and now unpaid, and now my people are freezing in the dark while a prince who should be dust lectures me about morality from a tower I am feeding him out of my own shrinking stores." She unfolded her hands. Underneath the document was a list, and Wren, map-reader that they were, understood its shape before understanding its contents: names, villages, numbers. The cull, written out fair.

"There is a way to restore the pact," the queen said. "The Regent will treat with us. Serah Dole's notes survived her—" Wren's chest tightened at the name; they had not let themselves think of Serah's last stand at the gate, the way she'd turned to face the thing that woke, the whisper she'd given Jonah— "and her notes describe the terms. A soul, freely surrendered, anchors the winter for another century. One soul. Against thirty thousand lives." Isolde looked at Jonah, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "You have already been below. You know the roads. You know the faces. You are, whether any of us likes it or not, the thorn that burns. I am asking you to finish what you started."

"You're asking me to find someone to sell," Jonah said.

"I am asking you to do arithmetic."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow on a roof. Wren found Jonah's hand under the table and held it, and felt the tremor in it, and held it harder.

"No," Wren heard themselves say.

The queen's eyes came around to them, cold and surprised, as though a piece of furniture had spoken.

"No," Wren said again, steadier, standing, because some things could not be said sitting down. "Your Majesty, I've read the original treaty. I copied it out twice during the trial. Do you remember what the deep court asked for, a hundred years ago? Before your ancestor offered them a prince instead?" They had spent long nights with that document, and it had haunted them ever since, and now, standing in the cold light of the council chamber, they finally understood why. "They asked for *gifts*. Freely given. Memories, songs, names, first snows—the record lists them. The Regent wanted willing tribute. Your ancestor's scribe wrote it down and then wrote underneath, in his own hand: *the crown will not pay in honesty; we shall pay in flesh instead, for flesh is cheaper.*"

Isolde's expression did not change. But her hands, Wren noticed, had gone still in a new way.

"The deep court has been starved for a hundred years," Wren went on, the words coming faster now, a map unrolling. "Starved of exactly the thing it originally asked for. Everything it has eaten since has been stolen goods—Alaric's soul, wrung out nightly, a century of forced feeding. Of course the court is monstrous. We made it monstrous. We taught it that the surface world only ever gives what it has first taken from someone else." They drew breath. Beside them Jonah had gone very quiet, in the particular way that meant their mind was moving fast. "So don't send anyone down to sell a soul. Send someone down to *negotiate*."

"With what currency?" the queen asked, and there was something in her voice now that was not quite contempt. Curiosity, maybe. Or the exhausted hunger of a ruler grasping at anything that wasn't a list of village names.

"Honesty," Wren said. "Memory, freely given. The things they asked for at the beginning. And an apology a hundred years overdue, delivered by the heir of the family that brokered the original sale." They glanced at Jonah. "We have one of those."

* * *

Jonah did not speak until they were halfway back across the palace, in a corridor of high windows where the snow piled against the glass in drifts taller than a man, blue-white and silent, pressing in like the sea.

"You realize what you proposed in there," they said at last.

"I proposed diplomacy."

"You proposed walking back into the court that nearly ate us and asking the Pale Regent—the creature that offered me a comfortable forgetting with a smile—to trust the descendants of the people who cheated it." Jonah stopped walking. Snow-light washed their face colorless. "Wren. I inherited the Eriksen claim and I burned it at the gate. That's done. But the name still opens doors down there, and every one of those doors opens onto a grudge."

"Yes."

"And you want to go anyway."

"I want *us* to go anyway," Wren said. "If you'll come. I'm not—I can't do that place without you. I won't pretend otherwise." They had learned, in the months since the thaw-that-wasn't, to stop dressing their fear in strategy. It got them further. "But Jonah—listen to me. Listen to what's happening out there." They pressed their palm flat against the frozen window, and the cold bit through skin, and beyond the glass the city lay under its burial of white, chimneys smoking thin and desperate, the river a black seam in all that pallor. "Your queen is doing arithmetic. Every day she doesn't hear a better answer, the list gets shorter and the arithmetic gets easier. Serah bought us time at that gate with her life. Alaric is testifying himself hoarse. If we wait for the crown to choose its soul, it will choose one, and it will choose someone who can't refuse, and in a hundred years some other pair of fools will be standing in a snowy corridor asking each other how it ever came to this."

Jonah looked at the window. At the city. At the drift pressing against the glass like something leaning in to listen.

"When I was small," they said, "the guild taught me that every locked thing has a key, and if you can't find the key, you picked the wrong lock. I spent my whole life believing problems were doors to be slipped past. Quietly. Alone." Their mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You're telling me to knock."

"I'm telling you to knock loudly," Wren said, "and bring witnesses."

For a long moment Jonah said nothing, and the snow ticked softly against the glass, and somewhere far below them a bell rang the hour in a voice muffled by weather, counting out the dwindling days.

Then Jonah reached up and touched the scar at their collarbone—the birthmark-shaped mark that had matched the gate, the mark that had faded to a pale ghost since the toll was paid—and Wren watched them make the decision the way they did everything: completely, without ceremony, like stepping off a ledge they'd already measured.

"All right," Jonah said. "We go to the thorn-door. We ask for parley. Freely-given memory for freely-given mercy, the old terms, the honest ones." Their eyes came up, sharp and scared and certain all at once. "But understand what we're carrying, Wren. We're not carrying a treaty. We're carrying an apology a century late, from the family that started it, to a court that's been starving on our ancestors' lie the whole time. If the Regent refuses—"

"If the Regent refuses," Wren said, "then we'll know the door truly was locked from our side all along. And at least we'll have knocked."

Outside, the wind rose along the mountain's flank with a sound like vast slow breathing, and the snow against the windows thickened until the city vanished entirely, and the two of them stood in the blue light planning an embassy into the dark—while far beneath their feet, under root and stone and a hundred years of silence, something ancient waited at a door in a hollowed tree, listening, perhaps, for footsteps it had almost stopped hoping to hear.
