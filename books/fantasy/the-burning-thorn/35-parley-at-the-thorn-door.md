## 35. Parley at the Thorn-Door

The deep court had a smell I would carry to my grave: cold iron and crushed violets and something older underneath, like the air in a cellar where nothing has ever rotted because nothing has ever lived. We came up the last stair from Alaric's cell with the soul-seed warm against my sternum, wrapped in silk and my own heartbeat, and the corridor opened before us like a throat.

Wren walked on my left, one hand hooked through my belt—not for balance but for proof. Alaric Vane walked on my right, and I still could not look at him too long. A hundred years of being fed upon had worn him down to something like a candle burned to its final ring of wax: you could see the shape he'd been, the tall shoulders, the dark hair gone white as frost at thirty-three, but mostly what you saw was light passing through him.

"They will hear us coming," Alaric said. His voice was dry paper. "They always hear everything."

"Good," I said. "I'm tired of whispering."

Wren squeezed my belt. *Liar*, that squeeze said. *You have whispered your whole life.* Both true. But there is a difference between whispering so no one sees you and whispering so the words land soft, and I meant to learn it before dawn.

* * *

The Pale Regent received us in the Hall of Mirrors Turned Wrong, which is what Wren called it later, though the courtiers below had another name I never learned to pronounce. It was the inversion of Queen Isolde's throne room the way a reflection is an inversion: same proportions, same long carpet, same dais—but the banners hung downward like roots, the chandeliers held cold blue fire that gave light without warmth, and the throne itself was carved of black ice that never melted and never cracked.

The Regent sat upon it, or stood beside it, or was it—I never did decide. The Pale Regent was tall the way winter is tall, all vertical distance and no substance, wearing a face like a face remembered by someone who hadn't seen the original in decades. When we entered, every courtier in the hall turned to look, and every courtier wore a borrowed face. I recognized three of them. One belonged to a shepherd who'd frozen in the Thornfells forty years ago. One belonged, gods help me, to my mother.

I stopped walking. Wren's hand tightened on my belt.

"That's not her," Wren murmured. "That's not anyone. It's a lure."

"I know." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It's just been a long time since I've seen her face anywhere but behind my eyelids."

The Regent raised one hand—a gesture like frost climbing a window—and the hall went silent in the way snow silences a field.

*Jonah Eriksen.* The voice arrived inside my skull rather than my ears, cold and vast and terribly tired. *Taster. Smuggler. Thief. You stand in my court with stolen goods against your chest.*

"Not stolen," I said. "Repossessed."

A sound moved through the assembled courtiers—laughter, or the memory of laughter, worn thin from disuse.

*Bold. The last Eriksen who stood here was bold too. Your great-grandmother sold me a prince with a smile on her face and gold in her eyes, and she told herself she was saving her family's farm. Everyone who sells tells themselves a story. What is yours?*

I made myself breathe. Made myself think of the tasting spoon, of the hundred small deaths I'd swallowed so a queen could eat safely, of the discipline of putting poison in your mouth and letting it pass through you without becoming it.

"My story is that I've come to renegotiate," I said. "Not to beg. Not to fight. To trade."

*Trade.* The Regent descended from the dais—or unfolded from it; the motion hurt to watch, like watching ice shift on a lake. *I have heard that word from your kind before. Your crown trades with me every century. They bring me a name written on vellum and I give them winter's mercy, and everyone above goes on eating bread and calling themselves civilized.*

"And how has that worked for you?" I asked.

The hall went very still.

"Because from where I'm standing," I went on, before fear could catch up with my mouth, "it looks like you're starving. You've got a hundred years of souls on a slow drip—one prince, fed upon nightly, rationed out like grain in a siege. You wear dead faces because you can't grow your own. Your fire is cold. Your music—" I gestured at the distant, thin sound that drifted through the halls down here, more echo than melody. "Your music is a memory of music. You are the most powerful thing under this mountain and you are dying of hunger in slow motion, and the pact your crown keeps renewing is not generosity. It's a leash. They give you exactly enough to keep you docile."

*You are very brave,* the Regent said, *for someone whose heart is beating fast enough to count from here.*

"I'm also right."

* * *

We had argued about this for two days in Alaric's cell, Wren pacing the little stone room while I sat with my back against the wall and Alaric watched us both with the patient hunger of a man who had not been consulted about his own fate in a century.

"You can't negotiate with it," Wren had said. "It's had a hundred years to practice being patient and we've had a week. It'll twist every word."

"Probably," I'd said. "But Jonah Eriksen is very good at twisting words back. It's most of what smuggling is. And most of what tasting is, if you think about it—reading what's really in the dish, beneath what it claims to be."

"And if it simply takes the seed back?"

"Then it takes the seed back," Alaric had said quietly, from his corner, "and I would ask you both to run and not look back, because I have been eaten slowly for a hundred years and I promise you there is very little left worth fighting over."

"No," I'd said. That word had cost me more than any poison ever had. "No. Nobody gets handed to the dark anymore. Not you, not Serah, not me. That's the whole point. That's the entire point of everything."

Now, standing in the wrong-mirrored hall with the Regent circling me like weather, I held onto that word like a handrail in a rockslide.

*Speak your trade, then,* the Regent said. *Amuse me before I take back what is mine.*

"The pact ends," I said. "Not broken—ended. Retired. And in its place, a new one. Freely given."

*There is no freely given. Everything above wants something.*

"Everything everywhere wants something. That's what makes it a trade instead of a theft." I unwrapped the silk from my chest, slowly, showing them—the courtiers leaning in like plants toward light—the small burning seed that had been Alaric Vane's soul. It glowed like a coal remembering summer. The Regent's borrowed face did not change, but the temperature of the hall dropped ten degrees, and every courtier took one involuntary step forward, and I knew then that I was right, that they were starving, that a century of rationed souls had brought the deep court to the edge of its own long famine.

"You feed on this," I said. "Nightly. A hundred years of nightly meals from one man, and look what it's done to him." I nodded at Alaric, who stood straighter than I'd yet seen him stand, offering himself as evidence the way only the truly spent can. "And look what it's done to you. This isn't sustenance. It's starvation with ceremony. You've been kept alive on scraps by a crown that calls it peace."

*Say what you offer,* the Regent said, and for the first time the vast cold voice sounded less like winter and more like want.

"Memories," I said. "Freely given. Willingly traded. Not torn out of a chained prince—offered, by people who choose to give them, in exchange for winter's protection, openly negotiated, renewed by consent rather than by secret sacrifice. Every year, at the thorn-door, whoever wishes may come and pay a memory into your keeping. A first kiss. A summer day. A grief they're tired of carrying. Small things, given gladly, instead of one enormous thing taken forever."

Silence. The cold fire in the chandeliers flickered—actually flickered, the first uncertain movement I'd seen from anything down here that wasn't hungry.

*Memories are thin fare,* the Regent said at last. *A soul is a feast.*

"A soul is a feast you eat once and then own," I said. "Memories are meals. Daily. Voluntary. Given with love instead of terror." I took a breath, because the next part was the part that mattered, the part I'd rehearsed until Wren threatened to gag me. "And here is the thing you don't know, because you've never been given anything: food tastes different when it's offered. I know. I've spent my life swallowing things. Poison taken in fear sits in the body like a stone. Poison taken in calm passes through like water. I am the finest taster in the kingdom above and I am telling you, Regent, that willingness changes the flavor of everything. You have been drinking from a well for a century. I am offering you rain."

* * *

Later, Wren would tell me they'd watched the Regent's face during that speech and seen something crack in it—not the face itself, which was borrowed and flawless, but whatever moved behind it. Wren, who read landscapes the way priests read scripture, said the whole hall shifted at that moment, the way a mountain shifts before it decides whether to slide.

The Regent was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I began composing my own eulogy. Then:

*Show me.*

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at me. This was the part we hadn't rehearsed, because you cannot rehearse giving away a piece of yourself, and because Wren had sworn—if it came to this—that they would be the one to pay first, and I had sworn the opposite, and we had agreed, finally, that we would let the moment choose.

The moment chose me. Of course it did. I was the taster. Tasting was the whole of my life: put the unknown in your mouth, let it do its worst, survive it.

I thought of the childhood summer I'd already paid at the gate—the toll that had opened our way down. That one was gone; I felt its absence like a missing tooth, a smooth place where something used to be. So I reached for another. I found one easily, because I had spent years curating my memories the way a smuggler curates routes, keeping the load light, keeping only what served. There were so many I barely needed.

I chose the night I'd first kissed Wren. In the archive, among the maps, ink on their fingers, lamplight turning their hair to bronze, the taste of cheap wine and older dust, the terrifying joy of choosing to be seen.

I held it up in my mind like a coin between two fingers. And then—and this was the trick, the sleight-of-hand no thief had ever taught me, the thing I understood only because my body was a ledger of swallowed harms—I did not clutch it. I opened my hand.

*Here,* I said. *Take it. Gladly. It's a good memory. It's one of the best ones I have. I give it to you because you are starving and I have known hunger, and because I am asking you to believe that the world above is capable of better than what we did to Alaric Vane, and belief should cost the believer something.*

The Regent reached toward me—or the winter reached, or the mountain's long patience reached—and took it.

I felt it go. Not ripped—*lifted*, the way you lift a thorn from flesh, cleanly, leaving the wound small and already closing. For half a heartbeat there was a gap in me shaped like lamplight and ink-stained fingers, and then even the gap smoothed over, and I stood in the cold hall knowing only that I had given something precious and could no longer remember what.

Beside me, Wren made a small sound. Later they would tell me what I'd lost, and I would grieve it secondhand, loving the story without owning it—which is its own strange gift, and maybe the Regent knew that, and maybe that was the point all along.

* * *

The Regent stood very still for the space of ten heartbeats. Twenty. The courtiers did not move. The cold fire did not flicker. Somewhere far above us, I imagined, snow was falling on the Thornfells, falling on the kingdom that had bought its comfort with a boy's soul, falling on Queen Isolde's palace where they were probably already arguing about what to do with the traitor Jonah Eriksen once the winter finished collapsing around their ears.

Then the Pale Regent laughed.

It was not a beautiful sound. It was rusted and enormous and it shook frost from the ceiling, and the courtiers flinched from it as from sunlight, and I understood that they had not heard it in a hundred years either.

*A meal,* the Regent said, and the vast voice had changed—thinned, quickened, come closer to the surface of things. *Given freely. Do you know how long—* It stopped. Gathered itself. When it spoke again, the cold was still there, but under the cold was something almost like shame. *Your great-grandmother stood in this hall and lied to me beautifully, and I accepted the lie because a soul is a soul however it comes, and I told myself hunger excuses everything. I have told myself that for a century while I ate a man alive. You come with one small memory and a speech, and it feeds me more truly than a year of Alaric Vane's suffering. I find that I hate you for showing me this. I find that I accept.*

"Then we have terms," I said, and my knees nearly buckled with relief that I converted, through long practice, into a bow.

*Terms.* The Regent turned—or rotated, or became briefly visible from all sides at once—to face Wren and Alaric. *The pact of vellum and blood is ended. In its place: the Door of Gifts. Once each season, at the turning, the thorn-door opens, and any who wish may bring memories freely given, and receive in exchange the winter's mercy—mild snows, safe passes, fields that sleep and wake. No names taken. No souls. No chains. If the gifts cease, the winter resumes as it was. This is my word, and my word is the only law I have ever kept.*

"And him?" Wren asked, nodding at Alaric. Their voice was rough. They had watched me pay; they had nearly paid themselves; they were holding on to composure the way a climber holds a ledge.

Alaric stepped forward. A hundred years of being eaten, and he stepped forward himself, unasked.

"I don't want vengeance," he said, in that dry-paper voice that was getting stronger by the hour now that the feeding had stopped, now that the seed in its silk wrapping pulsed gently against my chest like a second heart waiting to come home. "I want spring. I want to see what a hundred years did to the world. And I want—" he paused, and looked at the Regent with something that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was perhaps its foundation stone, "—I want to be the first witness that the old way is over. Let me walk out of here. Let me testify. That's my price for not hating you forever."

The Regent regarded him for a long moment.

*Walk out, then,* it said. *And when you tell them what was done to you, tell them also what was offered here tonight. Both truths. All of it. I am done with half-stories. Half-stories are how a hundred years of horror gets called peace.*

* * *

We climbed for hours. The Regent sent no escort, which Wren said was either trust or indifference, and I said was probably both, because power doesn't need to watch you leave when it knows the door will open again. The cold fire dimmed behind us. The mirrored halls gave way to root-hung tunnels, the tunnels to the hollow throat of the ancient thorn-tree, and then—suddenly, shockingly—there was sky.

Real sky. Grey and snow-heavy and absolutely, magnificently ordinary.

We came out of the tree into a world gone white and silent, the storm of the pact's collapse still raging but gentler now, confused, a winter that had forgotten what it was for. Alaric stood in the snow and let it fall on his face and wept without sound, and I stood beside him and let him, and Wren stood behind us both with a hand on each of our shoulders, mapping us, I think, adding us to some interior chart of everything worth keeping.

"We should move," I said finally. "The queen's hunters will have found the false gate by now. And there's a court above that needs to hear what happened below—from three living witnesses, before anyone spins it otherwise."

Alaric wiped his face. He looked older than the mountains and younger than me, both at once.

"Lead on, taster," he said. "You seem to know the way."

I did. That was the joke of it, the grace of it: the invisible girl who'd spent her life learning hidden roads, leading a resurrected prince and the woman who loved her up out of the dark, toward a throne room where every lie of a hundred years was about to come due.

The snow was already beginning, very faintly, to soften.
