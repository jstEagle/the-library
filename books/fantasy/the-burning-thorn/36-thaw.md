## 36. Thaw

The winter broke on a Tuesday.

Wren would remember that afterward — the smallness of it, the ordinariness of the day the world ended and began again. There had been no trumpet call, no proclamation nailed to the palace gates. Only a sound in the night like a held breath finally released, a long exhalation rolling down out of the Thornfells, and then water. Water everywhere, everywhere at once, as if the mountains had been keeping count of every frozen hour for a hundred years and meant to pay it all back before dawn.

Wren woke to it in the roadhouse loft above the stable at Hollowmere, Jonah's arm heavy across their chest, and lay listening to the roof drip. Drip — not the sharp ticking of melt through a bad shingle that they'd heard all winter, but a steady, generous drumming, the sound of a roof shedding weight. When Wren rose and pushed open the shutters, the world outside was running.

The valley had become a river's country overnight. Snow still lay deep on the high shoulders of the Thornfells, blinding white under a sky scrubbed clean and impossibly blue, but down here the fields were emerging from their long imprisonment, black and glistening, stitched with streams that hadn't existed yesterday. The millrace below the inn had swollen to twice its width and was tearing past with a sound like continuous applause. Ice that had sheathed the eaves since midwinter hung in melting daggers, letting go one by one, each fall a small bright detonation on the stones.

"Is it—" Jonah's voice, sleep-roughened, behind them. Wren turned. Jonah stood in the doorway wrapped in a blanket, hair wild, and for a moment neither of them said anything else, because what was there to say that the water wasn't already saying better?

"It's breaking," Wren said. "The thaw. It's really—"

And then they were both laughing, or crying, or some animal thing between the two, holding onto each other in the cold blue light while the whole valley roared its release around them.

* * *

By noon the low meadows were lakes. By evening, the first of the flood refugees came straggling up the cart-road to Hollowmere: a drover whose herds had been cut off by the drowned fords, two sisters from the weaving village whose cottage stood in four feet of snowmelt, an old man who had walked eleven miles through slush to reach higher ground and arrived weeping without knowing why. Maret fed them all from stores that had been meant to last until midsummer, and nobody asked her how she could spare it, because everyone understood now that the arithmetic of scarcity they'd lived by for a century had been quietly repealed.

Wren spent those first days doing what Wren always did when the world became too large to hold in the mind: drawing it. They sat at the big table by the window with paper stretched out before them and mapped the flood as it moved — the new courses of the rivers, the villages cut off, the fords that survived, the roads turned to bogs. It was work and it was prayer and it was penance, all three at once. Every line they inked was a line someone downstream could use to find dry ground, or a bridge, or each other.

The grief came with the water. That was the thing no one had prepared them for — that the thaw would not feel only like salvation. In the third week, when the worst of the flooding had passed and the valleys began to give up their dead, Wren understood.

The long winter had been gentle in its way, or rather it had been *consistent*, and consistency had let people build lives inside it. But the breaking of it was violent. Roofs collapsed under wet snow sliding from the heights. The Vane River took three bridges and the ferryman who'd worked the crossing at Aldermoor for forty years. A hillside above the terraced orchards, its roots rotted loose by a century of frost heave, came down in the night and buried six houses, and the kingdom learned the names of the families inside them one by one, read aloud in the market squares by criers whose voices cracked.

And beneath the named dead lay the uncounted ones — the ones the winter itself had taken over the hundred years, the children born blue-lipped in unheatable rooms, the old people who had simply lain down one February night and not gotten up, the generations of small surrenders that everyone had called *the price of protection* right up until they learned there had been a different price all along, paid in secret, by a boy who hadn't been asked.

Wren drew maps by day and went to the mourning-fields by dusk, where the towns burned pyres of wet wood that smoked sullenly for hours, and stood among strangers in the smoky light, and let themselves grieve for people they'd never met. It felt necessary. It felt like part of the mapping — charting the country of loss so that no one could pretend, later, that it had never been there.

"They'll plant early," Maret said one evening, watching the smoke rise. She had come out to stand beside Wren at the field's edge, leaning on her stick, her breath pluming even in the warming air. "That's what folk don't understand yet. They think the thaw is an ending. It's a beginning with teeth. Ground that's been frozen a hundred years is hungry ground, girl. It'll take seed like a starving thing takes bread." Her eyes, pale as skimmed milk, reflected the fire. "The question is whether this kingdom has learned to feed anything honestly. Or whether it'll just find itself another Alaric."

* * *

Alaric Vane came to Hollowmere in the fourth week of the thaw, riding a borrowed mule and wearing clothes that fit him badly, because everything fit him badly now — he was a man shaped by a century elsewhere, and the world above had moved on without consulting his proportions.

Wren met him at the gate and was struck, again, by how *ordinary* he looked. That had been the hardest truth of the rescue to hold onto: not a monster, not a ghost, not a burning relic of prophecy, but a thin, gray-eyed man of perhaps thirty-five years in appearance and a hundred and seven in endurance, who sometimes stopped mid-sentence because he'd forgotten the word for a common thing — *door*, once, and *rain*, which had made them all go quiet. He ate slowly, always, as if food might be taken back. He flinched at mirrors. And he remembered more every day, memory returning to him like feeling returning to a frostbitten limb — painfully, in waves, with a great deal of screaming that he mostly did alone, in private, where he thought no one could hear.

He stayed three days. On the second evening, Wren found him in the stable loft looking at Wren's maps, spread across the trestle table — the true maps, the smuggler roads and the sealed gates and the deep-court approaches, the cartography that had ended the guild's monopoly and would, in time, end a great many other monopolies besides.

"You drew all this," Alaric said. His voice was quiet, careful, a man handling fragile things. "From fragments. Guesses. Before you ever saw it."

"I had good sources." Wren smiled. "I had the best source in the kingdom. They just didn't know they were talking about themselves half the time."

Alaric touched the edge of the map where the thorn-tree stood marked, the door beneath the root, and his hand trembled slightly. "I remember the tree," he said. "From before. From when I was a boy. I used to climb it. Nobody knew it was hollow then — or nobody said." He looked up. "A hundred years, and I climbed over my own prison and never knew."

"That's not a failure of yours."

"No," he agreed mildly. "It's a failure of everyone else's. That's rather the point I keep arriving at." He rolled the map closed with unexpected gentleness, and something in his face settled into decision, the way stone settles after an earthquake finds its final position. "I'm leaving, you know. Tomorrow."

Wren waited. Outside, the millrace thundered; it seemed the valley would never again be entirely silent, and Wren found they loved it.

"The court wants me in the capital," Alaric said. "The new council — Isolde's remnant, whatever we're calling them now — wants me enthroned. Restored. The prince returned from the dark, a living symbol of the kingdom's renewal." He said the words flatly, without bitterness, which somehow made them worse. "They've already commissioned a statue. Did you know that? Marble. Me, in marble, holding a lantern. I asked who chose the lantern and nobody could tell me. Nobody had thought to ask whether I wanted to hold anything at all."

"What will you tell them?"

"I told them the truth." He almost smiled. "I told them I would not sit a throne built on my suffering, and that if they built it anyway, I would spend the rest of my life standing next to it telling visitors exactly what it was made of. They found this less inspiring than you'd expect." He set the map down. "I'm going south. To the coast, then over the sea. There are places — I've been reading, in your friend's books — places where they keep records properly. Where a witness can testify and be heard rather than managed. My testimony is the only weapon I have, Wren, and I mean to sharpen it somewhere it can't be confiscated."

"And when you're done?"

Alaric considered the question seriously, the way he considered everything, as though a century of being denied choices had made him refuse to waste a single one now. "Then perhaps I'll come back. Perhaps I'll grow apples. I'm told the valleys will be good for apples now." He glanced toward the window, toward the roaring water and the black fields drinking it in. "Your Jonah gave me back my soul. You gave me back my name — I'd nearly lost it, you know. Down there, near the end, I could remember everything except myself." He paused at the ladder, silhouetted against the lamplight from below. "Tell them both: I intend to be worth the price. Not out of duty. Out of spite, mostly. Spite keeps remarkably well."

He left at dawn on the borrowed mule, heading south on a road that existed on no official map but appeared, accurate to the fencepost, on Wren's. Wren watched him out of sight and then went inside and added a small annotation to the master copy: *Road south, passable year-round, traveled by a free man.*

* * *

Spring came up out of the ground like a rumor confirmed.

It happened faster than anyone believed possible — faster than the oldest songs described, faster than the almanacs' cautious projections. The hungry earth, a century-frozen and then flooded and then warmed, erupted. Grass came in green enough to hurt the eyes. The fruit trees, unpruned for generations, blossomed so heavily that the branches bowed and the air in the orchard lanes was thick enough to taste, sweet and faintly alcoholic, bees drunk in the flowers by midmorning. Farmers planted six weeks early and then, staring at their sprouting fields with the superstitious terror of people who had never been allowed good fortune, planted again just to be sure.

Wren walked the valley roads in those weeks with a surveyor's kit and a notebook, mapping the new world the way they'd mapped the old one, and everywhere they went they saw the same tableau: people standing in their doorways looking at the hills as if they'd never seen them before. Which, Wren realized, was true. No one under sixty had ever seen these mountains bare. Children pointed at ridgelines that had been white walls all their lives and asked what the brown streaks were, and their parents, who didn't know either, said *paths* or *rock* or *I don't know*, and the not-knowing rang through the valley like a bell — the whole kingdom illiterate in its own landscape, learning to read it together.

There were hard days too. The famine that had loomed through the collapse of the pact never quite arrived, but hunger's shadow passed close enough to touch: the granaries were half-empty after the flood year, the first plantings were weeks from harvest, and in the border districts there was rationing, and grumbling, and the first ugly whispers — *whose fault was this, who broke the winter, find them* — that Wren knew would need answering eventually, publicly, with names attached to deeds. Alaric's testimony, when it came back across the sea in letters and depositions, would answer some of it. The rest would take years.

But there were also evenings like the one in late spring when Wren climbed the ridge above Hollowmere at sunset and found the whole valley laid out below in gold and green, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the river running bright and unhurried in its recovered bed, and stood there until dark simply breathing. Jonah came up the path after a while — Wren always knew Jonah's footfall now, light and deliberate, a taster's tread — and stood beside them without speaking, shoulder warm against shoulder.

"Maret says the barley's already knee-high," Jonah said at last. "She says she's never seen anything like it. She says a lot of things she's never seen lately." A pause. "Do you think it'll hold? The parley. The trade. All of it."

Wren thought about the Pale Regent's face in the last moments below the mountain — that vast, starved attention, the way the cold fire had bent toward Jonah when the offer of freely given memory was made, the way a starving creature bends toward an open hand. The Regent had accepted the new terms with what might, in a being like that, have been gratitude. The memories came monthly now, carried down by volunteers who returned shaken but whole, paid honestly, thanked — *thanked*, a word no one had expected to apply. The winter-pact was becoming something no ledger had a column for: a relationship.

"I think," Wren said slowly, "that hiding made it cruel. Both sides. A hundred years of secrecy and everyone got hungrier and colder and smaller. And I think we've only had one spring. Ask me in ten winters." They turned to look at Jonah — at the face the prophecy books had drawn, the face the whole kingdom knew now, though Jonah still flinched a little at being recognized, still moved through crowds like someone expecting to be invisible and surprised each time they weren't. "Ask us. Plural. I'm not letting you do any more reckoning alone."

Jonah's mouth curved. "You've been talking to Maret."

"I've been talking to everyone. It's a roadhouse, love. Talking is the entire business model."

They stood a while longer as the light failed, and the first stars came out over the Thornfells — over peaks that were still white at the summits, and would be, Wren supposed, forever, but white the way mountains are supposed to be white: chosen by altitude, not by curse.

"One more thing," Jonah said quietly, as they turned back down the path. "There's something I have to do. Reckon it all up. What I paid, what I lost, what's gone for good. The summer, the name, the—" Jonah's hand brushed their own temple, where the toll had taken its quiet bites. "I keep putting it off. But the book's on the shelf downstairs with a blank last page, and I keep thinking... maybe I should write it down. What's missing. So it's accounted for. So it's real."

Wren laced their fingers through Jonah's. Below them the roadhouse windows glowed, full of travelers and stories and the smell of Maret's bread, and beyond it the whole thawing world ran with water, generous and loud, keeping its ancient promises at last.

"Then we'll reckon it together," Wren said. "Every piece. Starting tonight."

Jonah squeezed their hand once, and they went down together into the lamplight, where the accounting waited — and where, Wren suspected, some of what had been lost might yet be found, retold, and kept.
