## 37. The Price Paid

The thaw came to Thornfell House slowly, the way grief comes—first as a rumor of water under the eaves, then as a long dripping that would not stop.

Jonah heard it before they woke each morning now: *drip, drip*, from the icicles surrendering along the roofline, a sound like a clock counting down something they could not name. They lay in the narrow bed of the house Wren had rented at the mountain's foot—a real bed, with a quilt that smelled of lavender and someone else's grandmother—and they took inventory.

It was an old habit. The taster's habit. Every morning for eleven years, Jonah had run their tongue along their teeth and read their body like a ledger: the faint metallic ghost of yesterday's arsenic trial, the tightness behind the eyes that meant belladonna residue, the scar on the left forearm where a guildmaster had once tested them with adder's venom to see if the boy—or the girl, or whatever Jonah was deciding to be that season—would flinch. They had never flinched. Flinching was a luxury for people whose bodies belonged to themselves.

Now the body belonged to them again, and the ledger had changed its columns. Not poisons anymore. Losses.

Jonah lay still and counted.

*One summer.* That was the first entry, and it was the strangest, because it was not exactly gone. They knew it had existed. Wren had told them about it so many times now that the telling had worn grooves into Jonah's mind, smooth and deep as water-carved stone: the summer Jonah was seven, the summer before the guild took them, when the whole Eriksen household had gone up into the high meadows above the Thornfells to escape a fever in the low country. There had been a dog named Bracken who stole an entire wheel of cheese and buried it badly, so that every fox within three valleys came to dig, and the family had sat on the hillside at dusk watching the foxes quarrel and laughing until their ribs ached. There had been Jonah's mother teaching them to whistle through a blade of grass held between both thumbs. There had been a night sky so thick with stars that Jonah's father had said, solemnly, that if you looked long enough you could see the seams in it, the places where the world had been stitched together by someone in a hurry.

Jonah knew all of this. They could recite it. But when they reached for it—if they closed their eyes and tried to feel the grass, smell the cheese going off in its shallow grave, hear their mother's laugh—the reaching hand closed on nothing. It was like being told about a room in one's own house that had been bricked over. They knew the door was there. They could put their palm flat against the wall. The wall did not open.

*Two,* the ledger continued. *The name.*

Eriksen. They could say it. They could write it. But saying it no longer opened anything either; it was a key filed down past usefulness, a word that fit no lock in the world. The guild had taken it when they were eight, and the crown's records had been scoured of it a century before that, and now Jonah had paid it into the thorn-door besides—the ancestral claim, the seller's inheritance, the whole rotten root of it—and what remained was a sound. A shape the mouth made. They had introduced themselves to Old Maret's granddaughter last week as *Jonah*, just Jonah, and the girl had asked, incurious and kind, *just Jonah?* and Jonah had said yes, and meant it, and grieved it, all in the same breath.

*Three:* smaller things, scattered things, the shrapnel of the toll. The taste of honey—they had discovered this in the first week after coming down from the mountain, spooning it straight from the jar like a child, waiting for the sweetness to arrive. It arrived. But somewhere beneath it, some earlier sweetness was missing, some first sweetness, the original against which all others should have been measured, and everything tasted now like a copy of a copy. Their grandmother's face. They had never known they'd had a grandmother's face to lose until they found the hole where it should have been. A lullaby in a language they could no longer name, which surfaced sometimes at the edge of sleep as pure melody without words, haunting and orphaned, a boat with no harbor.

Jonah counted these the way they had once counted poisons: precisely, without flinching. Flinching was still a luxury. Or perhaps—it occurred to them, staring at the water-stained ceiling—perhaps flinching had always been a refusal, and refusal was what they were done with.

They got up. The floorboards were cold in the honest way of spring floors, cold but promising. Outside the window, the valley was running with meltwater, every stream swollen and loud, the whole world sounding like a river deciding where it wanted to go. Down the slope, the terraces of Thornfell village were black earth and green insistence, the first cabbages already in, the first lambs staggering around on legs like folded paper. Smoke rose straight from a dozen chimneys into a sky the color of a washed blue cloth.

A hundred years of winter, ended in six weeks. The kingdom was learning spring the way a prisoner learns daylight: squinting, disbelieving, weeping a little.

Jonah dressed, went down, and followed the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions to the kitchen, where Wren stood at the stove with ink stains still on their fingers from the night before, arguing cheerfully with a pan of eggs.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. You get a line right here—" Wren tapped the space between their own eyebrows with the wooden spoon, leaving, Jonah noted, a small smear of egg across it "—right when you're tallying up what the door took. I've watched you do it forty times since the thaw."

"And how many times have I caught you doing your version?" Jonah leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, letting the warmth of the kitchen soak through the cold morning still clinging to them. "The one where you redraw the same stretch of coastline seventeen times because you can't stop improving it."

"That's not grief, that's craft."

"It's grief with a compass."

Wren laughed—that laugh, unguarded, too loud for the small kitchen, the laugh Jonah had nearly traded away without ever knowing its price—and turned, and looked at them properly, the way Wren always looked at them: as though Jonah were terrain worth mapping, complicated and worth the effort. "Sit," Wren said. "Eat. And then tell me which one it is today. The wall or the melody."

"The wall," Jonah said. "And the name. And—" they paused, surprised by their own honesty, "the dog. I keep trying to remember what Bracken smelled like and there's just nothing there. You can give me every fact about him and it doesn't help. Facts aren't the dog."

Wren set the eggs down in front of them, sat opposite, and was quiet for a moment. When Wren spoke, their voice had the careful gentleness they used for fragile things—old maps, burned letters, Jonah on bad mornings.

"No," they agreed. "Facts aren't the dog." They turned their spoon over in their fingers. "But I've been thinking about something. What Maret said, back at the Hollow Inn. About how the deep court eats memories, and how a memory eaten secondhand still feeds you a little. She said her mother told her stories of the old winters and she could *feel* them, cold in her chest, though she'd never seen one. Remembering isn't only having. Sometimes it's being given."

Jonah looked up. "You want to give me my own childhood back."

"I want to try. Badly, probably. I'll get details wrong and you'll correct me and eventually we'll have something that belongs to neither of us alone." Wren shrugged, suddenly shy, which was rare enough that Jonah set down their fork. "It won't be the wall opening. I know that. But maybe it's—I don't know. Maybe it's building a new room where the bricked-up one was. Smaller. Different windows. But yours."

The meltwater dripped from the eaves. Somewhere down the valley a lamb was complaining about existence. Jonah found, to their astonishment, that their eyes were hot.

"Tell me about the foxes," they said.

* * *

So it became a practice, that spring, the way tasting had once been a practice and poison-taking had been a practice. Every evening, or most evenings, when the light went long and gold across the valley and the work was done, they sat by the fire and Wren told Jonah Jonah's life.

It should have been unbearable. Jonah had expected it to be unbearable—had dreaded it, those first nights, the way one dreads pressing on a bruise. But there was something in the telling that worked differently from remembering. When Wren described the hillside and the foxes, Jonah could not see it. But they could see *Wren seeing it*—could watch the story move across Wren's face, the delight at the foxes' outrage, the sly tenderness with which Wren had clearly already decided, months ago, that seven-year-old Jonah chasing a cheese-thieving dog uphill was the finest thing they had ever heard of. And that vision of themselves, reflected in the person who loved them, settled into the empty rooms like furniture carried in by a friend. Not the original furniture. Furniture nonetheless. Furniture that said *someone was expected here.*

Some nights Wren drew while they talked. That was the part that undid Jonah most thoroughly. Wren would take out the good paper—not commission paper, but the soft rag stock they saved for things that mattered—and sketch as the story unspooled: the meadow, the crooked farmhouse, a dog mid-leap with a wheel of cheese absurdly intact in his jaws, a small fierce child with Jonah's exact stubborn jaw drawn from a face Wren had studied for years. The sketches accumulated in a folder Wren kept on the shelf, and Jonah caught themselves, more than once, taking the folder down when Wren was out walking, and looking through it, and touching the edges of the pages very carefully, the way one touches relics.

Once—only once—something stranger happened. Wren was telling the story of the grass-whistle, demonstrating with a blade of new spring grass, thumbs together, and produced a thin, wavering note; and Jonah, laughing, took the blade and tried it themselves, and their hands remembered. Not the summer—never the summer—but the *shape*. Thumb over thumb, breath angled just so, and the note came out true on the third try, a clear silver thread of sound, and for a moment the kitchen was full of something that was not quite memory and not quite music but lived in the country between, and Jonah played it until their lips were numb, and Wren sat with tears running freely down their face and pretended to be studying the ceiling.

The body keeps what the mind cannot hold. Jonah had learned that as a taster, in reverse—learning to hold what would kill any other body. Now they learned it forward. The hands remembered whistling. The mouth remembered, faintly, under the honey, that there had once been a first sweetness. The spine remembered being carried, though they could not have said by whom, and woke some mornings with the phantom weight of it, warm between the shoulder blades, like sunlight.

Mourning and gratitude arrived together, inseparable, the way thaw and flood arrive together. Some evenings Jonah wept for the summer, the name, the grandmother, the lullaby. Some evenings they laughed until they ached at Wren's rendition of the great cheese burial, which grew more elaborate with each telling—by the third week there were nine foxes and a disgruntled badger, and Jonah no longer corrected the count, because the embellishments had become part of the inheritance, gifts Wren was adding to a gift. Grief for what was gone. Gratitude for what remained, and for what was being built in the gap. Both at once. Neither canceling the other. Jonah had spent a lifetime believing that a person was a ledger that must balance, debits against credits, and here at last was the proof that they didn't balance, couldn't, weren't meant to—you simply carried both columns, and the carrying was the whole of being alive.

There were hard nights too. The night the queen's pardon arrived, sealed in white wax, restoring to Jonah formally and legally a name that no longer meant anything to them—*the crown acknowledges the service and suffering of the house of Eriksen*—and Jonah had read it twice and set it in the fire and watched it curl, and Wren had not said a word, only moved closer on the bench. The name was paid. The debt was broken at the root. Whatever the parchment offered, it was offering it to a family that no longer existed, and Jonah felt no triumph in burning it, only a strange clean emptiness, like a field after harvest.

And one night, late, Jonah dreamed of the thorn-door itself—the hollow tree, the dark beyond, the voice like grinding stone—and in the dream the door spoke, not demanding but asking, gently, *was it worth it?* and Jonah had answered, in the dream, with perfect clarity: *I don't know yet. Ask me in the spring.* And had woken with those words still in their mouth, and written them down, because dreams that speak in your own voice are giving you instructions.

* * *

On a bright morning in late April, when the last dirty snow had retreated to the highest couloirs and the valley floor was loud with water and birdsong, Jonah walked up the goat-track behind the house—the same track, they realized, that they had once shown Wren in another life, back when the paths were secrets and secrecy was survival. The path was no secret now. Wren's maps had seen to that. Half the village used it to reach the high pasture, and children had worn a shortcut of their own across its middle switchback, indifferent to a century of smuggler tradition.

At the top, where the track crested the shoulder of the fell, Jonah stopped and looked north, up the long throat of the mountains, to where the Thornfells gathered themselves into the white silence of the high country. Somewhere up there was the hollowed tree, the root-hung door, the road down into the mirrored dark. Somewhere up there, the Pale Regent sat in a court lit by cold fire, eating—no. Not eating anymore. Being *given*. The first freely offered memories had gone down the thorn-road a month ago, borne by the parley party, and the reports that came back spoke of a change in the deep court, a slow warming of that frozen light, a Regent who had begun, tentatively, the way a starving man begins to trust bread, to believe the gifts would keep coming.

Somewhere up there, too, was Alaric Vane, living in exile in a stone tower above the pass, writing his testimony in a careful clerk's hand, refusing all offers of restoration, growing vegetables in boxes with the concentration of a man relearning how time works when it belongs to you. He had sent down a basket of the first radishes two weeks ago with a note that said only: *These are mine. I grew them. —A.* Jonah had laughed until they cried, and could not entirely explain to Wren why, and Wren had wisely not asked.

Jonah stood in the wind and took inventory one last time—not the ledger of losses, but the other one, the one they were only now learning to keep. A body that belonged to them, scarred and immune and finally, finally off duty. A lover downstairs drawing maps that would set half a kingdom free of its secrets. A name reduced to ash and a self that had survived the reduction. An empty shelf in the kitchen where, Wren had announced over breakfast, a certain prophecy book was going to live—*on display,* Wren had said, *with the last page blank, so that everyone who visits understands that the future is unwritten, and also so that I have somewhere to put my tea.*

Below, the cottage chimney sent up its thread of smoke. Below, Wren was spreading fresh paper across the kitchen table, and Jonah could see them even from here, through the little window, bent over the first blank sheet of what they had declared, with the gravity of a coronation, would be *the true map of the Thornfells, every road on it, every road, and let the guild choke on it.*

Jonah smiled, and turned back down the track, and went home to be told, once more, about the foxes.
