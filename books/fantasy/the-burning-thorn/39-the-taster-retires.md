## 39. The Taster Retires

The summons came on a morning so mild it felt like an apology.

A page in palace livery found Jonah at the door of the little house on Copper Lane—the house Wren had drawn for them, once, in the days when maps were still secrets—and handed over a card of heavy cream stock edged in silver. *Her Majesty requests the pleasure of your service at the midday meal.* The word *pleasure* sat strangely on the page, like a bird perched on a wire it did not trust.

Jonah turned the card over twice before they understood what they were holding. There had been a time—years thick as winter now—when such a card would have arrived without ceremony at all. A taster was summoned the way a spoon is summoned: taken up, used, set down. No one wrote *requests* to a spoon.

But that was before Alaric Vane had stood in the great hall and testified with a voice like dry paper kindling. Before the old guard had fallen, one by one, like teeth from a rotting jaw. Before Jonah Eriksen—a name that no longer existed anywhere in any ledger, because Jonah had fed it to a door beneath a mountain—had been dragged into the throne room in chains and walked out again to a silence you could have cut into panes and sold as glass.

They were not a spoon anymore. They were something the crown did not yet have a word for, and words were the queen's native currency.

"Tell Her Majesty," Jonah said to the page, "I'll come."

The boy bowed and fled, relieved to be dismissed by someone who spoke plainly. Jonah watched him go, then looked down at their hands. They were steadier than they had been a year ago. Steadier than they had been in the deep court, where every dish had been a question and every answer might be the last. The tremor that had lived in their fingers since the night of the venom practice—that long deliberate dying they had undertaken alone in a rented room, sweating poison out through their skin while the candle burned down to a puddle—had finally gone quiet. Their body, that ledger of everything they had ever swallowed, had balanced its accounts at last.

Wren was in the garden, on their knees in the thaw-soft earth, transplanting seedlings with the fierce tenderness they brought to everything that could be mapped or grown. They looked up when the door opened, and Jonah saw the question arrive in their face before it reached their mouth.

"The queen," Jonah said.

Wren's trowel stopped moving. "You don't have to."

"I know." And that was the whole miracle of it, wasn't it? That Wren said *you don't have to* and meant it, meant it all the way down, the way they meant every line they ever drew. "I want to. Once. On my own terms. I want to taste for her one last time because I choose to—not because she can't eat unless someone cheaper than her dies first."

Wren wiped their hands on their apron and stood, earth clinging to their knees. They crossed the garden and took Jonah's face in their soil-warm palms, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because some things are better said in the grammar of touch.

"You'll come home after," Wren said. Not a question.

"I'll come home after."

"And if she asks you to stay—"

"She won't ask. Queens don't ask. But if she offers—" Jonah smiled, and felt the smile settle into the new lines of their face, the ones the last year had carved. "I've been offered immortality by something older than her crown, my love. The palace has nothing in its pantry I'm hungry for."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them—and kissed them, tasting of loam and green things and the particular spring that had cost a kingdom so much grief to buy.

* * *

The palace had repainted itself.

That was Jonah's first thought, walking up the processional stair they had climbed a thousand times in the gray dawn hours when tasters moved like servants of servants, unseen, unremarkable. Someone had ordered the walls washed of their winter grime, and beneath the grime the old murals had emerged: hunting scenes, harvest scenes, a procession of kings marching eternally toward a horizon painted by someone who had never seen one. The colors were brighter than Jonah remembered. Brighter than they deserved.

Servants stepped aside for them. That was the second strangeness. For years Jonah had practiced a way of walking that made eyes slide off—shoulders loose, gaze down, footsteps timed to fall between other people's attention like a coin dropped between flagstones. They didn't bother with it now, and the absence of the habit felt like taking off boots after a long march. People *saw* them. A kitchen girl stared. An old chamberlain, half-blind, squinted and then whispered to his neighbor, and Jonah caught the shape of their own former title on his lips—*the taster, the taster who broke the winter*—as though they were a story being passed hand to hand instead of a person passing by.

Invisibility, Jonah thought, had been easier. Invisibility had been armor. But armor rusts, and there comes a day when you notice how heavy it's gotten, and how much of the world you never touched while wearing it.

The antechamber outside the royal dining hall smelled of beeswax and roasted pheasant and, beneath both, the faint mineral cold that still lingered in the palace stones—the ghost of the pact, seeping up from the foundations even now, a year after the winter broke. Workmen had been at the walls; the hairline frost-cracks that had webbed the masonry for a century were being filled with lime and plaster. Covering the scars. Of course they were. Kingdoms heal the way courts do: cosmetically, and only where visitors look.

Serah Dole was waiting by the doors.

Jonah stopped. Some part of them—the part honed in mirrored halls, the part that had learned to read a room the way a taster reads a broth—went taut and ready before the rest of them caught up. But Serah only inclined her head, stiffly, the salute of one soldier to another rather than an inquisitor to prey. She was thinner than she had been below the mountain. There was a scar along her jaw that hadn't been there before, and her left hand did not quite close all the way. Whatever had come through the false gate had taken its toll of her too, and she had crawled back up into the light carrying less than she'd descended with.

"Eriksen," she said. Then, catching herself, with something that might have been rueful humor: "Forgive me. Old habit. You're no one now, aren't you? Lucky you."

"Lucky," Jonah agreed, and let the word sit between them until it became what it actually was: a shared joke with a blade inside it.

"The queen asked me to attend today," Serah said. "She thought—you might find it reassuring. To have someone at the table who knows what you are." Her good hand flexed at her side. "I told her I wasn't sure anyone knows what you are. Including you."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in this building."

Something moved behind Serah's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "She once loved someone the crown erased," Jonah almost said. *Almost.* The whisper Serah had given them at the gate, holding back the dark with her body while they ran: *her name was Ilse, and there is no record of her, and I am the only monument she has.* Jonah kept it. Some gifts are not meant to be acknowledged in daylight.

"The doors," Serah said instead, and opened them.

* * *

The dining hall had been scaled down.

That was the third strangeness, and the one that made Jonah understand, finally, why they had been summoned. The long table where a hundred courtiers had once clinked goblets over the kingdom's frozen plenty was gone. In its place stood a small round table by the eastern windows, set for two, laid with a modest lunch: soup, bread, a roast bird, fruit, wine. Queen Isolde sat alone at it, without her crown, wearing a plain gray dress that Jonah had never seen on her and suspected few ever had.

"Sit," the queen said. "Please."

Jonah sat. Across the small table, the distance between them was four feet, and it had taken a broken winter, a stolen soul, and a dead dynasty of secrets to make it that short.

"You'll taste," Isolde said. It was not quite a command and not quite a request. It was the voice of a woman negotiating with a habit. "Everything, as before. And then we will talk, and afterward you will leave, and I will not summon you again. Those are the terms I've instructed my steward to offer. I'm told you respond well to terms."

"I've heard that about me." Jonah picked up the spoon.

The soup was white bean and smoked ham, the kind of honest peasant food that no one had served in this hall in living memory. Jonah tasted it the way they tasted everything: first the surface, then the depth, then the truth underneath. Salt, smoke, thyme, a suspicion of garlic scorched just slightly at the bottom of the pot—someone new in the kitchens, someone careful but unpracticed. And beneath all of it, clean as a bell note: nothing. No bitterness of arsenic, no almond ghost of cyanide, no numbing prickle on the gums that spoke of rarer things. Just soup. Just food, made by human hands, intended to feed.

It occurred to Jonah, with a strange floating sadness, that they could not remember the last time they had eaten anything simply because it was food. Every meal of their adult life had been a transaction, a shield raised, a death declined on someone else's behalf. Even now, tasting, they could feel the old machinery engage—the cataloguing, the weighing, the body reading its own responses like a clerk checking a ledger against fire.

But there was no danger to find. And so, for the first time in the history of their employment, Jonah finished the bowl.

Isolde watched them do it, and something flickered across her face that on a lesser woman would have been called envy.

"No poison," Jonah said, setting down the spoon.

"There hasn't been poison in this palace for a year." The queen said it flatly, but her hands, folded on the table, tightened on each other. "Do you know what that's like? For thirty years I ate nothing that someone hadn't tried to kill me with. Paranoia was the only honest advisor I had. And now—" She gestured at the untouched bird, the bright spring light lying across the cloth. "Now the cooks bring me dishes and I eat them like a merchant's wife and I lie awake wondering which is more dangerous: the world where everyone wants me dead, or the world where nobody does."

"Most people live in the second world, Majesty."

"Yes." Isolde looked out the window, at the gardens gone green and riotous, at the mountains beyond standing bare-headed for the first summer in a century. "And look what it got them. Famine. Flood. A hundred years of borrowed safety come due all at once." She turned back. "You renegotiated with the deep court. You gave away memories—freely, willingly, in trade. My grandfather gave away a prince and called it statecraft. Tell me, taster—which of us governed better?"

It was not a rhetorical question. That was the astonishing thing. Somewhere in the wreckage of the old guard, in the trials and the confessions and the long public unraveling of the pact, Queen Isolde had actually been *thinking*, and the thinking had led her here: to a small table, a plain dress, and a question she could not answer alone.

"You want me to say you," Jonah said. "Or him. You want a verdict."

"I want the truth. I'm told you deal in it."

"The truth is that he sold a soul to keep his throne warm, and you inherited the warmth and never asked where the fire came from. The truth is that my family sold the soul, and I inherited the debt and never knew I carried it. The truth is that hiding is the most expensive thing a kingdom can buy." Jonah leaned forward. "You're asking whether you'd have done differently. You wouldn't. Neither would I, at your age, with your teachers. That's not a verdict, Majesty. It's a diagnosis. And the cure isn't a better queen. It's fewer secrets."

Silence. Out in the garden, a gardener's shears snicked rhythmically, pruning the winter's dead wood off the roses.

"I'm abdicating," Isolde said.

Jonah kept their face still, but inside, something vast and quiet shifted, like a shelf of ice letting go of the shore. "Majesty—"

"Not today. Not publicly, not yet. There's a regency council to arrange, and Alaric refuses the throne—did you know that? Offered it to him myself, two months ago. He looked at me the way a man looks at the cell he was tortured in and said, *Madam, I have been a possession for a hundred years. I will not become a possession again, not even of a crown.*" For a moment, something raw moved under the queen's composure—grief, maybe, or relief, or both wearing the same coat. "So it will pass to the council, and the council will be answerable to parley records, open ones, the ones your cartographer publishes. Everyone will know what the winter cost. Everyone will watch the ledgers." She smiled, and it was a tired, genuine, unroyal smile. "You've made secrecy unfashionable, Eriksen. It's the cruelest thing anyone's ever done to this court."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you're leaving, and leaving people deserve to hear the ending of the story they started." Isolde rose, and Jonah rose with her, and for a moment the queen of a thawing kingdom and the taster who had broken her winter stood across a small table with nothing between them but daylight. "One more thing. The guild."

Jonah went very still.

"We dissolved it. You know that. Its masters are in custody, its roads are on Wren's maps, its ledgers are in the archive." Isolde's voice dropped, gentled. "But its ledgers say your parents didn't vanish. They were killed for trying to undo the sale—and the order came from within the guild, signed by a master whose name I've sealed in the evidence vault. You could open it. One word from you, and the trial reopens, and the name becomes public, and whatever family you have left gets its reckoning."

The hall was very quiet. Jonah could hear their own heart, steady, unhurried, the heart of a body that had survived everything it was ever asked to survive.

"No," Jonah said.

Isolde blinked. "No?"

"They're dust, Majesty. All of them. Opening the vault won't raise my parents; it'll just teach another generation of children that the past is a weapon you inherit." Jonah shook their head slowly. "I spent my whole life being useful to other people's purposes. The guild's. The crown's. The prophecy's. I'm done settling accounts for the dead. Let them sleep. I've got a roadhouse to build."

"A roadhouse." The queen repeated it the way she might repeat a word in a foreign language, testing its weight.

"At the foot of the Thornfells. Travelers trade stories for supper. Wren's drawing the map of the passes to hang behind the bar—all the smuggler roads, marked plain, free for anyone to use." Jonah smiled. "You should come sometime. Off season. Wear the gray dress."

For a heartbeat, Queen Isolde looked like a woman who had been invited somewhere ordinary, and did not know what to do with the wanting it woke in her. Then she straightened, and the crownless head lifted, and she was regal again—but a different kind of regal, Jonah thought. The kind that rules by consent rather than by cold.

"Thank you for your service," she said formally. And then, quietly, in the voice of a person and not a throne: "Thank you for the spring."

* * *

Jonah walked out through the palace the long way, because they wanted to.

Down the processional stair, past the brightened murals with their eternal processions of kings. Through the kitchens, where the new cook—young, freckled, careful—looked up from her pots and went pink when Jonah paused to tell her the soup needed nothing, not one thing, it was perfect as it was. Through the servants' corridors where they had spent years learning to be furniture, and out at last through the little postern gate near the stables, the one the tasters used, the one built for people the palace preferred not to see coming or going.

They stopped on the threshold.

Behind them: forty years of stone, of service, of swallowing dangers so that others could swallow dinner. The armor of usefulness, hanging on them still, heavier than they'd ever admitted. Jonah stood in the doorway and felt the full weight of it one last time—the poisoned childhood, the erased name, the parents who died for a debt they couldn't pay, the guild that owned them, the queen who summoned them, the prophecy that aimed them like an arrow at a door in the roots of a mountain. All of it. All of it behind them.

They did not look back.

It was harder than it sounded. The body wanted to turn; the body had spent a lifetime checking exits, counting threats, keeping the past in view because the past was where the knives came from. Jonah stood in the postern doorway with the spring air on their face and made themselves be still until the wanting passed, the way they had once made themselves be still while venom shut down their lungs one finger-width at a time. Discipline, but pointed the other direction. Toward life, instead of merely away from death.

Then they stepped through, and the door swung shut behind them with a sound like a book closing, and they were outside.

Outside. Where the road ran down toward the river, and the river ran silver with snowmelt, and the Thornfells stood up against the sky with their shoulders bare of ice for the first time in a hundred years. Outside, where a cart waited at the foot of the hill with a chestnut mare between the shafts and a cartographer sitting on the bench turning a piece of straw in their fingers, pretending they hadn't been waiting since dawn.

Wren saw them and stood up so fast the straw fell.

"Well?" they called. "Are you retired?"

Jonah came down the hill at a walk, then faster, then not walking at all, and when they reached the cart they grabbed the wheel and the bench and Wren's collar all at once and pulled them down into a kiss that tasted of straw and sunlight and the specific, unrepeatable flavor of a door closing forever behind you.

"Retired," Jonah said against their mouth. "As of noon. The position of royal food-taster is hereby vacant. Let them post it on a church door."

"Terrible succession prospects. Who'd take it?"

"Some poor fool who thinks immunity is a gift." Jonah climbed up onto the bench beside Wren and took the reins, and the mare tossed her head and started forward, and the palace shrank behind them the way palaces do when you finally stop feeding them your attention. "I told the queen to visit the roadhouse. Wearing the gray dress."

Wren laughed. "She'll come, you know. In ten years, maybe. When the council stops needing her. She'll come in disguise and you'll serve her stew and she'll pretend to be a widow from the coast, and we'll both pretend we don't recognize the most famous face in the kingdom."

"Obviously." Jonah glanced sideways at them—at the profile they had loved through poison and prophecy and the underside of the world, at the hands that held the reins loosely now because the road ahead was theirs, drawn by their own hand, known by heart. "What do we call it? The place. We never settled it."

"I've been thinking." Wren reached into their coat and produced a sheet of paper—a working draft, ink still sharp, the sketch of a low-roofed building at the foot of familiar mountains, and above it, in Wren's careful letterer's hand, a name. "Every traveler needs a reason to stop. Every road needs a landmark. And you always said the worst inn you ever stayed in was the one that existed on no map—so ours will exist on all of them."

Jonah took the paper. Read the name. Read it again.

Below the mountains, above the little drawn door of the roadhouse, Wren had written: **The Burning Thorn**—and beneath it, smaller, in letters meant to be argued over at supper tables for generations: *Prophecies fulfilled daily. Stories traded freely. Supper at sundown.*

"It's presumptuous," Jonah said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, and they didn't care.

"It's accurate," Wren said. "We earned it."

The mare clopped on. The road unwound ahead of them, downhill, toward the river, toward the valley, toward the plot of land at the mountain's foot where timber was already stacked and foundations already dug and a future waited to be built by two people who had finally, at terrible cost, been released from every purpose but their own.

Jonah shook the reins, and the mare picked up her pace, and the wind coming down off the bare brown shoulders of the Thornfells smelled—for the first time in a century, for the first time in anyone's living memory—like rain.
