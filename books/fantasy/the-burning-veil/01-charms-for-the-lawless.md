## 1. Charms for the Lawless

The dockworker's hands shook so badly he could barely hold the coin.

"Is it real?" Tam asked — third time he'd asked, and Freya had counted because counting kept her from sighing. "The luck. Is it *real* luck?"

"It's as real as you need it to be." She took the copper from his palm and pressed the charm into it in exchange: a knuckle of river-glass wrapped in red thread, sealed with a knot she'd tied at moonrise and murmured over until the thread hummed faintly against her fingers. Only she could feel the hum. That was the point. "You're working the *Marrow's Pride* run tomorrow?"

He nodded. The Marrow's Pride ran the deep channel past the old breakwater, where the water went strange and ships came back light on crew and heavy on silence. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it out loud, not since the Dragon Wars ended and saying things out loud became a way of getting yourself invited to a room with an Ashen Order interrogator and a single candle.

"Then you'll want this in your left pocket," Freya said. "Left. Not right. The current reads right-handed pockets like a ledger."

"That's not — that's not how pockets work."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He stared at her, then at the charm, then at her again, and whatever he saw in her face made him swallow and tuck it away left-handed, quick, like a man hiding contraband. Which, technically, he was. They both were. The whole stall was contraband: three feet of folding table under a striped awning at the edge of Saltrow Market, stocked with dried lavender, cheap candles, embroidered sachets, and — tucked between the lavender and the candles where no one honest would look twice — eleven charms of varying strength and one warding sigil stitched into a baby's blanket, which was the most illegal object in Vhalen smaller than a breadbox.

Ten years ago, magic had been a crime. Twenty years ago, it had been a war. Now it was mostly paperwork: fines, confiscations, the occasional public penance if you were stupid or poor enough to get caught. The Order called this mercy. Freya called it a leash long enough that you forgot you were wearing it, which was cleverer than a short leash and crueler too.

"You'll be here Thursday?" Tam asked, already backing away, already touching his pocket.

"I'm always here Thursday."

"And if the ship—"

"Then come tell me about it," Freya said, "and I'll charge you double for the next one."

He laughed — a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years — and went. Freya watched him go, small and hunched against the evening wind rolling off the harbor, and felt the familiar ache settle into her chest. It wasn't guilt exactly. Guilt implied she'd done something wrong. It was more like... loneliness wearing guilt's coat. Every charm she sold was a small act of faith in a city that had outlawed faith, and every customer walked away carrying a piece of her secret in their pocket, and none of them would ever know her name beyond *the lavender woman*, and that was the arrangement, and the arrangement worked, and she was fine.

She was fine. She'd been fine for ten years.

The bell above the market clock rang seven, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

*Seven.* She had a date at seven.

* *

Freya had agreed to the date three weeks ago, in a moment of weakness brought on by rain, a shared umbrella, and a man who'd said, "You look like someone who knows where the good noodles are," which was the best opening line anyone had used on her since before the wars ended. His name was Elias Thorn. He repaired clocks. He had a laugh that arrived half a second after his smile, like thunder deciding whether to commit, and when he'd held the umbrella he'd held it more over her than himself and gotten a soaked shoulder for his trouble and said nothing about it.

She'd spent those three weeks telling herself it was a bad idea. She was a criminal with a stall full of felonies and a second life beneath the city that no lover could ever be told about. She was twenty-nine years old and had not let anyone past the outer rooms of herself since her mother died. She was, by any reasonable audit, a terrible risk.

And then he'd smiled at her across the noodle stall in the night market, steam curling between them, and she'd thought: *one evening. One evening won't kill me.*

Famous last words. She just didn't know whose yet.

"There she is." Elias was leaning against the lamp-post at the mouth of Lantern Row, and the paper lanterns strung overhead caught him in slices of amber and rose, and Freya's traitor heart did something acrobatic and unlicensed. He wore a dark green coat with a mended elbow — visibly mended, unashamedly, with thread a slightly wrong shade of green, and somehow that undid her more than finery would have. A man who mended his own elbows. A man who *kept* things. "I was starting to think I'd invented you."

"You flatter yourself. I'm very inventable."

"Is that a yes or a no on having been invented?"

"It's a yes on noodles." She nodded down the row, where the night market unfolded in its nightly miracle: fifty stalls under a hundred lanterns, the canal running black and patient alongside, the smell of charred ginger and frying dough and brine and woodsmoke braided together into something that had no name but *home*. "The good ones are two streets in. The ones on this end are for tourists and people who hate themselves."

"Which are you tonight?"

"Ask me after the noodles."

They walked. The crowd carried them along in its slow warm eddy — fishwives closing up, children chasing each other between stalls with paper windmills, an old woman selling candied plums who winked at Freya like they shared a secret, which they did, though the old woman didn't know what it was. Elias bought two skewers of grilled eel without asking if she wanted one and handed her one without making it a moment, and Freya thought: *dangerous. This one is dangerous,* and ate the eel.

"So," he said. "Clocks. That's my whole personality, apparently. You should know that going in."

"I've accepted it. Tell me the worst one."

"The worst one." He considered, chewing. "A widow in Gilders' Row brought me a mantel clock last month. Stopped at the hour her husband died — forty years ago. She wanted it running again but she wanted it to *keep* stopping at that hour, once a year, on the anniversary. Just for a minute. Then start again."

Freya stopped walking. "What did you do?"

"Took the movement apart, put a little cam on the escapement. One minute of grief per year, precisely scheduled." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, looking at his shoes. "She cried. I cried a little, honestly. It was a whole thing. The clock community heard about it."

"The clock community."

"We're very tight-knit and extremely dramatic."

Freya laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised her on its way out, loud enough that a plum-seller glanced over. When was the last time she'd done that? She genuinely couldn't find it. There was a gap in her memory where laughter should have been logged, years of it missing, and standing here under the lanterns with grease on her fingers she felt the absence like a tooth.

They drifted onward. He told her about the clock community (four men and a woman named Petra who ran the guild out of spite). She told him about the stall, the safe parts: the lavender, the tourists, the regulars. She did not tell him about Tam and the Marrow's Pride. She did not tell him about the knot she'd tied at moonrise, or the words she'd murmured into it, or the fact that the thread had hummed because she had put something true inside it and true things sang.

"This is going to sound strange," Elias said, somewhere around the second street of noodles, "but have we met before? Before the umbrella?"

"No."

"Huh." He frowned at her, gentle and puzzled, like she was a mechanism that wasn't catching. "It's just — you feel familiar. Not your face. Something under it."

"Under it," Freya repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. She took a long drink of tea from a stall they were passing and used the motion to arrange her face into something amused. "That's either the worst line I've ever heard or the beginning of a very interesting problem."

"Both, probably." He grinned. Thunder arriving late. "I have a birthmark, you know. On my wrist. My grandmother said it meant I'd been marked by something old. She was very dramatic. The whole family was."

"Show me."

He turned his wrist over. There, below the heel of his hand: a birthmark shaped like a flame, or a leaf, or a keyhole — the kind of shape that depended on what you expected to see. Freya looked at it and felt, distinctly, unmistakably, the charm-thread in her own pocket begin to hum.

Not her pocket. Her *stall*. Eleven charms and a sigil, forty yards behind them, singing softly into the night like struck glass.

She hadn't warded them against resonance. Why would she? Nothing in Vhalen resonated. Resonance required magic meeting magic, and there was no magic left in the world except hers, hidden, hoarded, alone —

Except.

"Freya?" Elias's voice came from very far away and very close at once. "You've gone pale. Was it the eel? People warn me about the eel."

"It wasn't the eel." She smiled. It took everything she had. "I just remembered I left my till open."

At the far end of Lantern Row, past the plum-seller and the paper windmills, the lanterns guttered — all of them, all at once, a single indrawn breath of flame — and in that heartbeat of dimness Freya saw the canal water standing perfectly still, black glass, holding their reflection and something else besides: a shape beneath the surface, vast and coiled and patient, with eyes like banked coals.

Then the flames stood up straight again, the water moved, and the night market went on laughing and frying and haggling as if the world hadn't just shown its teeth.

"Freya."

She turned. Elias was watching her with an expression she couldn't read — concern, yes, but underneath it something older, something that fit his face strangely well, like a coat cut for someone else.

"You know," he said slowly, "my grandmother used to say that too. About the till. Right before things got strange."

"*Things got strange?*"

"She said the world talks to some people." He rubbed the birthmark absently, not looking at it. Looking at her. "I never believed her. But tonight—" He laughed, uncertain, and the sound arrived late even now. "Tonight I keep feeling like I'm walking through a story someone else already wrote."

Above them, the lanterns burned steady. Alongside them, the canal flowed black and innocent. And in Freya's chest, under the ribs, under the ten careful years, something that had been sleeping rolled over and opened one eye.

"One more street," she heard herself say. "The good noodles. Then I'll tell you a story too."

It was a lie. She had no story ready, no explanation, no safe version of herself to hand him. All she had was a humming stall behind her and a stillness in the water ahead, and a date she suddenly, desperately did not want to end — because some part of her, the part that had been alone for ten years, already knew that whatever came next, evenings like this one were about to become very rare.

Elias offered her his arm, courtly and ridiculous, and she took it.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the plum-seller closed her stall early and walked, quickly, toward the district where the Ashen Order kept its offices.
