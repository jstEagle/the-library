## 3. Second Date, First Omen

The second date was Freya's idea, which surprised me, because I'd spent the six days since the night market convinced I'd ruined everything.

I had talked too much. That was the diagnosis I kept arriving at, turning it over like a coin worn smooth. I'd told her about my mother's loom, about the year I'd spent apprenticed to a bookbinder in Corvess before the bindery burned, about the dream I still had of falling through ice into green water. Nobody needed all that on a first date. Nobody needed to hear a stranger describe his own dreams in that much detail, and certainly not while she was trying to eat fried eel on a stick.

But then, three mornings ago, a note had appeared under my door at the boarding house — actual paper, actual ink, in handwriting so precise it looked printed: *Embermarket stalls, sundown Thursday. Wear something you can run in.* No signature. Just a small charcoal sketch of a moth in the corner, wings folded, as if it were sleeping.

I wore the good coat anyway. The one you could run in, but good.

* * *

Embermarket earned its name honestly. It opened only after dark, strung along the old canal quarter where the lamplighters had given up on oil and switched to something cheaper and stranger — glass globes filled with what looked like trapped fireflies, or trapped embers, glowing amber-gold without heat. The light turned everyone's skin to bronze and made the canal water look like poured honey. Stalls crowded the cobbled banks in crooked rows, selling things that existed in the gray country between legal and merely tolerated: charms against damp, love-knots woven from canal reeds, tinctures labeled with Latin nobody could read, candied ginger, secondhand boots, songs.

Freya was already there when I arrived, standing at a stall that sold nothing but buttons.

"You came," she said, and there was something underneath the smile — relief, maybe, or its cousin.

"You threatened me with running."

"I incentivized you." She was out of her gatekeeper's grays tonight, dressed in a deep blue that made her eyes look like they'd been borrowed from a colder season. Her hair was down. I noticed that first, before anything else — that she'd let her hair down, which felt like being trusted with something fragile. "Buttons?" I asked, nodding at the stall.

"Distraction," she said. "You're early. I wasn't ready to be seen being pleased about it."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in a year."

"Don't get used to it." She bought two buttons — plain horn ones, nothing special — paid with coins counted out one at a time, and tucked them into her pocket like they mattered. Then she offered me her arm, formal as a duchess, and said, "Come on. I want to show you the part of Vhalen tourists don't survive."

"The part they don't *survive*?"

"The part they don't *find*," she amended, and pulled me into the crowd.

* * *

She knew Embermarket the way a fish knows water. Every third stall, someone greeted her — the eel-fryer who pressed an extra skewer into her hand and refused payment; a woman selling bottled fog who called her "little wren"; twin brothers who ran a dice game and groaned theatrically when she passed, as though her mere presence cost them money. She moved through their affection easily but never quite sank into it, the way a person stands in a warm river without letting the current take her weight.

And she asked questions. That was the thing about her. Not the polite volley of a first date — real questions, aimed, specific. What did the ice in my dream feel like. Why bookbinding. Whether I believed a burned thing remembered burning.

"It remembers being a book," I said. "If that counts."

"It counts," she said, and looked at me a moment too long, and I felt it like sunlight through glass.

We ate. We drank spiced wine from chipped clay cups. She beat the twins at dice in four throws flat, cheating so gracefully I almost missed it — her fingers doing something quick and small over the cup — and when I raised an eyebrow she said, "Luck is a skill," entirely deadpan, and I laughed hard enough that the wine went wrong places.

That was when I saw the birthmark thing happen.

A fortune-teller's stall stood at the market's far bend, draped in indigo cloth, its sign reading MADAME SERETH — FATES READ, DOUBTS SETTLED. As we passed, the fortune-teller — old, sharp-faced, wrapped in shawls despite the mild night — caught sight of me and stopped mid-sentence with another customer.

"Oh," the woman said softly. "Oh, the ash-leaf."

"What?"

"Your hand, dear." She pointed with a chin crusted in silver rings. "That mark. My grandmother called it the ash-leaf. Said folk born with it were born on a threshold. Doorways open for them whether they knock or no."

I looked down at my left palm, where the birthmark sat just below the thumb — a dark whorl, branching, that I'd had all my life and never thought twice about. It did look a little like a leaf. A leaf drawn in ash.

Beside me, Freya had gone very still.

Not dramatically. Not the way people flinch in stories. But I'd spent years handling books, feeling for the tremor in a page that meant the binding beneath was cracked, and I knew stillness when it was load-bearing. Her breath had changed. Her hand, resting near mine on the stall's edge, had curled slightly inward, fingers hiding.

"Superstition," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. That was how I knew it wasn't.

"All fortunes are," Madame Sereth agreed pleasantly, and turned back to her other customer.

We walked on. Freya didn't speak for half a canal-length, and when she did, it was to point out a juggler, brightly, a beat too soon, like a musician coming in early.

* * *

The phrase happened later, at a stall where an old man sold candles carved with prayers in languages neither of us read.

Freya held one up to the ember-light — a white taper wound with a thread of gold — and said, quietly, mostly to herself: *"What burns, keeps."*

The words went through me strangely. Like hearing my own name called in a crowd where no one knows me.

"My mother used to say that," I heard myself say. "Exactly that. When she banked the hearth at night. *What burns, keeps.* I haven't thought about it in years."

Freya set the candle down. Carefully. The way you put down something that might be listening.

"Common saying," she said. "Old hearth-craft. Lots of people's mothers said it."

"Did yours?"

A pause. One heartbeat, maybe two. "Mine died when I was small," she said, and it was true, and it was also a door closing. "Come on. There's a bridge I want to show you before the lamps cycle."

She took my hand this time instead of offering her arm, and her palm was cold, and I found myself cataloguing things I hadn't been cataloguing before. How she always stood facing entrances. How she'd checked the alley mouth behind us twice in the last hour without appearing to move her head. How the buttons she'd bought — plain horn buttons, nothing special — were gone from her pocket now, and I hadn't seen her use them.

I liked her. That was the trouble. I liked her enough that the cataloguing felt like betrayal, and I made myself stop, and we walked to the bridge, and the canal glittered below us like a spilled jewelry box, and for a few minutes it was easy again. She leaned on the rail. I leaned beside her. Our shoulders touched.

"Tell me something true," she said, watching the water. "You got a lot of my true last week. I owe you balance."

"Something true." I considered. "I almost didn't come tonight. Not because of you. Because I woke up sure something was going to go wrong. Some feeling in my chest like weather. And then your note was under my door and I decided feelings were bad data."

"They usually are," she murmured.

"And then I came anyway. Because—" I shrugged. "Because the alternative was lying in the dark cataloguing reasons, and I've done enough of that for one life."

She turned her head and looked at me, and in the ember-light her eyes weren't cold at all; they were the exact color of the moment a fire catches. "Elias Thorn," she said, "you are either the safest man in Vhalen or the most dangerous one, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Which would you prefer?"

"Yes," she said, and smiled, and I forgot, briefly, every strange thing the evening had done.

* * *

The lamps cycled at midnight. I learned this because the whole market dimmed at once — the ember-globes guttering low in some citywide rhythm, a held breath of darkness lasting perhaps ten heartbeats before they swelled bright again. In that dimness, Freya's hand found mine and gripped it.

Hard.

When the light returned, she wasn't looking at me. She was looking past me, down the canal bank, toward the mouth of Tanner's Row, and everything soft in her face had been replaced by something older and harder, a face from underneath the face.

Six figures were walking into Embermarket in formation. Gray cloaks, hoods back, faces bare by law — the Ashen Order didn't hide, hiding was for the guilty — moving in a spread line along the stalls with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they walked on. Their badges caught the ember-light: a closed eye worked in pewter. At their center walked a broad man with a ledger-chain across his chest and a face like a verdict.

"Sweep patrol," Freya said, barely a breath. "They do the markets sometimes. Random. Looking for unlicensed charm-work."

"Is there unlicensed charm-work here?"

"There is *nothing else* here," she said. "Half these stalls run on hedge-craft. They know it. Mostly they take bribes and move along." Her thumb was pressing circles into the back of my hand, fast, unconscious. "Mostly."

One of the gray cloaks peeled off the line and started toward the candle-seller. Another drifted toward the dice twins. The broad man in the center stood still as a mooring post, and his eyes were moving across the crowd, row by row, the way a man reads.

"Freya." I kept my voice low. "Do we have a problem? Do *you* have a problem?"

"No," she said. Too fast. "Yes. I—"

The broad man's gaze reached our stretch of railing.

It didn't linger. I watched it come toward us and slide past like water around a stone — but as it passed, something crossed his face, some flicker of recognition or recalculation, and he began, unhurriedly, to walk our way.

Freya moved.

There was no decision in it that I could see — no glance at me, no whispered plan. One moment we were leaning on the rail; the next she had my wrist and was drawing me off the promenade into the shadow between a fog-seller's stall and the canal wall, saying, low and rapid, "Trust me, don't ask, walk," and then we were walking, fast, through a gap in the stalls I would have sworn wasn't there, down three steps slick with river-damp, into a stone archway hung with drying eel-nets.

Behind us, I heard the broad man's voice, carrying pleasantly across the market: "Evening, all. Routine sweep. Nothing to fear from honest folk."

"Honest folk," Freya breathed, and pulled me left, into the dark.

* * *

The tunnels under Embermarket were older than the market, older maybe than the city above them. Brick gave way to rougher stone, stone to living rock furred with pale moss that glowed faintly, giving the passage a drowned-moonlight feel. Canal water ran somewhere below us, audible but unseen, a sound like slow breathing. The air smelled of wet stone and old smoke.

Freya moved through it like she'd been born there, one hand trailing the wall, counting turns under her breath. I followed, my heart doing work it had no business doing, my good coat gathering damp.

"How far does this go?" I asked, when I judged conversation safe.

"Miles. The undercity connects half of Vhalen. Old flood-channels, cellar roads, a temple district they paved over two hundred years ago and pretended didn't exist." She glanced back at me, and in the moss-light her face was half shadow, half silver. "Sorry. About back there. I should have explained before I dragged you."

"He recognized you?"

"He recognized *me*, yes. Cassian Vey. Warden of the Lower City. He—" Something complicated moved through her expression. "He's known me since I was nine years old. He won't arrest me. He'll ask me questions I don't want to answer, in front of witnesses, and one of those questions will be *who is the young man, Freya*. And I'm not ready for that question yet."

"Why not?"

She stopped walking.

The tunnel was narrow there, arched, the moss-light pooling around us like we stood inside a bell of glow. Water breathed below. She turned to face me fully, and I watched her weigh something — I could actually see it happening, options rising and sinking behind her eyes like bodies in a pond.

"Because the truth is strange," she said finally, "and once I give it to you, you can't give it back. And because I like you, Elias Thorn, more than is operationally sensible, and I am trying to decide how much of my life you can hold without it hurting you."

"That's a lot of words for *it's not you, it's me*."

That startled a laugh out of her — short, real, surprised out of hiding. "You're impossible."

"I'm a bookbinder. We're trained to sit with damaged things patiently until they tell us what's wrong."

The laugh faded into something quieter. She looked at my hand — the left one, hanging at my side — and then, slowly, deliberately, she took it and turned it palm-up, and looked at the ash-leaf mark below my thumb for a long time.

"Does it ever hurt?" she asked. "The mark. Ever ache? Burn? Feel like weather?"

My chest went tight. "How did you know about the weather feeling?"

She didn't answer. Her thumb brushed the whorl of the mark, feather-light, and the touch went through me like a struck chord — not pain, nothing like pain, but a resonance, a hum I felt in my teeth and behind my sternum, and for one absurd instant the moss around us seemed to brighten, every pale filament leaning toward my hand like grass toward sun.

Then it passed. Or I imagined it. The tunnel was only a tunnel, dim and dripping.

Freya had gone white.

"Freya—"

"We need to keep moving," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hand was shaking. "There's a stair ahead that comes up by the tannery. You should go home, Elias. Sleep. Forget the fortune-teller, forget the tunnels, forget—" She stopped herself. Started again, softer. "Forget me, if you're smart. People will tell you I'm smart. Be smart."

"And if I'm not?"

She looked up at me then, and whatever she was going to say — refusal, warning, apology — never arrived. Because from somewhere far behind us, deeper in the undercity than any patrol should be, came a sound.

A hum. Low, vast, patient, rising through the rock itself and up through the soles of my boots, and I knew — the way you know your own name — that it was answering the thing that had just passed between her thumb and my palm.

The moss blazed white.

Freya's face, lit stark in that impossible light, held an expression I would carry for a long time afterward: not fear exactly. Recognition. Grief. Hope. All three wearing the same face.

"That," she whispered, "is not supposed to be able to hear us from here."

The hum faded. The moss settled back to its drowned moonlight. Somewhere above us, muffled by centuries of stone, the midnight bells of Vhalen began to ring.

Freya didn't let go of my hand.
