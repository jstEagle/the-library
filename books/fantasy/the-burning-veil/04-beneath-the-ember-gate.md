## 4. Beneath the Ember Gate

The tunnels beneath Vhalen smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Freya had always found that comforting — right up until the night she brought a stranger down into them.

She had told herself a dozen lies on the walk from the night market. *I'm only showing him the cisterns. I'm only proving I'm not afraid of my own city's bones. I'll turn back at the third junction.* Each lie had lasted about as long as it took Elias Thorn to say something quietly funny in that low, unhurried voice of his, and then she'd find herself walking faster instead of slower, drawn downward like water finding a crack.

"You know," he said behind her, his boots careful on the slick steps, "most people end a second date with dessert."

"Most people don't live above a sealed door that hums when it's unhappy." She kept her tone light. It was easier to say terrible things lightly.

"That was a joke," he said. Then, after a pause: "That wasn't a joke, was it."

Freya stopped at the bottom of the stair and lit the lamp she'd carried from the gatehouse. The flame caught with a soft *whump*, and the light pushed back the dark just far enough to show him what she'd brought him to see: a corridor of fitted black stone, older than any street above, running toward an archway at its end where the air itself seemed to shimmer, like heat off summer cobblestones except there was no heat here at all. There was almost no warmth anywhere down here. That was the problem.

"Elias." She turned to face him properly, because this deserved her whole attention, not half of it aimed at the dark. "Before we go one step further, you should understand what you're agreeing to. Once you see what's past that arch, you can't unsee it. And once the Order learns you've seen it — if they learn it — there is no version of your life afterward that looks like the life you had yesterday. No quiet flat above the chandler's shop. No more dates at the night market. Nothing ordinary ever again."

He looked at her for a long moment. The lamp threw his shadow huge against the wall behind him, and his birthmark — that pale, veined crescent at his throat, the one that had made her stomach drop through the floorboards of the world two nights ago when she'd first seen it by candlelight — sat exactly where the Ledger's ink had placed it, a century before he was born.

"You read something," he said. Not a question. "In that book. About me."

"I read everything about you. Your name. Your face. Words you said to me on Thursday over fried dough, written down in a hand that's been dead for ninety years." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've spent ten days telling myself it was coincidence. I'm very good at believing in coincidence; it's practically my job. But I can't sit in the gatehouse another night pretending the book didn't also say what happens next, because what happens next involves *that*—" she nodded at the archway, "—and I will not decide your fate for you. Nobody decided mine. Well." A thin smile. "Somebody did. But they're dead now, so we won't invite them into the conversation."

Elias stepped closer. Down here, without the market's lantern-glow and the crowd's noise, she could see how tired he actually was — the sleeplessness around his eyes, the way he held himself like a man who'd been listening to his own house creak at night.

"Freya." He said her name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "Ten years ago I watched my mother burn our family records in the yard because a neighbor reported us for keeping a prayer-book. I was nine. I have spent my whole life being careful in ways I couldn't even name until tonight." He glanced past her, at the shimmering arch. "Whatever's down here, it's been humming under my feet my entire life, hasn't it? I think I'd rather finally see it than keep pretending the ground is just ground."

Something in her chest loosened and hurt at the same time. Trust, she thought, was a strange currency: worthless until spent, ruinous to spend, and impossible to take back.

"All right," she said softly. "Then stay close, and whatever happens — whatever you feel — don't run. Running wakes it up."

"What's *it*?"

She picked up the lamp and walked toward the arch. "The last dragon in the world."

* * *

The Ember Gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a wall — a seamless slab of grey stone set into the archway, twenty feet high and wide as a barn door, without handle or hinge or seam. Generations of Order masons had faced it with granite and carved wards across its surface in silvered lines, layer upon layer, so many that from a distance the whole thing looked like frost grown over glass.

But up close, in lamplight, you could see the truth the silverwork tried to hide.

The stone breathed.

Not moved, exactly — nothing so obvious. But the surface rose and fell by the width of a hair, slow as sleeping lungs, and where the wards crossed thickest, the granite had gone translucent as wax held near a flame. Behind it, deep down, something glowed. Not steadily. That was the thing Freya could never make herself stop watching: the light guttered. It flared and sank and flared again, like a candle in a drafty room, like a heart forgetting its own rhythm, and each time it sank, the silver ward-lines dimmed with it, and somewhere far above them the lamps of Vhalen flickered, and nobody up there ever knew why.

"Oh," Elias said. Just that. A small sound, knocked out of him.

"She's called Ysolde." Freya set the lamp on the ledge some forgotten keeper had cut into the rock, and pressed her palm flat against the cold stone. It was warm under her hand. Barely. Ten years ago it had been hot enough to sting. "Older than the city. Older than the Order. When the Dragon Wars ended, she was the last — wounded, they say, half-dead already — and the founders of the Ashen Order built this gate around her and told the world she was imprisoned. *Contained.* The great beast locked away so she could never burn us again." She traced one of the silver lines with her fingertip. "Every child in Vhalen learns that story. It's carved over the Order's front doors in letters you could read from a horse."

"But you don't believe it anymore."

"I stopped believing it my first week down here." She turned to look at him. The guttering light behind the stone played across his face, gold and shadow, gold and shadow. "You can't stand watch over something dying every night for ten years and keep calling it imprisonment. Prisoners rage at their walls. She hasn't raged in decades. She just... burns lower. And the wards drink what's left of her fire to keep the city's protections alive — the charms against wild-magic, the barriers under the riverbanks, the whole invisible net that keeps Vhalen safe. Every lamp that doesn't flicker wrong, every child who isn't born twisted by leaking magic — it all runs on her. We built a city on the back of a dying fire and called it peace."

Elias had gone very still. He was staring at the gate, but Freya had the sense he wasn't seeing the stone at all. His hand had drifted up, unconsciously, to rest against the birthmark at his throat.

"The book," he said quietly. "What does the book say I am?"

And there it was — the moment she'd been walking toward all night, all week, maybe all ten years of her service. Freya made herself breathe.

"It calls you the Veilborn. Born the hour the last dragon egg died, carrying the key in your blood. It says the gate opens for you the way a lock opens for its own key — willingly or otherwise. It says that when you come to the gate, the fire either dies forever..." She swallowed. "...or begins again. The old words are ambiguous. Prophecies usually are. That ambiguity has kept me sane for ten days and I'm about to stop leaning on it, so bear with me."

"Ambiguous," Elias repeated. He laughed — a short, unsteady sound with no humor in it. "I sell rope, Freya. My father sold rope. I have a birthmark and bad knees and I was going to ask if I could see you again on Sunday—"

"You can still ask." Her voice cracked on it, which infuriated her, so she pushed through. "That's why I brought you here instead of just deciding. Everyone else in my life has already decided what you are — the Ledger decided it a century ago, the Order will kill you for it the moment they confirm it, and the prophecy will use you however prophecies use people. The only person in this whole city who hasn't decided yet is you. So." She spread her hands. "Decide."

For a long moment the only sounds were the drip of water somewhere in the dark and the vast, slow, failing breath of the gate.

Then Elias stepped forward and laid his bare hand against the stone beside hers.

Freya felt it happen before she saw it. A tremor ran through the granite, fine as a plucked string. The guttering light behind the wards — which had been sinking, sinking, down to almost nothing — paused. Held. And then, slowly, like a drowning man turning toward a distant shore, it leaned toward the place where his fingers touched.

The silver ward-lines brightened. All of them, all at once, top to bottom, brighter than she had seen them in ten years of nights — and beneath her own palm the stone went from barely-warm to hot, hot enough that she snatched her hand back with a hiss.

Elias didn't pull away. He was looking at his own hand as though it belonged to someone else, and in the stone under his fingers, faint as veins under skin, threads of light were rising to meet his touch — gold threaded with a color she had no name for, a color that made her eyes water and her hidden magic rise screaming to the surface of her blood, wild and joyful and terrified all at once.

"Freya," he whispered. "It knows me."

"Yes," she said. Her heart was hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears. "It really does."

He took his hand back slowly, and the light sank again — but not all the way. Not to where it had been. Something had been given, or taken, or traded; she couldn't tell which, and that frightened her more than anything else that night.

They stood together in the lamplight, two people on the wrong side of an ordinary life, and Freya understood that she had just done the most reckless thing of her keeper's career: she had shown the prophecy its own key, and prayed the book was wrong about everything else.

"So," Elias said at last, hoarse. "Sunday, then?"

A laugh escaped her — cracked, disbelieving, real. "Sunday," she agreed. "We'll need a better plan than dinner."

"We'll need a miracle and a fast horse."

"We'll settle for a plan."

It was Elias who heard it first — his head coming up, sharp, like a deer scenting smoke. "Footsteps," he breathed. "Above. On the stairs."

Freya went cold from crown to heel. Only three people in all of Vhalen had keys to the under-tunnel, and she was standing next to one of them, and neither of them had come down these stairs alone.

She pinched out the lamp.

In the sudden dark, the Ember Gate glowed faintly between them — and far up the stairwell, torchlight bloomed against the wet stone, and a voice she knew better than her own conscience called down, calm and terrible:

"Keeper Marchetti. Bring the boy into the light."
