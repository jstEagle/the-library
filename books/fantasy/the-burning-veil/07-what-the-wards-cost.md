## 7. What the Wards Cost

The charm on my workbench cracked at dawn.

Not a gentle crack — not the little sigh of a luck-knot wearing thin, which I could mend with a needle and a muttered word. This was a *snap*, sharp as a knuckle popping, and the copper wire I'd coiled around the river-stone went dark from the inside out, like a vein filling with ash. I held it up to the grey light coming through the gatehouse window and felt the bottom drop out of my stomach.

Ward-charms didn't fail like that. Ward-charms faded. They thinned over months, fraying at the edges the way all magic did in Vhalen, starved slow by the Ember Gate's hunger until they were just pretty stones and good intentions. They did not *break*. Breaking meant something upstream had stopped feeding them.

Something upstream had stopped feeding them.

I stood very still in the cold of the gatehouse, listening to the silence beneath the floor — the deep, tidal hush of Ysolde's fire behind the stone, the sound I'd lived above for ten years. It was there. Still there. But thinner. The way a hearth sounds when the logs have burned down to coals and no one has fed them.

"Ten years," I said to the empty room, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "You've been dying for ten years and you couldn't have mentioned it sooner."

The silence didn't answer. It never did. That was the arrangement: I kept the gate's secrets, and the gate kept mine, and neither of us spoke of what it cost.

I wrapped the dead charm in cloth, buried it in the drawer where I kept the other failures — three this month now, four if you counted the weathervane blessing that had started pointing *down* — and pulled on my coat. The Lower City market opened at eight. If the wards were guttering, the first place it would show was the charm-sellers' row, and the first people it would hurt would be the ones who couldn't afford fresh charms anyway.

They never were.

* * *

Vhalen in autumn smelled of coal smoke and wet stone and frying onions, and if you walked fast enough with your collar up, you could pretend the smell underneath all of it wasn't fear. The Lower City wore its fear differently than the Heights. Up there, fear looked like marble and high walls and Order patrols moving in clean pairs. Down here it looked like chalk marks on doorposts — little protective sigils people paid street-witches to draw, illegal as anything, cheap as bread. Half of them were nonsense. The other half were mine.

I passed my own stall without stopping. Tam had it covered — a broad, gap-toothed boy who sold my charms with more enthusiasm than accuracy and believed, despite everything, that I was simply a very talented hedge-witch with an excellent supplier. Let him believe it. Belief was the only legal magic left in Vhalen.

The trouble showed itself two streets over, at the corner of Tanner's Row, where the city's oldest wardstone stood knee-high and moss-eaten, doing work nobody remembered commissioning it for. It was one of the anchor-points — a minor one, a stitch in the great hem of wards that kept wild-magic from pooling in the gutters of the world. Every city had them. Vhalen had hundreds, all of them drinking, all of them fed from the same source.

All of them fed from her.

I saw the crowd before I understood it. A knot of bodies around the wardstone, backs turned to me, and rising out of the middle of them a sound I knew from the Dragon Wars' last stories and had hoped never to hear in person: the low, glassy hum of unbound magic.

"Move," I said, and pushed through. "Order business. Move."

Nobody questioned the coat. That was the one mercy of the Ashen Order's grey livery — even a forged badge got you obedience, and I carried a real one, earned in ten years of quiet service nobody thanked me for. The crowd parted and I saw the child.

She couldn't have been more than six. She lay on the cobbles beside the wardstone with her mother kneeling over her, and her small right arm — the arm she'd presumably pressed against the stone, the way children touch everything — was *wrong*. Not bleeding. Not broken. Wrong the way frost is wrong on a window in summer: ferns of pale silver light crawling under her skin, branching from wrist to elbow, pulsing faintly in time with something deep underground. Wild-magic, leaking raw from a starving ward, writing itself into the nearest living thing.

She was screaming. Then, worse, she stopped screaming, and her eyes rolled back, and the silver crept past her elbow toward her shoulder, toward her heart.

"Back," I said. "Everyone back. Now."

They backed. Fear is obedient too.

I knelt, pulled off my gloves, and took the child's arm in both hands, and did the thing I had sworn on my mother's grave never to do in daylight, in public, in front of witnesses.

I healed her.

It isn't a dramatic thing, healing. The stories make it golden light and swelling music. In truth it is *subtraction*: you find the wild thread in the flesh and you draw it out, slowly, the way you draw a splinter, except the splinter fights and the flesh wants to keep it. You take the poison into yourself and hold it there, contained, burning, until you can bleed it off into something inert. My hands went silver to the wrist. My teeth locked together against the taste of it — wild-magic tastes like lightning chewed into tin, like the moment before a storm decides to be kind.

The ferns under the girl's skin dimmed. Faded. Vanished. She drew a breath like a diver surfacing and began, hiccupping, to cry, which was the most beautiful sound I'd heard all year.

Her mother grabbed me by the collar. "What did you — who *are* you —"

"A healer," I said. "Unlicensed. Which means none of this happened." I pressed a coin into her hand — too much, deliberately; guilt spends well. "Take her home. Keep her away from wardstones. And if anyone in grey asks, she fell."

"She fell," the woman repeated, dazed, clutching the coin and her daughter both.

"And madam?" I said, softer. "Get her a luck-charm from the stall on Miller's Walk. Tell them Freya sent you. Don't pay."

Then I stood, and turned, and found Warden Cassian Vey watching me from across the street.

* * *

He didn't approach. That was the worst part. He just stood there in his grey coat with his hands folded behind his back, the way he'd stood at a hundred inspections, and looked at me across four lanes of market traffic, and I could not read his face at all.

Ten years ago he'd pulled me, half-starved and veiled in my mother's ashes, out of a holding cell in the Spire, and told the tribunal I was too young to have inherited anything worth prosecuting. Ten years he'd been my commander, my sponsor, the closest thing to family the Order had ever let me keep. Ten years, and I had never once seen him look at me the way he was looking at me now — not with suspicion exactly. With *calculation*.

Then a cart rattled between us, and when it passed, he was gone.

I made myself walk, not run. Past the wardstone, which hummed faintly, wounded, its silver stitching coming loose. Past my stall, where Tam waved cheerfully and I raised a hand I was grateful had gloves back on. Down Miller's Walk, along the canal, through the fish-market with its reek and its shouting, until the crowds swallowed me and I could finally let my heart hammer the way it wanted to.

He'd seen. He must have seen. The silver light, the child, the healing — you didn't need Order training to know what that was. You needed eyes.

But if he'd known — if he was *sure* — why hadn't he crossed the street? Why hadn't he called the patrol stationed two blocks away? Cassian Vey did nothing without procedure. Procedure said: observe, report, detain. He had observed. He had not reported or detained, or I'd already be in irons.

Which meant he was deciding something. And Cassian deciding something about me was, in its way, more frightening than any cell.

I cut down the canal stairs and into the tunnel mouth the city pretended it didn't have, and walked the long dark way back toward the gatehouse, boots splashing, breath fogging, mind churning.

The system I served was starving the city. I had known it in the abstract for years — the Ledger's numbers, the ward-failures creeping up the charts, the way the anchor-stones drank deeper every season from a well that never refilled. But abstractions don't scream. Children do. And the child on Tanner's Row had screamed because the Ember Gate drank Ysolde's fire, and the fire fed the wards, and the wards were running dry, and nobody in the Spire — not one grey-coated guardian of the peace — had said aloud the simple arithmetic of it:

*The city's protection costs a life. Slowly. On purpose.*

And I had spent ten years keeping the ledger balanced. Checking the seals. Topping up the lies.

In the tunnel's deepest bend, where no light reached, I stopped and put my hand flat against the wet stone, and felt it — faint, far below, almost gone: the heartbeat of the last dragon in the world, stuttering like a candle in a doorway.

"I'm sorry," I whispered. "I didn't know how to help you either."

The stone gave back nothing but cold.

* * *

The gatehouse was exactly as I'd left it, which was somehow worse. Kettle cold. Ledger closed. Elias's ribbon — he'd tied it round the door-latch as a joke, a bit of blue silk from his sister's shop, *so you think of me* — fluttering cheerfully in the draft like nothing in the world was ending.

Elias. Of course my thoughts went to him; they went to him constantly now, treacherous and warm. The Veilborn. The name in the century-old ink. The man whose birthmark matched the prophecy's mark, whose careless phrases matched its verses, who had kissed me three nights ago under a paper lantern and called me *the best coincidence of my life* — not knowing how precisely he'd chosen the word.

If the wards were failing faster, then the Order's timeline was collapsing too. And everything the Ledger promised about the Veilborn — every terrible verse about keys and gates and the fire that wakes — would come due sooner rather than later. Cassian would want him. Ilse would want him. The prophecy wanted him, in the patient, hungry way prophecies want things.

And I was the only person alive who knew what he was, and the only person alive who loved him, and those two facts had been circling each other for days like dogs deciding whether to fight.

I lit the kettle. I opened the Ledger to the ward-logs, dipped my pen, and began the nightly ritual of recording the day's readings in my neat keeper's hand. Anchor-point, Tanner's Row: integrity failing, leakage event, civilian injury. Cause: sustained draw exceeding replenishment. Recommendation:

My pen hovered over the page.

Recommendation. What was the recommendation? *Increase rations to the anchor-stones* — impossible; there was nothing more to give. *Schedule a Renewal Rite* — the Solstice Rite, the grand ceremony the Order performed every year, the one the old texts hinted had once meant something very different than the pageant it had become. *Report to High Warden Ilse* — and watch the numbers vanish into the Spire's ledgers, filed and forgotten, the way they'd been filed and forgotten for a decade while the dragon starved and the city slept on top of a banked bomb.

I wrote: *Recommend full review of gate-draw schedule.* Which was true, and useless, and would buy me time.

Then, because I was alone and the kettle was singing and cowardice keeps poor company, I turned back one page in the Ledger. To the verse I'd read a hundred times since the night market. To the words in a dead scribe's hand that had ruined my sleep:

*When the Veilborn walks the market's glow,*
*and speaks the hour before he knows,*
*the keeper's heart shall weigh the cost:*
*the city saved, the fire lost.*
*One blood may feed what one flame fed —*
*and wake the sleeper, or leave her dead.*

One blood may feed what one flame fed.

I'd thought it was poetry. Standing there with my hand still faintly aching from the child's borrowed pain, watching the silver ghost of wild-light fade from under my own fingernails, I understood it was instructions.

A knock at the door.

Two knocks — measured, deliberate, spaced like a man who wanted to be recognized. I knew that knock the way I knew my own pulse. Ten years of inspections. Ten years of *Marchetti, open the logbook.*

Cassian Vey stood on my doorstep in the gathering dusk, alone, out of uniform, holding his grey coat folded over one arm like a man arriving at a funeral. His eyes went past me into the room — to the Ledger, to the kettle, to the blue silk ribbon on the latch — and when they came back to my face, he said quietly:

"We need to talk, Freya. About what you did today. And about what the Order intends to do at Solstice."

Behind him, the first stars were coming out over Vhalen, and somewhere far below us both, something ancient drew another ragged breath.
