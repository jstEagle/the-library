## 9. Running on Cobblestones

*Freya*

The bell above the charm-stall had barely stopped ringing when the first hunter turned the corner.

She knew the walk before she saw the face — that measured, unhurried stride the Ashen Order drilled into its wardens, the one that said *we have all night and you do not*. Three of them, grey-cloaked, moving through the night market's thinning crowd like knives through water. Their lanterns burned ash-white, stripped of every color a flame was supposed to have.

"Elias." She kept her voice light, a vendor's voice, bored even. "Time to go."

He looked up from the candied plum he'd been about to buy, and she watched him read her face in the space of a breath — watched him decide not to ask questions. That, more than anything, made her heart lurch. Most people asked. Most people hesitated until hesitation killed them.

"Left or right?" he said.

"Neither. Up."

The market stalls of Vhalen's Saltrow were built close and built crooked, awnings overlapping like scales. Freya caught the edge of a spice-seller's canopy and hauled herself up, boots finding the gap between awning-frame and wall that ten years of secret comings and goings had taught her better than any map. Below, the hunters reached the stall where they'd stood. One of them picked up Elias's abandoned plum and held it to his lantern-light, turning it slowly, as if it might confess.

Elias came up beside her with less grace and more stubbornness, palms scraping on wet canvas. "You've done this before," he whispered.

"I sell charms to people who need luck. I am intimately familiar with how badly it fails." She moved along the rooftop ridge, low, weight on the balls of her feet. "Stay behind me. Step where I step. The old tiles near the gutters are liars."

They ran. Not the desperate sprint of prey — she wouldn't give the Order that satisfaction — but a controlled, rolling flight across the shoulders of the city: over a dyer's roof where skeins of indigo cloth hung drying like captured sky, across the spine of a granary, down into the black seam between two townhouses where a drainpipe took her weight without complaint. Vhalen at night smelled of brine and coal smoke and the green rot of the canals, and beneath all of it, faint as a dying pulse, the ember-smell of the gate she'd left sealed and alone.

Behind them, a whistle cut the air. Then another, answering, from the east. They were being folded into a net.

"They're not shouting," Elias panted as they dropped onto a warehouse roof. "Shouldn't they be shouting? *Stop, in the name of—*"

"The Order doesn't shout. Shouting gives you time to think, and thinking gives you routes." She glanced back. Two lanterns now, ash-pale, drifting along the street below like will-o'-wisps that had learned discipline. "Cassian runs quiet hunts."

"You know the warden leading this one?"

"I know everyone worth knowing in the Order. It's the price of being worth catching." She didn't explain further. There wasn't time, and there wasn't enough of her courage left tonight for the whole truth. But as she pulled him toward the roof's far edge, she felt the strange, terrifying warmth of his hand trusting hers completely — no flinch, no calculation. Just grip.

*Don't*, she told herself. *You've known him three days. He's a line in a book you should have burned.*

But her hand did not let go.

The canal-tunnel mouth opened beneath an old tannery's loading dock, half-drowned, breathing cold air that tasted of stone and long water. Freya slid down the mooring post and landed ankle-deep in shadow. Above, the lanterns swept past without pausing. Hunters hated the tunnels; too many blind corners, too much old magic soaked into the brick from the years before the Wars, when the canals had carried blessing-barges instead of cargo.

"In here," she breathed. "Quickly."

He dropped beside her, and in the tunnel's absolute dark she heard rather than saw him turn toward her. "Freya. What did we walk into tonight?"

She pressed two fingers to the damp wall and murmured the opening syllable of a veilblood cantrip, the one her mother's people had used to light their way without fire. A thread of pale luminescence woke along the bricks, soft as moonrise, running ahead of them into the dark.

His breath caught. "That's—"

"Yes. It is. And if anyone else sees it, we're both ash by morning." She started walking, water sloshing at their shins. "So let's make sure no one does."

* * *

*Elias*

The glowing line led them through a world he hadn't known existed under the city he'd lived in all his life.

He catalogued things when he was afraid — his mother's habit, though she'd never known she'd given it to him. So he counted: forty-one arches, each carved with symbols worn nearly smooth. Sixteen side-passages, sealed with iron doors gone orange with rust. One moment, around the ninth arch, when Freya stopped so suddenly he walked into her back, and she stood listening to something he couldn't hear, hand flat against the stone, lips moving in what was either prayer or argument.

"What is it?" he'd asked.

"Old wards. Dead ones." Her jaw had tightened. "They used to sing down here. Now there's just... residue. Like hearing a song from the next room over and forgetting it halfway through."

Now they climbed out through a coal-chute into a cistern-house that smelled of clean rain, and Freya finally sat down on the wet brick rim and put her face in her hands.

Elias crouched in front of her. Up close, in the gray half-light seeping through the grating overhead, he could see how tired she was — not just tonight-tired but years-tired, the exhaustion of someone who had been holding a door shut for a very long time.

"Talk to me," he said. "Not everything. Just... the shape of it."

A laugh escaped her, small and cracked. "The shape of it. You say things like that. Like a person in a story."

"Why do you keep saying that? 'Like a person in a story.' On our first date, when I ordered the wine — you went white. Tonight, when I said—" he searched for it, "—when I said *the fire remembers what it fed on*. You grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise."

Her eyes came up. In the dimness they were the color of deep water over deep stone.

"Because I've read those words before," she said quietly. "In a book older than my grandmother. Written by someone who never met you." She told him then — haltingly, watching his face the whole way — about the Prophecy Ledger, about his name inked in a dead scribe's careful hand, about the Veilborn and the living key and the last dragon whose fire kept the wards alive. About the Ember Gate beneath the city, and her ten years keeping it.

When she finished, the silence had a texture, dense and humming.

"So," Elias said at last, and heard his own voice come out strangely steady, "either I'm the most important man in Vhalen, or you're the most elaborate pickpocket in its history."

That startled a real laugh out of her — bright, unguarded, gone almost as fast as it came. "Gods. You joke."

"It's a coping mechanism. I'm told it's charming." He sat down beside her on the cistern rim, shoulder to shoulder. The water below reflected the grate-light back up at them in trembling coins. "Freya. If any part of that prophecy is true — if I'm what it says I am — then whoever wrote it, a century ago, knew I'd exist. Knew what I'd say. Knew I'd meet *you*." He turned the thought over and found its sharp underside. "Which means none of this is chance. Which means maybe I don't get to choose anything."

"Or," Freya said, "it means someone wrote down a guess, and guesses only come true if people believe them." She was looking at the water, but her shoulder leaned into his, deliberate, warm. "I've spent ten years reading that Ledger, waiting for it to tell me what to do. You know what I've learned? Books are brave because they don't have to live with what happens after the last page. We do."

"And what happens after your last page?"

"I don't know yet." She turned her head, and her mouth was very close to his, and her voice dropped to something meant for a distance of inches. "That's the first time in ten years that sentence has felt like freedom instead of terror."

He kissed her, or she kissed him — afterward neither of them could honestly claim credit — and the cistern held them in its cool dark while somewhere above, the Order's lanterns swept the streets looking for two people who had, for one stolen minute, stopped running.

* * *

*Freya*

The respite lasted exactly as long as such things ever did.

The whistle came again — closer, wrong-directioned, from the north where no hunter should have been. Cassian had guessed the tunnels. Of course he had. He'd taught her half the routes himself, back when teaching her had felt like rescue instead of investment.

She broke away from Elias with a groan that was half laughter, half grief. "We have to move. Again."

"This city has a lot of running in it," he said, already on his feet.

"Vhalen was built by cowards and engineers. Same thing, mostly." She took his hand and pulled him toward the maintenance stair, mind racing faster than her feet. South, then. The Siltway, the flooded district where the Order's ash-lanterns guttered in the wet air and every warden assigned there volunteered out within a season. Maren's territory, though Freya wasn't ready to say that name aloud yet, even to him. Especially to him.

They ran again — down through the ribs of the old aqueduct, along a canal towpath silvered with mist, past barges asleep under their tarps. Once, crossing a bridge, they had to flatten themselves into the shadow of a shrine-niche while a patrol passed below, close enough that Freya could smell the ash-wardens' cloaks: cold cinders and sanctified oil. Elias's heartbeat hammered against her arm where they pressed together. Or maybe that was her own.

And once — this was the moment she would return to later, in the days when everything had burned down to embers and memory — they crossed a high footbridge over the Lantern Canal, and the whole waterway lay lit beneath them with the festival lamps the Lower City floated early, weeks before Solstice, because hope in Vhalen had always run ahead of permission.

Hundreds of little flames, gold and rose and green, riding the black water.

Elias stopped. She felt him stop, felt the pull of it, and stopped with him despite the whistles threading the air behind them.

"My mother used to bring me to see these," he said. "Before she died. She said the city lights the water so the dark knows it doesn't get to win."

Freya looked at him — at this impossible man transcribed a century ago, standing outlined against a river of defiant light with hunters closing in — and felt something in her chest crack open in a way that had nothing to do with prophecy and everything to do with choice.

"Elias," she said. "Whatever the Ledger says. Whatever the Order thinks you are. Whoever wrote you into their book—" She took his face in both hands. "This is the part they didn't write. This right here. Us, on a bridge, choosing each other with no idea how it ends."

"That's either very romantic," he said softly, "or a terrible tactical decision."

"Both. Everything worth anything is both." She kissed him once, quick and fierce, tasting of canal-mist and stolen plums. "Now run, Veilborn. The dark's catching up."

They ran — down off the bridge, into the Siltway's drowned alleys, her hand locked in his, the festival lights burning on the water behind them like a promise the city made to itself every year and mostly kept.

Behind them, the ash-lanterns multiplied. Ahead, the flooded district waited with its secrets.

And in the pocket of Elias's coat, against his heartbeat, the scrap of paper Maren's messenger had slipped him at the market — *if they hunt you, come to the Ash Archive; ask for the woman who remembers wrongly* — pressed itself against his skin like a second pulse, patient as ink.

He hadn't told her yet.

There hadn't been time. There was never time.

But as the whistles closed in and Freya pulled him toward the dark mouth of the Siltway, Elias Thorn understood, with the certainty of a man reading his own last page, that the next door they knocked on would open onto a truth neither of them was ready to carry —

and that the woman who remembered wrongly had been waiting for them both.
