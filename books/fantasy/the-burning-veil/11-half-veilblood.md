## 11. Half-Veilblood

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Maren finally set the ledger aside.

Freya hadn't moved in an hour. She sat on the low stool Maren had dragged from beneath a shelf of drowned books — the archive kept everything, even things ruined past reading, because ruin was itself a kind of testimony — and she watched the old scholar's face for the verdict before it came.

Maren's face gave nothing away. It was a face built for keeping secrets: deep lines like dry riverbeds, eyes the color of tea left too long, a mouth that had learned decades ago to hold its shape around dangerous words. She wore three shawls against the archive's damp, and her fingers, as they hovered over the open page, were stained black with ink so old it had become part of her skin.

"Well?" Freya said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Maren didn't answer immediately. She turned the page with a care that bordered on reverence, then another, then went back to the first one, as though the answer lived somewhere between them and she wanted to be sure of its address before she delivered it.

"You said your mother's name was Sera," Maren said at last. "Sera Marchetti."

"That's what Cassian told me. He was the one who—" Freya stopped. The words *saved me* caught in her throat like a fishbone. "He pulled me out of the fire. After."

"After the execution." Maren said it flatly, without cruelty, the way a physician names a wound. "Yes. You told me. And you said she was burned as a witch under the Third Statute. Unlicensed flame-working." The scholar's finger traced a line of text. "That is what the public record says. I wrote part of that record myself, child, before I understood what I was writing. And the public record is a lie stitched out of smaller lies, which is how the best lies are made."

Freya's hands had gone cold. She became aware of them distantly, as if they belonged to someone sitting behind her. "What does the real record say?"

Maren rose. It took her a moment — her knees crackled like green wood in a fire — and she shuffled down the aisle between the shelves, trailing one hand along the spines the way other people touch banisters. Freya heard her murmuring to herself, or perhaps to the books; Elias had mentioned, on the long walk here through the canal-tunnels, that Maren talked to her archive constantly, and that the archive, in its way, talked back.

Elias himself was somewhere deeper in the stacks, fetching water from the cistern. Freya could hear him faintly, the splash and clink of the tin dipper, and the ordinary sound of it steadied her more than any ward-sign ever had. Two days ago he'd been a stranger with kind eyes and a laugh she'd been foolish enough to want more of. Now he was the Veilborn, the living key, the hinge of a century-old prophecy, and every hunter in Vhalen wanted him opened like a letter.

And now, apparently, so was she.

Maren returned carrying a book that made Freya's breath catch.

It wasn't large. That was the strange thing — after everything, after the Prophecy Ledger's vast iron-bound weight, after the Order's gilded statutes and the towering spire where Cassian kept his office — the book in Maren's hands was small enough to hide in a coat pocket. Its cover was grey leather gone soft as cloth, cracked along the spine, unmarked by title or sigil. Only a single symbol was pressed into the leather near the bottom edge, so worn it was nearly smooth:

A veil, parted.

"This," Maren said, settling back onto her stool with a grunt, "is the Ritebook of the Veilbound. There were nine of them once. This is the only one that didn't burn." She smiled, thin and dry. "Because I stole it. In 1187, the night before the Archive Purge. I was younger than you are now, and faster, and considerably less honest."

"What's in it?"

"The truth about the women your Order calls witches." Maren opened it. The pages inside weren't paper — they were something finer, vellum-thin, and the writing on them wasn't ink at all but some silvered substance that shifted when the candlelight touched it, like frost forming and melting in the same instant. "The veilbloods. The priestesses of the Ember Gate. The ones who fed the fire."

Freya leaned forward despite herself. "Fed it?"

"Everything has an appetite, girl. Even holiness. Especially holiness." Maren turned the book so the light fell across a diagram — a human figure drawn in fine lines, and within the figure, a second set of lines, luminous, tracing channels from the heart outward through the arms, the throat, the eyes. "The dragon's fire keeps the wards alive. Everyone knows that much, even if they've dressed it up in statute and ceremony until the truth is unrecognizable. But fire is not self-sustaining. A flame needs feeding. For four hundred years, the veilblood priestesses fed it — willingly, deliberately, with their own blood. Their magic ran in their veins like yours runs in yours, except theirs was made for it. Shaped for it. They could pour themselves into the gate, or draw from it. Give, or take. The wards breathed because they breathed."

"And the Order—"

"The Order was founded by the priestesses' own students." Maren's voice didn't rise, but something in it sharpened, a blade being turned over. "When the Dragon Wars ended and the last egg died, the Order decided the feeding was barbaric. Old superstition. Worse — it was power, and power in the hands of women who answered to no warden was intolerable to men who wanted to answer to no one. So they called it witchcraft. They called the priestesses monsters. They burned the rites and wrote new ones, rites that needed no blood — only a sealed gate, a dying dragon, and a great deal of paperwork." She closed the Ritebook gently, almost tenderly. "They starved the fire and called it peace."

Freya thought of the guttering glow behind the Ember Gate's stone. Ten years she had stood watch over that slow dimming, reporting its decline in neat quarterly figures, believing — needing to believe — that the fading was natural, inevitable, a candle simply burning down. Not a murder conducted at the pace of geology.

"Ilse knows," she said quietly. "Doesn't she. High Warden Ilse knows exactly what the wards are doing."

"High Warden Ilse knows precisely what she is doing," Maren corrected. "There is a difference, and it matters. But we are not here to discuss Ilse. We are here to discuss you." The old woman's tea-colored eyes fixed on Freya with sudden, uncomfortable precision. "Show me your hand."

"My—"

"Your left hand. Palm up. Now, please. The candle won't last forever and neither will my patience."

Freya extended her hand. Maren took it in both of hers — her grip surprisingly strong, her skin cool and papery — and turned it toward the light. For a long moment she said nothing at all. Then she pressed her thumbnail hard into the center of Freya's palm, hard enough to hurt, and held it there.

"What are you—"

"Hush."

Under the thumbnail's pressure, in the exact center of Freya's palm, something answered.

It was faint — a shimmer beneath the skin, like moonlight moving under still water — but Freya felt it before she saw it: a warmth blooming outward from the point of contact, spreading up her wrist, into her forearm, pooling behind her sternum with a sensation she had no honest name for except *recognition*. As if some door inside her, locked for thirty years, had just heard a key turn in the lock from the other side.

Maren released her hand. Freya snatched it back and stared. In the center of her palm, already fading, a mark lingered: a small silvered whorl, like a fingerprint made of frost.

"Veilblood," Maren said. "Active lineage. Not dormant — active. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your mother wasn't merely descended from the priestesses. She was trained. Ordained, if the Order's word for it means anything. And she passed it to you whole." She tapped the Ritebook's cover. "This whorl appears in every initiate's palm on the day of their first feeding. I have seen it twice in my life. Once in this book's illustrations. Once tonight."

"No." Freya was shaking her head before she knew she was doing it. "No, you're wrong. I would know. I'd have *felt* something. I've had magic since I was a child — little things, charms, luck-work, the kind that gets dockworkers home safe — and none of it ever felt like *this*, like—"

"Like a river finding its bed." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Of course not. Because you've never been near the gate while it was open to you. Child, listen to me. What you call your magic — the charms, the cantrips, the small warm tricks — that isn't the veilblood. That's just leakage. Runoff. The way a wall weeps when the aquifer behind it is under pressure. Your mother's gift didn't skip you. It's been sitting in you all these years like a seed in frozen ground, waiting for two things: the gate, and the knowing."

Freya stood up. The stool scraped back and the sound was enormous in the hush of the archive. "You're saying I could feed the gate. Myself. My own—"

"Blood, breath, years — the Ritebook is specific and I'll spare you the specifics until you're ready for them. Yes. Feed it. Or drain it." Maren looked up at her, and for the first time since Freya had met her, the old scholar looked afraid. Not of Freya — or not only of Freya. Afraid the way a person is afraid holding something breakable and priceless in a room full of falling objects. "Do you understand yet? The Order spent four hundred years hunting the veilbloods because a veilblood is the one thing in this world that can open the Ember Gate without the Veilborn. Without Elias. Without prophecy, without rite, without permission. A trained priestess standing before that gate is more dangerous to the Order than every dragon that ever flew."

The cold came back into Freya's hands, and this time it climbed past her wrists, her elbows, settled into her chest like swallowed winter.

"They didn't burn my mother for witchcraft," she said slowly. Each word cost something. "They burned her because of what I am."

"They burned her because of what she refused to do," Maren said. "I've searched forty years for records of the last veilblood purges, and I found one entry, one, in a warden's private journal from 1193. A woman taken on the Salt Quay. She wouldn't give them the gate. She wouldn't give them *you*." The old woman's voice dropped to almost nothing. "She was pregnant when they took her, Freya. Or newly delivered. The journal doesn't say which. It only says the warden in charge reported the child unaccounted for, and recommended continued vigilance regarding 'the Marchetti line.'"

*Unaccounted for.* Freya pressed both palms flat against her thighs and felt, absurdly, the ghost of that silvered whorl in each one, twin small suns going out. Cassian. Cassian had carried her out of the smoke. Cassian had raised her, trained her, posted her at the very gate her mother died protecting — and either he had never known what she was, or he had always known, and had spent twenty years positioning her like a piece on a board whose game only he could see.

She thought of his face across the interrogation desk that was surely waiting for her somewhere in the future. She thought of the way he said her name — *Freya* — with that particular gravity, as if the name were heavier in his mouth than in anyone else's.

"She let them take her," Freya whispered. "To keep me from them."

"She let them take her so that someday, someone could finish what she started," Maren said. "Whether that someone is you — whether you choose it — that is the only question in this room that matters, and I am too old to answer it for you."

From deeper in the archive came the sound of returning footsteps, and Elias's voice calling softly, "Water's clean, mostly — Maren, did you find—" He appeared at the end of the aisle, tin cup in hand, hair damp from the cistern mist, and stopped dead when he saw their faces. "What happened?"

Freya opened her mouth. Nothing came.

It was Maren who answered, rising again with the Ritebook hugged to her chest like a sleeping child. "What happened," she said, "is that the Order has spent a century hunting a weapon, and the weapon has been hiding in plain sight, selling luck charms at a night-market stall and falling in love with their chosen key." She looked from Elias to Freya and back, and her dry smile returned, thin as paper. "Congratulations, children. You are, between you, the complete set."

Elias set the cup down very carefully on a shelf, as though the world had become fragile. "The complete set of *what*?"

"Of the lock and the key," Freya said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm and terrible. "Or maybe the key and the hand that turns it." She looked down at her left palm, empty now, ordinary, marked by nothing — and felt the whorl there anyway, patient as a coal under ash. "Elias. How long until the Solstice Rite?"

"Nine days," he said. "Why?"

"Because in nine days," Freya said, "either I feed the gate, or the Order feeds it *us*."

Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and earth and forty years of accumulated books, the bells of Vhalen began to ring the midnight hour — and beneath their bronze voices, so faint that only two people in the city could have heard it, something vast and ancient and starving turned over in its sleep, and listened.
