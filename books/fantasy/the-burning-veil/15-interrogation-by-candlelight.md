## 15. Interrogation by Candlelight

They took her boots.

That was the detail that undid her most, in the first hour. Not the manacles of cold iron laced with silver thread — she'd expected those, had dreamed of them often enough in the ten years since she'd sworn her oaths. Not the blindfold through the winding corridors of the Spire, or the way the guards' hands stayed on her shoulders even when there was nowhere left to fall. It was the boots, set neatly side by side outside the door of the interrogation room as if she might want them again on her way out, as if anyone who walked into this room ever walked out the same person who walked in.

The room itself was almost gentle. A table of dark ashwood, scarred by decades of nervous fingers. Two chairs. A single candle in a brass holder, its flame standing perfectly still in air that did not move. No windows. The walls were grey stone older than the Order, older than Vhalen's walls above, and somewhere beneath the floorboards Freya could feel it — a faint, arrhythmic pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a pillow.

The wards. Or what was left of them.

She sat with her cuffed hands flat on the table and counted the candle's shadows until the door opened behind her. She didn't turn. She knew his footsteps the way she knew the sound of rain on the gatehouse roof — Cassian Vey walked like a man apologizing to the floor for weighing on it.

"Freya."

"Warden." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You're late. I've been rehearsing my confession for an hour and I've gotten very good at it."

He came around the table slowly and sat across from her, and up close the candlelight was cruel to him. He had aged ten years in three weeks. The grey at his temples had gone to white in patches, and there was a tremor in his left hand that he hid by folding both hands together on the wood, knuckles tight as bridge cable.

"You always did joke," he said quietly.

"You always did let me. That was our arrangement. You pretended not to notice I was insolent; I pretended not to notice you noticed everything else."

Something moved behind his eyes — not quite a smile, not quite grief. He set a leather folder on the table but did not open it. "Do you know why there's only one candle?"

"Romance?"

"So the light can't lie." He turned the holder a quarter-turn, and the flame bent, throwing their joined shadows long against the far wall. "In a bright room, a man can hide his face in the glare. In the dark, he can hide everything. One flame shows me your eyes, Freya. And yours have always been terrible at lying."

"My mother's eyes," she said, before she could stop herself, and watched him flinch.

The silence that followed had weight and texture, like wet wool. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he used for report-writing, careful and bloodless. "Tell me about Elias Thorn."

"There's nothing to tell. One date. Two, if you count the one where your hunters chased us across half the Lower City, which felt less like a date and more like cardio."

"Freya—"

"He sells roasted chestnuts. He has a dog named Turnip. He laughs at my jokes, which should have been my first warning that something unnatural was happening." She leaned forward as far as the chain allowed. "Is this the part where you tell me he's dangerous? Because I've read the file you think I haven't read, Cassian. *Veilborn.* Living key. You people have been circling him for weeks like gulls around a fish cart, and now you've got me in a room with one candle and a folder full of my life, so let's skip to the part where you ask me what you actually want to know."

He opened the folder. Inside, she saw, were pages she recognized — her own handwriting, ward-checks copied from the gatehouse ledger, ten years of them. And beneath those, other documents. Older. Sealed with the black wax of the High Warden's office.

"Do you remember," Cassian said, "the winter you were nine? The fever that went through the foundling house?"

"I remember Sister Brann telling us it was bad air from the canals."

"It wasn't bad air." His thumb rested on one of the sealed pages without opening it. "It was a ward-failure in the Tanner's Quarter. Wild-magic bleed. Fourteen children sick, two dead. The Order covered it because admitting a failure meant admitting the wards *could* fail, and if the wards could fail—" He stopped.

"Then the whole city would start asking what they're actually for." Freya kept her voice level, though her heart had begun to hammer against the cage of her ribs. "I know, Cassian. I've known since I was old enough to read the maintenance schedules and do the arithmetic. The fire's been guttering for years. Every check takes more of my blood-sigils to hold the same line. Every year the Lower City bleeds a little more wild-magic into its gutters and its children." She tilted her head. "But here's my question, and I want you to look me in the eye when you answer, because the candle's right there and you said it doesn't lie."

He said nothing. The flame stood between them like a small accusing god.

"How long?" she asked. "How long have you known? Not suspected. Not filed reports nobody reads. *Known.*"

"Freya—"

"You saved me." Her voice cracked despite everything, and she hated it, hated the way the word *saved* came out of her like something torn. "I was six years old. My mother burned on the pyre in Wexley Square and you pulled me out of the crowd before the ash finished falling, and you carried me to the foundling house yourself, and every year after, on the anniversary, you sent someone to make sure I'd eaten. You taught me to hold a sigil-knife. You told me the Order was the wall between the city and the dark. So don't you dare give me the report voice. How long have you known the wall is made of paper?"

The candle flickered — not from any draft. From the pulse beneath the floorboards, the failing heart of the thing they all served. For a moment neither of them breathed.

"Twenty-two years," Cassian said.

The number landed in Freya's chest like a dropped stone.

"The Tanner's Quarter fever was the first," he went on, and now that the dam had cracked, the words came in a low, relentless flood, as though they had been backing up behind his teeth for two decades. "I was a junior warden then. I wrote it up honestly. My commander rewrote it, sealed it, and told me that truth was a resource, and resources were spent where they did the most good. I believed him. Gods help me, I believed him for another fifteen years." He unfolded his hands, and the tremor was worse now, naked in the candlelight. "Every year the dragon's fire dims. Every year Ilse's answer is the same: tighten the siphon protocols, extend the Rite, take more. And every year I sign the orders, because the alternative is telling a hundred thousand people that the thing keeping their roofs from catching fire is dying, and we have no plan except a boy who sells chestnuts."

"Elias."

"Elias." He said the name carefully, the way a man handles a blade by the flat. "The Veilborn. Born the night the last egg died, marked by a prophecy none of us wrote and none of us can unwrite. Ilse wants him at the Solstice Rite. She believes his blood will renew the wards — that the prophecy means him for sacrifice." His eyes lifted to hers, and in the single flame's light she saw the thing underneath twenty-two years of discipline: fear. Old, worn-smooth fear, like a stone carried in a pocket until it lost its edges. "And I no longer believe her. I haven't believed her for a long time. But belief isn't the same as proof, and proof isn't the same as a plan, and meanwhile the wards keep failing and children keep burning."

Freya sat back. The manacles clinked against the table edge, absurdly loud.

"You knew," she said slowly, "and you trained me anyway. You put me at the Gate. You gave the failing wall to the daughter of a woman the Order executed."

"I gave the Gate to the best keeper I ever trained." His jaw tightened. "And yes. To her daughter. Because I thought — I *hoped* — that if anyone would find another answer, it would be you. Your mother believed the Gate was never meant to be a prison. She died saying it. I let them burn her for it and I have spent every day since trying to earn the right to say it myself."

"She was right."

"I know."

"She was right and you *knew*—"

"I KNOW." It tore out of him, raw, and the candleflame leapt as though struck. For a moment he was not a warden at all, just an old tired man in a cold room with blood on hands too clean to see. Then he mastered himself, the way he always did, folding the storm away into the neat grey box of his office voice. "Knowing is not the same as being able to act. You of all people understand that. You stood at that Gate for ten years watching the fire die and signed your checks and said nothing."

"That's different."

"Is it?" He leaned forward. "Because from where I sit, keeper, we are the same crime committed twice. I lied with documents. You lied with diligence. We both told ourselves we were holding the line. The only difference is that you still have time to stop."

The pulse beneath the floor stuttered. Both of them felt it — a skipped beat, a held breath in the bones of the building. Freya closed her eyes and reached, instinctively, the way she had reached every morning for ten years, toward the ember behind the stone. It answered her, faintly, the way a dying hearth answers when you cup your hand near the coals. *Still here. Still burning. Barely.*

When she opened her eyes, Cassian was watching her with an expression she had never seen on his face before. Not suspicion. Recognition.

"You can feel it from here," he said softly. "Through the Spire's shielding. Through the iron in those cuffs."

"Yes."

"And Thorn — when he read the prophecy aloud, at the market. The resonance. Tell me exactly what you saw."

She hesitated. This was the hinge, and she knew it. Everything she said now would either arm him or arm Ilse. But the candle did not lie, and neither, tonight, would she.

"His veins glowed," she said. "Like veins of ore in stone. And the Gate hummed — not the alarm-tone, not the breach-tone. It hummed like..." She searched for it. "Like a lock recognizing its own key. Like a throat clearing before speech. Cassian, it wasn't hunger. Whatever Ilse thinks the Veilborn is for, the Gate didn't reach for him like food. It reached for him like *welcome*."

Cassian was silent for a long moment. Then he drew a key from inside his coat — not the cell key, not the cuff key, but a small black iron thing on a cord, worn smooth, and Freya's breath caught because she knew that key. Everyone raised in the Order knew it from the cautionary tales. The archivist's key. The key to the sealed levels, where the uncensored histories slept.

"Maren Ovesse is alive," he said. "Exiled eleven years ago for asking the same questions your mother asked. She has documents I have spent two decades pretending not to want. If you're right — if the Gate welcomes rather than hungers — then everything Ilse intends at the Solstice Rite is not renewal. It's execution. Of the boy. Of the dragon. Of whatever future was supposed to come after the veil thins." He set the key on the table between them, next to the candle, where the flame threw its shadow long and sharp as a blade. "I cannot open those doors. My name opens nothing anymore; Ilse watches me the way I once watched you. But yours..."

"Mine opens a cell," Freya said.

"Yours opens a choice." He rose, and for a moment his hand hovered near hers on the table — not touching, never quite touching, in all the years, because touch was a language they had agreed early and silently never to speak. "Twenty-two years, Freya. I am done spending truth where it does the most good. Take the key. Take the truth. And when you get to Maren — ask her what happened to the last keeper who tried to feed the Gate instead of bleeding it."

The floor pulsed again, longer this time, a shudder running up through the chair, the table, the candle — and the flame guttered down to a blue whisper before recovering. In that blue half-light, Cassian's face looked carved from the same grey stone as the walls.

"That's getting faster," Freya whispered.

"I know." He straightened his coat, and the warden settled back over him like a mask sliding home, but his eyes stayed hers. "You have until the third bell. After that, the guard rotation changes, and I can no longer promise which side of the door I'll be standing on."

He knocked twice on the inside of the door — the signal, she realized, that the interview was concluded and the prisoner was to be returned. As the bolts began to draw back, he paused, his back to her.

"For what it's worth," he said quietly, "your mother said something to me once, the night before the pyre. She said: *the wall was never the point, Cassian. The wall is just what we built while we were afraid to open the door.*" His shoulders rose and fell once. "I've been afraid for twenty-two years. Don't follow my example."

The door opened. Torchlight spilled in, harsh after the candle, and the guards' hands found her shoulders. But as they turned her toward the corridor, Freya looked back — one last look at the little table, the burnt-down candle drowning in its own wax, and the black iron key sitting in the puddle of light where he had left it, gleaming like a splinter of the night sky, waiting for her hands to be free enough to pick it up.

* * *

They marched her back down through the Spire's spine, past doors she had passed a hundred times in ceremony and never once in chains. The building was awake around her — wardens moving in pairs, runners on the stairs, the particular hush of an institution preparing for something large. Three days to the Solstice. She could smell it in the corridors, under the tallow and the cold stone: ozone and old smoke, the scent of a rite being staged.

They put her in a cell on the ninth level. Iron door, silver-threaded grate, a cot with a blanket rough as penance. Through the tiny vent slit, far below, the city glittered — the Lower City especially, its lanterns strung along the canal roads like a necklace someone had dropped and never picked up. Somewhere down there was a chestnut stall with its brazier cold. Somewhere down there, a child she had healed in secret was sleeping off wild-magic burns. Somewhere down there, Elias Thorn was either hiding well or hiding badly, and she could do precisely nothing about either.

She sat on the cot and pressed her palms together and did the only magic left to her: the small internal kind, the kind no cuff could find. She pictured the Gate. The ember behind the stone. *Hold on,* she told it, the way she had told it every night for ten years. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

The ember flickered. And then — for the first time in a decade — it answered back. Not with warmth. With urgency. A pressure against the inside of her mind like a palm shoved flat against a door, and threaded through it, thin and clear as wire, a sensation she had no word for until the word arrived on its own:

*Hurry.*

Freya opened her eyes. Across the cell, in the shadow beside the vent slit, something glinted. She crossed the room in three steps and found it wedged into the mortar where no search would plausibly have missed it and no honest searcher would have looked: a scrap of black cloth, and wrapped inside it, small and cold and impossibly heavy —

The key.

Taped to the cloth, in handwriting she knew better than her own, four words:

*Third bell. West stair.*

Below the stairs, the floor of the Spire pulsed again — longer, harder, close enough now to taste. Somewhere above her, bells were already deciding how much night remained.

Freya closed her fist around the key and began, silently, to count.
