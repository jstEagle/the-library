## 16. Maren's Price

The Ash Archive did not smell of ash at all.

That was the first lie Maren Oduya had learned to love about it. The Order's histories called the place a repository of the burned — the books confiscated after the Dragon Wars, the grimoires fed to pyres in Vhalen's great square, the names struck from every register. *Ash*, they said, because that was all that remained. But Maren had spent thirty years beneath the Spire's foundations, in this vault of salt-lamp light and breathing paper, and she knew better. Nothing down here had ever burned. The Order had not destroyed its forbidden knowledge; it had *buried* it, alive, and posted her above the grave like a mourner who was never allowed to weep aloud.

She ran her thumb along the spine of the ledger open before her — the uncensored one, the true one, inked by hands three generations dead — and listened to the sound of the two young people climbing down her ladder.

They came through the cistern passage the way she'd told Freya they would need to come, eventually, when the city turned on them. Maren had heard them before she saw them: the particular rhythm of frightened feet trying to be quiet, which is its own kind of loudness. Then Freya Marchetti dropped into the lamplight first, coat dusted white with tunnel chalk, one hand still raised to help Elias Thorn down after her. He landed badly, caught himself against a shelf, and flinched when the whole structure swayed and shed a fine rain of dust across an atlas of the Veil-lands worth more than the dock district he'd grown up in.

"Careful," Maren said, without looking up. "Some of these are older than your griefs."

Freya went very still, the way soldiers go still. "Maren."

"You brought him." She turned the page. The vellum whispered like something sleeping. "I wondered if you would. People in love hoard their treasures. They don't carry them into caves."

"We're not—" Elias began.

"You are," Maren and Freya said together, and then Freya looked away, and Elias looked at Freya looking away, and Maren allowed herself the smallest private satisfaction. Thirty years alone with dead men's handwriting made a woman greedy for any living comedy.

She let them stand there a moment longer. Let them take in the archive properly, because she wanted them to understand what was being asked of them tonight, and understanding began with seeing. Row upon row of shelving receding into dark beyond the reach of the salt-lamps, each shelf crowded with volumes wrapped in oilcloth, each oilcloth bundle labeled in the neat, merciless hand of the Order's cataloguers. *Confiscated. Confiscated. Confiscated.* And here and there, rarer than rubies, bundles marked instead with a single word in red: *Retained.*

Kept. Not destroyed. Kept, the way one keeps a knife.

"The history I showed you," Maren said, rising at last, joints announcing themselves the way they always did now, knees first, then hips, then the long complaint of her spine. "The gate. The siphons. What Ilse's predecessors actually built under your pretty city." She tapped the open ledger. "This is the only copy. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There is no other. Every duplicate was burned publicly so the burning could be witnessed, and the original was carried down here in a wet sack by a man named Corvus Aldane, who was executed the following spring for a crime invented for the occasion. I have read his execution order forty times. It is beautifully written. The Order has always been good at beautiful writing."

Elias had drifted toward the shelf nearest him. He touched the oilcloth the way people touch relics — two fingers, briefly, apologetically. "Why show us this? Why help us at all?"

And there it was. The question she had been waiting for, dreading, rehearsing answers to for eleven days since Freya Marchetti had first come down her ladder smelling of canal water and desperation.

Maren crossed to her worktable, where a kettle sat over a spirit flame, and poured three cups of the barley tea she drank because real tea required money and money required the Order's wages. She hated taking their coin. She took it anyway. Hypocrisy, she had found, was simply the interest paid on survival.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. This is not a short story, and my legs are done for the evening."

* * *

She told them about the year of the White Censors, when she was younger than Freya was now and already the Order's most promising archivist — a girl from the Salt Quarter with a scholarship earned by reciting the Reconciliation Catechism flawlessly before three wardens and a high judge. She told them how she had been given the archive as an honor, how she had wept with pride the first time the great iron door sealed behind her and she understood that she alone, of all living souls, would know everything.

"I thought they were giving me the truth," she said. "That's the joke of it. That's the whole joke, if you want it. They gave me the truth because truth kept underground is just another way of burying it, and they needed someone to keep the grave tidy. A censor who burns a book loses the book forever. A censor who *keeps* it — keeps it perfectly, indexed, preserved — can make sure no one else ever needs to burn anything again. The fire only has to happen once, you see, if the silence afterward is maintained well enough. I was not the archivist of the ashes. I was the archivist of the silence."

Freya's cup had gone untouched, cooling between her hands. "When did you figure it out?"

"When I was thirty-one. There was a warden — Cassian Vey's predecessor, a woman named Hetta Brandt — who came down asking questions about the gate's founding charters. Questions with edges on them. I answered honestly, because I was a fool and an honest servant, and six weeks later Warden Brandt was reassigned to a border post in the Grayreach and her house burned with all her papers inside. Accidentally." Maren smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "I recognized the handwriting on the fire report. It was mine. I had certified the inventory of her effects. So I sat down at this table, and I read everything. Everything I had spent nine years cataloguing without reading. It took me four months. I did not sleep more than two hours any night of those four months, and at the end of it I stood up from this chair and I walked to the ladder, and I climbed up, and I stood in front of the iron door with my hand on the release, and I—"

She stopped. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere far above, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, the Spire's bells rang the third hour, and the sound came down to them soft as snowfall.

"I didn't open it," she said. "Thirty years, and I have never once opened it. Because I told myself the same story every warden tells: that the wards hold, that the city is safe, that the truth could wait until there was someone strong enough to carry it. And then last month a child in the Lower City was burned by leaking wild-magic because a ward failed, and Freya Marchetti healed her in secret like a criminal in the city her own order built, and I sat here and realized that I had spent my entire life being the lock on a door while the house burned down around it."

Silence. Elias had gone pale in the salt-light; Freya was staring at her own hands as though they belonged to someone else.

"So yes," Maren said. "I helped you. I showed you the charters, the siphon diagrams, the correspondence between the first High Warden and the gate-masons. I identified your mother, Freya, God forgive me for how long I sat on that too. And now you've come back down my ladder, and I know why you've come, even if you haven't said it yet. You need the way out. The route through the foundation galleries to the Ember Gate that doesn't pass a single checkpoint. The path I promised you, the night you swore you'd never ask me for it."

Freya lifted her head. Her eyes were wet and furious and young, and Maren thought, not for the first time: *her mother had those exact eyes, according to the arrest record's physical description, and I have read that record so many times I have effectively met a dead woman.*

"There's a condition," Maren said.

* * *

She made them wait while she fetched it — the thing she had prepared weeks ago, in the small hours, telling herself she was being practical rather than morbid. From the drawer beneath her worktable she drew a folded square of oilcloth, and unwrapped it, and laid on the table between the teacups a slender glass vial half full of a thick, amber liquid, and beside it a scrap of parchment covered in her own cramped hand.

"What is that?" Elias asked.

"Phosphor oil. Archive-grade. One vial will take this entire room." She said it evenly, the way she had practiced saying it, and watched the words land on their faces like stones dropped into still water. "The parchment is a list. Shelf by shelf, case by case, in the order things should burn. The founding charters first. The siphon correspondence. The catechism manuscripts — the beautiful lies deserve to die with the ugly truths. Then everything else. All of it."

"No," Freya said immediately, standing, chair scraping. "No. Maren, this is the only copy of the truth in the world. We just spent two days learning why that matters. If the Order falls, if the veil thins, if anyone ever needs to know—"

"Then let them write it again," Maren said. "Let them write it *honestly*, this time, instead of inheriting mine. Don't you see? That's the whole disease. Everyone in this city lives inside a history someone else curated. The Order kept the truth safe the way a jailer keeps a prisoner safe — and look what that safety bought. A starving dragon. A failing veil. A child with wild-magic scars in the Lower City. Truth in a cage isn't truth. It's leverage. It's been leverage for a hundred years, and I have been its jailer, and I am *tired*, Freya. I am sixty-three years old and I am so tired of being the lock."

Her voice cracked on the last word. She had not intended that. She had rehearsed this speech forty times in the dark and it had never cracked, but rehearsal, she was learning late in life, was worthless against the actual presence of living faces.

She pressed on anyway.

"Here is my price. My only price. When you open the gate — *if* you open it, if Ysolde's fire takes your blood and the wards renew and the city survives what's coming — the archive burns. With me in it, if it comes to that. I don't require the second part. I'd prefer it, frankly; I've seen what the Order does to traitors it captures, and I've written enough inventories of their victims' effects to have opinions. But the fire is not negotiable. No one inherits this room. No High Warden, no reformed council, no well-meaning historian with good intentions and a grant of authority. The age of curated truth ends in smoke, and whatever comes after starts blank."

"That's madness," Elias said quietly. "You'd destroy the evidence of everything they did."

"I'd destroy the *weapon* of everything they did," Maren corrected. "Evidence is only evidence while someone holds it over others. Burn it, and the crimes become stories — stories the survivors tell each other, imperfectly, arguing about details, getting half of it wrong. That's what honest history looks like, boy. It looks like gossip among the grieving. It looks nothing like this." She gestured at the silent, perfect, suffocating rows. "Nothing like this ever again."

Freya hadn't sat back down. She stood with her fists at her sides, and Maren watched her wrestle it — watched the keeper's instinct war with the daughter's, the archivist's reverence war with the fugitive's pragmatism, all of it moving behind her face like weather.

"You're asking me to help you die," Freya said finally.

"I'm asking you to help me finish something. Those aren't the same thing, though I understand why they look alike from where you're standing." Maren slid the vial and the list across the table until they rested in front of Freya's cup. "You'll need the route regardless. Foundation Gallery Nine, past the old cistern, through the masons' breach — I've drawn it all on the back of the list, because I assume you'll lose the vial and keep the paper, and I find I don't mind which survives. Take it. Refuse the fire if your conscience demands it. But take the route, and take my answer with it: I will not be in this room when the Order comes to clean up after you. Whatever happens at that gate, this archive's fate gets decided in the next three days, by fire or by confiscation, and I would rather choose the fire myself."

For a long moment nobody moved. The salt-lamps hissed. Dust turned slowly in the light like the smallest possible snowfall.

Then Freya picked up the vial. Turned it once in her fingers, amber catching the light. And Maren saw her understand it — really understand, in the way of the young, all at once and forever — that complicity was not a thing you avoided but a thing you chose the shape of. That everyone who touched this history got stained by it. That the only question was what color the stain would be.

"If the gate opens," Freya said, "and we live — I'll come back for you first. Before the fire. Swear it goes easier if you swear you'll leave with us."

Maren laughed — a dry, startled sound, rusted from disuse. "Child, I have spent thirty years making promises to dead people. Let me make one to a living one for variety." She extended her hand, and Freya took it, and her grip was stronger than her face suggested. "If there is time, I'll climb your ladder. If there isn't, you'll pour that oil, strike the spark, and not look back. Both terms accepted?"

"Both terms accepted."

"Then get out of my archive," Maren said, gentler than the words. "You have a dragon to feed and a city to save, and I have a lifetime of tidying to undo."

* * *

She watched them climb. Freya went first, then Elias, pausing at the top rung to look back down at her — this stranger who had handed him his own history and a bottle of fire in the same hour. He opened his mouth, and Maren raised a hand before he could speak.

"Don't thank me. Thanking makes it finished, and nothing is finished." She hesitated, then added, because some debts deserved acknowledgment even unpaid: "Your birthmark. The left wrist. There's a sketch of it in Case Forty-One — a prophecy illustration, a hundred years old. I pulled it the night Freya first described you. I haven't slept properly since. Go."

He went. The hatch closed above her, and the archive settled back into its ancient hush, and Maren Oduya stood alone among thirty years of careful, terrible preservation and felt — for the first time since she was twenty-two years old — light.

She worked until dawn. She moved down her own list, shelf by shelf, unwrapping the oilcloths she had spent decades protecting, laying the founding charters open on the tables like patients prepared for surgery, stacking the catechisms in kindling-neat piles. If the fire was coming, it would find nothing hidden. Everything in the open. Everything in the light, for once, at the end.

Near morning, as she trimmed the salt-lamps, she heard it: footsteps overhead. Many of them. Measured, booted, unhurried — the cadence of people who owned the building they walked through, descending through the Spire's upper galleries toward the lower floors.

And threaded beneath the boots, faint but unmistakable, rising through thirty feet of stone: the sound of the archive's great iron door beginning, for the first time in her tenure, to unlock from the outside.

Maren set down the lamp-trimmer. She picked up the phosphor vial, and held it to the light, and began, calmly, to count the seconds she had left.
