## 17. The Breaking of the Spire

### Freya

They took her shoes.

That was the detail that undid her, more than the manacles or the windowless cell or the way the candle in the corridor guttered every time she breathed too deep — as if even flame feared what ran in her blood. They had taken her boots and left her barefoot on stone worn smooth by a century of other prisoners' pacing, and somehow that small indignity made everything else real. The Ashen Order did not torture. The Ashen Order *tidied*.

She had been counting hours by the candle. Four, maybe five, since Cassian's men had pulled her from the canal-tunnel and Elias's fingers had slipped from hers — his voice tearing down the dark after her, *Freya—*, and then a hood, and then nothing but the smell of cold iron and old incense.

Five hours. Enough time for them to move Elias to the spire's upper levels. Enough time for Ilse Vane to begin whatever gentle, terrible persuasion she was famous for.

Freya pressed her palm flat against the cell wall and felt it: the ward-lattice humming through the stone like a second heartbeat. She had spent ten years learning the grammar of wards — how they were built, how they fed, how they lied. And now, with her mother's blood finally awake in her veins, she could read this one the way she'd once read love letters: every line of it, every secret.

The lattice was starving. Of course it was. It drank from the Ember Gate same as everything in Vhalen, siphoned fire dressed up as holy maintenance, and it was drinking dregs. She could feel the thin places, hairline cracks where wild-magic seeped through like water through a failing dam. One of them ran directly beneath her cell floor.

She almost laughed. Almost.

Instead she sat down cross-legged on the smooth stone, closed her eyes, and did the thing she had spent thirty years refusing to do.

She listened to herself.

Her magic had always been there — a low tide under her ribs, something she'd dammed so long ago she'd forgotten the shape of the shore. Now, when she let the dam crack, it came up like a spring flood, silver-dark and tasting of night rain and her mother's name, which she had never known until Maren said it: *Sera. Your mother was Sera.* Veilblood. Priestess of the threshold. The kind of woman the Order burned and then built its whole religion on the ashes of.

The power rose, and Freya did not flinch from it.

*I am not sorry,* she told the ghost of every warden who had ever taught her to be afraid. *I am not ashamed. I am done.*

She laid her bare palm against the crack beneath the wall and pushed.

The first thing that happened was sound. A note — low, vast, below hearing and above it at once — rolled up out of the stone, and every candle in the corridor went out at the same instant, like a held breath. Then the ward-lattice, starved and brittle, tried to close around her power the way it had closed around a thousand mages before her.

It shattered.

Not broke — *shattered*, the way ice shatters, the way certainty shatters, a webwork of white fractures blooming through the air itself, and through those fractures Freya saw the true architecture of the spire for one blinding moment: threads of stolen dragonfire running through every wall, every blessing, every law, all of it draining downward into the dark, all of it borrowed from a dying creature who had never been asked.

The cell door didn't open. It simply ceased to matter. The iron went soft as wax where her shadow touched it, and Freya walked through into a corridor full of darkness and shouting.

She picked up a fallen guard's lantern with a hand that would not stop glowing, and she went to find Elias.

* * *

### Elias

The room they kept him in was beautiful, and that was the trap.

High in the spire, above the cloud-line of chimney smoke, with tall windows that showed all of Vhalen spread out like a jeweled map — the night market a scatter of gold in the Warrens, the canals ribbons of mercury, the great dome of the Wardhouse catching moonlight. Someone had left him bread and honey and a carafe of wine. Someone had left a fire burning. High Warden Ilse Vane sat across from him in a chair of pale wood, hands folded, gray eyes patient as winter, and she had spent two hours telling him, gently, reasonably, the story of a city that could only live if one more thing died for it.

"You've seen the wards fail," she said now, pouring him wine he hadn't touched. "You felt it in the Lower City, I'm told. A child hurt by leaking wild-magic. That is what the future looks like, Elias Thorn, if the gate stays sealed and the fire keeps dying. Not tyranny. *Leakage.* Chaos without even the dignity of intention."

"And your solution," Elias said, "is to finish draining the dragon at the Solstice Rite. Using me."

"Using the key the prophecy provided." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was worse. "The Order has never murdered anyone, whatever the archives in exile say. We have only ever *spent* people. Carefully. Gratefully. You would be remembered in every lit window in this city."

He thought of Freya's face in the tunnel-light, the first time she'd laughed at something he said — surprised by her own laughter, like a woman finding money in an old coat. He thought of her hand in his on the rooftops, both of them breathless above the sleeping city, and how for one whole hour he had believed the world might simply allow them that.

"I don't want to be remembered," he said. "I want to be *here*."

Ilse sighed, and for just a moment the kindness dropped and he saw the thing underneath: exhaustion, ancient and absolute, a woman who had carried a lie so long it had grown into her spine. "So does everyone," she said softly. "That is why we build cages."

Then the lights went out.

Every candle, every lamp, the fire itself — snuffed in a single instant, and through the tall windows Elias saw it happen across the whole spire, floor by floor going dark, and then the windows themselves *cracked*, frost-white lines racing through glass that had no reason to be cold, and the air filled with a sound like a struck bell heard from inside the bell.

Ilse stood. For the first time, she looked afraid.

"What," she said, "have they given her access to?"

Elias didn't know. But his body knew — the birthmark on his wrist flared warm, then hot, then bright as a coal, and the same low note that had killed the candles sang up through his bones, and somewhere far below, behind stone and seal and a century of lies, something enormous turned over in its sleep and *noticed him*.

The door burst inward off its hinges.

And there she was — barefoot, lanternless, lit instead from within, veins of silver light tracing her throat and wrists like rivers on a map of a country that had never existed until tonight. Her eyes found his across the ruined room, and they were wet and blazing and absolutely certain.

"Hi," Freya said. "Sorry I'm late. I had to break reality a little."

Elias crossed the room and held her, and she was shaking, and so was he, and the light coming off her skin was warm as a hearth and smelled — impossibly, wonderfully — of rain about to fall on dry ground.

"The stairs are guarded," he managed.

"They were." She glanced back down the shattered corridor, where three armored shapes lay sleeping, unharmed, their weapons rusted to blossoms of red iron-oxide flowers. She winced. "I'm still figuring out the... gentleness settings."

* * *

### Freya

Running through a collapsing spire with your whole body turned into an open wound of magic was, Freya discovered, a very particular experience.

Every ward they passed tried to grip her and came apart instead. Doors unlocked themselves in reverence or terror; she couldn't tell which. The stairwell lights reignited as she descended and blew out behind her, so that they moved inside a moving bubble of gold, two small figures carrying dawn down through a tower built to keep it out.

And the tower fought back.

On the twelfth landing the ceiling came alive — ward-script peeling off the stones in ribbons of burning white, forming shapes, forming *hands*, the lattice's last desperate immune response. Elias shouted her name. She raised her hand without thinking, without technique, without any of the discipline the Order had refused to teach her, and the hands caught fire with silver and unraveled into moths — actual moths, dozens of them, spiraling up into the dark, wings dusted with light.

She stared at her own fingers. "I did not know I could do that."

"Please don't do anything else you don't know you can do until we're outside," Elias said, gripping her hand, and his palm was warm where the birthmark glowed, and together — that was the strange part, the part that made her chest ache — together the descent felt less like fleeing and more like being *pulled*, the gate below calling its key and its keeper home through forty stories of stone.

They burst out onto the processional terrace at the spire's base, and the night received them like a verdict.

Below the terrace, the city: Vhalen in full alarm, bells clanging in the Wardhouse, Order patrols streaming toward the tower like iron filings toward a magnet. Above, the spire itself was *dying* — and she could see it plainly now, could see what her eruption had shown her only in flashes. Without its inner lattice, the building was just a shell around a machine, and the machine was exposed: the great siphon-works at its heart, the channels that had drunk dragonfire for a hundred years, all of it stuttering, sparking, weeping threads of fading ember-light into the air like a wound weeping heat.

Behind her, boots on marble. Ahead, the drop to the aqueduct and the tunnels beyond. Between, a courtyard full of wardens.

"Elias." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The archive tunnels. Maren's route. Can you remember it?"

"I remember everything about you," he said, absurdly, terribly sincere, "including the map you drew on my arm in charcoal. Yes. I can remember it."

"Then run when I say run."

She turned to face the wardens — twenty of them, maybe more, pikes leveled, and at their head a figure in gray whose face she knew better than her own reflection some days. Cassian Vey stood at the courtyard's edge, sword drawn but lowered, and his expression was the most terrible thing she had seen all night, because it was hope breaking apart in real time.

"Freya." His voice carried. "Stand down. Whatever you think you've learned—"

"I learned it from *you*," she called back, and the silver in her veins flared with every word. "Twenty years ago, in the ash-field, when you carried a half-dead child out of the smoke and told her the Order would keep her safe. You knew, Cassian. You've known for years the wards were failing. You knew what the gate really does. And you stayed anyway, because staying was easier than grieving."

His jaw tightened. Around him, the wardens shifted, uncertain — because everyone in the Order knew Warden Vey had saved a child from the purges, and none of them knew what that child had cost him since.

"So here is my bargain," Freya said, and lifted both hands, and let herself burn. Light poured off her like heat-shimmer, like the moment before a storm breaks, and the courtyard's ward-stones — every one of them fed from the spire's dying lattice — flickered, dimmed, *bowed*. "Step aside. No one has to be unmade tonight. I'm done spending people, carefully or otherwise."

For one long breath, Cassian Vey looked at her down the length of his own lowered sword.

Then, quietly, so only the front rank could hear — though her new senses caught every word — he said: "Wardens. The prisoner has breached the lower wards. Formation is compromised. Fall back to the Wardhouse perimeter and await orders."

"Sir—" a lieutenant began.

"*Await orders,*" Cassian said, and did not look at Freya, and did not need to. In the space between one heartbeat and the next, the courtyard emptied of everything except one old man standing very still among the ward-stones, and the ghost of a salute he did not give.

Freya's legs nearly gave out. Elias caught her elbow.

"That wasn't mercy," she whispered, as they ran for the aqueduct wall. "That was a man buying himself time to decide. Don't thank him yet."

"Wasn't going to," Elias said. "I was going to thank *you*. What you did back there — the light, the—" he gestured helplessly at the moths still spiraling from a broken window far above, "—that was you choosing. All of it. I saw you choose."

She wanted to tell him it hadn't felt like choosing. It had felt like a dam breaking, like thirty years of swallowed power demanding interest all at once, like innocence leaving her body through the skin and not planning to come back. She wanted to tell him that some part of her had died in that cell — the part that believed she could spend her whole life small and safe and half-herself.

What she said, hauling herself over the aqueduct wall with his hand braced under her arm, the city roaring faintly behind them and the Ember Gate waiting somewhere ahead beneath the earth like a held breath:

"When we get to the gate — whatever happens down there — don't let go of my hand. The prophecy needs a key. It doesn't get to take the rest of me."

Elias laced his fingers through hers, the birthmark against her silver-lit pulse, two forbidden things fitting together like they'd been designed to.

"Not letting go," he said. "Now run."

And they ran — down through the sleeping arteries of Vhalen, past canals gone mercury-still, down and down toward the oldest dark in the city, where behind a door no book had ever described correctly, something ancient and nearly extinguished waited to learn whether it had been freed or merely found.
