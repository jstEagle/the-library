## 18. Dragonfire, Dying

The last stair down to the Ember Gate was not a stair at all anymore.

It had been, once — ten years ago, when I first descended as a girl of seventeen with Cassian's hand on my shoulder and the Order's litany in my mouth: *the gate is a wound; we are the bandage; we are the mercy.* Back then it had been granite steps worn smooth by generations of keepers' feet. Now the stone had gone soft as tallow, sagging under its own weight, and each step gave slightly beneath my boots like walking on something that had once been alive and was trying very hard to remember how.

Elias felt it too. I heard his breath catch behind me, felt his hand find the small of my back — not pushing, not pulling, just *there*, the way he'd been there through the rooftops and the canal-tunnels and Maren's archive of forbidden ash-paper, through three days of running that had stripped us both down to something rawer than fear.

"It's warm," he said. "The air. Why is it warm?"

"Because something down here is still burning," I said. "Barely."

The tunnel opened.

I had stood at this threshold a thousand times and never once described it aloud, because there was no one to describe it to, and because words felt like a kind of theft. The Ember Gate filled the cavern's far wall like the face of a cliff that had decided, long ago, to become a door: forty feet of black iron veined with channels of gold that glowed and dimmed and glowed again, slow as breathing, slower now than any breath should be. The channels were the wards — the great arterial script that fed Vhalen's protective lattice from the fire on the other side. And the fire was failing. I could see it in the way the gold pulsed: once every seven heartbeats where it had been once every three when I took my oath. Once every nine, some nights. There had been a night in early spring when I'd counted eleven and sat down on the cold floor and put my head in my hands and let myself, for exactly one minute, grieve.

Behind me, Elias made a sound low in his throat. Not words. Recognition, maybe. Or dread wearing recognition's clothes.

"You've seen it before," I said. "In the Ledger. The illustration."

"I saw ink," he said. "This is—" He stopped. Started again. "Freya, it's *humming*. I can feel it in my teeth."

I could feel it too, and always had, but for me it had been background, the way a city-dweller stops hearing traffic. For him it was new, and watching him hear it — watching him tilt his head like a man listening for a voice inside a shell — I understood that the hum had been waiting for him. Ten years of keepers had stood on this floor and heard only machinery. He had been down here thirty seconds and already the iron knew his name.

*Veilborn.* Born the night the last egg died. A living key cut to fit a lock no one was supposed to open.

"We rest here," I said, because my legs wanted an excuse, and because part of me — the cowardly, human, ten-years-lonely part — wanted another hour of not knowing what waited on the other side of that door. "Maren said the resonance needs darkness to build. We shouldn't touch the gate until moonset."

Elias lowered himself onto a fallen slab near the wall, and I sat beside him, and for a while neither of us spoke. The gold veins breathed their slow dying light across his face. He had a bruise blooming along his jaw from a hunter's elbow on the rooftops two nights past, and soot still in the creases of his knuckles, and he was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I hated that I thought that, because beautiful things in prophecies are bait.

"Tell me what happens," he said quietly, "if we do nothing. If we walk away tonight and let the Order have its Solstice Rite."

"The Rite drains the last of Ysolde's fire into the wards all at once. It would hold the city for maybe twenty years. Thirty if they're careful." I turned a charm between my fingers — habit, the little luck-knots I sold dockworkers, though no luck I'd ever tied had survived this month. "And then the wards fail everywhere at once, and everything beyond the veil comes through into a city that has forgotten magic exists, because we outlawed it, because we executed everyone who remembered how to fight it. My mother among them."

"And if we open it?"

"Maren's histories say the gate was built to starve the dragon, not cage her. That the founders siphoned her fire to power the wards and called it protection." I looked at the great dark door. "But the Ledger says something else. The Ledger says the Veilborn opens the gate and the dragon rises and the war begins again. Both things can be true, Elias. That's what's been sitting in my chest since the night I read your name in a hundred-year-old book. Maybe the prophecy isn't a warning. Maybe it's a *recipe*. Maybe whoever wrote it wanted the war back."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "So we're standing in front of a locked door, and every book disagrees about what's behind it."

"No," I said. "Every book agrees about what's behind it. They disagree about whether she's a prisoner or a well or a weapon." I swallowed. "There's only one way to ask her."

* * *

Moonset came the way it does underground: not at all, only inferred, a thinning in the pressure of the dark that Maren had taught us to read. When I judged the hour, I rose, and Elias rose with me, and we crossed the floor to the gate together, our footsteps loud as accusations.

Up close, the iron was worse. The black metal had gone brittle at the edges, flaking into scales that crumbled when I brushed them, and beneath the flakes the metal gleamed wetly, like skin under skin. The golden channels ran within a handspan of our faces, and I pressed my palm flat against the cool iron between two of them and felt the pulse arrive late and weak, a tide going out.

"Now what?" Elias whispered.

"Now you do what you did in the archive without meaning to. You read."

His birthmark — the flame-shaped mark below his ear that had started this whole unraveling — caught the wardlight. He lifted his hand, hesitated, and laid it against the iron beside mine.

The gate woke.

Not all at once. First a shiver, deep in the metal, like a horse feeling a fly. Then the nearest gold channel brightened — actually brightened, flushed from ember-dim to true gold — and the hum climbed out of the floor and into the air and became, unmistakably, a voice. Not words. Not yet. A voice clearing itself after a century of disuse, and the sound of it went through me like cold water, because I had guarded this gate for ten years and it had never once spoken to *me*.

"Elias," I breathed. "Step back—"

"No." His hand stayed. His eyes had gone wide and wet in the gold light. "She's *tired*, Freya. Can't you hear it? She's so tired."

And then the iron moved.

It did not swing open. It thinned. The whole forty-foot face of the gate went translucent as smoked glass, and through it — vast, coiled, dim — I saw her.

Ysolde.

I had imagined dragons my whole life the way everyone in Vhalen imagined them: from war-murals and Order engravings, wings like storm-fronts, jaws full of cathedral bells, fire enough to unmake a skyline. What lay beyond the thinning gate was none of that, and more than that. She was enormous — her body filled the chamber beyond the way a mountain fills a valley — but she was *dimmed*, her scales the grey of banked coals, her wings folded tight against a body that had grown gaunt under them, the great bones showing like shipwreck timber. She lay with her head resting on her own foreleg, and her eyes —

Her eyes were open. Ancient, amber, ringed with the fine cracks of extreme age, and utterly, unbearably lucid. They found Elias first. Then me. And when she looked at me I felt it physically, a pressure like deep water, like being read by something that had watched empires be born and misplace them.

"*Keepers,*" she said. Her voice did not come through the air so much as through the stone, through my bones, through the ten years of my vigil. It was a woman's voice and a landslide's voice and underneath both, faintly, the crackle of a fire in its last hour. "*Two of you. After so long, they send two. One who feeds the lock, and one who is the key.*"

My mouth had gone dry as the archive ash. "You know why we've come."

"*I have known why you would come since before your mother's mother drew breath.*" One immense eye narrowed, and the amber in it guttered like a lamp in wind. "*The book. You brought the book's question. Ask it, then, keeper's daughter. Ask me if I am rage.*"

I hadn't said anything about the prophecy. I didn't need to. I stepped closer to the thinning iron, close enough that my breath fogged on it, and asked the question that had been eating me since the Ledger:

"If we open this gate — if Elias turns the key — will you burn Vhalen? Will the war begin again?"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for me to count nine pulses of the failing wards. Somewhere above us, faint as memory, the city slept on, two thousand feet of stone and ten thousand lies between it and this room.

"*Child,*" Ysolde said at last, and something in the word cracked, "*look at me. Do I look capable of burning anything?*"

And she didn't. That was the terrible truth of it. She was dying — not wounded, not sleeping, *dying*, the way a fire dies when it has been fed smoke instead of wood for a hundred years. The founders hadn't caged her fury. They had installed a tap in her heart and left it open, and every ward-light in Vhalen was a drop of her life burning in someone else's lamp.

"But the Ledger—" Elias started.

"*Is a trap dressed as a warning.*" Fire flickered in her throat when she spoke, a brief orange glow in the grey cavern of her mouth, gone as fast as it came. "*Your Order wrote it, or those who became your Order. Listen well, both of you, because I will say this once and I am spending strength I do not have to say it. If the gate is opened by the key alone — if the veilborn boy turns the lock with nothing else given — the wards will not fail gently. They will shatter. All of them, everywhere, in the same heartbeat. And the things beyond the veil, which have spent a century pressing their mouths against the tear, will come through into a city of defenseless sleepers before the boy's echo fades. That is the war the book promises. Not mine. Theirs. The book does not lie, little keeper. It simply arranges the truth like a knife on a table and waits for your hand.*"

My knees felt strange. I realized I was gripping the iron hard enough to hurt. "Then the Rite—"

"*The Rite is the other blade. At Solstice they will pour out what remains of me in a single draught. The wards will blaze brighter than they have in a century — long enough for the High Warden to be hailed as a saint. And then nothing. No fire, no wards, no dragon. Only the veil, thin as paper, and everything hungry behind it.*" The great eye closed, then opened again, and I understood she had done it to gather herself. "*They have designed it so that every road leads to ruin except one, and the one road costs more than either of you yet understands.*"

"Tell us," Elias said. His voice shook, but he didn't step back. I loved him for that, and feared for him because of it.

Ysolde's gaze settled on me then, fully, and I felt the weight of a century's calculation behind it.

"*The fire can be renewed. Not by the rite. Not by the key alone. By blood that belongs to both sides of the veil — freely given, poured into the dying flame, until the giver has nothing left to guard herself with. Veilblood. Your mother's blood, keeper's daughter. Yours.*"

The word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I thought of my mother's face, which I knew only from a single confiscated portrait Cassian had once, drunk on anniversary brandy, let me look at for the length of one candle. I thought of the wild-magic child in the Lower City whose burns I had healed with hands that glowed, and how I had told myself it was nothing, a trick, a residue.

"How much?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded very calm, which is how I knew I was in shock. "How much blood?"

"*All of it,*" Ysolde said gently, which is the cruelest way to say such a thing. "*Or nearly. Enough that what remains of you afterward will be changed, and lessened, and marked. Enough that you will feel the veil for the rest of your life, standing in every doorway between worlds. I will not pretend it is a small price. I will only tell you that it is the only coin the lock accepts.*"

"*No.*" Elias's hand found mine against the iron, gripped hard. "There has to be another way. Me — I'm the key, right? Use me. Take whatever you need from—"

"*You are a door, veilborn, not a hearth.*" But her tone softened toward him, almost tender. "*Though you are not nothing in this. The key must hold the gate open while the blood is given, and holding it open will cost you what it costs all doors: you will never entirely close again. You will hear the other side, always, the way your keeper hears the wards. You will be a threshold for the rest of your days. I say this so that no one can claim they were not warned. The book warned you too, in its crooked way. It simply neglected to mention that the war it promises begins only if you refuse to pay.*"

Above us, muffled through a hundred feet of rock, a bell began to ring.

Not the ward-bell. Not the market bell. The Solstice bell — the Order's great bronze voice in the Spire tower, rung only twice a year, and it was a full day early.

Elias and I looked at each other, and I saw my own horror reflected in his face. They had moved the Rite up. Ilse knew. Somehow she knew we were here, or she had simply decided not to wait, and somewhere above us the Order was assembling with knives and censers and a century of righteous lies, coming to drain the last light out of the world.

Ysolde's head lifted — an effort that made the whole chamber groan, that made dust sift from the ceiling like grey snow — and she looked up toward the distant sound with eyes that had seen the first war end.

"*They are coming,*" she said. "*So choose now, children. Run, and live short lives under a dying sky. Or stay, and pay, and give your city a future no book has written.*" The amber eyes fixed on me, and in them, for the first time, I saw something that undid me completely: hope, worn thin as the last leaf of the Ledger, but alive. "*I have been starving in the dark for a hundred years, little keeper. I swore I would not beg. I am begging.*"

The bell rang again, closer now, or perhaps just louder, and the gold veins in the gate pulsed — once, twice, three times — and I made my choice.

I rolled up my sleeve.
