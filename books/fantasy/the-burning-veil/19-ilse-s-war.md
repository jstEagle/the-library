## 19. Ilse's War

The war room of the Ashen Spire smelled of tallow and cold iron, and Cassian Vey had hated it for thirty years without ever once saying so.

He stood at the long table now, among the other wardens, watching High Warden Ilse drag her finger across the great map of Vhalen — the one with the Ember Gate marked not in ink but in a scorch-burn so old it had gone the color of old blood. Candles guttered in their iron cages along the walls. Outside the narrow windows, the solstice sun was going down over the Lower City, gilding the rooftops where ordinary people were lighting ordinary lamps, unaware that the men and women in this room were deciding whether they would wake tomorrow to wards or to ash.

"The Rite fails," Ilse said. She said it the way she said everything — flatly, precisely, as though the world were a ledger and she its auditor. "The Veilborn escaped. The Marchetti woman is in the tunnels with him. We have perhaps six hours before the dragon's fire gutters past the point of no return."

"Then we reinforce the siphons," said Warden Brandt, a heavy man with heavy hands. "We've done it before. In my father's time they—"

"In your father's time there was fire enough to steal." Ilse straightened. "There is not now. Reinforcement is triage on a corpse." Her finger came down on the burn-scar that was the gate. "We destroy it."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Cassian felt the ripples move through the other wardens — the small shifts of weight, the held breaths. He kept his own face still. He had learned stillness young, in the years after they burned his mother's name off the registry, and it had served him through two decades of service. It served him now, when what he actually felt was something cold uncoiling in his stomach, slow as smoke.

"Destroy the gate," he said. Not a question. He made it not a question because questions could be answered, and he did not want to hear the answer aloud.

"Shatter the seal, collapse the gatehouse, flood the vault-chambers with nullstone slurry." Ilse traced the sequence with her fingertip, each point a small tap, a metronome. "The dragon dies. The fire dies with it. The wards fail — all of them, everywhere, permanently — but the veil stays shut, because the veil does not run on the dragon's fire. It runs on the gate itself. On the seal."

"And the city?" Cassian asked. "A city without wards, High Warden. You know what walks the Lower Streets when the wards go dark. Wild-magic pools. Veil-leaks. Things that have been pressing against the skin of the world for a hundred years, waiting for exactly this."

"I know," Ilse said, "that a dead city behind a closed door is better than an open door."

*Is it?* The thought arrived unbidden, unwelcome, wearing a voice he had spent twenty years trying to forget — his mother's voice, low and amused, the voice she'd used over the cookfire before the Order took her. *Is it, little warden? Who taught you that doors are safer than windows?*

He put the thought down the way you put down a hot coal: quickly, and without looking at your palm.

"The archives disagree," he said instead. "The sealed histories. Maren's material, whatever survived her exile. There are accounts—"

"Maren is a traitor and her accounts are treason bound in calfskin." Ilse's eyes found his across the table, gray as slate, gray as the Spire at dawn. "You have read them. I know you have. I signed the permission myself, ten years ago, when I still thought you might be cured of your sentimentality." A pause, thin as a blade. "Tell me what they say, Warden Vey. Say it aloud. I want the room to hear how treason sounds in a loyal mouth."

Every warden at the table had gone very carefully neutral. Cassian felt the moment the way he felt ice underfoot — the exact instant when the surface stopped being ground and became a choice.

"They say," he said, evenly, "that the gate was built to feed, not to cage. That the founders siphoned Ysolde's fire into the ward-lattice deliberately, and that the prophecy of the Veilborn was written by the first Wardens themselves — not to warn us, but to keep a key in circulation. A key we were always meant to use up." He let that sit. "They say the threat was never the dragon. The threat is what the dragon has been holding back with the last of her strength. What burns on the far side of the veil. And that every year we take from her, the thing on the other side takes another step toward the seam."

Silence. The candles hissed.

"Lies," said Brandt, but softly, without conviction, the way men say *lies* when the truth has just walked through their chest.

"Fables," said Ilse. "Comfort-stories for magic-addled minds. The dragon is a beast, Warden. A magnificent beast, and beasts die, and cities survive the death of beasts. What cannot survive—" she leaned forward, both palms flat on the map, and for one moment Cassian saw something flicker beneath the slate of her face, something old and frightened and human, "—is a city whose walls depend on the goodwill of a living thing. We do not negotiate with living things. We do not *owe* them. The Order exists so that no child in Vhalen ever again kneels to a creature with teeth." She straightened. "The gate comes down. At moonrise. All of it."

"And the Marchetti girl?"

"Kill her if she interferes." No hesitation. None at all. "She is half-veilblood. Her mother burned for less."

Cassian heard himself say, "Her mother burned for nothing," and only realized he had said it aloud when the room went quiet around the words like water closing over a dropped coin.

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Something passed across her face — not anger. Recognition, maybe. Pity, even, which was worse.

"Yes," she said quietly. "So did mine."

* * *

He walked the Spire afterward because he could not sit still, and the Spire let him walk it the way a body lets a fever walk it — corridors of black stone, arrow-slit windows bleeding the last red light of the solstice, the hum of the ward-lattice overhead like a hive of glass bees. He knew every corridor. He had been brought here at nine years old, orphaned twice over — once by fire, once by paperwork — and the Order had raised him in these halls the way you raise a hound: fed, trained, praised for pointing at the right quarry.

His mother had been a hedge-witch. Nothing grand. She read weather in bird-flight and brewed fever-teas and once, famously in the annals of their street, talked a hailstorm out of flattening the barley crop. When the purges came she had hidden him in the flour chest and told him, through the lid, in a whisper he could still recite: *Whatever they ask you, little warden, remember that fear makes small rooms. Don't live in theirs.*

They had taken her at dawn. He had been pulled from the chest at noon by a young warden named Ilse Vaunt, who had knelt, looked at him with those slate eyes, and said: *You're safe now. No one will ever make you afraid again.*

It had taken him twenty-five years to understand that this was not a promise. It was a diagnosis.

He climbed to the archive level, past the sanctioned histories — the gilt-edged, censored volumes that told the Dragon Wars as a simple story of beast and hero — and stood outside the sealed door of the restricted stacks, where Maren's confiscated materials were catalogued under sedition numbers. His key opened it. It had always opened it; Ilse had seen to that, ten years ago, in one of her experiments with his conscience. *Let him look,* she'd said. *Truth is a leash too, if you hold the collar right.*

He had looked. He had looked and then he had filed it away in the small locked room inside himself where he kept everything that didn't fit the shape of his life — his mother's voice, the smell of fever-tea, the memory of Freya Marchetti at seven years old, sobbing in the Spire infirmary while he sat beside her cot all night because someone had to and no one else would.

*You saved me,* she'd told him once, years later, in the gatehouse, over bad tea. *You know that. Whatever else happens. You saved me.*

*No,* he'd said. *I just didn't finish what they started.*

Inside the restricted stacks he found what he was looking for without hunting for it, because he had known where it was for a decade: Maren's transcription of the founders' council, the original minutes, copied in her cramped, furious hand. *The beast is dying faster than projected,* the founders had written, a hundred years ago, in the tidy script of men who thought of a living mind as an engine. *Recommend acceleration of extraction. Recommend propagation of the Veilborn prophecy to ensure a successor key. The wards must not be allowed to fail while the outer threat persists. Let the city love its lights and never learn the price.*

And beneath it, in a different hand — older, scorched at the edges, salvaged from some burned archive — a single line that Cassian had memorized against his will, the way you memorize a wound:

*When the last flame gutters, the seam will not hold. Pray someone loves the door more than they fear what waits behind it.*

He stood in the dark between the shelves for a long time, listening to the lattice hum overhead, thinner than it had been yesterday. Thinner than it had been this morning. He could hear it failing the way you can hear a horse founder — a subtle wrongness in a rhythm you've ridden all your life.

Somewhere below, in the vaults, wardens were loading nullstone slurry into the siphon-chambers. Somewhere in the tunnels under the Lower City, a girl he had half-raised was running with the last key in the world, hunted by everyone he had ever called brother.

*Fear makes small rooms,* his mother whispered. *Don't live in theirs.*

He had lived in Ilse's room for twenty-five years. It had warm light and good bread and a bed, and it had no windows, and tonight he had finally turned around and looked at the wall where a window should be, and understood that the wall was a door, and the door was locked from his side.

* * *

He found Ilse alone in the map room at midnight, or what passed for midnight in a building that never slept — the candles renewed, the wardens dismissed, the great scorch-mark of the gate lit gold by a single lamp. She was standing very still, hands clasped behind her back, and for a moment, watching her, Cassian saw the young warden who had pulled a boy out of a flour chest: the same rigid spine, the same terrible discipline of grief.

"You're defecting," she said, without turning. "I've known since the war room. You have your mother's face when you lie, Cassian. You always have. It's why I never gave you a command that required one."

He said nothing. The lamp flame bent slightly in a draft from the corridor, and shadows swung across the map like birds.

"I want you to understand," Ilse went on, "that I am not doing this out of cruelty. I need you to carry that out of this room, wherever you're going. Whatever you tell yourself about me at the gate tonight, carrying your little torch of righteousness — I need one person alive who knows I am not a monster." Now she turned, and her face in the lamplight was carved and tired and old in a way it never showed the wardens. "I was there, Cassian. Before the Wars ended. I saw what came through the seam in the three days it stood open. I was eleven years old, and I watched it wear my father's face and call me by my name in my mother's voice, and then I watched it do what it does. The dragon didn't save us. The dragon *fled*, and slammed the door behind her, and we built our whole civilization on the latch." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. That was the most frightening thing about her. "I will not open that door for a girl's guilty conscience and a boy's pretty birthmark. If the price of keeping it shut is the dragon, the wards, the wild-magic, the leaks, the hurt children in the Lower City — then I pay it. I have been paying it my entire life. So have you. The only difference between us is that I stopped pretending the payments were temporary."

"The difference between us," Cassian said, "is that you think the door is the wall."

Something moved behind her eyes — the flicker again, the buried thing, the eleven-year-old in the burning house of her certainty. "It's too late for you to be right," she said softly. "Do you understand that? Even if everything Maren dug up is true. Even if the founders lied and the prophecy is a trap and the dragon is a martyr. It is *too late*. Six hours of fire left. Moonrise orders given. If I stand down now, everything I burned to build this peace stands revealed as — as what? A hundred years of theft? A century of murdered witches to protect a lie?" She laughed, briefly, terribly, without any humor in it at all. "I can't afford for you to be right, Cassian. Neither can you. That's what an Order *is*."

"No," he said. "That's what a prison is. I've lived in one a long time. I'm told the view is better outside."

He turned for the door.

"Cassian." Her voice stopped him, quiet as snowfall. "If you walk out of here, I will declare you traitor within the hour, and every warden in this Spire will hunt you, and I will not be able to protect you, and I will not try. You understand that."

He understood it. He understood, too, that some part of her — the part kneeling in the ashes of a burned house, holding out a hand to a boy in a flour chest — was offering him one last chance to stay in the small warm room. To keep the collar. To keep the bread and the bed and the humming false ceiling, and never again have to choose anything, ever, at all.

"I know," he said. "Thank you. For the flour chest. For all of it." He paused at the threshold, and did not turn back, because if he turned back he would see her face, and he had loved her the way you love a wall that sheltered you from a storm — gratefully, and knowing it was a wall. "But you should know something before you give the order at moonrise."

"What?"

"The Marchetti girl isn't opening the gate to free a beast." He stepped into the dark of the corridor, and the words followed him out like sparks off a brand. "She's opening it to feed a dying friend. And when the fire comes back — when the wards come back *honest*, the way they were meant to be — every soul in this Spire is going to have to decide which of us was guarding the door, and which of us was just leaning against it."

Behind him, in the map room, the lamp flame steadied. Somewhere above, the lattice hummed its thinning song. And somewhere beneath the city, in the deep places where the air tasted of cinders and old promises, a dragon's heart beat slower, and slower, and waited —

— as the bells of the Spire began to ring the alarm, and the whole tower woke around him, shouting his name.
