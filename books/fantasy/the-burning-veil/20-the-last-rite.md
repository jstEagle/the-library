## 20. The Last Rite

### FREYA

The Ember Gate had never looked so small.

Ten years of standing before it, and Freya had always thought of it as vast — a wall of black basalt veined with cooling ember-light, taller than the gatehouse, older than the city stacked above it like sediment. Now, with the wards failing in visible waves across its face, with the vaulted cavern shuddering around her like the ribs of a dying animal, it seemed almost pitiable. A door. Just a door, and everything on either side of it starving.

"Freya." Elias's voice came from behind her, hoarse from running, from smoke, from the spire's collapse still raining ash somewhere far above them. "Tell me again."

She didn't turn. Her palms were pressed flat against the stone, and through them she could feel it — the dragon's fire guttering like a candle in a draft, each pulse weaker than the last. Ysolde's heartbeat, if a fire could be said to have one. Ten years of listening to it and only now, with Maren's truth burning in her chest and her mother's blood singing under her skin, did she understand what she'd been listening to all along.

A slow murder. A century long. Sanctioned, scheduled, sanctified.

"The wards don't draw from the dragon," she said. "They draw *through* her. Every ward in Vhalen is a straw stuck into a living creature, and we've been drinking her dry for three hundred years and calling it protection." She swallowed. "And when she goes out—"

"The veil thins," Elias finished. He'd learned the litany fast; he'd had to. "And whatever's on the other side stops being a story."

Behind them, down the long throat of the approach tunnel, boots. Many boots. The rhythmic iron knock of Order sabatons on stone, and beneath them a sound that made Freya's teeth ache: the high, glassy whine of ward-breakers spinning up to full charge.

Cassian had bought them twenty minutes. Maybe less. She'd seen his face when he turned back toward the tunnel mouth to hold it — not the face of a man defecting, exactly. The face of a man paying a debt he'd been carrying for twenty years, and glad, finally, to set it down.

"Elias." She turned at last. In the ember-light his eyes were the color of the gate itself, dark shot through with gold, and the faint luminance still lived in the veins of his wrists where the resonance had woken him. Veilborn. Living key. The word the Ledger had used, a century before his birth, as if he were furniture. As if he were a lock waiting for its turn.

"Don't," he said, reading her.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You got the look. Cassian gets it too, right before he decides something terrible for my own good." He stepped closer. The heat off the gate played over them both, and where it touched the glow in his veins the light brightened, answering, and Freya felt the pull of it in her own blood like a hook behind her sternum. "So don't decide anything terrible."

"It's going to cost me," she said. "Feeding her. My blood — the veilblood half of me. Maren thinks—" Her voice caught. She made herself go on, because he deserved the whole arithmetic, not the rounded-down version. "Maren thinks there may not be much of *me* left after. The part that's mine. The part that's just Freya Marchetti, who sells luck charms and burns toast and—"

"And kisses like she's apologizing for it," Elias said. "Yeah. I've met her."

Despite everything — the boots, the whine of the breakers, the dying god behind the stone — Freya laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real.

"I need you to do your part," she said. "When I open the gate, the veil will want to close. It's been closing for three hundred years; it's good at it. You're the key. You have to *hold* it. Not open it — anyone could open it, that's the horror of the prophecy, that's why they needed you dead at the Solstice. Opening is easy. *Staying open* — that takes a person. That takes you, standing in the doorway, being the hinge, while she drinks and drinks and drinks." She took his hands. His pulse was fast against her fingers, and under the skin the gold light pulsed in time with the gate. In time with Ysolde. They were already attuned. The prophecy had done its work well. "It will hurt. It will try to make you let go. Do you understand?"

"I understand." He was pale. He was terrified. He did not step back. "And you understand that if there's nothing left of you after, I'm going to be extremely annoying about it. Grief-stricken and annoying. For decades."

"Noted."

The first ward-breaker discharge lit the tunnel behind them white.

* * *

### ELIAS

Later — if there was a later — Elias would remember the sound the gate made when it opened.

Not an explosion. Not the groan of stone. It was a note, single and enormous, low enough that he felt it in his molars and the fluid of his eyes, a note like the world clearing its throat before speaking. The basalt didn't break. It *unlocked*, seams of ember-light racing across its surface in patterns he suddenly recognized — the same patterns that lived in his own veins, the same branching, the same patient geometry. Of course. The gate had always known him. It had been carved by people who knew what would come through the generations, who had written his name in stone a hundred years early and called it prophecy because the truth — *we built a door and we are waiting for the key* — was unbearable.

The doors swung inward onto fire.

Or onto what had been fire. What remained of it. Ysolde lay coiled in a cavern beyond, and she was vast — her skull alone was the size of the gatehouse above — but her scales had gone the grey of banked charcoal, and the light in her chest came in long, failing intervals, like breathing through water. Her eye found them. One eye, amber going to ash at the rim, ancient and lucid and *tired* in a way that made Elias's knees soften.

*Little hinge,* she said, and her voice wasn't sound at all, it arrived directly behind his eyes, warm as a hearth he'd never had. *You came.*

"He came," Freya said. She was already walking forward, pulling the knife from her belt — her charm-crafting knife, the one with the worn bone handle, the first tool she'd ever owned. "Both of us. We brought you the bill for three hundred years, and we intend to pay it."

*Child.* The great head shifted; dust fell from the cavern roof in silver sheets. *I have watched them come to this door for a century. Wardens with their instruments. Priests with their rites. All of them measuring how much more they could take. None of them asking what I would give freely, if only someone asked.* The amber eye moved to Elias. *He asks. Even now, standing in the doorway, holding it — he is asking.*

Elias hadn't realized he was doing it. But he was. The moment the gates had opened, the veil had begun to press — he could feel it as pressure on every side, a hungering weight like deep water, like the whole attention of some vast dark thing narrowing to the point of this doorway, this thin place, wanting shut. And he held. Not with muscle. With *self*. He stood in the threshold and he refused, with every stubborn ordinary human gram of himself, to be moved, and the doorway stayed open because he said so.

It hurt the way Freya had promised. It hurt like holding a door against a flood. Somewhere in the pressure he could feel the other side of the veil — not evil, nothing so simple — just *other*, vast and cold and curious, pressing its face against the gap between worlds. Waiting to see what the small bright things would do.

Then Freya opened her wrist.

The knife went in shallow, deliberate, along the vein Maren had shown her, and what came out was not entirely red. It ran red at first and then the color *left* it, silver threading up through the crimson like dawn coming up wrong, and where the blood struck the cavern floor the stone remembered fire. Moss of ember-light bloomed in a spreading ring. The air itself warmed.

Ysolde breathed in.

Elias felt it through the doorway he was holding — felt the dragon's inhale as a pull at his own chest, felt the first sip of veilblood reach her like rain reaching a field after drought. The grey scales flushed. Deep in her chest, a coal that had been dying for a century sat up, looked around, and caught.

*Oh,* Ysolde said, and the word shivered the dust off the whole cavern. *Oh, little priestess. Your mother's blood. Your mother's courage. Drink, then — no. Give. Give, and I will show you what it becomes.*

Freya gave.

Elias watched her do it and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade, why the Order feared this. Why Ilse had built a century of lies on top of the simple fact of it. Because magic in Vhalen had been taught as theft — take from the wards, take from the dragon, take and hoard and ration — and here was a woman standing in front of a dying god *giving*, freely, at ruinous cost, and the giving worked. The giving worked better than the taking ever had. Fire climbed Ysolde's throat in rivers of visible light. Her scales kindled scale by scale, bronze and copper and old gold, and when she rose — when the last dragon in the world unfolded to her full height and the cavern became too small for her the way a story becomes too small for its meaning — the wards of Vhalen, miles above, must have flared like a sunrise, because every ward in the city drew a breath of honest fire for the first time in three hundred years.

And Freya swayed.

The silver in her blood was everywhere now — in the floor, in the fire, in the dragon — and Elias could see what it was costing, see it the way you can see water level dropping in a clear jug. The color was leaving *her*. Not her blood. Her. The edges of her going faint, going translucent, the particular irreducible Freya-ness of her thinning out into the general brightness of the fire.

"Hold the door," she said. She was smiling. That was the worst part. She sounded like herself and she was smiling and she was disappearing. "Almost — enough —"

"No." The word tore out of him. "No, Freya, that's *enough*, she's burning, stop—"

*I decide,* she said, or the fire said through her, or both. *This is the one thing in my whole life I get to decide.*

The tunnel behind them erupted.

* * *

### FREYA

She heard it through a haze that was becoming the whole of her: the crack of ward-breakers finding their range, the shriek of stone, and then High Warden Ilse Marchetti-Vey's voice — no, not Vey, Ilse had kept her own name, Ilse had never belonged to anyone — cutting through the din like a blade through silk.

"*Seal the cavern. Burn the abomination. Take the boy alive.*"

Freya floated somewhere near the ceiling of herself. Below, Ysolde blazed — truly blazed now, young-seeming, terrible, her wings unfurling to fill the cavern's dark — and Elias stood in the gate's mouth with his arms spread and his veins alight, holding the door against the pressure of another world, and neither of them could stop what came next. That had been the design of her sacrifice. Everything given to the fire. Nothing left to fight with.

Ilse's strike team poured through the broken tunnel mouth in white-and-gold, crossbows of glass and iron leveled, and at their center walked the High Warden herself in ceremonial plate that had not seen battle in a generation, her face lit with the serene certainty of a woman who has never once doubted that she is the protagonist of history.

"*Freya.*" Ilse's voice found her even through the haze, even through the fire. Something flickered across it — the ghost of the woman who had signed the forms placing a seven-year-old orphan in the keeper's gatehouse, who had visited twice a year with hard sweets and harder questions. "*Step away from it, child. You've been deceived. When the abomination dies and the boy is safely rited, the wards will be renewed properly, by lawful means, and this madness ends tonight.*"

*Lawful means.* Freya tried to laugh and couldn't find the machinery. *Finishing the draining, she means. Killing the last dragon and blinding the city to whatever presses on the veil, forever, and calling it peace.*

But Ysolde was watching Ilse too. And the dragon, newly fed, newly *awake*, turned her kindled head with the slow gravity of a continent moving, and her voice filled the cavern and the tunnel and perhaps, Freya thought distantly, the foundations of the whole city above:

*High Warden. You believe you command the fire.* A plume of true flame curled from her nostrils, gold-edged, alive. *Come closer, and learn what the fire commands.*

The first arrow loosed. Elias shouted. The veil, sensing weakness in its hinge, leaned its whole cold weight against the door—

—and Freya, floating in the bright ruin of her own giving, felt the last of her gather itself for one final act of will, and reached —

* * *

### ELIAS

The arrow never reached Ysolde. Fire took it midair, and the fire *answered back* — a lance of gold screaming down the tunnel that turned the front rank of Ilse's glass crossbows to dripping slag and sent white-and-gold soldiers diving for cover behind their own barricades. The cavern became a war of light: dragonfire against ward-breaker discharges, the veil's cold pressure against one exhausted man refusing to let a door close.

Elias held.

His nose was bleeding. His vision kept graying at the edges. The thing on the other side of the veil had stopped being abstract — it had a *attention* now, focused wholly on him, and it spoke without words: *close, rest, sleep, let go, everything ends, everything ends anyway.* And against it he had only this: her face. Freya's face, laughing at the night market over paper trays of fried lotus root, saying *you're going to be trouble, I can tell.* He planted himself on that memory like a man planting a flag on a ridge, and he held.

Through the smoke he saw Freya collapse.

Saw Ysolde catch her — impossibly gently, one vast claw cupped around a body gone light as ash — and saw the dragon lift her, and saw the last thread of silver rise from Freya's lips like breath in winter and stream into the fire, and the fire *roar*.

And saw Ilse, through it all, walking forward. Unhurried. Into the flame-light. Drawing the ritual blade the Order used for the Solstice Rite, its edge hungry with a century of stolen fire, her eyes fixed not on the dragon but on the boy in the doorway.

On him.

"*The key turns once,*" Ilse called, over the roar, over the dying of the wards, over the thinning of the world. "*One way or another, Veilborn. Tonight the gate closes — with her ashes inside it, or with yours.*"

And the veil, feeling the blade's intent, began — very softly, very patiently — to pull Elias *through*.
