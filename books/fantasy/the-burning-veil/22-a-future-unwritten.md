## 22. A Future Unwritten

The first thing I learned about a city waking from fear is that it wakes slowly, the way an old man rises from a chair — one hand on the armrest, one joint at a time, testing whether the floor will hold.

Vhalen had been afraid for a hundred years. It did not know how to be anything else yet.

I noticed it in small things, weeks after the night the sky over the Ember Gate turned the color of a forge at dawn. A baker in the Lower City left her shutters open past curfew because there was no longer a curfew worth the name, and she stood in her doorway each morning like a woman listening for a sound she'd been told never to expect. Children chalked spirals on the cobbles — the old ward-signs, the ones our grandmothers had scratched out with spit and shame — and no one washed them away. A fishmonger argued loudly with his neighbor about the price of eels, and halfway through the argument they both stopped, looked at each other, and laughed, because they could. Because arguing about eels was suddenly just arguing about eels, and not a performance of ordinariness staged for passing patrols.

The Ashen Order still walked the streets, but they walked differently now. Their grey cloaks were the same; their faces were not. Some of them had surrendered their badges to Cassian Vey's new administration with the relief of men setting down stones they'd forgotten they carried. Others simply vanished into the city, and the city, in its vast and grudging mercy, let them.

And magic — magic came back the way water comes back to a dried riverbed: first as a seep, then a trickle, then a green insistence nothing could argue with.

* * *

I saw the first licensed charm-seller on a Tuesday.

She'd set up a folding table outside the tax office of all places, with a hand-painted sign reading REGISTERED PRACTITIONER — MINOR WARDING & MENDING, and a queue of people waiting with broken kettles, cracked windowpanes, ailing houseplants, and one enormous tortoise whose shell had split in some long-ago accident. The practitioner was maybe nineteen, freckled, terrified, and glowing faintly at the fingertips every time someone thanked her, which made her more terrified still.

Freya watched her for a long time from across the street.

"She's leaking," Freya said. "Look at her hands. Nobody taught her containment."

"Will you teach her?"

"I don't know if anyone should teach anyone anything yet." But her voice said otherwise. Her voice had been saying otherwise since the night she poured a century of stolen fire back into a dying dragon and came away marked for it — thin silver lines running up both forearms like frost ferns, like rivers seen from very high above, the map of what she'd given written on her skin in a language only Ysolde could read fluently.

She touched one of the lines absently, the way you touch an old scar or a wedding band. "Someone has to," she admitted. "Before she learns it wrong from a book."

That was the other thing about a city waking from fear: everyone was learning everything wrong from books. The Prophecy Ledger itself sat in the gatehouse now behind glass, an exhibit rather than an oracle, and schoolchildren filed past it on field trips while teachers explained, in careful voices, how a hundred years of certainty had been built on a forgery of omissions. The final page remained blank. Cassian had ordered it left that way — ordered, in fact, that no scribe ever write upon it again.

"A book that ends," he'd said to me once, standing before the case with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral, "is a book that can't be used against us. Let it stay empty. Let them get used to the idea that the future is unwritten."

He'd glanced sideways at me when he said it. Cassian Vey had learned, in those weeks, to look at me without flinching, which I suspect cost him more than surrendering his badges would have. He had spent decades believing the wards needed feeding, and he had fed them — with laws, with executions, with a little girl's mother led to the pyre — and the truth that it had all been theft dressed as sacrifice was a weight he carried now visibly, the way Freya carried her marks. He wore it better than most men would have. That was the most I could honestly say of him, and some days it felt like enough.

* * *

Ysolde's fire burned honest now.

I went down to the gate perhaps once a week, because the dragon asked for me — or rather, she asked for Freya, and Freya brought me, and somewhere along the way the asking had come to include me too. The great doors stood open these days. There seemed little point in sealing them; the wards no longer drew from Ysolde's flame but harmonized with it, a choir instead of a leech, and the whole arrangement hummed through the stone like a held note that never tired.

She was awake more often than not. Ancient, lucid, her scales the color of banked coals with veins of living gold threading through where the silver scars of a hundred years of draining had been. She was smaller than the stories said. Everything true, I was learning, was smaller than its story.

"You are staring again, Veilborn," she said, without opening her eyes. Her voice moved through my ribs like warmth through cold hands.

"Sorry."

"Do not apologize for wonder. Apologize for waste." One eye opened, molten and patient. "You are thinking about the market."

I laughed. Even now, being known by a dragon took adjustment. "Is it obvious?"

"To me, everything is obvious. You smell of cedar shavings and nervousness. You have been building something."

Freya appeared at my shoulder, so quietly I still hadn't learned to hear her coming even after everything. She was carrying two cups of the terrible spiced wine the tunnel vendors sold, and she pressed one into my hand and drank from the other, and Ysolde watched us both with the expression — I swear this — of an elderly relative trying not to look smug.

"The stall is finished," Freya said. "If he ever stops sanding it."

"It needed sanding."

"It needed sanding three weeks ago. Now it's a piece of furniture. We're going to sell charms off a piece of furniture."

"We're going to sell *honest* charms off a well-made stall," I corrected. "There's a difference. The luck-bringers are real now. You tested them yourself."

"I tested one. On a dockworker. Who is now inexplicably lucky at cards and has told half the harbor about it." She sighed, but her eyes were bright, and the silver marks on her arms caught the dragonlight and threw it back in fine bright threads. "We're going to be overrun."

"That's the general idea, isn't it? Belonging somewhere usually means being overrun by it."

Ysolde made a sound deep in her chest that I eventually understood was laughter. It rattled the cups. It rattled my teeth. It sounded, a little, like the gate had sounded the night it opened — that same note of something immense choosing, deliberately, to be gentle.

* * *

The night market reopened on the eve of the Long Dark, which was no longer a night of shuttered windows and whispered prayers but — tentatively, experimentally — a festival.

They strung lanterns along the canal bridges, hundreds of them, and for the first time in living memory the lanterns weren't ward-lanterns, grey-glassed and grim, but ordinary paper in ordinary colors: red and gold and a green so bright it looked like the color had been waiting a century for permission. Musicians played on corners. Someone had set up a table where you could pay a copper to hear your fortune told by a woman with a deck of cards and, judging by her wink, absolutely no magical ability whatsoever, which Freya declared was the healthiest thing she'd seen all year.

Our stall stood between a honey-seller and a man who repaired clocks, which Freya said was fitting, because we were in the business of mending time.

I had built the frame from cedar, because cedar holds warmth, and I had carved the counter's edge with a spiral — the old ward-sign, the one the children chalked on the cobbles — and under it, small enough that you'd miss it unless you leaned close, I had carved a veilflower. Freya found it the morning we carried the stall down through the tunnels, and she went very quiet, and then she kissed me with the taste of salt on her lips, and neither of us said anything about her mother, and both of us were thinking it.

By full dark we couldn't keep up.

They came for luck-charms and love-knots and small protections for doorways and cradles and fishing boats. They came, mostly, to look — to stand in front of a witch working openly, sleeves rolled, silver marks bare to the lamplight, and simply watch her be unafraid. An old woman pressed Freya's hand between both of hers and said, "Your mother's daughter," and Freya didn't ask how she knew, and the old woman didn't explain, and something passed between them that I understood I was not meant to follow.

A girl of about ten pushed through the crowd, breathless, holding out a chipped clay figure of a dragon she'd clearly made herself and loved past all repair.

"Can you fix her wing?"

Freya knelt to be at eye level. "This is excellent work. Look at the scale detail." She turned the figure over, and I saw her hesitate — saw the flicker of the old arithmetic, the lifetime of *hide it, bury it, smaller, quieter* — and then I watched her set it down forever. She cupped the little clay dragon in both marked hands, and light moved through her fingers like water finding a channel, and the wing knit whole, and the girl gasped, and half the market turned to look.

Freya Marchetti, keeper of the Ember Gate, last of the veilbloods, stood up in front of two hundred strangers with her magic still glowing off her hands, and smiled.

"There," she said. "Good as new. Mind the tail; it was always fragile."

The applause was embarrassed and enormous. Freya hid her face against my shoulder until it stopped, laughing.

* * *

Near midnight, when the crowds thinned to stragglers and the honey-seller was packing up and singing to herself, we closed the stall and sat on the canal edge with our feet hanging over the black water and the lantern-light breaking apart beneath us in slow gold pieces.

Maren's archive had burned, as promised, with Maren standing in the doorway of it, watching, dry-eyed, holding one salvaged book against her chest — the uncensored history, the single copy, which she had delivered to the new Council herself with instructions that it be copied freely and never sealed. "Let everyone read the ugly parts," she'd said. "Especially the ugly parts. That's the only vaccine there is." She lived now in a room above a printshop and was, by all reports, insufferably happy.

Cassian had sent a message that afternoon. One line, in his precise clerk's hand: *The Solstice Rite is struck from the calendar. Permanently. — V.* Beneath it, after a long gap, as though he'd sat with the pen hovering: *Thank you both. I will not say it twice.*

"He'll say it twice someday," Freya had murmured. "When it matters."

Below us, the canal carried its cargo of reflected lights toward the sea. Above us, far up the mountain, the direction of the gate, the air glowed very faintly — not the angry flare of that night, but a steady ember-warmth, the color of a hearth in a house where someone is always home.

"Do you ever think about what comes next?" I asked. "Really next. Not the market, not the teaching. After that."

"All the time." Freya turned her cup in her fingers. "Ysolde says the veil is thinner than it's ever been. Says there are things on the other side that have noticed the fire burning honest again. She doesn't say it to frighten me. She says it the way you tell someone the tide schedule — here's the truth, plan accordingly." She leaned her head against my shoulder. "A hundred years ago the Order answered that kind of truth with a wall and a lie. I'd like to answer it with something better. I don't know what yet. Maybe that's the point. Maybe not knowing is the whole job now."

"The Ledger's last page," I said.

"The Ledger's last page." She smiled against my sleeve. "Blank. And staying blank. Every day I don't write on it is a day nobody else gets to either."

The festival noise swelled and faded around us in waves. Somewhere a fiddle struck up something old and fast, and people whooped, and the lanterns swung on their strings, and the girl with the clay dragon ran past clutching it overhead like a banner, and the ember-glow on the mountain breathed slow and steady, in and out, in and out, like the city itself had finally remembered how.

Then Freya went still against my shoulder.

Not the stillness of memory. The stillness of attention.

"Elias," she said quietly. "Look at the water."

The canal had gone strange. The reflections of the lanterns still broke and reformed in the current — but beneath them, deeper than the light could reach, something else was moving. A shimmer, like heat-haze underwater. Like the surface of the world remembering it was a surface, and that there was a depth to it, and that the depth had lately become aware of being looked at.

The silver marks on Freya's arms lit softly, all at once, unprompted — and from the mountain, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I had heard only once before, on the night everything changed:

Ysolde, humming a warning.

Freya's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip was calm. Her pulse was not.

"Well," she said, and the wry warmth in her voice was the same as it had been on our first date, at a night-market stall not so different from ours, in a city that thought its story was over. "So much for the unwritten future."

She stood, and pulled me up after her, and together we turned toward the mountain and the thinning veil and whatever was beginning to come through — two people with ink-stained hands and no prophecy left to follow, walking into a page no one had written yet.
