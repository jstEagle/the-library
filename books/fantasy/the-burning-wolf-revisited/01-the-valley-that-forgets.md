## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ewe was dying again.

Wren Vane knelt in the straw of the far pen with one hand flat against the animal's heaving flank and felt the fever moving through her like a bad wind through a wheat field. The ewe's name—her real name, the one she'd been given by her mother in the first bleating hour of her life—was *Small Rain*, because she'd been born during a drizzle so fine it barely wet the dust. Nobody else knew that. Nobody else could know that, because knowing the name of a sheep meant hearing it, and hearing it meant the gift, and the gift in Hollowmere was a thing you buried so deep even you sometimes forgot where you'd put the shovel.

"Easy," Wren murmured, in the way anyone might murmur to a sick animal. "Easy now."

But under the words, beneath them, in the space where words stop being human, Wren said: *Small Rain. Small Rain, sister-of-the-cold-morning. The fever is a fire and fires burn out. You are rain. Let yourself be rain.*

The ewe's breathing slowed. Her flanks stopped their terrible shuddering. She turned her long face toward Wren and made a sound that was not quite a bleat—not to any ear but Wren's—and it said: *warm*.

"I know," Wren said aloud, because the pen was empty and the barn loft above was empty and the whole eastern slope of the Vane holding was empty except for forty goats who kept other people's secrets better than most people did. "I know it's cold. Fever lies like that. It makes you cold when you're burning."

*Warm,* the ewe insisted, and pressed her skull into Wren's palm, and Wren let her, and for a moment—one stolen, ordinary moment—it was just this. Just a person and an animal and the gray light coming down through the gaps in the boards, and the smell of hay and lanolin and the mineral tang of the stream below. This was the part of the gift Wren loved, the only part worth loving: not the commanding, never that, but the *listening*. The world was so much louder than people thought. The world was a choir that had been singing since before there were ears, and Wren had been born with the door propped open on all of it.

Then boots crunched on gravel outside, and Wren shut the door.

"Wren!" Their father's voice, carrying the particular flatness he used when he wanted to sound casual and didn't quite manage it. "You out here?"

"Far pen," Wren called back, already standing, already brushing straw from their knees, already arranging their face into the mild blankness that was the family inheritance, passed down like a copper kettle from generation to generation. "Small Rain took bad in the night."

Tam Vane came around the corner of the pen with his cap in his hands, which he always did when he was worried, turning it, worrying the brim. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with Wren's same dark hair going iron at the temples and Wren's same brown eyes, except his had learned long ago how to look at nothing.

"Took bad how?"

"Fever. She'll come through." Wren said it lightly, the way you'd say anything unimportant. "Gave her willow bark steeped in the last of the honey."

Tam looked at the ewe. The ewe looked back at him with the placid stupidity of a healthy animal, because that was what Wren had asked of her without asking: *be dull, be nothing, be a sheep.* Small Rain chewed. Tam's shoulders came down half an inch.

"Well," he said. "Good."

It was a small conversation. It was the smallest kind of conversation two people could have while both of them were thinking enormous things. Wren watched their father decide, visibly, deliberately, not to ask why the ewe had calmed so fast, or why the other goats had gathered at the near fence to watch Wren with the fixed attention of an audience, or why the crows on the roof ridge had gone quiet as held breath. Twenty-two years of deciding not to ask. Tam Vane had gotten very good at it. They all had. It was practically the valley's chief export.

"Mother wants you up at the house after," he said. "There's flour to carry from the mill, and your aunt sent word about the wool fair."

"Aunt Ivy can keep her wool fair."

"She can, and she will, and you'll still carry the flour." But there was no edge in it. There was almost never any edge in it anymore; edges were dangerous things in a household built entirely out of what went unsaid. Tam jammed the cap back onto his head. "Wren."

"What?"

He looked at his daughter—or his son, or whatever the village had decided Wren was this season; Hollowmere had opinions the way still water has a surface, thin and easily broken—and something moved behind his eyes that he caught and killed before it could get out.

"Nothing," he said. "Mind the fence on the north pasture. Post is leaning."

He left. Wren stood in the straw with Small Rain's warm breath against their wrist and listened to their own heart doing something too fast for a morning that contained nothing.

*Nothing,* Wren told themselves. *This is a nothing day. Nothing days are the best days.*

That was the creed of Hollowmere, though no one had ever written it down. Nothing happens here. Nothing has happened here for three hundred years, and thank every saint and stone for it. The valley sat folded into the mountains like a coin in a closed fist, twelve miles end to end, one road in, one road out, and the road mostly forgot itself halfway down the pass. Travelers who did stumble through described Hollowmere afterward as *restful*, as *unremarkable*, as a place they couldn't quite remember clearly, like a dream about a room they'd once slept in. The miller said it was the altitude. The priest said it was God's peace. Old Marta at the crossing said, once, very drunk, that it was the wards, and then laughed, and then never drank at the crossing again.

Wren had known about the wards since before knowing was safe. Not from anyone telling—no one told—but from the animals, who felt them the way fish feel water. The valley hummed. Very faintly, very constantly, at the bottom of hearing, everything in Hollowmere hummed, and things from outside the hum came in muffled, like voices through a wall. A hawk from over the pass sounded like a hawk heard underwater. And the wild things—the truly wild things, the wolves and the old deer-bloodlines and the foxes with their layered, sly, ancient speech—those came through as almost nothing at all. A whisper of a whisper. Hollowmere's animals were tame animals, chatty and simple, and Wren had grown up thinking that was simply what animals were.

Until the winter they were nine, when a wolf had come down into the orchard, starving, snow-blind, and its voice had hit Wren like a wave hitting a swimmer—vast, cold, full of words Wren hadn't known language could hold—and Wren had answered it. Out loud. In the orchard. With their mother ten feet away.

Wren still didn't know what their mother had heard. Maren Vane had gone white as the snow and taken Wren inside and sat them down and said, in a voice like a woman reading her own death warrant: *Whatever you just did, you never do it again. Do you understand me? Never. Not where anyone can see. Not where anyone can hear.*

And Wren, nine years old and shaking, had understood perfectly, because children always understand the important things faster than adults want them to.

So: twenty-two years of silence. Of listening with the door cracked and speaking with the door barred. Of calming fevers and gentling frightened livestock and talking the bees into staying through bad summers, all of it done in whispers, in empty rooms, in the spaces between heartbeats. Of watching travelers pass through the valley and feeling the gift strain toward them like a dog at a rope's end. Of lying, in small ways, daily, until the lying wasn't even lying anymore. It was just the shape of a life.

Suffocating, Wren's aunt Ivy would have called it, if Ivy had known—which she didn't, which nobody did, which was the entire point. Ivy had left Hollowmere at nineteen and come back at thirty-five with a husband and a bitterness she wore like jewelry, and she said the valley smothered people, said it ate ambition, said the young ones left or shriveled. She wasn't wrong. But she didn't know the half of it. The valley didn't smother Wren. Wren smothered Wren, carefully, every hour, on purpose, because the alternative was a pyre. Everyone in Hollowmere knew the stories of the burning times, even if they told them like weather from another country. Gift-bearers. Hedge-witches. Beast-speakers. The crown's witch-hunters had come through the highlands twice in living memory, and the pyres had burned in towns Wren had never seen, and the smoke had somehow reached even here, in stories, in warnings, in mothers' voices dropping low.

Hollowmere was safe. Hollowmere was safe because it was silent, and silent because it was warded, and warded because—well. Because of something. Wren had never asked what. Asking questions was how you ended up interesting, and interesting was how you ended up dead.

*Nothing day,* Wren reminded themselves, and got the willow bark, and sat with Small Rain until the sun cleared the eastern ridge and the morning turned honest and gold.

* * *

Midmorning, Wren carried water up from the stream and let themselves, for exactly the length of the walk, listen to everything at once.

This was the indulgence. This was the one vice. Alone on the path with the pails swinging, Wren opened the door all the way and let the valley pour in: the goats arguing politics in the north pasture (the old billy, Bracken, was losing ground to a young upstart named Sorrel, and everyone knew it, and Bracken pretended not to); the hens conducting their endless litigation; the stream muttering to the stones in its flat liquid syllables; the pines overhead groaning their slow wooden thoughts; three moles underground complaining about the wet; a spider in the woodshed narrating, in its tiny clicking way, the construction of something ambitious. The world, talking. All of it talking. None of it able to hear Wren unless Wren chose to speak, and Wren almost never chose, and so walking the path with full ears was like being the only sighted person in a city of the blind—powerful, and lonely, and sweet, and awful.

Halfway up the path, the crows changed the subject.

Crows were the valley's gossips and its alarm bells, and Wren had learned their dialect better than any other—sharp, ironic, obsessed with shiny things and boundaries. What they said now, from the tall pine at the bend, was: *Man. Man-man-man. Ridge. Ridge-ridge. Not-ours.*

Wren stopped. Set down the pails.

*Not-ours* was a specific crow-word, reserved for things that didn't belong to the valley's deep pattern, and the crows used it maybe twice a year, usually for a lost dog or a peddler's cart. But the tone now was wrong. The tone was the tone they used for hawks.

*How many?* Wren asked—carefully, silently, shaping the question in the crow-way, which was less words than pressures.

A pause. Crows counted badly past four, but they tried: *Many-many. Metal-smell. Dog-smell-no-dog. Coming-down-slow.*

Metal-smell. Wren looked up at the ridge line, the northern rim of the bowl of the valley, where the pines broke against the sky. From here it was a clean black-green edge, motionless, innocent. But the crows had been watching it longer than Wren had, and crows did not cry metal-smell for nothing.

For a long moment Wren stood in the path with the water going still in the pails and felt the valley humming its old hum underneath everything, steady as a heartbeat, and tried to imagine it stopping. Tried to imagine the wall coming down. It was like imagining the sky coming down. It was like imagining your own bones leaving your body.

*Nothing,* Wren told the crows, and the crows gave back the crow-equivalent of a shrug, and Wren picked up the pails and walked on, and did not run, because running was interesting, and interesting was death.

But that afternoon, Wren climbed to the north pasture to check the leaning fence post, and stayed longer than the post required, and watched the ridge until the light went long and blue.

And saw, just at dusk, just for a breath—a flicker on the ridgeline. Not a man. Not a shape. A shimmer, the way air shimmers over a forge, a bending of the light along the crest of the valley's northern rim, as if something had touched the sky there and the sky had flinched.

The wards, Wren thought. The wards just—

No. The wards were three hundred years old. The wards were bedrock. The wards were the reason Wren was alive and unwritten and bored and safe.

The wards had flickered.

Down in the valley, the evening bells rang at the chapel, and the goats came down to be milked, and lamps bloomed yellow in the windows of Hollowmere village, and everything looked exactly the way it had looked every evening of Wren's entire life: small, warm, complete, a painting of a place rather than a place. Wren stood at the fence with the post leaning against their shoulder and felt, for the first time, the true weight of the silence they'd been keeping—the weight of it as a physical thing, a lid, pressing down on the whole valley, holding everything in.

Somewhere on the ridge, men who smelled of metal were looking down at the lid.

From the pine at the pasture's edge, the crows spoke one more time, softly, the way you speak at a funeral:

*Coming-down-slow. Coming-down-slow. Tell-the-one-who-listens.*

Wren went very still.

*Tell the one who listens.* Not *tell-a-person*. Not *tell-the-farmers*. The crows had a name for Wren, then. Had probably always had a name. Had been watching Wren listen for years and years and years, filing it away in their sharp ironical minds, waiting for a night worth spending it.

The light died off the ridge. In the gathering dark, Wren Vane stood alone in the north pasture with a leaning fence post and a heartbeat like a war drum, and understood, with the perfect clarity that comes only at the exact moment everything changes, that the nothing days were over.
