## 2. Iron in the Warding

The crow came at first light, and it was screaming.

Not the ordinary morning racket of a crow—Wren knew every dialect of that, the squabbles over roadkill and the territorial complaints of nesting season. This was different. This was the sound crows made when fire came to the rookery. When hawks stooped out of a clear sky.

*Strangers,* the crow said, wheeling above the goat pen in tight, frantic circles. *Strangers on the ridge. Iron. IRON.*

Wren dropped the milking pail. It rang against a stone, and old Bramble—the nanny goat who had been born before Wren was and would likely go on long after—lifted her head from the trough and gave them a look of mild reproach, as if to say that whatever the sky was falling, it could fall after breakfast.

"Quiet," Wren breathed, though there was no one to hear but goats. There was never anyone to hear but goats. "How many?"

*Many. Walking. Slow like they own the ground.* The crow banked, feathers ragged with agitation. *Iron on their chests. Iron in their hands. The ridge tastes wrong.*

Wren's hands had gone cold around the pail handle. They set it down carefully, wiped their palms on their trousers, and did what they had done every day of their seventeen years when the world pressed too close: they looked toward the house, and they lied by omission to everyone they loved.

"I'm going to check the high pasture," they called through the open door, where their mother was kneading bread and their younger brother Tam was still asleep in the loft, one arm hanging down like a rope.

"The high pasture can wait for porridge," their mother called back.

"It can't. Bramble's favoring her left foreleg again."

A pause. Their mother's flour-white face appeared in the window, squinting. "You'll eat when you come back."

"I will," Wren said, which was true, because they intended very much to come back, and to be alive while doing it.

They took the crook and the dog—old Nettle, half-deaf, more blanket than animal—and walked up the lane at a pace that said *nothing is wrong*, and then, once the house dropped behind the rise, they ran.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in its valley like a coin at the bottom of a well. That was how Wren had always thought of it, though they'd never told anyone, because you didn't tell people things like that. You didn't tell people anything true about yourself in Hollowmere. The valley kept them, and they kept themselves, and between those two keepings there was no room left for honesty.

But Wren had always been able to see the valley from the inside, in a way no one else could. Not with eyes—with something older than eyes, something that lived behind the breastbone and woke whenever Wren stood near living things. They could feel Hollowmere the way you feel your own skin: the wards, the great slow breathing hush that lay over the valley like snowfall. Everyone in Hollowmere knew the valley was warded. It was the first thing children learned, along with their letters. *Safe within, forgotten without.* No magic crossed the boundary. No magic lived here. That was the whole point. That was why great-grandparents had come, generations back, fleeing the wars of gift and crown, and why they'd built their lives on the one thing the world outside couldn't abide: an absence.

What nobody said aloud—what Wren had only ever felt, alone, in the dark, ashamed—was that the ward wasn't just a wall. It was a seal. And seals were made to hold something in as much as keep something out.

Wren climbed the sheep track past the high pasture, past the lightning-split rowan that marked the edge of good grazing, and into the treeline where the birches grew thin and pale as bones. From there the ridge was visible: a long green shoulder of hill against the morning sky, and on it—

Wren's stomach turned over.

There were nine of them. Maybe ten; the light made it hard to count. They came down off the ridge in loose formation, unhurried, the way men walk when they have never once needed to hurry for anyone. Even at this distance Wren could see the grey of their cloaks and the dull gleam of the things they wore at their throats and carried on short poles before them like banners.

Iron charms.

Wren had seen iron charms exactly once before, in a peddler's tray, years ago—a little horseshoe-shaped pendant the man swore could cure toothache and repel ill luck. Wren had touched it and felt nothing but cold metal, and had thought, with relief, *see, it's all superstition.*

This was not that. These charms radiated wrongness the way a fever radiates heat. Even from two hundred yards, even through the trees, Wren's chest-hungry sense flinched away from them like a hand from a hot stove. The iron didn't just sit there. It *ate*. Wren could feel it eating—could feel it gnawing at the edges of the valley's hush the way rust eats the edge of a blade, slowly, patiently, leaving a brown crumble of nothing behind.

And beneath the wards, deep down where Wren had spent a lifetime not looking, something stirred.

It was faint. It was like hearing, through a wall, the breathing of someone you'd believed dead. But it was there, and it turned over in its sleep, and Wren clapped a hand over their mouth because for one horrible second they thought they might be sick, or sing, or both.

*Down,* they told themselves. *Down and quiet.*

Nettle whined and pressed against their leg. Even the half-deaf old dog could feel it.

* * *

The hunters reached the valley floor by midmorning, and Wren watched everything.

They watched Maren Ostler come out of her dairy with her hands still wet from the churn, and the way she stopped when she saw the grey cloaks, and the way her wet hands curled into fists at her sides and then, deliberately, uncurled again. They watched the tall hunter at the front—a woman with a voice that carried up the whole valley like it had been built for pulpits—speak to Maren, and watched Maren shake her head, and shake it again, and then point, horribly, vaguely, toward the center of the village.

They watched the hunters fan out. They watched them knock on doors Wren had known their whole life. The Coopers'. The Hedleys. Old Tammas Bright, who had taught Wren to fish and never once asked why the fish came to Wren's hook without bait.

And they heard—because the wind was kind, or cruel, and carried voices up the slope—the questions.

"—gifted ones. Persons of unnatural faculty. Beasts obeying them. Fires lighting at a word. Dreams that come true." The huntress's voice was almost gentle. That was the worst part. It was the voice you used on a frightened animal. "We are not here to punish, mistress. We are here to *protect*. A gifted one in a village is a spark in a haybarn. You must see that. Your king sees that."

"We're farmers," Maren said, loud enough to carry. "We grow turnips. Turnips don't need protecting from us."

A pause. Then laughter—not mocking, somehow worse, warm and reasonable—and the scrape of boots moving on.

Wren pressed their back against a birch and breathed.

*Turnips don't need protecting from us.* Good Maren. Brave, stupid, wonderful Maren. And it wouldn't matter. It hadn't ever mattered anywhere else—that was the thing the stories all agreed on, the stories that came in with peddlers and went out with the same, whispered low so the walls wouldn't hear. The hunters always found someone. In Carrow's Ford they'd found the miller's daughter who could quiet bees. In Dunmark they'd found three brothers who shared one dream between them. Nobody had ever confessed. Somebody always talked. A neighbor. A debt. A grudge. A child who didn't know better.

Wren thought of Tam, asleep in the loft, arm hanging down. Tam, who was seven and loud and loved his sibling with the whole uncomplicated engine of his heart. Tam, who had once announced at midsummer feast, aged five, that Wren could talk to the goats, and had been laughed at, and had laughed too, because it was a joke, obviously, a child's fancy—and had never mentioned it again, but had also never quite looked at Wren the same way since. Had never quite stopped watching, sometimes, when Wren was in the pen.

*He doesn't know,* Wren told themselves. *He thinks he knows, but he doesn't know.*

But knowing and being able to prove were different things, and the hunters had iron, and the iron was fraying the wards, and under the wards something was turning over in its sleep, and if it woke—

If it woke, everyone in Hollowmere would know exactly what slept in the Vane family.

* * *

Noon came. The hunters did not leave.

That was the thing Wren had counted on, in the secret ledger of fears they kept behind their ribs: the hunters would come, see a valley of mud and turnips, and move on. Hollowmere had survived four hundred years by being beneath notice. But these ones weren't leaving. They'd set up at the crossroads by the well, and they'd brought out instruments—flat boxes of dark wood, strung with wire, that hummed when the tall huntress walked them slowly around the square. Dowsing rods, Wren realized. Or something worse. Something that listened for the same thing Wren listened with.

And the wards were flickering.

Wren saw it plainly now, from the treeline, in the way they had never been able to see it before—because until today the wards had been seamless, a fact of the world, invisible as air. Now the iron had chewed holes in them, small ones, and through the holes Wren could see the ward itself: a vast slow lattice laid over the valley like frost on glass, silver-grey and ancient, and it was *flickering*, guttering like a candle in draft, and each time it guttered the thing beneath it rolled closer to waking.

Wren understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the ward had never been made by human hands alone.

You didn't build a wall like that out of stones and prayers. The foxes knew. The foxes had always known—Wren had heard it in their yipping talk for years and never let the meaning land, the way you don't let a heavy thing land when you're holding too much already. *The valley sleeps, the valley dreams, the valley was promised.* Promised. Covenanted. The word rose in Wren unbidden and unwelcome, from wherever words like that came from, and Wren shoved it back down.

Because if the ward was a covenant, then the ward was dying, and if the ward was dying, then the silence that had kept Wren safe for seventeen years was dying with it, and everything—the goats, the bread, Tam's arm hanging from the loft, the whole soft suffocating fabric of home—was woven out of that silence.

Below, the huntress raised her humming box, and it began to shriek.

She had stopped in front of the Vane gate.

Wren's whole body went to ice. Their mother was in the yard, beating a rug. Their mother, who had never once asked why the ewes calmed when Wren sang to them, who had never once asked why crows followed Wren from field to field like gossipy black shadows, who had chosen not-knowing the way other people chose religion, completely and without question. She stood in the yard with her rug-beater raised, and the huntress stood at the gate with her shrieking box, and between them lay ten feet of trampled mud and four hundred years of silence.

The box shrieked. The huntress tilted her head. She looked at the house. She looked at the goat pen. She looked, for a moment that lasted a year, directly up the slope—at the treeline, at the birches, at the place where Wren stood pressed against bark with a deaf old dog trembling against their knee.

She smiled.

Then she closed the box, and the shrieking stopped, and she turned back to her men and said something Wren couldn't hear, and they laughed, and moved on down the lane toward the Bright farm.

Wren stayed in the treeline until the sun went red behind the ridge. They stayed until the hunters made camp at the crossroads, pitching grey tents in a neat ring around the well, iron charms hung on poles like scarecrows for souls. They stayed until the lamps of Hollowmere came on one by one, and their mother's window glowed gold, and somewhere in that house their brother was asking questions over supper that their mother was answering too smoothly.

Then Nettle whined, low, and leaned harder against Wren's leg, and Wren realized they had been standing so long their feet had gone numb, and that the numbness went higher than the feet.

Under the flickering wards, the sleeping thing had opened one eye.

Wren felt it looking at them. Felt it the way you feel a stranger's gaze across a market crowd—impersonal, assessing, and utterly certain of what it sees. It was old. It was patient. And it was hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food.

*Come down,* it seemed to say, in a voice like roots shifting under a road. *Little speaker. Come down and wake me properly.*

Wren turned and walked home in the dark, taking the long way, avoiding the crossroads, and rehearsed lies for supper the whole way down: the high pasture, the fence posts, Bramble's leg. Easy lies. Practiced lies. Lies worn smooth as river stones from seventeen years of handling.

But at the gate, with the hunters' campfires glowing orange beyond the rooftops and the wards guttering overhead like a dying lamp, Wren paused with a hand on the cold latch, and heard—faint, far off, from the direction of the ridge—the foxes begin to scream.

Not hunting-scream. Warning-scream.

And underneath it, so low only Wren could possibly have caught it, the crow's voice, ragged and urgent, dropping out of the dark like a stone:

*They are asking for the Vane house. They are asking for the Vane house next.*
