## 3. What the Foxes Knew

The foxes came to Wren at dusk on the third day after the hunters arrived, and they were afraid.

Wren knew it before a single word crossed the gap between them — fear has a smell, even across the beast-speech, something like scorched fur and cold iron — but it was still a shock to see the vixen standing in the open doorway of the goat shed, ears flat, tail low, in full view of the house. Foxes did not come to doorways. Foxes did not ask.

*Little speaker,* the vixen said. Her voice was thin as winter grass. *You must come. The old one is dying.*

Wren's hands went still on the milking stool. Around them the goats shifted and muttered, sensing the change in the air the way they sensed weather. *Which old one?*

*The valley,* said a second voice, and the dog fox slid out of the shadow of the woodpile, and Wren's stomach dropped, because the dog fox never left the deep brush, never came near human smell, not once in all the years Wren had been listening. *The ward is dying. Come and see with your own eyes, if your eyes will believe what ours tell you.*

Inside the house, Wren's mother was kneading bread. Through the window her silhouette moved in the lamplight, steady and ordinary, flour to the elbows, humming the tuneless hum she always hummed. Wren looked at that silhouette for a long moment — long enough that the foxes stirred impatiently — and felt the old familiar weight settle over their shoulders like a yoke. If they slipped away now, they would have to lie about where they'd been. They were getting tired of lying. That was the trouble with a life built on silence: eventually you noticed you were the one being buried.

*I'll come,* Wren said. *But not past the tree line. Not tonight.*

*It will be too late for tree lines soon,* the dog fox said, and turned back toward the darkening hills without waiting to see if he was followed.

* * *

They went up through the birch stand behind the pasture, Wren moving fast with the particular silence their body had learned without being taught — heel first, breath shallow, a trick they'd once assumed every child knew until the day they'd realized no other child could hear the rabbits scolding them for stepping too loud. The foxes flowed ahead and beside, pale shapes in the blue dusk, and neither spoke again until they reached the ridge where Hollowmere fell away below them: forty rooftops, the millpond going silver, the fields stitched neat as a quilt. Home. The word caught in Wren's chest like a fishbone.

*Look,* the vixen said. *Not at the valley. At the edges of it.*

Wren looked, and at first saw nothing, because there was nothing to see — which was itself wrong. There should have been something. Every gifted child raised on whispered stories knew the shape of a ward: a shimmer like heat-haze, a faint sweet pressure against the mind, the sense of walking inside a held breath. Hollowmere's ward had been the oldest kind, laid down so long ago that nobody living remembered who had set it, woven into the valley's bones so thoroughly that most folk had stopped believing it was magic at all. It was just how Hollowmere was. Safe. Sealed. Forgotten by the world on purpose.

Now, at the valley's rim, Wren saw the places where the nothing had holes in it.

They were small — ragged little gaps in the shimmer, like moth-bites in wool — but they were there, six or seven of them that Wren could count from the ridge, and through each one leaked a thin thread of the outside world's noise: cart wheels, dogs barking, the far-off clank of metal that might have been anything and made Wren's skin crawl anyway.

*The iron men,* Wren said. *Their charms did this?*

The dog fox sat down on his haunches and regarded Wren with eyes the color of amber held up to firelight. *The iron men are rain on a rotten roof,* he said. *They did not make the rot. Little speaker, how old do you think this valley is?*

*Old. Grandmother said the wards were older than the parish records.*

*The wards are older than the parish. Older than the crown. Older than the word for crown.* He rose and paced along the ridgeline, and the vixen followed, and Wren followed them both, drawn despite every instinct that said go home, bar the door, be small. *Your people think they live in a valley that hides them. That is half true. But a hiding place is built by someone who wants to keep something safe. This—* he paused at a bare stretch of granite, and pressed his muzzle briefly against the stone, an oddly tender gesture — *this is a seal. Do you know the difference?*

Wren didn't answer. The wind moved through the birches above them, and somewhere far below a dog began to bark, and Wren waited, heart tapping quick against the ribs.

*A hiding place keeps the world out,* the dog fox said. *A seal keeps something in. Or—* his ears twitched — *keeps two things apart. Your valley was not warded against magic, little speaker. It was warded as part of magic. A knot tied long ago, when the old covenant was struck between your kind and ours. One thread of a great weaving. And threads, when the weaving fails, do not fail alone.*

*The covenant.* Wren had heard the word only in fragments — grandmother's stories, half-dreams, the hens muttering about *the old promise* when the moon was full. Nobody in Hollowmere used it aloud. *What covenant? What does it have to do with us?*

*Everything,* said the vixen simply. She had settled close against Wren's leg, and her warmth was startling, alive, real in a way the valley suddenly didn't feel. *You hear us. You have always heard us. Why do you think that is? Why do you think the gift lives in Hollowmere at all, in the one place in the world where magic sleeps? Gifts do not grow in dead ground, little speaker. Yours grows because the ground is not dead. Only sleeping. And sleeping things wake hungry, or they die.*

Wren crouched slowly, until they were eye level with the two foxes, until the whole valley hung below them like a lamp about to gutter out. *You're saying the ward is failing. Not because of the hunters. Because it was always going to fail.*

*The hunters are a symptom, like the holes are a symptom,* the dog fox said. *The sickness is older. The covenant that sealed this valley is unraveling, and when it goes, it will not go gently. Everything bound up in it — the sleep, the safety, the silences your family has kept for generations — all of it comes undone at once. And the world outside has already smelled the change. That is why the iron men came now. They did not find Hollowmere, little speaker. Hollowmere is becoming findable.*

The words landed in Wren like stones dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through everything they'd ever believed. All those years of hushed voice and hidden gift. All those years of believing the silence was a wall around their life, when all along it had been a thread in something vast — and the something vast was coming apart beneath them.

*How long?* Wren asked.

The foxes exchanged a look that carried whole paragraphs of meaning Wren couldn't read.

*Moons,* the vixen said at last. *Thirteen, perhaps fewer. Count them while you can.*

* * *

Wren meant to say nothing. That was the plan, the same plan as always: carry the knowledge home folded small, tuck it away in the secret room behind the ribs where the gift lived, let the valley go on pretending. Thirteen moons was a long time. Thirteen moons was enough time to do nothing, which was the only thing Hollowmere had ever taught anyone to do well.

But the house was wrong when Wren got back to it.

The lamp in the kitchen burned low, and their mother wasn't humming. She stood at the table with her back to the door, and on the table lay the good shawl, the one she wore to funerals, and beside it a satchel Wren had never seen before — canvas, oiled, packed tight, the buckles already done up.

She'd heard the door. Of course she had. She didn't turn around right away, and in that stretched moment Wren understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, that their mother had known. Not everything. But enough. Mothers counted bread and noticed when a son or daughter came home smelling of fox and birch bark and secrets. Mothers watched their children talk to goats with their mouths closed and filed it away in the place where mothers keep the things they cannot afford to know.

"Wren." Her voice was level. That was worse than shouting. "Sit down."

"I was just checking the—"

"Sit *down*."

Wren sat. The kitchen smelled of bread gone cold and lamp oil and, underneath it, fear — the human kind, sour and quiet. Their mother finally turned, and her face in the low light was one Wren barely recognized: not the careful, pleasant mask she wore at market, nor the tired softness of evenings, but something rawer. Something that had been awake for days, judging by the shadows under her eyes.

"The miller's boy came by," she said. "While you were out. He says the iron men took Tam Osgood's daughter for questioning. Tam's girl. She's fourteen, Wren. She stutters and she's frightened of geese and they took her because a neighbor said she could calm a spooked horse." Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the table might lift off otherwise. "They walked her through the village in chains so everyone would see what happens to people who are different."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Mama—"

"Don't." The word cracked down the middle. "Don't *mama* me. Don't you dare stand there with fox-smell on your coat and birch leaves in your hair and look at me like I don't know." She came around the table then, fast, and gripped Wren's shoulders, and her fingers were shaking. "I have known since you were six years old. Since I found you in the barn telling the cows about your bad dream, and the cows *listened*, Wren, they turned their heads and their eyes went soft, and I stood in the doorway and I chose — God help me, I chose — to walk away and say nothing. Because saying something meant it was real, and if it was real, they would come for you someday, and I thought — I thought if we were quiet enough, if we were *small* enough—"

Her voice broke entirely. She pressed her forehead against Wren's shoulder, and Wren stood rigid, flooded, undone. Six years old. All these years. Every careful dinner, every rehearsed lie, every night their mother had turned a blind, deliberate eye — it hadn't been ignorance. It had been a siege. Two people holding a fortress together with silence, and the walls had been crumbling the whole time.

"Mama, listen to me." Wren's own voice sounded strange, thick. "There's something happening. To the ward. The animals told me — the ward isn't just protection, it's part of something old, a covenant, and it's *dying*, and when it goes—"

"I know." Muffled against Wren's shoulder. "I've known that too. Longer than you'd believe." She pulled back, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a woman doing chores. "My mother told me. Her mother told her. We're not farmers who happen to have a gift, Wren. We're the last of a line that was *put here*. Planted. Sealed into this valley like a letter into an envelope, and the envelope is tearing, and when it tears open, everything inside gets spilled out into the light." She picked up the satchel and shoved it against Wren's chest. "So you run. Tonight. Over the ridge, north, before the thirteenth moon — my mother said thirteen moons, always thirteen moons, I thought it was a story—"

"Everyone keeps saying that number. What happens at thirteen?"

"I don't *know*!" It came out as almost a scream, strangled down to a whisper at the last instant, and she glanced at the window, at the dark, at the world with its listening ears. "Nobody knows. That's the point. That's why you *go*." She pushed the satchel harder; Wren's hands came up and held it without deciding to. It was heavier than it looked. Inside, Wren could feel the shapes of things: bread wrapped in cloth, a knife, a small pouch that clinked like coins. How long had she been packing it? Days. Weeks. Years, maybe, in the way that some preparations are really prayers.

"And you?" Wren asked. "I'm not leaving you here."

Something moved across her face — love and grief in equal measure, tangled past separating. "I was born inside the silence, my darling. I'll die inside it if I have to. But you—" her voice gentled, broke, gentled again — "you were never meant for it. I've watched you suffocate in this valley for twenty years and called it keeping you safe. It was never safe. It was a trap with soft walls, and I helped build it, and I am so sorry, Wren. I am sorry for every year I asked you to be smaller."

Wren wanted to argue. Wanted to say *we go together*, wanted to say *the foxes know more, maybe there's another way*, wanted to say any of the hundred things crowding their throat. But outside, far off on the road, a sound drifted up through the broken places in the ward — the creak of wagon wheels, slow and unhurried, and beneath it the thin metallic music of many small iron charms knocking together, like bells, like teeth.

Their mother heard it too. She went very still, then very brisk, the way she did when a ewe was lambing wrong and there was no time for feeling.

"Out the back," she whispered. "Through the birch stand. The foxes will help you — yes, I know about the foxes, Wren, I've known about the foxes." She pressed something into Wren's hand, small and warm: her wedding ring, the one she never removed. "Go north. Find whoever remembers the old stories. And whatever you do—"

The knock at the front door was polite. Three measured raps. Then a voice, smooth as oil on water:

"Open in the name of the Crown. We know the gift is under this roof."

Their mother's hands tightened on Wren's once, hard, a whole farewell compressed into a single grip — and then she turned toward the door, chin up, and Wren fled out the back into the dark, into the birches, into the arms of a world that had, in the space of one night, become enormous.
