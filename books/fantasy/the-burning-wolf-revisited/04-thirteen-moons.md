## 4. Thirteen Moons

The hedge-witch's cottage sat where the maples thinned, at the crook of the stream that marked the last boundary anyone in Hollowmere bothered to name. Wren had passed it a hundred times and never once seen smoke from its chimney, never heard a dog, never seen the old woman who lived there. The village children called it the empty house. Mothers said it to frighten them: *misbehave and the empty house will take you.*

Now Wren stood at its door with a fox's words still ringing in their ears—*the ward is dying, little speaker, and you are the only one who can hear it die*—and knocked.

The door opened before the second knock landed.

Old Maren was smaller than Wren had imagined, which was strange, because Wren had never imagined her at all. She was wrapped in grey wool gone soft with age, her white hair braided flat against her skull, and her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of river ice. She looked at Wren for a long moment.

"You're Vane's child," she said. "The quiet one."

"I need to know about the wards."

"Do you." She stepped back from the doorway. "Then come in before something sees you standing there."

* * *

Inside smelled of dried yarrow and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air in deep caves. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam. A kettle muttered on the fire. And along one whole wall, floor to rafters, stood jars—dozens of them, each holding a sliver of moonlight that shouldn't have been possible indoors, each glowing faintly silver, each flickering.

Flickering like guttering candles.

Wren stopped in the middle of the room. "What are those?"

"Moons." Maren said it the way another woman might say *spoons*. "Thirteen of them. Or what's left of thirteen." She lowered herself into a chair by the fire with the care of someone whose joints had opinions. "Sit down, child. This is a long story and your legs will want to give out halfway through."

Wren didn't sit. "The foxes told me the ward was dying. They said it was a covenant-seal. They said—" Wren's voice caught. "They said I could hear it because I'm part of it."

Maren studied them over the kettle steam. "Foxes always did talk too much." She poured two cups of something dark and bitter-smelling and pushed one across the table. "Drink. It tastes like punishment but it will keep your hands steady."

Wren drank. It tasted like punishment.

"The valley wasn't warded by any spell," Maren began. "Not the way your schoolmaster would understand a spell—chalk and words and done by supper. Hollowmere sits inside a covenant. An agreement. Made long ago between the people who settled here and the wild magic itself. Your ancestors said: we will live quietly, we will not reach for power, and in exchange the magic will hide us. Wrap us in forgetting. Keep our gifted children safe from a world that burns them."

"And the moons?"

"Thirteen moons," Maren said. "That's what the covenant is made of. Thirteen vows, sworn under thirteen moons, woven together into one seal. Every vow has a moon. Every moon holds a vow." She nodded toward the wall of jars. "I've been keeping what I can of them alive. Bottled moonlight. Bandages on a wound."

Wren looked again at the jars and saw it now—the way some glowed strong and full, and others had dimmed to almost nothing, and one near the top had gone dark as a closed eye.

"How many are dead?"

"Four." Maren's voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly, around her cup. "A fifth is failing. When the thirteenth goes out, the covenant fails entire. The ward drops. The wild magic is severed from this world—pushed beyond the veil, unreachable, forever. And everyone born with the gift..." She looked at Wren with those ice-pale eyes. "Everyone like you goes silent. Or worse. The gift doesn't just leave, child. It tears coming out. Some survive it as hollowed things. Some don't survive it at all."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a crow called—one sharp note—and Wren flinched hard enough to slosh tea over their knuckles.

"How long?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Maren rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the wall of jars. She touched the dark one gently, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "When I was young, all thirteen burned bright. The first went out the winter I turned forty. The second, nine years after. They're going faster now. Months apart instead of years." She turned. "By my counting, thirteen moons remain. One lunar cycle for each dying vow. Then it's done."

"Thirteen moons," Wren repeated. It didn't sound real. Thirteen moons was a season and a half. Thirteen moons was until harvest. Thirteen moons was nothing.

"The witch-hunters felt it," Maren said. "That's why they've come now, after generations of never finding us. The seal is thinning. Power leaks through cracks, and men with iron charms can smell a leak from a hundred miles. They're not hunting you, child. They're hunting the wound. And you're standing in it."

Wren set down the cup because their hands had stopped being reliable. "My mother—"

"Knows?" Maren raised an eyebrow. "She carried you, didn't she? Mothers know more than they say. It's how they survive us."

* * *

Wren walked home through the dusk with the fox's voice and the witch's voice tangled together in their head, and found the house lit and wrong.

Their mother was standing in the yard, still in her work dress, arms wrapped around herself against the cold. Their father was not home yet from the low fields. And their mother's face—

Their mother's face was the face of someone who had been crying and had decided, with tremendous effort, to stop.

"Wren." Her voice came out steady. Too steady. "Come inside."

"Mama—"

"*Inside.*"

The kitchen was warm and smelled of bread that hadn't been finished. Their mother shut the door, latched it, and then stood with her hand on the latch as if making sure it would hold.

"I heard you," she said. "Last night. With the ewe."

The floor tilted. Wren gripped the edge of the table. "Mama, I—"

"I heard you talking to her, and I heard her *answer* you." Their mother turned around. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry now, fierce now. "Do you know what I did after? I sat on the end of your bed for an hour watching you sleep, trying to convince myself I'd dreamed it. And then I got up and I packed you a bag."

She pointed to the corner. A canvas pack sat there, stuffed full—bread, cheese, a flint, a heavy cloak, a coil of good rope. Packed with the efficiency of a woman who had spent the night doing the hardest work of her life.

"You knew," Wren whispered. "You always knew."

"I suspected. From when you were small. The way animals came to you. The way you'd go quiet and stare at nothing and the goats would all turn to look at the same patch of empty air." Their mother's jaw tightened. "I told myself it was nothing. I told myself the ward would hold, the way it held for your grandmother, the way it held for everyone. I chose the comfort over the truth, Wren, and I am not asking you to forgive me for that, because I haven't forgiven myself."

"Hollowmere is safe. The ward—"

"The ward is *failing*, Wren." The words cracked out of her like whips. "Old Maren sent word this afternoon—a boy, a note, a warning. Hunters on the north road by tomorrow. Maybe sooner. And when they come through, they'll do what they always do. They'll ask questions. They'll bring out the iron. And they'll find whatever's left to find." She crossed the kitchen in three strides and took Wren's face in both her hands, calloused palms, trembling fingers. "You cannot be here when they do."

"I can't leave. This is—everything I am is here. You're here."

"And if you stay, they will *burn you*." Her voice broke on the last word and she let it. "I have spent your whole life teaching you to be small and silent, and I thought I was keeping you alive, and maybe I was, but I was also teaching you to stand in a burning house and call it home." She pressed her forehead to Wren's. "Go over the ridge. Tonight. Before dawn. Follow the stream past the old mill and don't stop until the valley is a memory."

"Come with me."

"I can't." She said it fast, before Wren could beg. "Your father. The farm. If we both vanish they'll tear this valley apart looking, and every neighbor who ever covered for us pays for it. Someone has to stay and be ordinary. Someone has to be the reason no one looks twice." She pulled back and looked at Wren the way she used to when Wren was small and feverish—as if sheer force of love could burn the sickness out. "Listen to me. Whatever you are—the thing you talk to beasts with, the thing you've hated and hidden—it isn't a curse. Do you hear me? It isn't a sin. It's yours. It's the only thing that's truly yours, and someday, somewhere, you're going to stop being ashamed of it."

Outside, far off, a dog began to bark—not the lazy evening bark of a farm dog, but the sharp, rhythmic alarm of an animal that had smelled strangers.

Their mother went very still.

"That's the Hedley's dog," she breathed. "He only barks like that at the north road."

* * *

There was no time for goodbye after that. There was barely time for the pack, for the cloak, for one crushing embrace at the back door that Wren would carry in their ribs for the rest of their life.

"Stream path," their mother whispered into their hair. "Past the mill. Don't look back. Don't—"

"Mama."

"Don't *look back*, Wren."

Wren looked back. Of course they did—from the treeline above the pasture, one glance, one last image: their mother small in the doorway, lamplight behind her, one hand raised, not waving, just raised, like a promise or a surrender.

Then the dark took the valley, and Wren ran.

The stream path was a childhood memory made mud—every stone known, every bend anticipated—but Wren had never run it at night, never run it carrying everything they owned on their back, never run it while the dogs of Hollowmere barked one after another down the valley like a line of falling dominoes. Something was moving up the north road. Something with lanterns and iron.

At the old mill the fox was waiting.

It sat on the fallen lintel, eyes catching the starlight, tail curled neat around its feet. *Little speaker,* it said, in that dry rustling voice inside Wren's skull. *You're leaving.*

"I have to."

*Mm.* The fox tilted its head. *The ridge path forks above the birches. Take the left fork, though the right looks easier. The right is watched.* It stood, stretched, unhurried. *The ward is thinner up high. You'll feel it. Like walking out of a warm house into snow.*

"Will I ever come back?"

The fox considered this with the frankness of its kind. *Everything comes back. What matters is what it comes back as.* It bounded three steps down the slope, paused, looked over its shoulder. *Run well, little speaker. The mountain is listening tonight.*

And it was gone between the stones.

Wren climbed. The birches gave way to pine, the pine to scree, and somewhere in the scree it happened—the thing the fox had promised. It came as a silence. All Wren's life, even without knowing it, they had lived inside a hum, a low constant murmur at the edge of hearing, the valley's ten thousand small voices layered into one drowsy drone: worms in the soil, moths in the grass, the slow green thoughts of barley. Wren had never noticed it because it had never stopped.

Now it stopped.

One step, and the world went mute. Wren staggered against a boulder, gasping, deaf in a way that had nothing to do with ears. The valley below was a bowl of darkness and lamplight and barking dogs, and it said *nothing* to them. Nothing at all. For the first time in their life, Wren Vane was utterly, completely alone in the world.

They stood on the ridge with their heart slamming and the wind pulling at their cloak, and they waited for the terror to swallow them.

It didn't. Not entirely. Underneath the terror, small and shocking as a spring flower through frost, something else was growing.

No walls. No ward. No mother's careful lessons, no village's careful silence, no lifetime of *smaller, quieter, don't let them see*. Just the open dark, and the mountains going on forever, and a gift that was theirs—only theirs—unhidden, unashamed, unbound.

Terrifying. Terrifying and free.

Wren laughed—a raw, cracked, half-sob of a sound that the wind took and scattered—and turned away from the valley, away from everything, and started down the far side as the sky behind them paled toward dawn.

They were three switchbacks down when the crow found them, dropping out of the greying sky to land on a stone ahead, feathers ruffled, breath ragged from hard flying.

*They're in the valley,* the crow said, without preamble. *Iron men. Six of them, at the Vane farm, asking your mother questions with iron in their hands.* Its black head cocked. *She told them nothing, little speaker. But they took your father anyway—to make the family talk.*

The world narrowed to a single point of cold light.

*And there's more,* the crow went on, hopping closer, and its voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing about it. *On the far slope, in the deep pines—something is following you. Big. Grey. It's been behind you since the mill.*

Wren turned slowly to face the treeline.

Between two dark trunks, two eyes caught the first light of dawn—and held it, gold and ancient and unblinking.
