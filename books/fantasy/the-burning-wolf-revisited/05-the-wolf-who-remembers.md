## 5. The Wolf Who Remembers

Three days past the ridge, Wren had learned the forest's first lesson: it did not want them.

Not cruelly, the way hunters wanted them. The forest simply went on being itself—indifferent, enormous, and full of voices Wren could not stop hearing. Every mile of the deep wood pressed in with the chatter of squirrels arguing over acorn caches, the low grief-murmur of a doe who'd lost a fawn to winter, the endless gossiping complaint of crows. In Hollowmere, the gift had been a whisper under silence. Out here it was a flood.

Wren walked with their hood up and their ears ringing, following the fox's parting directions north by northwest, toward "the old teeth of the mountain," whatever that meant. They ate what they could find. They slept badly. And every night they checked the southern sky for the glow of torches, and every night it was there, faint as breath on glass.

The valley was burning its own wards now. Or the hunters were. Either way, home was a word that belonged to someone else.

On the fourth day, the voices stopped.

It happened between one step and the next. One moment the forest roared; the next, nothing—a silence so total Wren stumbled, hand out for a tree that wasn't there. Not silence like an empty room. Silence like held breath. Like every living thing within a mile had agreed at once to shut its mouth.

Wren stood very still. *Something is coming,* said the animal part of their brain, the part older than speech. *Something big.*

The trees ahead were old-growth, black-trunked, their canopy so dense the ground grew only moss and shadow. Wren watched the shadow, and the shadow watched back.

He came out of it without sound, which was wrong. A wolf that size should have announced himself to the county. He was grey the way storms are grey—not one color but many, silver over charcoal over the dark of rain coming—and he was large in the way that made Wren's hindbrain scream *run* while some deeper part whispered *too late.* His eyes were amber, and they did not look at Wren the way animals looked at people. Animals looked through you, or around you, or at your hands and what they might hold.

This wolf looked *at* Wren. Measured. Remembered.

"Vane," he said.

Wren's legs nearly folded. Not from fear—from the sheer wrongness of it. Because this wasn't the gift. The gift was a listening, a leaning-in, a catching of meaning on the edges of sound. This was a voice arriving whole, in words, pitched for human ears, from a throat built for howling. It bypassed everything Wren understood about their own power and landed somewhere beneath the ribs.

"You—" Wren's voice cracked. They tried again. "You can't speak."

"I can," the wolf agreed. "That has always been the difficulty." He circled slowly, and Wren turned with him, unwilling to let him at their back. "You hear us. That's your line's inheritance, passed down like a crooked nose. But I am not heard, Vane. I *speak.* Do you understand the difference?"

"No," Wren admitted. Their heart was going like a rabbit's. "Who are you? How do you know my name?"

"The foxes talk too much. It's why no one trusts them." The wolf sat, folding his haunches with terrible elegance, and regarded Wren across four feet of dark moss. "As for my name—the ones who swore the covenant called me Ashfall. The ones who broke it called me other things. You may use Ashfall. It's shorter than most of them."

*The covenant.* The hedge-witch's word, spoken over her dying fire: *thirteen moons, child, and the pact fails, and everything wild goes dark or mad.* Wren's mouth went dry. "You were there. When it was sworn."

"I was there when it was *made.* Swearing came later, when it needed maintaining." Something moved behind the amber eyes—old weather, old grief. "I have kept my half of it for a very long time. Longer than your valley. Longer than your wards, which, incidentally, are dead now, so you can stop flinching every time you think of home. Dead things don't need flinching at."

"They weren't just wards." Wren heard their own voice go flat and hard, the way it did when they were about to cry and refused. "The foxes said—they said it was a seal. A piece of the covenant. My whole life was inside a dying promise and nobody told me."

"And now you're outside it, in the woods, hunted, hearing voices, talking to a wolf." Ashfall's ears tilted. "Yes. Your situation is tragic. Sit down before you fall down."

Wren sat. It seemed wise. Their knees had opinions of their own.

* * *

They talked until the light went copper and then grey. Or rather, Ashfall talked, and Wren asked questions in the ragged way of someone drinking after a long thirst.

He told them things the hedge-witch had only gestured at. That the covenant was not a wall but a *vow*—an agreement struck between the first gifted humans and the elder beasts, that magic would flow between the worlds like water between two banks, each shaping the other, neither drowning. That the Moonstone Altar was where the vow lived, renewed each generation by hands that carried both blood-rights. That somewhere in the long centuries, the renewals had stopped, and the vow had been left to starve, and the wards—little local promises, village-sized knots of the great vow—had begun failing one by one, like lamps going out along a road.

"Hollowmere was one of the last lamps," Ashfall said. "Your ancestors didn't build it to hide you. They built it because hiding was all they knew how to do anymore. Hiding instead of keeping. There's a difference, and it has cost everyone dearly."

"My mother kept me hidden my whole life," Wren said. "She never told me any of this."

"She likely doesn't know it. Ignorance is also inherited." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "But here is what matters, Vane, and attend closely, because I will say it once. The thirteenth moon rises soon. When it wanes, the covenant fails entirely. The wild magic doesn't vanish—it *severs.* Everything gifted burns out or breaks. Every beast bound to the old tongue goes silent or mad. And the door between the worlds shuts so hard it cracks both frames." He rose, shook his coat once, and the shadows around him seemed to shiver. "I have watched this failure approach for three hundred years. I would prefer, on balance, to stop it."

"So stop it," Wren said. "You're—you're *Ashfall.* You swore the thing. Fix it yourself."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh, if laughter had teeth and fur. "The vow must be renewed by one who holds both blood-rights. Human gift and wild blood, freely given, freely joined. I hold the wild half. I have held it since before your language had a word for *wolf.*" His gaze pinned Wren like a moth to board. "I do not hold yours."

Understanding arrived slowly, coldly, like water seeping into boots. "You've been waiting. For someone like me."

"For someone exactly like you. The last of the speaking line who isn't yet burned, broken, or caged. The hunters have been efficient. You are, Vane, the last lamp still lit." He began to walk, unhurried, into the darkening trees, and paused. "I can teach you what the covenant was. What the rites were. What your gift actually *is*, which is not what you think—it is not commanding, whatever you've used it for. Command is a corruption. Listening is the root. But teaching costs me, and I am old, and old things do not spend themselves for free."

"What's your price?" Wren asked, though some instinct already knew.

"Truthfulness." Ashfall turned his head, and the last light caught his eyes and set them burning. "Between us, always, in all things. You will not lie to me, embellish to me, or withhold from me anything I directly ask. In return I will not lie to you—which, I warn you, will occasionally be worse. Most bargains trade gold or service. Mine trades the one thing hunted folk can least afford to give." He waited. "Because the covenant cannot be renewed by a liar. A vow is a truth given shape. If you come to that altar carrying falsehood, the stone will know, and the moon will know, and it will fail, and we will all die politely together. So. Truthfulness. Now, tonight, henceforth. Do you accept?"

The forest held its breath again. Somewhere far off, thunder that wasn't thunder rolled—the wards of some distant valley, perhaps, guttering out. Wren thought of their mother's face in the lamplight, saying *run.* Thought of the crow on the ridgepost. Thought of every lie they'd ever told to stay safe, the small daily murders of concealment, and how heavy the habit sat on the chest.

To be unable to lie. To someone who could smell fear at a mile and had three hundred years of patience.

"Yes," Wren said. And meant it, which was fortunate, because they suspected he'd have known otherwise.

"Good." Ashfall turned and loped two strides into the dark, then stopped, looking back. "Then ask me the question you've been swallowing since I spoke. The one sitting behind your teeth like a stone."

Wren's stomach dropped. Truthfulness cut both ways, apparently, starting immediately. "How do I know you're telling me the truth? About any of it? The covenant, the altar, the thirteenth moon. You could be anything. Luring me anywhere. Wolves in stories don't offer help. They offer dinner."

"Mm." The wolf considered this with what appeared to be genuine appreciation. "A fair question, honestly asked. Here is your honest answer: you don't know. Trust is not knowledge, Vane. It is a wager placed without guarantee—that is the whole of its nature. I will tell you only this." He stepped closer, close enough that Wren could see the individual hairs silvered at his muzzle, could feel heat rolling off him like a banked fire. "In three hundred years, I have eaten many things. I have never once eaten a speaker. We do not devour our own side of the vow." His muzzle split slightly, not quite a smile. "Also, if I wanted you dead, you would be dead already, and considerably more relaxed about it."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't offered as comfort. It was offered as truth. You'll learn the difference." He turned away. "Sleep. Tomorrow we walk north, toward the old teeth of the mountain, and I begin teaching you what your gift is for. And Vane—" the amber eyes slid back one last time, catching the first stars, "—sleep lightly. We are not the only ones hunting the last lamp. I smelled iron on the wind an hour ago. Crown hunters move faster than grief."

He vanished into the dark between the trees, soundless as snowfall, leaving Wren alone with a hammering heart, a bargain freshly signed in truth, and the sudden, vertiginous knowledge that for the first time in their life they were neither hidden nor alone nor in control of a single blessed thing.

Somewhere south, iron jingled faintly against leather, drawing nearer through the night.

Wren pulled their cloak tight, lay down in the moss with their back to a stone, and did not sleep at all.
