## 6. A Huntress Disgraced

The rain had followed Sera Kell for three days, as if the sky itself had opinions about where she was going.

She didn't mind it much. Rain softened tracks, blurred scent, made a woman in a grey oilskin cloak just another shape moving through a wet world. What she minded was the company she couldn't shake: the memory of a boy's face, eleven years old, freckled across the nose, looking up at her from the mud of a farmyard in Corrow Fen while she held a drawn blade and a writ that said his life was forfeit.

*Gifted one,* the writ said. *By order of His Majesty's Witch-Finders, to be rendered unto the Crown or destroyed.*

He hadn't run. That was the worst of it. He'd stood there with a sparrow perched on his shoulder—a wild sparrow, calm as a tame thing—and he'd looked at her like she was a person who might still choose.

She had chosen. She'd turned the blade aside, struck him just hard enough to bloody his lip, and shouted to her patrol that the gift-bearer had escaped into the reeds. Then she'd walked away from the finest commission of her career and let them strip her sash in front of the garrison at Greyhollow.

They'd called it mercy once, before they called it treason. Now they just called it *Kell*, the way you'd name a disease.

She pulled her hood lower and kept walking north, toward Hollowmere.

---

She'd heard the stories in the border taverns, told low over cups by men who thought a disgraced huntress was safe to talk around. The wards of Hollowmere were failing. Iron-bearers had been seen on the ridge road. And somewhere in that valley, if the whispers could be believed, was a gift-bearer young enough to be saved and stubborn enough not to want saving.

Sera knew what the Crown would do with such a one. She'd watched it done a dozen times before she stopped watching. The collars first—cold iron worked with sigils that ate at the nerves until the gift went quiet and the bearer went quiet with it, smiling at nothing, agreeing with everything. Then the questioning. Then, if the answers pleased no one, the pyre.

She also knew what she would have done, three years ago, before Corrow Fen. She'd have tracked this Wren Vane through the wards, taken them alive, delivered them to Greyhollow with a clean report and a promotion. She'd been good at it. That was the rot of it—she'd been *good*.

So now she walked north in the rain to do the only penance that had ever made sense to her: stand between the hunters and the hunted, once, properly, with no lies to hide behind.

If the gifted one refused her help—if they ran, if they spat, if they set some beast on her—she supposed she'd earned that too.

* * *

She found the patrol on the second morning, and it nearly ended her.

There were six of them, moving up the ridge road below the old ward-stones in loose formation, and they moved wrong. Royal hunters were trained to move like water, quiet and patient, reading a landscape before entering it. These six moved like men in a hurry, boots loud on wet stone, iron charms swinging from their belts and staffs in plain view—not concealed, but *displayed*, the way you'd carry a torch into a dark room to show it who owned the dark.

Their sergeant was a broad man with a burn-scarred jaw whom Sera recognized distantly: Doss Merren, once third-rank under her own command, promoted twice since her fall. He'd been a decent soldier. Decent soldiers made the worst witch-hunters, because they never questioned an order long enough to be ruined by it.

She lay flat in the bracken above the road and counted their gear. Six men, two hounds—no, one hound and something else on a lead, a lean grey shape that wasn't quite dog. A tracker-beast, then. Crossbred with something out of the deep woods. The Crown had started breeding them two years back, after someone in the College realized that beasts could smell a gift even when men couldn't.

That changed her arithmetic entirely. A tracker-beast would find Wren Vane whether or not the valley's dying wards gave any cover. It would find them today, maybe within the hour, because the beast's nose was already working the air and its hackles were up.

Sera did the thing she'd sworn she wouldn't do again, which was decide another person's fate without asking them. But there was no time for asking, and besides, she hadn't come all this way to watch a child be collared from a comfortable distance.

She began to work out how to break a six-man patrol alone, armed with a shortbow, a hunting knife, and a reputation.

* * *

The answer came from the valley itself, and it nearly broke her heart with relief.

The goats came first—a dozen of them boiling down the hillside in a panic, bleating, scattering the patrol's left flank. Then the hound—the false hound, the tracker-thing—went mad, lunging at its lead toward the treeline, and one of the hunters shouted something about *the gifted one, there, in the trees*, and Sera saw them.

A slight figure in a brown coat, half-hidden by an alder, frozen in the open the way prey freezes when it knows running is death. Young. Younger than she'd expected. Watching the patrol with the wide, terrible stillness of someone whose whole life has just caught fire.

Doss Merren raised his crossbow.

Sera had perhaps four seconds and one shot, and she took it without letting herself think, because thinking was how you talked yourself into watching.

Her arrow took the crossbow's stock, not the man—she was good enough to choose, even now, even shaking—and the weapon spun from Merren's hands with a crack of split wood. The patrol turned as one toward the sound, and Sera stood up out of the bracken with her hood thrown back so they could see her face, because there was exactly one card she held worth playing.

"Hold!" she shouted, in the parade-ground voice they'd drilled into her at Greyhollow, the voice that had commanded forty men. "Royal warrant! Kell, former First Huntress, acting under sealed commission! Drop your lines and form on me!"

It shouldn't have worked. Her face was known, her disgrace was known, and any sergeant with a spine would have called the bluff. But Merren had served under her; Merren had seen her break a boar charge with a shout; and for two full seconds—the seconds that mattered—six trained men hesitated because the voice belonged to someone they'd been taught to obey.

Two seconds was enough.

"Run," Sera said, without turning her head, to the figure under the alder. "Now. Don't look back."

The figure ran. And the moment broke—Merren's eyes narrowed, someone shouted *that's Kell, that's the traitor*—and the world became very fast and very simple.

She put two arrows into the space in front of the advancing line, close enough to spit gravel at their boots, and backed away down the slope while they wavered between pursuit and prudence. The tracker-beast screamed—that high, wrong, almost-human scream—and strained toward the fleeing figure, and Sera made the choice she'd have despised herself for not making: she loosed at the lead, cut it, and set the thing loose into the hills rather than let it run Wren Vane down.

Then she ran too, downhill, through wet bracken that tore at her sleeves, following the flash of a brown coat through the alders, hearing behind her the shouts thinning out as the patrol chose to regroup rather than chase a disgraced huntress into terrain they didn't know.

She caught up at a stream-cut, where the ground dropped away into a stony ravine, and found Wren Vane cornered against the rock with nowhere left to go and a shepherd's crook raised like a spear.

"Don't." The voice cracked on it. "Don't come near me."

"I'm not with them." Sera stopped, hands open and empty, bow slung. Rain ran off her chin. "I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds."

"You shot at them." Wren's eyes flicked to her belt, to the royal sigil still stamped on the buckle because she'd never bothered to replace it. "You're wearing their mark."

"It's the last thing I own from them, and I keep it because forgetting what I was is how people like me forgive ourselves." She crouched slowly, making herself small against the rain-grey stone. "My name is Sera Kell. Three years ago I was ordered to kill a gifted boy in Corrow Fen, and I refused, and they stripped my rank and named me traitor. I've spent every day since trying to be worth the refusing. I tracked you for a week to warn you, not to take you. And ten minutes ago I burned the last bridge I had left, because those men will report my face and my name by nightfall, and from tomorrow I'm hunted beside you whether you trust me or not."

Silence, except the stream and the rain. Wren hadn't lowered the crook. Their chest heaved; there was burr-stuck wool and goat-smell about them, and under the fear something fierce and assessing that reminded Sera, painfully, of the sparrow-boy in the fen.

"Why?" Wren said at last. "Why would you throw your life away for someone you've never met?"

Because guilt is a hunger that eats everything except itself, Sera thought. Because I have slept nine hundred nights in the shape of a boy I didn't kill, and none of them were sleep. Because somewhere in the world there is always another crossbow coming up, and I cannot stop all of them, but I can stop this one.

"Because I've seen what they do to the gifted," she said, "and I decided I'd rather die in the way than live on the side of it."

Wren searched her face for a long moment. Whatever they found there, it didn't comfort them—they lowered the crook by an inch, no more, and their voice when it came was flat and cold as the stream.

"The wolf says you're lying."

Sera went very still. "The wolf."

"The one that's been standing behind you for the past minute." Wren's chin lifted toward the ravine rim above her shoulder. "The one that's been following me since the ridge. The one that talks."

Sera turned her head, slow and careful, the way she'd turn toward anything in the wild that might be the last thing she ever saw.

On the rocks above the stream-cut, rain beading on a pelt the colour of ash and stormcloud, sat a grey wolf the size of a yearling calf, watching her with pale gold eyes that held nothing whatsoever of animal blankness in them.

"*Liar,*" the wolf agreed, in a voice like wind through a hollow bone. "*Or truth-teller. The two wear the same coat, little huntress. I have watched both pass by in it.*"

Sera Kell had faced boars, bears, blizzards, and the Witch-Finder General himself across a tribunal table, and had never once felt the ground tilt under her.

She felt it tilt now.

"Well," she managed, and heard her own voice come out younger than she'd meant it to. "I suppose that settles whether you're gifted."

The wolf's mouth opened—not quite a growl, not quite a grin. "*Settle? Nothing is settled. The moon is cracking, the wards are dying, and iron walks your roads. Come or don't, huntress. But decide quickly. The patrol behind you has found its courage, and the thirteenth moon waits for no one's permission.*"

Behind them, far up the slope, a horn sounded—twice, then twice again. The signal for *quarry sighted, converge.*

Wren looked at Sera. Sera looked at Wren. Between them, the wolf waited with the patience of something very old.

"Trust you?" Wren said quietly. "I don't. But it seems we're about to find out if we can afford each other anyway."

Sera shouldered her bow and turned uphill, toward the horn-calls, toward everything coming for them both.

"That," she said, "is more trust than I deserve. Try to keep some of it in reserve. You'll want it later—I promise you'll hate me before this is over."

And the three of them ran, together, into the deepening rain.
