## 7. Road of Ashes

**Wren**

The first burned village smelled like Hollowmere's hearth-smoke, and that was the worst of it.

Wren had expected something foreign—something to match the horror of it. Instead the wind carried woodsmoke and wet ash and, underneath, the ordinary sweetness of a kitchen garden gone untended. Cabbages still growing in their rows beside a house whose roof had fallen in. A well with its bucket still tied on. Life interrupted mid-sentence.

They came down out of the trees at dusk, keeping to the hedgerows, because Sera insisted on hedgerows even when there was nothing left to hide from. The village was called Brant's Ford—or had been; a sign at the crossroads still said so, the paint blistered but legible. Maybe forty structures. Maybe thirty of them blackened skeletons now, their beams charred down to white-grey bones that crumbled when Wren brushed past.

"Three weeks," Sera said. She crouched by a doorstep, touching the ash without rubbing it between her fingers, which told Wren she'd done this before. "Maybe four. Rain's kept it soft." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather.

Wren didn't answer. They were listening.

Not with ears—with the other thing, the thing they'd spent twenty years strangling quiet. In Hollowmere it had been a whisper always, goats and crows and the shy voices of field-mice, easy to pretend away. Here it was a roar. Every living thing for miles was talking at once: rats in the ruins, rooks arguing over the chimneys, a fox somewhere upslope complaining about the smell. And underneath all of it, threaded through everything—

Nothing. A silence shaped like people.

"No one's here," Wren said. Their voice came out strange. "I mean—no *people*. There are animals. But no one human."

Sera looked up at them sharply. "You can tell that?"

"The rooks would be nervous if there were. And the rats—" Wren stopped. The rats were fat and unbothered, and they didn't want to think about what that meant either.

Ashfall moved ahead of them both, grey as the smoke, nose low. The wolf had barely spoken since the ridge, since the horns, since the running. He walked through the ruined village like a king through a conquered province, and where he passed the rooks went silent—not afraid, Wren realized. *Attentive.* Like soldiers seeing an old banner go by.

"There," Ashfall said, and nodded toward the church.

It was the only building still whole, and only barely—the door scorched, half its shingles gone—but someone had made an effort. Someone had nailed planks over the broken windows and swept the step. On the door, in paint that hadn't finished drying three weeks ago, someone had drawn a circle, and inside the circle a crescent moon.

"The Moon Court," Sera said quietly.

"You know them."

"I know *of* them." She stood, brushing ash from her knees. "Rumors since spring. Gifted folk who ran instead of burning. Some say they're organized. Some say they're a fairy story the crown invented so it could hunt harder." Her mouth twisted. "Some say worse things about me, so I try not to judge."

Wren touched the crescent moon. The paint flaked under their thumb. Below it, scratched small and hurried into the wood, someone had written words in a hand shaking with cold or fear:

*Gone north to the gathering. If you hear the beasts speak, follow the beasts.*

They stood a long moment in the doorway while the rain started again, soft on the ashes, and Wren thought: *someone wrote this for me. Someone I've never met knew I might come, and left a door open in a burned world.*

It was the first kind thing the road had shown them.

"We don't sleep in the open houses," Sera said. "We sleep in the church, backs to the wall, fire in the corner if we dare one. And we're moving before light."

"Why before light?"

"Because whoever burned this place will send someone back to count the dead." Sera's eyes found theirs in the gloom. "The crown doesn't purge and forget, Wren. It purges and *audits*."

* * *

**Sera**

She took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep, and because watching was the only prayer she knew anymore.

The church smelled of wet stone and old incense and, faintly, of the people who'd sheltered here last—a family, by the leavings. Three bedrolls' worth of flattened straw. A child's wooden horse, forgotten or abandoned or left deliberately, the way you leave things you can't carry and can't bear to burn. Sera turned it over in her hands. One ear chewed round, the way a teething child does it.

She put it down carefully, exactly where it had been, and hated herself for the care of it.

Across the dark nave, Wren slept curled against Ashfall's flank like it was the most natural thing in the world, and maybe for them it was. Sera watched the rise and fall of the boy's—of *their* breathing, because she still didn't know what to call Wren Vane in her head. Quarry? Charge? Penance?

She'd tracked gifted ones for six years. She knew the signs: the way they moved among animals like the animals had agreed to it, the way they flinched at raised voices, the way they watched your hands instead of your eyes because hands were where the iron lived. Wren had all of it. And under it, something Sera had never seen in six years of hunting: anger that wasn't fear. Wren looked at the burned village the way Sera imagined a person looks at a debt they've just learned is theirs to pay.

The wolf opened one eye. Amber, catching the banked coals' glow.

"You watch her like a woman counting sins," Ashfall said. His voice was a rumble that didn't move the air so much as settle into it.

"She's not a her. Not a him either. I asked."

"And?"

"They said I could work it out or ask again when I'd earned it." Sera huffed, almost a laugh. "Cheeky."

"Wise." The eye closed. Opened again. "You led the Kettlebrook raid."

It wasn't a question, and the name landed in Sera's chest like a thrown knife. "That was before your time, beast."

"Nothing is before my time. That is rather the point of me." He shifted, settling his jaw on his paws, eyes still on her. "You will tell them. Soon. Secrets rot faster than wounds."

"You don't know anything about it."

"I know that you check the southern horizon every hour, though the hunters came from the north. I know you sleep facing the door with your sword unsheathed, though you claim to be guiding, not guarding. I know guilt when I smell it, huntress. It smells like you."

Sera said nothing. Outside, rain ticked on the patched roof. Somewhere in the ruin of the village a wall beam settled with a groan, and the sound was so much like a dying man's breath that her hand went to her sword before she caught herself.

"Kettlebrook," she said finally, because the silence was worse. "Eleven years ago. I was twenty-two and very good at my job. We had a list of names. One of them was wrong—it didn't matter then whether it was wrong; it mattered that the captain wanted his quota." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used for reports. "I kicked in the door myself. I was proud of how cleanly I did it."

The wolf waited.

"There was no gift in that house. Just a woman who kept bees and talked to them, the way beekeepers do, the way they've always done, and someone jealous two lanes over wrote her name on a list." Sera's hands were steady. She'd trained them to be steady through worse. "I've been telling myself for eleven years that I left the service over principle. The truth is I left over one door. Everything after that was just trying to find a door I could open right, for once."

Ashfall regarded her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't seen him do: he sighed, deep and old, like wind through a canyon.

"The covenant was broken by small doors," he said. "Ten thousand of them. Each one kicked by someone sure they were right." He closed his eye. "Sleep, huntress. I'll wake you if the world ends. It has an appointment elsewhere tonight."

* * *

**Wren**

The child saved them, and then Wren saved the child, and after that nothing was simple ever again.

They'd been three days out of Brant's Ford, following the beasts north as the message said—following, really, the thin bright thread of wild voices that grew stronger each day, as if the land itself were waking as they left the warded valley behind. Sera led them along drovers' roads, avoiding the king's highways, and taught Wren to read smoke-signals on the horizon: black and thick meant a purge already done; thin and fresh meant run.

The hamlet was called Netherby, or had been. Smaller than Brant's Ford. Burned hotter. As they picked through its edge at midmorning, Wren heard it before anyone—a sound too high and ragged for a bird.

"*There,*" they said, and broke from cover before Sera could grab their sleeve.

The dog was in the remains of a well-house, a lean sheepdog with one eye clouded white, standing over a gap in the floor stones. From the gap came the crying. Wren dropped to their knees at the edge and looked down into darkness, and the darkness looked back with two enormous eyes.

A child. Four years old, maybe five. Wedged between fallen beams in the well-house cellar, alive, alone—how long alone?—in the dark with the body of a dog's dead master somewhere above and the whole burned world pressing down.

"It's all right," Wren said, and then, helplessly, in the other tongue, the true one, the forbidden one: "*Little one. You fell. Are you hurt?*"

The crying stopped. The child stared up, sniffling, and answered in a wobbling voice: "You talk funny."

"Yes," Wren said. "Yes, I do. Can you climb to me? Your dog will help."

And the dog—this was the part Wren would remember later, in the bad nights, the part that made it worth everything—the dog understood. It had been standing guard over the hole for days, unable to reach its small charge, and now it turned and scrambled down into the dark with a grace that ignored its cloudy eye, and pressed itself against the child, and *pushed*, gently, herding the little one toward the rubble-slope toward Wren's reaching hands.

"*Good brother,*" Wren breathed to it. "*Good, good brother.*"

Hands found theirs. Small, grimy, astonishingly strong. Wren pulled, and the child came up out of the earth into the daylight, blinking, filthy, whole, clutching a rag doll by one arm—and behind Wren, Sera made a sound like a sob strangled at birth.

Then the moment ended, because the child's rescuer had spoken beast-speech in the open air of a purged countryside, and Sera's head snapped up toward the treeline with the reflexes of eleven years of hunted things, and she said one word:

"*Down.*"

The bolt took the well-house doorpost where Wren's head had been.

* * *

**Sera**

Two hunters. She counted them in the space of a heartbeat—crossbow in the trees, second man circling the tannery ruin with a drawn blade and a net-coil on his belt. Crown issue, green sashes, the new-style iron charms hanging at their collars that made her teeth ache and the air taste of struck flint.

They'd been following the trio for a day, probably. Drawn by the same thing that drew everyone now: rumor of a survivor in Netherby. Or drawn by *her*—her face was still on proclamations in three provinces, and she'd been careless at the ford two days back, letting a drover see her.

Either way: two hunters, one civilian child, one exposed witch, one wolf, and herself.

The arithmetic was ugly and she did it anyway.

"Sera—" Wren started, pulling the child close.

"Take the girl. Behind the well-house. *Now.*" She was already moving as she said it, angling left, putting the tannery between herself and the crossbow, loosing an arrow at the circling swordsman more to fix his feet than to hit him—he danced back, good instincts, and that bought her three seconds. "Ashfall! The bowman!"

The wolf came out of the smoke like the smoke deciding to have teeth. The crossbowman got one shot off—wide, panicked, splintering a beam—and then the trees swallowed him and gave back only screaming, and Sera felt no satisfaction in it at all, only the cold professional note that the scream would bring more hunters within the hour.

The swordsman was better. Young, scared, but drilled; he gave ground in circles, kept his blade between them, shouted the litany: "*In the crown's name, stand down, witch-hunter's law, resistance is—*" She shot the net off his belt, then his sword-hand's thumb, then had him pinned against a wall with an arrow at his throat, and he was crying, and he couldn't have been more than nineteen.

"Don't," he babbled. "Please. I only joined for the wage. My mam's sick, I only—"

"I know," Sera said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked. "Listen to me. You never saw us. You were attacked by a wolf and survived. Can you say that?"

He nodded so hard his helmet slipped.

"Then run home to your mam, and when they ask, you tell them the wolf story, and you *mean* it." She stepped back, arrow still drawn. "Go."

He went, crashing through the brambles, and Sera stood in the ash of Netherby with her heart slamming and turned around, and there was Wren, standing in the open with a five-year-old on their hip, looking at her like she was a puzzle written in a language they almost knew.

"You let him go," Wren said.

"He's a child in a bigger machine."

"So am I. So is *she*." The child had her face buried in Wren's neck, one fist knotted in their collar. "You killed for her today. Or he did." A nod toward the trees where the screaming had stopped. "Because I spoke aloud. Because I couldn't watch a child cry in a hole and hold my tongue."

"Yes," Sera said simply.

"That's the cost, isn't it. Every time I use it, someone bleeds."

"Every time you use it, someone *lives*," Sera said. "Today it was her. Sometimes it'll be you. That's the whole ledger, Wren. Nobody gets a clean page." She shouldered her bow. "But we can't stay to argue the accounting. That scream carries. We have maybe an hour, and we need to be a valley away."

Ashfall emerged from the treeline, muzzle dark, unhurt. He looked at the child, and the child looked at him, and—this was the thing Sera would turn over for nights afterward—the child *wasn't afraid*. Reached out one grubby hand and patted the great grey skull like a farm dog.

"Her name's Tilly," Wren said quietly. "The dog says so. Says she's Marda Cooper's girl, from the far cottage, and Marda went out the night of the fire and never came back." Their jaw tightened. "The dog's been keeping her alive for nine days. Nine days, Sera. No one came back for her. Everyone who might have was dead or fled or too afraid."

"Where's the dog now?"

Wren's face did something complicated. "Staying with her. Always. Until—" They didn't finish it.

Sera looked at the child, at the wolf, at the young hunter's blood cooling on the brambles, and thought about doors. About opening one right, for once.

"There's a settlement," she said slowly. "Two days north-east, off our line but not fatally so. People who ran the purges. They call themselves the Moon Court, or the rumors do. If anywhere takes a witch-touched orphan and doesn't ask questions—"

"It's them," Wren said. "Brant's Ford. *Follow the beasts.*"

"Then we deliver her, and we find out if the Moon Court is real or a fairy story." Sera was already walking, scanning the northern ridgeline out of habit, and stopped, and stared.

Smoke. Not one column. A dozen, maybe more, rising from beyond the hills in a long ragged line, like the land itself had been set alight along some terrible seam.

"Not a fairy story," she said. "Something's burning them out. All of them. All at once."

Behind her, Ashfall lifted his head and howled once, long and low, a sound that was less grief than summons—and from hills they couldn't even see, faint and scattered, other voices answered.

* * *

**Wren**

They walked through the night with Tilly asleep against Wren's chest, wrapped in Sera's cloak, and the answering howls threading the dark like stitches pulling a torn world together.

Wren didn't know yet what waited at the end of those voices. They only knew that for the first time since the crow had cried warning over Hollowmere, they weren't running *from* something.

They were running toward it, carrying a sleeping child, followed by a wolf who remembered, beside a huntress who grieved—and the road of ashes stretched north, and every step on it was a word in a sentence Wren was only beginning to learn how to speak.

Behind them, far to the south, a horn sounded. Once. Twice.

Then, for the first time, a third time—the signal Wren had never heard described, the one Sera went pale at hearing, the one that meant *quarry confirmed, kill order given.*

Sera broke into a run. "Don't look back," she said. "Whatever you hear behind us—don't look back."
