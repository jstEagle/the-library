## 8. The Gilded Cage

The house at the end of the orchard road was too beautiful, and Sera Kell had learned long ago that too-beautiful meant too-careful.

It rose out of the autumn mist like something remembered rather than built—white stone, blue shutters, a fountain singing in the courtyard where three fountains should have been silent this late in the year. Roses bloomed along the walls. In Frostfall. Sera counted them twice, then stopped counting, because counting wouldn't change what they were: flowers that had no business existing, kept alive by something that wasn't soil.

"Lord Vey's estate," the carter had said, tipping his hat with the particular reverence villagers reserve for men who own things. "He takes in the gifted ones, he does. Poor souls run out of everywhere else. He asks only a little service in return. A fair man."

A fair man. Sera had heard those words before, usually from people standing in front of houses she'd later been ordered to burn.

She dismounted before Wren could and checked the girth straps twice, buying time. Behind her, Wren sat their grey mare with the rigid stillness of someone who hadn't slept properly in four days, and Ashfall—hunched under a traveling cloak that fooled no one—stood with his ears flat beneath the fabric, reading the air.

*Too many heartbeats,* he'd said an hour back. *And none of them outside.*

Sera had felt it too. The estate was quiet in the wrong way. Servants moved through the grounds like figures in a painting, smooth and silent, never speaking to one another. The stables held horses that didn't startle when she passed. Everything here was calm the way a pond is calm—with weight underneath.

"We could ride on," she murmured to Wren. "There's a ferry crossing two days north."

"With what coin?" Wren's voice was flat with exhaustion. "And the hunters are behind us, not ahead. Vey's the first door that isn't locked."

That was the trouble with doors. Sera knew better than most that some of them only looked open.

She led them up the drive anyway, because exhausted people with no coin and a wounded wolf made poor judges of architecture, and because part of her—the part that had been a royal huntress for eleven years, the part that had learned to read a household the way other people read weather—wanted to see inside.

The man who met them at the door was handsome the way a coin is handsome. Silver-streaked dark hair, a smile that arrived half a beat before his eyes did, rings on every finger but worn loosely, carelessly, as though wealth were a habit he'd grown bored of. His doublet was wine-dark velvet. His boots were soft as whispers.

"Travelers." He spread his hands as if they were a gift he'd been waiting for. "And such travelers. Please—you look half-starved and entirely hunted, which in this season means you're either criminals or the crown's latest obsession. Come in. I'm Aldric Vey."

"Sera Kell." She gave him the name without embellishment. Beside her, Wren hesitated a fraction too long.

"Wren," they said finally. "Wren Marsh."

Vey's smile deepened by a degree. He'd caught the hesitation. Sera watched him catch it, watched him file it away behind those quick grey eyes, and understood that this man missed nothing and would pretend to have missed everything.

"And your companion?" Vey's gaze slid to the cloaked figure, lingered on the way the cloak moved where no body should move it. "A large friend."

"A dog," Sera said. "Bad temper. Bites."

"Then we shall all be very polite to him." Vey stepped aside, gesturing them into a hall that smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and, faintly, underneath it, of something else—something green and frightened, like a meadow after a storm. "Hot food. Dry beds. And tomorrow, perhaps, you'll tell me what brings three refugees down my road in the same week the witch-finders burned the mill at Harrow's Bend."

Sera's hand twitched toward her knife out of pure reflex. She turned it into adjusting her sword belt. "You know about Harrow's Bend?"

"I know about everything that happens within forty miles of my land, Mistress Kell. It's a hobby." He laughed, warm and easy, and led them into the light.

* * *

Dinner was pheasant and honeyed roots and a dark red wine that tasted of cherries and cost more than a village earned in a year. Vey talked brilliantly and listened carefully, which Sera recognized as the professional habit of a man who collected information the way other men collected stamps or sins.

He asked nothing direct. That was the artistry of it. He remarked that the roads east were dangerous, and waited to see who flinched. He mentioned that the crown had doubled its bounty for gift-bearers, unclaimed now, and studied their faces over the rim of his cup. He told a long, funny story about a tax collector and a mule, and while everyone laughed he watched Wren's hands, which stayed very still on the tablecloth, and Sera saw him notice that too.

"You've a kind face for a lord," Wren said at last, when the plates were cleared.

"I've a profitable face," Vey corrected pleasantly. "Kindness is what I do with the profits. This house shelters nineteen souls with gifts the world has decided to hate. A girl who can find water through stone. Two brothers who mend broken bones with a touch. An old woman whose dreams come true often enough to be inconvenient." He refilled Wren's cup himself, an intimacy that made Sera's skin prickle. "They fled to me with nothing, and I gave them everything. All I ask in return is service. Everyone serves someone, Wren Marsh. I merely make the arrangement honest."

"What kind of service?"

"Whatever they're best at." He smiled. "You'll find I'm very good at knowing what people are best at."

Later, shown to their rooms in the east wing, Sera stood at her window and did not sleep. Below, in the moonlight, servants crossed the courtyard carrying covered cages toward a gatehouse she hadn't noticed on arrival—a low stone building set apart from the main house, windowless, its door bound in banded iron.

Iron. Not for keeping people out.

For keeping something in.

* * *

In the morning, Vey gave them a tour.

It was, Sera had to admit, masterfully done. He showed them the refugee quarters first—clean rooms, real beds, children chasing each other down corridors with the particular shrieking joy of children who had forgotten to be afraid. A woman with river-grey eyes pressed a loaf of bread into Wren's hands and thanked them for arriving, as if guests were a blessing. The water-finder girl demonstrated her gift shyly, tapping a wall and naming the depth of the aquifer behind it, and Vey applauded like a proud patron at a recital.

"This is what I offer," he said quietly, watching Wren watch the girl. "Not charity. Sanctuary with dignity. These people work, create, belong. Compare it to the alternative—" he waved vaguely eastward, toward smoke and ash and the roads they'd walked, "—and tell me I'm not the kindest man in the province."

"It's remarkable," Wren said, and meant it, and Sera's chest tightened because Wren wanted so badly to mean it.

But Sera had drifted to the window during the demonstration, and from there she'd seen the gatehouse again, and seen something else besides: a servant carrying feed—not grain, but raw meat—toward the iron-bound door. And hanging from the man's belt, swinging gently with each step, a charm of cold iron wrapped in white silk.

The same charms the witch-hunters wore. The same charms that frayed wards and deadened magic. Here, in a sanctuary, carried openly by household staff.

She said nothing. She smiled at Vey's jokes through lunch. And when he offered to show them his famous gardens, she went along, cataloguing everything: the roses that bloomed out of season, the fountain that never froze, the topiary shaped like running deer that seemed, at certain angles, to shift its posture when no one was looking directly at it.

"These hedges are extraordinary," she said lightly.

"Old magic in the soil," Vey agreed. "This valley was covenant-land once, before the crown fenced it off. Things grow strangely here. Things *stay* strangely." He plucked a rose and handed it to Wren, and Sera watched the thorns slide across Wren's palm without breaking skin—as if the flower recognized something and chose courtesy. "I collect what the old days left behind. You might say preservation is my true passion."

"Preservation," Sera repeated.

"Of rare things." His eyes found hers, mild and pleasant. "Rare things are so easily destroyed these days. Someone must keep them safe."

*Safe.* The word landed wrong, the way a key lands wrong in a lock—close, almost fitting, fundamentally false. Sera thought of the gatehouse. Of meat and iron. Of servants who never spoke to one another, moving through their days with the smooth compliance of animals that had learned what happened when they made noise.

She thought, suddenly and completely unbidden, of a report she'd written eleven years ago, early in her career, filed and forgotten: *Estates in the western provinces reporting unusual livestock losses. Suspected poaching. Recommend investigation.* And the investigation had been quietly buried by someone above her, and she'd never thought about it again until this moment, standing in a garden full of impossible roses, understanding that Lord Aldric Vey had been collecting for a very long time.

* * *

She went over the wall that night.

Not far—just to the gatehouse, hugging the shadow of the orchard, Ashfall a grey ghost at her side. He'd insisted on coming when she told him where she was going, and his presence steadied her; whatever was in that building, a wolf who remembered covenants seemed an appropriate witness.

The gatehouse door was locked, but gatehouses need ventilation, and ventilation needs grates, and grates rust. Sera worked the oldest one loose with her knife and put her eye to the gap.

The interior was dim, lit by a single hooded lantern. Rows of cages lined the walls—and not cages, she realized as her eyes adjusted. *Kennels. Stalls. Aviaries.* Built to different scales, different shapes, each one fitted to its occupant the way a coffin is fitted to a corpse.

In the nearest cage, a fox the color of banked embers lay curled tight around itself, and its fur moved wrong—shimmering, shifting, as if it couldn't decide what shape to be. Above it, perched on a rail, a bird with feathers like hammered silver stared at nothing with eyes that had gone flat and distant. Further in, something large breathed slowly in a stall built for it, something that cast shadows with antlers, and beside its stall hung an iron charm the size of a dinner plate, humming faintly, leaching the air dry.

And in the corner, smallest of all, a wicker basket with a blanket in it, and in the blanket a lump that breathed, and beside the basket a card in elegant script that Sera read upside down through the grate:

*Shapeshifter, juvenile. Acquired Harrow's Bend. Promising.*

Harrow's Bend. The mill the witch-finders burned. The village where, according to the survivors on the road, no one had escaped.

No one except, apparently, whoever Vey had paid the hunters to pull from the ashes.

Sera stepped back from the grate. Her hands were shaking, and it took her a moment to identify the feeling underneath the shaking, because it wasn't fear. It was recognition. Eleven years ago she had stood in a burning farmhouse and held a gifted child in her arms while her captain shouted orders behind her, and she had refused, and the refusal had cost her everything—and she had told herself ever since that refusing was enough. That walking away was the moral act.

But Vey had never walked away from anything. Vey had *collected*. He'd built a sanctuary with a cellar, a haven with a hinge, and every gifted soul upstairs sleeping in clean beds was one refusal away from a kennel and a card in elegant script.

The fox stirred. Its ember-colored head lifted, and its eyes found the grate, found her, and in them Sera saw something that undid her more than any scream could have: not terror. *Recognition.* It knew what she was looking at. It had stopped expecting anyone to look.

"They can hear you," Ashfall said softly at her side. His voice was a rumble like distant rockfall. "The old ones. They remember being free, even when the iron makes them forget how freedom felt. That is the cruelest binding—not the cage, but the memory inside it."

"How long?"

"Some were taken young. Years." The wolf's lips peeled back from his teeth, and for the first time since she'd known him, Sera heard something in his voice that frightened her: hatred, old and patient and vast. "This is what becomes of the covenant when men find it. Not broken—*farmed*. They cannot renew what they have made into property."

Footsteps. Sera flattened against the wall, knife drawn, as a servant passed on his rounds—pausing at the door, checking the lock, touching the iron charm at his belt the way a man touches a talisman. Then gone.

Above them, in the great house, a lamp still burned in Vey's study. Through the study window, silhouetted against the light, Vey stood at his desk writing letters, unhurried, contented, a man attending to correspondence. As Sera watched, he paused, dipped his pen, and turned his head slightly—toward the window, toward the darkness, toward the exact spot where she stood.

He couldn't see her. There was no possible way he could see her.

He smiled anyway, and returned to his letters.

Sera backed away into the orchard shadows, heart hammering, and made her way to her room through the servants' stair. Wren was awake when she slipped in—they always seemed to be awake lately, listening to the house the way they listened to everything—and took one look at her face and sat up.

"What did you find?"

Sera closed the door. Checked the corridor. Drew the bolt.

"Nineteen souls under this roof," she said quietly, "and I don't think a single one of them knows what's downstairs. Tomorrow Vey's going to ask you what your gift is, Wren. He's going to ask kindly, over breakfast, with that smile." She met their eyes in the dark. "We need to decide what we tell him before he decides what we are. Because I've seen his collection, and I promise you—he already knows there's something about you worth keeping."

From the wardrobe where Ashfall hid, a low sound rolled out, barely audible, continuous—the growl of a wolf who had scented blood a long way off and was measuring the distance.

Down the hall, faint through the walls, a bell chimed midnight. And somewhere below the floorboards, so soft Sera might have imagined it, something in the gatehouse began to sing—a thin, high, wordless note of grief that rose and fell and rose again, like a creature calling into the dark for a home that no longer existed.

Wren's head came up sharply, their whole body turning toward the sound, and Sera saw their eyes catch the lamplight and flash—not human, not quite, not anymore.

"They're crying," Wren whispered. "Sera—all of them. They've been crying all night. I just couldn't hear it until now."
