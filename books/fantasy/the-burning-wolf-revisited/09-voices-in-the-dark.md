## 9. Voices in the Dark

The screaming started at moonrise.

Wren had been three days in Lord Aldric Vey's estate, long enough to learn its rhythms: the servants who never met your eyes, the sweetmeats left on silver trays, the way Vey's smile lingered a half-second too long when he watched his guests eat. Long enough to learn that gilded meant gilded, and nothing more.

But the screaming was new. Or not new—Wren realized with a cold drop in their stomach that it had been there all along, threaded beneath the estate's polite noises like water under ice. It was only now, lying awake in the soft bed they'd been given, that Wren finally *listened*.

It came from beneath the east wing. From the cellars Vey called his "menagerie."

The voices were wrong. Not animal-speech as Wren knew it—the quick bright chatter of foxes, the patient rumble of goats, the crow's dry commentary. This was older. Heavier. Words that bent in the middle like wind-bent trees. The old tongue, the one Ashfall used only when he spoke of the covenant. And every voice in it was saying the same thing, over and over, in different throats:

*Open. Open. Open.*

Wren sat up. The fire in the grate had burned to embers. Somewhere down the hall, Sera slept—or didn't—and Ashfall lay curled in the stable loft where he'd insisted on staying, too large and too wild to pass for anything but what he was, which even here, even in a house full of gifted refugees, he could not be.

*You hear it too,* Wren said silently, reaching out the way they'd learned to reach, letting the thought drift like smoke toward the stables.

Ashfall's answer came slow, like something surfacing from deep water. *I have heard it since we crossed the gate. I hoped you would sleep through it a few nights more.*

*What are they?*

*Bound things.* A pause, and then, with an edge Wren had rarely heard from him: *Caged things. Come and see, if you must. But know that once you have seen, you will not unsee it.*

* * *

The corridor outside Wren's room was dark except for a single lamp at the far end, guttering. Wren moved barefoot past Sera's door—paused, hand raised to knock—then kept walking. What would they say? *Sera, I hear creatures screaming under the house and I want to go down there.* She would say wait. She would say patience. She had been saying patience since they arrived, patience like a stone in her fist she squeezed whenever the questions got dangerous.

The cellar door was at the end of the east wing, behind a door painted to match the wall so well that Wren walked past it twice before the voices pulled them back. No lock. Of course no lock—who would come down here? Who would *want* to?

The stairs went down farther than the house stood tall.

And then the smell hit: straw and copper and something else, something like a storm about to break, the smell of magic held too long in too small a space. Wren's skin prickled. Their gift rose unbidden, straining toward the voices like a dog toward a whistle.

The first cage was iron.

Not bars—iron woven into shapes, into knots and spirals, patterns that hurt to look at directly, the same geometry Wren had seen on the hunters' charms fraying Hollowmere's wards. Inside it crouched a creature Wren had no name for: cat-sized, furred in moss-green, with eyes like two drops of midnight. It pressed itself against the far corner of its cage and screamed without sound now, having screamed itself hoarse, its mouth working around words in the old tongue.

*Open. Please. Please open. I did nothing. I did nothing. I did nothing.*

"I'm sorry," Wren whispered, and the creature flinched as if struck.

There were more. A dozen cages down the long cellar, each holding something the world had mostly forgotten: a river-thing like a woman made of reeds and water, drooping in her dry iron basin; a fox with fur the color of banked coals, pacing a circle worn smooth by years; hawks and owls; a badger the size of a calf with old scars across its muzzle; and at the very end, largest of all, a stag whose antlers grew leaves—real leaves, browned and dying now, dropping one by one onto the straw.

Every cage was iron-knotted. Every creature was quieting, dimming, going out like lamps.

*This is what he collects,* Ashfall had said once, of Vey's "shelter." *Ask yourself what a man shelters, and what a man keeps.*

Wren had asked. Vey had laughed his warm laugh and said, *Specimens, my dear. Curiosities. Someone must preserve the old wonders before the crown burns them all.* And Wren, drunk on safety after weeks of flight, had believed him.

Now, standing in the screaming dark, Wren understood. Vey wasn't preserving the old wonders. He was *banking* them. Each bound creature was a knot of wild magic, tied off and stored like grain against winter. And a man who stored that much power wanted, someday, to spend it.

*Open,* the creatures sang. *Please. Please. Open.*

"I can't," Wren whispered. "Not yet. If I free you and run, he'll hunt us all, and the crown will—" They stopped. Heard themselves. Heard the shape of the excuse, smooth and reasonable and rotten at the core.

A hawk in the third cage lifted its head. One wing hung wrong, bent at the middle where it should have swept, and there was dried blood on the iron knots where it had thrown itself against them, again and again, for how long? Its eyes found Wren's in the dark—not the flat panic of the others but something steadier. Something that looked at Wren the way Ashfall sometimes did, measuring.

*You hear us,* it said, in the old tongue, rusty and precise. *A speaker. After all these years, a speaker comes to the cages and says* I can't.

"Yes."

*Then say why. Truly. Not the excuse. The reason.*

Wren opened their mouth. Closed it. The reason was simple and shameful: *Because I'm afraid. Because I just got somewhere safe. Because helping you means losing what I've only just found.*

The hawk held their gaze. Around it, the other creatures had gone silent, listening.

*Yes,* the hawk said. *That is the truth. Now you know its taste. The man above has swallowed a great deal of it.*

* * *

"You went down there."

Sera was waiting in Wren's room when they came back up, sitting in the dark by the dead fire, whetstone in hand though her knife was sheathed. Her voice was flat, but her eyes weren't. They were afraid—for Wren, Wren realized, and maybe for herself.

"The hawk's wing is broken," Wren said. "Set badly. It's been throwing itself at the iron until it bled. Years, Sera. Some of those cages have been locked for *years*."

"I know."

"You *know*?" The word came out louder than intended, and Wren strangled it, glancing at the door. "You knew?"

"I suspected. Now I'm certain, same as you." Sera set down the whetstone. "And I'm telling you the same thing I've been telling myself for three days: we wait. We watch. Vey is planning something—the Moon Court, the altar, all of it—and if we bolt tonight with a lame hawk and a wolf that can't fit through windows, we learn nothing and lose everything. Patience, Wren."

"Patience." Wren tasted the word like the hawk had made them taste the truth. "They're screaming, Sera. All night, every night. You sleep ten feet away and you call it patience?"

"I call it *surviving*." For the first time, Sera's composure cracked, and underneath it Wren saw the thing she carried: the raid, the child she hadn't killed, the aunt she had. "I have been the person who stayed quiet because acting was dangerous. I know exactly what it costs to act, and exactly what it costs not to, and I am telling you—" her voice dropped, roughened, "—the second cost is worse. It's worse, Wren. But we are two blades and a wolf against a lord with private armies and a court of rebels, and if we die stupidly in his cellar, those creatures stay caged anyway. Do you understand? Dying doesn't free anyone. We need to be smart enough to live long enough to matter."

From the window, low and grave, Ashfall's voice arrived on the night air—he had climbed to the sill without a sound, and his eyes caught the starlight like still water.

"The huntress speaks like one who has buried her own conscience and mourns it," he said. "She is not wrong about the danger. But hear me, little speaker: there is a difference between patience and permission. She counsels the first. If you do nothing, night after night, you are granting the second—to him, over and over, with every dawn you let pass."

"And what would you have me do?" Wren asked. "Bite through the locks?"

"No. Iron-knots are not bitten; they are unwoven, and unwoven wrongly they bite back." Ashfall's gaze held theirs. "But you are not nothing, Wren Vane. You are a speaker of the old tongue, perhaps the last. And the old tongue has a truth at its root that the iron-magic forgot: *nothing wild can truly be owned*. The knots hold because the creatures believe they hold. Because they have despaired. Despair is the mortar of every cage ever built." His lips peeled back, not quite a snarl, not quite a smile. "Give one of them back its hope, and see what the iron becomes."

* * *

Wren didn't sleep. They lay in the soft bed listening to the screaming fade toward dawn as the creatures exhausted themselves, and they turned Ashfall's words over like stones, looking at the underside of each.

*Nothing wild can truly be owned.*

In Hollowmere, they had hidden the gift because hiding was survival. In the forest, they had learned the gift was listening. And now, in a lord's warm cellar, the gift was asking something harder than either: it was asking them to stop being a witness. Silence, Ashfall said, was complicity. Sera said silence was strategy. Both were true. That was the trap of it—that both were true, and Wren had to choose anyway.

Near dawn, they dressed, took the lamp, and went back down.

The cellar was quieter now, the creatures sunk in the exhausted stupor of the hopeless. Wren walked the row slowly, and this time they did not whisper apologies. They stopped at each cage and spoke—in the old tongue, aloud, letting the words roll out rough and unpracticed:

"You are seen. You are heard. You are not forgotten."

At first, nothing. Then the reed-woman lifted her dripping head. The coal-fox stopped pacing. One by one, the creatures came to the fronts of their cages, pressing close to the iron, drinking in the sound of their own language like water after drought.

The hawk was last. It stood on its good leg, broken wing cradled, and regarded Wren with that level, measuring stare.

"You," Wren said. "Your name. Tell me your name."

A long silence. Then, wonderingly, as if tasting a flavor remembered from another life: *Sky-cuts-the-river. My mother named me for the moment of the strike.*

"Sky-cuts-the-river. Your wing is broken and your cage is iron and I cannot carry you out tonight. But I swear this: before the thirteenth moon turns, you will fly, or I will die trying to make it so. That is not comfort. That is a vow. And vows in the old tongue bind the speaker." Wren's hands were shaking. They placed their palm flat against the iron knot at the front of the cage. "Do you believe me?"

The hawk studied them for a long moment. Then it leaned forward and touched its beak, gently, to Wren's knuckles through the bars.

*I believe you,* it said. *Now listen. The knots are woven from despair, as your wolf said—but also from consent, wrung out of us one cruelty at a time. We signed his paper. We agreed to be kept, in exchange for shelter from the burning. That is the hook in us, deeper than any iron. Break our despair and the iron loosens—but the consent must be *unsworn*, freely, by us. When the time comes, ask us. Ask us properly, in the old tongue, and we will take back what we gave.*

Behind it, softly, the whole cellar murmured assent—a rustle of feathers and fur and reeds, a chorus of small, exhausted voices saying *yes, yes, ask us.*

Wren stepped back, heart hammering. The iron knots hadn't changed. The cages stood exactly as they had. But the air in the cellar felt different—charged, like the moment before lightning, like the valley wards the night they first flickered.

Something had begun.

* * *

Wren was halfway up the stairs when the lamp flame bent sideways, as if the house had drawn breath.

They froze. Below, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the cellar door at the top of the far stair—the servants' entrance—opening. Slow footsteps on stone. The particular unhurried tread of someone who owns everything they walk upon.

Vey.

Wren killed the lamp and pressed into the shadow between the stairs, heart slamming, as lamplight bloomed at the bottom of the far passage and Vey's voice drifted down the cellar row—warm, melodic, terrible in its gentleness.

"There, there, my darlings. Restless again? I do apologize for the draught." A pause, and then, softer, almost tender: "Though I can't help noticing... someone has been speaking to you. Filling your heads with old words and older hopes." Another pause, longer. The scrape of a fingernail tracing iron. "No matter. Hopes are like wings, little ones. Easily clipped."

The footsteps paused. Directly below Wren.

"But if our mysterious visitor should come again..." Vey's voice smiled audibly. "...do give them my regards. And tell them the thirteenth moon waits for no one—not even the faithful."

The light withdrew. The door closed. And in the dark, against Wren's palm, the iron knot of the hawk's cage gave the smallest shudder—like a lock remembering it had once been a door.
