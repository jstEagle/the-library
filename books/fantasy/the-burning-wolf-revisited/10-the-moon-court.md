## 10. The Moon Court

The hall at Vey's estate had been a chapel once. Wren could see it in the bones of the place—the arched windows, now shuttered with black oak; the stone font in the corner, repurposed as a planter for night-blooming jasmine; the way the ceiling still curved upward, as if the room itself remembered reaching for something holy.

Now it held maps.

Dozens of them, pinned and layered across long trestle tables, weighted with daggers and candlesticks and river stones. Wren stood among them with Sera at their shoulder and Ashfall a grey shadow near the door—too large to pass for a hound, too strange to hide, so Vey had simply not asked them to.

"Three weeks," Lord Aldric Vey said, sweeping an arm across the largest map. "That is what remains before the thirteenth moon turns full and the covenant fails. Three weeks to do what no army, no crown, no prophet has managed in four hundred years."

He was younger than Wren had expected. That was the first deception, though they wouldn't understand it until later. In the stories Sera told on the road, the Moon Court's leader was a greybeard, a ghost, a name whispered by survivors of burned villages. Instead: a man perhaps forty, silver at the temples like frost arriving early, dressed not in noble silks but in a huntsman's leathers, well-worn at the elbows. He looked like someone who worked for a living. He looked trustworthy.

Wren had learned, in the past two days beneath his roof, to distrust that look.

"The crown believes the gifted are a disease," Vey continued. His voice was warm, low, the voice of a man who had talked frightened people into cellars and smuggled them past checkpoints. "They hunt us because they fear what they cannot command. And what does the crown do with everything it cannot command?"

"It burns it," Sera said flatly.

"It *harvests* it," Vey corrected, and tapped the map where Greyhollow fortress sat like a spider at the center of its web. "But we are not here to discuss the crown. We are here to discuss you, Wren Vane."

All eyes in the room turned. There were perhaps thirty people—refugees, Wren had thought, when they'd arrived. Now, watching them stand in disciplined silence, hands resting on weapons, Wren revised the assessment. Soldiers, then. Or something between.

"I don't want anything from you," Wren said.

Vey smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, which was the second deception. "Everyone wants something. The trick is wanting the right things." He crossed to the window, pulled back the black oak shutter a hand's width, and let in a blade of afternoon light. "You speak to beasts. You walked out of Hollowmere—a valley that has forgotten how to be found—and the first wolf you met didn't eat you. It *followed* you." He glanced at Ashfall. "I've had scholars and hedge-mages try for years to learn what you carry. They came back with nothing but scars. You were born with it."

"Ashfall chose me," Wren said carefully. "Not the other way around."

"And yet here he stands, in my hall, at your side, silent as a grave unless you bid him speak." Vey let the shutter fall. "The Moon Court has fought this purge for six years. We have hidden the gifted, moved them, armed them. Do you know what our survival rate is?" He didn't wait for an answer. "One in five. One in five, Wren Vane, because hiding is dying slowly. Because running is dying tired. I am offering you the chance to stop running."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," Vey said simply, spreading his hands. "You stay as my guest. You eat at my table. No one will touch you or your wolf. I am not the crown; I do not chain people." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "But I think you'll say yes. I think you've spent your whole life being quiet, and I think some part of you is starving to be heard."

The words landed harder than Wren wanted to admit. They thought of Hollowmere—the ewe they'd healed in secret, the crow's warnings swallowed, their mother's face the night she'd finally understood and begged them to go. All those years of silence, and here was a man offering a hall full of listeners.

"Yes," Wren heard themselves say. "I'll listen to what you're proposing."

"*Listening*," Vey repeated, delighted. "Oh, you are going to be useful."

* * *

Sera caught up with them in the east corridor, her boots loud on old flagstones. She waited until they were past two servants—servants who moved with a soldier's economy, Wren noticed, and whose livery hid sword calluses—before speaking.

"You shouldn't have agreed."

"I said I'd listen. That's not agreement."

"With Vey, listening *is* agreement. He builds everything on yeses. You give him one word and he'll build a cathedral on it." Sera glanced back the way they'd come. "Six years I wore the crown's colors. I know what resistance leaders look like, Wren. The good ones are desperate. The bad ones are patient. Vey is the patient kind."

"You said he shelters refugees."

"He does. Genuinely. That's what makes him dangerous." She stopped at a window overlooking the inner courtyard, and Wren followed her gaze. Below, handlers led creatures along the colonnade: a pair of elk with antlers like winter branches, a lynx that moved wrong, too fluid, its eyes catching light like coins. "Look at them. Tell me what you see."

Wren listened—not with ears, but with the thing under their ribs, the gift they'd spent twenty years strangling. The courtyard came alive. Not screams, not yet, but a low continuous murmur, like a village heard through a wall. Weariness. Resignation. And underneath it, threaded through every voice:

*Collared. Collared. Collared.*

"They wear iron," Wren said quietly. "Thin bands. Under the fur."

"Sera!" A man's voice, bright with surprise. Vey's captain—Dain, broad-shouldered, cheerful-faced—rounded the corner with two of his soldiers. "And the famous beast-speaker. Word travels fast in this house." His smile was easy. His eyes went to Wren's hands, their throat, cataloguing. "Lord Vey asks that you join him for the evening meal. Both of you. He's eager to hear about the valley wards—Hollowmere's seals were legendary, once."

"We'll come," Sera said, before Wren could answer.

Dain nodded and moved off. Wren watched him go. "His soldiers. Are they—"

"Moon Court? Some. Crown turncoats? Some." Sera resumed walking. "That's the point of a court, Wren. Everyone serves the throne. The only question is which one."

* * *

The evening meal was held in a smaller chamber, intimate, candlelit—Vey understood theater the way other men understood farming. Six places. Vey at the head, Wren at his right hand, Sera deliberately placed at the far end between two captains who plied her with questions about crown patrol routes. Separating us, Wren thought. Testing whether we compare notes.

"You grew up warded," Vey said, pouring wine himself, refusing the servant. "Tell me about it. Truly. I've read the accounts of Hollowmere—there aren't many—but accounts are dead things. I want the living version."

Wren turned the cup in their fingers. "It was quiet. Safe. Everyone knew everyone. The wards kept out more than hunters—they kept out weather, hard winters, most sickness. People lived long there. They forgot things gently."

"Forget what?"

"That the world was bigger than the valley." Wren met his eyes. "My mother used to say the wards weren't walls. They were a lullaby. And you can't hear a lullaby forever without starting to sleep."

Something flickered across Vey's face—appetite, quick and hot, gone in an instant. "Beautifully put. And the wards themselves—covenant-work, would you say? Old pact-magic?"

"I wouldn't know. I was a child. Children don't study the walls."

"No," Vey agreed softly. "Children study what's inside them." He leaned back. "Here is what I proposed tonight, plainly, because I find plainness disarms suspicion better than any oath. The covenant dies with the thirteenth moon. When it does, every gifted soul in these realms becomes either a corpse or a slave—unless someone stands at the Moonstone Altar and speaks a new covenant into being. Not restore the old one. The old one failed. A *new* one. Written by living hands."

"Whose hands?"

"Whoever stands at the altar." Vey smiled again, and this time the warmth in it had edges. "The Moon Court will hold the altar ground against crown and hunter both. But the speaking—that requires a voice the wild will actually answer. A beast-speaker. Perhaps the last true one." He raised his cup to Wren. "I'm asking you to be that voice."

"And if the wild doesn't answer me?"

"Then we adapt. We always adapt." He drank. "But it will answer. Ashfall swore the original covenant—he told you that much, I'd wager. The old magic remembers its own. And you, Wren Vane, are very much its own."

Later, walking back through the cold corridors with Sera silent beside them, Wren realized what had been missing from the whole evening. Vey had spoken of the covenant, the altar, the wild—all of it as *property*. Something to be held, spoken-for, claimed. Even his rebellion sounded like acquisition.

And worse—far worse—was the small, shameful ember in Wren's own chest that had warmed at his words. *A voice. Someone standing to listen.* After a lifetime of silence, part of them wanted it to be true.

* * *

Ashfall woke them an hour past midnight.

Not with sound—with presence. Wren surfaced from sleep already sitting up, heart hammering, and found the wolf's eyes gleaming at the foot of the bed like twin coals banked for morning.

*Come,* he said, in that voice that wasn't sound at all but meaning pressed directly into the mind. *There are things you must learn before you decide whom to trust. And you have decided too quickly already.*

They slipped through sleeping corridors, down a servants' stair, out into the walled garden where frost silvered the rose canes and the moon—Wren flinched at the sight of it—hung cracked and thin above the eastern wall. The hairline fracture across its face had widened since the ridge. Since the ford. Since Sera.

Ashfall sat beneath an ancient yew, and Wren settled into the frozen grass beside him, breath steaming.

"Teach me," they said. "If Vey's rite is false, I need to know what's true."

*First,* Ashfall said, *understand what he wants. He told you the truth wrapped in a lie, which is the most dangerous wrapping there is. Yes, a voice must speak at the altar. Yes, the wild will answer a speaker such as you. But hear the words he chose. A new covenant, written by living hands—his hands, guiding yours. He does not want the pact renewed. He wants it* rewritten*. He wants the wild bound to human will, with himself holding the pen. The crown would harvest the magic. He would farm it.*

Wren hugged their knees, watching their breath cloud. "And if I refuse him?"

*Then you become a guest who overstays. Dain's soldiers count the doors you pass. I have counted them too.* The wolf's ears swiveled toward the house. *We are inside a beautiful trap, little speaker. So we do what clever prey does. We pretend to be caught.*

"Pretend allegiance." Wren's stomach knotted even as they said it. Deception sat badly in them; Hollowmere honesty ran deep. "And while I smile at his table?"

*While you smile at his table, you learn.* Ashfall rose, shook the frost from his coat, and paced before them like a tutor in a courtyard of one. *The old rites are not spells. They are promises. Every covenant-word is a vow given and a vow received. Say them wrong—at his altar, in his ceremony—and you bind yourself instead of binding anything else. Say them right, and no power on this earth can turn your promise into his weapon. So we begin with the First Listening.*

He stopped. The cracked moonlight fell across him, and for a moment he seemed less a wolf than a monument to one.

*Breathe. Empty yourself. Not silence—Hollowmere taught you silence, and silence is just noise held in. Emptiness. Make room.* His voice dropped into something older, cadenced, almost sung. *The First Listening: I set down my want. I set down my fear. What remains when I am empty, let it enter. What enters, I will honor. What I honor, I cannot own.*

Wren closed their eyes. It was harder than any spell could be—harder than commanding, which came to them like breathing, like the sick ewe calm under their palms, like the foxes streaming news across the valley. Commanding was reaching out. This was the opposite: opening a door and having no idea what would walk through.

They breathed. Set down the want—to be safe, to be heard, to go home. Set down the fear—of Vey, of the hunters, of the cracking sky. And in the space left behind, the garden woke around them. The roses dreaming of summer. The yew counting its centuries. Somewhere beyond the wall, a caged creature turning its iron collar over and over in its mind like a stone it could not swallow.

Wren's eyes opened, wet.

"Now you feel it," Ashfall said quietly. "What Vey will never feel, because he listens only for what he can use. The gift is not a sword, little speaker. It never was. It is an ear. And an ear can be pressed to any door—including the door of a man's intent. Practice that, in his halls. Listen past his words. You will hear the pen moving behind his smile."

For the rest of that night and the nights after, they trained in stolen hours. Ashfall taught the Second Listening—I will not take what I have not been given—and the Third, which had no words Wren could keep, only a sensation like standing at the edge of a cliff and trusting the air. By day, Wren sat at Vey's table and nodded and asked shrewd questions and watched the lord's plans unfold across his maps: supply routes to the altar, garrisons to seize, a ceremony drafted in Vey's own handwriting with blanks where the speaker's vows should go.

*Blanks*, Wren noted. *He leaves my part blank.*

*Of course,* Ashfall said. *A puppeteer leaves the strings unwritten until the puppet is fitted.*

On the fifth night, Wren stood alone in the garden, practicing the Third Listening under the widening crack in the moon, and felt—for one vertiginous moment—the whole wild world lean toward them like a crowd toward a stage. Waiting. Wanting. Not to be commanded.

To be *answered*.

They thought of Vey's voice: *I'm asking you to be that voice.* And they understood, finally, the shape of the trap within the trap. Vey wanted a voice to speak his covenant. The wild wanted a voice to speak its own. And Wren Vane, who had spent twenty years learning to be silent, stood between them with the only mouth either side trusted.

Below in the courtyard, a handler's whip cracked, and one of the collared elk screamed—a short, terrible sound, quickly smothered.

Wren's hands curled into fists. Tomorrow, Vey would present the ceremony to his captains, and Wren would smile and nod and play the caught thing. But tomorrow night, they had decided, they would walk the colonnade where the cages stood. There was a hawk there—wounded, furious, awake at all hours—that had been calling to them since the second day in a voice like a struck bell.

*Free me,* it said, every night, the same two words. *Free me.*

Tomorrow night, Wren thought, watching their breath rise toward the broken moon. Tomorrow night, I will answer.
