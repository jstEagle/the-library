## 11. Lessons in Wild Tongues

The first lesson was a humiliation.

Ashfall brought Wren to the heron pool at dawn, mist lying on the water like spilled milk, and told them to ask the oldest creature there for its name.

Wren crouched at the reed line, reached out with the gift the way they'd always done—*show me, tell me, come here*—and the pool went silent. Not quiet. Silent. The frogs stopped mid-croak. The fish hung beneath the surface like stones. Even the mist seemed to hold its breath.

"You commanded," Ashfall said. He lay on the bank with his chin on his paws, grey as weathered slate, eyes half-lidded. "You did not listen."

"I asked."

"You *asked them to obey*. There is a difference, and every wild thing in this forest can hear it." He opened his eyes fully then, and they were the color of old ice over deep water. "Do it again. This time, want nothing."

"Want nothing? How do I—"

"That is the lesson."

Wren sat back on their heels and tried. It was like being asked to unlearn breathing. All their life, the gift had been a hand reaching out—grasp, soothe, turn, command. When the ewe was sick in Hollowmere, Wren hadn't asked the pain to leave; they'd *told* it to. When the crow warned of hunters on the ridge, it was because Wren had spent years teaching the crows to watch for them, a little garrison of spies built on a foundation of *do this for me*.

They breathed. They let the wanting drain out through their palms into the cold mud. And slowly, grudgingly, the pool began again—the frogs resuming mid-note as if no time had passed, the fish stirring, a dragonfly stitching the surface.

And underneath it all, faint as a voice through a wall, something said: *old-old-old, mud-and-memory, I was here before your valley had a name.*

Wren's eyes stung. "It has a name," they whispered. "It's been talking the whole time. I just never—"

"No," Ashfall said. "You never stopped shouting long enough to hear."

* * *

The days took on a shape after that. Dawn at the heron pool or the standing stones where the moss grew in rings. Midday walking the forest with Ashfall, who introduced Wren to beings the way a stern uncle introduces family he disapproves of but will not disown.

The boar sow with one tusk, who remembered the covenant from her great-great-grandsow's telling and considered humans a rumor that had gotten out of hand. The adder coiled in the sun-warmed ruins, who spoke in questions because, she said, answers were how you got trapped. A parliament of rooks that argued so loudly about everything that Wren could only follow one thread in ten.

Each one taught something different.

The boar taught weight. *"Your kind made vows with your mouths,"* she rumbled, plowing a furrow through the bracken, *"and thought the vow lived in the words. Words are wind. A vow lives in what you give up. We gave you our trust. What did you give us?"*

Wren didn't have an answer. The boar snorted and moved on, satisfied to have planted the thorn.

The adder taught patience. She would pose a question—"what does the river want?"—and refuse to accept any answer Wren gave until Wren had sat by the river long enough to realize the question had no answer, that wanting was a human shape laid over a thing too large to wear it. *"You keep asking what things want,"* she hissed, amused. *"Ask instead what they are saying. Wanting is your disease. Listening is the cure."*

The rooks taught humility, mostly by laughing at Wren's mistakes in chorus.

But it was Ashfall who taught the center of it, one evening on the ridge where the forest broke against bare stone, the sky going the color of a bruise behind the hills.

"Do you know what the covenant actually was?" he said. "Not what the hedge-witch told you. Not what the Moon Court claims. What it *was*."

"A pact. Human magic and wild magic, bound together."

"Bound." He tasted the word and disliked it. "Your Vey says bound. Your crown says bound. Even your witch probably said bound, because that is what humans hear when an old truth gets passed down too many mouths. Listen." He turned to face Wren fully, and even now, after weeks, the size of him still made something ancient flinch in Wren's chest. "There was a famine once. Before wards, before hunts, when magic ran through everything like blood through a body. Humans with the gift took what they wanted from the wild—commanded beasts, bent growth, pulled storms—and the wild bled. So the wild came to the gifted ones and offered not war but a *vow*. Mutual. Two hands clasping, neither on top."

"What kind of vow?"

"The wild said: we will lend you our strength, and you will carry our voices. You will be the bridge between what walks and what speaks. Not masters. Bridges." His ears flattened slightly. "And the first gifted ones swore it. Swore to listen before speaking, to take only what was given, to speak for the wild in the halls of men and for men in the courts of the wild. That was the covenant. A marriage, not a leash."

"And then?"

"And then your ancestors got comfortable. Generations passed, and listening started to feel like serving, and commanding felt like power, and somewhere along the way the bridge-builders decided they were kings." Ashfall's voice dropped low. "The covenant didn't break all at once, Wren Vane. It eroded. Every command instead of a question. Every taking instead of a gift. Until the wild pulled its hand back, and the moon began to crack, and men like your witch-hunters filled the silence with iron."

Wren looked at their own hands in the failing light. Hands that had calmed sick animals, yes—but also hands that had *held*. Held the goats still. Held the crows to their spy-work. Held everything at a polite, loving distance where it couldn't surprise them.

"I've been doing it wrong my whole life," they said quietly.

"You've been doing it the way you were taught to survive," Ashfall corrected. "In Hollowmere, a listener gets caught. A commander can hide behind control. But survival is not the same as truth." He nudged Wren's shoulder with his great head, almost gentle. "That ends now. From tonight, you don't command anything. Not a flea. If a beast serves you, it will be because you asked and it chose. Understood?"

"Understood."

"We'll see," said Ashfall, who had apparently been alive long enough to distrust all promises, including his own.

* * *

Some nights the lessons went inward, and those were harder than the boar or the adder or all the rooks together.

Because the gift had been there since before memory. Wren closed their eyes and found it waiting: four years old, in the pen behind the house, the runt lamb shivering and dying while the spring frost came down like a verdict. Wren hadn't known words yet, hadn't known it was forbidden, had only known the lamb was cold and afraid and alone—and had reached out with everything in them, not *warm yourself, live*, but something older and simpler: *I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.*

The lamb had lived. And their mother had found them asleep in the straw with the lamb curled against their chest, and had gone white as the frost, and had carried Wren inside and said, very quietly, *never again, Wren, never where anyone can see, promise me*—and Wren had promised, because a four-year-old will promise anything to make the fear leave their mother's face.

*I'm here,* they'd said. Not *obey*. Not *live*. Just *I'm here.*

Even then. Even before the world taught them to grasp.

"You're smiling," Ashfall observed. They were lying under the stars, the crack in the moon a thin silver scar overhead, visible now if you knew to look.

"I remembered something. I think—" Wren exhaled. "I think the gift was never what they said it was. Not even in Hollowmere. Not even to me. I thought I was hiding a weapon. Maybe I was hiding a *hearing ear*."

"The first step of the vow," Ashfall said. "The last step takes longer."

"How much longer?"

"Until the thirteenth moon. Which is why we don't sleep as much as we used to."

* * *

The breakthrough came on a morning of hard rain, and it came, as breakthroughs do, sideways.

A young stag had gotten a leg caught in a hunter's snare left from some earlier patrol—one of the iron-jawed things that frayed the wards just by existing. It had thrashed itself bloody and now stood trembling in the ferns, and when Wren approached, it fixed them with the flat black terror-eyes of a creature deciding whether to fight.

Every instinct screamed the old way. *Be still. Don't struggle. Let me help.* Wren felt the words rising like water behind a dam.

They let the dam break differently.

Instead of pushing, they opened. Opened all the way, the way the adder had drilled into them, the way the pool had finally spoken when Wren stopped demanding. They let the stag feel them—not their intent, not their plan, just *them*: scared too, tired too, far from home too, holding out empty hands in the rain.

*I'm here,* they said. Nothing else. *I'm here.*

The stag stood a long moment, rain streaming off its rack. Then, deliberately, it lowered its head and turned its wounded leg toward Wren. An offering. A choice.

Wren worked the snare open with shaking fingers, cleaned the wound with moss and riverwater, and when it was done the stag pressed its cool nose briefly against their brow—a touch light as a falling leaf—and walked away into the trees without looking back.

Wren knelt in the wet ferns and laughed, or cried, or both.

"Well," said Ashfall from the treeline, soaked and dignified about it. "Now you're beginning."

"It chose me." Wren's voice cracked on the word. "Ashfall, it *chose*—"

"Yes. That is the whole covenant, in miniature. That is what your Vey will never understand, and what your king would burn ten kingdoms to possess, and what you have carried since you were four years old without knowing its name." He came forward and shook the rain from his coat like a man settling into his authority. "Strength as receptivity. The loudest thing in the forest is the one everyone flees. The listening one, everything comes to."

Wren stood, dripping, lighter than they had ever felt. For the first time since the ridge above Hollowmere, since the crow's warning and the iron charms and the wards flickering out like a dying lamp—for the first time since home became a trap—they weren't running from the gift or hiding it or gripping it like a knife.

They were wearing it.

"So teach me the rest," Wren said. "The rites. The words of the vow. If the thirteenth moon is coming, I want every lesson you have."

Ashfall studied them for a long moment, and something moved behind the ice-color of his eyes—pride, maybe, or grief, or the two wearing each other's faces.

"There is one more teacher you need," he said at last. "One I cannot give you. The old tongue isn't learned from a wolf, Wren Vane. It's learned from the altar itself. And the road to the Moonstone runs through country where Vey's people are already moving—and where, I think, someone is following us who has been following since the ford."

Wren went still. "Sera?"

"Sera sleeps twenty paces behind us and has for weeks." Ashfall turned his head toward the southern trees, nostrils flaring. "This is someone else. Someone patient. And they've stopped hiding."

Out of the rain-dark between the trunks, a figure stepped onto the path ahead—cloaked, hooded, standing with the stillness of a person who had been waiting a very long time to be seen.
