## 12. Sera's Confession

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sera decided she could not carry it another mile.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, a place Ashfall chose with the particular care of an old soldier picking ground—back to stone, sightlines open, one way in and one way out. Wren sat across the flames turning a strip of dried venison over and over without eating it. The hawk they'd freed from Vey's mews had circled the manor twice as they fled south, and now it was somewhere above them in the dark, and every time Sera looked up she half expected to see Vey's riders beneath it.

Three days from Greyhollow. Four from the Moonstone Altar, if the maps were honest, and Sera had stopped trusting maps the day she'd learned how easily men redraw them.

*Tell them,* she thought. *Tell them now, while there's still firelight to see each other by. Tell them before the thirteenth moon turns and it's too late for anything but lies.*

She had been telling herself that for two days.

"Wren." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "There's something you need to hear from me. Before you hear it from anyone else."

Wren looked up. In the firelight their face was all sharp planes and shadow, older than it had any right to be. "That sounds like a speech."

"It is."

"Then I already hate it."

Ashfall lifted his head from his paws. The great wolf said nothing, but his amber eyes moved between them with the patience of someone who had watched this exact fire burn down a hundred times, in a hundred different camps, over centuries of human cowardice and human courage. *Say it,* those eyes said. *Or don't. But stop bleeding on the silence.*

Sera pulled her knife from her belt—not to threaten, just to have something in her hands—and laid it flat on her knee where Wren could see it. An old habit. A hunter's honesty: *here is the thing that can hurt you, and here are my hands, empty.*

"When I was nineteen," she began, "I was the youngest hunt-captain the crown had ever commissioned. Do you know why?"

"Because you were good at killing gifted people?"

"Because I was good at following orders," Sera said. "It's not the same skill, though the crown spends a great deal of money convincing everyone it is. I was raised in the Citadel yards. My father was a witch-finder. His mother before him. The Kells have been hunting the gift since before there was a name for it. I never once questioned what we did until—" She stopped. Started again. "Until the autumn I was twenty-two. There was a village called Thornbrook, up in the eastern fells. You've never heard of it because after that autumn there wasn't a village anymore."

Wren had gone very still. Sera watched the stillness and knew, with the sick certainty of a woman walking toward her own gallows, that Wren already understood where this was going. Gifted blood recognized its own tragedies.

"There was a woman there," Sera said. "A healer. The villagers loved her—she'd delivered half their children, set their bones, talked fevers out of houses like drawing poison from a wound. Someone informed on her anyway. A neighbor with a grudge, or a debt, or just the ordinary hunger to watch a neighbor burn. The order came down. I led the raid. Twelve riders under my command, iron charms, the whole liturgy of it."

"Sera." Wren's voice was thin. "What was her name?"

And there it was. The question Sera had rehearsed a hundred answers to and found every one of them ash.

"Maren," she said. "Maren Vane."

The venison strip fell from Wren's fingers into the dirt. They didn't reach for it. They didn't move at all, except that their breath changed—the small, ragged hitch of someone taking a blow they'd been braced for and finding no brace helps.

"She was my mother's sister," Wren said finally. "My aunt Maren. She used to—" Their voice cracked and they hated it, Sera could see them hating it, jaw clenching against the break. "She used to send us honeycomb every spring. Wild comb, from hives nobody tended. She'd wrap it in cloth and write *for the little ones* on it, and my mother would always say, *your aunt has strange friends,* and I'd eat the honey and never once wonder who kept bees for a woman three valleys away."

"The bees kept themselves," Sera said quietly. "For her. That was her gift. Things grew toward her. Things gave to her willingly. It was the gentlest magic I ever saw, and I signed the order anyway, because the order said the gentleness was the proof. That things *loved* her unnaturally. That was the crime, Wren. That wild things trusted her."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope an owl called, and Ashfall's ears swiveled toward it and back.

"I didn't kill her myself," Sera went on, because stopping now would be mercy and she had not earned mercy. "One of my riders put a bolt through her when she ran for the tree line. But I planned it. I drew the approach on the map. I chose dawn because dawn is when gifted folk sleep deepest—I told myself it was tactics. Twelve years I've told myself it was tactics." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, a dry broken thing. "You want to know the worst part? Afterward, the village wouldn't speak. Not one name given, not one accusation. They just stood in the square and watched us ride out, and an old man spat in the road behind my horse, and I remember thinking, *good, that's fair,* and then thinking, *why do I think that's fair,* and then not letting myself think anything at all for six years."

"You're telling me this now." Wren's voice had gone flat and cold, the warmth scoured out of it. "Now. On the road. With four days left before the moon cracks apart and everything ends."

"Yes."

"Why?" And it wasn't really a question; it was a blade turned edge-up. "Why tell me this unless you want something? Absolution? Is that what this is? You want me to stand here and absolve you so you can sleep tonight?"

"No." Sera met their eyes. "I'm telling you because Vey will use it. He reads people the way I read tracks—it's why he collects them—and he'll find some way to put my face in front of yours beside that memory, if he hasn't already. Or the crown will. Or some survivor of Thornbrook who wandered into the Moon Court's orbit. Someone will hand you this truth wrapped in their own purpose, and they'll time it to break you at the worst possible hour. So I'm handing it to you first. Unwrapped. Ugly. Mine."

"That's very noble," Wren said. "Very tidy. You've clearly practiced this confession."

"I have. For twelve years. To an empty room."

"And did the empty room forgive you?"

Ashfall made a low sound—not quite a growl, not quite a warning. Wren ignored him.

"Because here's what I keep coming back to," they said, standing now, and the firelight threw their shadow huge against the oak. "You tracked me. From Hollowmere. Across the ridge, through the burns, all the way to Vey's gilded cage. You said you wanted to warn me. Fine. Maybe true. But you knew. You knew whose niece you were following, didn't you? Vane. You saw the name and you followed it anyway. Was that redemption-hunting too? Was I your second chance at Maren? Because I am not her, Sera. I am not your penance. I am not a road you get to walk to make yourself clean."

"No," Sera said. "You're not."

"Then what am I?"

"A person," Sera said. "Who needed help, and got it, from someone who owed a debt she can never pay to anyone with your blood. I know the arithmetic doesn't work. I've done it ten thousand times. One life saved does not balance one life taken. Ten don't. A hundred don't. Forgiveness isn't a ledger, and I'm not asking you to balance it. I'm asking you to know the truth of who walks beside you, and decide—with open eyes—if you can bear the weight of it. If you can't, I'll go. Tonight. You and Ashfall take the east road, I'll draw the hunters west, and you'll likely live longer for it."

"Don't offer me that," Wren snapped. "Don't you dare dress abandonment as sacrifice."

"I'm not dressing it as anything. I'm offering it because it's true. You'd be safer without me. Everyone I've ever tried to protect is dead or worse, and the pattern is old enough now that I'd be a fool to call it coincidence."

"Then maybe stop protecting people," Wren said, "and start standing *with* them. There's a difference. Did they teach you that in the Citadel yards, hunt-captain? No. Of course not. They taught you that protection is something done *to* people, from above, with iron."

The words landed harder than Wren probably knew, because they were true, and Sera flinched from them visibly, and Wren saw the flinch and pressed.

"My aunt died at dawn," they said, quieter now, which was somehow worse. "My mother woke for thirty years hearing bolts hit a door that never opened again. And you rode at the front. You drew the map. And then you spent twelve years being *sorry*, sorry in comfortable rooms, sorry on long rides, sorry the way soldiers are sorry—which is to say, sorry about how it sits in your chest, not sorry enough to change anything until changing something cost you nothing."

"That's not—" Sera started.

"The child," Wren said. "In the capital. The one you refused to kill. That's the story, isn't it? That's the moment you became good. You refused one child and lost your commission and now you wear disgrace like armor, like it proves something. But refusing one killing doesn't unmake leading hundreds. Disgrace isn't redemption, Sera. It's just a smaller cage."

Silence. The owl called again. Ashfall rose, slow and enormous, and stood between them—not blocking, exactly. Just present. A grey wall of patience.

"He's right," Sera said, to the wolf as much as to Wren, because saying it to Wren directly felt like begging. "Everything they said. Every word. I've said worse to myself at three in the morning for twelve years." She looked up. "But here's what I know that three-in-the-morning-Sera doesn't. You're right that disgrace isn't redemption. Nothing is redemption. There is no version of me that pays this back. Maren Vane is dead and I helped kill her and no number of saved children changes the shape of that. So the question was never *how do I become clean.* The question is *what do I do with the hands I have.* And these hands know how to read ground, and track riders, and fight, and lie to witch-finders in their own grammar. Those are ugly skills. They're also the ones that might get you to that altar alive. Hate me if you need to. Use me regardless. I'd rather be useful and hated than forgiven and useless."

Wren stared at her for a long moment. The fury in their face didn't fade—that would have been too easy, and neither of them believed in easy anymore—but it shifted, settled, compressed itself into something denser and more survivable. Grief wearing anger's clothes.

"You should have told me at the ridge," Wren said. "The first night. Before I trusted you."

"Yes."

"You should have told me at the burned villages. When I asked why you flinched every time a survivor looked at you."

"Yes."

"You should have told me before Vey's house. Before I slept under his roof believing you'd die for me."

"Yes." Sera's voice cracked on it, finally, the discipline failing at last. "All of it, yes. I was a coward. I am a coward. I told myself you'd turn me away and I couldn't bear it, and then I told myself you'd turn me away and I *deserved* it, and both of those were just cowardice wearing different coats. I'm sorry. Not the tidy kind. The kind that doesn't fix anything."

Wren sat back down. Slowly, like someone lowering themselves onto ice they hadn't yet tested. They picked the venison strip out of the dirt, looked at it, and tossed it aside.

"I can't forgive you," they said. "Not tonight. Maybe not ever. My mother gets a vote in this and she's not here, and my aunt certainly doesn't get one, and I won't spend hers for her."

"I'm not asking for forgiveness."

"I know. That's the only reason I'm still sitting here." Wren fed a stick to the fire. "But I need you to understand something. I'm not keeping you close because I trust you. I'm keeping you close because in four days the sky breaks, and I would rather walk into that with someone whose sins I know than someone whose sins I don't. You've shown me your worst. That's worth more than your best, right now. Do you understand? Your honesty is the only part of you I trust. Don't ever take it back."

"Never," Sera said. "It's the only thing I have left that's mine."

Ashfall exhaled—a vast, gusting sigh, the sound of a judge closing a case he'd seen argued a thousand times—and lay back down, though his eyes stayed open, watching the dark beyond the firelight.

They sat in silence awhile. It was not a comfortable silence, but it was an honest one, and Sera found she could breathe inside it.

It was Wren who broke it, eventually, voice low. "The honeycomb. Every spring. She sent it every spring until the year she died." A pause. "Did you eat any of it? At Thornbrook. After."

Sera closed her eyes. "There were hives behind her house. Still humming. My riders wanted to smoke them out—wild bees near a raid site, protocol says destroy anything that answered her. I ordered them left alone." She opened her eyes. "First order I ever gave that contradicted the crown. It took me six more years to give the second."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes—not forgiveness, nothing so generous—but recognition, maybe. The terrible kinship of people shaped by the same loss from opposite sides.

Then the hawk screamed.

It came out of the north, high and ragged, circling once above the dead oak before dropping to a low branch—*their* hawk, the one from Vey's mews, feathers still crooked from the cage. Wren tilted their head, listening, and Sera watched the color drain from their face.

"What is it," Sera said, already reaching for her bow. "Wren. What is it."

"They're coming," Wren said. "Riders. Six of them, maybe eight, moving fast along the river road—they passed a farmstead an hour ago and the dogs are still hiding." They looked up, and the firelight caught something new in their expression: not fear. Arithmetic. "Vey didn't sell us to the crown, Sera. He sold us to the *witch-finders*. The general himself is riding with them."

Ashfall was already on his feet, hackles lifted, nose to the north wind.

"How long," Sera said.

"Before dawn," Wren said. "Maybe less."

* * *

They killed the fire and moved out under a sky already bruising toward its last quarter-moon, and Sera ran point because running point was the only prayer she knew. Behind her, Wren's footsteps were silent as falling snow, and ahead, somewhere in the black throat of the forest, the witch-finder general was already choosing his ground.

Sera had faced him once before, years ago, across a tribunal table. He had smiled at her then, too.

This time, she promised the dark, only one of them would smile at the end.
