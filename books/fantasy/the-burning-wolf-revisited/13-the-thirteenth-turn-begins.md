## 13. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

Wren saw the crack in the moon three nights before anyone else did.

They were camped in a hollow of birches above the Ashwater ford, and Wren had taken the last watch because sleep had become a country they could no longer find the border of. The moon hung low and swollen over the ridge, one night shy of its last quarter, and across its face—thin as a spider's thread of ice on a windowpane—ran a line of darkness that had no business being there.

Wren sat up so fast their spine cracked.

*Ashfall.*

The wolf's eyes opened without his body moving first, which was always unsettling, as if he slept with some other part of himself and kept the rest on duty. *You see it.*

*I see it.*

*Then it has begun.* He rose, joints unfolding like something ancient deciding to be young again for one more season. *Come. Wake the huntress. We have less time than I promised you.*

Sera was awake before Wren reached her bedroll—she was always awake before anyone reached her, a woman who slept like a knife in a drawer—and when Wren pointed at the sky she went very still, the way she did before violence or grief.

"That's not cloud," she said.

"No."

"Moons don't crack, Vane."

"This one is." Wren heard their own voice come out flat and strange, stripped of everything but fact. "The hedge-witch told me. When the covenant starts to fail, the moon shows it first. It's not really the moon. It's—" They searched for the words Ashfall had used, the old words. "It's the seam between the realms. And someone left it unstitched."

Below them, in the sleeping fields along the river, every crow in the valley began to scream at once.

* * *

By morning the world had started coming apart in small ways, and the small ways were worse than the large ones would have been.

The wheat first. They passed a farmstead where the stalks stood grey and brittle though autumn was weeks away, each head collapsed into itself like a fist unclenching after death. A woman stood in the ruined field pulling off her gloves finger by finger, slowly, as if the slowness might undo what her hands already knew. She didn't look at them. Nobody looked at anybody anymore; looking meant asking, and asking meant answers, and answers had stopped being survivable around the time the moon cracked.

"It's not blight," Wren murmured. They could feel it through their boots, through the soles of their feet, an absence where there should have been presence. Every living thing they passed had gone quiet in a way living things never were. "There's wild magic in soil. In seed. In the rot that feeds the roots. It's draining out."

"Draining where?"

"Nowhere." Ashfall loped beside them, head low. "That is what failing means. Not stolen. Not spent. Simply... ending. A fire does not go somewhere when it dies. It goes out."

Sera walked with her hand near her sword hilt, which was how she prayed. "And the beasts?"

As if summoned—the word was accurate and everyone knew it—a sound came down from the high pasture: dogs, many dogs, barking in a register Wren had never heard from dogs before. Not alarm. Alarm had shape and reason. This was the sound of animals who had lost the ability to be animals, who were feeling the covenant's absence as a kind of vertigo, a floor removed beneath instinct.

They crested the rise and found the shepherd's flock running in circles. Not fleeing anything. Running in a perfect, tireless circle, forty sheep moving as one body, hooves tearing the turf to mud, eyes rolling white. Two dogs snapped at their flanks trying to break the pattern and the sheep simply flowed around them like water around stones.

"They'll run until they die," Sera said.

"They're listening for something that isn't answering." Wren stepped forward before they decided to, before any part of them voted on it, and opened the door they had kept locked their whole life in Hollowmere.

They didn't command. That was the thing Ashfall had beaten into them through six weeks of brutal, patient lessons—that commanding was the corruption, the human arrogance stitched into the gift like a splinter of iron. They *listened*. They let their hearing open wide enough to hold the whole frantic flock, and then they made themselves into the thing the sheep were searching for: a still point, a voice in the silence, the old answer to the old question.

*Here,* Wren said, in the tongue that had no words. *I am here. You are not alone in your bodies. Rest.*

The circle broke like a dropped rope.

Forty sheep stood swaying in the mud, breathing hard, and then—one by one—they began to graze, heads down, ordinary again, and the two dogs lay down among them and shivered and were still.

The shepherd came running from her hut with a crook raised, saw the sheep calm, saw Wren standing in the field's center with their arms slightly out and their face wet without knowing why, and understood exactly what she was seeing. Wren braced for the scream, the running, the word *witch* flung like a stone.

Instead the old woman sank onto her knees in the mud and said, "Thank you," and wept.

Sera watched the whole exchange with an expression Wren couldn't read, and later, walking, she said only: "A month ago that woman would have reported you by sundown." A pause. "The world breaking makes strange room for mercy."

"The world breaking is also why we can't stop," Wren said. "Every day the crack widens, more flocks, more fields, more—" They gestured at everything. At the grey wheat, at the silent hedgerows where the songbirds should have been arguing about territory and weren't, at the sky itself with its wounded moon waiting patiently behind the daylight to show them all its hairline shame.

At the horizon to the north, where the Moonstone Altar waited in its ring of standing hills, a smudge of dust moved. Wagons. Many wagons.

Vey was already marching.

* * *

They caught the Moon Court's column two days later, or rather it caught them—a line of outriders sweeping the road ahead of the main force, and Sera had them off the road and flat in a drainage ditch before Wren even registered the hoofbeats. Through the dead nettles Wren watched riders pass close enough to count buttons: grey cloaks, silver crescents sewn at the shoulder, faces grim with purpose rather than cruelty. These were not crown hunters. These were believers.

"He's pulled half the resistance with him," Sera breathed. "Look at the supply train. That's not a raid. That's a migration."

"He told them the truth," Wren said, and hated how much it cost to say it. "The covenant's failing. The thirteenth turn has begun. Everything else he told them was a lie wrapped around it, but the truth was the hook, and people are drowning, so they bit."

From the ditch they watched the column pass for the better part of an hour. Cages rolled past on two of the wagons, and the creatures inside them—Wren refused to look, then made themselves look, because Ashfall had taught them that looking was the first duty—were quiet now. Too quiet. Whatever Vey was feeding them, whatever bindings he'd wound around them, the wild had been pressed out of them like water from a rag. One hawk turned its head as the wagon jolted, and its eye found Wren's across forty feet of road and nettles, and there was nothing in it. Nothing. An eye like a button. An eye like the wheat.

Wren's hands closed in the dirt.

*Not yet,* Ashfall said quietly, beside them. His wound from the ford had healed to a deep ache he carried in his gait, and Wren felt it as if it were their own hip. *Not yet. But soon, and when it comes, it will not be a rescue. Remember what he is building toward.*

"I know what he's building toward."

*Say it anyway. Say it until it bores you. Truth repeated becomes armor.*

"He wants to stand at the altar when the moon turns full," Wren whispered. "He wants to seize the renewal instead of make it. He wants the covenant reborn with him holding the leash end." They swallowed. "And if the stone rejects him the way it rejected everything else he's ever bound—"

*It will not reject him gently.*

"—then he won't just kill himself. He'll take the last chance with him. For everyone. Both realms."

Sera shifted beside them, mud to her jaw. "So we get there first."

"We get there *rightly* first," Ashfall corrected. "Rushing ahead of Vey accomplishes nothing if Wren arrives at the altar still half-convinced the gift is a weapon. The rite will know. The stone will know. The moon will know." He looked up at the pale day-ghost of the cracked moon hanging in the blue. "You cannot lie to a moon, little listener. It has watched every lie ever told and outlasted every liar. When you stand on that stone, whatever you truly believe about yourself will be the only thing you bring."

* * *

That night the frenzy spread.

They sheltered in the shell of a mill, and from the dark around it came sounds that Wren catalogued with a professional horror they hadn't possessed a year ago: foxes screaming in voices too big for foxes, a stallion somewhere running fences until something broke, insects gone silent in whole counties at once, and under all of it a hum Wren felt in their teeth—the sound, Ashfall said, of the wild magic thinning, of ten thousand small covenants between creature and creature fraying to threads.

Wren sat in the doorway with their knees drawn up and listened to the world lose its grammar.

"Tell me again," Sera said from the shadows inside. She asked every night now. Not because she forgot. Because saying it aloud was how she kept her nerve strung tight. "How long."

"Nine days until the full moon. The thirteenth turn ends when it reaches zenith over the altar hill. If no one renews the covenant before then—" Wren made themselves finish it, because unfinished sentences were a luxury. "The seam closes forever. Wild magic dies out of the world entirely. Every gifted person alive loses the gift or loses their mind, sometimes both. The beasts go feral or empty. And the realms seal apart so completely that no one born after will even know there was ever another side."

"And if Vey seizes it instead."

"Same ending. Different master."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "My grandmother used to say the moon was God's lantern, and the crack in it was Him checking whether anyone was still worth lighting the dark for." She laughed, short and bitter. "I thought it was a peasant's poetry. Now I catch myself looking up like she'll be proven right."

"She might be," Wren said. "In a way. The stone doesn't judge worth, exactly. But it judges truth. And right now there aren't many people walking toward that altar carrying any."

Ashfall rose from the corner where he'd been a darker shadow in the dark. *Sleep,* he said. *Both of you. Tomorrow we leave the roads entirely. There is a way to the altar hills that runs through the old forest, through ground that still remembers me, and it will take us there faster than Vey's wagons—but the forest is frightened, and frightened old things test strangers.* His eyes caught the moonlight and held it, cracked and all. *Tomorrow, Wren Vane, you must speak to a forest.*

Wren looked out at the broken dark and felt the fear arrive on schedule and sit down beside them like an old acquaintance.

"Will it listen?" they asked.

*That depends,* Ashfall said, *on whether you ask it as a commander or as a cousin. You have nine days to decide which one you are.*

Above the mill, through the ruined roof, the cracked moon climbed its slow arc toward the only fullness it had left, and the hairline of darkness across its face had grown, since the first night, long enough to name.

Everyone could see it now. Everyone was counting.

Nine days.
