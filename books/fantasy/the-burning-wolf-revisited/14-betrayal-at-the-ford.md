## 14. Betrayal at the Ford

**WREN**

The river at Greywater Ford ran shallow and quick over pale stones, and for three days it had been talking to me.

Not in words. Rivers didn't have words, not the way foxes did or hawks did. Rivers had *moods*—long slow sentences of cold and current—and for three days the mood had been wrong. Restless. The fish were holding deep. The heron that should have stood sentry at the crossing had gone somewhere else entirely.

"Something's coming," I told Ashfall.

The wolf lay on the bank with his grey flank half in the water, letting the current comb through his fur. He was thinner than he'd been at Vey's estate. We all were. Three weeks of moving by night, sleeping in ditches and hay barns, following a road that Sera swore would take us north to the Moonstone Altar before the thirteenth moon rose full.

"Something is always coming," Ashfall said. His voice was that strange doubled thing, like wind through two valleys at once. "The question is whether it comes for us."

"Sera thinks we can cross here and lose them in the fells."

"Sera thinks many things." There was no malice in it. Just an old animal patience, the kind that waits out storms. "I think the river is afraid, little listener. And rivers are rarely wrong about what's in them."

I crouched and put my hand flat on the water. Cold shot up my arm, and beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat under wool: iron. A lot of it. Upstream, maybe, or downstream—I couldn't tell direction from stone and silt—but iron, moving, and the particular sourness of men who carried witch-charms blessed by crown priests.

I pulled my hand back like I'd touched a stove.

"Ashfall—"

"I know." He was already up, water streaming off him, ears forward. "Get Sera."

I found her at the tree line, checking the straps on the packhorse we'd bought off a dead man outside Thornbury. She looked up when she heard me running, and whatever was in my face made her drop the strap mid-buckle.

"They're here," I said. "Hunters. In the water, maybe both banks. I don't know how many."

Her jaw went tight. That was the thing about Sera Kell—she never wasted a second on disbelief. She'd been a royal huntress for eleven years; she knew exactly what hunters in a river meant.

"How close?"

"Close enough that the fish left."

She cursed, low and fluent, and started pulling the pack apart, shoving what mattered into one bag. "There's a game trail, quarter mile east. Steep but fast. If we're over the ridge before they fan out—"

"If," said Ashfall, arriving silent as falling snow.

"You have a better idea?" Sera snapped. She'd stopped flinching at his voice weeks ago, which somehow made the arguing easier and stranger.

"No. But I want you to hear it plainly: this ford has been safe for two days. It is not safe now. Someone moved against us deliberately." His amber eyes found mine. "Someone who knew where we would be."

The thought landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone. Only four people alive knew our route. Me. Sera. Ashfall.

And Lord Aldric Vey, who had kissed my hands in his gilded hall and called me *the voice the wild has been waiting for*, and who had let us ride out at dawn with his blessing and his maps.

"He wouldn't," I said. Even as I said it, I heard how young I sounded.

Sera didn't say anything at all. She just stood there with the repacked bag in her fist, and her face did something complicated and old—the face of a woman doing arithmetic she'd done before, in another life, with other bodies.

"We argue later," she said finally. "Move now."

We moved.

* * *

**SERÁ**

The trail was everything Wren had promised and worse—loose shale, switchbacks, a drop on the left that went down and down into green shadow. The horse balked twice and Sera nearly left it, but the supplies were life, so she cursed it forward with her shoulder against its flank while Wren scrambled ahead and the wolf ghosted along above them on the ridge line, a grey shadow reading the wind.

They'd made perhaps a mile when the horns sounded behind them.

Two notes. Then a third, answered from ahead.

Sera froze, one hand on the horse's bridle, and did the map in her head. Horns south—they'd been seen at the ford. Horns east—a second party, already past them, already waiting. Which meant riders had been positioned along the trail before dawn, which meant someone had given the crown not just their location but their *route*.

Vey.

Gods rot him. Gods rot her own stupid hope, too, because some traitor part of her had known since the night she'd found the hawk cage in his cellar and said nothing loud enough. She'd told herself patience. Learn his plans. Use his roads. And all the while she'd been leading a gifted child and the last sworn wolf of the old covenant straight down a corridor with hunters at both ends.

"Wren." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "How many horns?"

Wren's eyes had gone distant—that listening look, head tilted, lips parted. "Six," they whispered. "No—seven. And dogs. Not hunting dogs. *Charm-dogs.* They'll smell the gift on me no matter how far I run."

Charm-dogs. Sera had handled them once, early in her career. Beasts bred in the king's kennels and fed tinctures until their noses could find magic like blood. They didn't bark when they found you. They *sang*. She'd heard one sing at a farmhouse outside Corleigh, eleven years ago, right before—

Right before.

"There's a stream bed below," she said, forcing the memory down where it lived with all the others. "It cuts under the trail. If we get in the water—"

"The water won't hide the gift." Wren's voice cracked. "Sera, the dogs aren't tracking *me*. They're tracking—" They pressed a hand flat against their own chest. "This."

Ashfall flowed down the slope to join them, hackles lifted, nose working the wind. "Then we split the scent," he said. "The dog-song follows the loudest gift. Little listener, your gift is louder than mine now—it has been since the lessons began. So I will run loud, and you will run quiet, and the huntress will take you north."

"No," Sera said.

"It wasn't a request, huntress."

"It wasn't yours to make." She stepped between them, between the wolf and the kid, and hated how her hands were already shaking. "Nobody splits up. Nobody plays decoy. We go down to the stream together and we—"

The first charm-dog sang.

It came up through the trees below them, a sound Sera had spent eleven years trying to forget—a high, wavering note like a child singing a lullaby wrong, wrong on purpose, wrong in a way that made the back of your neck crawl. The horse screamed and tore free of her grip, bolted down-trail, and was gone with half their food.

And then the woods filled with men.

* * *

**WREN**

Later, I would try to remember it as a sequence of decisions. That's what Sera always said survival was—not bravery, just decisions, made fast and clean. But what I remembered was noise: boots on shale, the wrong-lullaby song swelling from two directions, the flash of iron charms on black cloaks, and Sera's voice cutting through all of it like a whip.

"*Down!*"

She shoved me off the trail. We went down the bank in a slide of stones and roots, into the stream bed, cold water to the knee, and Ashfall came after us with a hunter's arrow already standing in the muscle of his shoulder. I hadn't even seen him shot. I hadn't seen anything except Sera turning around.

Turning *back up* the slope, toward the men.

"Sera—!"

"Seven of them, and only one of me knows how they think!" she shouted over her shoulder. Her sword was out—I'd never actually watched her draw it before; it moved like it was part of her arm. "The stream bends north in two hundred paces. Follow it. Don't stop, don't listen to the dogs, don't look back. *Ashfall, make her go.*"

"*Make him* go," Ashfall snarled, and then he put his huge head against my hip and shoved, and I stumbled north through the water because my body obeyed before my heart could vote.

I looked back anyway. Of course I looked back.

Sera Kell stood on the stream bank with the morning light behind her and seven crown hunters in front of her, and she fought the way water fights—no wasted motion, every parry becoming a cut, every cut buying a step. I saw her open a man's thigh to the bone. I saw her break a wrist with her pommel. I saw her take a crossbow bolt high in the left shoulder and barely stagger, spinning with it, using the turn.

And I saw the witch-finder's lieutenant—the tall one with the silver chain, the one whose voice I'd heard through the warding back in Hollowmere a lifetime ago—raise his hand and speak a word I didn't know, and the iron charms on every hunter's collar flare white.

Sera dropped like a puppet with cut strings.

I screamed. I don't remember deciding to scream. The sound came out of me raw and wordless, and the whole valley *heard* it—every bird went up at once, every beast in the fells answered, a chorus of panic and fury rolling down the slopes, and for one heartbeat the hunters froze, staring at the treeline, at the sky, at each other.

One heartbeat. Maybe two.

Ashfall's teeth closed on my sleeve and hauled, and then we were running—running north through the shallows while the water tried to pull my boots off and my lungs burned and somewhere behind us the charm-dogs' song climbed higher and higher, searching, searching, and losing.

Because Ashfall was running loud. I felt it—felt him pour himself into the gift like a struck bell, felt his old covenant-blood ring out across the valley, bright and enormous, dragging every enchanted nose in the hunt after him like a lure. He was bleeding as he ran. The arrow-head worked deeper with every stride. He didn't slow.

By full dark we had left the stream, crossed a sheep track, and collapsed in a hollow under a fallen rowan, and Ashfall finally let the bell go silent, and fell over.

* * *

**SERÁ**

Consciousness came back in pieces: rope at her wrists, the smell of wet canvas and horse, the jolt of a cart. Night. Rain starting. Voices ahead, low, bored—the voices of men on escort duty who thought the hard part was over.

Sera kept her breathing shallow and her eyes shut and took inventory. Shoulder wound, bolt's still in it, wrapped—someone had field-dressed her, which meant they wanted her alive. Wrists bound in front, badly. Boots still on. Sword gone, obviously. Knife gone. Everything gone except—

Except the small bone whistle on its cord under her collar, the one Wren had carved for her at Vey's estate from a rowan branch, joking that it was useless, that Sera couldn't call beasts anyway, that it was just so she'd have something of theirs.

Useless. Except that Wren had breathed on it every night for a week without knowing why, the way gifted folk breathed luck into things, and Sera had watched, and said nothing, and kept it.

The cart hit a rut. She used the jolt to test the ropes—one seam gave, a finger's width. Good. Sloppy knots meant tired men meant a camp with a lax watch rotation, probably, if she could stay alive long enough to reach it.

Alive long enough. She almost laughed, and turned it into a cough instead. Eleven years ago she'd led a raid on a farmhouse outside Corleigh and put a gifted woman in chains very much like these, and that woman hadn't gotten a sloppy knot and a field dressing. That woman had gotten a pyre. Sera had stood at attention for it. Had written the report. Had slept afterward, God help her, *slept*.

Now the wheel had come around, and she was the cargo, and somewhere north a kid with beast-speech and a wounded wolf were running blind through the fells believing she was dead or worse, and Aldric Vey sat in his gilded hall counting his thirty pieces.

Because it *was* Vey. She'd known it on the trail and she knew it now, colder. Who else had the route? Who else had wanted them away from his estate, alone, unguarded, delivered into crown hands without his fingerprints? He hadn't sold them to destroy them. He'd sold them to *clear the board*—let the hunters kill the wolf and cage the girl, and then the Moon Court's precious covenant-race would have only one runner left: him. He'd use Wren's capture as proof the crown couldn't be fought, recruit the terrified, march on the altar with an army of orphans.

Or he'd have done, if Wren was captured. If the wolf was dead.

The cart slowed. Torchlight bled orange through her eyelids. A gate groaned—wood, not iron, a waystation then, not a fortress. Two men argued about wine. A third unhitched the horse.

Sera opened her eyes a slit and saw the watch rotation, and it was worse than lax. It was a joke. Four men, one fire, all of them facing the warmth.

Her shoulder screamed when she moved. She made herself breathe through it, the way she'd taught recruits to breathe through it, and worked the frayed seam, fiber by fiber, while the rain came down soft as forgiveness she hadn't earned.

Hold on, kid, she thought, to a girl a valley away who might be dead, who had screamed with a voice that woke the whole wild world. Hold on. I'm coming. I've got eleven years of sins and one good map in my head, and I am *coming*.

The rope parted.

* * *

**WREN**

He died twice that night, I think. Or came close enough that the difference stopped mattering.

I knelt in the mud under the rowan with my hands buried in his fur, and the arrowhead was deep, past where I could reach, and the wound smelled wrong—there was something on the barb, some hunter's alchemy, a sickness that crept outward from the metal like frost. Every time I pushed my listening into him I felt the same terrible thing: not pain, exactly. *Distance.* Ashfall going away from me by inches, retreating into some old dark place where the covenant was young and he was young and none of this had happened yet.

"Stay," I whispered. "Stay with me. You swore the first covenant—you don't get to leave before the last one."

His eye rolled up to me, amber gone dull. "*Little listener,*" he breathed, and the doubled voice had gone thin, one valley instead of two. "If I go under—do not let them take the altar without a wolf. Find... the old packs. In the northern fells. Tell them Ashfall kept the vow. Tell them..."

"Don't." My voice broke. "Don't give me a message. Give me tomorrow."

He huffed something that might have been a laugh, and then his eyes closed, and his breathing went shallow and slow, and I sat in the freezing mud holding a dying god's head in my lap while the rain came down and the moon rose—waxing, fat, and scarred now with a hairline crack of shadow that no astronomer could explain.

Thirteen moons, the hedge-witch had said. The count was nearly done.

I had no Sera. I had no route. I had no idea which of the people I'd trusted had sold us, and no way to know if the next one would. Every trust I owned was either broken or bleeding out under my hands.

But the rowan above us stirred, though there was no wind. And far off, down the southern slopes, something howled—not a challenge, not a hunt-call. A question. Old-voiced, wild-throated, asking in the true tongue:

*Who keeps the vow?*

Ashfall's ear twitched, once, toward the sound.

And I understood that the wild had heard us fall—and that it was waiting, crack-mooned and desperate, to see what I would do next.
