## 15. Alone Under a Cracking Sky

The cave was narrow and dry, its mouth hidden behind a curtain of hanging birch roots that Wren had spent an hour weaving into something like camouflage. Inside, the dark was total except for the small fire they dared to keep—a fist of flame in a ring of stones, fed with deadfall gathered from far away so the smoke would carry no scent of home.

Ashfall lay on his side near the fire, his grey flank rising and falling in long, labored breaths. The wound in his shoulder had stopped bleeding by the time they'd reached the cave, but the flesh around it was hot and swollen, and when Wren pressed a poultice of yarrow and comfrey against it, the wolf made no sound at all. That silence frightened Wren more than any cry could have. Ashfall, who had spoken the first words of the old tongue Wren had ever heard from another mouth, who remembered the covenant before it broke—Ashfall, silent.

"Drink," Wren said, holding a hollowed gourd of water to his muzzle.

The wolf's amber eye opened, found them, and held there for a long moment. Then he drank, slow and careful, and closed his eye again.

*You should not have come back for me,* he said at last, the words arriving not through Wren's gift but directly, the way he always spoke—inside the skull, behind the eyes, older than sound.

"You shouldn't have thrown yourself between me and that bolt."

*It is what I swore.* A pause. His breathing hitched. *Though I confess I did not imagine the oath would be tested quite so soon.*

"Don't joke."

*I am not joking. I am terrified.* The eye opened again, and there was something in it Wren had never seen before—not the ancient calm, not the wry patience, but something rawer. *I am three hundred years old, little listener, and I am afraid of a crossbow bolt. The world has grown strange.*

Wren sat back against the cave wall and let the silence settle. Outside, the sky was doing what it had done for six days now: cracking. Not visibly—not yet—but anyone who knew how to look could see it. The moon rose each night with another hairline fracture across its face, thin as a spider's thread, and each night the wild things grew more restless. The foxes had gone quiet. The birds flew in patterns that made no sense. And Wren's gift—the listening that had been their whole life's secret, then their whole life's shame, then their whole life's purpose—came to them now through a haze, as if every voice were speaking underwater.

They changed Ashfall's dressing again as the light outside went from grey to violet to black. The poultice was working, or seemed to be; the swelling had crept down half a finger's width since morning. But fever still burned in him, and fever in a creature that old, a creature woven through with wild magic, was a dangerous thing. When the moon cracked, the magic failed. And when the magic failed—

*When the magic fails,* Ashfall said, reading the thought as easily as Wren read a crow's warning, *I do not know what happens to one such as me. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything.* He huffed, a ghost of his usual humor. *Perhaps I become merely a very large dog with opinions.*

Despite everything, Wren laughed—a short, broken sound—and then pressed their face against the wolf's uninjured shoulder and stayed there until the shaking stopped.

* * *

It came in the smallest hour of the night, when the fire had burned down to embers and the cold pressed in against the birch-root curtain like a living thing.

Wren woke to a smell of frost and river-stones, though there was no river within miles. The embers did not gutter. They simply turned pale, all their heat draining upward into nothing, and the shadows in the cave rearranged themselves—not moving, exactly, but agreeing among themselves to be different shapes.

Standing at the mouth of the cave, inside the curtain of roots where nothing had entered, was a figure made of the moment just before dawn. Grey-white, translucent, neither tall nor short, with a face that Wren's eyes refused to hold—every time they looked directly at it, the face slid toward being someone they had known. Their mother. Sera. Themselves.

*Wren Vane,* it said, and its voice was the voice of the valley wind, the one that used to sing through Hollowmere's wards. *Listener. Child of the between.*

Wren's hand found a stone. It was a stupid gesture and they both knew it.

"What are you?"

*A passerby. A well-wisher. One who walks the edges of things.* The spirit drifted closer without seeming to move. *I heard your grief from a long way off. Grief carries, this season. Everything carries, this season—it has nowhere else to go.*

"Ashfall needs a healer. There's a woman in the lowlands—"

*He does not need a healer.* The spirit's face settled, briefly, into something almost kind. *He needs the magic that is leaving the world. And you need what you have always needed, Wren Vane, if you will forgive my plainness: you need to stop being hunted.*

The words landed in the center of Wren's chest and stayed there, glowing like a coal.

"I don't know what you mean."

*You do.* The spirit crouched beside Ashfall—not touching, but near, the way one crouches near a dying fire. *Your gift sings out of you like a beacon. Every hunter, every witch-finder, every iron charm in these mountains turns toward it the way a compass turns north. As long as you carry it, you will run until your legs give out, and then they will take you, and they will take the old wolf too, and the covenant will die unwitnessed.* It raised a hand, and in its palm appeared something small and dull and ordinary: a smooth grey pebble. *But it does not have to be that way.*

Wren stared at the pebble. "That's nothing."

*It is everything. It is a renunciation. Swallow it—or press it to your heart, or bury it beneath your blood, the method hardly matters—and your gift will pass out of you. Quietly. Cleanly. No pain, or almost none. You will be deaf to beasts forever after, yes. But you will also be invisible. The hunters will walk past you in the road and see only a goatherd. You could go home, Wren Vane. Back to Hollowmere. Back to your mother's kitchen and the goats and the long safe silence.* The spirit's voice softened into something like tenderness. *You could live.*

The fire crackled. Somewhere beyond the birch roots, an owl called—a real owl, a normal owl, saying nothing Wren could understand anymore because the gift had grown so faint.

And Wren understood, with a lurch, that the spirit was right about the facts. Every word of it. The gift was a beacon. The hunters were closing. Sera was gone—taken at the ford, dragged toward Greyhollow, her fate unknown. Ashfall was wounded and might die regardless. The thirteenth moon was failing, and the rite at the Moonstone Altar required a bearer of both blood-rights, which meant it required Wren, which meant that every power in the world—crown and court and crumbling heaven itself—was converging on the single point where Wren stood.

Renounce, and all of it fell away. Renounce, and be free.

*You are thinking of your mother's kitchen,* the spirit said gently. *Good. That is where thoughts of safety belong.*

"I'm thinking," Wren said slowly, "that you're offering me exactly what the hunters want."

The spirit went very still.

"The crown wants gifted folk emptied of their gifts," Wren continued, and their voice shook but did not stop. "They burn us or they break us, and either way the world goes quiet. And here you come, out of a cracking sky, offering to empty me painlessly. No fire. No iron. Just a little grey pebble and a long safe silence." They stood up. Their legs were unsteady and their eyes were hot and they kept talking anyway. "How is that different? Tell me how that's different from what the witch-finder general does."

*It is different because you would choose it.*

"No." Wren's voice cracked. "No, it isn't. Because they'd choose it too, in the end. Everyone breaks. You're just asking me to break early, while it's comfortable, while there's still a kitchen to go back to." They looked down at Ashfall, at the great grey mountain of him, breathing shallowly in the ember-light. "He told me the covenant wasn't domination. It was promise. Humans and wild things, vowing to hear each other. My whole life I thought my gift was a sickness I hid. Now I know it's half of an old vow, and the other half is dying, and you want me to spit out my half so I can sleep warm at night."

*The world will end anyway,* the spirit said, and now its voice had lost its tenderness, gone flat and cold as the frost-smell. *The moon cracks whether you speak or not. The realms sever whether you listen or not. Your vow will save nothing. It will only cost you everything, and the cost will buy a grave.*

"Maybe." Wren picked up the pebble from where the spirit had set it on the stone floor. It was heavier than it looked. Or maybe that was only their hand shaking. "Maybe the rite fails. Maybe I die at that altar. Maybe Ashfall dies in this cave tomorrow and I bury him alone under a broken sky." They turned the pebble over once, twice. "But I'd rather die a bridge than live a wall."

For a long moment the spirit said nothing. The shadows held their new shapes. The embers gave no heat.

Then the figure smiled—really smiled, for the first time, and the smile was terrible and beautiful and old—and the pebble in Wren's palm dissolved into cold water and ran away between their fingers.

*Well answered,* it said. *Though I confess I never doubted. I am not what I claimed, little listener. I am the last fragment of the valley's ward—the seal your foxes told you of, the covenant-seal of Hollowmere, dying as all seals die. I came to offer you the coward's road because someone had to offer it, and because the choosing must be true. A vow spoken with no other path open is no vow at all.* It began to fade, growing thinner than dawn-mist. *You chose. So the vow can be made. Carry it to the altar. And when you speak there—if you speak there—speak loudly enough for those of us who could not choose.*

"What's your name?" Wren asked, as the figure thinned to a shimmer, then to a memory of shimmer.

*We were never given names,* the spirit said, from everywhere and nowhere. *Only functions. Perhaps that was our mistake. Name me, someday, if you remember. Names are promises too.*

And then the cave was only a cave, and the fire was only a fire, and the cold was only cold.

* * *

Wren sat awake until the grey light came, the pebble's chill still ghosting their palm.

Ashfall woke near dawn, fevered but lucid, and Wren told him everything—the spirit, the offer, the refusal, the dissolving stone. The wolf listened with his ears forward, motionless, and when it was done he was quiet a long time.

*You refused survival,* he said finally. *Do you understand how rare that is? In three hundred years I have watched humans choose safety over selfhood so many times that I stopped counting. Whole peoples. Whole generations, folding themselves smaller and smaller until nothing remained but the hiding.* His tail moved, once, against the stone. *You were offered the kitchen, and you chose the storm.*

"I chose the truth," Wren said. "The storm just comes with it."

*The same thing,* Ashfall said. *It was always the same thing.*

His fever broke that afternoon. By evening he could stand, swaying, leaning hard against Wren's shoulder, and he drank the broth Wren made from the last of their dried meat and licked the yarrow paste from the fresh dressing with something approaching appetite. He would live. Wren let themselves believe it fully only when the wolf walked, stiff and slow, to the cave mouth and looked out at the night.

The moon was rising. Wren joined him at the threshold and looked up.

The crack had widened. What had been a hairline was now a seam, plain to any eye, silver-black against the silver-white, running from the moon's northern rim nearly to its heart. And as they watched, a second fracture branched from the first, fine as a lightning scar.

*Three nights,* Ashfall said quietly. *Perhaps four. Then the thirteenth turn completes, and whatever is decided at the Moonstone Altar is decided forever.*

"Then we leave tomorrow at first light."

*We leave tonight.* The wolf turned his head, and his amber eye reflected the broken moon in two shattered halves. *Because we are not the only ones who watched it widen. Look at the ridgeline, little listener. Look at the fires.*

Wren looked. Along the distant ridge, tiny in the black, fires were kindling—one, then three, then a dozen, strung eastward like beads on a wire. Too many for hunters. Too organized for refugees.

*Vey marches for the altar,* Ashfall said. *And the crown marches behind him. And somewhere between here and there, in a fortress called Greyhollow, a huntress who refused to kill a child is deciding whether she is still ours.* He stepped out onto the ledge, favoring his wounded shoulder, and lifted his muzzle to the fractured sky. *We march tonight, Wren Vane. On three legs if we must. Because the moon is breaking—and I think, I truly think, that it is waiting for you.*
