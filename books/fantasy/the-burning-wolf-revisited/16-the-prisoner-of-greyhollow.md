## 16. The Prisoner of Greyhollow

They took her weapons first, then her name.

"State your rank and company," said the gate-sergeant at Greyhollow, a squat man with a waxed mustache and the eyes of someone who had counted too many corpses to be impressed by one more.

"Sera Kell," she said. "Formerly First Huntress of the Crown's Order. Currently a prisoner of my own poor choices."

The sergeant blinked. Most captives lied. It was almost refreshing, this woman handing him the truth like a coin she no longer wanted.

They marched her through the outer yard, past the kennels where hounds bayed at nothing—no, not nothing, Sera thought. At the wards. Greyhollow had been built on an old covenant-stone; she'd known that much from her years in the Order. The crown had raised its fortress on the bones of something older, the way children build forts on graves. The hounds could smell it. That was why they never stopped barking.

The cell they gave her was better than most. A window slit, a straw pallet, a bucket. Luxury, by hunter standards. She sat on the pallet and began doing what she did best, which was waiting, and while she waited, she began doing the second thing she did best, which was counting.

Three guards on the wall rotation, changed every two hours. One captain, Verrick, who drank but never enough to dull him. A witch-finder's delegation arrived yesterday—she'd heard the horses, seen the grey cloaks from her window slit—and with them came maps. Maps meant plans. Plans meant the altar.

And somewhere out in the dark, a wounded wolf and a frightened kid were marching toward that altar believing she was dead or worse.

*Hold on,* she thought at them, uselessly, the way you throw a rope across a river knowing it won't reach. *I'm coming.*

She just had to figure out how.

* * *

On the third day, they came to question her, and she was ready.

Not Witch-Finder General Corvane himself—his reputation preceded him by about six feet and a lifetime of cruelty—but his under-finder, a nervous young man named Pell who carried a ledger and asked questions in the order they were written rather than the order that mattered.

"You were First Huntress," Pell said. "You refused the order at Millbrook."

"I refused to cut a child's throat, yes. I prefer the fuller phrasing."

He flushed and consulted his ledger. "The General wishes to know what you know of the gifted one traveling with the wolf. There are reports. A beast-speaker, unregistered, heading east."

Sera leaned back against the stone and smiled the smile she'd once used on bandits and barons alike—the one that said *I know something you want, and we both know it.*

"I know everything about them," she said. "I've been tracking them for weeks. Closer than your riders ever got. Ask yourself why the General is questioning a disgraced prisoner instead of reading his scouts' reports."

Pell's pen hovered. "Because the scouts' reports are... inconsistent."

"They're garbage. Your riders spook the birds, the birds warn the quarry, and the quarry vanishes. You need someone who knows how the gifted move." She let the silence stretch. "You need me."

"The General doesn't negotiate with traitors."

"No," Sera agreed pleasantly. "He uses them. There's a difference, and it works in my favor. Here's my offer: information, accurate and verifiable, in exchange for time. Not freedom—I know better than to ask. Time. Keep me fed, keep me breathing, and give me access to the war room charts. I'll tell you where the beast-speaker will be three days before your riders find empty air."

It was a dangerous game and they both knew it. Pell chewed the end of his pen.

"Why would you betray them? You refused to kill a child for them."

Sera looked at him steadily. "Because I'm going to be hanged either way, Pell. The only question is whether I hang useful or useless. And because—" she let a crack show in her voice, carefully measured, "—because I watched what the gift does when it goes untrained. I have reasons."

All of it was true except the shape of it. That was the trick of lying well: build the house out of true boards.

Pell left without agreeing. But he left the ledger behind for a moment, and Sera read it upside down from across the cell, as she had been trained to do twenty years ago by a woman whose name she still didn't say aloud. Supply manifests. Grain counts. And one line, circled twice in Corvane's own hand:

*Altar requisition: containment vessels, six. Blood-iron chains, twelve pairs. The King requires the covenant whole and portable.*

Whole and portable. Sera turned the words over all night, and by dawn she understood, and understanding felt like swallowing ice.

They weren't marching to destroy the covenant. They weren't even marching to stop Wren. They were marching to *harvest* it—to catch the renewal rite in iron vessels, bottle the pact between human and wild, and carry it back to the capital so the king could own magic the way he owned grain and gallows and girls' futures.

And if the covenant couldn't be caught whole—if it renewed freely, wild and unowned—they would break it themselves and call the shattering mercy.

* * *

They gave her the war room on the fifth day.

Not trust—arrogance. Corvane had returned from the eastern ridges, and he liked to display his prisoners like trophies, liked them to see the machine they'd failed to outrun. So Sera stood in the great map room between two guards, studying the charts of the Moonstone Altar and its approaches, memorizing everything while pretending to be broken.

Corvane stood at the table's head, a tall man with a voice like dry paper. "The gifted one will run to the altar," he said. "They always do. The wolf will die there. The huntress—" his pale eyes found hers, "—will be dealt with after. Tell me, Kell. Where does the beast-speaker cross the Sorrel Ford?"

"North bank," Sera said. "High ground side. The gifted distrust water—it muffles the voices they hear."

It was true, and it was also a lie, because Wren had crossed at the south shallows days ago and was miles beyond it now. Every truth she sold them bought her another day inside the walls. Every day inside the walls taught her another piece of the machine.

And the machine had a heart she hadn't expected: the containment vessels weren't in the supply train. They were already at the altar. Corvane's engineers had gone ahead weeks ago, under cover of the purge, and set them into the altar stones themselves—six iron hearts buried in the Moonstone's ring, waiting to drink whatever power woke there.

Which meant the crown wasn't chasing the thirteenth moon.

The crown had been *preparing* for it since before Wren ever fled Hollowmere. Since before the wards broke. Perhaps—cold thought, colder certainty—since before Wren was born.

The purge wasn't a hunt. It was a harvest crew clearing the field.

Sera kept her face still and her breathing slow and thought about a girl in Millbrook with her mother's green eyes, and about a kid named Wren Vane who spoke to goats and wolves and had never hurt anything that didn't deserve it, and about a wolf with a torn shoulder who remembered the world when it was whole.

*Cunning,* her old teacher used to say, *is just love wearing armor. Don't be ashamed of the armor. Be ashamed if you forget what's underneath it.*

"Captain Verrick," Corvane said, "double the watch tonight. Our guest has been very helpful, which means she is planning something."

So much for subtlety. Sera bowed her head, humble as a nun, and began revising.

* * *

She escaped on the seventh night, during the change of the wall watch, in the hour when the kennel hounds finally went quiet—which they only did, she realized too late to be surprised, when something in the old covenant-stone beneath the fortress *answered* them.

She'd spent two days preparing. Bribing the turnkey with a ring she'd hidden in her boot heel—he was a soft-eyed man missing a son, and she told him his boy had been seen alive in the southern camps, which might even have been true. Loosening the bars of the storeroom vent over four nights with a spoon handle and patience. Stealing, from the war room's disordered margins, the thing she valued above her own skin: a hand-drawn chart of the altar site, marked with the six vessel positions, the crown's approach routes, and—circled in Corvane's ink—a single annotation that had made her blood run cold:

*If renewal cannot be contained, collapse the ring. Casualties acceptable. The King prefers a dead covenant to a free one.*

She folded the map into oilcloth and sewed it flat against her ribs, under the shirt, where a knife wound wouldn't reach it.

The vent was narrow and the night was freezing and she crawled through rat filth thinking, with a kind of hysterical clarity, *I escaped a royal fortress through a hole, at forty-one years of age, for a wolf and a goat-herd.* Her old comrades would have laughed until they wept.

She dropped into the inner yard, rolled under a cart, froze as boots passed, then moved—wall shadow to wall shadow, the way she'd taught recruits a decade ago, back when she still believed the Order stood for something.

At the kennels she stopped. The hounds were silent, all of them, standing at their chains and staring east with their ears pricked, and in the silence Sera heard—or imagined she heard, though she knew better than to imagine anything anymore—a low sound underneath the world, like a voice too large to fit through the air.

The old stone under Greyhollow, waking up. The covenant, fraying, reaching for anyone who could hear.

"Hold on," she whispered to it, to the hounds, to herself. "We're coming. All of us."

Then she went over the wall on a rope made of knotted bed-linens, like a storybook thief, and ran east into the dark with a stolen map against her ribs and seven days of the enemy's plans folded into her memory.

Behind her, the alarm bell began to toll. Ahead of her, over the black ridgeline, the cracked moon hung low and bleeding light, and beneath it—small, distant, unmistakable—moved two shapes: one limping, one upright, one wolf and one Wren, silhouetted against the breaking sky.

Sera put her head down and ran toward them, and the last thought she had before the chase swallowed everything else was simple and fierce as a struck flint:

*I'm coming. And this time, I'm bringing the whole war with me.*
