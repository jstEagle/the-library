## 17. Reunion of Strays

Sera Kell had followed worse trails through worse country, but she had never followed one that hurt like this.

The signs were everywhere once she knew to look. A smear of blood on the bark of an alder, high up—too high for a man, the right height for a wolf's shoulder. A broken arrow fletched in crown grey, snapped and discarded in the shallows of a stream, as if someone had wanted it found. And beneath it all, faint as breath on a winter window, the smell of burnt fur and old iron that she had come to know too well in her years as a huntress.

They had taken Sera at the ford because she had let them. That was the truth she carried now like a stone in her boot. She could have run. She should have run. But Vey's riders had been closing on Wren's position, and a huntress knows which way the quarry flees, and so Sera had turned her horse into their path and shouted words she didn't mean and bought thirty seconds with her freedom. Thirty seconds. It had felt cheap at the time. In Greyhollow's cells, listening to the witch-finder general describe what he intended for the gifted girl from Hollowmere, it had come to feel like the only honest coin she'd ever spent.

She had escaped with a stolen map and a stolen knife and a stolen hour of darkness, and she had not stopped moving since. Three days. Her ribs ached where a guard's boot had found them. Her hands were raw from climbing the ravine wall. None of it mattered. What mattered was the trail, and the trail was fresh.

The blood was getting worse.

Sera crouched at the edge of a clearing where the pines thinned toward high scree, and her stomach dropped. The prints in the soft ground told the story plainly: a wolf, limping badly, favoring the left foreleg, its gait shortening with every stride. Beside the prints, human boots—smaller, lighter, the stride of someone who kept stopping to wait. Wren. Dragging behind a wounded animal out of loyalty or stubbornness or both, slowing themselves down for a creature most of the world would have put a bolt through without a second thought.

*You should have left him,* Sera thought, and then, immediately, *no. Thank every god there is that you didn't.* Because if Wren Vane was the kind of person who abandoned a wounded friend to save their own skin, then everything Sera had bled for at the ford would have been wasted on the wrong soul.

The trail bent north, toward the caves above the river gorge. Sera knew them. Every royal huntress knew them—shallow mouths, deep bellies, cold stone that hid heat-signature from hounds. If Wren had any instinct at all, they'd gone to ground there.

If the hunters had any instinct at all, they'd already thought of it too.

She moved faster after that, ignoring the fire in her ribs, keeping to the shadow line where the trees threw their longest shade. Twice she heard voices on the wind—patrols, sweeping wide, methodical. The crown did not give up. That was the thing people never understood about witch-hunters: they weren't fanatics, mostly. They were patient. They treated the hunting of gifted folk like a harvest, and harvests do not hurry.

Near dusk, she found the cave.

She knew it was the right one because a raven sat on the ledge above the mouth, watching her with an attention no ordinary bird gave anything. Sera stopped in the open, well away from the shadows, and raised both hands, palms out—the old gesture, the surrender sign, the one that meant *I am empty-handed and I know whose territory this is.*

"I'm alone," she said. "I'm the one from the ford."

The raven tilted its head. From inside the cave came a low sound—not quite a growl, not quite a word. A question in a language older than either.

"I know you can hear me," Sera said carefully. "Both of you. I don't have much time before those patrols swing back this way, so I'll say it plain. I let them take me so Wren could run. I got free. I stole a map out of Greyhollow that says the crown means to take the covenant's power at the altar—for the king, not to destroy it. And I came here because I made a promise I haven't kept yet, and I don't leave promises broken."

Silence. Long enough that her arms began to ache from holding them up.

Then Wren stepped out of the dark.

They looked terrible. Hollow-cheeked, mud-splattered, eyes rimmed red with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion. They held a knife low against their thigh, and their stance said they hadn't decided whether to use it. Behind them, half-visible in the cave's gloom, a vast grey shape lay on its side, flank rising and falling in shallow, labored breaths. Ashfall. Even wounded and dimmed, the wolf filled the space the way a fire fills a room.

"You," Wren said. Their voice was hoarse. "You're supposed to be in a cell."

"I was." Sera lowered her hands slowly. "Cells are easier to get out of than graves. Ask anyone who's dug one."

"Don't." The knife came up an inch. "Don't make jokes. Not—" Wren's breath caught, and Sera saw the crack run through them, grief and fury fighting for the same door. "Vey sold us. He sold *us*, and you—you were captured covering my escape, and I ran, I just ran, and Ashfall took a crossbow bolt meant for my spine, and now you walk out of the woods like none of it—"

"Like none of it cost?" Sera stepped forward. Wren flinched; she kept coming anyway, until she stood close enough to see the tear tracks cutting clean lines through the dirt on their face. "Look at me, Wren Vane. Look at my hands." She turned them over, showing the rope burns, the split skin from the ravine climb. "Look at my ribs, if you want proof—I cracked two getting over the wall, and I'd crack the other ten tomorrow. I'm not walking around untouched. Nobody walks away from this untouched. The only question is what we carry forward and what we leave in the mud."

Wren's jaw worked. The knife wavered.

"He betrayed us," they whispered. "Aldric Vey. He smiled at us and fed us and called us the future of the Moon Court, and he sold our location to the witch-finders like we were—like we were *stock*."

"Yes."

"And I trusted him."

"Yes," Sera said again, gentler now. "So did I, once, in another life, with another charming man wearing another fine coat. Trust isn't the sin, Wren. Trusting the wrong person is just the tuition we pay for learning to read faces. You've paid it. I've paid it twice. We're graduates now, gods help us."

Something in Wren's shoulders loosened—a fraction, barely visible, but Sera had spent twenty years reading bodies and she caught it. The knife lowered. Not sheathed. Lowered.

"Ashfall's bad," Wren said, and their voice broke open on the words. "The bolt was barbed. I got it out but the wound's fouled, and he won't let me pack it properly, and he keeps saying—"

"I keep saying," rumbled a voice from the cave, deep as a landslide, "that I have survived worse and the cub fusses like an old nanny goat."

Sera's breath left her. She had known, intellectually, that the wolf spoke—that Wren had told her as much on the road, in fragments, between burned villages. Knowing and hearing were different countries. The sound of it went through her like a bell through water.

Ashfall's head lifted from the stone, amber eyes finding her across the distance. One ear was torn. The great grey body was streaked with dried blood and the yellow crust of infection, and still he regarded her with the unhurried gravity of a king reviewing a petition.

"So," he said. "The disgraced huntress. My cub has spoken of you. Mostly unkindly, lately. Come in, Sera Kell. If you mean us harm, you've walked very far to deliver it, and I respect the commitment."

* * *

Wren

The cave smelled of pine smoke and poultice and the copper-sweetness of a wound that wasn't healing right. Wren knelt beside Ashfall with their hands buried in his fur, feeling the heat radiating off the injured leg, and tried not to think about how thin he'd gotten in three days. Wolves were not supposed to look fragile. Ashfall looked like he was carved from winter.

"The barbed head tore the muscle when it came out," he was saying, in that voice that always seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his throat. "Your stitching is adequate. Your fretting is not."

"If you die, I will be so angry at you."

"Noted." His tail moved—a ghost of its old sweep. "The huntress watches us. Ask her why she really came. Not the map. The other reason."

Wren glanced up. Sera sat across the fire ring, having insisted on building it small and screened, having insisted on taking first watch despite her ribs, having insisted on everything with that stubborn competence that Wren was trying very hard not to find comforting. She was turning the stolen map over in her fingers, and her face in the firelight was unreadable in the way trained faces are.

"She doesn't need a reason beyond the map," Wren said.

"Everyone needs a reason beyond the map." Ashfall's eye closed. "Ask anyway. It matters more than she thinks."

So Wren asked. "Why did you really come, Sera? The map's a reason. It's not the whole one."

Sera was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When she spoke, she didn't look up from the map.

"When I refused the order at Millbrook—the gifted child, the one they made me choose about—I told myself it was principle. Rules are rules, even for hunters, and killing children breaks them. Clean logic. Easy to live with." Her thumb traced the map's edge. "But that's not true, and I've known it wasn't true for years. The child had your eyes, Wren. Not the color. The *look*. That look of someone listening to something no one else can hear, terrified of being caught at it. I saw that look and something in me said: that was my sister. That was Mara, before they came for her." She finally raised her head. "I hunted you to warn you because I couldn't save her. That's the whole ugly truth. I've been trying to save her for eleven years by saving everyone who looks like her."

The cave was very still. Somewhere outside, water dripped from the gorge rocks, counting seconds.

"Mara," Wren said slowly. "Mara Vane."

Sera's face didn't change, but her hands went white-knuckled on the map. "You know the name."

"She was my aunt." Wren's voice came out strange, distant, like it belonged to someone else. "My mother's sister. She left Hollowmere before I was born. Mother never speaks of her except once a year, on a night in autumn, when she lights a candle in the window and won't say why. I used to think—" Wren stopped. Started again. "The raid. The one in the eastern valleys. That was you."

"That was me," Sera said. "I led it. I was good at leading raids. I was the best they had, and I was proud of it, right up until the moment I stood in a doorway looking at a frightened child with your eyes and understood exactly what I'd spent my life becoming." She set the map down. "I'm not asking forgiveness tonight. I may never ask it. I'm telling you because Ashfall's price is truth, and because you deserve to know who's standing between you and the witch-finders—an honest enemy of your family, or nothing at all."

Wren's hands had gone still in Ashfall's fur. Something enormous was moving through them, slow and tectonic—rage, yes, hot and bright, but underneath it something else, something that sounded horribly like understanding. *She lit a candle. Mother lights a candle. And this woman has been walking toward that candle for eleven years through the dark.*

"I'm furious with you," Wren said at last.

"I know."

"But Ashfall took a bolt for me and you took a cell for me, and I can't—" Wren pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "I can't do the arithmetic tonight. Tomorrow maybe. Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Sera agreed softly. "That's fair arithmetic."

From the shadows, Ashfall made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Cubs," he said. "Both of you. Now stop circling and look at the map. The moon does not wait for forgiveness, and neither does the thirteenth turn."

* * *

Sera

They spread the map flat on the stone between them, weighted at the corners with pebbles, and Sera watched Wren's face as the stolen intelligence unfolded under the firelight. This was the part she'd rehearsed on the road, the part that mattered: not the escape, not the confession, but this. Knowledge was the only weapon she had left worth carrying.

"Greyhollow isn't just a fortress," she said. "It's a counting house. The crown has been cataloguing gifted folk for decades—names, gifts, bloodlines. And this—" she tapped a sealed annotation in the margin, written in the witch-finder general's own cramped hand, "—is why. Read it aloud. I want to hear how it sounds in your voice."

Wren leaned close. Their lips moved silently first, then aloud: "'The covenant-power cannot be harvested from a bearer directly. It must be renewed at the source to be taken at the source. Prepare the Altar. Await the thirteenth moon.'" They looked up. "Harvested. They talk about it like grain."

"They talk about everything like grain. People, power, realms—it's all yield to them." Sera pulled the map's second sheet from her coat, the one she'd nearly died for, covered in diagrams copied from the fortress archive. "Here's what the archives actually say about the original covenant, stripped of all the church gloss. Look."

The diagrams showed the Moonstone Altar from four angles, and around it, inscribed in careful copyist's script, fragments of the old rite. Wren went very still over them.

"This matches what Ashfall taught me," they breathed. "The vows. The—here, this phrase, *by the blood that sows and the blood that runs wild*—he made me repeat it until I dreamed it."

"Because it's the hinge of the whole thing." Sera traced the inscription. "I spent three days in a cell doing nothing but listening, and the guards talk when they think the prisoner's asleep. The crown's scholars have been arguing about this line for a century. *Blood that sows*—that's settled earth, farmland, the human claim. *Blood that runs wild*—the old magic, the beasts, the other realm. The rite requires a renewer who holds both rights. Both bloods. Freely given, freely held."

"Every scholar assumes that means a pact between two parties," Wren said slowly. "One human, one wild. Two voices at the altar."

"Doesn't it?"

"No." Wren's gaze had gone somewhere else—to the middle distance, to some interior landscape Sera couldn't follow. "Ashfall. Tell her."

The wolf dragged himself upright with a grunt of effort, and Sera's hand twitched toward him instinctively before she mastered it. Up close, wounded, he was more terrifying, not less—age and pain had refined him down to essentials, and the essentials were vast.

"The covenant was never signed by ambassadors," Ashfall said. "It was signed by a child of both worlds. There were such children once, in the early years, before the realms grew suspicious of each other. Human stock, wild blood-right—born of unions the old chronicles blush to describe. The last of them spoke the first covenant alone at the altar, one throat holding both vows, because only such a one could promise *for* both sides rather than merely *between* them." His amber eyes moved to Wren, and there was something in them Sera had never seen in any creature, tame or wild: reverence. "And the line did not end, little cub. It went underground. Into hiding. Into valleys with wards woven from the covenant itself, where families forgot what ran in their veins."

The silence stretched so long Sera could hear her own heartbeat.

"Wren," she said carefully. "What's he saying?"

"My mother hums to the goats," Wren said, very quietly. "Not songs. Not quite songs. Things that make the ewes calm and the dogs sit and the bees stay in their yard without smoke. She always said it was just a habit. Just nonsense her own mother taught her." Their hand rose to their chest, pressing flat, as if checking that their heart still beat behind it. "And my grandmother came to Hollowmere from *outside*. Nobody ever said from where. And the ward—the foxes told me the ward was a covenant-seal, woven by someone with the gift, and it answered to *me*, Sera. It frayed when the hunters came, but it *answered to me*. I thought that was because of my beast-speech. I thought—"

"It's because of your blood," Ashfall finished. "The ward knew its maker's heir. You are not merely gifted, Wren Vane. You are the last vessel of the double blood-right. The crown wants to harvest the covenant's renewal through whatever vessel performs it—and there is only one vessel in all the world. You."

Sera watched Wren absorb it the way ground absorbs rain: slowly, deeply, with visible settling. Fear first. Then a strange, dawning steadiness, like someone who has wandered lost for months suddenly recognizing a landmark.

"Then the crown doesn't need to capture me to use me," Wren said. "They need me willing. Or they need me desperate enough to be forced. Either way, everything converges on the altar, and everything converges on *me*."

"Which is why," Sera said, and here she allowed herself the smallest grim smile, "we arrive first, prepared, and on our terms. I have the map. I have the patrol rotations memorized. And I have—" she hesitated, then reached into her coat and drew out the last thing she'd stolen from Greyhollow, a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, "—the general's private journal. He annotates everything. Ego like a cathedral. It'll tell us his timing, his forces, his assumptions. His assumptions are always his weakest wall."

Wren stared at the journal, then at her. "You stole three things from an impregnable fortress."

"I stole four. The fourth was myself." She tucked the journal into Wren's hands. "We have nine days until the thirteenth moon. Ashfall needs to heal enough to travel—there are herbs in the gorge, yarrow and comfrey, I saw them on the climb. Wren, you need to learn the rest of the rite. And I need to get a message to the survivors we met on the road of ashes. The Moon Court may be Vey's poisoned chalice, but the people who whispered to us in the burned villages—they're real, and they're angry, and they'll want to stand at that altar when it happens."

"Together," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question. They looked at her across the fire, and Sera saw the fury still banked there, and the grief, and underneath both—fragile as frost on a morning window, but present—the beginning of trust. "You and me and him. Strays. All three of us strays."

"All the best packs start that way," Ashfall rumbled.

Sera almost smiled. Then the raven on the ledge above the cave mouth croaked—once, sharp, urgent—and every hair on her arms stood up.

Ashfall's head swung toward the entrance. "Riders," he said. "Two miles out. Moving fast. They've found the stream trail."

Sera was already on her feet, ribs screaming, mind snapping into the old cold clarity. "Then we've got until moonrise to break trail north," she said. "Nine days to the altar, witch-finders at our backs, and the fate of both realms sitting in a cave eating yarrow." She grabbed her pack. "I hope everyone's rested. Because the easy part is over."

Outside, the cracked moon was rising over the gorge, and its hairline fracture gleamed like a scar across the face of heaven.
