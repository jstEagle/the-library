## 18. March of the Broken Moon

The moon cracked on a Tuesday.

That was how Wren would remember it afterward—not as an omen or a wonder, but as an ordinary day made suddenly unbearable. They had been mending a saddle strap in the cave's mouth when the light changed. Not dimmed. *Changed*—as if someone had run a fingernail down the face of the sky and left a seam.

Ashfall saw it first. The wolf had been sleeping, or something like sleeping, his wound still weeping through Sera's poultices, but he lifted his head with a sound Wren felt in their own chest, low and involuntary, the way a dog howls at a bell only it can hear.

"Look," he said.

Wren looked. High above the eastern ridge, where the moon hung pale and full even in daylight, a hairline fracture ran from horn to horn of it, thin as a spider's thread, glinting like frost on glass.

"It's begun," Wren said. Their voice came out flat. There was no room in it for anything else.

"Six nights," Ashfall said. "Perhaps five. When the thirteenth moon sets whole, the covenant dies with it. And everything that depends on the covenant—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Wren could feel it already, a faint static under their skin where the beast-speech lived, like a radio losing its signal. That morning the sparrows' chatter had come to them blurred, syllables smeared together, and a stag they'd spoken with two weeks past had stared at them with eyes gone dull and confused, no longer certain what they were.

The world was forgetting them. Or they were forgetting the world. Either way, the door was closing.

Sera came up the trail at a run, her grey cloak snapping, a dead hare over her shoulder and news in her face before she'd said a word.

"Vey's moving," she said, dropping the hare. "A farmer two valleys over saw them—forty riders, maybe more, all in moon-white livery, heading for the Altar road. And behind them—" She paused, and Wren watched her decide whether to say it. "Behind them, crown banners. The witch-finder general is marching too. They're not chasing each other anymore. They're racing."

"For the altar."

"For whatever's left inside it." Sera crouched, caught her breath, looked up at Wren with those flat grey eyes that had once been a huntress's eyes and were now something harder to name. "We're three people and a wounded wolf, Vane. We need to stop pretending we can win this alone."

Wren looked at the cracked moon. Then at Ashfall, who was watching them with the patient amber attention he always gave when he already knew what they were going to say.

"No," Wren agreed. "But I know some people who owe us favors."

* * *

The hawk came first.

Wren had freed her from Lord Aldric Vey's gilded cages a lifetime ago—or eleven days ago, which amounted to the same thing—and they had found her again by accident, or by something that wasn't accident, circling the cave at dawn with a broken wing feather and an expression of unmistakable intent. Her name, in the old tongue, was a sound like wind through a snare-wire. Wren called her Kess because their mouth couldn't make the other sound anymore, not cleanly, and she answered to it anyway, which told Wren something about forgiveness that they weren't ready to think about.

Now she sat on Sera's forearm, one talon gripping leather, and listened while Wren laid out the problem: the Altar road, the converging armies, six nights, three bodies.

*Kess,* Wren spoke into the failing signal of the wild tongue, *I need eyes. All of them.*

*I know what you need,* she sent back, sharp as a knife drawn slow. *What I want to know is what you'll give.*

"The truth," Wren said aloud, because speaking aloud cost nothing and meant everything. "When this is done, there will be no cages. Not mine, not anyone's. That's the promise I'm marching under. If you fly for me, you fly for that."

Kess tilted her head, one eye a gold coin reflecting a cracked moon.

*Then I'll bring you the mountain,* she said. *Every wing that can still hear me. The falcons of the high crags. The owls who remember the Moon Court when it was honest.* A pause, and something almost like humor: *And the crows. The crows will come for free. Crows love a funeral.*

She launched off Sera's arm and was gone into the white sky, and Wren stood watching until the speck of her vanished, feeling—for the first time since the ford, since Ashfall's blood on their hands and Sera's scream swallowed by the river—not entirely alone.

"She'll do it?" Sera asked.

"She'd have done it anyway. But now she knows why."

* * *

They went down out of the hills toward the valley villages, because Wren had done the arithmetic Sera refused to do and understood that three fighters could not hold an altar—but a crowd might delay an army long enough to matter.

The first village was called Brackenlow, and it had burned twice. Once by the purge, months ago, and once by accident, or by grief, or by the particular carelessness of people who had stopped believing tomorrow would come. Half its houses were charred skeletons. The other half leaned together like old men sharing a secret.

The survivors remembered Wren.

Not kindly, all of them. It had been here, on the Road of Ashes, that Wren had used the beast-speech openly for the first time—had called a runaway cart-horse to a halt with a word no human throat should shape, saving a child named Tam from beneath its hooves, and in doing so had painted a target on every soul in the square. Three days later the hunters came. Tam's family had fled; others hadn't moved fast enough.

An old woman named Maren met them at the well, arms crossed, face like a closed door.

"You," she said. "You're the reason the soldiers came."

"I'm the reason they came," Wren said. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But they're coming again—worse ones, more of them, and after them a lord who wants to chain every wild thing left in the world. And after *him*, nothing. Silence. The end of the old pact." They held up a hand toward the sky, where the crack in the moon was visible even now, even in afternoon light, if you knew to look. "You can see it. Everyone can see it. Ask yourselves why."

Maren looked. The village looked. For a long moment nobody breathed.

"My grandmother used to say," Maren said finally, slowly, "that the moon was a door, and the door was held shut by a promise. She said it the way you say things your grandmother says. Like a story." Her jaw worked. "It doesn't look like a story now."

"No," Wren said. "It doesn't."

"What do you want?"

"Not your swords. Most of you don't have any. I want your carts, your food, your knowledge of these roads—and I want your voices. When this is over, whoever wins, someone will have to tell the truth about what happened here. About what was taken and what was given back. I can't be the only one telling it."

Maren studied them for a long time. Behind her, the villagers had gathered—the burned and the half-burned, the ones with nothing left to lose except the losing itself.

"My son died in the purge," she said. "Shot running. He wasn't gifted. He was just standing near someone who was." She spat into the dust. "I've been waiting eleven years for a reason to hate the crown properly. You've brought me one with a cracked moon on top of it. That's better than most reasons."

By dusk, four carts were loaded, and nine people of Brackenlow were coming with them.

* * *

The repentant hunter found *them*, which was somehow worse.

He stepped out of the treeline on the second morning with his hands raised and his iron charm torn from his neck, lying in the mud where he'd dropped it, and Wren's heart stopped before Sera's blade cleared its sheath.

His name was Dannel Rusk. Wren knew him. Everyone who had ever fled a hunter patrol knew him—he was the tall one, the quiet one, the one whose face had been on the wrong side of every burning for a decade. He stood there in his black-and-silver coat with his hands open and said, "I heard the hawk talking about you."

Sera's blade did not lower. "Hawks don't talk."

"This one does. To people who've learned to listen." His voice was steady, but his hands shook, and Wren—whose whole life had been listening—heard what was underneath the steadiness. Grief, worn smooth as river stone. "I've spent ten years hunting gift-bearers. I told myself it was law. Then I told myself it was mercy, because the alternative was worse men doing it crueler. Last month I watched the witch-finder general burn a boy of fourteen in a market square, and I stopped being able to tell myself anything at all." He swallowed. "I know the general's route to the altar. I know his numbers, his supply line, where he sleeps. I know because I helped plan it."

"Why should we believe you?" Sera said. It wasn't a question; it was a wall.

"Because I'm going to stand between you and him whether you trust me or not," Rusk said simply. "Trust is yours to give. My part isn't asking for it."

Silence. Ashfall padded forward, and Wren felt the wolf's attention settle on the man like a weight—smelling him, reading him in the way wolves read, in sweat and heartbeat and the chemistry of fear.

*He is telling the truth,* Ashfall said at last. *Or he has told himself the same lie so long it has become true. With humans, I am told, these are often the same thing.*

"That's not comforting," Sera muttered.

"It wasn't meant to be."

They took him with them. Sera walked ten paces behind him the entire day, and Wren did not ask her to stop.

* * *

They gathered like rain gathering.

By the fourth night, camped in a hollow below the Altar road, Wren counted what they had become: nine villagers of Brackenlow with four carts and a smuggler's knowledge of goat-paths; Kess and eleven birds of prey roosting in the pines above, silent as held breath; a murder of crows that came and went like rumors; Dannel Rusk, cleaning a sword he swore never to use again unless there was no other way; Sera Kell, sharpening hers without any such oath; and Ashfall, who lay at the fire's edge with his wound bound and his eyes open, watching the cracked moon climb.

Word had spread ahead of them, carried by wings and by the strange new urgency in the wild tongue—a language fraying at its edges, words dissolving even as they were spoken, so that messages arrived half-guessed, like letters read through water. Still they came. Two sisters from the fen country who could call eels. A stonemason whose hands ached near old wards. A boy of twelve who turned out to be able to speak to moths, which everyone agreed was nearly useless until the moths began reporting the positions of the crown's night patrols with perfect accuracy.

"We look like a beggar's parade," Sera said, sitting down beside Wren at the fire.

"We look like what we are."

"And what's that?"

Wren thought about it. Around the fire, the fen-sisters were teaching the moth-boy a counting song; Rusk sat apart, as he always did, but nearer than yesterday; Maren of Brackenlow was arguing cheerfully with a falcon about the ethics of stealing sausages.

"A door," Wren said finally. "Opening."

Sera snorted, but she didn't argue, and after a while she said, quietly, "If we fail tomorrow—"

"When."

"If we fail," she insisted, "I want you to know it wasn't for nothing. Any of it. Your aunt. The road. The—" She stopped. She had said *your aunt* and both of them heard the confession underneath it, the one made in chapter and chapter ago, the raid, the fire, the name Wren had screamed at her across a riverbank. Things between them had healed the way bone heals: crooked, aching, but holding.

"I know," Wren said. "It wasn't for nothing. None of it."

Ashfall rose, stiff-legged, and came to sit between them, and for a moment the three of them watched the fire and the fractured moon above it, and nobody needed to say anything else.

* * *

On the fifth morning, the reports came in from every direction at once.

Kess dove into camp before dawn with the falcons wheeling behind her, and her message came through the failing tongue in fragments, sharp and urgent: *Riders—white—altar road—two days—behind them the black coats—faster—they mean to arrive together—*

"They'll reach the altar the same night the moon sets," Wren translated for the others. "Both of them. Vey and the witch-finder, arriving side by side, each planning to betray the other the moment the stone opens."

"Charming," Sera said. "At least they're consistent."

"There's a goat-path," Maren said, spreading her map on a cart-tail. "Shepherd's track, comes up the north face. Hard going, but it puts us above the altar terrace a full day before either army crests the south road. If we move tonight—"

"We move tonight," Wren said.

The camp broke apart and reassembled itself into a column: carts first, then the walkers, then the riders, then the shapes in the trees that were not people at all. The fen-sisters walked barefoot. The moth-boy rode on a cart between sacks of barley, whispering directions to a cloud of grey wings that scattered ahead of them like scouts. Rusk took point, because he knew the crown's pickets and could walk through them like weather. Sera walked beside Wren, and Ashfall ranged the flanks, a grey shadow among shadows.

They climbed through the last of the daylight and into the dark, and somewhere around midnight they came over the shoulder of the north ridge and saw it: the Moonstone Altar, far below in its bowl of ancient stones, pale as a knuckle of bone under the broken moon. Even at this distance Wren could feel it—a hum in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, the sensation of standing near something vast and asleep.

Around the altar's rim, torches were already being planted. White livery. Moon-white. Vey's vanguard had arrived early, and they were digging in.

"They beat us here," Sera breathed.

"To the ground," Wren said. "Not to the stone." They looked at Ashfall, and Ashfall looked back, and something passed between them that needed no tongue at all, old or new. "The rite needs a hand that holds both blood-rights. Vey has neither. The crown has neither. All they can do is wait by a locked door and fight each other over the handle."

"And us?"

"We're the key." Wren turned to the ragged column behind them—the villagers, the sisters, the boy, the penitent, the huntress, the wolf, the wings in the dark overhead. Every face turned toward them, lit by a cracked and failing moon, waiting. Wren had spent their whole life learning to be small, to be silent, to be nothing. Standing here, they felt the size of what they had become, and it terrified them, and they let it.

"Rest," Wren said. "Eat. Tomorrow at moonrise, we go down."

And below them, in the bowl of stones, the torches of two armies burned against each other's light, and the cracked moon climbed toward its final setting, and the hairline seam in the sky glittered like the first crack in an egg—something old straining toward something new, and no one, anywhere, certain which would hatch.
