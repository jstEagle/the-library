## 19. The Moonstone Altar

The moon rose cracked.

Wren saw it first from the saddle of the ridge above the vale, a hairline of black splitting the white face like ice on a pond, and felt it in their teeth—a wrongness, a note held too long. Below, the Moonstone Altar stood in its ring of standing stones, pale as a knuckle of bone pushed up through the earth. It had waited three hundred years for this night. So had everyone coming to kill each other around it.

"You feel that?" Sera said beside them, nocking an arrow without looking at the sky. She'd been doing that all day—nocking arrows at nothing, at the shape of what was coming. "The whole valley's holding its breath."

*The stone is waking,* Ashfall said. He limped at Wren's left side, his wound from the ford bound in moss and hawk-feather charm, his gait still carrying the memory of the hunter's blade. *It has been asleep so long it does not know whether it wakes to a vow or to a theft.*

"Then we make sure it's a vow," Wren said.

They came down through the birches as the last light failed, and the vale was already filling. Vey's Moon Court held the near ground—two hundred strong in grey and silver, banners of the crescent snapping in a wind that had no business being cold in high summer. Across the water meadow, torchlight: the crown's witch-hunters, iron charms swinging from every standard, and at their center a palanquin of black lacquer from which the witch-finder general had not yet emerged.

And between them, the altar, empty, patient, indifferent to both armies the way a mountain is indifferent to weather.

"There," Sera breathed.

Vey had come down to the stones alone. That was the first wrong thing. He wore no armor. He wore white, actually—white like a bridegroom or a sacrifice—and he walked the circle of standing stones with his hands open at his sides, touching each one as he passed, and where he touched, the old carvings *glowed*, faint as foxfire, and Wren's stomach dropped because they should not have done that. The stones answered no one. The stones answered only the covenant.

"He's been feeding them," Ashfall growled. "Bound creatures. Blood. For months. He has taught the stones to know his hand."

* * *

They meant to reach the altar before moonrise. They made it to the outer ring of birches before Vey turned, found Wren across two hundred yards of open meadow with the unerring instinct of a man who had spent weeks learning exactly what Wren Vane was worth, and smiled.

"Wren!" His voice carried effortlessly, pitched for the armies both. "Come and stand witness! You of all people deserve to see the old lie end!"

He raised his arms to the cracked moon and began to speak.

Not the true tongue. Wren knew the true tongue now the way you know your mother's voice—Ashfall had drilled it into them through a winter of nights, listening-not-commanding, promise-not-demand. What Vey spoke was the covenant's grammar broken into pieces and reassembled as a chain: words of binding, words of seizure, the old vow inverted so that instead of *I offer, the wild accepts* it ran *I take, the wild submits.*

The stones flared.

For one heartbeat it looked like it worked. Light poured up out of the altar like water finding a breach, silver-white, blinding, and the Moon Court went to its knees, and somewhere behind Wren a hundred hunters began to shout, and Vey laughed—a rich, delighted, terrible sound—and reached into the light as if into a river to gather it in his fists.

Then the mountain got weather of its own.

The light stopped. Simply stopped, mid-pour, hanging in the air like a held breath. And the altar spoke—not in words Wren understood but in a pressure, a frequency, a sound below sound that Wren felt in the marrow and the goats had always felt first back in Hollowmere when storms were coming.

Vey's hands closed on nothing. The light slid through his fingers like minnows.

"No," he said. Quietly at first. Then again, louder, the composure cracking off him like paint: "*No.* I have given you everything! I have kept your creatures alive when the crown would have burned them! I have *fed* you—"

The stones answered.

The nearest monolith—the one his palm had touched, the one that glowed for him—all its foxfire light reversed, pouring *into* the carving rather than out, and Vey was lifted off his feet as though the ground had decided he weighed nothing. He hung there a moment, arms wide, white clothes streaming, a marionette of the earth itself, and the light that had loved his touch now crawled over him hungrily, reading him, weighing him, finding in him everything the covenant was not.

He screamed. It was not a noble scream.

The stones threw him. There was no gentler word. He landed twenty feet away in the meadow grass, smoking faintly, and did not rise, and the silence that followed lasted exactly long enough for the witch-finder general's palanquin to crack open like a seedpod.

* * *

She was smaller than Wren expected. Grey-robed, iron-crowned, her face seamed and patient as a plowman's, and she stepped down onto the wet grass without ceremony while her hunters formed ranks around her with the smooth mechanical grace of people who had done this a thousand times.

"The abomination fails," she said, in a voice like dry paper, addressing no one and everyone. "As all abominations fail. Seize the stone. Burn the gifted. Begin with the wolf."

Crossbows came up along the whole line.

"*Down!*" Sera shouted, and hauled Wren flat as the first volley hissed overhead, and then the night came apart.

The Moon Court, leaderless, broke toward the hunters with the desperate fury of people who had just watched their salvation throw their savior away. The hunters advanced in squares, iron charms blazing, and where iron met wild magic the air itself screamed—that same sub-sound, that wrongness, magnified until Wren's vision swam. Fire arced from the Court's hedge-mages and died against the iron wards. Arrows took mages out of the air mid-gesture. It was not a battle; it was a threshing floor, and both sides were wheat.

"We can't cross that," Sera said, flat, professional, already moving anyway, using a dead hedgerow for cover, pulling Wren by the wrist. "But we don't have to. Look—the stones. The circle's warded against *them* too. Iron won't pass the ring. If we get inside—"

"If we get inside, then what? Vey couldn't hold the rite. Why would I be able to?"

Sera stopped. Turned. In the strobing light of burning hedge-magic her face was very young and very old at once.

"Because he tried to *take*," she said. "You're going to *give*. Ashfall told me what he's been teaching you. I didn't understand it until right now." She gripped Wren's shoulder, hard, huntress-hard. "That's the whole rite, isn't it? That's been the whole rite since Hollowmere. Everything you learned about listening." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "Go listen, Wren Vane. I'll keep them off you."

"Sera—"

"I dueled better men than that paper crow for ten years." She was already turning, arrow to string. "Go."

* * *

Wren ran.

Across the meadow, through the threshing, low under the arcs of dying fire, Ashfall a grey shadow at their flank, taking a crossbow bolt meant for them on the shoulder-plate of old scar tissue and not even breaking stride. The ring of stones rose ahead, and the hunters saw them coming—of course they did, a lone figure sprinting for the altar on the night the world ended—and a wedge of them peeled off in pursuit, and at their head, unhurried, terrible, the witch-finder general herself.

Iron-crowned. Iron-charmed. Walking through wild magic's screaming like a woman walking through rain.

Wren crossed the ring.

It was like passing through a curtain of cold water. The battle's noise cut to nothing—not silence but *attention*, the whole circle leaning inward, the stones' glow steadying now, warming, recognizing something. The carved spirals brightened under Wren's feet with every step, and the cracked moon overhead poured down light that felt less like light than like a question asked thirteen times.

At the altar's heart, the Moonstone itself: waist-high, egg-smooth, veined with silver. Warm. Warm as a living thing.

Wren laid both palms on it.

And heard everything.

Every bound creature Vey had caged, every prayer the gifted had whispered into hiding for three hundred years, every vow the old world had spoken and the new world had forgotten—the whole broken conversation between human and wild, still going, all this time, fainter and fainter, like voices calling down a well. And beneath it all, the covenant itself, frayed to its last threads, asking the only question it had ever asked:

*Will you?*

"Yes," Wren whispered. "I will. I—"

The circle's ward shattered.

Not broke—*shattered*, iron-driven, the general standing at the ring's edge with her crown blazing, her hunters driving an iron pike into the earth at the boundary like a nail into a coffin lid, and the cold-water curtain tore apart and the battle's roar flooded back in and hunters poured through the gap with steel drawn.

Wren spun, palms still on the stone, and could not leave it—the rite had hold of them now, threads of silver running from the Moonstone up their arms, and if they let go now it would all fray past saving. Trapped at the heart of the circle, gift spread open like a snared bird, watching hunters close from every side.

The general walked through them. Her hunters parted around their own commander, and she came on alone, unhurried, drawing a long thin blade that drank the moonlight, and stopped three paces from the altar.

"Child," she said, almost kindly. "I have burned forty years of your kind. Do you know what they all say, at the end? *I only wanted to help.*" She raised the blade. "The stone cannot save you. Stone is stone."

"It was never stone," Wren said.

And the vale answered.

A howl—from the treeline, then another, then dozens, wolves and things older than wolves, the freed creatures of Vey's cages come home to the oldest altar in the world. The standing stones blazed full-white. The cracked moon overhead shuddered like a struck bell.

And between the general and the altar, out of the smoke and the threshing-fire, walked Sera Kell—bloody, bowless, sword drawn, grinning like the disgrace they'd cast her out for.

"General Voss," she said. "You signed my warrant yourself. I've been meaning to return it."

The witch-finder general regarded her for a long, cold moment, and something almost like pleasure moved behind her plowman's face.

"Huntress Kell. The one who wouldn't kill the child." She settled into her guard, blade low, patient. "I have wondered for six years what became of you. Come and show me."

They hit like the end of the world in miniature—steel ringing steel, two schools of killing distilled to their last argument—and Wren could only watch, palms bleeding on the Moonstone, the covenant's question still hanging in the air, *will you, will you, will you,* while ten feet away the woman who'd saved them fought for the seconds Wren needed, and was losing them, step by step, backward, toward the stones—

The general's blade opened Sera's sword-arm to the elbow.

Sera staggered. Dropped to one knee. Did not fall.

"**WREN!**" she roared, blood flying, grin gone feral. "**STOP WATCHING ME AND FINISH IT!**"

And the moon cracked wider, and the stone burned under Wren's hands, and every voice in the well of the world went silent, waiting on the answer.
