## 22. Homecoming, Rewritten

The road into Hollowmere had not changed, but the valley had.

Wren felt it before they saw it—a silence gone from the air, like a hand lifted from a throat. For as long as Wren could remember, crossing the ridge had meant passing through something unseen and heavy, a hush laid over the land by hands long dead. The wards. Now there was nothing. The wind moved through the pines without flinching. A hawk rode the thermals over the eastern slope without circling twice, wary of nothing.

"It's strange," Sera said beside her, leading her mare at an easy walk. "I've breached warded valleys before. There's always a moment where the hair on your arms stands up. Like walking through a spiderweb you can't see." She shook her head. "There's nothing here now. Just a valley."

"Just a valley," Wren echoed, and found the words sat strangely in their mouth.

Behind them, Ashfall moved through the bracken with the particular silence of a wolf who has decided to be heard when he chooses and unheard otherwise. He had said little in three days. Wolves, Wren had learned, did not spend words the way people spent coin. They saved them for what mattered.

"You're afraid," Ashfall said now, falling into step on Wren's other side. His voice was that low gravel-and-river sound that still, after everything, made the hairs on Wren's neck rise—not with fear anymore, but with recognition.

"Of course I'm afraid."

"Good," the wolf said. "Fear means you understand what home is worth."

* * *

They came down past the shepherd's cairn, past the blackthorn hedge where Wren had once hidden from chores and whispered to the field mice until the mice grew bold enough to sit in their cupped palms. The hedge was in bloom out of season—white flowers opening in the late autumn cold, drinking in the wild magic that flowed unbroken through the valley now. Everywhere Wren looked, the land was doing things it had never dared do under the wards. Moss climbed higher on the stones. A fox watched them from a fencepost with open curiosity instead of skulking flight. Somewhere upslope, a nightingale sang at midday, confused and joyful.

The village proper came into view at the bend of the road: the low stone houses, the smoke rising straight in the windless air, the mill wheel turning slow and brown-gold over the mere. People were in the fields. That was the first thing that stopped Wren's breath—people standing in the autumn fields, and among them, moving freely, deer. A heron stood in the shallows of the millrace not six feet from a fisherman who was pointedly ignoring it. A child ran along the fence line pursued by an enormous shaggy dog that was, unmistakably, laughing.

"They came back," Wren said quietly. "The wild ones. When the wards fell, they just... came back."

"They never left," Ashfall said. "They were kept out. There's a difference your valley took two hundred years to forget."

Sera reined up and looked down the street toward the Vane house—the stone cottage with the blue door, the herb garden gone gloriously riotous since no one had been trimming it to ward-approved dullness. "I'll wait at the inn," she said. "This isn't a conversation for an audience."

"Sera—"

"I shot your aunt's killers' commander in the shoulder four months ago and helped topple a witch-finder general last week. I think I've earned the right to give you one afternoon alone with your mother." But her voice was gentle, and her hand, brief on Wren's shoulder, was gentler still. Then she turned the mare and clattered off toward the inn, and Wren watched her go with the specific ache of someone watching a person they had learned to need walk away even temporarily.

"She'll be back before dark," Ashfall observed.

"I know."

"You worry anyway."

"I know that too."

* * *

The blue door was open.

That was the second thing that stopped Wren's breath. In all their childhood, the blue door had been closed against the world—closed against gossip, against questions, against the possibility that anyone might see inside and notice something wrong. Their mother had kept that door shut the way other people kept secrets: completely, and at cost.

Now it stood wide to the afternoon light, and Maren Vane was in the garden on her knees, her hands deep in the soil, transplanting something. She looked older than Wren remembered and younger at the same time—the lines deeper around her eyes, but her shoulders loose in a way Wren had never seen them. Loose. Unbraced. As if she had spent thirty years waiting for a blow and had finally, recently, been told it wasn't coming.

She looked up. And froze.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The trowel hung in her dirt-caked hand. A bee droned past between them, fat and golden and utterly indifferent to the weight of the moment.

"You look thin," her mother said finally, which was the most Maren Vane sentence in the history of mothers, and Wren laughed—a cracked, wet sound—and crossed the garden in five strides and knelt in the dirt and held her.

Her mother smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and turned earth. Her arms came around Wren hard enough to hurt, and she was shaking, Wren realized. Shaking and gripping the back of Wren's coat like the ground might take Wren back if she let go.

"I thought—" she began, and stopped, and started again. "Every night. Every night I lit the lamp in the window. Your father said I was wasting oil. I told him if he touched that lamp I'd put him out with it."

"The lamp in the window," Wren said into her shoulder. "I saw it. From the ridge. Three nights ago. I almost came then, but—"

"But you had a moon to mend." Her mother pulled back and took Wren's face in both hands, and studied them the way she used to study loaves of bread, turning, assessing, deciding whether the thing before her had come out right. Whatever she saw, her eyes filled. "You did it, then. It's true. The whole valley felt it—that night the sky went silver and every animal in Hollowmere stood up and looked at the moon at once. I thought my heart would stop."

"That was the covenant renewing. Everyone felt it?"

"Everyone felt it. Half the village fainted. Old Tam Brackle swore on his mother's grave it was the end of days, and then his cow, which hadn't given milk in six years, gave enough to drown a calf, and now he swears it was a blessing instead." Her mother's mouth twitched. "People are very flexible about the end of days when the milk comes back."

Wren laughed again, and then couldn't stop the tears, and her mother didn't pretend not to see them. She just wiped them with her thumbs, smearing dirt across Wren's cheekbones, exactly as she had when Wren was small.

"I have to tell you something," Wren said. "Before anything else. Before we eat or sleep or any of it."

"Wren—"

"No. Listen. Please." Wren took a breath that hurt. "I hid it from you. My whole life. You didn't know what I was, and I let you not know, because I was afraid, and because—" their voice caught— "because I thought if you knew, you'd look at me the way the village looks at things it's afraid of. And I was wrong to carry that alone, and I was wrong to decide for you what you could bear. You bore everything else. You should have borne that too. I'm sorry."

Her mother was quiet for a long time. The bee droned. Down the lane, the laughing dog barked at nothing.

"Do you remember," Maren Vane said at last, "the winter you were nine? When the ewe was sick. The one with the crooked horn."

Wren went still. "You knew."

"I woke in the night and your bed was empty and I found you in the barn with your forehead pressed to that animal's head, whispering. And the ewe looked at me—looked at me, Wren, like a person looks—and I understood that my child was speaking to it, and it was answering." Her mother's hands tightened on Wren's face. "And I walked back to the house and I lay down next to your father and I stared at the ceiling until morning, and in the morning I got up and made porridge and I never said a word. Because I knew—if I named it, it would become real, and if it became real, they would come for you. So I decided that I had seen a dream. I decided it so hard that I nearly believed it myself."

"You knew," Wren said again, hollow. "All those years. All those years I thought I was protecting you, and you were—"

"We were both hiding in the same house," her mother said simply. "From each other. From everyone. That's what fear does—it makes liars of love." She brushed the dirt from Wren's cheek, tenderly. "I'm not sorry I kept silent. I'd keep silent again, to keep you alive. But I am sorry you ever believed, even for one day, that knowing what you are would make me love you less. There was never a version of you I wouldn't have burned the whole valley down for."

"Mother."

"There wasn't," she said fiercely, and then her fierceness crumbled and she pulled Wren in again, and they stayed like that, kneeling in the rich dark earth of the garden, while somewhere above them the out-of-season nightingale sang its confused, joyful song.

* * *

Evening came down gold and violet over the mere. Word had traveled, the way word does in villages—carried faster now, Wren suspected, by birds who considered gossip a professional obligation. By the time the lamps were lit, half of Hollowmere had found reasons to pass the Vane gate. Tam Brackle came to deliver butter he clearly could not spare and stayed to shake Wren's hand with a grip like a bear trap and eyes suspiciously bright. The miller's daughter, who had once thrown stones at Wren for talking to crows, stood at the fence for a long time and finally said, "My brother's boy is gifted. We think. He talks to the geese. What—what do we do now?" and Wren spent an hour telling her, and when she left she was crying and smiling at once.

That was the thing no one had warned Wren about: that saving the world would turn out to mean this. Doors. Conversations. Butter. The slow, unglamorous work of a valley learning to stop being afraid.

Ashfall sat at the edge of the lamplight through all of it, and the villagers gave him a wide, respectful berth, and the children gave him none at all. By full dark, three of them had worked up the courage to approach the great grey wolf, and Ashfall—who had faced witch-hunters and the breaking of worlds without blinking—looked profoundly put upon as a small girl braided flowers into the fur behind his ear.

"You are enjoying this," Wren accused, finding him there after the last visitor had gone.

"I am enduring it," Ashfall said, with dignity undermined by the flowers. "There is a difference."

Inside, Sera and Wren's mother had gotten on like a house fire, which terrified Wren more than the witch-finder general had. They were at the table over mulled cider, and Sera was in the middle of some story involving a rooftop in Greyhollow and a stolen map, and Maren Vane was laughing—laughing, open-mouthed and unguarded, a sound Wren had heard perhaps ten times in their entire childhood.

"—and then the map turned out to be upside down," Sera was saying.

"Upside down."

"For three days. I navigated by an inverted mountain range. It's a miracle we found the altar at all and not a duck pond."

Maren laughed harder, and Sera grinned, and Wren stood in the doorway of their own home feeling like a stranger at a wonderful party, until Sera caught sight of them and her grin softened into something quieter, something just for Wren, and Wren's chest did the thing it always did now, the warm treacherous lurch that they had stopped fighting weeks ago.

"Your mother's threatening to teach me bread," Sera said.

"Baking bread."

"That's what I said."

* * *

Later—much later, when the lamps were out and Sera slept in the loft and the valley breathed slow around them—Wren slipped out the blue door and found Ashfall waiting at the garden gate, the flower braid still in his fur, the moon above them whole and silver and unbroken.

He was looking east, toward the deep forest, toward the high country where the old pines stood and the wild things had begun, cautiously, joyfully, to return to their ancient roads.

"You're leaving," Wren said. Not a question. They had known since the ford, maybe since the beginning. Wolves who remember covenants are not creatures who settle in villages.

"The wild needs walking," Ashfall said. "Two hundred years of wards left scars on the roads between realms. Someone must pace them out. Someone must remind the deer where the old crossings are, and the rivers where their voices used to run." He glanced at Wren, amber eyes catching the moonlight. "And there are others like me. Old ones. Sleepers. Some will wake angry. Better they wake to a friend."

Wren nodded. Their throat was tight, but they had learned, these last months, that tight throats were survivable. That grief and gratitude could live in the same breath.

"Will I see you?"

Ashfall stepped close, and pressed his great head briefly against Wren's chest, the way he had done once at the altar, when the covenant's power had run through them both like a struck bell.

"Listen," he said. "That is all. That was always all. Listen, and I will answer. On any night. Under any moon. However far the roads run." He pulled back, and his mouth curved in the wolf way that was almost a smile. "You are the bridge now, Wren Vane. Bridges don't get to keep the river on one side."

"And you're sure you won't stay? Even a season? Mother would feed you. She'd complain about how much you eat, but she'd feed you."

"A wolf at a hearth," Ashfall said, "is a story with a sad ending. Besides—" and here his voice dropped into something softer, older, "—you don't need me at your heel anymore. You never needed me to lead. You needed me to witness. Consider yourself witnessed."

He turned, then, and trotted to the edge of the lamplight, and paused there—one last silhouette, grey as storm-cloud, flowers absurd and perfect behind his ear.

"The girl child," he said. "The one with the geese-brother. Bring her to the forest edge at spring thaw. There are teachers in the deep places, and it's time the teaching started again." A beat. "Tell her the wolf says the geese are terrible gossips and she should trust them anyway."

Then he was gone, a shadow folding into shadows, and the night took him the way the night takes all wild things—not as an ending, but as a turning of the page.

Wren stood a long while at the gate, listening. To the mere lapping at its banks. To the nightingale, still singing, still confused, still joyful. To the far-off conversation of foxes on the ridge, casual and easy, neighbors chatting over fences. To the vast quiet hum of a world with its covenant mended, magic running clean through root and stone and blood.

And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat, from somewhere in the trackless east: a single long howl, rising and falling, unmistakable in its meaning.

*I hear you,* it said. *Do you hear me?*

Wren smiled, tipped their head back to the whole unbroken moon, and answered the only way that had ever mattered—not with command, not with power, but with a promise sent out plain and open across the listening dark:

"Yes."

Behind them, the blue door stood open on lamplight and laughter and the smell of tomorrow's bread. Ahead, spring was coming, and a goose-talking child would need walking to the forest's edge, and the roads between the realms were long and needed pacing, and there was work—good work, chosen work—as far as the heart could reach.

Wren Vane turned toward the open door, and went inside, and left it open behind them.
