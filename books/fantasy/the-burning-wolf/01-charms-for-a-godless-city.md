## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The rain came off the harbor in gray sheets, and Felix Hartwell counted it a good omen. Bad weather meant frightened people, and frightened people bought charms.

He kept his shop on Tallow Row, wedged between a chandler and a pawnbroker who owed him money from three years back and had stopped meeting his eyes. The sign above the door read *HARTWELL — CURIOS & KEEPSAKES* in paint that was peeling at the corners, because Felix repainted only what customers noticed. Inside, the shelves were arranged with the care of a man who understood that order was a kind of armor: river-stones polished smooth, knotted cords in seven colors, dried lavender bundled with red thread, small brass bells that rang when the wind found them through the ill-fitted shutters.

None of it worked. That was the joke he never told anyone. None of it had ever worked, not really—not since the night twelve years ago when his god went silent mid-prayer, like a voice cut off by a closing door, and every charm Felix had ever blessed went dull as river gravel.

But the people of Vaelmoor didn't need their charms to work. They needed to hold something while they waited for the world to end, and Felix sold them things to hold.

"Master Hartwell." The woman from the chandlery leaned in out of the rain, shaking water from her shawl. "You got any of the knot-charms left? The blue ones."

"For safe travel?"

"For my boy. He's taking the coastal run tomorrow. Fisherman's son, first time out past the breakwater." She said it the way people said such things now—flatly, with the fear packed down tight underneath, like cargo lashed against a storm. "His father wants him to have something."

Felix took down one of the blue knots from the third shelf, where he kept the charms for protection at sea. He'd learned the knotwork from a brother of his old order whose name he no longer let himself say aloud. The man had tied them while humming. Felix tied them in silence, and if his fingers moved with a precision that came from somewhere deeper than craft, well. Muscle memory was just grief that had learned to be useful.

"One silver," he said. "And tell him to keep it under his shirt, against the skin. Not because it matters where it sits." He met her eyes. "Because it matters that he chose to carry it."

She paid without haggling, which told him how scared she was, and left into the rain. Felix watched her go through the fogged glass, then turned the little brass bell on the counter so its clapper wouldn't ring, an old habit, an old superstition of his own that he'd never managed to kill.

Through the window, Vaelmoor went about its morning the way a man walks past a graveyard: quickly, eyes forward, whistling sometimes to prove he isn't afraid.

The city had outlawed magic eleven years ago, one year after the silence, when the king's edicts came down from the capital like stones dropped into still water. *No unlicensed workings. No hedge-craft. No charms of power.* The witch-hunters of the Pale Ledger had burned the hedge-witches first, then the cunning men, then anyone whose grandmother had ever muttered over a fever. The pyres had run for two seasons. Felix remembered standing in a crowd watching a woman burn on Meridian Hill—a woman whose only crime, as far as he could tell, had been curing a child's croup—and remembering that he could have stopped it with a word, once, and couldn't now, and hadn't even tried to try.

That was the arithmetic he lived inside. Twelve years of silence. Eleven years of law. One man selling dried lavender to people who needed miracles and settling for comfort.

It wasn't heroism, keeping the shop open. He'd want to be clear about that, if anyone ever asked, though no one ever did. It was the opposite of heroism. It was hiding in plain sight, behind a counter, behind a peeling sign, behind the name *Hartwell*, which was itself a hiding place—he'd chosen it from a gravestone in the Low Churchyard, a merchant dead forty years, because a man needed a name that belonged to someone else if he wanted to disappear properly.

Before Hartwell, he'd been something else. Before the shop, he'd carried a sword for the Order of the Quiet Oath, sworn to powers older than the crown, and he'd been good at it in the way that made old men look at you sadly. But the Order was scattered now, thinned by the purges, gone to ground in cellars and attics across the city, keeping vigils over gods who no longer answered. And Felix—Felix had put down the sword, taken up the knotwork, and told himself the lie so many times it had worn smooth as one of his river-stones:

*I'm done. This is enough. Selling comfort is a kind of kindness.*

Some mornings he almost believed it.

* * *

The dockworker came in just past noon, dripping, wringing his cap in both hands like he was trying to strangle it.

Felix knew the type. Big man, sunburnt neck, hands like hams, and eyes that kept flicking to the door. Dockworkers didn't come to Tallow Row for curios. They came because someone on the night shift had whispered about the quiet shop with the quiet owner, the one who didn't ask questions and didn't report what he heard.

"Shut the door behind you," Felix said. "You're flooding my floor."

The man shut it. Stood there a moment, working up to whatever he'd come to work up to. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the street, the town crier was reading the evening proclamations in a bored voice—the grain tariffs, the curfew hours, the standing reward for information regarding *unlicensed workings*, which everyone in Vaelmoor had learned to hear as the background music of their lives.

"You're Hartwell," the man said. Not a question.

"I'm Hartwell."

"They say you sell charms."

Felix set down the cord he was splicing and gave the man his shopkeeper's face, mild and slightly bored. "I sell keepsakes. Knots and stones and dried flowers. If they happen to make a person feel better, that's between them and their own heart. I don't claim anything else." He said it loudly enough to carry, the way he always did, because you never knew who was listening through the wall, and the wall between his shop and the pawnbroker's was thin as a confession. "If you're looking for magic, friend, you've got the wrong shop. Magic's illegal in Vaelmoor. Hasn't your crier told you?"

The dockworker flushed. "I'm not—" He glanced at the door again. Lowered his voice. "Look. I don't want magic. I want luck. There's a difference. Everyone knows there's a difference."

"There isn't," Felix said quietly. "But sit down anyway."

The man sat on the stool by the counter, perched on it like a bird on a wire, too big for the furniture and too nervous to notice. Up close Felix could read the story in him: a night's work undone by fear, sleeplessness ground into the skin under his eyes, and beneath all of it the particular tension of a man who has seen something he wishes he hadn't.

"What's your name?" Felix asked.

"Tomas."

"What did you see, Tomas?"

The big man flinched like he'd been struck. "Who says I saw anything?"

"Nobody. Nobody says you saw anything." Felix rose, unhurried, and took down a jar of the river-stones. "But you came here instead of a tavern, and you're sitting like a man waiting for soldiers, and you asked about luck before you asked about price. So either you saw something, or you're about to do something dangerous, or both. I've sold a lot of luck in this city. I know the faces."

Tomas stared at his own hands. When he spoke, it came out low and fast, words tumbling like they'd been held behind his teeth all day. "Night shift. East quay. We were loading the salt barges, and near dawn the water went wrong." His throat worked. "Not rough. Wrong. Flat. All the way out to the harbor mouth, flat as poured glass, and cold—cold like winter, in the middle of the season. And the gulls went quiet. All of them, all at once, like somebody'd shut a door on them." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and ashamed of being wet. "Old Maren, she's worked the quay forty years, and she dropped her line and started praying. Out loud. To the old names. And then the foreman come running down the pier shouting that we'd all be flogged for listening to her, and the water went normal, and everyone laughed, except nobody was laughing. You know? Everybody laughing and nobody laughing."

Felix had gone very still.

He was good at going still—it had kept him alive through more fights than he could count—but this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man hearing a sound from a locked room in a house he thought empty.

Flat water. Cold out of season. Birds silenced. He knew those signs. Every member of the Quiet Oath knew them, drilled into them in the years when the drills had seemed like ritual rather than necessity. They were the signs of a ward under strain. A great ward, an old one, stretched thin—the border wards, the ones laid down centuries ago by smith-priests whose names had become curses in the current court, woven into the very coastline of Vaelmoor like thread through cloth.

They were also, if his memory served, the signs of a ward beginning to fail.

"When did Maren start praying?" he asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later.

"Dawn. Just at dawn."

"And the water smoothed before or after?"

"After. Right after. Like—" Tomas frowned, groping for it. "Like it was listening to her. Like it flinched."

Felix turned to the shelf and selected a charm without looking at it, his hands moving on their own while his mind ran elsewhere, down corridors he'd bricked shut years ago. He chose a stone, gray-green, wrapped in copper wire, and set it on the counter between them.

"For luck," he said. "Keep it in your pocket. Left side."

"How much?"

"Two coppers."

Tomas blinked. "Maren said you charged fair, but that's—"

"Maren's right. Two coppers." Felix slid it across. "Tomas. The east quay. Are the border stones visible from there? The old waymarkers, out past the breakwater."

Something shifted in the dockworker's face—the look of a man realizing the shopkeeper knows more than the shopkeeper should. "You can see them on a clear day. Why?"

"No reason. Old curiosity." Felix smiled his shopkeeper's smile, and hated how easily it fit. "Mind the left pocket. And Tomas—if the water goes flat again, get off the quay. Don't wait for the foreman. Don't argue. Just go."

* * *

He closed early, which he almost never did, and stood in the back room among crates of unsold stock, doing the thing he did perhaps twice a year: he unwrapped the cloth from his left wrist.

The mark lay there under the lamplight, fine as spider silk, a sigil inked into the skin in colors that weren't quite black and weren't quite silver. His god's last gift—or last accident, depending on the hour of night and how honest he was willing to be. It had appeared the morning after the silence, twelve years ago, drawn on him while he slept by no hand he could name, and it had been dormant ever since. Dead ink. A scar in the shape of meaning. He'd shown it to the Order's loremasters once, in the early years, and watched an old woman go pale and refuse to speak of it, and after that he'd kept it wrapped, the way you keep a wound covered, or a letter you can't bring yourself to burn.

Twelve years of nothing. He'd made his peace with nothing. Nothing was almost comfortable, the way an empty house is comfortable once you stop expecting anyone to come home.

He looked at it now, and told himself he was checking it the way you check an old injury before weather changes. Routine. Nothing more.

The mark twinged.

It was small—a prickle, a warmth, the ghost of a pin dragged along the skin—and it was gone almost before he registered it. But Felix Hartwell had spent twelve years learning to notice nothing, and so he noticed everything, and everything in him went cold and quiet, the way the harbor had gone cold and quiet, the way the gulls had gone quiet, all at once, like somebody shutting a door.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Down on the east quay, he knew, men were laughing and not laughing. And far away, past the breakwater, past the horizon, along the whole ragged edge of the kingdom, the wards that held back whatever the wards held back were straining like thread through cloth—

—and somewhere in the dark below the world, something had begun, patiently, hungrily, to pull.
