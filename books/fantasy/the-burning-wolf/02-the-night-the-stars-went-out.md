## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Felix was counting coins behind the counter of his shuttered shop when the wards fell.

He didn't hear it first. He felt it — a wrongness in his teeth, a pressure behind his eyes like a held breath that had been held for years and finally, finally broke. The copper bit in his palm went cold. Then cold wasn't the word for it anymore. The coin went *absent*, the way a tooth goes absent from your tongue after the pull: you keep reaching for it with your mind and finding nothing.

Then came the sound.

It rolled across Vaelmoor like tearing silk stretched over the whole width of the sky — a long, high shriek that no throat could make, rising and rising until it stopped being sound and became absence, a hole in the world's hearing. Every dog in the city began to scream at once.

Felix dropped the coins.

He was through the door before he'd decided to move, twelve years of habit folding him into the alley shadows out of pure reflex, and then he made himself stop, made himself stand in the open street like an ordinary man, because ordinary men were pouring out of their houses right now, ordinary men were looking up —

Looking up.

The sky had no stars.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone.* The whole black bowl of night above Vaelmoor sat smooth and empty as the inside of a closed eye. No constellations. No wandering lights. No pale smear of the Sisters where the old maps said the gods kept their campfires. Just dark, total and seamless, as if someone had reached up and pulled a cloth over everything humanity had ever used to find its way home.

Felix's knees nearly went. He caught himself on a doorpost and stood there with his head tipped back like every other fool in the street, and he thought, with a strange detached clarity: *I have seen this before.*

He hadn't. He knew he hadn't. But twelve years ago his god had gone silent, and the silence had felt exactly like this — not an ending but an erasure, something taken rather than lost, a page cut cleanly from a book so skillfully you only knew it by the seam.

The screaming started in earnest then. A woman two doors down fell to her knees and began to pray to the Sisters, loud and ragged, and a man tried to drag her inside because praying was illegal now, had been illegal for six years, and the Watch didn't care that the sky had just been murdered. Somewhere a bell began to ring — the harbor bell, the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one — and boots hammered along the quay as the garrison turned out with torches against an enemy that lived above the clouds.

Felix did none of it. He stood very still and watched his own left wrist.

The mark had been gray for twelve years. A faded knot of lines just below the heel of his palm, like a brand that had healed badly, like a scar from a rope burn. He'd told himself a hundred stories about it — a childhood scald, a soldier's tattoo gone wrong, nothing, nothing at all. He'd worn a leather cuff over it for six years, since the edicts, since wearing the sigil of a dead order became worth a cell in the Undercroft or worse. He never looked at it. Looking at it was like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt.

It hurt.

The gray lines had gone white. Not glowing, exactly — *lit*, the way bone is lit when lightning stands behind it, light coming from inside and out through the skin. And it was pulling. There was no gentler word for it. Something in the mark had woken after twelve years of death and set its teeth into him, and it wanted him to walk, and it did not care what Felix Hartwell wanted.

"No," Felix said aloud, to his own wrist, like a madman.

The mark pulled harder.

* * *

He fought it for four streets. That was longer than most people would have managed, and shorter than he'd have liked to believe of himself.

The crowd was a river running the wrong way — everyone fleeing inland from the wall district, away from the border, toward the harbor, toward anywhere that felt like center and safety. Felix shoved against that current, shoulder-down, elbows working, and the mark burned hotter with every step he took in the wrong direction until his cuff smoked faintly and he smelled his own skin scorching and gave up, turned, and let the current take him.

It carried him east, toward the Saltgate wall, and the closer he got the more the crowd thinned and changed. These weren't families with bundles on their backs. These were soldiers, wardens, the robed emergency-wardens of the Vigilance who policed magic in Vaelmoor with ledgers and irons. Torches everywhere. And over it all, that torn-silk shriek still hanging in the air, thinner now, fraying, like the last thread of something enormous unraveling somewhere beyond the hills.

"Clear the street! Clear it, in the king's name—" A sergeant with a crossbow, waving men back from the gate stairs. "Nobody goes to the wall! Nobody—"

Felix went to the wall.

He told himself it was the mark, and mostly it was. But there was a splinter of honesty in him that he'd spent twelve years keeping buried, and it surfaced now, gasping: *you want to see.* Twelve years of small commerce and small rooms and small lies, twelve years of being nobody named nobody, and some starved animal part of him had heard the sky tear open and thought, *finally.* He hated that animal. He fed it anyway, walking faster.

The Saltgate district opened around him like a wound.

The wall itself — the Wards of Vaelmoor, seven miles of standing stones older than the kingdom, each one taller than three men and carved with script that predated writing — lay broken. Not toppled. *Broken*, the way a bone breaks, jagged ends jutting, and between the stones hung shreds of light. That was the wards themselves, Felix realized. What people called the wards wasn't the stone; it was the weaving strung between them, a loom-work of force laid across the whole border, and it was coming apart in long ribbons that drifted upward and dissolved. Each ribbon that died made a small sound, a sighing snap, and he understood with a lurch that the great tearing cry had been a thousand of these at once, a chord, all the strings cut by one hand.

Beyond the wall, where the hills should have been visible under starlight, there was nothing. Not darkness — darkness would have been a relief. It was a *lack*, a region of the world where light had been declared invalid, and Felix's eyes slid off it again and again, unable to hold the shape of it, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth.

Something moved in that lack. He couldn't say how he knew; there was nothing to see. But the nothing shifted, slow and vast, the way a whale moves under ice, and every hair on Felix's body rose, and the mark on his wrist blazed so bright that the nearest soldiers spun toward him with their crossbows half-raised.

"You there! What's that light? What are you—"

Felix ran.

* * *

He didn't decide anything for the next hour. The mark decided, and Felix's body obeyed, and his mind rode along in the back like a passenger in a runaway cart, cataloguing horrors.

The breach in the wall was worse up close. Ward-stones lay shattered in fields that had been wheat yesterday and were now gray ash, not burned ash — *drained* ash, colorless, as if the green had been drunk out of them. Cattle stood dead in the furrows, unmarked, upright, simply stopped. And scattered among it all were people. Some moving. Some not.

The mark dragged him past all of it. Past a warden crawling on her elbows with her legs folded wrong. Past a boy, no older than sixteen, pinned under half a ton of fallen stone and screaming a name over and over — "Da! DA!" — while three grown men strained at the rock and could not shift it. Felix's hands actually moved toward the stone, old mercenary reflexes reaching for leverage and prayer in equal measure, and the mark flared white-hot and *yanked*, and his feet went sideways, away, and he heard himself make a sound low in his throat that he hadn't made since the war.

"I'm going," he snarled at it, at the thing wearing his wrist. "I'm going. Stop *pulling*."

It didn't stop. It didn't care about the boy under the stone. Whatever intelligence lived in those lit lines — and Felix was done pretending there wasn't one; whatever had slept for twelve years was awake now and *urgent* — it had a destination and a purpose, and neither of them involved mercy.

That was the moment the grief arrived. Not the terror — terror had come and gone already, quick as a wave. This was older and heavier. Because Felix knew this feeling. He'd felt it once before, twelve years ago, kneeling in a shrine with his god's name in his mouth, feeling that presence recede down some infinite distance without answering, without even saying goodbye, and he had learned then the specific loneliness of being *used* by powers that do not explain themselves. He had sworn he would never be a tool again. He had built a whole small gray life on that vow, charm-shop and shuttered windows and a false name worn smooth as river stone.

And here was his wrist, dragging him like a mule on a rope, and here was the sky, empty as a robbed grave, and here was Felix Hartwell — no. Not Felix Hartwell. That name was the disguise. Here was whoever he'd been before, being hauled back into the story he'd fled, and the worst of it, the truly unforgivable part, was that some starving corner of him was *glad*.

He hated himself for it. He kept running.

* * *

The mark brought him to a collapsed watch-post at the base of the third fallen stone, and there it finally slowed, circling him in place like a dog that has found what it was sent to find.

A woman lay in the rubble.

She was maybe forty, gray-shot black hair cropped short, dressed in the layered leathers of a road-smith with tools scattered around her like shed feathers — fine tools, Felix noted even through the horror, ward-smith's tools, the kind that had been confiscated on sight for six years. Her left arm ended at the elbow in a cauterized stump, self-cauterized by the look of the burn, which meant she'd done it herself, awake, which meant she was either the toughest person in Vaelmoor or already dead and too stubborn to mention it.

Her eyes found him. They were sharp and furious and alive.

"You," she rasped. "Your wrist."

Felix crouched, instinct overriding sense, checking her for wounds he couldn't fix. "Hold still. I'll find help—"

"There's no help." She grabbed his sleeve with her remaining hand, grip like a winch. "There's no help coming and no time and you're marked, I can *see* it from here, so listen." Blood at the corner of her mouth. She spat it aside and kept talking, fast, a smith dictating to a slow apprentice. "The weave didn't fail. Understand me. Seven miles of anchor-stone don't fail at once, not in a hundred years of storms. It was *cut*. From outside. By something that knew the pattern." Her hand tightened. "And that thing on your wrist woke up tonight, same hour, and it's pulling you somewhere, yes?"

Felix said nothing. His silence answered.

"Then wherever it's pulling you," she said, "that's where the answer is. And I'm coming with you, because I'm the last loom-smith on this border who can read the cut, and if I die in this field the kingdom dies stupid." She tried to rise, failed, swore with a fluency that would have embarrassed a dockworker. "Help me up. That's the help I need. Go on."

Above them, in the absolute starless dark, the vast unseen shape shifted again — closer now, or larger, or both — and every drained stalk of ash in the field trembled though there was no wind.

Felix looked at the ruined wall, at the woman bleeding in the wreckage of the world, at his own blazing wrist. Twelve years of shutters and false names, and it had all lasted exactly as long as it took the sky to fall.

He put his shoulder under her good arm and lifted.

"Name," she grunted, as they staggered toward the dark together.

"Felix," he said. Just Felix. Even now, even with the end of everything, some habits held.

"Sera," she said. "Sera Ives. And whatever that mark of yours is leading us to, boy — pray it's not what I think it is."

The mark flared once, bright as a struck match, and pointed them both into the starless dark.
