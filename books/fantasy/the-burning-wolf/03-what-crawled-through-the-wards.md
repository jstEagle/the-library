## 3. What Crawled Through the Wards

The wall had been screaming for an hour before Felix reached it.

Not the stone—the wards. He could hear them dying the way you hear a man drowning: a rising note that kept climbing past what a sound should be, thinning, stretching, and then snapping into silence so complete his ears rang with the absence. Around him the crowd on the Processional had stopped running in every direction at once and started running in one direction only—away from the river district, away from the old border wall that hadn't mattered to anyone in three hundred years.

Felix was running toward it.

He hated himself for it even as he did it. Twelve years of discipline said *blend in, survive, keep your head down*, and twelve years of discipline lost to a burning itch on his wrist like a fishhook set behind the skin. The mark dragged him through the fleeing crowd the way a current drags a swimmer, and he let it, because the alternative was standing still while half of Vaelmoor screamed, and there were limits to what kind of coward he was willing to be.

"Get off the street," he told a woman clutching two children, one under each arm like sacks of grain. "Cellars. Anywhere underground."

She stared at him—at the charm-seller's apron still tied over his coat, at the face she'd bought a luck-knot from not three days ago—and then she ran, and that was the whole of their conversation. That was Vaelmoor now. No time for anything but the next step away from death.

The sky overhead was wrong in a way his mind kept trying to refuse. Not cloudy. Not dark. *Empty.* He'd lived under stars his whole life, even in cities, even through smoke and storm—they were always *there*, a pressure behind the clouds, a fact of the world. Now when he tilted his head back there was nothing above him at all. No depth. It was like standing under a lid.

His god had gone silent twelve years ago, and Felix had learned to live inside that silence the way a man learns to live without a limb—badly, then adequately, then so well he almost forgot. But this was different. This wasn't one voice going quiet. This was the whole choir.

*Something ate the sky,* he thought, and the thought had no bottom to it.

* * *

The border wall of Vaelmoor was older than the kingdom that bore its name—a ring of standing stones and mortared watchtowers raised by people whose language no one spoke anymore, laced through with ward-work so old that the guilds called it the First Loom. Every child learned the rhyme: *the Loom holds, the dark stays out*. Nobody believed in it. It was like believing in the floor.

Felix came up the last hill with his lungs full of ash-flavored air and saw the floor had broken.

A hundred paces of the wall simply weren't there anymore. Not collapsed—*unmade*. The stones hadn't fallen; they'd come apart into gravel and powder, spread across the fields like something had ground them between enormous teeth. Through the gap, where the land beyond should have rolled away into the Marches under starlight, there was a darkness with texture to it. A darkness that moved.

He stopped at the crest of the hill because his body refused to go further, and for a long moment he just looked.

It was vast. That was the only word his mind would hold onto, and even *vast* felt like calling the sea *wet*. Something out there in the stolen dark was turning over, slow as a whale under ice, and each movement of it erased another handful of whatever it passed through. He couldn't see it. That was the worst part—he could see everything *around* it, the hills and the ruined wall and the burning watchtower, and where the thing was, there was simply nothing to see. A hole shaped like hunger.

The mark on his wrist flared white-hot, and Felix made a sound he was not proud of.

Then it pulled.

Not gently. It hauled at him like a hooked line, dragging him down the hillside, stumbling through the gravel field where the wall used to be, and he dug his heels in and swore at it—swore at it the way you swear at a dead man, because the mark had been his patron's last working, pressed into his skin the night the old mercenary captain died with Felix's hand around his. *Keep this. It will matter later.* Twelve years of a dormant scar, and now it had opinions, and its opinion was *go here*, and Felix went, because the alternative was tearing his own arm off.

"Where," he snarled. "Where are you taking me?"

The answer, when it came, was a hand's-breadth of color in all that gray: a woman, facedown among the shattered stones, her hair fanned out black against the pale dust, one arm bent wrong beneath her.

* * *

She was breathing. Shallow, wet, but breathing, and Felix knelt in the ruin of the First Loom and put two fingers against her throat and felt a pulse that stuttered like a bad lamp. Her left side was a map of burns—not fire-burns, but the specific silvered scarring of ward-feedback, the kind that happened when a smith stood too close to her own work when it died. He'd seen it once before, on a hedge-witch the hunters caught in Corvane. She hadn't survived the week.

"Wake up." He turned her carefully, keeping the broken arm braced. "Hey. Wake up."

Her eyes opened. They were gray, sharp even half-shut with pain, and they found his face and catalogued it in about a second and a half—stranger, armed, not a hunter, maybe safe, probably not. Ward-smiths, he'd heard, were trained to assess rooms the way soldiers assessed battlefields. Even dying, apparently.

"The anchor-stone," she rasped. "Third tower. If the anchor-stone—"

"It's gone." He nodded at the gravel field around them. "All of it's gone."

Her face did something complicated. Grief and fury fighting for the same ground, and losing both to exhaustion. "Then get me up," she said, "because I need to see how bad—"

"You're not getting up. Your arm's broken and you're burned from hip to shoulder."

"I know what I am." She tried anyway. He let her get halfway to sitting before her body betrayed her and she sagged against his shoulder, breathing in short furious pulls. "Ward-smith Sera Ives," she managed, as if the name were a formality owed. "You?"

"Felix." No surname. Old habit. "Charm-seller."

"Charm-sellers don't run toward a breach in the First Loom." Her eyes flicked down to his wrist, where the mark had faded from white-hot to a dull ember glow. Something moved behind her expression—recognition, or the start of it—but pain swallowed it before he could read it. "What is that?"

"A scar."

"That's not a scar."

"It's mine, and it's not your problem." He got his arm under hers. "Can you walk if I take most of your weight? There's a cellar district two miles east. We can't stay in the open."

She laughed, which cost her; he heard the breath catch on something wet. "Two miles. You want to walk two miles while that—" she jerked her chin at the moving darkness beyond the breach, and Felix noticed her hand shook, "—that *thing* finishes deciding whether we're worth eating."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because my wrist won't let me do anything else, he didn't say. Because some dead man's last spell has decided I'm useful, and I don't get a vote. He said: "Because staying here is worse."

* * *

They made it four hundred paces before the screaming started.

It came from the west, from the stretch of wall that still stood—a garrison tower, one of the old watch-posts, and now Felix could see figures on it. Soldiers. Six, maybe eight, silhouetted against a signal-fire they'd lit for a sky that had nothing left to signal to. And below them, spilling out of the breach-darkness like water finding a crack, came shapes.

Not the vast thing itself. These were smaller. *Made*, maybe, the way a body makes sweat—or shed, the way a snake sheds skin. They moved wrong, low and liquid, and where they touched the grass the grass went gray and stayed gray, and Felix understood with a calm, distant horror that the land they crossed wasn't being burned. It was being *eaten*. Taken out of the world and into whatever passed for a stomach out there in the dark.

The soldiers on the tower saw them too. He heard shouting, the snap of a crossbow, bolts vanishing into the shapes with no effect anyone could see from this distance. One of the shapes reached the tower's base and began, unhurried, to climb.

"We have to help them." Sera had gone rigid against his side. "There's a relief road—cavalry from Hallowmere barracks, twenty minutes if—"

"There's no cavalry coming. Look at the road." The Hallowmere road was empty in both directions, and would stay empty, because every soldier in Vaelmoor was looking at a starless sky and asking the same question Felix was asking: *what's the point of a kingdom if the night eats it?*

"Then we go ourselves. You have a sword—I can see the sword, charm-seller, nobody sells charms with a cavalry saber under their apron—"

"And do what?" His voice came out harder than he intended. "Eight men on a tower. Two of us, one of them half-dead. You want to die with them, fine, but you'll die *uselessly*, and I've buried enough people who died usefully to know the difference."

"They're *soldiers*. They're holding the line."

"The line is *gone*, Sera." He pointed at the unmade wall, at the gray grass, at the lidless dark above. "There is no line. There's just where the thing hasn't gotten to yet."

She stared at him, and he watched her hate him for it, and watched her know he was right, and hated himself along with her. Because that was the truth underneath everything: the mark hadn't dragged him toward the tower. It had dragged him toward *her*. Toward one half-dead ward-smith lying in the rubble, as if she were the only thing in this whole catastrophe that mattered—as if some dead man's magic had already decided who was worth saving tonight, and everyone else could burn.

And the tower was burning now. He could see the fire catching, the soldiers' silhouettes clustering at the top, and one of them—a boy, he looked like a boy, sixteen at most—turned and looked out across the fields, and Felix would swear until his dying day that the boy looked directly at him. Saw him. Saw the two of them, retreating east with their backs to the fight.

The boy raised one hand. Not waving. Just—raised it. Like a question.

Then the shape reached the top of the tower, and Felix turned away, and he carried Sera Ives east through the gray grass while the screaming started and then stopped, and the mark on his wrist cooled to nothing, satisfied, its errand done.

* * *

They walked most of the night. She didn't talk much—pain took most of her breath, and pride took the rest—but somewhere in the third mile she said, quietly, "You knew. Before you turned away. You knew you couldn't save them and you turned anyway, fast, so you wouldn't have to watch."

"Yes."

"My master taught me that trick. Turning first." Her voice was flat. "He said a smith who watches her work fail learns to stop building. He was wrong, by the way. I watched, and I'm still going to build."

"Build what? The Loom's dust."

"The Loom was *stone*, charm-seller. Stone is just where the weaving lives." She shifted against his shoulder, wincing. "I've rebuilt a breached ward once before. Small one. A chapel boundary, corrupted. It took me a year and it nearly killed me and everyone said it couldn't be done." A pause. "This one's bigger."

Felix laughed despite everything—a short, ugly sound. "That thing out there isn't a corrupted chapel."

"No," she agreed. "But the principle holds. Everything woven can be rewoven. Everything bound can be rebound." Her head turned, and he felt her eyes on his wrist again, on the dull ember-glow that pulsed faintly in time with his heartbeat. "And everything *bound wrongly* can be cut loose. That's what your scar is, isn't it? I've only seen that geometry once. In the restricted folios, the ones they burned the smiths for reading."

"What geometry?"

"An unbinding sigil." Her voice dropped, and the night seemed to lean in around the word. "The kind you use to sever a person from a power that's wearing them."

Felix kept walking. Kept his face still. But cold moved up his spine, because he remembered the old captain's last words—*it will matter later*—and he remembered the king of Vaelmoor riding past the Processional last spring, and how the crowds had cheered, and how Felix had thought, for one unaccountable moment, *that man's eyes are empty as a dead god's mouth.*

Ahead of them, the road to the capital stretched away under a sky with nothing in it.

Behind them, where the tower had been, the dark was spreading.

And on his wrist, patient as a debt, the mark waited to be asked the right question.
