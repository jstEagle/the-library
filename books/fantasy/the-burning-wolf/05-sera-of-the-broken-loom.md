## 5. Sera of the Broken Loom

Sera Ives woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone else's cooking, and for three full seconds she did not know where she was.

Then she remembered: the wall, the tearing, the dark coming through like water through a cracked dam. The stones singing as they died. Her hands—

She sat up too fast and the room tilted. A low-ceilinged cellar, candlelit, walls of rough granite sweating with damp. A cot under her that was not hers. Bandages on both forearms, wound tight and clean, and beneath them a pain like her bones had been dipped in acid.

Someone had bandaged her. Someone who knew what they were doing.

"You're leaking again," said a voice from the corner, and there he was—the man from the breach. The one she'd seen standing in the crowd when everything came apart, the only person in that screaming tide moving *toward* the wall instead of away from it. He sat on an upturned crate with a bowl of something steaming in his lap, eating like a man who expected to be interrupted. Dark hair going gray at the temples. A face built for forgetting. "Your left hand. The dressing's soaked through."

"I can see that." Her voice came out as gravel. She looked down at her fingers—swollen, blistered along every line a ward-smith's fingers have lines—and flexed them anyway, because the first lesson of the craft is that you test what hurts before it decides to stop working forever. Two fingers answered. The third answered slowly. The fourth sent a white bolt up her arm and she hissed through her teeth.

"Fourth finger's bad," the man said. Not asking. Observing, the way you'd observe weather.

"Third and fourth. The ring finger matters most." She said it flatly, because if she didn't say it flatly she would say it in some other way entirely. "A ward-smith without her ring finger is a carpenter without a thumb. So either it heals or my career ended four days ago in a ditch."

"Five days," he said. "You've been out five days."

Five days. The number landed in her stomach like a dropped stone. Five days since the stars went out. Five days since the wards of Vaelmoor—the work of nine generations of smiths, the Loom, her *family's* loom, her grandmother's grandmother's grand design—had come apart in her hands. Five days during which anything could have crawled through that breach and gone anywhere.

She'd been asleep while the world ended. Again.

"Where are we?"

"Under a tannery outside Millbrook. Old smuggler's cellar. Nobody comes here." He set the bowl aside. "The town's half-empty. People are walking south, toward the capital, because they think the king will protect them." Something crossed his face when he said *king*, quick as a fish under ice. "They're wrong, but nobody's asked me."

"And you are?"

"Felix." A pause, exactly long enough to notice. "Felix Hartwell."

*Hartwell.* She turned the name over and found nothing attached to it, which was itself information. In five days this man had hauled her out of the rubble of the border wall, carried her—she estimated from the state of her boots, caked in river clay—most of a day downstream, dressed her burns with a salve that smelled of comfrey and something older, and never once told her his name until she asked. Men who withheld their names were usually withholding other things.

"You're not a soldier," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd watched him move while she pretended to sleep yesterday—watched him check the cellar door, check the light, check the angle of the single window, all with the bored economy of a man doing sums he'd done ten thousand times. "Soldiers fidget. You don't fidget."

"I sell charms," Felix Hartwell said.

"In Vaelmoor. Where magic has been outlawed for six years."

"Luck-charms. Dried herbs. Nothing anyone could prosecute."

"Uh-huh." She swung her legs off the cot, and the floor swayed, and she gripped the frame and waited it out with her eyes shut. When she opened them he was watching her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone deciding how much of themselves to spend today.

"Your wrist," she said.

His hand moved before he could stop it—a small, fast motion, covering the left wrist with the right palm. Then he caught himself and made it into adjusting his sleeve, which fooled neither of them.

"What about it?"

"It woke up," Sera said. "At the breach. Don't bother denying it—I was twenty feet from you, facedown in the rubble, and even half-dead I felt it. Like a struck bell inside my skull. Every ward-smith within fifty miles felt it, and most of them are dead now, so congratulations, you may be the loudest thing left in the province." She pushed herself upright, stood, swayed less than she expected. "I've spent five days unconscious and I intend to spend about thirty seconds more being polite. Show me your wrist."

"No."

"Show me your wrist or I walk out of this cellar on legs that barely work, and in two days I collapse in a ditch, and whatever ate the stars eats me, and then you'll have saved me for nothing. Is that the outcome you're managing toward? Because you strike me as a man who hates wasted effort."

He looked at her for a long moment. Candlelight moved in his eyes, and behind the blankness she saw the thing she'd actually been looking for since she woke: not suspicion. *Recognition.* He wanted to show her. He was terrified of wanting to.

Then he pulled back the sleeve.

The mark sat on the inside of his left wrist, just above the pulse point: a sigil the size of a coin, ink-dark, but not ink. Ink didn't move. This moved—not visibly, not when she stared straight at it, but in the corner of her eye, the way deep water moves. Lines within lines, a geometry that kept almost resolving into sense and then sliding away, like a word on the tip of the tongue. And around its edges, faint as breath on glass, a corona of gold that pulsed in time with his heartbeat.

Sera forgot, briefly, to breathe.

"That's not a charm," she said quietly. "That's not a brand, it's not a guild-mark, it's not any of the forty-one things a man might legally carry on his skin in this kingdom." She reached out—stopped herself, hand hovering, because some marks bit strangers. "May I?"

He hesitated. Then he extended his arm like a man offering his arm to a headsman.

Her fingertips were ruined, blistered things, but touch was touch, and when she laid two fingers against the mark the world *rang*. Not sound—structure. She felt the sigil the way she felt stone, the way she felt the Loom: as load-bearing architecture. Except this wasn't holding anything up. It was tuned to hold something *apart*. A boundary drawn around a person, keeping two things that should never touch locked on opposite sides of a line thinner than thought.

"Oh," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Oh, you poor bastard."

"Charming."

"This is an unbinding sigil." She pulled her hand back and pressed it against her own sternum, as if she could press the knowledge deeper. "Not the hedge-witch kind, not the kind in the banned pamphlets that 'unbind' a bad marriage or a debt. The real kind. Pre-Imperial. The kind they cut into the foundation-stones of the old sanctuaries." She was talking fast now and couldn't stop; five days of silence were coming out of her like blood from a reopened cut. "It's a severance matrix. It doesn't create power—it *ends* attachment. It cuts a possessing power loose from the thing it possesses. God from vessel. Demon from host. Crown from—"

She stopped.

Felix had gone very still. Not the stillness of confusion. The stillness of a man hearing his own secret spoken aloud in a stranger's voice and finding out what it sounded like.

"Crown," he repeated.

"The king rode past Millbrook yesterday," Sera said. "I know because the tanner's wife told me, weeping with joy, that His Majesty had come to comfort the border towns himself. That he stood on the walls and promised the stars would return." She heard her own voice go cold and precise, the voice she used for damage reports. "And I lay here unable to move, feeling it every time he spoke. Feeling the *pull*. Like a drain opening in the world. Whatever wears that crown isn't wearing it, Hartwell. It's *feeding* through it. And it has been for years—we just couldn't see the teeth because the sky was still full."

"How do you know what it feels like?" he asked. Softly. Too softly.

"Because I helped build the cage it's sitting in." The words came out of her before pride could catch them, and once they were out there was no taking them back, so she made herself stand straight while they landed. "Six years ago the crown commissioned a renewal of the royal wards. The greatest project of the age. Forty ward-smiths, the best in Vaelmoor, working in the palace cellars for eleven months. I was the youngest ever elevated to lead-weaver. My grandmother held the post before me. We wove the Loom that anchors every ward in the kingdom to the palace heart-stone." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "We thought we were building the strongest protection in history. We were building a *funnel*. Every thread we spun ran straight into the crown. We fed it. We built its mouth and called it a blessing, and when I finally saw what the threads were bending toward—when I brought my findings to the Master of Works—he had me stripped of rank and named a fraud in front of the entire guild. Disgraced. Exiled. My name struck from the rolls." She looked at her burned hands. "So yes. I know what it feels like. I've felt it for six years, from a hundred miles away, every time I closed my eyes. Everyone told me I was mad. I almost believed them."

The silence after that was long. Felix hadn't moved except to let his sleeve fall back over the mark, and when he finally spoke, his voice had changed—some of the careful blankness burned off it.

"They killed my order last night," he said. "The people I—" A pause. Recalibration. "There were others like me. Others who carried things. We kept a safehouse near the river. Last night I came back and the door was open and everyone inside was dead, and the one still breathing told me the crown ate our god first. Before the wards fell. Years ago. Everything since has been—it eating its way outward, and us mistaking the silence for peace." His jaw worked. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I'm the first person in twelve years who looked at your mark and knew what it was instead of flinching," Sera said. "People tell the truth to their tools. It's nothing personal."

Something that was almost a smile touched his mouth and died young.

"So," she said, and made herself say the rest of it plainly, because plainness was all she had left. "Here is what I know. Your sigil is a severance matrix—the only kind in existence, as far as I know, unless the palace archives still have the sanctuary stones, which they won't, because I catalogued those archives myself and the relevant folios were missing even then. Which means the mark on your wrist may be the only instrument in Vaelmoor capable of cutting a possessing power free of its host. And the largest possessed host in the kingdom is currently sitting on a throne in Callowmere, digesting the sky."

"You're saying I'm a weapon."

"I'm saying you're a *key*, and you've been using yourself as a lucky charm for twelve years because it was safer." She stepped closer, and her burned hand trembled and she let it. "I can't build wards anymore—maybe forever, maybe not, ask me again when the blisters heal. But I can read a matrix better than anyone alive, including whoever carved yours, because I guarantee they're dead. If you're going to do what that mark exists for, you will not do it blind. You'll do it with me."

"Why?" Felix said. "Why would you tie yourself to this? You've lost your guild, your name, your hands. Walk south. Find a town the dark hasn't reached. Start over."

"Start over." She tasted the words and spat them out. "My grandmother wove the first anchor-thread of the Loom when she was nineteen. My mother died maintaining it. I gave it eleven months of my life and six years of my reputation, and the thing I built is being used to unmake everything it was built to protect. There is no starting over from that, Hartwell. There's only finishing it properly." She held out her ruined hand. "One condition."

"One."

"Not the capital. Not yet. First we rebuild one ward—one, small, mine—a working example of what the Loom *should* have been, anchored to nothing but itself and the people it shelters. Because if I'm going to help you sever a god-eater from a king, I need to prove to myself that I can still weave something that holds. And because—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—because somewhere out there tonight there are children looking up at a black sky, and I put it there, and I will not take another step toward that throne until I've given one town back its ceiling. One ward. Then I guide you to Callowmere, archives, gates, cellars, all of it. I know ways into the palace that aren't on any map. Deal?"

Felix looked at her hand. At her face. At whatever he saw there that made him stop calculating.

"One ward," he said, and took her hand, gently, the way you handle something already broken. "Where?"

"The chapel on Harrow Hill," Sera said. "Two miles east. It had a threshold-ward woven by my grandmother—old work, pre-Imperial, the same tradition your sigil comes from. If the framework survived the fall, I can re-tune it. If it didn't..." She shrugged, and winced. "Then we'll know quickly."

* * *

They walked at dusk, because Sera insisted—dusk was when wards showed their seams, the hour when the weave revealed what daylight smoothed over. Felix carried a lantern with the shutter narrowed to a blade of light. Wren—no, that came later; on this night it was only the two of them, two disgraced things limping up a hill through ash-fall so fine it hung in the air like gray snow that refused to land.

Harrow Hill's chapel was older than the kingdom, squat and stone-toothed, its bell long since stolen, its roof half-collapsed. And above its door, as they climbed the last rise, Sera saw it and her heart turned over.

The threshold-ward was dead. Of course it was—everything was dead—but it hadn't shattered. It had *burned*, the way a wick burns, deliberately, from the inside out, spending itself. She could read the death the way other people read letters: the flare pattern, the sequence of failure, a master-smith's final act, feeding her own lattice into the ward to buy it one more night, one more hour, one more—

"She stayed," Sera whispered. She was at the doorway without remembering crossing the yard, her fingers hovering over the scorched stone. "Whoever kept this chapel. She felt the Loom failing and she poured herself into the ward to slow it, and it bought—" she did the arithmetic of the burn-patterns, and her throat closed, "—it bought this valley three extra hours of starlight. Three hours. That's what a life weighs, at the end. Three hours of sky for whoever happened to look up."

Behind her, Felix said nothing. But when she glanced back, he had taken off his hood, standing bareheaded in the ash-fall like a man at a graveside, and she understood that he was counting his own hours too—all the ones other people had spent to keep him breathing.

"Well," she said briskly, because grief was a luxury and she'd already spent this year's allowance. "Framework's intact underneath the scorching. Grandmother overbuilt everything; it's a family flaw." She knelt at the threshold, laid her blistered palms against the stone, and closed her eyes, and reached.

For a moment—nothing. Dead stone, dead air, the great silence where the Loom's hum should have been.

Then, deep in the lattice, something answered. Faint. Threadbare. But *there*—a single strand of her grandmother's weaving that the burning hadn't reached, coiled in the foundation like an ember banked under ash, waiting.

Sera opened her eyes and found she was crying, and decided, for once, not to care.

"It's alive," she said. "One thread. Enough to graft from. Give me your knife, Hartwell, and your hands—you're going to learn what a ward feels like from the inside. And then, in a few weeks, you're going to stand in a palace cellar and cut a king loose from the thing that's eating him, and when you do, you'll want to remember what saving something looks like. Because it looks like this. Small. Slow. On your knees in the dirt."

Felix drew his knife and handed it over hilt-first.

And that was when the ash-fall stopped.

Not thinned—*stopped*, mid-air, every gray mote hanging frozen around them as though the world had paused to listen. The lantern-flame stood up straight and rigid. Somewhere far to the north, beyond the dead hills, something vast turned its attention across the miles, the way a man in a dark room turns toward a struck match.

Sera felt it find them. Felt it consider.

Then the ash resumed falling, the flame bent and guttered, and the night went on pretending to be ordinary.

"It noticed the thread," she breathed. "Felix. It noticed."

He was already scanning the darkness, his hand finding the hidden place at his belt where men like him keep the things they hope not to use. "Then we'd better teach it," he said, "that it was looking at the wrong fire."

Far off across the valley, where the road bent down toward the refugee columns strung out along the river, a thin sound rose into the starless dark—the high, carrying cry of a child, screaming where no child should be alone.
