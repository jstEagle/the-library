## 6. The Child Who Remembers Stars

Sera Ives had rebuilt wards in her sleep. She had rebuilt them bleeding, drunk, and once—she still didn't talk about it—with a broken arm held together by a splint and spite. But she had never built one out of a refugee column's worth of borrowed hope, and the work was teaching her things about herself she'd rather not know.

The column stretched along the old salt road like a wound that wouldn't close. Three thousand people, maybe more, moving at the speed of the slowest child, away from the coast where the dark had come ashore. Carts with one wheel. Grandmothers carried on doors torn off hinges. A man walking backward the whole way because he refused to stop watching the empty sky behind him.

Felix walked the line of it like a man inspecting a wall for cracks, and Sera walked beside him, and neither of them said much, because there wasn't much to say that wasn't either obvious or unbearable.

"Third night," Felix said finally. "The hunters are keeping pace."

"I know."

"You've seen them?"

"I've seen what they leave." She nodded ahead, where the column bent around a burned waystation. Two bodies lay by the roadside under cloaks someone had laid over them, and on the cloak of each was chalked a small, neat symbol: a ledger-line, ruled clean. The Pale Ledger marked its accounts. *Found wanting.* "They're not chasing us yet. They're auditing everyone else first."

"That's worse."

"It's efficient," Sera said. "That's what frightens me. Witch-hunters used to be fanatics. Fanatics make mistakes. These ones are doing arithmetic."

Felix's hand drifted toward his wrist—the mark beneath the wrappings—and stopped halfway, as if he'd caught himself reaching for something that bit. Sera pretended not to notice. She'd gotten good at pretending not to notice Felix Hartwell noticing things.

The girl appeared on the fourth hour, the way trouble always did: quietly, and from an angle no one was watching.

* * *

She was sitting alone on a milestone at the road's edge, feet bare, a blanket around her shoulders that had once been somebody's banner—Sera could see the faded gold thread of some house sigil worn down to ghosts. Around her, the column flowed past like water around a stone. No one claimed her. No one even looked at her twice, which in a column of three thousand frightened people was its own kind of wrong.

"She's been passed along," Sera murmured. "Hand to hand. Nobody wants to be the one holding her when the hunters ask questions."

"Mute?"

"Or smart enough to be."

They stopped. The girl looked up at them with a face that was maybe eight years old and eyes that were not. Not uncanny—not the starless void of the king Sera had never seen but already dreaded—but old in a way that made Sera think of ward-stones: patient, load-bearing, holding up something enormous without complaint.

Felix crouched. It was, Sera thought, the most natural thing he'd done in four days of travel. Everything else about him was armor—his silences, his deflections, the careful way he kept his wrapped wrist turned inward like a man hiding a debt. But he crouched in front of this child the way soldiers crouch in front of children in burned towns, with a gentleness that clearly embarrassed him the moment he noticed himself doing it.

"Hello," he said. "You got a name?"

The girl didn't answer. She reached into the folds of her blanket and produced a piece of charcoal—real charcoal, hoarded, precious—and a scrap of leather. And she began to draw.

Sera had spent twenty years reading schematics. She knew every conventional notation for the sky: the navigational charts, the ward-lattice star maps, the twelve houses of the zodiac that every schoolchild learned. She knew what stars were supposed to look like when people drew them.

This wasn't that.

The girl worked fast, strokes sure as a smith's hammer. A curve of seven points, arced like a sickle—but the spacing was wrong, or rather right in a way no living sky had ever been, each point locked to the next by fine lines the girl drew with geometric precision. Then below it, a cluster of nine. Then a long diagonal chain, eleven stars running down the leather like a falling ladder. None of it matched any chart Sera knew. All of it was *structured*—constellations don't happen by accident; they're architecture—and all of it was wrong.

"What is that?" Felix asked softly.

The girl looked at him. Then she pointed up, at the blank black nothing where the stars should have been, and tapped the leather.

Sera felt the ground tilt slightly, the way it did when a ward you'd trusted your whole life hummed a note you'd never heard before.

"That's not the old sky," she said. Her voice came out strange. "I know the old sky. That's not it."

The girl tilted her head at her, unimpressed, and added three more stars to the sickle-curve, connecting them, and then—deliberately, looking Sera dead in the eye—drew a circle around the whole figure and put a single dot in its center.

A ward-anchor. Any ward-smith would recognize it. You anchored a lattice to a fixed point, and the fixed point held everything else true.

The girl had drawn constellations with anchor-points. As if the stars themselves had been warded into place. As if the sky were a construction, and this child had memorized the blueprint.

"Where did you learn this?" Sera crouched now too, forgetting her knees, forgetting the road, forgetting the three thousand people streaming past. "Who taught you?"

The girl shrugged—a child's shrug, sudden and total—and pointed at her own chest.

Nobody taught her. Or nobody alive. Sera looked at Felix and saw her own vertigo reflected there.

"We can't leave her on a milestone," Felix said.

"No," Sera agreed. "We also can't walk into the capital trailed by a mystery the Pale Ledger would burn a province to collect."

Felix looked at the girl a moment longer. Something moved behind his face—Sera was learning to read the flickers, the brief thawings—and whatever it was, it decided the argument before he opened his mouth.

"Wren," he said. "We'll call you Wren. Small bird. Hard to catch."

The girl considered this. Then she took up her charcoal and wrote two letters on the corner of the leather, blocky and careful:

**W. R.**

Then she stood, took the hem of Felix's cloak in one small fist, and fell into step beside him as if she'd been marching with them for years.

"Well," Sera said. "That's settled, apparently."

* * *

Three days later, Sera caught Wren drawing again, and this time she sat down beside her and didn't pretend she wasn't staring.

They'd made camp in a quarry town called Marrow's Rest, a place of slag heaps and abandoned kilns where the column paused to rest its animals. The refugees had organized themselves with the grim competence of people who understood the old world wasn't coming back soon. Fires in rings. Watch rotations. And in the lee of a cold kiln, a child with charcoal and an ever-growing collection of leather scraps, drawing skies.

Wren had spoken nothing since the milestone. But she had opinions. She tugged Felix's sleeve when he walked too far from the fire. She arranged their gear with an efficiency that shamed them both. She had, on the second day, taken Sera's scarred hands in both of hers, turned them over, traced the burn scars across the knuckles with one finger, and looked up with an expression of such frank assessment that Sera had laughed for the first time since the wall fell—a short, rusty sound that startled them all.

Now Sera watched the charcoal move and let her professional mind do what it did best: find the pattern.

Forty-one scraps now. Forty-one figures. And over three evenings of quiet study, Sera had done what no one in the column had thought to do, because no one else could read it: she'd begun plotting the constellations against each other. Relative positions. Angles. Spacing.

They fit together.

Not loosely, not approximately—they fit the way stones fit in a wall Sera herself might have built. Every constellation connected to at least two others through those fine drawn lines, and the lines met at points, and the points were anchors, and the anchors formed a lattice, and the lattice—

Sera set down the scrap she was holding very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Felix."

He was sharpening his knife by the fire, a ritual he performed whether the blade needed it or not, and she'd come to understand it as the same thing she did when she re-sorted her tools: order against chaos, hands busy so the mind doesn't scream. He looked up.

"Come here. Bring the light."

He came. She laid out six scraps in a row, then overlaid two more, angling them. "Tell me what you see."

Felix studied it. He was no ward-smith, but twelve years of mercenary work meant he could read terrain, and terrain was what this was. "A map," he said slowly. "Of... the sky? Seen from somewhere."

"Seen from everywhere. That's the trick of it." Sera's finger traced the lattice. "These aren't pictures of constellations. They're a schematic. The stars aren't just lights, Felix—they're nodes. Anchored. Someone warded the sky itself, ages ago, and this—" she touched the center-dot on the sickle-figure, "—this is the master anchor. One point that holds the whole lattice true."

"And the thing wearing the king's crown is eating the stars."

"Eating the anchors." Sera heard her own voice go flat, which it did when the mathematics turned ugly. "You don't eat a lattice node by node unless you want the structure to fail gracefully. Slowly. So nobody notices until it's too late to rebuild. The border wards didn't fall because something broke through. They fell because the sky above them stopped holding them up. We weren't guarding the wall. The sky was, and it's being dismantled."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the camp a baby cried and was hushed.

Felix sat down heavily on the kiln stones. "And she knows the blueprint."

"She *is* the blueprint, as far as I can tell. Ask her how, and you'll get a shrug. But watch this." Sera beckoned Wren over, and the girl came, charcoal-stained, solemn. Sera held up a fresh scrap and pointed at the blank space between two of the drawn constellations. "What goes here?"

Wren took the charcoal. Drew four stars, linked, and then—this was the part that stopped Sera's heart—drew the connecting lines to the neighboring figures *before* drawing the stars themselves. Structure first. Light after. The way a smith builds the frame and hangs the bell.

"She's not remembering pictures," Sera said quietly. "She's remembering the *engineering*. Felix, the old sky was built. Built and maintained. And either she was there, or someone who was there poured it into her head, and either way—" 

"Either way the Pale Ledger would call her the greatest abomination on the continent," Felix finished.

"The crown-thing would call her dessert," Sera said. "It's eating anchors. She's a person-shaped archive of every anchor left."

They looked at the child, who was looking back at them with those old, patient, load-bearing eyes, entirely aware of what they'd just said about her, and entirely unafraid, and somehow that was the worst part.

* * *

The hunters came at moonset, when the camp was thickest with sleep.

Sera woke to silence—the specific, engineered silence of a camp whose dogs had been dealt with first. She was on her feet with a knife before her mind finished the thought, shaking Felix, pressing Wren's boots into the girl's hands, and by the time the first scream rose from the eastern fire-rings they were already moving through the slag heaps, low and fast, Wren riding Felix's back with her fists knotted in his collar.

There were six of them. Sera counted by lantern-light as they swept the camp's edge in pairs, moving with that terrible unhurried arithmetic she'd started to hate. Chalk-ledgers in hand. Checking faces against a list. And on the list, she'd have bet her remaining tools, was a disgraced ward-smith, a mark-bearer, and now a child.

"They'll sweep toward the kilns last," Felix breathed. "Open ground. We cut north through the slag tunnels."

"The tunnels collapse."

"The tunnels collapse *eventually*. Hunters are immediate."

It was, infuriatingly, good tactics. They went.

The slag tunnels were old ventilation shafts from the smelting days, ribbed with brick, breathing cold air that tasted of iron. Felix went first with Wren on his back, and Sera followed with a shuttered lantern, and for a hundred yards the only sounds were footsteps and the child's breath steady against Felix's neck.

Then light bloomed behind them—white, hard, hunter's-glamour—and a voice rolled down the tunnel, amplified by the brickwork, calm as a clerk reading accounts:

"*By the authority of the Pale Ledger: surrender the unregistered practitioners. Surrender the child. Resistance will be entered as evidence.*"

Felix stopped. In the lantern-light Sera saw his face do the thing it did—the war behind it, the wrapped wrist, the twelve years of a god's silence pressing down on a man who'd sworn off exactly this kind of moment.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm not going to—"

"You're doing the math. I can hear it. Whatever answers when you open that door, Felix, it isn't going to stop at knocking hunters down."

His jaw worked. Behind them the light grew. Ahead, the tunnel forked, and Sera didn't know these workings, didn't know if the left branch ran fifty yards or five hundred or straight into a collapsed shaft—

Wren slid off Felix's back.

She walked to the fork, stood between the two branches, and raised one small arm. Pointed left. Then she looked back at them, and in the failing glamour-light Sera watched the child do something no child should be able to do: she *held* the darkness. Just for a heartbeat—just long enough that the white light behind them seemed to stumble, to lose its footing, to gutter like a candle in a draft.

Then she pointed left again, insistent, and climbed back onto Felix's back, and pressed her face against his neck, done, spent, trembling faintly.

Left branch. Fifty yards later it bent, and bent again, and came out through a hidden vent onto the moor north of town, and behind them the hunters' voices tangled and doubled back on themselves in the labyrinth, chasing an account that wouldn't balance.

They walked until dawn. When the gray light came, Sera looked at Felix over the sleeping child on his back, and saw that his hands were shaking—not from fear. From the effort of not using whatever lived behind his wrist. From carrying a child who had just spent power older than kingdoms to save them, and asking nothing.

"Tenderness is going to get us killed," Sera said.

"Probably," Felix agreed.

Neither of them suggested letting go of it.

They crested the moor road as the sun rose on a starless sky, and Wren stirred, lifted her head, and looked east at the light. Then she tugged Felix's collar and pointed—not at the sun, not at the road, but south. Toward the capital. Toward the crown.

And scratched into the dirt at the roadside, quick and certain, she drew a single figure: the sickle-curve of seven stars, and its master anchor-point.

Underneath it, in her blocky careful letters, she wrote a word she had never written before, the first word anyone had seen from her:

**HURRY.**
