## 7. Witch-Hunters of the Pale Ledger

The quarry came out of the rain like a wound in the world.

Felix had been watching the road behind them for two days—watching for the pale cloaks, the ledger-books chained to their belts, the particular way witch-hunters walked as though the ground owed them an accounting. He'd told himself they'd lost the trail at the river ford. He'd almost believed it.

Then Sera stopped at the quarry's rim and said, "Dead end," in the flat voice she used when she was furious at herself, and he knew.

Below them, the flooded pit spread out gray-green under a sky that had no stars even at noon anymore. Rain dimpled the water. A rusted crane arm leaned over the edge like a hanged man. The only way through was down the switchback cart-road and up the far side, three hundred paces of open ground with nowhere to hide.

"They're not rushing," Sera said. She had Wren by the hand, and the girl stood pressed against her leg, silent as always, her eyes moving across the water with that unsettling steadiness she brought to everything. "If they were sure of us, they'd already be here."

"Then what are they waiting for?"

Sera didn't answer, but Felix felt it before she said it—the pressure behind his eyes, the wrongness in the air, like the moment before lightning except stretched thin over minutes instead of seconds. The mark on his wrist woke. Not burning this time. *Listening.*

"The ledger," Sera said quietly. "They don't chase you, Hartwell. They read you. They've been reading us since the ford. Every time I touch stone, every time you—" she glanced at his wrist, "—every time that thing on your arm so much as itches, they write it down. They're waiting until they know exactly what we are."

"And then?"

"Then they stop being careful."

The first pale cloak crested the road behind them while she was still speaking. Then a second. A third. Six of them, fanned out in a line with the ease of men who had done this a hundred times, their gray-white mantles darkening with rain, their faces hidden under wide-brimmed hats. Each carried a long hooked staff, and each wore at the belt a book bound in something that wasn't leather. The books were open. The pages turned themselves.

"Witch-hunters of the Pale Ledger," Sera breathed. "I thought they were a story mothers tell."

"They're real." Felix's mouth tasted like copper. "They burned my company's hedge-witch outside Corvane when I was twenty-two. They made us watch. They made us *thank* them after." He remembered the smell. Twelve years later, he still remembered the smell.

Down in the quarry, the water lay still beneath the rain, and Felix understood, all at once, why the hunters had chosen this place. Not because of the open ground.

Because of the echo.

* * *

They went down the switchbacks fast, boots skidding on wet clay, because going back was dying and going forward was only probably dying. Wren rode Sera's back with her arms locked around the woman's neck, and Sera climbed like someone who had spent her life hauling stone, which, Felix supposed, she had.

Halfway down, the hunters began to speak.

Not words. Not yet. A low chanted drone that rolled down into the pit and came back off the water doubled and strange, so that it seemed to come from everywhere, from below the ground, from inside the stone. Felix's mark flared hot enough that he gasped.

"What are they saying?" he demanded.

"Your name," Sera said grimly. "Or the shape of it. They're writing you into the ledger. Once your true name is fully inscribed, they can find you anywhere. Anywhere, Hartwell. There is no hiding from a page that knows you."

"Then we kill them before they finish the sentence."

"With what?" She landed on the quarry floor and set Wren down, and for a moment the three of them stood in the vast wet bowl of the place, small as insects, the water stretching away toward the far wall where the road climbed out. "I have half a ward left in me and no loom to weave it on. You have—" Her eyes dropped to his wrist. "You have whatever that is, and you won't use it."

"I can't use it."

"You mean you're afraid to."

"Yes," Felix said. "That's what *can't* means, for people like me."

The hunters reached the rim above. Their chanting deepened. And the water began to move.

Not from wind. It rose at the edges first, climbing the quarry walls in slow ropes, and Felix watched with a cold crawling horror as the floodwater shaped itself—not waves, not spray, but *letters*. Vast dripping glyphs hanging in the air, each one a fragment of him: the name he'd been born with, the name he'd buried, the oath he'd sworn to the Quiet Order, the twelve years of silence scored through like a canceled debt. The hunters were transcribing his soul into the rain, and the rain was cooperating.

Wren tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed at the water, then at him, then made a small urgent motion with both hands—*push*, the gesture said—and looked at him with those old, old eyes.

"She's right," Sera said. Her voice had gone strange. "Hartwell. The mark. It's not just a brand. I told you—an unbinding sigil. Do you understand what that means? Unbinding cuts both ways. It doesn't just sever. It *releases*. Whatever your god left in you, whatever's been sitting behind that silence for twelve years—it isn't gone. It's *held*. And those hunters are about to write a cage around you that nothing escapes."

"So I let them?"

"So you choose," Sera said. "For once in your cowardly, careful life, you choose *before* they choose for you."

The first glyph finished itself above the water—a complete word, his birth-name, blazing white and dripping—and every hunter below raised their hooked staves, and the world narrowed to a point.

Felix reached for the mark.

He hadn't done it in twelve years. He'd built a whole life on not doing it, a life of small charms and smaller lies, luck-sigils sold to dockworkers, healing salves brewed weak enough to pass as medicine. He'd told himself the silence was grief. He'd told himself the silence was punishment. He had never once, in all those years, admitted the truth he knew now as his fingers closed over the burning sigil:

He'd kept it caged because he was afraid of how much he wanted it.

*Come,* he thought—or said, or screamed, he would never afterward be sure—and the mark opened.

* * *

It answered too easily.

That was the first thing. No struggle, no resistance, none of the grinding effort he remembered from the wars, when calling power had been like dragging a plow through frozen ground. This was like opening a door in a flood. The power didn't come to him; it had been *waiting*, pressed against the other side of the seal for twelve years, and the moment he gave it a crack it poured through him like he was the one being poured.

The second thing was the hunger in it.

Old combat magic had always had a taste—ozone, iron, the sharp green smell of a storm. This had a taste too, and the taste was *appetite*. It moved through his veins looking for things to consume, and Felix felt it cataloguing him as it passed: his fear, his guilt, his twelve years of self-erasure, tasting each one and finding them insufficient, finding them *edible*, reaching past them toward something deeper—

*No,* he thought. *Take the hunters. Only the hunters.*

The power agreed the way a wolf agrees to a leash it hasn't yet tested.

He raised his hand, and the quarry changed.

The glyphs of his name shattered—not broken, *unwritten*, the letters collapsing back into rain mid-air, and the hunters' chant stuttered as their ledger-pages began turning backward, erasing themselves, shrieking in thin papery voices. Felix walked forward across the flooded quarry floor and the water parted around his boots in a perfect circle, and he could hear himself speaking words in a language he had never learned and somehow had always known, and the words were not a spell.

They were a *question*. Asked very politely. Asked the way a wolf asks a sheep.

The nearest hunter threw down his staff and ran. The second raised his ledger-book as a shield and the book burst into white flame that consumed nothing but the writing, leaving the hunter staring at blank pages, screaming—not in pain, Felix noted distantly, in *emptiness*, in the specific agony of a man whose record of himself has just been deleted. The third and fourth and fifth came on together, hooks swinging, and Felix caught the first staff barehanded and the wood *unmade* itself in his grip, rotted to dust between heartbeats, and the hunter looked at his own empty hands with the expression of a man who has just watched his god die.

*Stop,* Felix told the power. *This is enough. This is enough.*

The power surged toward the sixth hunter—the one at the rear, young, hat blown off, face white and ordinary and terrified—and Felix felt, with sick clarity, exactly what it intended. Not disarming. Not driving off. It intended to reach down the boy's throat and pull out everything he was, the way the crown-thing pulled stars out of the sky, the way it had pulled Felix's god out of heaven, and it intended to do it *through Felix's hand*, and it intended to enjoy it.

He wrenched his arm down. The bolt meant for the young hunter tore a trench through the quarry wall instead, a smoking furrow fifty feet long, and the remaining hunters broke and ran, and the silence they left behind was enormous.

The power receded slowly. Reluctantly. Like a tide that had discovered it liked the shore.

Felix stood in the circle of dry stone with his hand still raised, shaking, and realized he was smiling.

That was the worst part. That was the part he would carry up the far side of the quarry and into every night that followed: somewhere in the middle of it, in the white heat of the unwriting, some buried splinter of Felix Hartwell had been *joyful*.

"Hartwell." Sera's voice, carefully level. She had Wren's face pressed into her shoulder, shielding her eyes, though the girl was peeking around anyway—of course she was, she remembered stars, of course she watched. "Are you still in there?"

"Yeah," Felix said. His voice came out scraped. "Yeah. I'm—I'm here."

"Good." She crossed to him, boots splashing, and grabbed his raised wrist and turned it over. The sigil was still glowing, faint gold under the skin, pulsing like a second heartbeat. "Put it away."

"I'm trying."

"*Try harder.*"

He closed his fist. Breathed. Counted, the way the Quiet Order had taught him, the way he hadn't needed to count since the wars. One for the breath. Two for the fear. Three for the joy, and four for the shame of the joy, and five, six, seven, until the glow guttered and died and the mark went dormant again, sullen, a banked fire pretending to be ash.

When he could speak normally, he said: "It answered too fast."

"I noticed."

"It shouldn't have. Combat magic takes years. It took me ten years to throw lightning that didn't scorch my own arms, and today I unwrote a Pale Ledger in the time it takes to sneeze." He looked at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. "Twelve years it sat quiet, and the moment I called, it was *right there*. Like it had been standing at the door the whole time. Like it had been listening to every prayer I didn't say."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the water. Far above, the hunters' pale cloaks dwindled along the rim road, running, and did not come back.

"Do you know what I think?" she said finally. "I think your god didn't just go silent. I think it *concentrated*. Everything it was, packed down into one sigil on one man's wrist, sealed and stored. And stored power doesn't fade, Hartwell. Stored power *ferments*." Her scarred hands flexed at her sides. "Whatever answered you today, it's more than your god was. Or less. Or other. I don't know which frightens me most."

"And if using it again makes it worse?"

"Then we don't use it again." She met his eyes, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—not trust, not quite, but its foundation being laid, stone by stubborn stone. "Unless we have to. Which we will. Because that thing wearing the king's crown is eating the sky, and you and I both know that somewhere between here and the capital, 'we don't use it again' stops being an option."

She turned and lifted Wren onto her hip, and started for the far road without waiting for an answer, because Sera Ives never waited for answers she'd already decided on.

Felix followed. The trench his power had torn through the quarry wall steamed gently beside them all the way up, a black seam in the living rock, straight as a surveyor's line.

*That's what you did,* he told himself. *One moment of anger, and that's the line you draw in the world.*

But underneath the shame, quieter, in a voice he refused to examine, another thought kept pace with them up the switchbacks:

*And God help me, it felt like coming home.*

* * *

They made eight miles before dark, holed up in a shepherd's bothy with a leaking roof and a view of a sky that stayed black no matter how long they waited. Wren slept between them, dead weight and warm, one fist curled around a scrap of charcoal she'd been drawing with. On the floor beside her: constellations. Dozens of them, drawn from memory, named in no atlas Sera had ever seen.

Felix sat by the door with his back to the wall and his wrist wrapped in linen, though wrapping it was like bandaging a tooth. Sleep kept sliding away from him whenever he got close. Every time he drifted, he was back in the quarry, hand raised, smiling.

Across the room, Sera spoke without opening her eyes. "You'll do it again."

"I know."

"When the time comes, you'll call it, and it'll answer, and it'll be worse than the quarry, and you'll survive it, because surviving is the one thing you've never failed at." She shifted, pulling the blanket higher over Wren. "Just make sure there's still a *you* left on the other side to survive. That's all. That's the whole speech."

"That's a terrible speech."

"I'm a ward-smith, not a priest."

Felix almost smiled. Then, through the bothy's cracked shutter, he saw it—a light on the horizon behind them, back the way they'd come. Not torchlight. Not lightning. A column of pale radiance rising off the earth like an inverted waterfall, miles distant, and at its base, faint but unmistakable, the glint of open pages catching light that had no source.

The hunters weren't running.

They were reporting. Somewhere north, something with authority over pale books was reading what Felix had done in the quarry—and answering.

The light held for a long moment. Then it folded itself away, and the dark came back, and in the dark Felix heard, very softly, from the mark beneath its linen wrappings, a sound he had not heard in twelve years and had never expected to hear again.

A heartbeat. Slow, patient, content.

Like something that had just gotten what it wanted.
