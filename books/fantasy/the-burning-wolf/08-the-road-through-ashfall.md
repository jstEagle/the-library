## 8. The Road Through Ashfall

Wren knew the land was dead before anyone said it out loud.

She knew it the way she knew things now—before words, before pictures, in the place behind her ribs where the sky used to live. The road bent north around a hill that should have had a village on it, and where the village should have been there was only gray. Not burned-gray. Burned things still had shapes underneath, black bones of houses, the question of what they'd been. This was different. This was *unmade* gray, soft as ash and hard as iron, a color with nothing under it.

The wheat fields on either side of the road stood in rows like people waiting for something that never came. They weren't brown. Brown meant dead from living. These were the color of the inside of her mouth when she woke up scared.

Sera stopped at the top of the rise and pulled her scarf up over her nose. "Ashfall," she said through the cloth. Her voice came out flat and strange. "I've heard about this. Thought it was hunters' talk."

"It's not talk," Felix said.

He was standing beside Wren, close enough that his coat brushed her arm when the wind moved him. He did that a lot. He thought she didn't notice, but Wren noticed everything now; noticing was all she had. He kept himself between her and the open country the way a wall keeps itself between a room and the weather, and he probably believed he was being subtle about it.

He was not subtle. He was the least subtle person Wren had ever been bound to, and she loved him for it in the fierce, wordless way you love a roof in a storm.

"How far does it go?" Sera asked.

Felix shaded his eyes, though there was no sun to shade them from—just the flat white nothing where the sky used to have a face. "Miles. Maybe more. The hunger spreads like spilled milk." He looked down at Wren, and his mouth did the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth a child could carry. "You can ride on my shoulders if the ground gets bad."

Wren shook her head. She could walk. Walking was one of the few things she was still good at.

* * *

They went down into the gray together, three small dark shapes moving through a country that had forgotten its own name.

Wren walked in the middle because they made her walk in the middle, Felix ahead and Sera behind, and she let them arrange her that way even though she didn't need protecting. She needed something else, something neither of them could give her yet, and so she walked and watched and kept her hands closed around the secret she'd been carrying since the quarry.

The ash got into everything. It sifted down out of the dead air in a fine, endless snowfall—not falling from clouds, because there were no clouds, just seeping out of the sky itself like the whole world above them was quietly crumbling. It settled in the creases of Sera's gloves. It turned Felix's dark hair gray at the temples, which made Wren want to laugh, which made her throat ache, because laughing was another thing she couldn't do anymore.

It got into her mouth when she breathed. It tasted like the end of a sentence.

That was the thing about Ashfall that nobody who hadn't been here would understand: it wasn't quiet. Quiet would have been bearable. This was *silent*, which is what quiet becomes after it dies. In the quarry, hiding from the hunters, there had been water sounds, wind sounds, the far-off machinery of birds. Here there was nothing to hear except their own bodies—their boots, their breath, the creak of Sera's pack—and every sound they made fell straight down and died without an echo, as if the land refused to repeat anything back.

As if the land had learned that repeating things gets you eaten.

Wren hugged her arms around the bundle at her chest—her drawings, wrapped in oilcloth, the constellations no adult could name. She'd added a new one two nights ago, by firelight while the men slept: a shape she remembered from before, a great wolf made of burning lines, running across a darkness that wasn't empty but full. She didn't know why she'd drawn it. She only knew that when she looked at it, the place behind her ribs warmed, and warmth was rare enough now to hoard.

"Stay close," Felix said, not turning around. He always knew when she drifted, even a step. "Don't touch anything that looks like it's still growing."

Nothing looked like it was still growing. That was the horror of it. But she nodded against the back of his shoulder, because nodding was cheap and it made him feel better, and making Felix feel better had become, without her ever deciding it, one of her jobs.

* * *

They found the first house an hour past noon, or what noon would have been if the light had any opinion about time.

It had been a farmhouse once—you could tell from the well, and the stone trough, and the child's swing hanging from the oak by the door. The oak was gray. The swing was gray. Even the rope, which should have rotted, was gray, preserved the way things are preserved at the bottom of the sea, in a place where time has been told to stop.

But the house itself—

Wren stopped walking.

The house was still *there*. That was the wrong part. Everything else in Ashfall had been rubbed down to its bones, but the house stood complete, walls and roof and shutters, as solid as the day it was built. Solid and wrong, like a face with no expression at all. Like a face someone had wiped clean to draw on later.

"The hunger doesn't take buildings," Sera murmured. She'd come up beside Wren, and her scarred hands were doing the thing they did when she was frightened—flexing, opening, closing, as if warming themselves at a fire that wasn't there. "It takes what's *in* them. What they're for." She looked at the swing. She looked away fast.

Felix drew his knife anyway. He always drew his knife, even when a knife was useless, and Wren understood that too: the knife wasn't for the enemy. It was for Felix. Holding it reminded his hands what they were for.

"We go around," he said.

They went around. But as they passed the doorway, Wren felt it—a pull, faint and cold, like a finger hooking gently into the cloth over her heart. She turned her head, and through the open door she saw the inside of the house, and the inside of the house was full of the family.

Not their bodies. Their *shapes*. Standing in the kitchen, sitting at the table, bent over a cradle in the corner—gray outlines, human-shaped absences, the memory of people worn into the room the way footsteps wear into stone. A mother-shape stirred a pot that wasn't there. A father-shape mended a shoe. Two child-shapes chased each other around a table, around and around, forever, and none of them had faces, and none of them would ever stop.

They weren't ghosts. Ghosts were dead people who wouldn't leave. These were living people who'd been *taken out*, leaving the hole of them behind, and the hole went on performing the life it used to hold because nothing had told it to stop.

Wren's hand found Felix's sleeve and gripped it.

He followed her eyes through the doorway. She felt him go rigid. She felt the moment he saw the cradle, and the small shape bent over it, and she felt the breath go out of him slow and controlled, the way he breathed when he was putting his grief somewhere it couldn't reach his hands.

"Yeah," he said softly. "We go around."

They went around. But Wren looked back until the house was gone, and the last thing she saw was the mother-shape lifting the pot to taste a meal that had no taste, offering it to children who had no mouths, and she thought: *this is what it eats first.* Sera had told them on the road, in her flat ward-smith's voice, laying it out like a diagram. Memory first. Then magic. Then everything else. It eats the remembering out of a thing and leaves the shape, because a shape is easier to wear.

Wren pressed her arms tighter around her drawings.

Because that was the thing Felix and Sera didn't know—couldn't know, because she couldn't tell them—that the crown-thing had already tried to eat her. Back in the refugee column, back before they found her, back when the stars went out and everyone screamed and she didn't scream because screaming requires a voice and hers had gone somewhere else. Something vast had leaned down through the dark where the sky used to be and looked for her memories, the way a man searches a drawer.

And it hadn't found them.

It had found the drawer empty—or worse than empty, *locked*, and behind the lock a sky older than its theft, constellations no adult could name because those adults had never seen them, and the crown-thing had flinched. Wren had felt it flinch. She had felt something older than the crown-thing look back at her through her own eyes, briefly, like a stranger checking a window, and then withdraw.

She didn't know what lived in her memory. She only knew it was still there, and that the crown-thing wanted it, and that wanting it made her the most dangerous thing in Ashfall despite being seven years old and unable to speak.

She kept walking. She kept the secret. Secrets were heavy, but they were also warm, like stones from a fire, and you carried them close.

* * *

The second night, they sheltered in the lee of a mile-marker whose carved distance-numbers had been smoothed blank, and Felix built a fire that gave off heat but almost no light, because light traveled in Ashfall and they'd learned that lesson near the quarry.

They ate in silence. Sera cleaned her tools. Felix sharpened his knife, though it didn't need it, and Wren sat between them drawing by feel, her fingers reading the charcoal the way other people read letters.

She was drawing the wolf again. The burning wolf. She'd drawn it four times now, and each time it came out clearer, each time the lines knew better where to go, and tonight, halfway through, her hand stopped.

Because she suddenly knew whose wolf it was.

It wasn't a constellation. Or—it was, but it was more than that. It was a name wearing a picture, the way a king wears a crown, and the name belonged to the man sitting six feet away from her, sharpening a knife in the dark, keeping watch over a dead country for a god that had gone silent twelve years ago.

Wren hadn't known his name. Nobody had said it. He was *Felix* the way a coat is a coat—something put on over the real person, something with pockets. But the wolf knew. The wolf had been running through her head since the quarry, since the night the forbidden magic answered him too easily, too hungrily, and she'd lain awake feeling it pace, and somewhere in the pacing the wolf had left its name in her, the way a dog leaves hair on your clothes.

She knew his true name.

And knowing it, she understood things she hadn't understood before. She understood why the mark on his wrist had chosen him. She understood why the silence of his god hurt him in the specific place it hurt, like a tooth gone bad in a jaw of gold. She understood that Felix Hartwell was a coat, and underneath the coat was a man who had given up his own name to serve something greater, and that the giving-up had worked exactly as intended right up until the night a mute child started dreaming wolves.

Being truly seen, Wren thought—being truly seen terrifies more than any enemy. She knew this because she could see herself being seen, from the outside, in advance: Felix's face when the word left her mouth. First confusion. Then stillness. Then the terrible naked look of a man whose last wall has turned to glass. She could see it so clearly it was almost cruel, and she loved him, and she was going to do it anyway.

Because names weren't secrets. Names were *keys*. And somewhere north of here, in a capital full of hungry light, there was a king with empty eyes and a crown that ate remembering, and Wren's locked drawer of old stars was going to matter, and keys were going to matter, and a man who knew who he really was would stand straighter than a man hiding in a coat.

Also—she was tired. She was so tired of carrying it alone.

"Felix," Sera said quietly, across the fire. "Your wrist."

He looked down. Even in the fireless dark, Wren could see the faint glow through his sleeve, pulsing slow, like something breathing under the skin. It had been doing that more often since the quarry. Since the magic answered him.

"It's been doing that all day," he said. His voice was carefully bored. "Since we entered the ash."

"Ash is what's left when it feeds," Sera said. "Maybe the mark can smell its own—" She stopped. She was looking past him, up the road, the way they both went still when the world offered them information for free.

Wren looked too.

Far up the dead road, where the gray met the white nothing of the sky, there was a smudge of movement. Dust, maybe. Except dust didn't move in ranks. Dust didn't glint. And nothing else in Ashfall moved at all.

"Riders," Felix said. He was already on his feet, knife away, bow out—he'd taken a hunter's bow off a corpse in the quarry and had gotten disturbingly good with it in four days. "A dozen. Maybe more. Coming down the marker line." He paused, and Wren heard him arrive at the same conclusion she'd reached seconds ago. "There's nothing else on this road. There's nothing else *alive* on this road. They're not patrolling. They're looking for something."

"For us," Sera said. "The Pale Ledger doesn't give up a debt."

"They can't know we took this road."

"They don't need to know. They need to suspect, and sweep, and this is the only road." Sera was packing with the speed of a woman who'd fled things before, her scarred hands sure and quick. "Off the road. Now. There was a cut in the hills half a mile back—we passed it—"

"I saw it." Felix scooped up Wren's bedroll one-handed and held his other hand down to her, palm open, an offer and a habit. "Wren. Up."

She took his hand. His grip was warm and callused and certain, and it closed around her fingers the way it always did, and that was when she decided.

Not because of strategy. Strategy was Sera's country. She decided because the riders were coming and the world was ending by inches and she was done letting the people she loved walk around unnamed, unprotected, unclaimed. A name was a rope between two people. She wanted the rope. If the riders caught them tonight, then whatever happened, Felix Hartwell would die—if he died—knowing someone had known him. Really known him. All the way down to the wolf.

They ran, the three of them, low along the dead fields, ash kicking up around their boots, the silent land swallowing every sound. Behind them the riders came on, patient as weather. Ahead the hills opened their gray cut like a wound, and Felix pulled her along, and Sera covered their back trail, and the lightless sky pressed down like a lid.

At the mouth of the cut, Felix stopped—one heartbeat, checking the dark, checking her, checking that his small strange family was intact—and in that heartbeat, with his hand still wrapped around hers, Wren filled her ruined lungs with ash and silence and spoke her first word in longer than she could count.

Not *Felix.*

His true name. The wolf's name. The name he'd surrendered so long ago that hearing it hit him like a blow from behind—she felt it land, felt his whole body flinch, felt his hand tighten on hers hard enough to hurt, and in the glow of his waking mark she saw his face turn toward her, and it was exactly the face she'd seen coming, confusion and stillness and the terrible glass-clear shock of being known.

Behind them, faint across the dead miles, the riders' horns began to call each other home.

And Wren, seven years old, keeper of a stolen sky, tugged him by the hand into the dark of the hills—because now he had to live long enough to ask her how.
