## 9. A King's Empty Eyes

Sera had built wards for eleven years before the Loom broke, and in that time she had learned one law above all others: magic told the truth even when people lied. A ward did not care about titles. It did not bow to crowns. It read what a thing *was*, and it answered accordingly.

So when the road to Corvane began to hum under her feet—low, wrong, like a struck bell that never finished ringing—she believed it before she believed anything else.

They had come out of the ashfall country two days past, and the land here was still alive, but barely. Orchards stood in rows with fruit gone black on the branch, not rotted but *emptied*, skins hanging like shed gloves. The villages they passed through were full of people who smiled too easily and asked no questions. That was the part that frightened Sera most. Not the hunger itself—hunger she understood, hunger was honest—but the way it made its victims comfortable.

"Third village," Felix said quietly, walking beside her with Wren perched on his shoulders like she'd always belonged there. "Third place where nobody's angry."

"People forget how to be angry," Sera said. "It's expensive. Costs more than they have left."

Wren tugged Felix's hair and pointed ahead, down the long straight causeway where poplars stood stripped of their leaves though it was high summer. Beyond them, the walls of Corvane rose pale against a sky that had no stars in it day or night—a sky Sera was beginning to think of as a mouth.

The hum grew louder.

* * *

They found lodging in a dyer's house at the city's edge, an old woman named Maren who took their coin without counting it and fed them stew without tasting it herself. Her eyes had the flat shine of river stones. When Wren dropped her spoon, Maren didn't flinch. When Felix asked about the king's upcoming vigil—the renewal ceremony, the posters plastered on every wall showing a crowned silhouette against a field of white—Maren smiled the same smile everyone smiled.

"His Majesty is generous," she said. "His Majesty keeps us safe."

"From what?" Sera asked.

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and behind the stone-shine something flickered—something small and drowning—and then it was gone, smoothed over like a hand passing over water.

"From the dark," Maren said pleasantly. "There is so much dark now."

That night Sera sat by the window of their rented room and worked. She had taken apart her kit on the floor: copper wire, silver filings, ground quartz, three intact loom-stones salvaged from the border breach that had cost her everything. She was trying to build something small—a listener, a needle of woven light that could taste the shape of power nearby without announcing itself. It was delicate work, the kind she used to do with apprentices breathing down her neck, and doing it alone in a stranger's house with a mute child asleep two feet away felt like performing surgery in a windstorm.

Felix sat across from her, cleaning a knife he hadn't needed to clean in hours.

"You're going to ask me," he said finally.

"I'm going to work."

"You've been rewinding the same coil for twenty minutes." He nodded at her hands. "You're going to ask me what I think we'll find in there."

Sera set down the wire. Outside, the city hummed. She realized she could feel it through the windowsill, through the floorboards, faint as a second heartbeat laid over her own.

"I think we'll find a man who isn't there anymore," she said. "I think whatever's wearing him has been wearing him for years, maybe since before the wards fell. I think the wards fell because the thing holding them up stopped being human enough to hold them." She turned the loom-stone in her fingers. "And I think we're going to walk into the largest concentration of devoured magic on this continent and hope it doesn't notice us."

"That's not dread. That's just inventory."

"My dread is very organized."

Something almost like a laugh moved through Felix's shoulders. Then Wren stirred in her sleep, and both of them went quiet in the particular way they'd learned to go quiet around her—two people who had forgotten how to be gentle, practicing.

"She spoke again today," Felix said. "Not a word. But she made the sound. At the orchard." He rubbed his wrist absently, where the mark lay dormant under his sleeve. "She pointed at the dead trees and made a sound like—" He stopped.

"Like what?"

"Like pity."

Sera looked at the sleeping girl. Wren remembered stars no adult could name. Wren had walked out of a refugee column into Sera's arms as if she'd been sent. And now, apparently, Wren pitied the trees the crown-thing had drained.

Children remember what adults are made to forget. Sera's grandmother had said that, back when grandmother's sayings were about weather and bread and not about the end of the world.

"We need to see him," Sera said. "Before the vigil. I need to taste what's in that palace from close enough to be sure. If the possession is complete—if there's nothing left of the man to pull back—then the unbinding won't work. There'd be nothing to unbind *to*. We'd be killing a king to save a country, and I need to know which war we're fighting before I build the rite around it."

"And if there is something left?"

"Then we have a deadline." She met his eyes. "We've had one all along. We just haven't been able to read it yet."

* * *

The king rode out on the third morning, and Sera understood, watching, why the old orders had forbidden their smiths from looking directly at great works of power. Not because it blinded you. Because it *taught* you things you couldn't unlearn.

They heard him before they saw him—or rather, they heard the absence of what should have been. No horns. No drums. The procession came up the causeway in perfect silence, fifty riders in white and gold, hooves wrapped in cloth, and the only sound in all of Corvane's outskirts was the hum, swelling now until Sera felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of her old scars, in the loom-stones in her pack, which had begun to shiver against each other like frightened animals.

She stood with Felix and Wren among the crowd at the roadside, pressed between a fishmonger and a family of candlemakers, all of them smiling, all of them waving little white banners with the crowned silhouette printed on them.

"There," Felix breathed.

The king came at the center of the formation on a grey horse that walked as though it were asleep standing up. He was not old—that surprised her. The portraits on the vigil posters showed a stern man in his fifties, heavy-browed, the kind of face built for coinage. The man on the horse was younger than that, or had been frozen at some age between forty and forever, handsome in the way marble is handsome, everything symmetrical and nothing warm.

He wore the crown low over his brow. And his eyes—

Sera had seen voids before. She had once repaired a ward over a mine shaft so deep the lamplight died ten feet down, and she had leaned over that darkness and felt it lean back. The king's eyes were that. Not dark. Dark was a color; this was an *absence*, two holes punched in a face where the light went in and nothing came out, and as the horse carried him past—slow, ceremonial, close enough that Sera could have reached out and touched the stirrup—she felt those eyes pass over the crowd like a lantern being dragged along a row of cells.

The crowd sighed. All of them, together, one soft exhalation of pleasure, like lovers, like the dying.

Beside her, Felix had gone rigid. His hand had found hers without either of them deciding it, and his grip was crushing, and his lips were moving around words she couldn't hear—no, not words. A name. The name of a god twelve years silent, spoken the way a man speaks to a locked door he knows no one is behind.

And Wren—

Wren had climbed onto Felix's shoulders without asking, the way she never did with strangers' movements, and she was staring at the king with her head tilted, and her small hands were pressed flat against her own ears as if the hum were a shout only she could properly hear. Her eyes were wide and wet and furious.

The king's horse slowed.

It was nothing anyone else would notice. The procession kept its pace; the guards kept their spacing. But the animal's stride hitched, half a beat, and the king's head began to turn—not toward the crowd, not toward any face, but downward, slightly, toward the small girl silhouetted against the sky on a mercenary's shoulders, the way a man turns toward a smell from a kitchen he thought he'd burned down years ago.

Sera acted without thinking, because thinking took too long. She pulled the thread of her half-built listener off her wrist where she'd worn it disguised as a bracelet, crushed the fragile weave of light in her fist, and let it die. A needle of power going dark was a whisper. A needle of power living near *that* was a bell. The hum stuttered—

—and the king's head completed its turn, forward again, gracious and empty, and the procession rolled on toward the gates, and the crowd sighed again, and somewhere a woman began to weep with joy and couldn't explain to her husband why.

They walked away from the road without running. Running would have been a bell too.

* * *

Back in the room, Sera put her hands flat on the table and made herself speak like a smith delivering an assessment, because if she spoke like a person she was going to be sick.

"It's nearly done," she said. "The possession. What's left of him—" She stopped. Started again. "When I look at a ward-stone that's failing, there's a moment, right at the end, where the original pattern still shows through the damage. You can see what the smith intended. You can see the shape of the working underneath the ruin. That's what I saw. Underneath the thing wearing him, there's still a man-shaped pattern. Faint. Fainter than anything I've ever tried to restore." She looked up. "But it's there. Which means the rite can work. Which means—"

"Which means there's a deadline," Felix said.

"Yes."

"How long?"

Sera laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I don't know. That's the horror of it, Felix. Hunger doesn't keep a calendar. It eats until it's full, and it gets closer to full with every ward it swallows, every charm, every blessing, every—" she gestured vaguely at the city, at the smiling woman on the road, at Maren downstairs with her river-stone eyes, "—every *person* who gives themselves up gladly because forgetting feels like rest. The vigil is in nine days. The renewal ceremony. Whatever the crown does to finish eating the sky, it will do then. So the deadline is either nine days or tomorrow, and I cannot tell you which, and neither can your mark, and neither can—"

"Wren can."

Sera stopped.

The girl was sitting on the bed, cross-legged, awake—had been awake, Sera realized, for all of it. In her lap she had arranged the scraps of chalk she carried everywhere, and she was drawing on the floorboard: quick, sure strokes, the constellations she alone remembered, except these weren't constellations. They were numbers. Or they were arranged like numbers, grouped and regrouped, and beside them she had drawn a circle with a crown on top of it, and she had crossed the whole figure out with a single hard line.

Then she looked up at Sera, and pointed at the drawing, and pointed at the window, in the direction the king had ridden, and held up nine fingers.

Then, slowly, she folded four of them down.

Five.

Sera felt the deadline crystallize in her chest like a stone dropping through deep water—five days, the child said, five days and the pattern under the king would be too thin to catch, five days and there would be nothing left to unbind *to*—and she looked at Felix and saw the same arithmetic landing behind his eyes, and neither of them said it aloud, because saying it aloud would make it real, and it was already real, it had been real since the night the stars went out and they had simply refused to read the number.

"Five days," Felix said anyway, because he was the kind of man who walked into burning buildings to check whether the fire was real.

Wren nodded once, gravely, and wiped her nose with her sleeve, and went back to her chalk.

Outside, the sun was setting over a city that had forgotten how to be afraid, and the first evening bell rang from the palace, and the hum rose to meet it, harmonizing, patient, certain—as certain as a mouth that knows the meal is already served and only waits to be allowed to swallow.

Sera began packing her kit. Five days meant leaving tonight.

They were halfway to the door when the knocking started downstairs—three slow blows, courteous, unhurried—and Maren's voice, still pleasant, still smooth as poured wax, calling up the stairs:

"Pilgrims? There are gentlemen here from the palace. They're asking after the woman with the broken hands."
