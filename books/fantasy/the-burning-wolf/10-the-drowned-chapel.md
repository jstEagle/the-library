## 10. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had drowned a hundred years before the stars did, and the water had never bothered to leave.

It stood in the old flood-meadows south of the capital road, half-swallowed, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against the sky that no longer held anything worth leaning toward. The nave lay beneath three feet of black, still water. Stained glass windows rose out of it like the spines of drowned animals, their saints long since faded to featureless panes of green and gold.

"We can't stay here," Sera said.

"We can't *not* stay here," Felix said. "Wren hasn't slept in two days. You're walking on a leg I'm fairly sure is broken, not just cut. And there are witch-hunters on every road between here and the capital, carrying ledgers with my face sketched in them." He looked at the flooded doorway, the dark water breathing gently against its frame. "Nobody hunts a drowned church."

"Everything about you is a bad idea wearing a coat," Sera said, but she was already wading in.

They made camp on the rood loft, the raised gallery above the nave where the choir had once stood. It was dry, or dry enough—Sera laid out her warding kit and built a perimeter of chalk and copper wire across the two stairways, small honest magic, the kind that didn't sing to anything hungry. Wren curled up against Felix's pack and was asleep before he finished unlacing his boots.

He sat a long time watching her breathe.

*Felix,* she'd said, on the ash road. His true name, spoken by a child who had never heard it. He still hadn't asked her how. He wasn't sure he wanted the answer, because there were only two possible answers: she was something more than a girl, or he was something less than a man, and either way the ground under him shifted.

The mark on his wrist pulsed. Once. Softly. Like a knock.

He'd been feeling it for hours—the way you feel someone standing behind you in an empty room. Since they'd crossed into the flood-meadows it had been building, a warmth under the skin, a pressure like words swallowed. Here, in this ruined house of a god whose name Felix had never been permitted to speak aloud, the mark was almost loud.

"You should sleep," Sera said quietly. She sat at the edge of the loft, her splinted leg stretched out, whittling a new stylus from a pew slat she'd pried loose. "You look like something the river refused."

"In a minute."

She studied him. She was getting better at reading him, which he resented. "It's talking to you, isn't it. The mark."

"It doesn't talk. It hasn't talked in twelve years. It just—" He flexed his hand. Under the firelight the sigil looked like a brand, a wolf-shaped knot of scar tissue that had never quite healed right. "It pulls. That's all it's ever done. Point me somewhere, drag me somewhere, get people killed."

"And here?"

Here it didn't pull. Here it *waited*, the way a dog waits at a door it knows will open.

"I'm going down there," Felix said.

Sera's stylus stopped moving. "Into the water."

"The chapel was consecrated to him. To—" He gestured vaguely at the mark. "To whatever he was. If any part of what's left of my god is anywhere in this world, it'll be in a place like this. Drowned. Forgotten. Out of reach of whatever ate the sky."

"That thing ate your god twelve years ago. That's the whole point. That's why you've been deaf ever since."

"Then there's nothing down there," Felix said, "and I lose an hour of sleep. But if there's *something*—" He stopped. He heard his own voice doing the thing he hated, the thing he'd done in the quarry when the combat magic answered too easily: wanting. "I served him for twenty years, Sera. Before the silence. Nobody ever told me what happened that night. The order said *the god withdrew*, like he was a landlord giving notice. I carried that. Twelve years of wondering if I'd failed some test, if I'd prayed wrong, fought wrong, believed wrong. If there's an answer at the bottom of this water, I want it."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. In the light of the small lamp her face was all angles and exhaustion, and underneath both, unexpectedly, kindness.

"Felix Hartwell," she said—which wasn't his name, but she used it the way Wren had used the real one, as if names were doors you could choose to open—"if it hurts you, you come back up. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Swear it like a man who's been wrong before."

"I swear it like a man who's been wrong so many times it's practically a trade." He stood, stripped off his coat and boots, and buckled the knife back on anyway, because some habits were load-bearing.

* * *

The water was cold in the way of things that had been cold for a century.

He went down the nave stair slowly, one hand on the rail, letting the black water climb him—knees, waist, chest. His lamp threw a trembling circle across the flooded floor. Below him, under a fathom of still water, he could see the shapes of pews, an altar stone, the pale suggestion of a mosaic floor. Fish scattered from his light like thrown coins.

The mark burned hotter with every step.

At the altar steps the water reached his chin. Beyond them the apse opened into deeper dark, where the mosaic floor dropped away into what had once been a crypt—a reliquary, maybe, or a baptismal pool, some holy deep place. He could see nothing down there but darkness that his lamp couldn't argue with.

He filled his lungs and let himself sink.

The cold closed over his head and the world went quiet in the particular way underwater is quiet—not silent, but *muffled*, held, like the inside of a struck bell. His boots found the top step of the descent. He crouched there in the dark, lamp guttering, lungs burning, ready to go back up—

—and the mark opened.

Not pain. Worse than pain: *attention*. The sensation of being looked at from inside his own skin. And then, for the first time in twelve years, a voice.

It was not a voice the way men have voices. It came as images, as pressure, as meaning arriving whole—but Felix's mind, starved for twelve years, translated it instantly into sound, into the low warm register he remembered from prayers answered on battlefields, from the night his oath was sworn.

***Little wolf.***

Felix would have wept, if he hadn't been underwater. His whole body shook with it. Twelve years of silence, and the first word was the old name, the order's name for him, the name that meant *beloved hound of the borderlands*, and it undid him more thoroughly than any wound ever had.

***You came.***

"You're here," he said, and the water took the words and turned them to bubbles, and it didn't matter, because the god wasn't hearing with ears. "You're *here*. Where—what happened to you? Where did you go? Why did you leave us? Why did you leave *me*?"

The presence flinched. He felt it flinch, felt it the way you feel a wounded animal draw back from a reaching hand, and something cold slid through his relief, because gods do not flinch unless they are guilty.

***I did not leave, little wolf.***

"Then explain the silence. Explain the wards falling. Explain why the thing in the king's crown wears your absence like a trophy." He was shouting now, bubbles streaming, lungs emptying, and he didn't care; he'd drowned himself for this and he'd drown entirely if he had to. "Twelve years. Do you know what twelve years of silence does to a man? I thought I was abandoned. I thought I was *punished*. I built a whole life out of not mattering because it hurt less than mattering to a god who wouldn't answer!"

***Show me,*** the presence said, gentle and terrible. ***No. Let me show you.***

And the mark took him.

* * *

He saw the night it happened.

Not remembered—*saw*, the way you see through another man's eyes, except these eyes were vast and burning and full of borders. He was the god. He was the thing the old books called the Burning Wolf, the ward-father, the keeper of Vaelmoor's edges, and he was dying.

It had come up from beneath the world, from the deep places where the crown-thing had slept since before the kingdom had a name. It did not fight the way armies fight. It *ate*. It put its hunger against the god's substance and drank, and everything it drank became part of its slow return, and everything it touched was forgotten—by the world, by history, by itself.

The god could have run. Felix understood that immediately, with the sick clarity of hindsight. The god was old and wide and woven through the land itself; he could have thinned himself into rivers and root-systems and rain, scattered past the hunger's reach, gone dormant for a century, a millennium. Many of his kind had done exactly that. It was what gods did. They endured.

Instead, the Burning Wolf gathered himself together—all of himself, every ward he powered, every blessing he owed, every prayer he'd ever accepted—and he walked toward the hunger.

And he bargained.

Felix felt the bargain form, felt it in the god's own grammar, which was oaths and boundaries and the mathematics of sacrifice. The crown-thing wanted to rise *now*. Tonight. It wanted to swallow the wards and pour through them and take the sky while the kingdom slept. The god offered an alternative: *take me instead. All of me. Eat me slowly, and let the eating hold you. Let my substance be your chains. Twelve years, hunger. I am large enough to keep you fed for twelve years.*

*And in exchange:* the wards hold. The sky keeps its stars. The kingdom gets time.

*Time for what?* the hunger had asked, in its voice like a well falling upward.

*For a herald,* the god had said. ***For the one who carries my last charm.***

The vision lurched. Now Felix was seeing through different eyes—his own, younger, twelve years younger, asleep in a border fort with his brothers of the Quiet Oath around him—and he watched the god kneel beside his sleeping body. Watched the great burning shape press something into the meat of his wrist, a seed of himself, a fragment folded seven times, the last unspent coin of a bankrupted treasury.

*He will hate me,* the god had murmured, and even dying, even bargaining away his own eternity, there had been grief in it, real grief, the kind that floods. *He will think I abandoned him. Better that. A herald who grieves walks carefully. A herald who hopes runs headlong into the dark.*

*I am sorry, little wolf. This is the only door I can build you.*

*Make it count.*

* * *

Felix came up out of the water gasping, hauling himself onto the altar steps, coughing black water, shaking so hard his teeth chattered. The lamp had gone out. Above him, faint through the flooded nave, Sera's voice was calling his name, edged with panic.

He held up a hand. Heard her stop. Heard her swearing at him in ward-smith's cant, which meant she'd decided he was probably alive.

He knelt on the drowned altar steps in the dark and the fury arrived.

It came up through him like water through a cracked dam, slow at first and then all at once, and it was not the clean rage of battle, which he knew and trusted. This was worse. This was personal.

*Without my consent.*

That was the heart of it. Not the death—gods died, men died, he'd made his peace with the arithmetic of sacrifice long ago. Not even the deception, though that was a knife in itself. It was that the god had looked at him—his servant, his sworn man, the one soul in Vaelmoor who had given the Burning Wolf twenty years of blood and obedience—and had seen not a person but a *resource*. A door. A piece on a board. Had decided, alone, unilaterally, in the dark above a sleeping man's body, what Felix's life was for.

Twelve years. Twelve years of self-erasure, of selling luck-charms in outlawed cities, of burying his name and his past and his grief under small commerce and smaller lies. Twelve years of believing he'd been *left*, and the whole time he'd been *assigned*. Spent like currency by a god who couldn't even be bothered to tell him the price.

"You could have asked," Felix said to the dark. His voice came out ragged. "Do you understand that? You could have *woken me*. One sentence. One night. 'This is what it costs, little wolf. Will you carry it?' And I would have—I might have—" His fists clenched. "I would have said yes. That's the worst part. I would have said yes, and then it would have been *mine*, my choice, my sacrifice, and I could have lived inside it. Instead you made me a man who spent twelve years flinching from his own reflection."

The mark pulsed. Warm. Contrite. The presence was still there, thin as the last ring of a bell, and it was listening, and it was ashamed, and somehow that made it worse.

***There was no time,*** it offered.

"There's always time for the truth. You just knew the truth would cost you the argument." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know what the crown-thing showed me? Nothing yet. But it's going to. It eats memory, Sera says. First food, then leverage. And when it opens its ledger and shows me everything you hid from me—every decision you made over my head, every year you edited—it'll find the crack you left and drive straight into it. Your sacrifice bought twelve years, and you spent them building a weapon with a fault line down the middle. *Me.*"

***I built a man who survives,***

"You built a man who *endures*," Felix snapped. "There's a difference, and you'd know it if you'd ever been one."

Silence. The water lapped. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled over a sky with nothing in it.

Then, softly, the presence did something it had never done. It stopped defending itself.

***Yes,*** it said. ***You are right. I chose for you, because choosing with you risked your refusal, and I could not bear the arithmetic of your refusal. That was not faith, little wolf. That was fear. I was dying and I was afraid, and I spent you the way frightened men spend everything—they spend it fast, and they don't look at the cost.***

***I am sorry.***

Felix knelt in the dark with the apology sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone, and discovered that forgiveness was not available to him tonight. Maybe not for a long time. Grief needed somewhere to go first, and it had twelve years of backlog.

"What's left of you?" he asked finally. "Honestly. If I take the hunger into myself—if that's what the rite needs—do you come back? Is that the plan? Was that always the plan?"

A long pause. Longer than the others. When the answer came, it came slowly, each fragment placed like a man laying down cards he knows are losing.

***What remains of me is this mark, and this moment, and perhaps an hour more of speaking. I am not coming back, little wolf. Whatever happens in the capital, I am a spent flame. What I bought is all I am now.***

***But hear me. The hunger cannot abide a willing host. It has only ever taken. If you give yourself to it freely—not in surrender, but in *defiance*, holding your name like a blade—your refusal will burn it from the inside. That is the door I built. Not my return. Yours.***

***I did not save you, Felix Hartwell. I only bought you time to save yourself. Whether you forgive me—that is yours to keep. I release all claim on it.***

The presence began to thin. Felix felt it going, felt the twelve-years silence reassembling around the mark like ice closing over water, and one last thing came through, small and human-sized, before the dark:

***The child. Protect the child. She remembers what it must forget. She is the other half of the door.***

Then nothing. Just the mark, warm and mute, and the water, and the dark.

* * *

He climbed back up to the rood loft dripping and hollowed out. Sera took one look at him and thrust a blanket at him without a word, and he wrapped it around his shoulders and sat down hard beside Wren's sleeping shape, and for a while nobody spoke.

"Well?" Sera said at last.

"He bargained," Felix said. "Twelve years ago. He let the thing eat him on purpose—slowly—to buy time. Time for a herald. For me." He stared at the dead saints in their drowned windows. "He chose for me. Decided what my life was for, wrote the terms, signed my name to it, and never told me. And now he's gone, really gone, and all he left me is an apology and a job."

Sera was quiet. Then: "My guild masters did the same. Signed my name to the failure at Harrow Ford. Made me the disgraced one so the loom could keep its reputation." Her jaw tightened. "People with power love to call their cowardice *sacrifice* when someone else is paying it."

"Yes," Felix said. "Exactly that."

"But?" She'd learned to hear the word he didn't say.

"But he also gave us the truth about the rite." Felix flexed his marked wrist. "The hunger can't abide a willing host. If I take it in freely and hold my name—hold *myself*—it burns. That's the door. That's how we sever the king." He looked at Wren, small and breathing in the lamplight. "And he said she's the other half of it. She remembers what it must forget. Whatever that means, it means we can't go around the capital anymore. We go through. Soon."

Sera followed his gaze to the girl. Something moved behind her eyes—fear, and tenderness, and the terrible mathematics already running.

"Through the front gate," she said. "Past the hunters. Past the court. Into the throne room of a thing that eats gods." She exhaled. "Pilgrims, then. There's a starless vigil at the palace in four days. Every faithful soul in the city will be crowding the gates to mourn the stolen sky."

Felix looked at her. "You want to walk into the lion's mouth dressed as mourners."

"I want to walk in dressed as *anything they don't shoot*." She picked up her half-carved stylus. "Four days, Felix. We'd better teach Wren to pray."

Above them, through the broken roof, the starless dark pressed down like a lid. And in Felix's wrist, gray and quiet and final, the last of his god kept its silence—but for the first time in twelve years, the silence felt less like abandonment, and more like a hand on his shoulder, pointing forward.

He hated that he found comfort in it.

He suspected that was exactly the kind of man he was.
