## 11. The Court of Hungry Light

The pilgrims' road into Corvane was paved with good intentions and worse mortar, and Felix walked it in a borrowed robe that smelled of someone else's sweat and incense.

"Stop fidgeting," Sera murmured beside him. She wore the gray hood of a ward-pilgrim, hands folded in the attitude of prayer she'd practiced until it looked natural. Under the sleeve, her scarred fingers twitched with the habit of work they weren't allowed to do.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're counting exits."

He was. Four visible from where they stood in the queue: the great western gate, two posterns, and a servants' stair he'd spotted because the guards ignored it, which meant it wasn't ignored at all. "Habit."

"Your habits are going to get us killed." But there was no heat in it. Sera had stopped pretending she didn't rely on his habits somewhere outside Ashfall, around the time Wren had fallen asleep against his shoulder for the third night running and neither of them had moved to wake her.

Wren walked between them now, small hand tucked in Felix's, wearing the plain smock of a pilgrim's child. Her eyes were fixed on the palace.

Everyone's eyes were fixed on the palace.

Corvane had turned out for the Vigil of the Hollow Crown—the one feast day the new king had added to the calendar himself, though no one seemed to find that strange. Ten thousand people packed the processional way, holding candles that gave off light but no warmth, singing hymns Felix didn't know in a key that made his back teeth ache. The palace rose above them all, white as a tooth, its towers crowned with iron shapes that might have been decorations or might have been cages.

"Look," Wren said. Not to him—to the sky.

There was nothing to look at. That was the point. The stars had been gone for weeks now, and the city had stopped noticing, which frightened Felix more than grief would have. In Vaelmoor they'd torn their hair and burned effigies. Here they sang about how lovely the dark was.

* * *

They got through the gates because pilgrims were waved through—because the guards checked faces and found what they expected to find, which was devotion. Felix kept his wrist wrapped in pilgrim bandages, the mark beneath it quiet but present, like a coal under ash. Twice a guard's gaze lingered on him, and twice the man's eyes slid away with the smooth disinterest of someone who had decided, in advance, that everything here was fine.

Inside the outer court, the vigil proper had begun. Thousands of candles. A choir of children in white. And at the far end, on a dais beneath an empty throne canopy, the king's courtiers knelt in ranks so perfect they looked poured rather than placed.

"That's wrong," Sera breathed.

"What?"

"Their breathing." She nodded at the kneeling courtiers. "Watch. They breathe together. All of them. Same rhythm."

Felix watched. She was right. A hundred chests rising and falling as one, a hundred hearts keeping a single time, and above them the king sat in shadow with his starless eyes and did not appear to breathe at all.

"We need to get closer," he said.

"Why? We've seen enough to confirm—"

"No." His voice came out lower than he intended. "You don't understand. I've stood in sieges. I've seen what possession looks like—what a demon does to a host, what a haunting does to a house. It fights. The host fights. There's always a war behind the eyes." He looked at the serene, candlelit faces. "There's no war here. They're not prisoners. They're *comfortable*."

* * *

They moved through the palace on pilgrim privilege—a guide in silver livery led groups of the devout through the public galleries, murmuring the history of each hall in a voice like warm oil. Felix drifted at the edge of his group, counting doors, marking guard rotations, doing the mercenary's arithmetic he'd sworn off twelve years ago and found, to his shame, still fluent.

It was in the third gallery that he saw the nursery.

It was a beautiful room—sun-frescoed, toy-strewn, built for joy—and it was empty. Not abandoned-empty. *Maintained*-empty. Fresh rushes on the floor. Toys dusted and arranged. A cradle with clean linens, waiting.

A woman in fine dress stood at the window, gazing out at nothing.

"Pardon me," Felix said, in his best pilgrim's accent. "Whose children play here?"

She turned and smiled at him, and it was a lovely smile, warm as the candles, and it reached all the way to her eyes, which were perfectly, terribly blank.

"Children?" she said pleasantly.

"The ones who use this nursery. The royal children. The court's children."

The smile held. Behind it, something worked—not resistance, not suspicion, just a gentle, bottomless absence, like asking a man to remember a room he'd never entered. "What a charming idea," she said. "A place for children. I don't believe anyone has used this room in years. Such a waste of a lovely space."

Felix looked at the toys. At the small wooden wolf with its paint worn away by small hands. At the height-marks carved into the doorframe—*Alenna, age six. Tam, age four*—and the fresh varnish someone had applied over them without reading them.

"You have children," he said quietly. "You must. Look at the doorframe."

She glanced at it. Something flickered—deep down, deep down, a fish turning under ice—and then the smile smoothed it away. "How odd," she said fondly. "Someone must have been very bored."

She wandered back to the window. Felix stood in the doorway of the beautiful empty room and felt the floor tilt under his certainties.

*Sera,* he thought, and found her three galleries later, white-faced, gripping a pillar.

"You saw," she said.

"The nursery. You?"

"A man in the treasury. He showed me the tax rolls with pride. Every family in the capital pays a child's tithe—has for years, he said, since the king's gracious renewal. And when I asked where the tithed children went, he smiled at me—" Her voice cracked. "He smiled at me like the money was the child. Like the ledger entry was the boy. He couldn't remember his own daughter's face, Sera. He remembered her *assessed value*."

"It eats memory first," Felix said. The words came out flat, and he hated how steady they sounded. "I've heard of things that eat flesh, eat souls, eat magic. This is worse. It doesn't take what you'd miss. It takes what you wouldn't notice missing until it's gone. Then it leaves the shape of your life standing, furnished and tidy, and moves in."

"And everyone's happy." Sera's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Gods, Felix, do you see it? That's the horror of it. No one here is suffering. The courtiers glow. The servants sing. The whole city is *content*. It's not devouring them—it's *digesting* them, slowly, painlessly, and they're grateful for the meal."

Below them, through the gallery windows, the vigil continued. Ten thousand candles. Ten thousand upturned, peaceful faces. The choir of children sang a hymn about the blessed dark, and Felix realized with a lurch that none of the singers could be older than ten—that every child on that stage had been born after the silence, raised in a sky with no stars, taught that the dark was holy.

Except one child knew otherwise.

He turned. "Where's Wren?"

Sera went rigid. "She was right behind me. At the reliquary display. She was looking at—"

They ran.

* * *

They found her in the Star Gallery, which was the palace's cruelest joke: a vaulted hall painted, generations ago, with every constellation of the old sky, gold leaf on midnight blue. Before the theft, it must have been magnificent. Now it was the only stars left in Vaelmoor, and the crown-thing had turned it into a shrine to their absence—candles extinguished, drapes hung, the painted heavens curtained off section by section as the vigil progressed, as if the dark needed help being complete.

Wren stood alone in the middle of the floor, staring straight up.

Around her neck, on a cord, hung a scrap of parchment she'd taken—or been given; Felix never learned which—from the reliquary. On it, in a dead priest's hand, was a drawing of a constellation. One of the old ones. The Burning Wolf.

She was pointing at the ceiling, at the painted original, and her mouth was moving.

Felix crossed the floor fast, Sera flanking, scanning for guards—but the gallery was empty, sealed for the vigil, and Wren was safe, was fine, was—

Was speaking.

Not words. Names. Her voice, rusty from months of silence, working through the old names one by one, her finger tracing the painted sky: "*Vessa's Loom. The Pale Ledger. The Drowned Chapel. The Burning Wolf.*" Each name landed in the silent hall like a stone dropped down a well.

And the paintings began to glow.

Faintly. Faint as embers under ash. But gold leaf that had hung dark for weeks kindled where her finger passed, and Felix felt the mark on his wrist wake—not burning, not pulling, just *attending*, the way a dog lifts its head.

"Wren." He knelt beside her, gently took her pointing hand. "Sweetheart. Listen to me. What are you doing?"

She looked at him with those eyes that remembered the sky, and for the first time since he'd known her, she looked afraid—not of him, not of the hunters, but of something she was only now understanding.

"They're trying to forget it," she whispered. "All of them. It makes them forget. But the pictures can't forget. Pictures remember." Her small hand closed around the parchment. "If nobody remembers the names, the dark wins forever. So somebody has to say them."

Above them, down the length of the gallery, the last uncurtained stretch of painted heaven began, very softly, to dim—as if something vast and patient had noticed a candle it had missed.

Felix got Wren's hand in his, Sera at his shoulder, and turned for the door.

It opened before they reached it.

A chamberlain in silver stood there, flanked by six guards whose eyes held the same warm, bottomless contentment as the nursery woman's smile. The chamberlain bowed, gracious as a host at his own table.

"The king," he said pleasantly, "would like to meet the little girl who remembers."

Behind Felix, very softly, the painted Burning Wolf went dark.
