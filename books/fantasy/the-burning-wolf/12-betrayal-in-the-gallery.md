## 12. Betrayal in the Gallery

The insider's name was Corvane, and Sera had trusted him for exactly as long as it took to learn his face.

That had been three days. Three days of whispered meetings in the palace undercroft, where the wine cellars ran deep enough to swallow voices and the servants' priest—a soft-bellied man with ink-stained fingers—had pressed a map of the vigil route into her hands like he was handing over his firstborn.

"The Gallery of Hours," he'd said, tapping the map with one stained fingertip. "During the vigil, the court processes through it at midnight. All of them. The king walks last, alone. If you mean to reach him, that corridor is where he is most himself."

"Most himself," Felix had repeated flatly. "Meaning least eaten."

"Meaning the crown has to concentrate to hold him together in front of witnesses." Corvane had smiled then, a small apologetic thing. "Even gods have to work."

Sera should have known better. She'd told herself she did know better—that trust was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, back when the Wardens' Guild stripped her name from its rolls and her own master testified against her with a straight face and steady hands. She had built her whole life since on load-bearing stone: wards, geometry, mathematics. Things that held because they could not do otherwise.

But Corvane had wept when he spoke of the children. That was the thing. He'd described how the courtiers forgot them—not abandoned, *forgot*, the way you forget a dream by noon—and how the nursery wing had gone silent as a tomb, and how he'd found his own daughter's name written in his own hand in a journal he had no memory of keeping. He'd wept, and Sera had watched the tears come, and some rusted hinge inside her had swung open just wide enough.

Now she stood in the gallery's shadowed mouth and understood that the tears had been rehearsal.

* * *

They came in as pilgrims, which was almost funny. The vigil drew thousands of the faithful every year—or had, before the stars went out—and the palace guards barely glanced at their rough-spun cloaks and ash-marked foreheads. Grief made everyone look alike. Sera kept Wren between herself and Felix, one hand resting on the girl's shoulder the way you'd steady a lantern in wind.

Wren hadn't spoken since the quarry. Not since Felix's magic had answered him too easily, too hungrily, and the hunters' blood had soaked into flooded stone. But she watched everything. She watched now, her small hand finding Sera's fingers and gripping hard enough to hurt.

The Gallery of Hours stretched before them like a throat. It was a corridor built to be walked slowly—vaulted, windowless, lit by a hundred oil lamps whose flames burned perfectly still, as if the air itself had been instructed not to move. Along both walls ran the hours of an old king's reign, carved in relief: coronation, harvest, war, wedding, funeral. Sera counted doorways as they moved. Service doors behind the third hour. The antechamber beyond the ninth. And at the far end, past the twelfth—the throne room doors, tall enough for something larger than a king to walk through.

"Positions," Felix murmured. "We wait for the procession. When the court passes, the gallery empties. We take the service door, circle ahead, and meet the throne room from the antechamber while the vigil still holds them."

"And if it doesn't empty?" Sera asked.

Felix didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They found their alcove behind the carving of the seventh hour—a harvest scene, wheat-stalked kings offering sheaves to a god whose face had been chiseled off centuries ago. Sera settled into the shadow with her back against cold stone and let her hands do what they always did when her mind wanted to scream: they mapped. Seam lines in the masonry. Load paths in the vaulting. The resonant frequency of the floor tiles, which she could feel faintly through her boots, humming with the same wrongness that hummed through all of Vaelmoor now—the city eating its own magic to feed the thing upstairs.

Wren leaned into her side. After a moment, the girl took Sera's hand and traced something on her palm.

A circle. A line through it. A second circle, broken.

Sera knew those shapes. Every ward-smith knew them, though most had never seen them drawn in sequence. It was the opening figure of the unbinding rite—the one Sera had been reconstructing from memory and scar tissue for two weeks, the one that required a bearer willing to take the hunger into himself. She looked down at Wren, and Wren looked up at her with eyes that held more sky than any child's eyes should.

"You've seen this before," Sera breathed. "Haven't you. In there." She nodded upward, toward the nursery wing, toward whatever the king's household had become. "You saw it somewhere."

Wren didn't nod. But she didn't look away either, and after a long moment she traced the figures again, slower, adding a fourth mark Sera didn't recognize—a spiral, tight as a wound spring.

Then the lamps went out.

Not all at once. They guttered in sequence, from the gallery's far end toward the near, like a wave rolling through black water, and in the sudden dark Sera heard the procession begin: hundreds of feet, shuffling, synchronized, and beneath them a sound like slow breathing that came from no throat at all.

"They're early," Felix whispered. "Vigil doesn't start until midnight. It's not even—"

He stopped. Because the feet weren't passing them. They were stopping.

Lamps relit, one by one, and by their light Sera saw the court of Vaelmoor standing in the gallery—not processing, but arranged. Facing the walls. Facing the carvings. Faces tilted up toward the hour-scenes with expressions of gentle, vacant devotion, like children watching a puppet show only they could see. And between them, moving down the line, touching each courtier's shoulder in turn, walked a man in servant's livery with ink-stained fingers.

Corvane.

He wasn't looking at the courtiers. He was looking at the alcoves. Counting them.

"No," Sera said, very quietly. "No, no—"

"He sold us," Felix said. His voice had gone flat and cold, the mercenary's voice, the voice Sera had learned meant violence was one breath away. "The whole story about his daughter. The map. The route. All of it bait."

"Why?" But even as she asked it, Sera understood. She looked at the courtiers' upturned faces, at the perfect stillness of the lamp flames, at the breathing sound underneath everything, and she understood. "Because they needed us to come *inside*. The vigil isn't a deadline, Felix. It's a trap with a schedule."

The guards came from both ends of the gallery at once. Not running—walking, unhurried, the way men move when resistance has already been subtracted from the equation. Twelve of them. Their eyes were normal. That was somehow worse than if they'd been voids. These were men who still had their own faces, their own names, who would remember tomorrow exactly what they did tonight and feel nothing about it, because the part of them that felt things had been filed away somewhere Sera couldn't reach.

"Felix." She grabbed his arm. "The service door. Behind the third hour—we can still—"

"Wren," Felix said.

Sera turned.

The girl was gone from her side.

For one horrible second Sera's mind simply refused the fact—refused the absence of the small warm weight, refused the open dark of the gallery where a seven-year-old could vanish. Then she saw the guards near the ninth hour closing inward, not on her, not on Felix, but on a point between them, and she saw a small figure in a pilgrim's cloak being lifted off her feet by a man old enough to be her grandfather, and she heard Wren make a sound Sera had never heard her make.

It wasn't a scream. It was worse. It was the sound of someone who had decided screaming wouldn't help, which meant she'd learned that lesson somewhere, recently, from someone.

"*No!*" Sera was moving before thought caught up, ward-smith's hands already weaving the first shapes of a binding pattern, raw force gathering in her palms like heat before flame. A guard stepped into her path and she hit him with a concussive burst that sent him sprawling into the harvest carving, wheat-sheaves cracking, plaster raining down. Another grabbed her cloak; she burned the fabric off her own shoulders to keep moving.

"Sera!" Felix was fighting too, and she heard the wrongness in the sounds he made—not the clean percussion of fists and steel but something deeper, hungrier, the sound of twelve forbidden years answering all at once. "The door! Get to the *door*, I'll—"

She got within ten feet of Wren. She got within ten feet, close enough to see the tear tracks on the girl's face, close enough to see that Wren had stopped struggling and gone rigid instead, her lips moving silently, tracing shapes—*the spiral, the wound-spring spiral*—and then the lamps above the ninth hour flared white, and the world folded.

There was no other word for it. Space bent the way light bends through water, and the ten feet between Sera and the child became a hundred yards, and the guards became statues, and Corvane stood directly in front of her with his soft belly and his ink-stained fingers and his dry, dry eyes.

"I'm sorry about the tears," he said. "I practiced them in a mirror. I'm told they were convincing."

"What are you?" Sera snarled. She threw a binding at him. It broke against his skin like surf on a seawall.

"A bookkeeper." He said it without pride or shame, the way you'd say *tall* or *left-handed*. "The Pale Ledger keeps accounts. Your friends the witch-hunters write in it. So does the crown. I balance." He glanced past her, at Felix, who was frozen mid-stride with fury carved into every line of him. "The mercenary was never the prize, ward-smith. He's the blade. Blades come to the hand eventually—they always do. But the child—" his voice softened into something that might have been reverence in a better man, "—the child is dessert. Do you know what the king eats first? Memory. And do you know what a child is? A memory that hasn't finished making itself. She remembers the old sky. She remembers *names*. When the crown finishes the vigil tonight, it will drink the last of this world's light, and it wants something sweet for the occasion."

Sera lunged. The world folded again, and she was on her knees at the gallery's entrance, and the doors at the far end were closing on a last image: Wren's small body carried over a guard's shoulder, her hand extended backward, fingers spread, tracing one final shape in the air.

The spiral. Tighter than before. Unwinding.

Then the doors met, and the sound was final as a coffin lid.

* * *

They put Felix and Sera in cells below the palace, which surprised her until she thought about it. The crown-thing didn't kill them. Killing them was subtraction, and subtraction wasn't in its nature—it added, it consumed, it *kept*. Everything it ate, it kept. Somewhere in that vast hollow king, every star it had swallowed was still shining, every god still whispering, every forgotten child still laughing. It was less a devourer than a hoard with a hunger.

The cell was clean. That was the detail that broke something loose in her chest. Clean straw, a water jug, a bucket, a slot in the door for bread. They intended to keep her functional. Useful things stayed functional.

She sat against the wall in the dark and did the mathematics, because mathematics was all she had left, and because the alternative was thinking about Wren's hand reaching backward over a guard's shoulder, and she could not think about that yet. Thinking about that yet would stop her hands from working forever.

Inventory: no tools. No chalk, no iron, no silver wire. Her ward-kit confiscated, naturally—but they'd missed the needles in her boot seam, three slivers of bone no longer than a fingernail, because they'd searched for weapons and bone needles were tools, and the difference mattered only to a ward-smith. Inventory: Felix in the next cell. She could hear him breathing. Occasionally she heard him speaking low and rapid, and she chose not to wonder to whom.

Inventory: time. The vigil began at midnight. Corvane had said the crown would finish drinking the light *tonight*. Which meant she had hours, not days, to reconstruct an unbinding rite she'd only ever seen performed once, by a dying master, in a language the Guild had outlawed, using a bearer—

Using Felix. Who would have to take the crown-thing's hunger into his own body. Who had spent twelve years starving himself of power precisely so nothing could ever use him again, and whom she would have to ask, in whatever words remained to her, to unstarve himself completely.

Her scarred hands lay open in her lap. She turned them over. The burns, the lattice of old cuts where wire had bitten deep during a decade of illegal craft, the callus ridges from twenty thousand hours of patient geometry. Love expressed as terrible mathematics, her master used to say, mocking her. You build the ward that costs what you cannot pay, and you pay it anyway, because the alternative is arithmetic done in other people's blood.

Somewhere above her, the palace began to sing. Hundreds of voices, the whole entranced court, rising through stone in a hymn with no words she recognized—a sound like comfort, like being held, like forgetting. That was the horror of it, she thought. The crown-thing didn't feel like evil from the inside. It felt like rest.

In the next cell, Felix's voice stopped.

"Sera." His voice came through the wall, low. "Are you awake?"

"Working," she said.

A pause. "On what?"

She looked at her hands. At the bone needles she'd already begun arranging in patterns on the straw, circles and broken circles, the opening figure, the shape Wren had traced into her palm twice—as if the girl had been trying to give her something. As if the child had known, even then, in the gallery, that this moment was coming.

"On how to get us out," Sera said. "And then on how to get *her* back. And then on how to cut a god out of a king without killing either one." She closed her fists around the needles. "So yes. Awake."

Through the wall, she heard Felix exhale—something that was half laugh, half grief.

"Good," he said. "Because I think my god just started talking again. And I don't trust what it's asking me to do."

Above them, the singing swelled, and the first hour of the vigil began.
