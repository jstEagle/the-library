## 14. The Unbinding Sigil

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Sera Ives had been awake for what her body insisted was two days and her mind refused to confirm.

She sat cross-legged on the floor with a bowl of gutter-water before her, and in the water's black mirror she drew the sigil with one scarred fingertip, over and over, because she could not risk chalk and could not risk light and could not risk being wrong even once more.

The water shivered each time her finger touched it. The sigil held for three heartbeats, then unraveled.

"Again," she whispered to herself, the way she'd whispered it in the ward-smiths' halls at Cael Duren, twenty years ago, when she'd been the youngest journeyman ever admitted and the only one who talked to her work. *Again.* It had been her whole religion then: the belief that anything broken could be mended if you were willing to break your hands on it.

She looked at her hands now. They were forty years old and they looked sixty, burn-scarred from the loom-accident that had ended her career, nicked and calloused from twelve years of hiding her craft in barns and back rooms, mending luck-charms for farmers while real wards rotted across Vaelmoor because the crown had outlawed them. The right index finger bent wrong where it had been broken by a witch-hunter's boot in Corvane. She flexed it anyway. Pain was just information. Her master had said that too, before the order fell, before everything.

*Anything broken can be mended.*

Except, she was beginning to understand, the unbinding sigil wasn't a mend. That was the lie she'd been telling herself since the quarry, since Felix had burned through forbidden magic like a man drinking poison because it tasted like home. An unbinding didn't repair. It *severed*. And every severance needed somewhere for the severed thing to go.

In the water, the sigil failed again.

*Again.*

* * *

They had been in the cells for six days.

Six days since the gallery, since Lord Aldous Merrin—Sera still could not say his name without spitting, though there was no one to hear it—had smiled his soft courtier's smile and signaled the guards with nothing more than a turn of his head. Six days since Wren had been carried away up the stairs, small and silent, her hand reaching back toward them until the door closed. Six days since Felix had let himself be taken rather than fight, because fighting meant dying here in the dark where it would accomplish nothing, and Felix Hartwell had never once in all the time she'd known him spent a life he couldn't spend well.

He was in the cell across the corridor. She could hear him breathing when the guards weren't dragging him out for questioning, which they did every day, and which he endured without giving them anything except, Sera suspected, exactly as much as he wanted them to have. He was very good at losing on purpose. It was almost the same skill as winning.

"Felix," she said now, quietly, to the dark between the bars. "I need to talk to you about the mathematics."

A pause. Then his voice, rough with exhaustion but steady, the way it always was, the way she'd once found infuriating and now found—she searched for the word and refused to find it—*necessary*. "You've been muttering numbers for two days. I assumed it was a prayer."

"It's not a prayer." She pulled herself up to the bars. In the faint gray seepage of light from some vent above, she could see the shape of him sitting against the far wall of his cell, wrists chained low, head tipped back. "It's arithmetic. Sit forward. I need you to listen."

He sat forward. He always listened. That was the other thing about him she'd stopped finding infuriating.

"The unbinding rite," she said. "The one that can sever a person from a possessing power. I learned its theory at Cael Duren. Nobody ever performed it. Do you know why?"

"Because it's forbidden?"

"Because it's *stupid*," she said, and heard the crack in her own voice and hated it. "Because the theory is elegant and the practice is monstrous. A possession isn't a nail you pull out, Felix. It's a root system. The crown-thing has been feeding on the king for years—it's threaded through his blood, his memory, his name. You can't cut it free without cutting him apart. Unless—" she made herself say it, made the words leave her mouth where they would become real, "—unless you give the severed thing somewhere else to go. A vessel. A bearer. Someone who takes the hunger into themselves as it comes out."

The silence from the other cell went on long enough that she counted his breaths. Four. Five. Six.

"You're saying someone has to hold it," he said finally. "The hunger. While the king comes free."

"I'm saying more than that." She pressed her forehead against the cold bar. This was the part she had turned over and over in the dark, looking for a flaw, praying for a flaw, and finding none. "The rite doesn't transfer the hunger like moving water between cups. The bearer doesn't hold it temporarily. The bearer *anchors* it. Permanently. Whatever goes into you stays in you, burning, until either it consumes you or—" her voice caught, steadied, "—or you starve it. And the only way anyone has ever starved it is refusal. Absolute, total, lifelong refusal to be what it wants you to be. Which means the rest of your life, Felix. However long that is. Every day a war. No relief. No rest. Not a cure. A sentence."

More silence. Then, horribly, gently: "And how long does the bearer usually last?"

She told him the truth, because lying to him had become impossible somewhere on the road through Ashfall, somewhere around the moment a mute child had spoken his true name and Sera had watched him come apart and reassemble himself around it. "The records mention three attempts. Two died during the rite itself. The third lived eleven days."

"And the king?"

"Lives. Whole. Human." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the mathematics, Felix. One life weighed against a kingdom. One man's remaining years against everyone the crown-thing hasn't eaten yet. Against Wren. Against the sky." She swallowed. "I've run it every way. There's no version where the cost gets smaller. There's no clever warding trick. I checked. Believe me, I checked."

"You're going to ask me anyway."

"No," Sera said. "I'm going to tell you, and then you're going to decide, and whatever you decide I'll finish building the rite, because that's my job. But I want you to know something first." She lifted her head. The gray light caught the scars on her hands as she gripped the bars. "I rebuilt this rite from memory in a dungeon because a child is sitting somewhere above us in a room full of stolen memories waiting to be dessert, and because a king is dying by inches in a chair, and because the sky is empty. Those are the reasons. Those are good reasons. They are *not* the reason I'm telling you first instead of just doing it."

"What's the reason?"

"Because I watched you use that magic in the quarry," she said, "and it answered you too fast, and you liked it, and you hated yourself for liking it, and I have spent twelve years watching people feed pieces of themselves to powers that flatter them. I will not build an altar and shove you onto it without asking whether you want to be a sacrifice or a man. So." Her throat was tight. "Do you want to be a sacrifice, Felix Hartwell? Or do you want to live?"

* * *

He asked for time. She gave him the night, which was all any of them had anymore—the vigil was coming; the whole palace above them hummed with preparation, servants scrubbing floors for a coronation-renewal that would end with the crown-thing finishing its meal of the sky.

Sera worked.

She worked by touch and memory, scratching the sigil's components into the floor of her cell with a loose stone, learning them blind the way a musician learns a piece in the dark. The unbinding sigil was not one mark but a lattice of nine, each one a severance of a different thread: name, memory, will, hunger, voice, shape, oath, blood, and the last one, the ninth, which the old texts called simply *the door*, and which no text explained because no text survived its use.

Her fingers remembered what her mind had tried to bury. That was the cruelty of craft: the hands kept their knowledge even after the knowledge ruined you. She traced the fifth mark—*voice*—and thought of Felix's god, twelve years silent, and wondered if the crown-thing had used this very lattice to eat it, and hated the elegance of that so much she had to stop and press her palms flat against the floor until the shaking passed.

Near midnight—or what she guessed was midnight; the stars were gone, and without stars midnight was just a rumor—a sound woke her from a half-doze she hadn't meant to fall into.

Felix was singing.

Very softly, barely more than breath, in a language she didn't know, a low looping melody like a tide going out. Through the bars she saw him sitting upright, eyes closed, chains slack, and on his wrist the dormant mark glowed a faint ember-orange, pulsing in time with the song.

"What are you doing?" she whispered.

His eyes opened. They were calm in a way that frightened her more than panic would have. "Talking to what's left of my god. It lives in the mark. It's been trying to reach me for days—I couldn't hear it over everything else." He paused. "It knew about the rite. It says the third bearer, the one who lasted eleven days—it says he lasted eleven days because he fought the hunger. Fighting feeds it. It grows on resistance the way fire grows on wind."

Sera's stomach dropped. "Then how—"

"You don't fight it. You refuse it. Fighting is a relationship. Refusal is—" he searched for it. "Refusal is closing the account. Walking away from the table. It can't feed on a man who won't sit down to play." His mouth curved, tired and wry. "My god says I've had twelve years of practice refusing things."

"Felix." She gripped the bars. "Don't do this because it's the only move left. There's always another move. Give me another day, I can—"

"There isn't," he said simply. "Wren's upstairs. The vigil is tomorrow night. And Sera—" he leaned forward into the gray light, and she saw that his face was wet, and that he was smiling anyway, "—you asked if I wanted to be a sacrifice or a man. I've been a sacrifice my whole life. My order raised me for it. My god chose me for it. Everyone I've ever loved has needed me to be useful and I have never once, not one single time, been asked what I wanted." He wiped his face with his chained hands, matter-of-fact, like a man brushing off rain. "So here's what I want. I want the kid to see stars. I want you to get her out of this city and teach her ward-craft and yell at her when she's sloppy. I want the king to wake up human and have to live with what he did. And I want—" his voice frayed, held, "—I want to do this one thing as a choice. Not a duty. Not an oath. A choice. That's the only version of the mathematics I can live inside. Even if I don't live long inside it."

Sera discovered she was crying, which annoyed her intensely, and she let it happen anyway, because some things deserved tears more than dignity.

"Then I need your blood," she said, when she could speak. "For the anchor-mark. And your true name, spoken freely, given to the rite—not taken. The rite fails if the name is coerced."

"You have my name already. Wren said it."

"Wren said it. That's different. It has to be yours." She slid the loose stone under the bars, along with a strip of cloth torn from her shift. "Cut deep. The mark needs to drink."

He took the stone. Before he cut, he looked at her across the corridor, across six days of dark, and said, "When this is done—if it works—don't let them make you a hero. Heroes get statues. Statues get forgotten. Stay a smith. Build things."

"Build things," she agreed. "Now bleed."

He drew the stone across his palm, and held his fist through the bars, and she pressed the cloth to it and felt the blood soak through warm against her fingers, and beneath her hands the ninth sigil—the door—rose unbidden in her mind, complete at last, terrible and perfect, the whole lattice assembled now around a single willing name.

* * *

She finished the final component an hour before dawn, and it was then, kneeling over the completed lattice scratched into her cell floor, that she heard footsteps on the stairs—heavy, unhurried, wrong—and saw torchlight climb the wall below.

Not the daily guards. Too many. Too quiet.

The door at the stairhead opened, and Lord Merrin stood framed in it, flanked by six of the pale-ledger hunters, and behind them, small and barefoot and clutching a guard's sleeve, came Wren.

The girl's eyes found Sera through the bars. She didn't cry out. She didn't struggle. She lifted one small hand and, with one deliberate finger, tapped twice against her own wrist—twice, the signal they'd built on the road, the signal that meant *I know something you need*—and then her gaze flicked, quick as a bird, toward the servants' passage door at the far end of the corridor, the one Sera had noticed the first day and dismissed as locked.

Merrin smiled his soft smile. "The king thanks you for your craftsmanship, ward-smith," he said. "The rite you've built. He'd like it finished. Tomorrow night. On the throne room floor. With your friend as the bearer—willingly, of course, or we'll begin with the child instead."

Sera rose slowly, hands open, heart hammering out the terrible arithmetic of the next twelve hours—and met Wren's eyes one more time, and saw there not fear but patience, the patience of someone who has read the last page.

"Yes," Sera said. "We'll cooperate."

And behind Merrin's shoulder, unnoticed, the servants' passage door drifted open an inch, and stayed open.
