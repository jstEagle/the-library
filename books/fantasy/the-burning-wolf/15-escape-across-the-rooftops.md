## 15. Escape Across the Rooftops

The cell had no door.

That was the first thing Felix understood, lying on cold stone with his wrists still bound in pale ledger-ribbon: there was no door because nothing down here was ever meant to leave. The crown-thing did not build cages. It built appetites with walls around them. The cells beneath the palace were shaped like hunger—curved inward, throat-like, every surface polished to a wet black shine that gave back no reflection at all. He had been looking at himself for an hour and seeing nothing. It was the most honest mirror he'd ever stood before.

The ribbon on his wrists was thin as paper and strong as oath-iron. Witch-hunters' work. The Pale Ledger wrote its bindings in a script that fed on consent, and somewhere between the flooded quarry and this place, Felix realized, he must have signed something without knowing it. A touch. A breath. A name spoken too casually over wine that tasted of ash.

He sat up. His head throbbed where they'd struck him, and deeper than that, something else throbbed—the mark on his wrist, gray and dead since the night his god went silent, now faintly warm. Faintly *hungry*.

Because the hunger was here. All around him. Soaked into the stone of the palace foundations like grease into old bread. Twelve years he'd carried a silenced god's charm on his skin, and in all that time he'd never felt it pull. Now, in the dark under the palace of Vaelmoor, it pulled like a fishhook set behind his navel, drawing him up and out and toward—

Toward the throne room. Toward the renewal vigil, three nights hence, when the crown would finish drinking the sky.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the mark, to the dark, to whatever fragment of his god still smoldered in the gray lines. "Not yet."

But even as he said it, he understood what the pull actually was. Not a summons. An instruction manual.

The hunger wanted him. That was the whole of chapter thirteen's bargain, offered in the dark by a voice wearing his own memories like stolen clothes: *walk away, and I'll give you back your god's voice.* The thing below the crown had marked him as desirable. And desire, Sera had once told him over a dying ward-stone, drunk on cheap plum wine and grief, *is a vector. Everything that wants has a direction. Everything with a direction can be steered.*

Felix pressed his bound wrists against the black wall and let the pull run through him.

It surged. Of course it did. The hunger in the stones recognized the hunger-shaped vacancy in Felix Hartwell—a man who'd spent twelve years hollowing himself out, scraping away his name, his past, his face, leaving exactly the kind of empty space that appetite loves to fill. The pull strengthened, and the ribbon on his wrists drank at his consent, waiting for him to want it, and he did want it, gods help him, he wanted it so badly his teeth ached—

He turned the wanting sideways.

Not toward the throne room. Toward the wall. Toward *out*. He took the whole vast tidal drag of the crown-thing's appetite and aimed it like a man aiming a battering ram, and the pale ribbon, which fed on consent, got exactly the consent it was written for: *yes, I want to go through this wall.*

The ledger-script flared white, overwhelmed, and tore itself apart trying to digest a wish bigger than its binding. The ribbon crumbled to ash. The wall—polished hunger-stone, throat-flesh, whatever it was—cracked like river ice in spring.

Felix stepped through into a corridor full of screaming torchlight and ran.

* * *

The palace above him was holding a rehearsal for the end of the world.

He came up through a servant stair into a gallery hung with starless banners—black silk where the constellations of Vaelmoor should have been embroidered, each one replaced with a single stitched eye—and the court moved through the corridors around him like sleepwalkers in silk. He passed two ladies-in-waiting standing at a window, gazing up at a sky with nothing in it, smiling. He heard one say to the other, *"Isn't it lovely, how quiet it's become,"* and the other said, *"I'd forgotten we used to have children,"* and they laughed together, softly, and walked on.

Felix kept his hood up and his eyes down and ran.

The mark pulled at him constantly now, a leash with slack in it, tugging throne-ward. He let it tug just enough to look natural to anything watching—because something *was* watching; he could feel the attention of the crown spread through the palace like oil through water—and meanwhile he mapped. Twelve years as a mercenary teaches you to read a building the way a ward-smith reads stone: where the weight sits, where the exits lie, where the guards are fat and where they're lean. The palace of Vaelmoor had been built across four reigns, and the oldest part—the part nobody renovated because nobody remembered it existed—climbed the eastern face in a warren of roof-decks and bell-towers and abandoned astrologers' platforms, all of them useless now, all of them *empty*, because who needs stars when you've eaten them?

He reached the window at the gallery's end, shouldered the shutters open, and stepped out onto the slates of Vaelmoor's night.

No moon. No stars. Just the city's lamps below, orange and few and getting fewer, and above him a darkness so total it had texture, like velvet laid over the world. The wind coming off that darkness smelled of nothing at all. That was the worst part. Wind should smell of rain or dust or distance. This wind smelled of *consumed*.

Behind him, inside, a bell began to ring—not the alarm pattern, not yet, but the low triple toll that meant *the king is displeased.* Somewhere below, doors started opening.

Felix ran along the rooftop spine of the east wing, boots finding the old lead flashing by feel, and allowed himself—one breath, only one—to feel it.

Gods, it felt *good.*

That was the shame of it, the thing he'd carry like a stone in his boot for the rest of his life: running felt good. The cold air, the drop on either side, the city spread out beneath him like embers in a grate, hunters behind and death ahead and his body remembering everything it had ever known about survival—it was the first time in twelve years he'd felt entirely awake. In the safehouse years, in the charm-shop years, in all the small gray days of hiding, he had been a man walking through his own life with the sound turned off. Now the sound was on, all of it, terrifying and bright, and some animal part of him that predated his oaths and his grief and his god wanted to laugh out loud.

He didn't laugh. But he ran faster.

* * *

They came up onto the roofs ninety seconds behind him, and they were better than he'd hoped, which meant worse than he'd feared.

Witch-hunters moved like their ledger-books: flat, precise, economical. Four shapes in pale coats flowing over the ridgeline of the west tower, spreading into a hunting fan without a word spoken between them. And behind them, worse, came the guards—not possessed in the shambling way of stories, but *smoothed*, their faces worn featureless as river stones, moving with the terrible coordination of fingers on one hand. Six of them. They didn't call out. The crown didn't waste breath.

Felix put a chimney between himself and them and thought, fast.

The hunters would have more ledger-ribbon. Ribbon needed consent. Fine—he'd spend the rest of the night consenting to nothing, wanting nothing, a closed fist of a man. But the guards answered to the crown's attention, and the crown's attention was already on him, had been on him since the cells, because the crown *wanted* him, and wanting, Sera's voice reminded him from a year and a lifetime ago, is a vector.

So he stopped running.

He stood on the ridge of the astrologers' platform, arms loose, and opened himself to the pull—the great slow tidal drag toward the throne room, the hunger's leash on his wrist-mark—and instead of resisting it, he *rode* it. He let the crown feel his surrender. Let it taste a man breaking, running true to its summons, coming home to be devoured. The attention on him sharpened, eager, greedy, and the guards adjusted course to intercept him along the pull-line, exactly where the hunger expected him to be.

Which was not where he was going to be.

At the last moment—when the six smoothed faces were ten feet away and closing, when the nearest hunter's hand was already unspooling pale ribbon—Felix wrenched the vector sideways again. Dropped off the platform's north edge, caught the gutter, swung, and let go over a four-story gap that no sane man crosses. The hunger's own momentum carried the guards forward onto the platform; two of them went over the edge after him on pure borrowed inertia and hit the courtyard flagstones with sounds like dropped timber. The hunters checked at the gap, calculating, and lost him the length of one heartbeat.

One heartbeat was a currency Felix Hartwell had been spending wisely for twelve years.

He crossed the gap. Then the next one. Then a third, longer, over a street canyon where lamplight rose thin as incense smoke, and behind him the bells finally found the alarm pattern and the whole eastern roofline woke up with lanterns and shouting and the soft terrible whisper of ledger-ribbon unspooling in the dark.

* * *

He found her where he'd told her to be, three days ago, in the one conversation they'd risked through the wall of adjacent cells: the old bell-tower of Saint Verene-of-the-Loom, deconsecrated, roofless, its bell long since melted down for the crown's war. Sera crouched in the shadow of the empty bell-yoke with her scarred hands wrapped around a ward-stone the size of a fist, and when Felix dropped over the parapet she didn't startle—just looked up with those gray eyes that had seen too many looms break, and said:

"You're late."

"Had to sign myself out." He showed her the ash residue on his wrists. "Ledger-ribbon. It eats consent, so I fed it a lie big enough to choke on."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and died young. "Wren?"

"Gone. Taken to the king." His voice cracked on it and he hated that it cracked. "I'm getting her back."

"I know." Sera stood, joints cracking, and slung her tool-roll across her back. Below them, lanterns were converging on the tower base like moths finding a flame made of them. "And I know why you came back for me instead of running for the gates, which was the smart move, which you never make, which is why I keep you alive." She tossed him the ward-stone. He caught it. It hummed against his palm, faintly warm, faintly hungry—the first working ward he'd held in twelve years. "Rooftops to the river district. There's a drain, then a barge, then a very forgiving smuggler who owes me. We rebuild the sigil tonight. You go back tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Felix agreed. And then, because the exhilaration was still singing in his blood and honesty was the only prayer he had left: "Sera. Running just now—I liked it. Gods forgive me, I *liked* it."

She looked at him for a long moment, wind pulling at her cropped hair, the starless dark pressing down on both of them like a lid.

"Good," she said. "Means you've still got something left to lose. Come on."

They went out over the rooftops together, two shadows crossing a city with no sky, and behind them the bells rang and rang, and ahead—three nights off, at the end of the pull that never stopped tugging at his wrist—the throne room waited, and in it a child who remembered stars, and a king with empty eyes, and a hunger that had already begun to learn Felix Hartwell's name.
