## 16. What Wren Saw

The nursery had no children in it.

That was the first wrong thing Wren noticed, before the golden light, before the shelves of little glass bottles, before the woman in the gray dress who smiled too much. The room at the top of the palace was called a nursery—she'd heard the guards say it, heard the word come down the stairwell like a stone dropped into a well—and it had everything a nursery should have. A rocking horse painted white and gold. A crib with ribbons on its posts. Wooden blocks stacked in a tower no one had knocked over.

But there were no children. There was only Wren.

She sat on the floor where they'd put her, knees drawn up, and counted the wrong things because counting kept her quiet and being quiet kept her safe. One: no children. Two: the windows were painted shut, and painted over, so that the glass showed a sky that wasn't there—a blue sky with a fat yellow sun, cheerful as a lie. Three: the woman in gray never blinked when she thought Wren wasn't looking.

"You can play," the woman said. Her voice was warm the way milk left out is warm. "Anything you like."

Wren didn't move. She had learned, in the long road from the burned village to here, that stillness was a kind of armor. People forgot you if you were small enough and still enough. Mostly.

Except the man with the crown hadn't forgotten her. The man with the crown had looked straight through all his courtiers, through the witch-hunters who brought her up the stairs, through the woman in gray, and found her the way a hook finds a fish. *The star-child,* he'd said, and his voice had been two voices, one above the other, and the lower one had made her teeth ache. *Bring me the star-child.*

So she was not going to be forgotten. She was going to be eaten.

Wren looked at the blocks. She picked one up because refusing would be interesting, and interesting was dangerous, and she turned it over in her hands. A letter was carved on its face. She knew letters—the hedge-priest back home had taught her before the sky went out—but this letter did nothing. It was just wood.

Behind her, the woman in gray hummed.

Wren's eyes went to the shelves instead. Little bottles, hundreds of them, each stoppered with wax, each glowing faintly with a color that shifted when you weren't looking directly at it. And inside some of them—not all, but some—there were pictures. Not pictures like drawings. Pictures like remembering. A woman laughing in a kitchen. A boy running through wheat. A man holding a baby up toward a night sky full—

Full of stars.

Wren's breath caught. She crawled closer to the shelf, slow, the way you approach a bird, and put her face near one bottle. Inside, tiny as a seed, a memory turned over on itself: a night sky, dense with stars, more stars than she had ever seen, stars in shapes—*her* shapes, the shapes she drew in the dirt with sticks, the shapes no adult could name. The Hunter with his crooked bow. The Wolf That Burns. The Loom, and the Door, and the Long Mother walking her nine children across the dark.

They were real. They had been real. The sky had actually looked like that, once, and someone had stood under it and loved it enough that the loving had stayed, even here, even bottled.

"Those aren't for touching," the woman said, behind her. Still warm. Still smiling. Wren could hear the smile without turning around, and that was another wrong thing to add to her count.

Wren took her hand off the shelf. But she had seen what she needed to see, and seeing, for Wren, had always been the beginning of everything.

* * *

They had taken her voice years ago—or she had lost it; she couldn't remember which, and the not-remembering was its own kind of wound—and so the world came to her mostly through her eyes, and her eyes missed nothing. That was the trade, maybe. Maybe whatever ate her voice had left her this instead: the seeing.

She saw that the woman in gray left three times a day, always after the bell, always locking the door with a key on a ribbon around her neck.

She saw that the lock was old, and the door was older, and the frame had a crack running up one side where the wood had shrunk away from the iron.

She saw that the rocking horse's paint was fresh but its rockers were worn bare, as if something had rocked on it for a very long time, and nothing in this room had children in it.

And on the second day—because they fed her well and watched her constantly and did nothing else, waiting for something, letting her grow sweeter like fruit for a pie—on the second day she saw the king come into the nursery himself.

He didn't knock. The door simply opened and he was there, filling it, wearing robes the color of deep water and the crown that was not really a crown. Wren had thought, from a distance, that it was gold. Up close she saw it was not gold at all. It was dark, and it moved, slowly, the way oil moves on water, and it sat on the king's head the way a spider sits on a wrapped fly.

The king's eyes were holes. Not empty—holes. Things went on behind them, far away, like lights at the bottom of a well.

"There she is," said the upper voice, the king's voice, and it sounded tired and sad and human, and Wren understood suddenly that somewhere inside all that darkness a real man was still screaming. "There's my little star."

The lower voice said nothing. It didn't need to. The whole room leaned toward her slightly, the way grass leans toward wind.

The woman in gray curtsied. "She doesn't speak, Majesty. The hunters say she's mute. Damaged." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Perhaps the journey spoiled the flavor."

*Don't let them know what you are,* Felix had told her, back on the ash road, the only time he'd ever knelt to be at her eye level. He'd held her shoulders gently. *Whatever happens, don't show them the shapes. Promise me.* She had promised. She had crossed her heart the way Sera taught her, twice over and once for luck.

But the crown-thing was already looking at her, and looking, Wren discovered, worked both ways.

It didn't read her mind. It was worse than that. It *remembered* at her. The dark behind the king's eyes opened, and out of it poured memories that were not hers—memories of the sky, the true sky, the first sky, when the stars were not decorations but *doors*, when things walked between them, when the dark between stars was not empty but full, humming, alive. She saw a hunger older than kingdoms. She saw it fall. She saw it buried under the world's forgetting, starved for eons because no one remembered its name, because names are chains and memory is the hand that holds them.

And she saw why it had climbed up out of that burial, why it wore a king, why it drank magic and ate wards and swallowed stars one by one:

Because it was afraid.

The knowledge went into her like cold water into a dry cloth. The crown-thing, vast and ancient and terrible, was *afraid*. It devoured everything bright and named and remembered because every remembered thing was a mirror, and in every mirror it caught a glimpse of itself, and it could not bear what it saw. It could not bear *being known*. Forgetting was its food and its fortress both.

That was why it had taken the stars first. Stars are the oldest names we have.

The pressure lifted. The king swayed, caught himself on the doorframe with a hand that trembled—the real hand, the human hand—and for one heartbeat his ruined face was only a man's face, exhausted, drowning.

"I'm sorry," the king whispered, in the upper voice, to no one, to everyone, to the little girl on the floor. "I'm so sorry. Run, if you ever get the chance. Whatever it tells you—"

Then the dark closed over him again, and the crown-thing looked out, and it smiled with the king's mouth.

"What a clever child," it said in both voices. "You saw. You *saw*."

Wren looked down at her hands and did not move and did not make a sound, and hated, for the first time in her life, that she had no voice to scream with anyway.

* * *

That night, alone in the locked nursery, Wren did the bravest thing she had ever done.

She remembered.

Not quietly. Not the way she usually kept things, folded small and hidden. She lay in the crib with the ribbons—she was far too big for it, and that was another wrong thing, they wanted her small, they wanted her *babyish*, they wanted her young and sweet and unformed—and she reached into herself and pulled out every shape, every constellation, every star-name the hedge-priest and the road and her own strange heart had ever given her.

The Wolf That Burns, who runs ahead of winter.

The Loom, where the sky is woven and rewoven.

The Long Mother and her nine children.

The Door, which opens only from the other side.

And one more. The one she had never drawn, never shown Felix or Sera, the shape that lived deepest, the one she'd kept hidden even from herself because it frightened her: a great coiled thing beneath the roots of the sky, blind and hungry and *named*, named long ago by people who wove that name into the oldest constellations so it would never be forgotten, so it would always, always be *watched*.

The name rose in her like a bubble rising through deep water.

She couldn't say it. She had no voice. But she didn't need a voice, not entirely, not for this—because the mark on Felix's wrist had brushed her forehead once, on the ash road, when she'd been fevered, and some of it had stayed. A spark. A seed of a god that had given itself away, planted in a girl who remembered too much.

Wren pressed her finger to the floorboards and drew.

She drew the coiled thing. She drew its name around it, the old letters, the ones shaped like falling stars. And as she drew, the nursery changed.

The painted-over windows flickered. The false blue sky stuttered like a candle in wind. Every bottle on the shelves flared at once—all those stolen memories, all those bottled rememberings, waking, straining against their wax like moths. Somewhere below them, deep in the palace, something screamed. It was not a sound in the air. It was a sound in the *world*, in the grain of the walls, in the teeth of everyone sleeping, and it was the sound of an ancient thing being *seen*.

The crown-thing could not abide being remembered. And Wren had just remembered it completely, truly, down to its buried name—and written the remembering into the bones of the palace itself, where no amount of eating could reach it, because you cannot devour what has already been given away.

The door burst open. The woman in gray stood there, and her smile was gone, and behind her smile-gone face Wren saw something else: the woman was hollow. There was nothing behind her eyes at all anymore, just a puppet-shape full of borrowed light, and the borrowed light was flickering, panicking.

"*What did you do?*" the woman shrieked, in a voice that was three voices now, none of them hers. "*What did you—stop. STOP—*"

Wren was already moving.

She had planned it the way she planned everything: patiently, in silence, with her eyes. The crack in the doorframe. The key on the ribbon, which the woman, in her panic, had not thought to guard. The servants' passage behind the rocking horse—she'd found it on the first day, a seam in the paneling, because children see seams that adults have forgotten, and this palace had forgotten almost everything.

She snatched the key. The woman lunged, and Wren ducked under her arms—small, still, *quick*, all the armor she had ever needed—and wrenched the panel open and slipped sideways into the dark.

The passage swallowed her. Dust and narrow walls and the smell of old bread and older stone. Behind her the nursery door slammed and the woman's stolen voice howled her rage, and beneath the howl, everywhere, in the walls themselves, that other sound continued: the vast, wounded, furious keening of a thing that had just been *remembered* after ten thousand years of comfortable oblivion.

Wren ran. Barefoot, silent, small. Down the passage, past doors, following the one thing she trusted in this whole rotten palace: the pull in her chest, faint but steady, like a thread tied to her ribs. Felix's spark. Felix's god-remnant. It tugged her downward and outward, toward the drowned chapel by the river where the others were hiding, toward the scarred ward-smith and the marked man who carried a dead god's last gift.

They would need what she carried. She understood that now with perfect clarity. The rite Sera meant to perform, the unbinding—it would fail without a name. You cannot cut loose what you cannot name. And Wren was the only soul in Vaelmoor who knew the crown-thing's true name, who had carried it out of the dark behind the king's eyes and written it into the palace walls where it could never be eaten.

Her feet knew the way. Her heart hammered. Above her, through the floors, she felt the palace begin to wake—guards shouting, bells starting, the whole hive stirring because a little girl had done the one thing the monster could not survive.

At a junction in the passage, a grate opened onto moonless night air and the black river below, and beyond the river, hunched and half-sunken among the reeds, the chapel.

Wren paused, one hand on the cold grate, and looked back once at the palace looming against the starless sky. Its highest window glowed with angry golden light. Something up there was looking for her. Something ancient was afraid of her.

Good, she thought, with all the fierce, wordless courage of the small.

She squeezed through the grate and began to climb down.
