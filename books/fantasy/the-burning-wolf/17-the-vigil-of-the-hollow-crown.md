## 17. The Vigil of the Hollow Crown

The chapel water had gone still by the time Felix made his decision, which felt like an omen, though he'd stopped trusting omens around the same time he'd stopped trusting gods.

He sat on the flooded steps with his boots in the shallows and the mark burning on his wrist like a coal someone had forgotten to bury. Across the nave, Sera worked by candlelight, her scarred hands moving through the unbinding rite one more time—chalk lines on wet stone, a circle of iron nails, a spool of silver thread she'd unraveled from her own ward-smith's badge. Wren slept against a fallen pew, curled small, her fingers twitching through constellations even in her dreams.

"You're doing it again," Sera said without looking up.

"Doing what."

"Sitting there deciding something terrible and calling it thinking." She tied off a knot, blew chalk dust from her palms. "I can hear your jaw working from here."

Felix almost smiled. Almost. "The vigil's tonight. At moonrise. The whole court will be in the great hall for the renewal—the crown-thing will be at its most open, its most *hungry*. If we wait for another night, there might not be another night. It finishes eating the sky, Sera, and then there's no rite in the world that pulls a king back out of something that size."

"I know the arithmetic." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were flat and gray as slate in the candlelight. "I've known it since the cells. What I don't know is why you're talking about *we* when you've clearly already decided to go alone."

The water lapped at the steps. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and flood, the city of Vaelmoor was preparing for its coronation-renewal—bells that didn't ring so much as hum, a sound like a throat clearing before swallowing. He'd heard it all day. Every hour it grew a little louder, a little more satisfied.

"Wren has to stay here," Felix said. "Whatever happens, she stays here with the rite memorized. If I don't come back—"

"*When* you don't come back—"

"—she takes the true name to the restored order's survivors in the east. There are still brothers who kept the quiet oath. She remembers stars no one else does. That makes her the most important person alive, and I'll drown this chapel before I let that thing use her as dessert twice."

Sera set down her thread. Slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything, she stood and waded across the nave until she stood over him, and for a moment Felix thought she was going to strike him. Instead she crouched, took his marked wrist in both hands, and turned it toward the light.

The sigil had changed. He'd been avoiding looking at it for two days, but there was no avoiding it now: the unbinding pattern Sera had inked into his skin was no longer hers. It had grown. Threads of black-gold light ran beneath the old lines like veins under skin, and where her careful geometry had been, something older and hungrier had redrawn itself—a shape that hurt to follow with the eye, a spiral that seemed to tighten the longer he looked.

"It's ready," Sera said quietly. "That's what I've been trying not to tell you. The sigil isn't waiting on my hands anymore. It's waiting on yours. On your *yes*." Her grip tightened. "And once you say yes, once you take that thing's hunger into yourself, there's no version of this where you walk out of the throne room whole. You understand that? I built the mathematics, Felix. I know what the numbers say."

"I know what they say."

"Then say it out loud. Say what you're planning, because if you make me guess I'll assume the stupidest possible version, and I'll be right."

Felix looked at his wrist. Twelve years ago, a god had pressed its last charm into his skin and gone silent, and he had spent those twelve years pretending the silence was peace. Selling luck-charms to dockworkers. Keeping his head down. Calling survival a life.

"The crown-thing eats," he said. "That's all it is—that's the whole of it. It eats magic, memory, sky. And the mark is an invitation. A door. When I stand in front of it tomorrow night, I'm not going to fight it. I'm going to let it eat *me*."

Sera went very still.

"While it's swallowing, Sera works the rite. While it's busy pulling me apart, the unbinding severs it from the king. And Wren—" his voice caught, and he hated that, and pushed through it, "—Wren says its name. It can't abide being remembered. She remembered it once and it couldn't hold her. From inside, with the hunger anchored in a host that refuses it—" He shrugged, a mercenary's shrug, cheap and practiced. "Maybe it tears itself apart trying to digest a man who won't be food."

"And you?"

"Numbers," Felix said. "You said it yourself."

For a long moment Sera said nothing. Then she released his wrist and sat down beside him on the step, close enough that their shoulders touched, and stared out across the black water of the drowned nave.

"When I was disgraced," she said, "they broke my hands first. Did I ever tell you that? Before the trial, before the ledger, before any of it. Four men held me down and a fifth broke every finger on my right hand with a smith's hammer, because a ward-smith's hands are her testimony and they wanted my testimony given in advance." She flexed her fingers in the candlelight—straight now, healed crooked, precise as tools. "I spent six years believing that was the worst thing that could happen to a person. Being made into a warning."

"Sera—"

"Let me finish. Because then I met you, and I watched you spend twelve years doing it to yourself. Nobody broke you, Felix. You just decided surviving was the same as living, and you whittled yourself down small enough to fit inside that idea." She turned to look at him, and there was no pity in her face, which was why he could bear it. "So here's my testimony, given freely: going back into that palace to die is easy. I've seen a hundred fools do it. What's hard—and what I'm asking you to do—is go back in there intending to *live*, and accept that the man who walks out won't be the man who walked in. Don't surrender your survival. Surrender the idea that you were ever meant to survive *alone*."

Felix opened his mouth. Closed it. The mark pulsed against his pulse, patient as a held breath.

Behind them, small feet splashed.

They both turned. Wren stood at the edge of the candlelight, awake, her dark eyes enormous. She hadn't spoken since the ashfall road—not since she'd said his true name, the one he'd buried with everything else—but she crossed the flooded floor now with the certainty of a child walking a path only she could see, and she climbed onto the step between them, and she took one of Felix's hands and one of Sera's.

Then she looked up at Felix and said, in her small cracked voice, the voice that had named a god-eater on a dead road:

"You come back."

It wasn't a plea. Children that young didn't understand pleas yet. It was a statement, the way she stated the shapes of vanished constellations—this is how it is, this is what the sky looked like, this is what you will do.

Felix's throat closed. He knelt, water soaking through his trousers, and pressed his forehead to hers.

"I'll come back," he said. And for the first time in twelve years, it wasn't a lie he told to make a moment easier. It was a plan.

* * *

They entered the palace district at dusk, three pilgrims among hundreds, hooded and gray-robed, carrying candles they were not permitted to light. The vigil of the hollow crown demanded darkness from its faithful: the whole capital moved through the streets unlit, thousands of people shuffling shoulder to shoulder toward the great hall, silent except for that low humming bell-note that came from everywhere and nowhere. Felix had infiltrated fortresses before. He had never walked into one that *wanted* to be full, that drew the faithful in like a lung drawing breath.

The crowd frightened him more than any garrison. Not because they were hostile—they weren't. They were serene. Every face he passed wore the same soft, unfocused calm, the look of people who had forgotten something important and found the forgetting comfortable. An old woman smiled at Wren and said, "What a lovely child. Whose is she?" and then, before anyone could answer, her smile deepened and she said, "Whose is *anyone*, on a night like this," and drifted on, content.

"They've eaten the court's children out of its memory," Sera murmured. "And the parents are grateful."

"Not grateful." Felix watched a man ahead of them walk with his hand resting on empty air, cradling the ghost-weight of a child no longer in his mind. "Relieved. That's worse."

Guards lined the processional route, and these were not serene. These were the crown's own—hollow-eyed, moving in perfect unison, and when Felix brushed past one he felt the mark on his wrist *lean* toward the man like a compass needle finding north. The hunger recognized its own. He pulled his sleeve lower and kept walking.

The great hall swallowed them with the crowd.

Felix had seen it once, years ago, from a rooftop during a job he'd never finished: a vaulted cavern of white stone where the kings of Vaelmoor had renewed their oaths for nine hundred years. Tonight it was lit by nothing but the crown itself. The king sat on the high throne beneath the empty dome, and the crown on his head burned with a cold black-gold light—the same black-gold that lived now under Felix's skin—and the light poured upward, streaming off the circlet in slow ribbons, rising past the dome, past the stone, past the world, into the starless dark where the sky used to be.

Feeding.

The court knelt in ranks below the throne, hundreds of them, faces upturned, drinking in the reflected glow with expressions of perfect bliss. And the king—

Felix had seen the king ride past on the outskirts, days ago. Empty eyes, entranced courtiers. He had told himself he understood what waited for them.

He had not understood. Nothing prepared him for the king's face now, because the king was *weeping*. Silent tears cut channels through the ceremonial dust on his cheeks, and his mouth was twisted in an expression that no possession should have permitted: grief. Pure, human, bottomless grief, playing across features the crown-thing had long since stopped bothering to control, because who was left to see? Who would remember?

"He's still in there," Felix breathed. "After all this. He's still—"

"Don't," Sera whispered. "Don't feel sorry for it yet. Feel sorry after. Pity first gets people killed."

The bells hummed. The ribbons of light thickened. And Felix felt the mark begin to answer.

It started as warmth, then heat, then a pulling, a tide running backward through his veins toward the throne. The crown-thing had noticed him—or rather, it had noticed the door in him, the open gate where a god used to live, and its attention came down the hall like a physical weight, pressing the air flat, bending the candle flames of ten thousand unlit pilgrims' faith into a single direction: *him*.

One of the kneeling courtiers rose. Then another. Heads turning in sequence, a wave rolling backward through the ranks, and every face that turned wore the same new expression, and the expression was *recognition*.

"Herald," said the nearest courtier, in a voice that was not his own—it came from somewhere deeper, layered, ancient, a chorus speaking through one soft mouth. "Herald. You brought it back to us. You brought the *door*."

The crowd began to part. All the way to the throne.

Sera's hand found Felix's arm. "Now or never."

"Now," Felix said.

He stepped out of the crowd, threw back his hood, and walked down the aisle of kneeling bodies toward the weeping king, and with every step he did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the quarry, harder than the cells, harder than twelve years of silence:

He stopped holding the door shut.

The mark ignited. Black-gold fire ran up his arm, over his shoulder, across his chest, and the hunger came to meet him like a river meeting the sea, vast and delighted and certain, and Felix Hartwell—mercenary, charm-seller, herald, man—opened himself completely and let it pour in.

The pain was beyond describing, so he didn't try. He walked. The hall tilted; the dome filled his vision; the crown's light bent toward him like a plant toward sun. Somewhere behind him Sera was shouting the opening words of the unbinding rite, chalk and iron and silver thread, and somewhere beyond her Wren stood in the doorway with a candle she had finally, defiantly, lit.

The crown-thing spoke directly into his skull, warm as a bath, kind as a mother:

*Little door. Little empty house. I ate your god in one swallow. I will drink you slower, because you taste of devotion.*

"Yeah," Felix gasped, sinking to one knee before the throne, the hunger flooding every chamber of him, filling every room he'd kept locked for twelve years. Through the black fire he saw the king's face above him, mortal and drowning and *watching*, hope flickering in those void eyes like the last star in a dead sky. "But here's what your dinner forgot to mention."

He raised his marked wrist, and the unbinding sigil blazed white through the hunger's gold.

"I'm not empty. I never was. And I'm not *yours*."

The rite took hold. The crown screamed.
