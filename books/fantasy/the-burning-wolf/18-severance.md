## 18. Severance

**FELIX**

The throne room doors opened the way a mouth opens.

Not swung—parted, the black iron groaning inward on hinges that had not been touched in years, as if the room itself had been waiting for him and saw no reason to pretend otherwise. Beyond them stretched a hall built for light: vaulted ceiling painted with constellations, gold leaf stars scattered across midnight blue, a thousand points of false sky that had once been the pride of Vaelmoor. Now they were the only stars left in the world, and even they were dying—the crown-thing's hunger had reached up into the paint itself, and whole constellations were gone, eaten to bare plaster.

Felix walked in through the gap with his hands open at his sides.

Behind him, Sera's boots rang on marble. Behind her, smaller, quicker, Wren's bare feet made almost no sound at all. The guards who should have barred the way stood along the walls like furniture, their eyes starless voids, their halberds grounded. They did not see him. They had forgotten how to see anything that wasn't food.

*You came back,* said the hunger, everywhere at once, a voice like a cellar full of whispering. *I offered you your god's voice. I offered you your own name back. And still you walk into my mouth.*

"You didn't offer," Felix said. His voice echoed strangely, swallowed by the dark at the far end of the hall where the throne sat. "You dangled. There's a difference."

*Is there? You have spent twelve years unable to tell.*

He kept walking. The mark on his wrist burned—not the dull ache he'd carried since the night the stars went out, but a white heat now, the last of his god's power gathering behind his skin like a held breath. He could feel Sera behind him, feel the rite already beginning in her scarred hands, chalk dust sifting from her fingers onto the marble with every step. She was drawing the unbinding sigil as she walked, laying down the first lines of a circle that would have to close around the throne before the thing wearing the king understood what they were doing.

It understood already. Of course it did. But understanding and stopping were different matters, and the hunger was old, and old things are slow to believe that small creatures can hurt them.

The throne rose ahead of them, black iron and antler bone, and on it sat the king of Vaelmoor.

He was smaller than Felix expected. That was the first horror—that the man who had eaten the sky sat slumped like an old farmer at the end of harvest day, his crown too heavy for his neck, his hands loose in his lap. His robes were magnificent. His face beneath them was gray as ash, and his eyes—

His eyes were holes. Not dark. *Holes.* Looking into them was like looking into the space where stars used to be, and Felix felt Wren's small hand close around two of his fingers, gripping hard.

*I ate him slowly,* the hunger said, almost fond. *Kings taste of duty. Duty is the sweetest thing there is. I left him just enough to sit upright for the renewal tonight, because a coronation requires a body, and this one has served me well. Would you like to know what he remembers? Nothing. Not his wife. Not his sons. Not the color of his own banner. I began with memory, little herald, because memory is where a soul keeps its door.*

"Then you won't mind," Felix said, "if we knock."

He stepped over the first line of Sera's circle.

* * *

**SERA**

The chalk broke twice in her fingers before she learned to hold it looser.

Her hands were ruined—she had known that for twenty years, since the Loom at Greyharrow tore itself apart under her and took the skin of her palms as tuition. Every ward-smith's hands were ruined eventually; it was the trade's quiet joke, told over bad wine. But tonight her scars mattered. Tonight the sigil needed to be drawn by someone who had already been unmade once and survived it, because the unbinding rite did not take its power from chalk or blood or prayer.

It took its power from willingness.

She drew the great circle around the dais in long, sure strokes, and where the chalk passed, the marble remembered being stone instead of throat. She could feel the hunger pressing against her work like a tide against a seawall—not attacking, not yet, just *weighing*, patient as erosion. It had eaten wards older and stronger than hers. It had eaten her Loom. It had eaten a god.

But it had never eaten this particular arrangement of stubbornness.

"Felix." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Second line. Now. Before it finishes deciding whether we're worth the effort."

He moved—her mercenary, her mark-bearer, her impossible man—and dragged his heel through the chalk where she pointed, opening the channel that would let the circle breathe. A proper unbinding wasn't a closed loop. That was the mistake every text made, the mistake that had gotten the order killed. A binding closed. An unbinding *opened*—it cut a door between the possessor and the possessed and held the door wide until everything that had been poured into the vessel poured back out.

Which meant someone had to stand in the doorway.

She had not lied to him about that. In the cells below the palace, with the hunters' boots ringing overhead, she had laid it out with terrible mathematics: *the rite needs a bearer. The hunger has to go somewhere when it leaves the king, and it can't go into stone or air or me—it would eat me in a heartbeat. It needs a host who invites it. Who holds the door open from the inside.*

And Felix Hartwell, who had spent twelve years hiding behind a silenced god and a dead man's charm trade, had looked at her with those tired gray eyes and said, *Then I'll hold it.*

She hadn't let herself cry then. She wasn't going to cry now. Crying smudged chalk.

The second line closed. The third began. Above them the painted stars flickered, and the hunger's voice filled the hall like water filling a lung:

*Ward-smith. I know your name. Sera Ives, who broke the Loom of Greyharrow, who killed forty-one of her own order with a misplaced rune. Shall I say it louder? Shall I tell them what you—*

"They know," Sera said, and did not stop drawing. "I told them myself. That's the difference between us, you rotting scrap of night. Everything I am, I've already confessed."

The chalk sang under her hand. The circle breathed.

* * *

**WREN**

The hole-eyed man on the chair was almost empty, and the thing inside him was almost full, and between those two facts Wren could see the seam.

Nobody else could see it. Sera saw runes. Felix saw debts—he always looked at things like they owed him something or he owed them, and maybe that was true, but it wasn't what Wren saw. Wren saw the sky. She had seen the real sky once, before the theft, though everyone said that was impossible, though she couldn't speak to argue. She had seen the Wolf-that-Burns leap the gap between the Hunter and the Hearth, and she had seen the Seven Sisters holding hands across the dark, and so when she looked at the king she did not see a man or a monster.

She saw a torn constellation. Something that used to belong to the pattern above, ripped down and stuffed inside a crown, and all its stars wrong-way-round, burning inward instead of out.

It hurt to look at. Like a song sung off-key by something enormous.

The chalk lady was drawing. The mark-man was standing very still near the chair, and his wrist was glowing so bright it lit his face from below, and Wren knew—she knew the way she knew the stars—that soon the mark-man would open himself like a door, and the wrong-way-round burning would pour into him, and it would be very bad for a while.

There was something she was supposed to do. She had learned it in the nursery of stolen memories, in the dark among other people's childhoods, when the crown-thing had tried to eat her remembering and found that her remembering went all the way down, past its reach, into a sky older than its hunger. Down there, in the oldest layer, she had found its name.

Names were doors too. She understood that now. Everyone in this room was opening doors.

The thing in the king turned its attention on her then—all of it, the whole vast starving weight of it, pulling away from Felix and Sera like a tide going out—and its voice pressed against the inside of her skull, gentle as drowning:

*Little star-child. You remember too much. Come, sit on my knee, and I will make the remembering quiet. I will make everything quiet. Wouldn't you like to rest?*

Wren looked at Felix. Felix looked at Wren.

He nodded, once, small. *Now,* his face said. *Whatever it is. Now.*

She opened her mouth, which had been shut for three years, which had shut itself the night the stars went out because a sky without stars had no words worth saying—

—and spoke.

* * *

**FELIX**

"*Vhal-ithuun-karai,*" said Wren, and the world flinched.

Three syllables in a language that predated language, and Felix felt them pass through the hall like a blade through silk. The painted stars stopped flickering. The guards along the walls shuddered as one body. And the hunger—the vast, patient, ancient hunger that had eaten a god and a border and a sky—made a sound Felix had not thought it capable of making.

It screamed.

*No,* it said, and its voice cracked down the middle. *No, that name is buried, that name is under ten thousand years of forgetting, no one living—*

"She isn't living the way you understand living," Felix said quietly. "She's older than you. She's the part of the sky you couldn't digest."

He raised his marked wrist.

This was the moment Sera had described in the cells, the terrible mathematics of it, and he had understood her perfectly and agreed anyway, because there had never really been a choice—not since the night the wards fell, not since the safehouse, not since a dockworker's frightened coins and a charm that never worked. Twelve years ago his god had thrown itself down the crown-thing's throat to buy time, silently, without asking him, and Felix had spent twelve years furious at a silence he'd mistaken for abandonment. Now he finally understood the silence. It hadn't been absence. It had been a held breath, waiting to see what he would do with the time.

He opened the door.

"Come on, then," Felix said to the hunger. "You wanted a herald. Take the herald. All of me—you'll find I'm mostly scar tissue and stubbornness, and neither of those digests well."

The mark split open like a wound of light, and the hunger came.

It came the way a flood comes, the way winter comes, and it hit him and *filled* him, and Felix Hartwell ceased, for one endless instant, to be a man and became a doorway. He felt it pouring in—ten thousand years of appetite, the taste of gods and wards and stars, the cold joy of unmaking—and he held the door open from the inside exactly as he'd promised, and it was worse than dying, it was being *eaten while remaining aware*, and somewhere beyond the roaring he heard Sera shouting the closing lines of the rite, heard chalk snapping, heard her voice break and continue anyway—

—and through it all, clear as a bell in floodwater, Wren's voice again, speaking the name a second time, a third, each syllable a knife:

"*Vhal-ithuun. Vhal-ithuun-karai.*"

Because that was the weakness she'd carried out of the nursery of stolen memories, the secret at the bottom of the oldest sky: the crown-thing had survived ten millennia by making itself forgettable. It erased its name from every record, every mind, every star-chart. But it could not abide being *remembered*. To be remembered was to be located. To be named was to be *bound*—pinned into the pattern like any other constellation, forced to hold a shape, forced to be a thing among things instead of the hunger behind all things.

And Wren remembered it. All the way down.

The hunger thrashed inside Felix, trying to pour back out, trying to flee up into the rafters, into the crown, into anywhere—but the rite held the door, and the name held the shape, and between Sera's circle and Wren's syllables and Felix's unwillingness to let go, the vast thing was squeezed down and down and down, from storm to wind to whisper to—

—to something small.

Something small and terrified, coiled in Felix's chest, burning itself against a heart that refused, absolutely refused, to be defined by it.

*What are you,* it whispered, in a voice suddenly no bigger than a child's. *You are nothing. You are a charm-seller. You are nobody. Why won't you stop being—*

"Because I'm not nobody," Felix said, through teeth gritted against the burning. "I'm Felix Hartwell. I'm Sera's friend. I'm Wren's—" his voice caught— "I'm hers. And you're *leaving*."

* * *

**SERA**

The king woke like a man surfacing from deep water.

One moment the throne held a hollow vessel with holes for eyes; the next, the holes filled in—gray, human, streaming—and Aldric the Third of Vaelmoor made a sound that Sera would hear for the rest of her life. Not a scream. A sob. The sob of a man returning to a house he'd forgotten he owned and finding every room stripped bare.

"My—" he said, and stopped. His hands rose and turned over in front of his face, examining them, these strangers' hands. "My sons. I had—I had sons. What were their names?"

The hunger was gone. Sera felt its absence the way you feel a tooth pulled—a raw, aching socket where something had been. The circle around the dais lay in broken chalk, spent. The painted stars overhead had gone dark entirely, every last one, as if the thing's death-throes had taken even the counterfeit sky with it.

And Felix was on his knees at the foot of the throne, folded forward, arms wrapped around himself, steam rising off his shoulders in the cold air. The mark on his wrist had gone from white-hot to a dull ember-red, and even from across the dais Sera could see it changing—dimming, graying, cooling toward something inert.

"No," she said, and it came out of her before she could stop it, and she was moving, chalk dust flying off her ruined hands, dropping to the marble beside him. "No, no, look at me, you absolute mule, you don't get to—"

"I'm here." His voice was gravel and ash, but it was his. "Sera. I'm here."

"You're burning up. You're—" She grabbed his face in both hands and made herself look, made herself assess, because love expressed as panic helped no one. His eyes were still his own. His pulse, under her fingers, was fast but present. Whatever the hunger was doing inside him, it hadn't finished him yet. "Hold on. Do you hear me? Hold on."

"Trying," he managed. "Practiced at it."

Wren arrived like a small missile, folding herself against his chest, and Felix got one arm around her, and the king of Vaelmoor leaned forward on his antler-bone throne and looked down at the three of them—at the disgraced ward-smith, the dying mercenary, the mute girl who wasn't mute anymore—with eyes that were only eyes, wet and lost and mortal.

"What were their names?" the king asked again, desperately. "My sons. Someone must know. Please. Someone in this room must remember my sons."

Sera looked up at the ruined man in the ruined crown, and then at Felix, whose ember-mark pulsed once, twice, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"We'll find them," she heard herself say. "Your Majesty. We'll find them together."

Above them, through the high windows of the throne room, the sky over the capital was still black and starless—but at the eastern edge of the glass, faint as breath on glass, something was beginning to glow.

Felix saw it too. His eyes fixed on it, and his lips moved around words no one else could hear, and the mark on his wrist flared one final time—not with hunger, not with power, but with something that felt, impossibly, like a voice twelve years silent, beginning to speak.
