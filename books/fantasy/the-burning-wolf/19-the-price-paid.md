## 19. The Price Paid

Felix had died before. Twice, if you counted the fever in Kessrin that had burned four days out of his memory and left him thin as a lath for a season after. Once more properly, on a riverbank in the border wars, when a Vaelmoori spear had gone through him below the ribs and an army surgeon with wine-stained breath had sewn him shut like a torn coat.

Dying was familiar country. He knew its landmarks: the cold that started in the hands and walked inward, the way sound went distant and important at once, the strange clarity—the world sharpening just as you lost your grip on it.

This was different. This was not his body failing. This was something *eating* it from the inside, patiently, with what felt almost like courtesy.

He lay on the flagstones below the dais where the throne stood empty, because Sera's rite had torn the crown-thing out of the king and into him, and the throne room around him was wreckage—scorched stone, shattered glass, the great windows blown inward onto a sky still black and starless. Wren's voice had done its work; he'd heard the true name spoken, heard the thing scream in a register that wasn't sound at all. The king was weeping somewhere to his left, mortal and small and alive. Sera was saying words over him, her scarred hands pressed flat against his chest, and her hands were shaking.

"Felix." Her voice came through water. "Felix, look at me."

He looked. It cost him something, the looking. Everything cost him something now.

"I'm here," he said, which was a lie, because most of him was elsewhere, folded around a hunger the size of a cathedral, holding it the way you hold a door shut against a flood—with your whole body, knowing the door is wood and the flood is not.

"You're burning," she said. "The rite anchored it in you but it's—it's feeding. I can't—" Her voice cracked along a fault line he'd never heard in it. Sera Ives did not crack. "Tell me how to help you."

"There's nothing to help with." He tried to smile. His face barely obeyed. "You did the mathematics. We both knew the sum."

"That doesn't mean I have to like the answer."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't."

Wren was there suddenly, kneeling on his other side, her small hand finding his. Her grip was astonishingly strong for someone so slight. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She had said the one word that mattered already, screamed it into the face of a god, and now she simply held on, and her hand was warm, and warmth was going out of him like tide off a flat.

*Hold on*, her hand said. *I remember stars. You can remember too.*

He held on.

* * *

Time went strange after that. The throne room filled and emptied. He heard voices—soldiers, priests, the restored king's raw broken tenor giving orders he could barely stand to give. Someone brought blankets. Someone argued about moving him and was overruled by Sera, whose argument consisted mostly of standing very still and being terrifying.

At some point the pain changed key. It stopped being a flood pressing on a door and became something quieter and worse: a conversation. A pressure at the edge of his mind, vast and patient, testing the walls of him the way water tests ice.

And beneath it, faint as a candle seen through fog, something else.

*Felix.*

He knew that voice. He had spent twelve years learning the shape of its absence, the way a man who loses a tooth keeps finding the gap with his tongue. Twelve years of silence, and now, at the end, when there was almost nothing left of either of them—

"Hello," Felix said aloud, or thought aloud, or both. Around him the throne room dimmed, or he dimmed within it. Hard to say which. "You're late."

*I am sorry.* The voice was warm and ruined, like a hearth after the fire has gone through. *There is so little of me left. What you carry ate most of the rest.*

"Yeah." He might have laughed if laughing hadn't hurt. "We've got that in common now."

*It is in you,* the god said, and there was grief in it, old grief, twelve years deep. *My devourer. The thing that unmade me sits behind your ribs, and it will finish you before dawn unless—*

"Unless?"

A pause. In that pause Felix felt the fragment gather itself, the way a person gathers themselves to say something they know will not be forgiven.

*I can take it back.*

The cold in his hands stopped. For one heartbeat—one impossible, suspended heartbeat—the pain lifted entirely, and Felix floated in a silence so complete it was almost sweet.

*What remains of me is small,* the god went on, gentle, reasonable, the voice of every bargain ever struck in a dark hour. *But it is enough. Enough to draw the hunger out of you and hold it, and burn it, and be burned with it. That was always my nature—to be spent. I spent myself once already to buy your world twelve years. Let me spend the last of it now, and buy you a morning.*

"And the price," Felix said. His mouth was dry. Somewhere far away, Wren's hand tightened on his. "There's always a price. You taught me that. Or—you would have, if you'd stuck around to teach me anything."

*The hunger cannot simply be moved. It must be displaced, and what fills the space it leaves must be given freely.* The voice softened further, which should not have been possible. *It will take your memories of this journey. All of them. The wall falling. Sera. The child. The quarry, the ashfall road, the chapel, the court. Everything since the night the stars went out. You would wake tomorrow whole and healed, and it would all be a story someone told you. You would not grieve it, because you would not know it was yours to grieve.*

Felix lay still.

Around him, at the edge of his fading senses, he could feel the throne room continuing—Sera's breathing, ragged and controlled; Wren's small weight against his arm; the king's weeping finally quieted into something steadier. A world putting itself back together around a man who was coming apart.

*A clean trade,* the god whispered. *Your scars for your stories. You have carried enough, Felix. Twelve years carrying my silence. Let me carry this last thing, and let you go free.*

Free. That was the hook in the bait, and it was a good hook, and Felix hated how well he understood it. Free of the mark. Free of the guilt. Free of the memory of standing in the breach at the fallen wall and walking away because the charm on his wrist pulled and he obeyed. Free of the quarry, where the old magic had answered him too easily, too hungrily, and some part of him had liked it. Free of the drowned chapel, where he'd learned his god had chosen to die without asking him, and where he'd sworn—if only to himself—that no one would ever again make his choices for him.

*You are dying,* the god said. *This is mercy.*

"You don't get to call it that," Felix said.

*Felix—*

"Twelve years." His voice came out low and rough, gravel dragged over iron. "Twelve years I built my whole life around your absence. I told myself you were testing me. Then I told myself you were dead. Then I told myself I didn't care. And the whole time, the truth was you were in my wrist getting eaten, and you knew, and you let me walk around thinking I'd been abandoned because the truth would have cost you something."

*It would have cost everything. If you had known the hunger existed, you would have sought it. You were young and you were angry and you would have found it, and it would have worn you the way it wore the king.*

"So you decided for me."

*Yes.*

"And now you're doing it again." He opened his eyes—he hadn't known they were closed—and the throne room swam into focus above him: the shattered windows, the black sky, Sera's face bent close, wet-eyed and furious with fear. "You're offering to fix me by erasing me. By taking the only part of the last twelve years that was actually mine."

*They are memories of suffering.*

"They're memories of *people*." The words came up from somewhere deeper than his lungs. "You want to give me back my luck-charm shop and my shuttered windows and my nice safe namelessness, and call it healing. But that man—the one before all this—he wasn't free. He was hiding. He sold charms to frightened people and never gave them his real name and called it peace, and it wasn't peace, it was just a smaller cage."

The silence that followed was long. In it, Felix felt the hunger shift behind his ribs, restless, sensing the negotiation happening over its head.

*Sera Ives will grieve you,* the god said quietly. *The child will grieve you. Is it kindness, to leave them that?*

"It's honesty." He turned his head, slow as a ship coming about, until he could see them. Sera's face. Wren's face. Two people who had every reason to keep their armor on and had taken it off anyway, for him, at the worst possible hour. "They get to choose their grief. Same as I get to choose mine. That's the whole point. That's what that thing in the crown never understood—it ate choice. It ate memory because memory is how people choose who they are. And you're offering me the same deal with better manners."

*That is unjust.*

"Maybe." He breathed. It was getting hard to breathe. The cold had reached his shoulders now, walking inward, patient. "But I'd rather die as the man who made these choices than live as the man who got to skip them. You bought twelve years with yourself. Fine. It was a good gift, even if I didn't ask for it. But the last of you isn't yours to spend anymore. It's mine. And I'm spending it on staying *me*."

* * *

The god was silent for a long time.

Felix drifted. The borders of him thinned. He felt the hunger probe again at the walls—not maliciously, not exactly. Hunger didn't hate what it ate. That was the horror of it. It consumed with perfect indifference, the way winter consumes.

Then, softly, the voice came back. Smaller than before. Almost human.

*You have become stubborn.*

"You knew me when I was worse."

*I did.* Something that was almost laughter moved through the dark. *I knew you at nineteen, stealing bread and calling it strategy. I chose you because you never once asked me to make things easy. Only to make them possible. And then I broke my own rule at the end, and offered you easy, because I could not bear—* The voice caught. A god, catching. *Because I could not bear to watch you pay for what I started.*

"Well," Felix said. "Now you know how I felt for twelve years."

*Yes.* A pause. *I am beginning to.*

The presence gathered itself. Felix braced for a final argument, a last temptation, the offer repeated with new refinements. Instead:

*Then I will do what I can, which is little. I will hold the edges of you together while you burn it out. Not instead of you. Beside you. If you will have even that.*

Felix found, to his surprise, that his eyes were wet. "Yeah," he managed. "I'll have that."

*Then listen, herald. The hunger is anchored in your refusal. Every moment you remain yourself, it starves. It feeds on surrender—on the dissolving of the self into appetite. You are, at present, the least nourishing host it has ever had. Hold. Burn. Refuse. And when dawn comes—*

"When dawn comes?"

*When dawn comes, look at what remains. Not at the sky. At what remains.*

The voice thinned, frayed, spread itself along his failing edges like mortar into cracks—and Felix felt it there, faint and stubborn, a warmth no bigger than a coin, holding one small line of him together while everything else burned.

* * *

The night was long.

He surfaced sometimes. Once to find Sera asleep sitting up, her hand still on his chest, her fingers curled like she'd been mid-rite even in dreams. Once to find Wren awake beside him, drawing in the dust of the flagstones with one finger—constellations, he realized. Old ones. New ones. One that was unmistakably a wolf, drawn in lines of fire-bright chalk she'd gotten from somewhere, and he understood, distantly, through the roaring dark inside him, that she had named him too, in her way, and that the name was not a burden.

Once he surfaced to find the king himself sitting nearby, hollow-eyed and human, keeping vigil in silence because he had no right to speak and knew it. Felix, who could not speak either, nodded once. The king nodded back. It was enough. It would have to be enough.

Mostly he burned.

The hunger threw everything it had at him. It wore shapes. It showed him the shop with the shutters open and his name on the door—a future, it whispered, you could still have it, put me down, put it all down, rest. It showed him the riverbank where he'd died the first time and offered to let him stay there, in the green grass before the spear, before the silence, before any of it. It showed him nothing at all, a blessed white emptiness, and begged him to step in.

Each time, Felix refused. Not grandly. Not with speeches. He refused the way a man refuses sleep on watch—gritting through, one breath, then another, then another. *I am Felix Hartwell. I am the man who walked away from the breach and regretted it. I am the man who came back. I am the man Sera trusts and Wren named and the king owes. I am all of it, the shame and the love and the fear, all of it or none of it, and you get none of it.*

Behind his ribs, the hunger began, very slowly, to starve.

And at the edges of him, small as a coin, warm as a coal, the last fragment of a dead god held the line beside him, and did not ask for anything, and did not decide anything for him, and stayed.

* * *

Near the end of the night—or what he guessed was near the end; time had come apart hours ago—Wren leaned close to his ear.

"It's leaving you," she whispered. Not mute anymore, not for a long time now, but still spending words like coins she'd saved all her life. "I can see it. It's getting smaller."

"Good," Felix rasped.

"It's scared of you." Wonder in her voice. "Did you know that? It's never been scared before."

Felix tried to laugh. What came out was closer to a cough, but it was a laugh underneath. "Tell it," he said, "it picked the wrong man."

She smiled—the first full smile he'd ever seen on her, wide and crooked and blazing—and then her eyes went past him, past the broken windows, to the east, and went very wide.

"Felix," she breathed. "Felix, look."

He couldn't turn his head. He didn't need to. Because through the shattered glass, across the ruined throne room, up over the black silhouette of the palace wall—

the sky was changing.

Not light. Not yet. Something smaller than light, and older. A single point of silver, trembling at the edge of the horizon like a coin balanced on its rim, like a charm waiting to be sold, like a god's last ember deciding whether to catch.

The first star.

Felix Hartwell lay dying on the stones of a freed kingdom, hunger and divinity both guttering inside him, and watched the sky begin to come home.
