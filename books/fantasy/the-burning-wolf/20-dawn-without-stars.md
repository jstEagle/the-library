## 20. Dawn Without Stars

Felix woke to the smell of beeswax and blood.

The blood was his own; he could tell by the way it pulled at him when he moved, a thousand small tugs like threads stitched through his skin. The beeswax was Sera's doing. Someone had been burning candles over him for hours—days?—and the wax had pooled in the hollow of his throat, gone cold there, a little gray island he couldn't be bothered to brush away.

He was lying on a cot in the undercroft of the drowned chapel. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock. His body felt like a house that had been emptied by thieves and then set on fire and then rained on.

*Alive,* he thought, testing the word the way you'd test a floorboard in a burned building. *I'm alive.*

He wasn't sure he'd believed he would be. He remembered the throne room in pieces: Sera's chalk lines glowing white-hot, Wren standing on the dais steps with her small fists clenched, shouting a name that no human throat should have been able to hold—and himself, holding out his arms to the crown-thing like a man welcoming home a dog that had gone feral, saying the words Sera had drilled into him for three sleepless days:

*Come, then. I'm the door.*

It had come. Gods, it had come.

He remembered it entering him not as pain but as *appetite*—a vast, cold hunger pouring through his chest like water through a broken dam, looking for things to eat. His memories first. It went for those greedily, gulping down Sera's face, Wren's drawings, twelve years of silence, the dockworker who'd bought a luck-charm on the last ordinary morning of the world—

And found nothing there it could keep.

That was the part Felix kept turning over, lying still so the stitches wouldn't tear. The hunger had eaten from him and been *unfed*. Because everything it swallowed, he had already given away freely—to the order, to the oath, to Sera and Wren, to the strangers on the road. You cannot steal what a man has already handed over. The crown-thing had spent centuries feeding on hoarded magic, hoarded memory, hoarded sky, and it had never once met a host who owned nothing worth taking.

So it had starved inside him. Burned itself against his refusal like a fire fed on wet stone.

Wren had spoken its name. Sera had cut the last thread. And the thing that had worn a king for a hundred years and eaten a god for breakfast had come apart in Felix Hartwell's chest like smoke deciding, finally, to be smoke.

He lay there and listened to the dripping water and thought: *so this is what's left.*

His wrist was bare.

He knew before he looked. He had known for—how long? Hours. He turned his arm anyway, slowly, because some part of him needed to see it. Where the mark had burned for twelve years—the dead god's last charm, the herald's brand, the thing that had dragged him through crowds and across ashfields and into the teeth of witch-hunters—there was now only skin. Old skin. Ordinary skin, scarred in three places from the quarry fight, tanned at the wrist where his sleeve used to ride up.

Gray, Sera would say later, when she described what she'd seen in the rite's final moments. The mark hadn't vanished; it had *gone gray*, like a coal after the fire, like a coin rubbed featureless by too many hands. A charm with nothing left to charm.

His god was truly gone now. Not silent. Gone. And the strange thing—the thing Felix kept waiting to break over him like grief and never quite did—was that the silence felt different. For twelve years the silence had been an absence shaped like a question: *why did you leave me, why did you leave me, why.* Now it was just quiet. The kind of quiet a house makes when everyone who lived in it has died peacefully, in their beds, old.

*You bought twelve years,* he told the empty air, because someone should say it aloud even if no one heard. *You bought them without asking me. I'm still angry about that. But I'll carry it.*

The air didn't answer. That was fine. He was going to have to get used to fine.

* * *

Sera found him trying to stand an hour later—or what he guessed was an hour later, judging by the candle stubs. She came down the chapel stairs with a bowl of something steaming and stopped dead on the bottom step, and for a moment her face did something complicated that she would have denied under torture.

"You're supposed to be unconscious," she said.

"I'm bad at following instructions. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone," she repeated softly. "There's hardly anyone left to ask." She set the bowl down on a crate—barley broth, by the smell, with real salt in it, which meant someone had looted a nobleman's pantry—and crossed the room to look at his wrist without touching it. Her hands were bandaged to the fingertips. In the candlelight they looked like two bundles of dirty linen.

"Let me see your eyes," she said.

"Romantic."

"Felix."

He let her look. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her, or at least to fail to horrify her, which amounted to the same thing in Sera's economy of expression.

"The king?" he asked.

"Asleep. Actually asleep, not whatever he was doing before. The physicians don't know what to call what's wrong with him, so they've settled on *weakness*, which is what physicians call everything they can't bill for." She sat down heavily on the crate beside the broth. "He wept for six hours yesterday. Asked for his daughter. Nobody could tell him where his daughter was, because nobody could remember having seen her in twenty years, and then one of the chambermaids said—" Sera's voice caught, just once, a hairline crack. "One of the chambermaids said she thinks the girl was taken to the nursery. The nursery where the memories were kept. We haven't opened it yet. I can't. Not yet."

"We will," Felix said. "Together. When I can walk."

"When you can walk," she agreed, and then, because she was Sera and mercy embarrassed her: "You look terrible."

"I feel worse. Describe me the rest."

She described him the rest. The city waking up confused, like a sleeper after a nightmare already fading. The courtiers wandering the palace grounds touching trees, weeping at the feel of bark, unable to say why. Three hundred people in the great hall who had forgotten their own children beginning, piece by piece, to remember—not all at once, but in fragments, the way frost melts. The hunters of the Pale Ledger had scattered; their ledger, it turned out, had been written in the crown-thing's ink, and with the ink gone the pages had gone blank, and men who had built their whole lives around a list had discovered they no longer had one.

"Some of them are just standing in the streets," Sera said. "Holding their blank books. One of them asked me what he was supposed to do now. I didn't have an answer. I gave him soup."

"That's more than I would've done."

"No, it isn't." She said it flatly, factually, the way she said everything that mattered. Then she nodded at his wrist. "Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Liar."

"It aches," Felix admitted. "Like a tooth that got pulled. Like the space where something was is still shaped like it." He flexed his fingers. They worked. That was the miracle nobody would write songs about: everything worked. He was just—less. Diminished. A lamp turned down to its lowest wick. Twelve years ago he had carried a god's residual fire in his veins and pretended it was nothing; now he actually had nothing, and the pretending was over, and underneath the pretending there turned out to be a middle-aged mercenary with bad knees and a talent for small commerce.

"Do you know what the strangest part is?" he said.

"Tell me."

"I keep reaching for it. The mark. Not the power—I barely ever used the power. The *weight* of it. Twelve years I hated the thing, and now my arm feels light in a way that's wrong, like I've lost a limb and the ghost of it itches." He laughed, short and humorless. "I defined myself by my damage, Sera. That was the whole architecture. *The man whose god went silent.* Take that away and I'm—"

"A man," Sera said.

"That's the terrifying part, yes."

She picked up the bowl and put it in his hands, closing his fingers around it, warm ceramic against palms he hadn't realized were cold. "Eat," she said. "Then sleep. Then, if you're well enough, there's a girl upstairs who hasn't slept properly since the throne room because she keeps coming down here to check whether you're breathing, and I would very much like her to stop."

* * *

Wren was not upstairs. She was on the third stair from the bottom, sitting with her knees drawn up, and she had clearly been there long enough to hear everything, which meant she had heard him say *a girl upstairs* and had elected not to correct the record.

She looked at him the way she had looked at the stars in her drawings—with that fierce, unblinking attention that made most adults look away first.

"Hey, sprout," Felix said.

She crossed the space between them fast and hit him—not hard, nowhere near hard enough to hurt, which was how he knew it wasn't about hurting. She pressed her forehead against his shoulder and stayed there, breathing, while he held the broth in one hand and put the other carefully on top of her head.

"Yeah," he said, because his voice wouldn't do anything fancier. "Yeah. I'm here."

When she pulled back, she signed to him—quick, fluid, the language the three of them had built on the road out of necessity and habit. *You went somewhere. When the hunger was in you. Where did you go?*

He considered lying. He considered the truth. With Wren the two options had always been closer together than with anyone else he'd known.

"Nowhere," he signed back. "That was the whole trick. It wanted to take me somewhere and I had nowhere left to take. Everything I am was already—" he searched for the sign, made one up, pointed at her, at the stairs where Sera sat listening and pretending not to, at the chapel around them. "*Given.*"

Wren frowned at that, unconvinced, the way she frowned at adults' explanations of weather. Then she reached out and took his bare wrist in both hands, turned it over, and studied the gray place where the mark had been. She touched it with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a scar you weren't sure was finished healing.

Then she did something she had never done. She leaned down and kissed it.

Felix felt his throat close. Twelve years of silence, a war, a god devoured, a sky stolen and half-returned, and it was a mute child kissing a dead charm that finally cracked him open. He didn't cry—he was fairly sure he didn't—but something in his chest shifted its weight and settled, like furniture moved into a new room, like a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"What do I do now?" he asked her, out loud, because it wasn't really a question for her. "That's the problem, sprout. Everyone's going to want things. The king's people, the order, what's left of it. They'll want the man who walked into the throne room. They'll want a commander, a symbol, a story." He shook his head. "I sold charms. That was the whole life I built. Small commerce. Small kindnesses. I don't know how to be large again, and I don't think I want to learn."

Wren tilted her head. Then she took his hand, the one with the gray mark, and folded his fingers closed over his own palm—as if wrapping something small inside it, a seed, a coin, a charm.

She signed, slow so he'd follow: *Open the shop.*

* * *

Three mornings later, Felix walked to the end of the chapel road and stood in the gray light and watched the sun rise into a sky with no stars in it and understood, finally, what kind of victory they had won.

Because the stars had not come back.

He had known it, in the way you know a thing and refuse to look at it directly. The crown-thing had eaten the sky for a century, and eating, unlike killing, does not leave a body. Wren's constellations existed now only in her memory and her drawings and the memories of a handful of others old enough or strange enough to have kept them. The wards Sera talked about rebuilding would hold back whatever else crawled in the dark beyond the dark—but the light they'd hold it back *from* was gone, and no rite, no sacrifice, no willing host could un-eat a century of devouring.

They had saved what was left. That was all. The king was mortal and weeping and would reign over a starless kingdom. The court would spend a generation remembering its children. The land through Ashfall would take fifty years to forgive them, if it ever did.

And Felix stood in the cold dawn with his dead god's silence in one arm and his living family asleep behind him in a flooded chapel, and he waited for the hollowness to arrive, the emptiness, the *what was it all for*.

It didn't come.

What came instead was smaller and harder to name. The smell of woodsmoke from the chapel chimney, where Sera was already awake, already working, because Sera grieved by building. The sound of Wren's footsteps overhead, quick and light, teaching the chambermaid's daughter a constellation by tapping it out on a table. The weight of his own body, diminished, ordinary, entirely his own for the first time since he was twenty-three years old.

A hollow victory, he thought. But hollow things are where you pour the wine.

He turned back toward the chapel, and that was when he saw it—a figure at the gate, travel-worn, leaning on a staff, wearing the faded gray cloak of an order that Felix had believed extinct as of four nights ago. The figure raised one hand, and Felix's stomach dropped, because he recognized the hand, the ring on it, the particular way it moved.

Brother Cassian. Dead in the safehouse massacre. Felix had knelt beside his body.

Cassian smiled at him across the morning mist, and said, in a voice Felix had last heard whispering *the crown ate him first*:

"You're going to want to hear this, Hartwell. There are others like it. And one of them is already awake."
