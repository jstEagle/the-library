## 22. The Burning Wolf Rests

The shutters were the first thing Felix changed.

For twelve years he had kept them closed—painted them the gray of weathered slate, wedged them tight against their frames, and told himself it was prudence. A man selling charms in a city where magic was outlawed did not advertise. A man hiding from his own name did not put that name on a door. The shop on Candlemaker's Row had been a tomb with a till in it, and he had lain down inside it every morning and called it living.

Now the shutters stood open, folded back against the wall like wings, and morning light came all the way to the back counter for the first time since he'd leased the place. He'd sanded the window frames himself. Sera had re-laid the warding stones beneath the threshold—not to hide, this time, but to hold: a lattice that welcomed what belonged and turned away what didn't. Her work was beautiful in a way his never would be. She still apologized for the scars in it, the places where her hands shook or the old burns made her grip uneven, and he still told her the same thing every time.

"The flaws are how I know you made it."

She'd thrown a rag at him. Progress.

The sign had gone up three days ago. HARTWELL'S CHARMS, in letters Sera had cut by hand and Wren had painted over twice because she liked the smell of the paint. His actual name. On a door. In Vaelmoor, where for years saying *Hartwell* aloud in certain districts could get a man followed home. The king's new edicts had undone the proscriptions, but edicts moved slower than fear, and fear had been the only law of Felix's life for longer than he cared to count.

He caught himself checking the street anyway. Old habit. He doubted he'd ever fully kill it—the flinch toward doorways, the catalog of exits, the way his eyes went first to hands rather than faces. Twelve years as a ghost left sediment. But the street held nothing worse than a fishwife arguing with a gull and two children playing at knights with barrel staves, so he let the breath go and turned back to his work.

The work itself was humbling.

His mark was gone—or nearly. Where the burning sigil had lived on his wrist there remained only a pale patch of skin, smooth as scar tissue, cold to the touch on some mornings. Whatever power had answered him in the quarry, whatever hunger he had carried out of the throne room and burned down to ash inside his own body, it had taken the god's residue with it when it died. He was, by every measure that had ever mattered to the old orders, empty.

And yet the charms sold.

Not because they worked—not the way they used to, not with the low warm thrum of borrowed divinity in them—but because Felix Hartwell had spent twenty years learning what frightened people needed, and grief does not require miracles. He braided knot-charms for sailors' wives and pressed iron nails into wax for doorways. He wrote small true things on slips of paper and sealed them in tin: *You survived the dark. You will survive the light too.* People bought those fastest. They bought them with the furtive, grateful look of the starving, and Felix took their coins and met their eyes and did not pretend to be more than he was.

A woman came in near midmorning with a boy of perhaps six clinging to her skirt. She set a copper on the counter without ceremony.

"For the door," she said. "He won't sleep since the stars went out. Keeps asking if they'll go away again."

Felix looked at the boy. The boy looked at the floor with the particular ferocity of a child determined not to cry in front of a stranger.

"They went out once," Felix said. "Which means someone noticed. Which means someone brought them back." He wrapped a nail-charm in cloth and slid it across. "Hang it low, where he can see it from his bed. And tell him—" he paused, choosing, "—tell him a man who was there says the sky remembers everything it ever lost. It just takes its time."

The woman's face did something complicated. She nodded once, took the charm, and steered the boy out. At the threshold the child looked back, and Felix gave him the smallest nod, soldier to soldier, and the boy straightened like something had been pinned to his chest.

Sera appeared from the stairwell a moment later, ink-stained and squinting, a ledger under one arm. "That was the third mother this week."

"Word travels."

"Your word travels." She dropped the ledger on the counter and studied him with the frank, unsentimental gaze he'd learned to stop bracing against somewhere on the road through Ashfall. "The restored king's steward came by again yesterday while you were at market. Third time. They want you to head the new order, Felix. Ward-smiths, oath-keepers, the whole rebuilt apparatus. There's a salary. A title. A *seat*."

"I heard him the first two times."

"And?"

"And I told him the same thing I'll tell you before you ask a fourth time." Felix tied off the braid in his hands and set it in the display case between the luck-knots and the mourning-cords. "I spent twelve years belonging to a purpose. It ate my god, my brothers, my name, and very nearly my sky. I'm not going to hand it my mornings too."

"It isn't the same order."

"No," he agreed. "It's better. That's what makes it dangerous. Good purposes are hungrier than bad ones—they don't have to threaten you. They just have to *matter*." He tapped the counter, once, where the wood was worn pale in the shape of his own elbow. "I matter here. That's the whole ambition now. It fits in one room."

Sera looked at him a long moment. Then she pulled the ledger back and opened it, which was her way of conceding a point without saying so. "Fine. But you're coming to the ward-raising at the river gate next week regardless. I need steady hands and you owe me for the quarry."

"I saved our lives at the quarry."

"You *hysterically* saved our lives at the quarry. Different thing. You owe me."

He laughed—actually laughed, the sound surprising him on its way out—and behind her, halfway down the stairs, Wren stopped and watched them with the grave delight of a child cataloguing evidence that adults were, occasionally, people.

* * *

Wren spoke now. Not much, and never in a hurry, but the silence she'd arrived in had cracked open on the road through Ashfall and never fully closed again. She talked most in the evenings, sitting on the shop's back step with her knees drawn up, naming stars that weren't there yet.

"Vaelmoor's Crown," she said one night, pointing at a blank stretch of eastern sky. "It comes back last. It's shy."

"Why would a constellation be shy?" Sera asked, mending a net by feel in the dark.

"Because everyone looked at it wrong for a hundred years. Called it the King's Crown instead. It doesn't like being owned." Wren said these things the way other children reported the weather—as plain fact, unbothered by whether anyone believed her. No one had taught her the old names. She simply had them, the way she'd had the crown-thing's true name, the way she'd walked out of a nursery of stolen memories carrying the one weapon that mattered. Some children remember the stars. Wren remembered *for* them.

Felix sat beside her and said little. He'd learned that from her, too—that presence didn't require narration.

But tonight, watching her trace shapes on the dark, he found his chest doing the thing it still did sometimes: a slow ache, half grief and half gratitude, with no clean border between them. His god had chosen silence to buy the world twelve years. His brothers of the Quiet Oath had died holding that line without knowing what it purchased. And Felix had spent those twelve years furious at the silence, then hollow inside it, then—worst of all—comfortable, mistaking numbness for peace. It had taken a dead god's charm, a broken ward-smith, and a mute child to teach him the difference.

Peace wasn't the absence of war. Peace was the presence of people.

"Wren," he said.

She looked up. In the lamplight spilling from the open shop door her eyes were ordinary brown, starless, entirely human. He loved them more than any sky.

"When the stars come back—all the way back—I want you to teach me their names. All of them. Even the shy ones."

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all negotiations. "It'll take years."

"Good." He meant it more than he'd meant anything in a decade. "I've got time now. Turns out that's the whole point."

* * *

It happened on an evening in late autumn, six weeks after the coronation-renewal, when the city had begun to believe in ordinary time again.

Felix was closing up—shutters still open, always open now, but the till counted and the door locked against nothing in particular—when Wren came down the stairs two at a time, breathless, hair wild, grabbing his sleeve with both hands.

"Felix. *Felix.* Come *look.*"

They spilled out into the street together, and Sera came behind with a lamp she immediately forgot to hold up, and the three of them stood in the middle of Candlemaker's Row craning upward like fools along with half the neighborhood, because there, low above the eastern rooftops, in the exact place a seven-year-old had promised it would return—

One star.

Small. Unsteady. Burning like a struck match held against the wind.

The street erupted. Someone wept openly by the chandler's. The two boys with the barrel staves abandoned their game to scream at the sky. An old man took off his hat, which in Vaelmoor was either prayer or apology, possibly both.

And Felix—

Felix felt the pull. Of course he did. Every eye in the city went up; his own neck ached with the old reflex, the mercenary's habit of locating the threat, the oathsman's habit of locating the duty, the herald's scarred instinct that any light in the dark was a summons. For one heartbeat he stood at the center of every pull he'd ever obeyed.

Then he looked down.

Sera had found his hand without either of them deciding to. Her grip was ink-stained and burn-scarred and steady as good stonework. Wren had wedged herself against his side, one arm around his ribs, pointing up with her free hand as if she personally were responsible for the star—which, in fairness, she might as well have been. Lamplight from his own open doorway lay gold across the cobbles. Upstairs, a kettle Sera had forgotten was beginning to sing.

"Vaelmoor's Crown," Wren whispered, triumphant and tearful at once. "Told you. Told you it comes back."

"You told us," Felix said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, and he found he did not care who heard it. "First name on the list. You're collecting."

The star trembled overhead. Tomorrow there would be meetings about it—stewards, scholars, the restored king's people wanting testimony from the mark-bearer, the ward-smith, the star-child. Tomorrow the world would start pulling again, gently, the way worlds do.

But tonight there was a kettle singing upstairs, a family on a cold street, and a sky that had begun, at last, to keep its promises.

Felix didn't look up again.

He looked at them.
