## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle by the time Petra Winterbourne found the error.

It was a small one. They always were. A widow in the Saltmarket had sold back three years to the Guild—years she'd bought at nineteen, when her husband was dying and she'd wanted more of him than the gods meant to give—and the ledger said *four*. Somewhere between the notary's table and this desk, a year had been invented. A year that belonged to no one, that a woman would live without ever knowing it was borrowed from nothing.

Petra dipped the pen and corrected it. The ink was Guild ink, black as a closed eye, and it took to the page the way all impossible things did: quietly, without argument, as if they'd always been there.

That was the trick of Ashvale. Not the trading—the accounting. Anyone could sell a man an extra decade if they didn't care whether he survived the interest. The Guild's genius was that every impossible thing came with a line item, a date, a signature, a balance carried forward. Grief in bottles, each one labeled with the name of the person who'd wept into it. Names pried loose from their owners and kept in wax until someone paid to wear them. Years, months, hours, the last warm minute of a conversation—all of it fungible, all of it filed.

And all of it balanced. That was the oath. That was the whole religion of the place, though the Guildmaster would never say so aloud: *the books must balance.* What was taken had to be given. What was given had to be taken. Petra had sworn that oath ten years ago on a shrine to a god who had, at the time, still been speaking, and the words had gone into them like nails into green wood. They could feel the oath even now, some nights, sitting under the ledger-room's single lamp—a faint ache along the inside of the wrist, where a promise becomes a scar if you keep it long enough.

The ledger room sat at the top of the Guildhouse, above the vaults and the salt door and the counting floors where brokers argued over the price of a dead man's regrets. It had one window, and the window looked out on nothing but roof-slate and the grey belly of the sky, because the Guildmaster believed a book-keeper should see nothing that might make them sentimental. Sentiment unbalanced accounts.

Petra had been invisible for ten years and had gotten very good at it. Not the way spies were invisible—spies wanted to be looked at once, briefly, and forgotten. This was a slower craft. Petra wore grey because grey gave the eye nothing to hold. They spoke only when spoken to, and then in the flat, precise cadence of a person reciting figures. They ate alone, drank alone, walked home alone through streets that had learned not to see them. In ten years they had made no friends, kept no vices worth blackmailing, and written nothing in their own hand except numbers.

Numbers didn't lie. Numbers couldn't be used against you. A number was the closest thing to silence that could still be read aloud.

* * *

The second account of the night waited in the tray marked with a black ribbon: settlements. Things returned.

This was Petra's favorite hour of the work, though *favorite* was a word they'd have denied under torture. There was a particular entry form for restorations, printed on paper the color of old milk, and it began with a question in the Guild's own cramped typeface: *What is being given back, and to whom?*

Tonight's restoration concerned a ghost.

Not a spirit—Ashvale didn't deal in spirits; too much paperwork, too little yield. This ghost had been a man named Corvin Hale, a ferryman who'd drowned forty years ago in the Greywater with his name still in his mouth, and his name had been fished out after him by a broker working the riverbanks. Names come loose like that, near death. They slip off a person the way a glove slips off a hand, and if you're quick, you can catch them before they dissolve.

Corvin Hale's name had spent four decades in a wax cylinder in Vault Nine, and tonight it was going home—or as close to home as anything got in Ashvale. His daughter was seventy-one years old now. She had paid the restoration fee in installments across nine years, selling small things to raise the coin: a memory of her wedding morning, a season's worth of dreams, the taste of her mother's bread. She had never once asked what her father's name cost the Guild to keep. Nobody asked that. The answer was always the same anyway: *what the market would bear.*

Petra wrote out the release in full. *One (1) name, to wit: Corvin Hale, restored to bloodline, condition good, minor fading at the edges consistent with age of asset.* Their pen paused at *minor fading.* That was a kindness, a lie of omission. Forty years in wax left a name threadbare. When the daughter spoke it over the water tomorrow, it would come out sounding almost right—close enough that grief would do the rest of the work, filling in the worn places with love the way water fills a footprint.

They signed, sealed it, set it aside. And then, because the night was long and the candle was dying and no one was watching, Petra let themselves do the thing they never let themselves do during daylight hours, when the counting floors were loud and the enforcers walked the halls with their iron-tipped staves.

They read the old ledgers.

Not the current ones. The deep ones—the ones chained to the shelf behind their chair, the ones written before Petra's time, in hands long dead or long retired. There was a pleasure in them that Petra had never examined too closely, the way you don't examine a floorboard you suspect is rotten. The old entries had a music to them. *Borrowed against: one childhood summer, redeemed in full by the debtor's granddaughter, who remembered it for him at his graveside.* Or: *Asset: the last words of a hanged man, sold twice by fraud, recovered, held pending arbitration.* Whole lives reduced to lines, and yet the lines glowed. You could feel the people in them, pressing against the margins like faces against glass.

Petra told themselves it was professional study. Knowing how the Guild had priced things in the past made you better at pricing things in the present. That was true, as far as it went. But it didn't explain why they always turned, eventually, to the same volume—the one bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its spine cracked from handling—and why they always stopped at the same place in it.

The entry was eleven years old. It sat on a page otherwise devoted to routine transfers, wedged between the sale of a miller's courage and the purchase of six hours of somebody's happiness, and it read:

*Received into custody: one (1) memory, sealed by donor's request. Contents withheld from ledger per Article Twelve. To be opened by the donor only, upon terms the donor will know.*

No amount. No price. No description. Every other entry in eleven hundred pages obeyed the grammar of trade—this one refused it, the way a locked door refuses a keyring.

And it was written in Petra's handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. Identical—the slight forward lean of the letters, the way the *t* crossed low, the little hook on the *r* that Petra had been mocked for in their first year at the Guild and had never managed to train out. They had compared it a hundred times over the years, holding the page beside fresh entries, tilting both toward the candlelight. Ink age said eleven years. Petra had been twenty-two then, newly oathed, already keeping books—and they had no memory whatsoever of writing it.

They had reported stranger discrepancies before. That was the job: find the error, correct the error. Once they'd found a broker double-selling bottled grief and had turned him in before breakfast; he'd been sold downriver by noon, his own name added to inventory. The books must balance. Petra believed that the way other people believed in weather.

But they had never reported this.

Ten years, and they had never once carried the grey ledger to the Guildmaster's office and laid it open on his desk and said, *here is an entry I cannot account for, here is my own hand doing something I did not do.* Because some part of them—the part that flinched, the part that felt along the inside of the wrist where the oath lived—knew, with the dumb certainty of a body avoiding a blade, that reporting it would be worse than carrying it. That the moment the entry was spoken aloud, something would begin that could not be entered into any ledger and corrected.

So instead they touched it. That was all. Tonight, as every night, Petra laid two fingers on the page, just below the last line, where the paper had gone smooth from years of exactly this touch, and they sat with the small cold fact of it: *I wrote something I cannot remember writing, and I am afraid of it, and I keep it the way you keep a wound clean—not because you love the wound, but because you are afraid of what's underneath.*

Outside, the thirteenth bell of the night rang from the Saltmarket tower, which was wrong, because the tower rang twelve bells and no more—it had rung twelve bells for two hundred years. Petra frowned at the window, counted backward through the evening's bells, found twelve accounted for and one unaccounted for, and decided, in the end, that they had miscounted. That was the sensible conclusion. That was the conclusion a careful, invisible person would reach.

They closed the grey ledger. They chained it shut. They snuffed the candle and sat a moment in the dark, letting their eyes adjust, listening to the Guildhouse settle around them—stone ticking, pipes knocking, somewhere below the vault doors being checked by the night wardens, each lock answering with its small iron voice.

Then they gathered the day's completed entries, squared their edges against the desk, and stood.

The stair down was narrow and smelled of tallow and salt. Halfway down, on the landing where the light from the counting floor spilled up through the grate, Petra heard voices—two of them, low, urgent, the clipped rhythm of enforcers who thought they were alone.

"—found him at the salt door, or what was left. Courier's colors. Somebody'd already gone through his pockets."

"Guildmaster know?"

"He knows somebody beat us to whatever the man was carrying. He doesn't know who." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone of a man saying something he shouldn't: "There was blood on the third-floor stair. Fresh."

Petra stood motionless in the dark, the completed entries pressed flat against their chest, and did not breathe.

They had seen no courier. They had touched no blood. Whatever those men were hunting, it wasn't them—it couldn't be them; Petra Winterbourne had spent ten years becoming a person nobody hunted, a grey man in a grey room with grey numbers, and the books balanced, and the books always balanced—

Except one entry didn't. One entry had never balanced, and it was written in their own hand, and tonight, for the first time in ten years, the Guildhouse was awake past the twelfth bell, looking for something that had slipped through the salt door and vanished.

Petra descended the rest of the stairs without making a sound, crossed the empty counting floor with their eyes fixed on the middle distance, and stepped out into the street, where the wind off the Greywater carried the smell of salt and, faintly, unmistakably, blood.
