## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The salt door of the Ashvale Guild was older than the Guild itself, which was saying something, because the Guild was older than the kingdom that pretended to license it. Nobody remembered why the threshold had been packed with white salt instead of stone—some said it kept out the dead, some said it kept them in—but every member crossed it barefoot on their first day and left a coin in the crystals, and after that the door knew you.

It knew Petra Winterbourne the way an old dog knows its master: without affection, without surprise.

That night, balancing ledgers by lamplight in the counting room above, Petra heard the door scream.

Not creak. Not groan. Scream—a high, mineral shriek, like a struck flint, the sound the salt made only when something crossed it that did not belong. Petra's pen stopped mid-figure. Ink pooled at the nib and bled into the margin of the year-ledger, ruining a column of borrowed decades that had taken an hour to total.

"Damn," Petra said quietly, because quiet was a habit ten years deep, and went down to see who was dying on the doorstep.

* * *

The courier had almost made it inside. That was the first thing Petra noticed—the intentionality of it. He hadn't collapsed in the street like a drunk or a stabbed man with no particular destination. He'd crawled. Both hands bloody to the wrist, knees ruined through his trousers, he had dragged himself up the three steps from Cutpurse Row and across the salt line, and now lay half in the doorway with his boots still outside, as if he'd wanted very badly for the Guild to be able to say he'd never entered.

He was maybe thirty. Grey-faced. The front of his coat was dark and wet and kept getting wetter.

Petra knelt. This was procedure. The ledger said: *attend all arrivals; record all arrivals.* There was a form for this. There was always a form.

"You're in the Guildhouse," Petra said. "You're across the salt. You're safe."

The courier laughed. It came out mostly as blood. "No," he managed, "I'm really not," and then his hand shot out and closed around Petra's wrist with the desperate strength of a man holding onto the edge of a well.

Under his fingers, pressed flat between his palm and Petra's skin, was something stiff and crackling: parchment, folded small, sealed in wax the color of dried arterial blood. He pushed it into Petra's hand and then folded Petra's fingers over it, one by one, deliberately, like a priest administering something.

"No," Petra said. "Whatever this is—"

"Thirteen turns." His eyes were wrong. Not glassy the way dying men's eyes go glassy—focused. Terribly focused, the focus of someone spending his last currency on a single purchase. "Listen. Thirteen turns. It eats him from the edges inward. The crown is not—" He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it stopped there was a bright thread of red at the corner of his mouth. "The crown is not a crown. You understand? It was never a crown. Find the floor. The floor under the city. The map knows the way but the map lies about the—"

He stopped.

Not trailed off. Stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-motion, mid-word, his mouth still shaped around whatever came after *lies about the*. His hand stayed clamped on Petra's wrist for three heartbeats—Petra counted them, because counting was what Petra did instead of feeling—and then the strength went out of it all at once, and he sagged back against the doorframe, and the salt beneath him began, very slowly, to turn grey.

Petra had seen the salt do that once before, years ago, when old Mistress Corvane died in her chair by the fire and nobody found her for two days. The salt mourned. Or it recorded. No one at the Guild could agree which, and no one liked to talk about it.

Petra sat back on their heels in the doorway of the Ashvale Guild, holding a dead man's message, and listened to the night.

Cutpurse Row was quiet. Too quiet. Somewhere a few streets over, a watchman's bell rang the third hour, and the sound seemed to arrive late, as if even the bells were reluctant to be heard near here.

And underneath the quiet—boots. More than one pair. Moving fast. Coming up the Row.

* * *

There are perhaps forty seconds between hearing boots and seeing their owners, if they're running and you know the streets. Petra spent twenty-nine of them making a decision that would have been unthinkable an hour earlier, and eleven of them acting on it.

The map went into the counting room. Not hidden—Petra would have time to hide it later, properly, in the false spine of the 4th-year ledger where the Guild kept its records of debts owed by the dead. For now, simply out of sight, under a stack of unentered receipts, beneath the lamp with its shade turned low.

Then Petra went back downstairs, knelt beside the courier again, and waited.

The enforcers came around the corner of Cutpurse Row at a pace just short of a run, which was how enforcers moved when they wanted to look official rather than urgent. Three of them. Petra knew all three by name and by entry: Vosk, broad and patient, who collected on borrowed youth from vain merchants; the twins Ilse and Maren Kettering, who handled names—stripping them from living people and selling them to ghosts who missed having one. All three wore the grey-and-ash of Guild service, and all three had the particular blankness of faces arranged for work.

Vosk reached the steps first. He looked at the body. He looked at Petra. He did not look surprised, and Petra filed that away the way one files everything: carefully, in a place where it could be found again.

"Winterbourne." Vosk's voice was gravel poured slow. "Late delivery?"

"A courier." Petra heard their own voice come out level, bored, faintly annoyed at being kept from the ledgers. Ten years of practice lived in that voice. "Dead on arrival. I was recording the particulars."

"The particulars." Vosk came up the steps, careful not to touch the salt, and looked down at the courier with the mild interest of a man checking a horse's teeth. "Name?"

"I don't know yet. He carried nothing identifiable."

Ilse Kettering drifted past Petra into the doorway, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed theirs, and stood looking down at the dead man with her head tilted, the way she did when she was listening for a name the way other people listened for music. After a moment she frowned.

"He hasn't got one," she said.

"People have names," Maren said from the bottom of the steps.

"Not him." Ilse crouched, touched two fingers to the courier's temple, and withdrew them quickly, shaking them as if she'd touched a stove. "Or he had one and it's gone. Taken clean. There's just—shape. Where the name used to be." She looked up at Petra, and her eyes were wide and young and unsettled, and Petra understood suddenly that whatever had killed this man had started eating him before his heart stopped, and had begun with the part of him that said *I am.*

"That happens sometimes," Petra said. "Couriers who trade their names for passage. It's cheaper than paying tolls."

"It's against Guild law," Maren said.

"It's against Guild law to strip a name without license. Trading your own is your own affair." Petra recited it smoothly. It was even true. It was also, Petra realized with a cold, distant sort of wonder, entirely beside the point, and they were saying it anyway, fluently, because the alternative was to let these three stand here while the map sat upstairs under a stack of receipts, and so the words came out the way water comes out of a cracked pipe: automatically, and impossible to stop.

Vosk studied Petra for a long moment. He had a face built for studying things; the rest of it was spare and brown and leathery, and his eyes were pale as river ice.

"You didn't report a runner coming up the Row," he said. "Salt door screamed. Half the street heard it. You were upstairs."

"I was working. I came down when I heard it. He was already dying." Petra paused, and then—because the lie needed a bone in it, needed something true to hang its flesh on—added: "He grabbed my wrist. Tried to give me something. Died before he finished the sentence."

That much was true. Petra was discovering that lying was easier when you fed it truths, like feeding a fire.

"What did he try to give you?" Vosk asked.

"His hand. Nothing else. Whatever he was carrying, he didn't get it out." Petra spread their empty hands, palms up, and held Vosk's river-ice eyes, and felt something shift and settle somewhere behind their sternum—a small, permanent-feeling click, like a lock engaging. *Ten years,* Petra thought. *Ten years of honest entries and exact totals, and it takes one dead stranger to teach me I'm good at this.*

Vosk looked at the hands. Looked at the face. Whatever he saw there—or didn't see—seemed to satisfy him, or at least to fail to incriminate.

"Guildmaster will want a full entry," he said finally. "Cause of death. Time. Effects of the deceased."

"There were no effects."

"So enter it." He turned away, then stopped. "Winterbourne. The salt's gone grey under him."

"I noticed."

"Corvane took two days to do that."

"Yes," Petra said. "She did."

Vosk nodded slowly, as if Petra had confirmed something, though Petra couldn't imagine what, and gestured to the twins. Between them they lifted the courier's body—carefully, respectfully, the way the Guild handled anything that might still hold value—and carried it down the steps and away into the dark of Cutpurse Row, toward the cold room where the Guild kept its inventory of the recently departed until their accounts could be settled.

Petra watched them go. Then Petra looked down at the salt.

Where the courier had lain, the white crystals had faded to the color of old bone, and in the middle of the greyness, fine as frost-ferns, a pattern had formed. Petra crouched over it for a long time before admitting what it looked like.

A floor plan. Corridors, chambers, a long central nave. Like the ground-plan of a building drawn by someone who had only ever seen buildings from underneath.

* * *

Upstairs, Petra locked the counting-room door, which was a thing members of the Ashvale Guild did not do to one another, and which therefore worked perfectly, because no one ever thought to check.

The map came out from under the receipts. Petra turned the lamp wick up and unfolded the parchment on the counting table, weighting the corners with four brass measures, and looked at what a dead man had bought with the last of his blood.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it—it was genuinely, undeniably beautiful, drawn in the fine confident hand of a master cartographer, in inks that shifted color slightly when the light moved: corridors of deep blue, chambers of warm amber, stairways in a green like pond-water. And it was a floor plan, unmistakably, but no building in Ashvale or any city Petra had ever walked matched it. The proportions were wrong for human habitation. The corridors bent at angles that hurt to follow with the eye. At the center, rendered in that arterial red that matched the seal, was a large round chamber, and within the chamber, drawn small and precise, was a shape Petra recognized instantly and wished they hadn't:

A crown.

Petra sat with it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Below, the salt door kept its silence, and the grey patch where the courier had died was already, Petra was almost certain, beginning to fade—as if the salt, having recorded him, was now erasing him, the way the Guild erased things, the way the Guild erased everything eventually, debts and names and whole years, all in the name of balance.

*Thirteen turns,* the man had said. *It eats him from the edges inward.*

Him. The courier hadn't said the king's name. Hadn't needed to. Everyone in Ashvale knew the king slept more than he woke these days; everyone joked about it in taverns, gently, the way you joke about a rich uncle's drinking. *Old Aldric sleeps like the dead. Old Aldric sleeps like the blessed.* And everyone knew the crown had grown strange in recent years—heavier-looking, somehow, though gold doesn't gain weight—and that the king's shadow, when he did appear at court, behaved oddly in candlelight.

Everyone knew, and no one said, because the crown was the crown, and some things you don't put in ledgers.

But this man had crawled up three steps on ruined knees to put it in a ledger. To put it in *this* ledger. In Petra's hands specifically—or had he? Had he known whose door he reached, or had the salt door simply been the nearest door that screamed?

Petra looked at the map again, and this time forced themselves to read it as a ledger-keeper reads: line by line, entry by entry, looking for the hand.

The cartographer's hand was fine and confident and utterly unfamiliar, except—

Except.

In the lower left quadrant, where the blue corridors tangled like roots, one small chamber was labeled. Every other room on the map was numbered, or marked with symbols, or left blank. But this one bore a word, written small, in ink that had gone brown with age:

*Warden.*

And the handwriting was Petra's own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. It was Petra's hand—the slight forward slant, the way the *d* looped shut, the little hook on the tail of the *n* that Petra's schoolmasters had beaten out of every other student and failed to beat out of them. Petra knew their own writing the way they knew their own heartbeat, and their own heartbeat was looking up at them off a map drawn, by the color of its inks and the style of its compass-work, at least fifty years ago.

Petra was twenty-nine years old.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the salt door settled with a sound like a sigh.

Petra sat very still for a long time, doing the arithmetic that ledger-keepers do, the cold arithmetic of assets and liabilities, and arrived at a number they didn't like.

If they reported the map, it went to the Guildmaster. Everything went to the Guildmaster; that was the architecture of the place, the whole point of the place. And the Guildmaster was a careful man, a balanced man, a man who had never in Petra's memory let a liability walk out of the building. If the map showed the way to something wearing the king's crown—if it showed the way to *anything*—the Guildmaster would want it weighed, priced, and either sold or buried, and the courier's thirteen turns would become an entry in a book, and the book would go on a shelf, and the shelf would outlive them all.

And if Petra reported the map, they would also have to report the handwriting. And then someone would ask how Petra's hand appeared on a fifty-year-old document, and Petra would have to answer honestly, and the honest answer was: *I don't know. I don't remember. There are things I don't remember, there have always been things I don't remember, and I have never once asked why, because asking why is not in my contract.*

Petra looked at the word *Warden* in their own dead-seeming hand, and felt, for the first time in ten years of faithful service, the specific vertigo of a man standing on a floor that has begun, very quietly, to tilt.

Below, faint as breath, the salt door screamed again—short, startled, the sound of a single crystal cracking.

Petra blew out the lamp, folded the map along its old creases, and went to find the false spine of the fourth-year ledger.

Some entries, they decided, wait until morning. Some entries wait a great deal longer than that.
