## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

I did not sleep. That was the first mistake, though I would not understand it was a mistake until much later, when sleeping would have been the only thing that saved me.

Instead I sat at my desk with the dead courier's map unrolled between two inkwells and a candle stub burning down toward its brass dish, and I told myself I was being careful. Careful was my religion, more than the silent god ever was. Careful had kept me alive in the Guild for ten years, and the Guild's first commandment—written nowhere, spoken never, understood by everyone who survived past their first winter—was *you do not keep what you are not supposed to have*.

The courier's blood had dried to a brown crescent along the map's lower edge. I should have wiped it away. I didn't. It seemed important, somehow, that the thing stay exactly as it had come to me—as if cleaning it might count as tampering with evidence, and evidence was what this was. Evidence of what, I couldn't yet say.

The map itself was wax-sealed vellum, good stock, Guild-issue by the watermark if you held it to the light: a crowned hand crushing a quill, the mark of the palace chancery. Which meant the courier had stolen it from the palace, or been given it by someone inside the palace, or had copied it from something that lived in the palace. Any of those three answers meant trouble. All three together meant the kind of trouble that got ledgers burned and ledger-keepers buried under new names in the salt marshes.

I turned it over twice before I let myself actually read it.

It was a floor plan. That was the first absurdity. Not a map of streets or sewers or the palace itself, which is what I'd braced for—a floor plan of a cathedral, drawn in the old style, nave and transepts and radiating chapels, each chamber labeled in a fine clerk's hand. But no cathedral in Ashvale matched it. I know them all; I've appraised enough stolen reliquaries to have the city's holy architecture memorized like a debt schedule. The proportions were wrong. The nave ran too long. The chapels numbered thirteen, and no cathedral in Christendom or anywhere else builds thirteen of anything on purpose.

And then I read the labels.

*The Vestibule of Small Regrets.* *The Nave of Ordinary Days.* *The Chapel Where Mother Sings.* *The Cloister of the Unfinished Argument.* *The Crypt of Things Sworn Never to Repeat.*

These were not rooms. I sat back from the desk, and the candle flame leaned as if I'd exhaled on it, though I hadn't. These were not rooms in any building. These were rooms in a *mind*. The king's sleeping mind—the courier had said nothing about a king, he'd said *thirteen turns*, but the seal was the palace's, and there was only one mind in Ashvale sealed well enough to need a map drawn of it.

The King's Sleep. Every child knew the phrase. King Aldric had not woken properly in years—they said he dreamed aloud now, they said his decrees came to the chancery in his own handwriting even on days he hadn't held a pen. The court called it piety. The Guild called it inventory. There were entries in our ledgers, older than my tenure, referencing tithes paid *to the Crown's Dreaming*, and I had always assumed it was a euphemism for taxes, because everything in Ashvale is eventually a euphemism for taxes.

It was not a euphemism.

I traced the nave with one finger, careful not to touch the dried blood. The vellum was warm. Not candle-warm—I checked, moved my hand through the air above it, held the back of my wrist against the desk beside it. The desk was cold. The map was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, faintly, stubbornly, from the inside.

*All right,* I told it silently. *You're impossible. You're an entry waiting to happen. Let's find out what column you go in.*

Because that was the other thing, the thing I kept circling back to while telling myself I was being methodical: everything impossible that came into Ashvale came into the Guild, and everything in the Guild went into a ledger, and every ledger answered in the end to the Guildmaster. The correct move—the *only* move, the move any survivor would make—was to carry this map up three flights of stairs at dawn, lay it on Master Corvane's desk, and say, *this came through the salt door tonight, attached to a dying man, and I thought you'd want it before the enforcers finished picking the courtyard clean.*

That was the correct move. I rehearsed it. I said it out loud once, quietly, to hear how the lie of simplicity sounded in my own voice.

It sounded fine. It sounded like ten years of practice.

I did not get up from the chair.

* * *

Here is what I did instead, for the next two hours, in the small hours when even the Guild's night-birds go quiet: I watched the map move.

Not at first. For the better part of an hour it behaved, lying flat and warm and smug under my eyes while I catalogued its chambers and tried to build a taxonomy of a stranger's regrets. The labels fell into patterns, once you looked. Grief clustered in the north transept—*the Reliquary of Her Voice*, *the Ambulatory of Last Words*. Duties lined the nave like pillars. And down past the crypt, past a chapel labeled only *Do Not Enter While He Dreams*, the plan narrowed to a single corridor running off the edge of the vellum, unfinished, as if whoever drew it had run out of either ink or courage.

Then the candle guttered—not from any draft, the room was sealed—and when the flame steadied, the map had changed.

The thirteenth chapel was gone. In its place, where it had been, stood a cloister labeled *The Long Patience*. The unfinished corridor still ran off the edge, but shorter now, as if the room at its end had drifted closer.

I did not move. I am proud of that, in retrospect; my heart was doing something arrhythmic and humiliating behind my ribs, but my hands stayed flat on the desk, and my face stayed a ledger-keeper's face, which is to say nothing at all.

"Show me again," I whispered. Stupid. Maps don't take requests.

But when I blinked—deliberately, slowly, the way you test whether a thing responds to observation—it changed again. The *Reliquary of Her Voice* had become *the Reliquary of a Voice*, and the warmth of the vellum had shifted, migrated, so that the heat now sat under my palm like a pulse.

Rooms that rearrange themselves when unobserved. I had seen enchanted objects before; the Guild traded in them weekly. A compass that points at what you fear. A mirror that shows tomorrow's weather. Those are toys, tricks, fixed enchantments wearing the costume of mystery. This was different. This wasn't an object performing an enchantment. This was a living thing being *mapped*—and resenting it, and shifting its furniture around whenever the cartographer blinked, the way a sleeper turns over when you watch them too long.

The king's sleeping mind did not want to be read. And it was awake enough, somewhere underneath, to know it was being read.

I should have stopped then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one keeps them anymore: I knew I should stop, and I can't even claim curiosity as an excuse, because curiosity implies wanting to know, and what I felt was closer to recognition—that cold vertigo of standing at the top of a stairwell in the dark, knowing the steps are there, knowing you've climbed them before, and feeling your foot reach for a step that isn't where your body remembers.

I unrolled the far edge of the map. The unfinished corridor. The room drifting closer.

And there, at the corridor's end, in a small chamber no bigger than a monk's cell, was a label in handwriting I knew.

My own.

* * *

Let me tell you what recognizing your own handwriting does to you when you have no memory of writing the words.

It doesn't feel like surprise. Surprise is fast and clean; it arrives, it leaves. This was slower. This was the sensation of a floorboard giving way beneath you a half-second after your weight has already committed—your body knows before your mind does, and for that half-second you are falling in a place you thought was solid, in a building you thought you knew, and every certainty you own goes tumbling past you into dark.

The label read: *The Chamber of the Second Oath.*

I write a particular way. Everyone does. My letters lean left, cramped, the descenders hooked—I've balanced the Guild's accounts for a decade and my hand hasn't varied a hair's width since my second year, when Master Corvane made me copy the tariff schedules forty times until my numbers stopped wandering. I could pick my handwriting out of a thousand samples. Ledger-keepers can; it's how we catch forged entries.

This was mine. The leftward lean. The hooked descenders. The little tick I put over the letter *o* without thinking, a habit I've never been able to break and never bothered to trace to its origin.

*The Chamber of the Second Oath.*

I have one oath. Everyone in the mercenary companies has one oath, sworn at the shrine, bound into the flesh—you break it, it wounds you; that's not metaphor, that's mechanics, I've seen men cough blood over broken promises and scar in the shape of their perjury. One oath, one god, one binding. The idea of a *second* oath was not just wrong. It was close to blasphemy. You do not swear twice. Swearing twice is how you tear.

Except.

Except that for ten years, every night at the shrine, when I finish the rite and the god says nothing—which it always says nothing, which is the whole shape of my faith, a decade of silence offered up like a coin I keep paying into a slot that gives nothing back—except that some nights, on the worst nights, I have caught myself adding a few words under my breath after the formal prayer ends. Words I don't decide to say. Words that arrive already formed, the way a yawn arrives, the way a name arrives on the tip of the tongue.

I had never examined those words. You don't examine the things that keep you upright.

Now I sat in my cold room with a warm map and my own handwriting staring up at me from the bottom of a dead king's dreaming mind, and I examined them. I said them aloud, slowly, for the first time, listening to myself the way you'd listen to a stranger:

*"Hold the door. Hold the door. Hold the door."*

Three words, repeated. A second oath, sworn nightly, in secret, by me, to something, for something—and I did not know what door, or for whom, or against what, or when I had sworn it the first time. Ten years of the god's silence, and underneath the silence, all along, my own voice keeping a promise I couldn't remember making.

The candle guttered again. When the light steadied, the chamber at the corridor's end had moved closer still. Close enough now that I could see, beneath the label, in smaller script, a second line I would have sworn on the Guild's entire inventory had not been there a moment before:

*She keeps it still.*

*She.* I read it four times. My handwriting, calling me *she*. The map knew me. Or something in the map knew me—or something in me had written this map, or part of it, long ago, in a life whose edges I couldn't find—and had left a message the way you leave a lamp lit in a window for someone coming home late.

The courier's blood-crescent caught the candlelight. *Thirteen turns*, he'd said. Thirteen chapels. Thirteen turns of what—the moon, the year, the lock? And why hand it to the Guild's salt door, bleeding out, unless he'd known the Guild was already inside this story somewhere? Unless he'd known that a ledger-keeper with a second oath hidden under her tongue was the only reader this document would open for?

Outside, the bells rang the hour before dawn. Somewhere above me, the Guild was waking—clerks lighting lamps, enforcers polishing the courtyard clean of last night's mess, the Guildmaster taking his first bitter cup of tea and asking, as he asked every morning, *anything come through the doors worth noting?*

The correct answer was forming itself in my mouth. I could feel it sitting there, smooth and practiced: *Nothing, master. A dying man, nothing on him. The enforcers took the body.*

One lie. I had lied to the Guildmaster once already tonight, in the courtyard, with the courier's blood still wet on the stones—said *nothing on him* while the map burned against my ribs like a second heartbeat. That lie had cost me something I couldn't name; it had gone into me like a splinter, and I could feel it working deeper with every hour I didn't report the map. Lies to the Guild compound interest. Everyone knows that too.

But here is what I kept returning to, turning over like a coin of uncertain mint: the Guildmaster's enforcers had arrived *minutes* after the courier died. Minutes. On a quiet night, at the salt door, in the dead hours. Either the Guild had eyes everywhere—or someone had told them to be there. And if someone had told them to be there, then the person who sent that courier to die at my feet had also sold him to the enforcers, and the map was meant to be recovered before I ever touched it.

Or the map was meant to reach me, and the enforcers were meant to miss it, and I was meant to sit in this room at this hour reading my own handwriting at the bottom of a king's dream.

Either way, carrying it upstairs put it in the hands of whichever answer was true.

I looked at the map a long time. The little chamber waited at the corridor's end, patient as a shrine. *She keeps it still.*

"All right," I said aloud, to the map, to the room, to whatever version of myself had written those words and trusted me to find them. "I'll hold the door."

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the vellum glowed very faintly, the way snow glows, the way things glow when they're not objects at all but openings. I folded it along its old creases, wrapped it in oilcloth, and sewed it into the lining of my coat with quick stitches, hands steady, mind anything but.

Then I sat back down at my desk, opened the current ledger to a fresh page, and made the entry I would show anyone who asked about my night:

*No acquisitions. Salt door incident referred to enforcement. Nothing further to report.*

My handwriting didn't waver once. That frightened me most of all—how easily the lie came, how natural my hand looked committing it, as though some part of me had been practicing deceit for years in a classroom I couldn't remember.

Above me, boots crossed the ceiling. The Guildmaster was early today.

He was coming down the stairs.
