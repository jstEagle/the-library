## 4. The God Who Stopped Answering

The shrine fit inside a drawer.

That was the joke Petra had made once, ten years ago, to a fellow mercenary who'd asked where they prayed. *In a drawer,* they'd said. *My god lives in a drawer.* The mercenary had laughed. Petra had not been joking. The Vow-Kindred did not build temples; temples could be burned, taxed, desecrated. They built shrines small enough to carry through a rout, small enough to hide in a boot heel, small enough that when your god went quiet you could close the drawer on it and pretend you were only putting away socks.

Petra slid the drawer open now, the way they did every night at the hour the bells of Saint Verre counted nine and always rang one extra, for reasons no one remembered.

Inside: a square of black cloth. A thimble-sized cup of salt. A coin worn smooth past all minting, so old it might have been currency in a kingdom that no longer existed, or might never have been currency at all—just a disc of metal someone had decided meant something, which was how most gods got started. And a stub of tallow candle, burned down to nearly nothing, replaced each month with another stub of nearly nothing, because the Vow-Kindred believed a god should never see you comfortable.

The ritual was seven steps. Petra had performed them every night for ten years without an answer, which either made them the most patient devotee in Ashvale or the most stubborn fool, and they had stopped caring which.

First: light the candle. The flame caught on the third strike of the flint, as it always did, as if even fire had negotiated terms with them.

Second: salt in the cup, poured in a thin line, because oaths were measured out like grain and a mercenary's word was worth exactly what it weighed.

Third: the coin, turned face-down. You showed your god its own absence. Some nights this felt like humility. Most nights it felt like an accusation.

Fourth: name the day's kept promises, aloud, in order. *I said I would balance the ledgers by moonrise, and I balanced them. I said I would pay the widow Corren her husband's borrowed year, and I paid it. I said I would not open the map again tonight, and* — Petra's jaw tightened — *I have not opened it yet.*

Fifth: name the broken ones. Tonight there was one. *I told the courier I would fetch him water.* He had died before the water came. It was not, technically, a broken promise—he had died, and death dissolved all contracts, everyone knew that—but the Vow-Kindred's god had never been a stickler for technicalities, and neither, in their bones, was Petra.

Sixth: wait.

Seventh: accept the silence.

They knelt on the cold floorboards of their lodgings above the chandler's shop, palms flat on their thighs, spine straight, and waited. Ten years ago the waiting had lasted three breaths before the answer came—a warmth behind the sternum, a pressure like a hand pressed flat against the inside of the ribs, a single syllable of presence that needed no language. *Heard. Kept. Enough.*

Tonight the waiting lasted as long as Petra let it. Two hundred candles' worth of nights, and the warmth had never come back.

"Good talk," Petra said to the drawer, and blew out the candle.

That was the discipline. That was the whole of it. Grief was a luxury for people whose gods answered, and Petra Winterbourne had work in the morning.

* * *

They had not always been a ledger-keeper. There had been a decade—no, less, four years, though it felt geological in retrospect—when Petra Winterbourne had been a blade for hire with a reputation for keeping their word, which in the mercenary trade was worth more than skill with a sword, because skill was common and honor was not.

Here is what civilians never understood about the Vow-Kindred: our oaths are not metaphors. Say *I swear I will hold this bridge until dawn*, and if you flee the bridge at midnight, dawn will find you with the bridge in you—the splinters of it grown through your forearms, the stones of it lodged under your tongue, your body becoming the promise you failed to keep, because a promise unkept has to go somewhere, and flesh is where the world files its debts.

Petra had seen a brother-of-the-oath who swore to guard a merchant's daughter and let her be taken anyway wake up with the girl's ribbon knotted through his heart muscle, visible only when they cut him open to see why he was drowning on dry land. They had seen a woman who broke a truce grow bark over half her face, the exact shape of the tree she'd sworn the truce beneath. The body keeps the books. The god merely audits.

And the god had *answered*, then. Answered constantly, irritably, the way a quartermaster answers a soldier asking for boots. Petra's oath-mark—a thin silver line circling the left wrist, appearing the day they took their vows at nineteen—would warm when a promise was sound and burn when one was strained, and twice in their career it had gone cold as riverbed stone, and both times Petra had felt the god's attention swing onto them like a lantern, vast and unimpressed, and had corrected their course immediately.

You did not lie to a god who audited your veins. Which was why, ten years ago, on the last night the god ever spoke, Petra had lied to it anyway.

* * *

It had rained that night too. Rain in memory is always convenient that way.

They had been twenty-six, four years sworn, camped in the palace undercrofts with eleven other Kindred on a contract so secret the contract itself had been sealed from them—they knew the client only as *the Crown's Steward*, a soft-voiced man whose gloves never matched his face—and the work was masonry. Binding-work. The kind the Kindred took maybe once a generation, when someone needed a thing held rather than killed or carried or found.

Held. That was the word on the writ. *To be held.* Not destroyed. Not banished. Held, the way you hold a door shut, the way you hold your breath, the way you hold a man underwater just long enough.

"We're jailers," said Maren Kest, the eldest of them, spitting into the mud. "We don't do jails."

"We do contracts," Petra had said, and hated themselves for saying it, because even then some part of them had already read the fine print of their own soul and known what it said.

The thing beneath the palace—it had no name they were given, only a designation in the steward's documents, a string of letters that hurt to read, that made the candle flames lean away—had to be bound before the turn of the season, and binding required hands. Sworn hands. Thirteen of them, because of course thirteen, because everything in that place came in unlucky numbers eventually, though Petra would not understand why for another decade.

They built the binding out of oaths. That was the craft of it, the terrible elegance: each Kindred swore a sentence, and the sentences interlocked like masonry, and the thing beneath the palace was walled in promises instead of stone, because promises cannot be tunneled through or burned or bribed. Only broken. And none of them intended to break theirs.

Except the binding needed a keystone. One oath above the others, load-bearing, the arch-stone that held the weight of all the rest. The steward looked at the twelve of them kneeling in the rain-slick dark, and then he looked at Petra, and Petra understood, with the clarity that comes only at moments you will spend the rest of your life trying to unsee, that the whole contract had been arranged around this moment, that the twelve others were scaffolding, that *the Crown's Steward* had chosen them months ago for reasons written in a hand Petra would one day recognize as their own.

"You," the steward said. "Swear it holds forever."

And here was the lie. Here was the sin Petra had carried so long it had worn smooth as the coin in the drawer: Petra swore it. Knelt in the wet dark with the thing below them pressing up against the fresh-laid walls of their comrades' promises like a drowned man against ice, and Petra swore *it holds forever*, and the oath sank into place, and the binding sealed, and the thing below stopped screaming—

—and Petra had never meant it.

Not entirely. Not in the marrow. Because in the instant before speaking, Petra had felt the god's attention come down on them like weather, had felt the audit begin, and had thought, in a flash of cowardice dressed up as prudence: *Forever is a long time. Forever is not mine to give. If I swear it and it fails, the wound will kill me, and if I refuse, the steward will find another hand, and what kind of soldier refuses the crown?*

So they had sworn with a reservation hidden in the cellar of their heart, a hairline crack in the keystone, and the god—who heard everything, who audited everything—had heard it.

The oath-mark had not burned. That was the horror of it. It should have burned; a flawed vow should sear like a brand. Instead the silver line had gone perfectly, terribly silent, and then the warmth behind Petra's sternum—the constant low warmth of being watched over, accounted for, *kept*—had simply withdrawn, the way a tide withdraws, so gradually that at first Petra thought they were imagining it, and then the prayers started going unanswered, one night, ten nights, a hundred, and by winter Petra understood:

The god had not abandoned them. The god had *hidden* them. A cracked keystone, discovered, would shatter the whole binding—and whatever was sealed below would know exactly which hand to reach for. So the god had done the only merciful thing available: it had stopped answering, stopped marking, stopped *knowing* Petra, severing the thread between them so thoroughly that no parasite crawling through the kingdom's oaths could follow it back to the flaw.

Silence, as sanctuary. Silence as a wall thrown up around a fugitive.

Or so Petra had decided, somewhere around year three of the silence, constructing the theology the way a starving man constructs a meal from wallpaper. It was a story. It was possibly even true. But it was also, Petra admitted to the empty room, the story of a coward explaining a punishment as a gift.

Because there was another version. In the other version, the god had simply seen what Petra was—a person who would swear forever while keeping a door open in the back of their mind—and had closed its account and walked away, and the silence was not sanctuary but contempt, going on ten years, patient as interest.

Petra had never been able to prove which version was true. That was the cruelest part. The god had taken the evidence with it.

* * *

Now they sat back on their heels in front of the open drawer, the candle smoke curling up toward the ceiling beams, and let themselves do the thing they permitted exactly once a year, on the anniversary: they spoke to it.

"I kept the rest," Petra said quietly. "Ten years. Every promise since, kept to the letter. You'd audit clean, if you were listening. I've made myself into someone you could—" They stopped. Started again, lower. "There's a map now. Of a mind. And my handwriting in it, from a night I don't remember, and I think—I think it goes back to the undercrofts. To what we held. To what I *held*."

The candle stayed out. The coin stayed face-down. The salt lay in its little line, weighing nothing, meaning everything.

"If you're hiding me," Petra said, "then I'm sorry, but I'm coming out of hiding. Someone has to unbind the king, and apparently the binding has my signature on it." A breath. "And if you left—if you looked at what I am and walked—"

They couldn't finish that one. Ten years of discipline, and it still caught in the throat like a fishhook.

Instead Petra did the seventh step. Accepted the silence. Closed the drawer on the god the size of a prayer, the god the size of a grudge, and stood, knees cracking, thirty-six years old with an old soldier's knees and a liar's steady hands.

And that was when they saw it.

On the floor beside the shrine-drawer, where nothing had been when they knelt: a single line of salt, spilled across the floorboards, running from beneath the drawer to beneath the door. As if something had been poured. As if something had *walked*.

Petra crouched. Touched it. It was warm.

Their oath-mark—the dead silver circle, ten years cold—was warming too. Faintly. Unsteadily. Like a pulse returning after a long faint, or like a signal lit far away by someone who wanted them to know, before whoever else was coming arrived, that they had never actually been alone.

Downstairs, very softly, the chandler's shop bell rang.

At the ninth-hour-plus-one bell. When no customer had ever come, in six years of lodging above the shop, except the ones who knew the Guild's private hours.

Petra put out their hand, and the salt line brightened along its whole length, pointing—not to the door, not to the window, but down. Toward the undercrofts. Toward the buried places.

Toward the cathedral.

"*Now* you talk," Petra whispered—and reached for their coat, and the map sewn into its lining, and the first stair into the dark.
