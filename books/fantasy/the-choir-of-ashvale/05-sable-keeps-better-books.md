## 5. Sable Keeps Better Books

The Guild's ledgers balanced to the copper on the last night of the ninth moon, and Sable Vane was the only person in Ashvale who found that suspicious.

She sat in the counting room with a lamp turned low and the day-book open before her, running her thumb down the column of entries like a woman reading a lover's letters. Around her the Guild slept—or pretended to. The Salt House creaked as it settled against its own foundations, that old sound like a ship at anchor, and somewhere below, the tide moved through the flooded crypts where they kept the inventory that couldn't be kept anywhere else. Bottled grief doesn't like sunlight. Borrowed years curdle if you store them above ground. Everything impossible about Ashvale lived below the waterline, and everything honest lived above it, which told you most of what you needed to know about honesty.

Sable had been ledger-keeper for six years, which made her the second-best in the building. She knew this the way she knew her own pulse: not as opinion but as fact. The best was Winterbourne, up on the third floor, who kept accounts so clean they were almost a form of prayer, and who had never once, in all the years Sable had watched them, made an error worth catching.

That was the problem. Nobody was that clean. Cleanliness like Petra's wasn't accuracy—it was concealment with good posture.

Sable turned back to the courier rolls.

Every parcel the Guild moved came through the rolls: name of carrier, name of sender, nature of the cargo written in the Guild's private shorthand, fee, date, seal. Nine hundred entries a season. She'd been through them twice tonight, once forward and once backward, because numbers lie differently depending on which direction you catch them walking.

And there it was. Or rather—there it wasn't.

Entry four-forty-one. The line existed; she could see the ghost of it, the faint depression left in the paper where a quill had pressed before someone scraped the vellum down with a knife and burnished it smooth. A removal done by hand, done recently—the fibers around the scar still stood up slightly, still caught the lamplight wrong. Whoever had erased it had been careful. Careful, but hurried. And they'd missed one thing, because everyone always missed one thing.

On the facing page, bleed-through. Mirror-written, faint as breath on glass, but legible if you held the page to the lamp at an angle and read it like a left-handed child.

*Courier: Maren Ostreicher. Cargo: sealed map, wax, no docket. Sender: —*

The sender's name hadn't bled through. But *Maren Ostreicher* had, and Sable said the name aloud once, quietly, tasting it, because names were her trade too and this one felt like a coin from a country she'd never visited.

Then she went looking for Maren Ostreicher in every other book in the room.

Not in the carrier rolls. Not in the wage book. Not in the death register, which was the interesting absence, because the Guild recorded deaths the way churches recorded baptisms—religiously, and with an eye toward what the family might owe. A courier died on Guild business, there was a page for it. Widow's stipend, gear recovery, a small cold entry closing the account. Sable had written enough of those pages herself. She knew their shape the way you know the shape of a key you've cut a hundred times.

There was no shape here at all. Maren Ostreicher had walked into the Guild's books carrying a sealed map with no docket, and had then walked out of existence without leaving so much as a receipt.

Sable closed the day-book and sat very still for a while, listening to the Salt House breathe.

Here was the thing about being charming: it worked best when nobody could tell whether you meant it. Sable Vane smiled easily, laughed at the right moments, remembered the names of enforcers' children and the vintages the Guildmaster pretended not to favor. People called her pleasant the way they'd call a well-made lock pleasant—admiring the craft, trusting the function. And underneath the charm, running quiet as groundwater, was the simple engine of her: *find out what people are hiding, and hold it.* Not out of malice. Malice was wasteful. Out of the same instinct that made her balance a column twice. The world was full of unrecorded debts. Somebody ought to keep track.

She lit a second lamp, took out fresh paper, and began her audit properly.

First question: when had the entry been removed? She checked the ink on neighboring lines—standard issue, iron-gall, browning on schedule—and then the scrape itself. The vellum under the scar was still pale. Fresh work. Within the week, probably within three days.

Second question: who had access? The day-book lived in the counting room, locked, two keys. One hung on the Guildmaster's belt. The other—

The other hung on a hook outside Petra Winterbourne's door, because Winterbourne refused to carry keys. *A key is a promise you can lose,* they'd said once, in that flat careful voice of theirs, and Sable had filed the sentence away the way she filed everything, under *things people say that are really confessions.*

Third question: why would the Guild's cleanest hand erase a courier?

Sable leaned back and let herself imagine the obvious answer first, because the obvious answer is usually wearing a disguise and it helps to look at it plainly. Petra had taken something off the books. Smuggling, skimming, a private transaction—a courier paid in cash to move an item the Guild would want taxed or seized. Greed, basically. It happened. Even the devout skimmed; piety and theft shared a long border.

But the obvious answer didn't fit, and Sable trusted misfits more than fits. If Petra were skimming, they'd have done it cleanly—no scrape, no scar, no bleed-through. They'd have rewritten the whole page in a matching hand, or better, arranged for the entry never to exist. What had happened here was panic. Someone had killed a line in a hurry, badly, and the someone kept two keys and one of them was currently lying to the entire building with their immaculate columns and their ten years of perfect silence.

Because that was the other thing about Petra Winterbourne, the thing the whole Guild knew and no one said: they'd stopped speaking to their god a decade ago, and they'd stayed anyway. Who stays loyal to a god that won't answer? Either a fool or a penitent. And Sable had audited fools before. Fools were messy. Winterbourne's messes were archaeological—they were buried deep and ancient and swept over with such care that you only ever found the edges.

Like now. Like a scraped line and a mirror-name bleeding through vellum.

Sable wrote *Maren Ostreicher* on her private sheet, and beneath it: *map, sealed, no docket*, and beneath that: *thirteen?*

That last word she'd gotten from a drunk astronomer two nights ago, one of the Guild's debtors, babbling happily into his third stolen cup about lunar tables that wouldn't reconcile. The moon was coming around early, he'd said. Thirteenth turn inside the year instead of after it. He'd thought it was funny. Sable hadn't laughed, because she'd seen the word *thirteen* scratched into the margin of a shipping manifest three weeks prior, in handwriting she hadn't recognized, and she'd been waiting since for the number to surface again. Numbers surfacing twice weren't coincidence. Numbers surfacing twice were a current, and something was swimming upstream in it.

She blew out the second lamp and considered her options, of which there were three.

Option one: report the anomaly to the Guildmaster. Honest, correct, and fatal to the hunt. The moment she filed it, the enforcers would take the inquiry, the inquiry would become a raid, the raid would become ash and blood, and whatever Winterbourne was carrying—whatever *map*—would end up in a strongbox under the Salt House, catalogued and dead. Sable would get a commendation and a bonus and the particular emptiness of a solved case she hadn't understood.

Option two: confront Petra directly. Tempting. Also stupid. You didn't show your hand to a penitent with a soldier's shoulders and a god-shaped hole where their conscience used to be. You watched. You let them run, because runners revealed their routes.

Option three—

Option three was the one her fingers were already reaching for, because option three was the fun one, and Sable had learned long ago that when all her instincts pointed at the fun one, it usually meant the fun one was also the true one.

She took a clean report form from the drawer, dipped her pen, and lied.

It was a good lie—economical, verifiable in its dull parts, boring in the way that makes officials stop reading. *Discrepancy noted in courier rolls, entry 441, likely clerical error during seasonal transfer. Corrective entry appended. No further action recommended.* She signed it, dated it, and in the morning it would go into the Guildmaster's tray and buy her exactly what she wanted: a quiet week. No enforcers sniffing at the counting room. No questions about why the second-best ledger-keeper in Ashvale was suddenly interested in dead couriers. Just seven days of unwatched time, hers alone, to find out what Maren Ostreicher had been carrying and who had killed her name.

Because Sable was quite sure the courier was dead. You didn't erase a living person from the rolls. Living people could talk, could resurface, could sue. You erased the dead, because the dead were the only ones whose accounts could actually be closed.

She blotted the false report, squared its edges, and set it where the morning clerk would find it. Then she sat a moment longer in the dark with her private sheet, and allowed herself the small dishonesty of admitting what she was really doing.

This wasn't about the Guild. The Guild could drown in its own salt water for all she cared. This was about the shape of the erasure—the haste of it, the fear in it. Someone in this building had been frightened enough of a map to kill a line of text over it, and frightened people with maps were either victims or conspirators, and either way, whatever was coming for them would eventually come for everyone standing nearby. Sable Vane intended to be standing nearby, but holding the leash.

She folded her notes into her sleeve, pinched out the lamp, and let her eyes adjust to the dark the way they'd learned to in rooms she'd grown up in that she did not think about.

As she reached the counting-room door, she heard footsteps on the stair. Soft ones. Measured. The particular tread of someone climbing carefully at an hour when careful climbing means guilt.

Sable stepped back into the shadow of the shelving and waited, and sure enough, a figure crossed the landing below—third floor, hook by the door, a key lifted and returned in less time than a heartbeat. Even in the dark she knew the silhouette: straight-backed, close-cropped, moving like a person rehearsing innocence.

Petra Winterbourne, come down in the night to check on a corpse they'd buried in vellum.

Sable smiled in the dark, alone, where no one could see it and no one could mistake it for kindness.

*Run, then,* she thought, listening to the footsteps recede upward. *You've got seven days.*

She gave it until the count of fifty, then climbed the stairs herself, past the third-floor hook, and paused at Winterbourne's door just long enough to confirm what she already knew: lamplight under the door, and beneath it, faintly, the smell of hot wax.

Someone was sealing a letter. Or opening one.

Or pressing a map flat between sheets of blotting paper, trying to decide whether to keep it, sell it, or burn it—and doing it badly enough that the whole building could smell the candle.

Sable went to bed smiling, and in the morning she filed her lie, and the hunt began.

* * *

The astronomer's name was Pell Wick, and he owed the Guild eleven years, which he was paying off at the rate of star-charts and bad news.

Sable found him at noon in the observatory loft above the chandlery, squinting through a brass tube at a sky that didn't deserve the attention. She brought wine. It loosened him faster than threats would have, and cost less.

"The thirteenth turn," she said, pouring. "When?"

He took the cup with the hands of a man who'd stopped pretending he wasn't an alcoholic sometime in the previous decade. "Early," he said. "Dawn after next, if my tables are right. Which they are, which is the problem." He drank. "Moons don't hurry, mistress. Something's pulling."

"Something," Sable repeated. "Such as?"

"Such as I don't know, and such as I'd like not to." He looked at her then, and behind the drink his eyes were clear and frightened. "You're the second person to ask me that this week."

The wine went cold in Sable's mouth. "Who was the first?"

"Courier woman. Ostreicher." Pell shrugged. "Paid me in silver to copy my tables. Never picked them up."

Sable set down her cup very carefully, the way you do when your hands have opinions you'd rather not share.

Seven days, she'd given herself. Standing in the counting room last night, it had felt generous.

Now, watching the astronomer flinch at his own arithmetic, watching the light come through the loft window at the wrong angle for the hour, she understood that the deadline had never belonged to her.

Dawn after next. Two days.

She thanked Pell, left the bottle, and went down the stairs two at a time, already composing in her head the message she'd slip under a certain door on the third floor—because the hunt was over, and the race had begun, and there was exactly one person in Ashvale who knew where the map was going.

*Meet me,* she'd write. *I know about Maren. I know about the moon. And I'm not reporting either one.*

It wasn't blackmail. Sable preferred to think of it as an opening bid.
