## 6. The Thirteenth Moon Rises Early

The astronomer lived above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, in a room that smelled of tallow and old paper, and he answered the door before Petra finished knocking.

"I knew you'd come," he said. "I've been waiting three days."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know the moon is wrong." He stepped back to let them in, moving with the careful economy of a man who had learned not to trust his own legs. His name was Emeric Voss, and he owed the Guild eleven years—borrowed against a body that was now spending them faster than he'd liked. He'd bought them back at forty to see a comet, and the comet had disappointed him, and now he had perhaps two years left and a lifetime of bitterness about it. "Sit. No—don't sit. Look."

He pulled back the curtain over his workbench, and there it was: charts, dozens of them, layered one atop another like the strata of some geological crime. Lunar tables going back decades. Petra had seen such things before—the Guild kept astronomers the way it kept everyone, on hooks made of debt—but they had never seen a man look like this. Voss looked like a priest whose god had started lying.

"Show me," Petra said.

His finger came down on the tables, tapping each column in turn. "Here. The moon's synodic period. Twenty-nine days, twelve hours, forty-four minutes. It has been that, within a margin you could fit inside a heartbeat, for as long as men have counted." Tap. "Three months ago, it shortened by six minutes. I assumed instrument error. I recalibrated everything." Tap. "Two months ago, another nine minutes. Last month, fourteen." He turned, and his eyes were wet and furious. "This month, Petra Winterbourne, the moon reached full four hours and eleven minutes early. And the shortening is accelerating. It isn't drift. It's *hunger*. Something is pulling on the calendar itself."

Petra stood very still. Outside, the chandler's boy was singing a nonsense song about a pig and a peddler, and the ordinary sound of it made the room feel like the bottom of a well.

"The map said thirteen turns," they said carefully. "Thirteen turns of the moon."

"And how many turns did you think you had?"

"Thirteen."

Voss laughed—a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it anywhere. "You have seven. Perhaps eight, if the acceleration holds steady, which it won't. It's compounding. Whatever cycle your map is counting, it's counting down faster than the sky promised." He gathered the topmost chart and pressed it into Petra's hands, unasked. "Take it. Proof costs me nothing now; I'm already dead on an installment plan. But listen to me." He gripped their wrist, and his fingers were cold as coins. "When the thirteenth moon rises early, it will not rise alone. Cycles don't shorten because time is generous. Something down there—" he tapped the floor, the street, the buried bones of Ashvale generally, "—is *pulling*."

* * *

Petra walked back through the city without hurrying, which took discipline.

Every instinct screamed at them to run—to take the map sewn into their coat lining and go east past the walls until Ashvale was a smudge of smoke behind them. But running was what guilty people did, and the Guild watched for guilty people the way spiders watch for anything that trembles. So they walked. They bought chestnuts from a brazier-cart and ate them slowly. They paused to watch a fishmonger gut a river pike with surgical indifference. They let the map's weight press against their ribs like a second heart, and they thought about Emeric Voss's cold fingers, and about the word *hunger*, and about the fact that somewhere above them the moon was being dragged out of its ancient rhythm by something that wore a king's face during the day.

Seven turns. Maybe eight.

They had spent ten years learning to be invisible, and invisibility had rules: never hurry, never linger, never be interesting. But under the rules, older than the rules, was the thing they had been before—a mercenary with an oath-shrine scarred into their forearm and a god who no longer answered. That thing understood deadlines. That thing knew that when the clock starts eating itself, caution becomes just a slower way to die.

At the corner of Tallow Street, they felt it: a wrongness in the crowd's texture. Not eyes on them—worse. Eyes *avoiding* them. Two men by the fountain who were very interested in the water. A woman mending a net who hadn't looked at her net once.

Enforcers didn't follow. Enforcers *arrived*, all at once, everywhere, like weather.

Petra turned down a side lane, unhurried, and began to count doors.

* * *

Their lodgings were three rooms above a dyer's shop, chosen five years ago for exactly this moment: two exits, a flat roof shared with the building behind, a landlord paid in silence rather than coin. Petra climbed the stairs at a tenant's tired pace, key already in hand, and let themselves in.

The room had been searched.

Not ransacked—searched. Every book reshelved a half-inch off true. The chair angled differently. Their spare shirt folded by someone else's hands. On the table, centered with deliberate courtesy, sat a single white feather, and beneath it a card bearing the Guildmaster's seal.

Petra didn't touch either. They stood in the doorway and read the room the way they read ledgers, line by line. The bed: undisturbed, but the mattress dipped wrong at one corner—heavier. Under the window, the floorboard that creaked had been oiled. Someone wanted them to notice the feather and not notice the rest.

Someone wanted them to run *specifically*.

Or—and this thought arrived cold and clear—or someone wanted them to stay, and the feather was the trap's other half.

Ten years ago, Petra would have known instantly. Ten years ago they had instincts honed on real battlefields, before they'd traded blood-work for bookwork and taught themselves to be small. Ten years ago there had also been a god in their ear, quiet as a hand on the shoulder, saying *left* or *wait* or *now*.

The shrine-scar on their forearm ached. It always ached lately, since the courier died whispering numbers against their palm. As if the silence itself were thinning.

*Now,* said nothing, in a voice they almost remembered.

Petra moved.

They went through the window, not the door—out onto the dyer's roof, where bolts of drying cloth hung in long colored sheets, blue and madder-red and saffron, snapping in the wind like a fleet at sail. Behind them, below, the salt door of the stairwell boomed open. Boots. Three sets, maybe four, taking the stairs at parade pace, which meant they'd stopped pretending.

Petra ran.

The rooftops of Ashvale at dusk were a country unto themselves—slate ridges and lead valleys, chimney forests, the great dark shoulders of warehouses, clotheslines strung between parishes like the rigging of a drowned ship. Petra crossed them the way they'd once crossed no-man's-land: fast, low, committed. A tile cracked under their heel and skittered away into the dark, and they didn't stop to mourn it. Behind them a whistle sounded—one note, high, repeated—and was answered from the west, then the north. A triangle closing.

They dropped through a gap between chimneys, rolled across a felted roof still warm from the day's sun, and came up running along a ridgepole with the whole city spread out below: lamplighters working their rounds, the river a black ribbon stitched with bridge-lights, and above it all, rising fat and pale over the eastern wall—

The moon.

Nearly full. Four days early. Hanging in the sky like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

*Something is pulling,* Voss had said. Looking at it now, swollen and slightly wrong-colored, faintly bruised at one edge like fruit going over, Petra believed him completely. It didn't look like a moon. It looked like a countdown.

A crossbow bolt shattered slate a hand's width from their head.

Petra threw themselves sideways off the ridge, caught a drainpipe, slid, hit the next roof rolling, and came up with their shoulder screaming and the map a hard little rectangle against their ribs. Above, silhouetted against the too-early moon, an enforcer in Guild grey reloaded with terrible calm. Petra knew him vaguely—Harrow, a big quiet man who once, years ago, had bought them soup when they worked late. He sighted again.

Petra ran.

They ran through the laundry lines of Weavers' Walk, bursting through hanging sheets that billowed after them like ghosts giving chase. They ran the spine of the tannery, leaping vats that breathed chemical steam into the night. Twice more bolts came, and twice the roofs saved them—Ashvale's crooked skyline, built by a thousand years of builders who never agreed on anything, was a maze only the desperate bothered to learn. They cut south, always south, toward the river district, toward the debtors' taverns and the tangled lanes where even enforcers moved carefully, and with every stride the panic in their chest burned down into something harder and cleaner.

Resolve. That was the old word for it. The feeling of having decided, finally, beyond any appeal.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Petra had always been good at arithmetic: the map could not be reported, because the Guild had built the crown's prison—that was the shape the evidence made, however much they flinched from it. The map could not be hidden, because seven moons was not enough time to hide anything from an organization that traded in stolen names. Which left one option, the one they'd been walking toward since a dying man pressed wax into their hands:

Carry it. Through the undercrofts. To the buried cathedral. Before the thirteenth moon rose early and hungry over a kingdom that had forgotten what it owed.

And they could not do it alone.

That thought should have frightened them more than Harrow's crossbow. It didn't. It settled into place with the grim rightness of a final entry in a balanced ledger.

They dropped from the last roof into a tanner's yard, landed badly, and lay in the reeking dark listening to the whistles fade westward, hunting a ghost that had already gone. Overhead, through the smoke-holes, the bruised moon climbed.

Seven turns. Maybe fewer.

Somewhere in the debtors' quarter, in a tavern called the Salt Cellar, a red-haired ledger-keeper was drinking on the Guild's coin and keeping better books than anyone knew. Petra got up, wiped the slate dust and blood from their hands, and went to find her.
