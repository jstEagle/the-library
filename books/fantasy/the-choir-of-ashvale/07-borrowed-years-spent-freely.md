## 7. Borrowed Years, Spent Freely

Sable Vane had been keeping books since she was nine years old, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other: the interesting entries are never the ones people write down. They are the ones people erase.

The courier's death had left a hole in the Guild rolls the size of a fist. A man named Corren Hale, registered as a runner eleven years ago, paid out of the Salt Door account quarterly, marked *reliable* in a clerk's careful hand—and then nothing. No termination entry. No death notice. No final payment to next of kin, which the Guild always made, because the Guild was sentimental about its dead in exactly the way that cost it nothing. Someone had taken a knife to the ledger page itself, and Sable had found the ghost of the cut: a faint ridge where the blade had skipped, a half-moon of paper gone from the corner.

She had spent three days on that half-moon of paper. She had pulled the delivery schedules, cross-checked the salt ledgers against the wine accounts, and traced Corren Hale's last recorded route through the Tanners' Quarter on the night he died. And at the end of all that arithmetic she had arrived at an answer so simple it embarrassed her: whoever erased him had done it from inside Petra Winterbourne's own desk.

Which meant either Petra had killed a courier and hidden the body—or someone had died pressing something into Petra's hands, and Petra had chosen silence over report.

Sable knew which possibility she preferred. She had known for years, if she was honest, which was a thing she almost never was. She preferred it the way you prefer a door left unlocked: not because you intend to walk through it, but because you like knowing you could.

So she waited. She did her work, balanced her columns, smiled at the enforcers in the corridor, and filed her weekly audit with a line about irregularities in the Tanner's Quarter accounts that would occupy the Guildmaster's clerks for a week. Then she followed the only thread that hung loose from the knot: where does a hunted mercenary drink when they think no one is watching?

The answer was the Drowned Bell, a debtors' tavern sunk half below street level in the Ropewalk district, where the ceiling sweated and the wine came in chipped cups and the interest rates were written on the wall in chalk, updated nightly like tide tables. It was the kind of place where a person could be anonymous for the price of two coppers, or recognized for the price of one wrong glance.

Petra Winterbourne took the far table by the dripping wall, back to the corner, face to the room. Old soldier habits. Sable approved professionally and noted, with some private irritation, that she found it attractive anyway.

She bought a bottle of Vellish red off the taverner—paid full price, which hurt more than any theft would have—and then, on her way to the table, simply didn't put it down when the taverner reached for the second cup. Stolen wine tasted better. Everyone knew that. It was practically accounting.

"Mind if I ruin your evening?" she said.

Petra looked up at her with the expression of a person watching a trap close around their own foot and finding it, against all sense, comfortable. "Sable Vane."

"You know my name."

"I keep accounts." Petra turned their cup slowly on the table. "You're in mine. Column headed *problems*."

"I'm flattered. Most people file me under *later*." She sat down uninvited, set the bottle between them, and poured two cups without asking. "Drink. It's stolen, so it's good."

"I don't drink with the Guild."

"This isn't the Guild. This is me, off the clock, committing petty larceny." She slid a cup across. "There's a difference. The Guild would have sent enforcers. I sent myself. Consider it a courtesy."

"A courtesy," Petra repeated flatly.

"I'm going to say a name," Sable said, "and I want to watch your face while I say it. Corren Hale."

She watched. She was very good at watching; it was most of her job. And what she saw was not guilt. It was grief, arriving a beat late, like thunder after lightning—the face of someone who had already mourned and been interrupted mid-funeral. Petra's hand stopped moving on the cup. Their jaw tightened once, and then they made themselves relax it, which was worse. Guilt doesn't make itself relax. Grief does, because grief has practice.

"He was erased from the rolls," Sable said quietly. "Not crossed out. Erased. Paper cut out with a knife, and the knife-work was done at your desk, Winterbourne, because the blade caught on the third nail from the left in your drawer and everyone in this building knows that drawer sticks. So before you decide how much to lie to me, understand that I've already done the arithmetic. You're not deciding whether I know. You're deciding what I know."

For a long moment Petra said nothing. The tavern noise washed over them both—the clink of cups, a debtors' argument going sour in the corner, the chalk boy updating the wall. Somewhere above them a cart rolled past and the ceiling shed a fine rain of dust into Sable's cup. She drank it anyway.

"If I tell you," Petra said finally, "you'll have to report it. It's your job."

"Yes."

"And if you don't report it, it's your neck."

"Yes," Sable said again, and smiled, and let the smile do the work her words weren't ready for. "Aren't we a pair. Two people whose entire lives consist of things they'd be killed for."

"Why aren't the enforcers here?" Petra asked. "You found the gap. You could have brought them. Why a bottle of wine instead?"

Because, Sable thought, because I read the route Corren Hale took the night he died and it wasn't a delivery route, it was a flight path, and because whatever he carried was worth killing a registered runner and mutilating a ledger over, and because the Guildmaster's clerks have been pulling palace records for six months under cover of a tax audit, and because I have a brand on my wrist that says I used to be property and I swore, in the only prayer I've ever managed, that I would never again carry anything for the people who owned me—

What she said was: "Because I want the crown gone too."

Petra went very still.

It was small, the reaction—a stillness rather than a start—but Sable had spent years reading entries that weren't written down, and stillness was the loudest entry there was. Petra Winterbourne had just confirmed everything.

"The crown," Petra said carefully.

"Don't do that. Don't give me the blank face. I said I want it gone and you went quiet as a closed book, which means I guessed right, which means there's something to guess." She leaned forward. "I don't know what you're carrying. I don't need to know yet. But I know the shape of it. Something came out of the palace, or pointed at it, and now you're running from your own Guild with it sewn somewhere on your person, and you haven't gone to the king's men, which means the king's men can't be trusted, which means—" she spread her hands—"the crown itself is the problem. Am I warm?"

"You're warm enough to burn us both."

"Good. I run hot. It's a character flaw."

Petra studied her across the cup, and Sable let herself be studied. That was part of the offer, though Petra couldn't know it yet: here is my face, unguarded, memorize it, use it against me if you must. Trust was a currency and she was making a deposit she might never recover, and doing it anyway, which frightened her more than the enforcers did.

"Why?" Petra asked. "Why do you want the crown gone? Everyone in Ashvale wants something from the crown. Nobody wants it *gone*. Gone means chaos. Gone means the oaths fray. Gone means—"

"Gone means free," Sable said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she'd planned. She softened it with effort. "Look. I'll trade you. One truth each. That's the whole offer. Not confession—not trust, gods, no, I wouldn't trust you to water my plants—one truth each, sworn, and then we decide whether we're partners or whether I walk out of here and forget your face."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I finish my wine, I leave, and tomorrow morning the Guildmaster learns that his missing courier died at the Salt Door with his throat open, and that you lied about it in your incident report, and you spend the rest of your short life learning what Guild enforcers do to liars." She shrugged. "I'm not threatening you, Winterbourne. I'm describing Tuesday."

The silence stretched. Sable counted it the way she counted everything—in costs. Petra was costing themselves something right now; she could see the arithmetic running behind their eyes, the weighing of a stranger against a grave.

"One truth each," Petra said at last. "Sworn how?"

"However you swear things. You're the mercenary. You people have rituals for everything."

Something moved through Petra's face then—something old and tired and wounded—that Sable didn't have the map to read. "We do," they said quietly. "All right. Me first, since you're holding the knife."

They straightened, laid their palm flat on the scarred table between them, and said, in a voice that had changed registers entirely—lower, older, worn smooth like a stair stone:

"What I hold true: a man named Corren Hale died at the Salt Door four nights ago with his blood on my hands, in the sense that I held him while it left him. He pressed a sealed thing into my keeping and told me the moon turns thirteen times, and then he died before he could tell me the rest. I did not kill him. I hid him from the Guild because the Guild cannot be allowed to have what he carried. That is my truth. Sworn on the god who no longer answers me, may it cost me what it costs."

The candle flame between them bent sideways, though there was no draft. Sable felt the hair rise on her arms and told herself firmly that it was the damp.

She had expected many truths. She had prepared responses to several. What she had not prepared for was the way Petra's voice cracked, just slightly, on *the god who no longer answers me*—the sound of a wound being described in clinical language by someone who had forgotten it wasn't clinical.

"My turn," she said, before she could lose her nerve.

She put her palm on the table opposite theirs. Her wrist, she noticed, was turned so the brand faced down. Habit. Ten years of habit.

"What I hold true: I was Guild property until I was nineteen. Bought, trained, bound. The brand on my wrist isn't decorative. I bought my freedom with seven years of ledger work and one act of arson I've never been charged for, and the first thing I did as a free woman was swear I would never again serve anything that wore a crown of anyone's making." She paused. Here it was. The edge of the real thing. "And the reason I want yours gone specifically—the truth underneath the truth—is that my mother sold years to the crown. Sold them through the Guild, properly, all stamped and legal, to pay a debt she never told me about. She aged twenty years in one winter and died smiling, because the contract required her to be satisfied with the price. I was fourteen. I've read the contract since. The buyer's seal on it was the privy seal of King Aldric's household."

She lifted her palm from the table. "That's mine. Sworn on nothing, because I don't have a god. Sworn on arithmetic, which has never once lied to me."

The candle flame stood up straight again.

Petra was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—too raw for pity, too soft for alliance, too much like the beginning of something she had no column for. "Your mother's years," Petra said slowly. "Bought by the crown."

"Bought by the crown," Sable agreed. "And I have spent ten years telling myself it was just commerce. Just the trade. Just the way things work." She laughed, short and ugly. "And then a courier dies at your feet talking about moons, and you go silent as a tomb when I mention the crown, and suddenly I'm wondering what else the crown has been buying. What else it's been *eating*, Winterbourne. Because I've kept these books for fifteen years and I will tell you what I know that nobody else knows: the impossible trades are getting worse. Years come back thinner than they went out. Names come back frayed. Grief comes back *used*. Something upstream of us is drinking, and everything I've ever balanced is downstream of its mouth."

Petra's hand had gone to their coat—to the left breast, briefly, unconsciously, the gesture of a person touching a wound or a secret—and Sable pretended not to see, and loved them a little for doing it, and hated herself for the word *loved*, and poured more wine to drown all of it.

"So," she said. "Partners?"

"That depends." Petra turned the cup again. "On what happens when this ends. When the moon turns, or whatever it does. When you get what you want. What then?"

"Then I write it up honestly," Sable said. "In my own ledger, in my own hand, and I file it nowhere, because I'm burning my filing cabinet and starting a new system. Honestly, Winterbourne, what do you want to happen? You want absolution? From whom? The Guild? Your god?" She saw the flinch and gentled, which was rare enough that it surprised her coming out of her own mouth. "Here's what I can promise. I promise you this: I am better at this than you are. I will find the doors you can't find, read the pages you can't read, and lie to people so beautifully they'll thank me. And when it goes wrong—and it will go wrong—I will not sell you. Whatever else I am, I have never once sold a person, and I was raised by people who sold everything."

Petra was quiet for a long time. Outside, the cart traffic had thinned; the chalk boy was erasing the day's rates and writing tomorrow's, and the numbers climbing the wall in fresh white were higher than yesterday's, higher than last week's, the whole city borrowing against a dawn it might not reach.

"One condition," Petra said finally.

"Name it."

"We don't lie to each other. Not about the work. We can lie to everyone else—we should, professionally, constantly—but between us, about the work, no lies. Small truths whenever they're needed. I've spent ten years being lied to by a god and a decade lying to a Guild, and I can't build anything on top of that. If we do this, we do it clean between ourselves."

Sable considered pointing out that they had just met, that *clean* was a strong word for two strangers and a stolen bottle, that the entire architecture of her life was load-bearing lies. Instead she heard herself say:

"Agreed. With one amendment. We each keep one thing back—anything, our choice, no questions—and we name it now, so we both know the shape of the hole even if not what's in it." She held up her hand before Petra could speak. "Mine is named already. You heard it tonight. There's a reason I picked this fight, a reason I've been circling you for three days, and it isn't in anything I've said, and I'm not ready to pay it yet. But I'll tell you it exists. That's the deal. Full honesty about the location of every locked door."

Petra looked at her for a long moment, and something in their face shifted—the look of a person deciding to stop counting exits.

"There's a room," Petra said slowly, "on a thing I'm carrying. A room with my handwriting on it. My handwriting, Sable, from years ago, and I don't remember writing it. That's my locked door. That's the shape of my hole. I don't know what's behind it, but I know I built the lock."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"Then we're agreed," Sable said softly. "Two liars, one truth each, and a list of locked doors we've promised to knock on eventually."

"To eventual knocking." Petra lifted the chipped cup.

She touched hers to it. The clay clicked, cheap and absurd, the least ceremonial oath ever sworn in the history of Ashvale, and Sable felt it bind anyway—felt it settle into her ribs like a swallowed coal. Oaths, she thought. The city ran on them. They leaked out of everyone, all day, every day, into the air and the walls and the crown, wherever oaths went when they left a mouth. She wondered, not for the first time, where hers went. She wondered if they pooled somewhere. She wondered if anything down there was getting fat on them.

She was still wondering when the tavern door banged open and the cold came in ahead of three men in grey oilcloth who moved like people paid by the hour to find someone, and Sable watched Petra's face do the arithmetic of the room in half a second—saw them register the exits, the weight of the coat, the distance to the cellar hatch—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like joy, that she was about to find out what kind of partner she had just sworn to.

"Back door," she murmured, "is bricked up. I checked yesterday. Cellar, then."

"You checked yesterday," Petra breathed.

"Of course I checked yesterday. I told you—I'm better at this than you are." She stood, unhurried, lifting the bottle. "Now smile at them and follow me, partner. And when we're running—"

The tallest grey man's eyes found their table.

"—when we're running," Sable finished, "try to keep up."
