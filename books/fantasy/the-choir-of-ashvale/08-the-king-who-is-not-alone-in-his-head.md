## 8. The King Who Is Not Alone in His Head

The writ took Sable three days and cost her a favor she refused to name, and when she slid it across the table of the debtors' tavern—folded twice, sealed in green wax that smelled faintly of hot metal—Petra understood they were past the point where either of them could pretend this was a small thing.

"Master Corvin Hale," Sable said, "assessor of tithes, second chair, Office of the Royal Ledger. You've been summoned to court to reconcile the autumn grain accounts before the Feast of Lanterns." She tapped the seal. "The real Master Hale is in Vell with the flux and won't be back for a month. Nobody at court has seen his face in two years. They'll be looking at your hands, not your face. Ledgers do that to people."

"And if someone *has* seen his face?"

"Then we're both dead, and I'd have wasted a perfectly good forgery." She smiled her coin-counter's smile. "You wanted a look at the king. This is how you get one."

Petra turned the writ over. The forgery was flawless—the way everything Sable touched was flawless—and that was its own kind of frightening. "Why are you helping me? Really."

"I told you my truth. My brother died carrying that map." Her smile didn't move but something behind it did. "You want to know what killed him. So do I. That's not help, Petra. That's appetite."

* * *

Court was worse than Petra had imagined, and they had imagined it badly on purpose.

They had dressed the part—Sable's doing again, a grey doublet cut plain enough to be honest and fine enough to be invisible, ink stains worked into the right hand's fingertips with lampblack so that even up close Master Hale looked like a man who had never held anything but a pen. The Palace of Ashvale rose above the city like a jaw of white stone, all spires and buttresses, and inside it was warm and golden and loud with the particular noise of powerful people pretending not to be afraid of each other.

The throne room was smaller than the cathedral above which it had been built; Petra felt that immediately, the way you feel a bruise through clothing. The old bones of the place pressed up under the marble. Somewhere below their feet, buried and drowned and singing, was the beginning of the map.

*Later,* they told themselves. *First, look at him.*

King Aldric sat on the Ember Throne beneath the great lantern-chandelier, and he was not what Petra expected.

They had expected a monster. A hollow man, a puppet with something else working the strings from inside, a face gone wrong the way drowned faces go wrong. Instead they saw a king in his fifties, broad-shouldered still, grey at the temples, wearing the weight of the crown the way an ox wears a yoke—with the terrible patience of a creature that has forgotten there was ever anything else.

He was listening to a petition. A wool merchant from the river districts, red-faced, gesturing, complaining about tariffs. And Aldric listened the way good kings listen: head tilted, hands folded, eyes kind.

His shadow did not.

It fell long across the marble floor in the lantern light, thrown behind the throne, and when the merchant bowed—and the whole court bowed with him, a rustling wave of silk and deference—the shadow stayed upright.

A half-second. Maybe less. Then it folded itself down into a bow so smooth that anyone who wasn't watching for it would have called it imagination, would have doubted their own eyes rather than the king's shadow.

Petra watched. Ten years of keeping ledgers had taught them that the truth lived in the half-seconds, in the entries that didn't balance, in the numbers that were almost right. Almost right was where every lie in the world made its home.

*You imagined that,* said the sensible voice, the Guild-trained voice, the voice that had kept them alive and invisible for a decade. *Shadows lag. Light does strange things in old rooms.*

Then the king opened his mouth and spoke, granting the merchant's petition in a clear, mild, reasonable voice—

—and while he spoke, his lips were moving differently.

Not much. Not enough for anyone to notice unless they stood where Petra stood, close to the dais, holding a ledger no one would ever ask to see. But the words his mouth shaped and the words his voice produced were two different sentences running at once, like a page printed with two texts overlaid, each legible only if you stopped trying to read the other.

The voice said: *Your petition is granted, master merchant, and the crown thanks you for your service.*

The mouth said, silently, over and over, with the desperate precision of a man tapping out a message through a wall:

*Help me. Help me. Help me.*

* * *

Petra did not remember walking away from the dais. They remembered standing afterward in a side gallery with a cup of wine they had no memory of taking, watching the court swirl below, and understanding that their hands were shaking and that Master Corvin Hale could not afford shaking hands.

*He's still in there.* The thought arrived with the force of a physical blow, and behind it came its twin, colder: *and he has been in there for years, screaming politely, while an entire kingdom bowed to the thing wearing him.*

Every story about Aldric's reign suddenly reorganized itself around that half-second delay. The peace treaties. The fair judgments. The king's famous mildness, his patience, his refusal to anger—of course he never raged. Rage would have been *his*. Everything the king did that was genuinely, unmistakably Aldric must have been a flicker, a leak, a man hammering on the inside of his own skull. And everything else—

Everything else was the crown.

Petra thought of the map sewn into their coat lining, the cathedral floor plan of a sleeping mind, the shifting rooms, the chamber labeled in their own handwriting. They thought of a god who had not spoken in ten years, and of a courier dying in the salt door saying *thirteen turns*, and for the first time the pieces did not just fit—they *locked*, with the ugly click of a mechanism engaging.

The parasite fed on oaths. Every vow sworn in the kingdom flowed down to it like water finding a low place, and the largest reservoir of sworn obligation in Ashvale was the crown itself—the king's oath, renewed at coronation, renewed at every feast, every judgment, every *we so swear* spoken in his name. It hadn't devoured him quickly because quick feeding kills the herd. It had kept him alive and functioning the way a farmer keeps a field: tended, rotated, harvested.

And now the thirteenth moon was coming early, and the field was due.

"Master Hale?"

Petra turned. A young clerk stood at their elbow, earnest, holding a stack of papers. "The Lord Steward asks whether the grain reconciliation can wait until after the Feast. His Grace finds account-talk tiresome during festivals."

"His Grace," Petra heard themselves say, in Hale's dry register, "will find the accounts more tiresome if they're wrong."

The clerk blinked, bowed, and retreated, and Petra realized they had just impersonated a royal assessor convincingly enough to frighten a courtier, and felt nothing about it at all, which was perhaps the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

Below, the court was bowing again—a delegation this time, some border lord and his retinue—and again the shadow lagged, caught up, smoothed itself over. And again, as the king raised his head, his lips moved against his own voice.

But this time the shape of the silent words was different.

Petra stared. Read the mouth the way they read a ledger, line by line, refusing the easy entry.

Not *help me*.

*Thirteen. Thirteen. Thirteen.*

* * *

They found Sable at dusk on the palace steps, in the crowd dispersing toward the Feast of Lanterns, and she read their face before they said a word. She was very good at faces. She took Petra's arm as if they were old friends leaving a party and steered them down into the streets, and only when they were three turns deep into the lamplighters' quarter did she say, quietly:

"You saw it."

"He's alive in there." Petra's voice came out flat and strange. "Sable, he's *in there*. He's been begging for help through his own mouth for—I don't know how long. Years. And today—" They stopped. Made themselves say it. "Today he was counting. Thirteen. Over and over. He knows what's coming. He's been counting down from inside his own prison, and nobody in that entire golden room could hear him except a forger and a liar."

Sable was silent for half a block. When she spoke, her voice had lost its polish; what was underneath was older and harder. "My brother wrote in his last letter that the king dreamed of drowning. That the dream-sellers in the low town were selling copies of it—everyone who bought one dreamed the same dream. A man underwater, in a crown, knocking on the underside of a lid." She looked straight ahead. "I thought it was sedition. Clever sedition. Someone making a point about the crown." Her jaw tightened. "It was a distress signal. Sold for two coppers a copy, and the whole city slept through it."

The lanterns were being lit along the Processional, hundreds of them, the Feast of Lanterns beginning early because the moon was coming early, because everything was coming early now, the whole calendar of the kingdom sliding forward like a ledger whose columns wouldn't balance. The light gilded the white spires of the palace above them, and somewhere beneath those spires, beneath the marble and the drowned nave and the singing, a man was knocking.

Petra thought of their god. Ten years of silence. Ten years of prayers falling into a well with no bottom and no echo. They had told themselves it was abandonment, or death, or the ordinary cruelty of heaven. Standing in the lantern light with the taste of court still in their mouth, they understood for the first time that silence could also be a *sentence*—that a god might be muted the way a witness is silenced, and that the thing capable of doing it wore a crown and sat in a golden room smiling with a stolen face.

"We can't wait for the thirteenth turn," Petra said. "We go under now. Tomorrow. Tonight, if we can."

"The undercrofts need preparation. The map needs—"

"*Tonight,* Sable. He counted thirteen today. I don't think that was the number of what's coming. I think it was the number of what's *left*."

Above them, at the top of the palace's highest tower, a single window burned brighter than all the others, and for one moment—one half-second, the width of a shadow's hesitation—the light behind it guttered, as if something enormous had passed between the flame and the glass.

Sable followed their gaze. Then she did something Petra had never seen her do.

She took their hand.

"Tonight, then," she said. "God help us both."

And far below the palace, beneath the marble, beneath the water, something that had been singing in the dark for a very long time changed its note—as if it had heard.
