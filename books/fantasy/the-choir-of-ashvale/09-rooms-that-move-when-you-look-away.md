## 9. Rooms That Move When You Look Away

**Petra**

The undercrofts beneath Candlemaker's Row smelled of wet stone and older sins.

I had walked these tunnels a hundred times in ten years of Guild work—deliveries, collections, the occasional quiet removal of something that shouldn't exist—and I had never once noticed the door at the end of the third passage. A plain door, oak gone black with damp, set into a wall I could have sworn was solid brick.

The map said otherwise.

I held it open by lantern light while Sable watched the passage behind us, and the ink did its slow, patient work: a chamber drawn where no chamber should be, labeled in my own handwriting—*the room where the years are kept*—in a hand slightly younger than mine, rounder, less careful. The hand of someone who hadn't yet learned to write like they were invisible.

"That's your script," Sable said. Not a question. She'd spent three days auditing my ledgers by candlelight; she knew my letters better than I did.

"It's my script from before."

"Before what?"

"Before." I folded the map. "Are you coming or not?"

She came. Of course she came. Sable Vane went through doors the way other people went through arguments—fast, smiling, certain she'd win whatever waited on the other side.

The door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It swung inward at my touch with a sound like a held breath released, and cold air rolled out carrying a smell I knew from somewhere I couldn't name: tallow and iron and something sweet underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room.

Inside, shelves. Hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, and on every shelf, jars. Not bottled grief—I knew the Guild's grief stock by its blue glass and its weeping seals. These were plain clay pots, unmarked, unsealed, each one warm to the touch.

I picked one up and the warmth moved. Under the clay, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something turned over in its sleep.

"Years," Sable said quietly. "Borrowed years. Someone's been warehousing them down here instead of burning them off." Her voice had changed—the drawl gone out of it, the ledger-keeper's flat precision underneath. "That's not possible. Spent years decay. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the Guild taught everyone," I said. "Which is exactly what the Guild would teach if it wanted a warehouse nobody would look for."

We stood there a moment, two liars in a room full of stolen time, and then Sable reached for a jar and I caught her wrist.

"Don't."

"Why not?"

"Because the map's been right about everything so far," I said, "and it hasn't told us yet what things cost."

She looked at me. Lantern light does strange things to a face; hers went sharp and young and briefly honest. "You think opening a door has a price."

"I think nothing down here is free."

* * *

**Sable**

Here is what I knew about Petra Winterbourne after four days of watching them: they counted everything twice, they trusted no one, and they flinched—actually flinched, a small involuntary twitch of the left hand—whenever anyone mentioned the god they served.

Here is what I learned about them in the room of jars: they were right about the price.

Because when we left—when we stepped back through the black door into the third passage and I turned to make some remark about reporting this to absolutely no one—I found a hole in my head where a memory used to be.

Not a big one. That was the horror of it. Not a wound, not a theft I could point to. Just a smooth blank patch, like a page torn cleanly from a book, and around the edges of the tear I could feel what had been there: a kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough. A word—

A word I couldn't find anymore.

"My hometown," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "I can't remember the name of my hometown."

Petra had gone very still. "You're sure?"

"I grew up there. Sixteen years. I remember the harbor and the fish smell and my mother's hands and the name of every street except—" I stopped. Pressed at the blank the way you press a bruise, hoping to map its shape. Nothing. Smooth all the way through. "It cost us a memory to open that door. One each, I'd guess. You lost something too. What was it?"

And here was the thing about Petra Winterbourne: they didn't lie well, but they lied fast.

"A face," they said. "Just a face. I don't know whose."

They were lying. I could see the seam of it, the way I could see the seam in any false entry—a fraction too smooth, a number that balanced too neatly. But I let it go, because I had just discovered that I was willing to trade pieces of myself for this, and I needed a moment alone with that discovery.

"You knew," I said instead. "In the room. You knew before we opened it."

"I suspected."

"Suspected." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud in the tunnel. "I've spent my whole life keeping accounts for other people's sins, Winterbourne. Do you know how many entries I've written that read *one memory, paid in full*? Hundreds. Thousands. I wrote them like they were grain shipments." I touched my temple, where the blank lived. "It doesn't feel like anything. That's the trick of it. You don't feel robbed. You just reach for something and it isn't there, and you have to decide whether it was ever there at all."

"Yes," Petra said softly. "That's exactly the trick of it."

There was something in their voice I didn't understand yet. Something that sounded like a man reading his own obituary and recognizing the spelling of his name.

"We should go back in," I said. "Count the jars. Map the room properly."

"No."

"No?"

"The door will be somewhere else next time anyway." They lifted the map, and I saw their jaw tighten. "Look."

I looked. On the vellum, the chamber we had just left had shifted—not far, half an inch of ink, but unmistakably—from the third passage to a position beneath the river. And below it, where before there had been only blank parchment, a new line had surfaced, rising through the paper like a body through water:

*the room where the names are kept.*

"Well," I said. "It's giving us a tour."

"It's giving us a bill," Petra said. "Itemized."

* * *

**Petra**

We found the second room two nights later, under the foundations of the old assize court, and I paid for it with the taste of my mother's cooking.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I have left. I did not lose my mother. I did not lose her face, her voice, the fact of her. I lost a specific evening—rain on the shutters, her hands dusted with flour, the particular sweetness of whatever she was making—and now when I reach for it I find the shape of the evening with the middle scooped out, a perfect absence wearing the evening's outline. I know I loved that memory. I know it because I grieve it. I cannot, however, remember it.

Sable watched me figure this out, standing in the room of names.

This room was worse than the first. No jars here—names don't keep well in clay. Instead, long strips of vellum hung from the ceiling like drying laundry, thousands of them, each inscribed with a single name in a different hand, and the air hummed. Not sound, exactly. Pressure. The feeling of a room where many people are speaking at once just below hearing.

"Stolen names," Sable murmured. "Guild inventory says we hold maybe forty. This is—" she counted hangers, fast, the way she counted everything, "—this is tens of thousands. Whose are they?"

I touched one strip. The vellum was warm, like the jars had been warm, and under my fingertip the ink seemed to shift, the way a sleeper shifts.

And then I understood, all at once, the way you understand a thing you've been refusing to understand for ten years.

"They're not stolen," I said. "They're stored. Every name the crown ever erased. Every traitor struck from the rolls, every heir written out, every witness made into nobody. The Guild holds the small inventory. This is the royal vault." My mouth had gone dry. "The thing wearing Aldric's crown didn't just take his mind. It's been archiving the whole kingdom."

Sable had gone quiet in the way she went quiet before she did something decisive. Then she pulled a knife and cut down the nearest strip of vellum.

"What are you doing?"

"Testing a theory." She held the strip to the lantern. A name was written on it in a fine clerk's hand—*Merrivale of the Ashen Court*—and as we watched, the ink faded, letter by letter, like breath off glass. When the last stroke vanished, Sable blinked twice and frowned.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing. I thought—" She shook her head. "For a second I almost remembered why Merrivale sounded familiar. Gone now." She dropped the blank vellum and wiped her knife with the absent efficiency of long habit. "So erasure runs both directions. The crown archives a name, the name dies up top. We destroy the archive copy—" she shrugged, "—and whatever was left of him down here goes out like a candle."

"Then we touch nothing else."

"Agreed." But she was looking at the hanging strips with an expression I couldn't read, and I realized, with a small cold turn in my stomach, that she was searching them. Scanning the thousands of names for one in particular.

"Whose name are you looking for?" I asked.

Her face closed like a ledger snapping shut. "None. Habit. Auditors check everything."

Another lie, and another one I let stand, because I was keeping my own secrets in the same room. Because the map had shown me a third chamber on our walk out—ink surfacing as we climbed, patient as tide:

*the room where the oaths are kept.*

And beneath it, smaller, in that younger version of my own hand, a note I had apparently written to myself a decade ago and forgotten writing:

*Do not open this one. — P.*

* * *

**Sable**

He pretended not to notice me pretending not to care. That's the arrangement we'd come to, Winterbourne and I: two people trading silences like currency, each convinced they were getting the better rate.

But walking back up through the assize foundations, I did the arithmetic I do when I can't sleep, which is most nights.

One hometown. That was the invoice so far. Sixteen years of cobbled streets and gull noise and my sister teaching me to swim in water cold enough to stop your heart, and the town itself now sat in my head like furniture under a sheet—I knew the shape of it, could walk its rooms blindfolded, but the name, the word that unlocked all of it, was gone. When I tried to say where I was from, my mouth produced a pause.

And here was the part I didn't say out loud, not to him, not to anyone: I'd do it again. Tomorrow. For the next room, and the one after that. Because somewhere under this city was an archive with my brother's name in it—Corren Vane, courier, dead three days, erased from the Guild rolls within the hour of his death by someone with excellent penmanship—and if the crown kept names, the crown might keep more than names. Might keep the last thing Corren carried. Might keep the reason he was running.

Every room between me and that answer cost a memory. Fine. I had memories to burn. I'd been spending them for years anyway—one job, one betrayal, one necessary cruelty at a time—so the crown could take its tithe and welcome to it. Let it eat the parts of me I liked least.

Let it eat the parts I liked best, if it came to that.

"Winterbourne," I said, at the top of the stairs, where the ordinary city noise came flooding back in—carts, gulls, a drunk singing badly two streets over. "The next room. The one the map showed you. The oaths."

They stopped. In the grey pre-dawn light they looked carved rather than built, all edges and shadow, and their left hand did the flinch.

"You saw the note," they said.

"*Do not open this one.* Your own handwriting telling you to stay out. And you're going to open it anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Petra looked at me for a long moment, and I watched them decide—watched the decision move through their face like weather—whether to spend another truth on me.

"Because I think it's the room where my god is," they said.

And then, before I could answer, they added, quieter, in the voice of a person setting down something heavy they'd carried a long way:

"And I think I'm the one who locked the door."
