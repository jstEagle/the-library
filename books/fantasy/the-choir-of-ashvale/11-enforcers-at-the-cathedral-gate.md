## 11. Enforcers at the Cathedral Gate

The cathedral found us before we found it.

One moment we were running through the undercrofts—Petra ahead of me with the map pressed flat against their chest like a second heartbeat—and the next the ceiling opened into vaulting, and there it was: Ashvale's drowned nave, rising out of the dark like the ribcage of something that had died standing up. Moonlight came through the broken rose window in long pale blades. The air tasted of wet stone and old incense and, underneath both, faintly, of salt.

"Gate," Petra said, which was not a word so much as an order.

I saw it. The great west doors, half off their hinges, hanging into the dark like a jaw. Beyond them, the street. Beyond the street, torchlight moving in a pattern I recognized because I had helped design it—the Guild's sweep formation, three points closing on a center, patient as arithmetic.

Three enforcers. I counted them by their lanterns, because enforcers travel dark and let the light do the announcing. One at the mouth of the alley. Two flanking, wide, cutting the angles. They weren't hurrying. That was the worst part. They knew the city would do their work for them, and they were right.

"They'll be inside in ninety seconds," I said.

"Then we have ninety seconds." Petra was already moving toward the choir stair that led down into the undercrofts proper, toward the first chamber on the map, the one that cost a memory to open. They stopped when they realized I wasn't following. "Sable."

"I'm not coming down yet."

"Sable."

"Somebody has to close the door behind us," I said, "and you're the only one who can read the map, and I'm the only one who fights like I mean it. Go."

They looked at me for a long moment. In the moonlight their face did something complicated—gratitude and guilt fighting for the same ground—and then they said, "Don't die keeping my books," and went down the stair, and the dark took them the way the dark takes everything in Ashvale: completely, and without comment.

Which left me alone in a dead god's house with ninety seconds and three professionals coming to collect me.

* * *

Here is what the Guild teaches its ledger-keepers about combat: nothing. We are pens, not swords. Our weapons are entries, cross-references, the slow knife of an audit done correctly. When I joined—when I was *bought*, more precisely, but the Guild prefers *joined*—they told me my hands were too valuable to risk. They said it like a compliment. It was an inventory assessment.

What they never learned, because they never asked, is what I did before the Guild. What the brand on my wrist says I was.

I pulled my sleeve back and checked it anyway, out of habit. The brand sits just below the wristbone, a small closed circle with a line through it, seared into me when I was nineteen by a man who apologized while he did it. Guild property. Recovered asset. Returned to service. The words are official; the scar tissue doesn't care about official. Itches when it rains. Itches now, actually, which meant the damp was rising, which meant—

Ninety seconds was optimistic. It was more like sixty.

I crossed the nave quickly, staying out of the moon-blades, and reached the west doors. They were oak, older than the kingdom, studded with iron gone soft as candle wax from centuries of rain getting in. One hung by its lower hinge. The other still sat in its frame, and behind it I could see the lantern-light swinging closer, steady as a metronome.

There was a bar. Of course there was a bar—a cathedral this old would have been built to be defended—but it had rotted through somewhere in the last century and snapped when I lifted it. So much for architecture.

Fine. Improvise.

There were pews along the north wall, stacked and warped, most of them too rotten to hold a kneeling grandmother, let alone a door. But near the baptistry there was a stone font cover, a slab the size of a coffin lid, and beneath it the old counterweight channel from when the doors had been rigged to swing. I got my shoulder under the slab and heaved. It moved maybe four inches, grinding, shrieking, announcing my position to anyone with ears—which, fine, they already knew the building, if not the room.

Sixty seconds became forty-five.

I got the slab up onto the pew stack beside the door, wedged it against the frame at hip height, and braced it with a broken pew-end driven into the flagstone gap like a stake. It was ugly work. It would hold for perhaps a minute against a determined man with a ram shoulder. Against three, less.

But I didn't need it to hold forever. I needed it to hold long enough to matter.

Forty seconds.

I heard them at the doors. Not knocking—enforcers don't knock. A pause, the particular silence of men listening, and then the quiet scrape of someone testing the hinge side with a knife blade, checking for wire or alarm thread. There wasn't any. I'd have known; I'd have set it myself if there'd been time.

A voice through the gap in the doors, low and pleasant, the way Guild voices go pleasant right before they stop being pleasant:

"Ledger-keeper Vane. The Guildmaster requests your presence."

"I'm busy," I said.

"The audit will proceed with or without you."

"That's very poetic. Did he write that himself?"

A short laugh from behind the door—not unkind, which frightened me more than anger would have. Anger you can predict. These were Vess's people, and Vess trained them to be courteous the way butchers are courteous to the meat. "You've been running two days," the voice said. "You're tired. You're hurt—you favor your left side since the rooftops. Come in, sit down, tell us where Winterbourne keeps the map, and the Guildmaster has authorized full remission of your contract. You could walk out of this a free woman."

I looked down at my wrist. At the circle and the line. At the word the brand didn't say out loud but said to me every single day of my life since I was nineteen: *mine.*

"You know what's funny?" I called back. "He offered me the same deal three years ago, when I ran the first time. And the time before that. He always authorizes remission. He never means it. It's in your own files—I filed the copies myself, entry 4471 through 4490. Check your ledgers, sweetheart. I keep better books than he does."

Silence. Then, quieter, almost admiring: "We're coming in."

"Obviously."

* * *

They came in the way water comes in—through every gap at once, no respect for the door I'd barricaded. The slab held against the first shoulder-hit, cracked against the second, and by the third they'd simply gone around, two of them peeling off toward the collapsed transept entrance while the third kept hammering the west doors to pin my attention.

It nearly worked. It would have worked on anyone who thought like a ledger-keeper, linearly, watching the thing in front of them. But I had spent six years reading the Guild's operational manuals for pleasure, the way other people read adventure serials, and I knew their favorite trick: the loud door and the quiet wall.

So when the transept rubble shifted, I was already moving.

The first one through was a big man named Corren—I knew his file, debts of honor, ex-soldier, methodical—and he came over the stones with his baton low and his weight forward, exactly the way the manual says, which is exactly the way you want him to come if you've read the manual. I let him commit to the drop. Then I gave him the font slab.

Not threw it—threw implies distance and drama. I shoved it off the pew stack as he landed, and four hundred pounds of baptismal stone caught him across the thigh, and the sound he made was brief and sincere, and then he was down among the pews trying to remember how legs worked.

The second one was faster. A woman, young, quick, came off the rubble in a low rush with her baton already whistling, and I will be honest with myself here, because I keep honest books even when no one audits them: she nearly had me. The first stroke I turned with my forearm and felt it all the way up to the elbow, a bright white line of pain, and the second stroke I didn't turn at all—I stepped *inside* it, the way you're taught once, properly, by someone whose name I am not going to think about tonight, and put my elbow into her throat, and then my knee into whatever part of her seemed structurally important.

She folded. I didn't watch her land.

Which left the third, the patient one, the voice at the door, and he hadn't rushed the way the others had because rushing was for people who needed to be somewhere. He stood in the west doorway with the ruined bar at his feet and his baton resting against his shoulder like a shepherd's crook, and he looked at me—really looked, the inventory look, the one that prices you—and said:

"There it is."

I knew what he meant before he said the rest. My sleeve had ridden up. The brand was showing, circle and line, silver-pink in the moonlight.

"Former property," he said. "Recovery clause. You know how this goes, Vane. I don't enjoy it."

"You don't enjoy it," I repeated. "That must make it very difficult for you."

"It does, actually."

"Good," I said, and came at him.

* * *

I want to record this next part accurately, because later—assuming there is a later—someone may ask me what happened in the cathedral, and I intend to lie to them beautifully, but the ledger in my head should stay true.

He was better than me. That's the true entry. Corren was strong and the girl was fast, but this one—Hale, his name was Hale, senior enforcer, eleven years in—was *good*, in the way that costs money, and for about forty seconds he showed me exactly what eleven years buys. He took my first three attacks apart without appearing to move. He read my left-side favor, the one the voice at the door had mentioned, and started feeding everything to that side, patient as interest accruing. By the fourth exchange my forearm was numb and my breathing was ragged and I was backing toward the choir stair without meaning to, which was the whole design, of course. Back me onto the stairs, take me high, let gravity finish the argument.

And I might even have let it happen, might have spent my last coin on one desperate exchange and lost, except that mid-retreat my heel came down on the broken pew-end I'd staked into the flagstones earlier, and it rolled, and I went down hard on one knee with my arm flung out for balance—

—and my sleeve tore.

Not all the way. Just enough. Just enough that in the moonlight, as I caught myself, the brand flashed clear between us: the circle, the line, the small neat signature of the man who had owned me at nineteen.

Hale stopped.

I want to be fair to him in the record. He actually stopped. Baton half-raised, breath measured, and for one full second he looked at the mark on my wrist with something that in a better man you would call regret.

"Contract's still valid," he said quietly. "I'm sorry."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm not sorry."

And then I did the thing I had been saving since the rooftops, the thing I'd told myself I wouldn't need, the thing I'd promised myself I'd never use again because the last time I used it I was nineteen and it was used *on* me and I swore—out loud, in a room with witnesses, a vow entered into no book—that I would never make another person feel what the brand made me feel.

I grabbed the lantern off the floor where it had fallen in the scuffle, and I threw it at him.

Not at him, precisely. At the puddle of ancient lamp-oil that had pooled around the west doors from a century of leaking votive lamps, the puddle I'd noticed when I first came in and filed away, because noticing things and filing them away is the only weapon they ever actually gave me.

Flame went up the doors like it had been waiting a hundred years for permission.

Hale moved—of course he moved, he was good, good men move away from fire on reflex—and in the half-second his eyes left me, I was up and through the gap between him and the burning door, past him, into the smoke, and I heard him cough and swear and choose, sensibly, to save himself rather than chase a branded runaway through a burning nave.

Behind me the fire found the pew stacks and began, with terrible enthusiasm, to sing.

* * *

I took the choir stair down three steps at a time, and halfway down my left leg informed me, in the language of hot wire, that the girl with the baton had connected after all, somewhere above the knee, and that I had been running on adrenaline and bookkeeping ever since. Fine. Noted. Entered into the record. Bleeding, moderately. Ambulatory, barely. Regrets: none.

The undercroft corridor was dark and smelled of the sea doing something unspeakable to old foundations. I followed the sound of dripping until I found the side chamber where I'd told Petra to wait, and I pushed the door open, and there they were—map spread on the floor, a stub of pencil in their hand, marking the route to the first chamber with the concentration of a person copying out scripture.

They looked up. They saw the blood. Their face did the complicated thing again, guilt winning this round.

"You're hurt."

"Flesh wound. Very dramatic. The cathedral's on fire, by the way."

"*What?*"

"Occupational hazard of holding a gate. Which I held, thank you for asking. Three enforcers, one of whom is currently reconsidering his career, one of whom will walk with a limp, and one of whom I genuinely hope reads my file someday and learns what I cost." I slid down the wall to sitting, because my leg had finished negotiating. "The map's intact?"

Petra patted their coat lining, where the map was sewn. "Intact."

"Good." I pulled the torn sleeve back and looked at my own wrist, at the circle and the line, lit now by the faint orange glow bleeding down the stairwell from the burning nave above. "Because I just burned down a national monument to protect a piece of paper, and I'd hate to have done it for a draft."

Petra knelt beside me. They didn't reach for the wound, not yet. They looked at the brand instead—at the mark they must have seen a hundred times and never asked about, because asking about things is not what we do, we are ledger-keepers, we record, we do not inquire—and they said, quietly:

"What does it mean?"

I laughed. It hurt. Most things did, suddenly.

"It means," I said, "that I used to be Guild property. Before I was Guild staff. There's a difference, and the difference is that staff get paid." I flexed my hand, watched the firelight move on the scar. "It also means that when the Guildmaster says *recovery clause*, he isn't speaking rhetorically. If they take me alive, they take me back. Permanently, this time. No third remission."

"So we don't get taken alive."

"'We.'" I raised an eyebrow at them. "Since when am I a 'we'?"

"Since you burned down a cathedral for my map without being asked."

"Someone had to close the door," I said. "You're the only one who can read it. I'm the only one who fights like I mean it. Simple accounting."

Petra was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of a person opening a ledger to a page they've been avoiding: "When we get to the first chamber—it costs a memory to open. I told you. I need to know you understand what—"

"I understood when you told me. I've already decided which one I'd pay, if it comes to it." I leaned my head back against the cold stone and closed my eyes, and above us, muffled by centuries of masonry, the cathedral sang its crackling hymn. "Now check my leg, Winterbourne, and then show me where we're going. Because the thirteenth moon isn't waiting, the Guild isn't waiting, and I've recently discovered I'm extremely motivated."

Petra bent to the wound. And far below us, deeper than the undercrofts, deeper than anything with a name on any map, something heard the fire above and shifted in its sleep—and the first note of the choir drifted up through the stone, so faint I would spend the rest of my life half-convinced I imagined it.
