## 12. One Truth Each, As Promised

The choir's note faded before I could decide whether it had been real. Sable heard it too—I knew because she stopped breathing for the length of it, the way people do when a sound reaches past their ears and touches something older. Then she exhaled, said, "Well. That's ominous," and ruined the moment on purpose, which I was beginning to understand was her native language.

We didn't speak again until we were behind the locked door of a candle-maker's storeroom three streets from the cathedral, a place I'd used twice before when the Guild wanted me found and I preferred to be lost. It smelled of tallow and beeswax and old smoke, and it had one window, bricked over, and no name on any ledger that mattered.

Sable sat on an upturned crate while I built a fire in the little iron stove, and she watched me with the particular patience of someone cataloguing my movements for later use. Her left arm hung at an angle she was pretending wasn't wrong. The enforcer's blade had opened her from elbow to wrist—a long, shallow line, the kind that bleeds impressively and kills slowly, which is the worst kind, because it gives you time to think about it.

"Coat off," I said.

"Buy me dinner first."

"Sable."

She sighed, the theatrical sigh of a woman who has decided to let you win this round so she can win the next three, and worked the coat off her good arm. Underneath: the shirt soaked dark from elbow to cuff, and above the wound, on the inside of her forearm where the skin is thin, a brand I had never seen and recognized instantly anyway.

A circle broken by a vertical line. The Guild's mark of property. Not membership—property. The mark they put on foundlings bought outright, on debtors sold by their families, on the children of people who owed the Guild more than coin. I'd seen it on two people in ten years. Both of them had died owing.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm reading."

"Then you know it's old." She turned her arm so the firelight caught it. The scar tissue had gone silver years ago. "Before I could write. Before I could do anything worth writing about. They buy them young, did you know that? Young enough that they don't remember being anything else." A pause, and then, lightly, as if commenting on weather: "That's why I keep better books than you, Winterbourne. I learned arithmetic before I learned my own name. Numbers don't lie to you. People do. Names especially."

I knelt with the basin and the clean cloth and the needle I'd threaded by feel, and I did not say what I was thinking, which was that every theory I'd built about Sable Vane in the last six days had just been quietly demolished and rebuilt around a single fact: she had been owned before she had been anyone, and she had spent her whole life since making sure no one would own her again.

"Hold still," I said. "This will hurt."

"Everything does. That's rather the theme of the evening."

* * *

Cleaning a wound is honest work. There's no room in it for performance. When the antiseptic spirit touched the cut she hissed through her teeth and gripped the edge of the crate hard enough to crack it, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the room but blood and water and breath, and I found I was grateful for that. Grateful for a task that required nothing of me but competence.

Because I knew what came after. We had made a bargain in the debtors' tavern over stolen wine—two liars agreeing to tell each other exactly one truth each—and I had spent the walk here, and the fight at the gate, and all the minutes since, trying to decide whether I meant to keep my half.

Oaths are not decorative things for people like me. My god may have gone silent a decade ago, but the binding underneath the silence still held, the way a dead man's debts hold against his estate. Every promise I made had weight. Every promise I broke would find its way back to my body and collect. I had seen mercenaries who lied on sworn contracts wake with their tongues blackened, their sword hands curled shut, their lungs filling with the memory of every word they'd swallowed. The oaths of Ashvale are not enforced by men. Men can be bribed. The enforcement is older than the city.

So when I told Sable I would give her one truth, I had bound myself without quite admitting it. And now, stitching her arm closed with small careful pulls, I could feel the truth sitting in my chest like a stone swallowed whole, waiting.

"There," I said, tying off the last stitch. "Eight of them. You'll scar."

"Good. Scars are evidence." She flexed her fingers, testing, wincing, satisfied. "Evidence of what, though? That's the question, isn't it. Three enforcers at the gate, and me between them and your map, and I never asked why I was doing it." She looked at me across the stove, firelight moving on her face, and her voice lost its lacquer for the first time since I'd known her. "I've been asking myself since we ran. Why does Sable Vane bleed for Petra Winterbourne? I don't do unpaid work. Everyone who's ever met me knows that. So either I've gone mad, or—" she spread her good hand, "—or I already knew why, and I've been keeping that entry in a book even I don't get to read."

"The truth," I said. "You want yours first?"

"I want mine last," she said. "Yours first. Because if I go second, I'll spend your entire confession rehearsing mine instead of listening, and I've decided—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that looked almost like fear, "—I've decided I want to actually hear it."

* * *

I fed the stove another split of wood and sat down across from her, close enough that our knees nearly touched, far enough that I could watch her face while I spoke. Old mercenary habit. You learn to deliver bad news in a position where you can see how it lands.

"Ten years ago," I said, "I was twenty-two, and I was already the best blade in a company called the Greywater Nine, and I was arrogant enough to think that meant something. We took contracts out of Ashvale. Escort work, mostly. Retrieval. One winter we took a contract that paid triple rate and came with a seal I'd never seen—the crown's own, but struck through with a line, which means deniable. Off the books. The kind of contract that doesn't exist if anyone asks about it."

Sable had gone very still. Stillness, I was learning, was her version of flinching.

"Eleven of us went down under the palace. There are rooms under the palace that aren't on any survey—not hidden, exactly. Just *unlisted*. Rooms the architects drew and then burned the drawings for. We were told we were sealing a vault. That was the word they used. Sealing. Like it was wine, or grain. Something you put away for later, something that keeps."

My hands had started to shake. I watched them shake with a kind of distant irritation, the way you'd watch someone else's hands.

"What was down there wasn't a vault. I only saw it for a moment—the moment before we began—but I saw it, and I have spent ten years telling myself I misremembered, because the alternative is that I have known for a decade what's eating the king and said nothing." I made myself look at her. "It was wearing faces. Dozens of them. Faces I knew—my captain's face, my mother's face, faces of people I'd killed. It wore them the way you'd wear coats. And it was *hungry*, Sable. Not for flesh. For vows. You could feel it pulling at every promise you'd ever made, tasting them, sorting them. It fed on oathed things. And a kingdom is made of almost nothing else."

"And the god," Sable said quietly. "Your silent god."

"My god was there." The words came out of me like teeth being pulled, one at a time. "Not present—*present*. Standing between us and it. My god had chosen me as its hand—that's what the oath-shrine rite means, that's what I've been performing every night for ten years to no answer—and it led eleven mercenaries into the dark to do something gods apparently can't do themselves. Because the thing below couldn't be destroyed. Only bound. And bindings require ink. Blood-oath ink. Sworn hands."

I stopped. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us a cart rattled past on the street, an ordinary sound, obscene in its ordinariness.

"We built the cage," I said. "Eleven of us, cutting our palms, swearing the binding into the stone while my god held the thing's attention. And when it was done—when the last syllable was sworn—there was a problem. The binding needed to be *forgotten*. That was the final term. A prison that is remembered can be found; a prison that is found can be unmade. The thing below had tasted every vow we'd ever made. If any of us carried the memory intact, it could read it out of us through our dreams, through our oaths, through the shape of our sleeping minds. So the memory had to go somewhere it couldn't be reached."

"Your god took it," Sable said.

"My god took it. From all eleven of us. Took the whole night—every face, every word, every drop of blood—and folded it into itself, behind its own silence, where the thing below couldn't follow. And then my god went quiet. Completely quiet. Because a god carrying that memory couldn't risk speaking to me, couldn't risk letting me feel its attention, couldn't risk anything that might draw the prisoner's eye toward the place where the blueprint of its cage was kept." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ten years I thought I'd been abandoned. Ten years I performed the nightly rite to a god that wouldn't answer, and I thought: *this is what I deserve, or this is what faith costs, or this is what the world is.* And the whole time—"

"The whole time it was protecting you," Sable finished. "By staying silent. By taking the blame-memory into itself and going mute so you'd never come looking." She was pale under the firelight. "Petra. The map. The chamber labeled in your handwriting."

"Bindings remember their makers. The map isn't showing me the king's mind. It's showing me the prison I built—or the prison my god built through my hands. The rooms shift because the binding shifts. The choir—" and there it was again, faintly, under the floor, that single sustained note like a held breath, "—the choir is the binding singing itself. Keeping the walls true. And my god is in it. Somewhere in that song. Silent even in captivity, because that's the shape of the sacrifice it made."

I had said it. All of it. And the strange thing, the thing I hadn't expected, was that the sky didn't fall. No wound opened on my body. The oath held, the truth was paid, and I simply sat there in the wax-smelling dark feeling lighter and more hollowed-out than I had in ten years, like a house after the furniture's been carried out.

Sable didn't touch me. I understood later that this was its own kind of mercy—she knew, somehow, that being touched in that moment would have broken something. Instead she said, "You let your god take the blame. You've spent ten years calling yourself abandoned when you were the one who asked it to carry the worst of it." A pause. "That's not a confession, Winterbourne. That's a eulogy you never got to give."

"It gets worse," I said. "There were eleven of us. I'm the only one still alive. I checked, over the years, without knowing why I was checking. Eight dead within five years—accidents, fevers, one drowning in a dry riverbed. Two more gone in the last decade. And now a courier dies at the salt door pressing a map of the king's mind into my hands specifically. Into *mine*. Whatever's happening, it's happening to the last hand that held the pen."

* * *

Sable was quiet for a long time. Then she stood, crossed the small room, and sat down beside me on the floor, shoulder to shoulder, facing the stove, because some things are easier to say to a fire than to a face.

"My turn," she said. "One truth. As promised."

The note beneath the floor had grown slightly louder. Neither of us mentioned it.

"You remember the courier," she said. "At the salt door. Bleeding out. He pressed the map into your hands and said *thirteen turns*, and he died mid-sentence, and the enforcers came four minutes too late, and you lied to them—first lie in ten years, I'd wager, and you weren't even good at it." She smiled, briefly, without humor. "His name was Corren Vane."

The cold went through me slowly, like water finding a crack.

"He was my brother," Sable said. "Two years younger. Sold to the Guild the same year I was—we came in the same cart, if you can believe it, two children and a mother's debt. They kept us together until I was twelve and useful and he was ten and not. Then they sent him to the courier corps, because couriers die often and orphans cost less to replace than trained ledger-keepers." Her voice stayed level. That was the terrible part. It stayed level the way a frozen lake stays level. "I bought his freedom when I was nineteen. First thing I ever stole was the ledger page with his debt on it. Second thing I ever stole was him. We had six years. He was the only person alive who remembered me before the brand. The only person who called me by the name our mother gave us, which I have not spoken aloud in fifteen years and am not going to tell you tonight, so don't ask."

"I wasn't going to."

"Yes, you were. Everyone wants the real name. It's the one commodity nobody's ever managed to put a price on." She turned her head and looked at me, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely terrifying. "Corren found something. I don't know what yet—he sent me half a message three weeks ago, unsigned, untraceable, the kind of tradecraft I taught him myself. It said: *the king is not alone in his head, and the Guild built the walls.* Then nothing. Then the rolls updated. Courier Vane, deceased, cause of death: fever. Except I keep better books than the Guild does, and there was no fever season, and his last route didn't pass through any fever country, and his body was never returned for burning, which is Guild law even for property." She breathed out. "So I started an audit. Quietly. And the audit led nowhere, because someone had erased him very well—until a certain ledger-keeper with a reputation for invisibility suddenly appeared in three separate reports carrying a sealed map that matched the description of the thing my brother died holding."

"You think I killed him." 

"I thought everything," she said simply. "For six days I thought everything. That's the job. You think everything until the evidence picks a side." She lifted her bandaged arm slightly. "Then three enforcers came to take that map, and I stood in a doorway and bled for it, and somewhere in the middle of the third one I realized I wasn't defending the map. I was finishing Corren's route. He carried it as far as he could carry it. I'm carrying it the rest of the way. That's all family is, in the end. Someone who finishes your routes."

The stove had burned low. Outside, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its impossible early fullness, and beneath us the note from the deep places had become two notes, then three—a chord assembling itself in the dark under the city, patient as tide.

"One truth each," Sable said. "Paid in full. Which means, by the terms of our extremely stupid tavern bargain, we are now the only two people in Ashvale who know the whole shape of this thing." She rose, wincing, and offered me her good hand. "So here's what I propose, oath-keeper. Tomorrow we go down. Past the drowned nave, into whatever's singing. You because you wrote the cage and I because my brother died drawing the map of it. And before we go—" her grip tightened on my hand, "—you're going to teach me the binding words. All of them. Because if something happens to you down there, someone has to finish *your* route."

I took her hand and stood, and as I did, the chord beneath the floor resolved into melody—clearly, unmistakably, a human voice among many voices, singing a line I knew.

Not remembered. Knew. The way you know a room you grew up in.

It was the first verse of the binding. My binding. Sung back to me in a voice I had spent ten years praying to and hearing nothing.

Sable saw my face change. "What," she said. "What is it."

"They're not singing to keep the walls true," I whispered. "They're singing to me. The choir knows I'm coming."

And far below, the song opened like a door.
