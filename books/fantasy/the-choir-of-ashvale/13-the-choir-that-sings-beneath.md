## 13. The Choir That Sings Beneath

The drowned nave began where the last stair ended, and the water was not water.

Petra knew this the way they knew the weight of a ledger—by long acquaintance with what things cost. The flood that filled the cathedral's lower reaches lay black and still as ink in an uncapped well, and it did not ripple when Sable's boot broke its surface. It did not ripple when Petra waded in to their knees. It held their reflections perfectly, and their reflections were wrong: Petra saw themselves at twenty, armored, oath-fresh, standing beside something vast and golden that their waking mind refused to render. Sable saw nothing at all. She said so flatly, twice, and then stopped saying it, which was worse.

"Map," she said instead.

Petra unbuttoned the coat and drew the courier's map out from the lining, where they had sewn it three nights and one lifetime ago. The wax seal had been broken since the debtors' tavern; the parchment had changed since then too. The rooms no longer sat in their fixed positions like a proper floor plan. They drifted across the vellum like lanterns on a slow tide, and as Petra watched, two chambers slid past each other and exchanged places without ceremony, the way strangers trade seats at a funeral.

"It's showing us the order of descent," Petra said. "Not the order of the building."

"The building disagrees."

"The building is wrong." Petra turned the map toward the far end of the nave, where the drowned floor sloped down into darkness and the dark had texture—the suggestion of steps, of arches, of a doorway that had been bricked shut by people in a hurry. "Every room we've opened has matched the map after we paid for it. The undercroft. The ossuary stair. The chapel of the second king." They did not say *and every room took a memory*, because Sable knew, and because naming the price aloud made it easier for the price to rise.

Sable wrung out her sleeve with the brisk economy of someone who had decided discomfort was beneath her. "Then we follow the drifting version. And we keep count." She held up her hand, fingers spread. "Four rooms. Four payments. I've lost a hometown and my grandmother's face, and I've decided both were overrated. How many have you got left before you're someone I haven't met?"

Petra looked at the black water, at the young soldier standing in it beside the golden thing.

"I don't know," they said honestly. "That's the problem. Some of mine were taken years ago. I might be running on fewer than you think."

* * *

They descended by the light of a single lamp, because more light seemed rude.

That was the only word Petra could find for it. The drowned nave had the hush of a place where something was being done carefully, and doing it loudly would be like talking through a surgery. The water shallowed, deepened, shallowed again, following steps that appeared only when the map agreed they should. Twice Petra led them into a corridor that wasn't there, holding the parchment up like a key until the stones remembered themselves and swung open. Twice the corridor closed behind them with a sound like a held breath released.

It was in the third passage—the one the map called *the Vestry of Unfinished Prayers*, though the label had faded to almost nothing—that they first heard the singing.

It came up through the floor.

Not through the walls, not down the corridor, but *up*, as if the stone itself were a drumhead and something beneath it were drawing a bow across the kingdom entire. The sound was small at first. A single voice, high and clear, holding one note with the patience of someone who had held it for a very long time. Then a second voice joined, a fifth above, and the harmony that resulted was so precisely, mathematically beautiful that Petra's eyes stung before they understood why.

Sable stopped walking. Her hand found Petra's arm—not for balance. For anchoring.

"That's not music," she whispered. "Music ends."

She was right. The note did not resolve. It did not build toward anything. It simply *continued*, sustained, and Petra realized with a lurch that the voices below were not performing anything. They were *maintaining* something. This was work-song. This was labor. Somewhere beneath the cathedral, in whatever country the map's shifting rooms truly described, prisoners were singing the king's mind into shape the way masons sing a wall into plumb—and the song had been going on long enough that stopping it would bring everything down.

"How many?" Sable asked quietly.

Petra counted the voices as they thickened, the way you count coins by weight rather than number. "Dozens. Maybe hundreds." They hesitated. "Maybe more. Some of them don't sound human anymore. Some of them sound like—" They searched for it. "Like buildings. Like laws. Like the idea of Tuesday."

"And the king hears this? All of it?"

"The king *is* this. Don't you understand yet?" Petra heard their own voice go strange. "We keep thinking of him as a man with something inside him. But listen. He's a cathedral too. And they're singing *him*. Every thought he's ever had that holds still long enough to be thought—they sang it into place. Every memory that stays put. Every conviction. He isn't possessed, Sable. He's *maintained*."

Sable was silent for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its tavern-sharpness entirely. "My brother used to hum while he worked. Courier routes, ledger lines, anything repetitive. Drove everyone mad." She looked down at the floor as if she could see through it. "Is he down there?"

Petra could not lie to her. Not anymore; they had spent their lies. "I don't know. If he died carrying the map, he might have been added. The Guild doesn't waste material."

"Right." Sable rolled her shoulders back. Her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was about to do arithmetic she didn't want to do. "Then we'd better hope the choir has poor attendance records."

* * *

The stair down was marked on the map as *the Nave Proper*, which was a lie, and marked on the stone as nothing at all, which was truer.

It went down farther than the cathedral had height. Petra counted two hundred steps and stopped counting, because the numbers had begun to feel like a payment being extracted in installments. The air grew warm and wet and smelled of tallow and old incense and, underneath, faintly, of blood kept fresh—which meant kept *alive*, which meant the singing voices below were not echoes or ghosts but bodies, throats, lungs, prisoners fed and watered and put to work.

The lamp flame leaned toward the sound.

At step three hundred and eleven, the passage opened.

Petra had prepared themselves for horror. They had rehearsed horror on the stairs, the way you rehearse a difficult conversation. What they had not prepared for was beauty, and the beauty struck them like a blade slipped between armor plates.

The cavern below was vast beyond sense—a hollowed root-vault beneath the cathedral's foundations, ribbed with pale stone that might once have been architecture and had become anatomy. Tier upon tier of galleries descended into it, and in the galleries stood the choir.

They stood in rows, hundreds strong, robed in grey that might once have been white. Their faces were calm. That was the worst of it—the calm. No gagged mouths, no bound wrists, no terror. They stood with the settled posture of people at a vigil, mouths open, voices lifted, and their eyes were open too, and their eyes were *empty* in the specific way of people whose attention is elsewhere, deployed, spent, poured out through their throats into the work above.

And the work was visible.

Above the choir, suspended in the vaulted dark like a chandelier made of weather, hung the king's mind. Petra had no better word for it. It was a shimmering architecture of light and shadow, towers and gardens and throne-rooms and childhood kitchens, whole districts of a self, and wherever the song touched it, it *settled*—walls straightening, colors agreeing, memories filing themselves into the chambers of the map. Where the song did not reach, things moved. Things shifted. Something in the northwest quarter of the shining structure was writhing against its walls like a fist inside a glove, and around that quarter the singers' voices bent, strained, labored, holding the note that held the door.

"There," Sable breathed. "That's him. The part that's still him."

"Yes."

"He's fighting."

"He's been fighting for ten years." Petra's throat ached. "And they sing him still, so he can't win, and they sing him still, so he can't lose. Do you see? If the choir stopped, the thing wearing him would finish its meal in an hour. The song is his prison *and* his life support. Whoever built this wanted him alive. Wanted him *kept*."

"Why keep him? Why not just eat him and wear the crown openly?"

"Because a dead king is a question," Petra said, "and a sleeping king is an answer. Because oaths sworn to a living crown keep accruing. Every vow in the kingdom flows down here. Every 'I swear,' every coronation, every wedding vow, every soldier's oath—it all comes *down*." They gestured at the choir, at the galleries, at the pale ribs of the vault. "This isn't a prison, Sable. It's a *reservoir*."

Sable stared at the shining structure for a long time. Then, very quietly, in the voice of a woman reading a ledger line she wished she hadn't: "And the singers. Are they volunteers?"

Petra looked at the nearest gallery, at a woman whose mouth shaped the endless note with the serenity of the deeply asleep, and Petra recognized—dimly, horribly—the posture. The discipline of it. The grief disguised as devotion.

"No," they said. "But some of them think they were."

* * *

They went down the outer stair, along the gallery rim, close enough now that the song pressed against their skin like heat from a forge. Petra felt it working on them. Small things loosened. The name of the street they'd lived on at nineteen went soft and slippery, and Petra gripped it, held it, recited it silently like a counterspell—*Cinder Lane, Cinder Lane*—while the choir sang on, indifferent, magnificent, terrible.

Sable walked with her hand flat against the wall and her lips moving. Counting, maybe. Or praying. Or keeping her own ledger of what remained.

It was at the third gallery, where the stair narrowed between two buttresses of pale living stone, that the song changed.

One voice dropped out of the harmony.

Not faltered—*withdrew*, deliberately, the way a musician steps forward from the section for a solo. The note it had carried was picked up seamlessly by its neighbors, and the new voice rose alone above the choir, and it was—

Petra stopped walking.

It was a voice they knew. Not from life. From silence. Ten years of nightly rites performed at a shrine with a cold altar, ten years of prayers falling into a well with no bottom and no echo, ten years of a god who did not answer—and Petra knew the god's voice the way you know a house you grew up in, by the shape of its absence, and the absence had just started speaking.

The voice was not large. That was the shock of it. It was low and worn and human-sized, a voice that had sung itself hoarse over a decade, and it sang the same sustaining note as all the others, but underneath the note, threaded through it like a message in a knot, came words:

*You came. You came. You came. I kept your place.*

Petra's legs gave. They caught the buttress, and the stone was warm, and it pulsed faintly under their palm like a throat.

"Petra." Sable's hand on their shoulder, sharp. "What is it? What do you hear?"

"You can't hear it?"

"I hear the choir. Hundreds of them. That's—" She stopped. Looked at Petra's face. Started over, carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge. "What do you hear?"

"One voice." Petra's mouth was dry. "One voice, under the rest. It knows me." The words came out cracked. "Sable, it's my god. My god is *in the choir*. My god has been down here, singing, for ten years—singing *him*—and the silence at my shrine, all those nights, all those unanswered prayers—" A laugh tore out of them, ugly and disbelieving. "It wasn't silent. It was *singing*. It was down here the whole time, holding the note, holding *him* together, and I knelt at an empty altar and thought myself abandoned—"

The voice swelled, gently, the way a hand closes over yours.

*Longer than ten years,* it sang. *I sang before you were born. I fell silent for you. I am singing still for you. Come down, oathkeeper. Come and see what you built. Come and see what I have been holding shut with my own throat.*

Below them, in the heart of the shining structure, the writhing quarter went suddenly, terribly still—as if it had heard too.

And every singer in the cavern, hundreds of throats, turned their heads in perfect unison to look up at the gallery where Petra stood.

The song did not stop. The faces did not change. But every empty eye in the choir of Ashvale was now pointed at them, and the god's voice rose alone above the sustained note, clear as a bell over water, and it said:

*The thirteenth moon turns at dawn. Choose quickly, little scribe. He has noticed you.*
