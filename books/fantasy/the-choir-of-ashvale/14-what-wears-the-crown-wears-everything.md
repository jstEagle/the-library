## 14. What Wears the Crown Wears Everything

The last door in the map was not a door.

That was the first thing I understood about it, standing in the dark beneath the drowned nave with Sable's blood still drying on my sleeve and the choir's song pressing against my eardrums like deep water. The map showed a chamber at the heart of the king's mind—a small room, unremarkable, drawn with the same careful lines as all the others. But where every other chamber had an entrance, this one had only a seam. A line in the stone so fine you'd mistake it for a crack, if cracks could breathe.

"It opens from the inside," Sable said behind me. Her voice was rough. She'd been holding her side since the gate, and she hadn't once asked me to slow down, which was its own kind of confession. "Or it doesn't open at all."

"Then we knock."

"With what? Courtesy?"

I put my palm flat against the seam. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm, not fire-warm—*body*-warm, the way a sleeping man's forehead is warm, and that was worse than any cold I had ever stood in. Under my hand, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something turned over.

The seam widened.

Not dramatically. No grinding of ancient mechanisms, no burst of light. It simply decided, the way a mouth decides to open when it has already tasted you coming, and the dark inside was not empty. It was *attentive*.

I looked back at Sable. In the light of the lamp she carried, her face was pale and set and beautiful in the way that a blade is beautiful—because of what it's willing to do. She had told me one truth in a debtors' tavern, and I had paid mine back, and between those two truths we had built something neither of us had a name for yet. I didn't want to die without naming it. That seemed like a stupid thing to want, down here, and I wanted it anyway.

"If I'm not out by the time the song changes," I said, "go up. Take the map. Burn it or sell it, I don't care which."

"You care enormously which."

"I care enormously everything. Go anyway."

She didn't answer, which from Sable Vane was a promise to consider it, and considering it was more loyalty than I'd earned from anyone in ten years. I stepped through the seam, and the stone closed behind me like water closing over a dropped coin.

* * *

The chamber inside was the throne room.

Of course it was. Of course it wasn't. It was both at once—the way the map's rooms had been both themselves and their buried counterparts, the undercroft and the idea of the undercroft, the place and the memory of the place. I stood on a floor I knew: black marble veined with silver, worn pale in two paths where generations of petitioners had shuffled toward the dais. I had stood on that floor three days ago wearing a forged writ and a courier's livery, watching King Aldric's shadow bow half a second late.

But there were no windows here. No courtiers. No light except the light the room made itself, which came from everywhere and cast nothing, and the dais at the far end held not a throne but a shape.

Aldric sat on it.

He looked better than he had at court. That was the horror of it. At court, behind his eyes, something had been screaming politely; here, in the dark, the screaming had stopped, because here there was nothing left to scream *from*. He wore the crown, and the crown fit him the way a glove fits a hand—snug, obedient, and entirely wrong if you thought about who was moving the fingers.

"Petra Winterbourne," said the king's mouth.

The voice was Aldric's voice, pitched low and reasonable, the voice of a man who has never once needed to raise it. But it came from everywhere in the room, from the walls and the floor and the warm dark above, and the king's lips moved a half-second after each word, like a puppet lagging behind its strings.

"You know my name."

"I know everyone's name." The king tilted his head, and the gesture was almost playful, almost human, and the wrongness of it made my oath-scar ache under my collar. "That is rather the point of me. Sit down, ledger-keeper. You've walked a long way through your own handwriting to get here. You must be tired."

There was no chair. There was a chair—I felt it against the backs of my legs before I saw it, and then I saw it, and I sat, because refusing would have cost me something I couldn't name and sitting cost me only my dignity, and I'd spent worse.

"What are you?" I asked.

"A good question. Everyone asks it eventually." The king leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the crown caught light that didn't exist, and I saw—not the gold, not the jewels, but *through* them, into the thing underneath. "You've read ledgers your whole life, Petra Winterbourne. So read me. What am I?"

I looked. I was good at looking; it was the one skill the Guild had sharpened in me past the point of cruelty. And what I saw, when I stopped flinching, was arithmetic.

Every vow ever sworn in Ashvale. Every promise a farmer made to his dying wife. Every soldier's oath, every guild-bond, every wedding vow, every debt acknowledged aloud, every *I will* spoken in earnest anywhere inside the kingdom's borders—and further, older, vows from before the borders had names. They were all here. Not recorded. *Incorporated.* Woven together strand over strand until the mass of them had weight, and hunger, and the shape of a man, because men were the ones who swore things and the thing had learned to wear the shape of its food.

"You're a debt," I said slowly. "You're every promise anyone ever made, called in at once."

"*Paid,*" the king corrected gently. "Debts are ugly words. Think of me as a treasury. Every vow is a coin, and coins want to circulate. I simply... hold them." His smile was Aldric's smile, warm and tired and kingly. "Do you know how many promises are made in this kingdom on any given day? Four hundred thousand, give or take. Most are broken within the year. Broken vows have to go somewhere, ledger-keeper. Your Guild knows this. Your Guild *profited* from knowing this, long before you were born. Where do you think borrowed years come from? Someone pays. Someone always pays. I am merely the account into which everything settles."

"And the king?"

"The king is my glove." He raised Aldric's hand and flexed the fingers, admiring them. "A fine one. Strong hands, this kingdom. I feed on what they swear, and they swear constantly—it's a pious country, yours, full of little gods and little oaths. Every *I promise* is a meal. Every coronation is a feast. Do you know what a coronation is, Petra Winterbourne? It is ten thousand people swearing allegiance at once, to one man, through one object." He touched the crown. "I did not choose the crown. The crown chose itself. It was simply the largest vessel available."

The choir sang on beyond the walls, muffled now, distant, and I understood suddenly why the song existed. It wasn't worship. It was *bookkeeping*. Somewhere below us, the imprisoned were singing the king's mind into order—or into captivity—and every note was a line item, holding the parasite's attention, keeping it occupied with the maintenance of its own vast accounts while the man inside it screamed politely and waited for someone to come.

Sable was down there holding the song steady. I could feel it fraying at the edges even now, threads of it going thin, and I knew I did not have long.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "Why let me in?"

"Because you're the one who wrote the binding." The king said it the way you'd remark on weather. "And I have been waiting ten years for you to visit."

* * *

The room changed around the words. Not the walls—the air. The warmth in it curdled into something sweeter, and the light bent, and suddenly I was not sitting across from a parasite wearing my king. I was sitting across from a merchant making an offer, and merchants, I knew, always opened with the customer's own desire laid out on the table like goods.

"You lost something," the king said. "Ten years ago. More than one something. A night, a choice, a god's voice in your ear. You've spent a decade feeling around the hole where they used to be, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth." He spread Aldric's hands, palms up, an honest trader's gesture. "I have them."

"No," I said. "You don't. My memories aren't currency."

"Everything is currency. That is the first lesson of your Guild and the last lesson of mine." He rose, and the crown burned steady on his brow, and he walked toward me with Aldric's easy grace, and I could not make myself stand. "Let me show you the goods, at least. You may refuse afterward. Refusal is free. Nothing else in this room is, but refusal—refusal I give away."

He stopped in front of me and reached out, and I should have moved, should have run, should have done anything but sit—but his fingers touched my temple as lightly as a mother brushing hair from a child's face, and the chamber filled with—

Rain. A courtyard behind the palace, rain hammering the flagstones, and my own young hands—smoother, surer, twenty-four years old and certain of everything—tracing a circle of binding in blood that was not entirely mine. A voice beside me, vast and tired and fond, saying *you do not have to do this, little scribe*, and my answer, my arrogant, terrified, certain answer: *yes I do*. The seal closing. The thing beneath the palace thrashing once, twice, going still. And then the god turning to me and saying, in a voice gone quiet with grief, *they will blame me for this. Let them. Let them forget me instead of forgetting you*—

The vision cut off. I was on my feet. I didn't remember standing.

"That's not yours to show," I said, and my voice shook, and I hated it for shaking.

"No. It's *yours*. That's rather my offer." The king sat back down on the throne that was not a throne, unhurried, and folded Aldric's hands. "Here are my terms, ledger-keeper, and I'll make them plain, because I respect plain dealing and I know you do too. Walk away. Take the red-haired woman, take the map, take whatever road leads out of Ashvale, and I will return to you every memory that was taken. All of them. The night under the palace. The binding in your own hand. The god's true face and true voice and the last thing it said to you before it went silent to hide you from me. You will be whole. You will finally know what you did, and why, and what it cost—and you will be free of it, because you'll be far away when the thirteenth moon turns."

"And if I don't walk away?"

"Then you unbind the king, and the binding breaks, and everything I hold comes due at once—including the silence your god bought for you with its own voice. You cannot restore it and save him. Even your blood-oath can't square that ledger. One truth or the other, Petra Winterbourne. That is the arithmetic, and you know I'm right, because you've known it since the astronomer counted the moons." He smiled again, and it was gentle, and that was the cruelest part. "Walk away with your whole self, or stay and lose everything twice. I am not threatening you. I am *offering*. There is a difference, and the difference is that I mean it."

And the terrible thing—the thing I would carry out of that room if I carried anything—was that he did mean it. There was no malice in him. Malice requires wanting, and the thing in the crown wanted nothing except what it was: a treasury, an account, a place where promises went to be kept forever whether their makers wished it or not. It was offering me a fair trade. It was offering me *myself*, priced honestly, and asking only that I abandon a stranger who screamed politely in his own skull and a god who had gone mute to protect me.

It was almost reasonable. That was the trap. Not the temptation—reasonableness. Because every evil I had ever seen in the Guild's ledgers had been entered in a fair hand, at a fair price, with both parties consenting, and the world had rotted anyway.

"The memories," I said. "If I took them. Would I still be the person who walked in here?"

"No," said the king, simply. "You would be the person from before. That person would never have come."

* * *

Beyond the walls, the choir's song thinned another thread. Somewhere below, Sable was bleeding and singing and holding the line, and she didn't even believe in my god, and she was doing it anyway.

I thought about the entry in my own hand I hadn't remembered writing, back in the Guildhall, on the quiet night when all of this began. I thought about the courier dying mid-sentence at the salt door. *Thirteen turns.* I thought about a voice I had loved saying *let them forget me instead of forgetting you*, and how I had spent ten years being exactly the person that sacrifice purchased, and how the thing in the crown was now offering to refund the purchase and unmake the person, and call it mercy.

"You're right about the arithmetic," I said.

The king inclined his head, gracious in victory.

"But you've made one error in the books," I went on, and I felt my hand drift toward the knife at my belt, the one I'd used to sign the last door, the one whose edge I had already promised to worse purposes than this. "You think I came here to choose between truths. I didn't. I came here to write a new entry."

The crown flickered. Just once. Just for a heartbeat, the light in it guttered like a candle in a draft, and behind Aldric's eyes something that was not the parasite and not the king lifted its head.

The choir changed key.

And in the space where the next note should have been, a voice I had not heard in ten years said one word, and the word was not for the king, and it was not for the kingdom, and it was not even, quite, for me.

It said: *Remember.*

The chamber went white.
