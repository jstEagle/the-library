## 15. The Memory They Took From Me Was Mine

The song found the seam in me the way water finds a crack in stone.

I had thought I was whole. I had thought, in the arrogant way of people who keep other people's accounts, that I knew every entry in my own ledger—that the missing decade of my god's voice was a wound, yes, but a clean one, cauterized, with edges I could trace. The choir proved me wrong. It sang through the drowned nave and up through the flooded stairwells and into the chamber where Sable and I stood ankle-deep in black water, and it did not wash over me. It *unlocked* me.

The first thing to come back was not a memory. It was a smell: tallow smoke and cold iron, the smell of the oath-shrine at Vell's Crossing, ten years ago, before Ashvale, before the ledgers, before I learned to make myself small enough to slip through any room unnoticed.

I gripped the wall. The stones were slick and singing.

"Petra." Sable's hand on my arm. "Petra, your eyes—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't touch me. If you touch me it'll take you too."

"It won't." She didn't let go. Of course she didn't. "It's not taking anything. Look at you. It's *giving*."

And the choir swelled, and the giving began.

* * *

Vell's Crossing. Autumn, ten years ago. I was twenty-four and already tired in the way mercenaries get tired—not in the body but in the part of the body that keeps promises.

You have to understand what an oath-bound mercenary was, before the crown made us illegal. We were not soldiers for hire. Soldiers fight for coin and go home when the coin runs out. We fought for *vows*, and our vows had weight—literal weight, literal substance. Break a promise sworn on the shrine-stone and the promise did not simply fail. It turned on you. A lie became a scar across the tongue. An abandoned post became a limp that no physician could name. The god we served—the god whose name I have spent ten years not writing even in my own private thoughts—was the god of Kept Words. Every oath spoken at his shrines became a thread in his keeping, and he held them all, thousands upon thousands, the way a spider holds silk: lightly, patiently, ready to bear weight.

I was good at my work. Not the fighting—I was adequate at fighting—but the *keeping*. When my company took a contract, I was the one who wrote the terms on the shrine-scroll, who bound each clause with a drop of blood and a spoken line. Other mercenaries trusted their swords. Their employers trusted me, because I could read a vow the way a jeweler reads a stone, and I had never once let a flawed one pass my hand.

Which is why they came to me. That autumn. The ones from the palace.

There were three of them, and they wore no livery, which was itself a kind of livery—the kind worn by people whose master's face is on every coin in the realm. They came at dusk, when the shrine was empty, and the eldest of them set a scroll on the altar-stone without unrolling it.

"Winterbourne," he said. "We're told you write bindings that hold."

"I write bindings that are *true*," I said. "Holding is the god's department."

Something passed across his face—pity, maybe, or its cheaper cousin. "This binding is for the god himself."

I laughed. I remember laughing. I remember the sound of it going up into the rafters where the shrine-swifts nested, and how wrong it sounded even as I laughed.

"You want me to bind a god."

"We want you to bind what's under the palace," he said. "And the god is the only ink that will take."

* * *

Here is what I remembered then, standing in the flooded dark while the choir sang: here is what came back to me in order, each piece clicking into place like a bone being reset.

Under the old palace—under the older palace, the one King Aldric's grandfather had built atop and called a cellar—there was a thing. The records called it the Accumulation. The priests had better names. It was what happened when a kingdom swears too much and keeps none of it. Every broken coronation vow, every abandoned treaty, every lie told with a hand on a holy book—all of it went somewhere. Oaths do not evaporate. Words have weight; the god of Kept Words had taught us that, or rather, we had built him out of knowing it. And the weight pooled beneath the capital like water beneath a field, and after four hundred years of royal perjury it had pooled into something with appetite.

It fed on vows. Sworn ones, kept ones—it wasn't picky. It drank the strength out of promises the way a leech drinks blood, and the kingdom had been growing slowly weaker for generations: kings who couldn't hold to a single term of a single treaty, subjects who forgot their own wedding vows mid-ceremony, a slow national softening that everyone blamed on everything except the actual cause.

The plan the three of them brought me was elegant. It was so elegant I loved it immediately, which should have been my first warning, because elegance is the costume hunger wears.

They would not destroy the Accumulation—you cannot destroy accumulated debt; you can only service it. Instead they would bind it: seal it beneath the palace inside a structure made of a single, perfect, self-renewing vow. A vow that would feed it just enough to keep it docile. A vow that would require a keeper—a mortal hand to maintain the binding, renew its clauses, walk its perimeter once a year and speak the renewal aloud.

"And the vow itself?" I asked. "What do we swear?"

The eldest of the three smiled, and I saw for the first time that his teeth were very white and very even, the way teeth are when they are not entirely his own.

"The king will swear to serve the kingdom," he said. "Every dawn, renewed. The Accumulation feeds on the renewal. The kingdom is served by the binding. Everyone keeps their word." He slid the blank scroll toward me. "Except we need someone to write it. Someone whose hand the god trusts. Because the binding must be written in the god's own covenant-ink, and the god lends that ink only to his chosen."

The choir was singing now in the flooded chamber, and I could hear what I had refused to hear for ten years: the song had layers, and the deepest layer was not the imprisoned souls of the king's mind. The deepest layer was *my own handwriting*, recited back to me. Clauses I had drafted at a desk by candlelight. Terms I had negotiated. I knew the phrasing of the fourth clause the way you know your own signature—because it *was* my signature, in the old covenant-script, the one I hadn't written since.

Sable was saying something. Her voice came from far away, down a long corridor of years. I didn't answer her. I was twenty-four years old again, holding a quill over a scroll, asking the question I now understood I had asked precisely wrong:

"Why does the god need to be silent during the renewal? Why does the binding require his silence?"

And the eldest of the three had leaned close, and his breath had smelled of cold cellars, and he had said:

"Because if the god speaks, he will tell you what we are."

* * *

I wrote it.

God forgive me—or don't, since forgiveness was never his department either; keeping was his department, and I am the great unkept thing.

I wrote the binding in three nights of work, and I wrote it well, because I have always written well, because precision is the only prayer I ever truly mastered. I wrote the king's dawn-vow so that it would renew itself, feeding the Accumulation a measured ration of royal devotion. I wrote the perimeter-clauses. I wrote the containment architecture—every wall of the prison was a sentence, and I chose every word.

And then, on the third night, I did the thing I have been fleeing ever since. I did not merely transcribe the binding the three visitors dictated. I *improved* it. I saw a flaw—they had left the binding dependent on the god's ongoing attention, a dependency they claimed was necessary—and I rewrote it so the binding would stand alone, sealed, self-maintaining, requiring nothing from anyone.

I thought I was protecting the god. I thought: if the binding needs nothing from him, then whatever is under the palace can never leverage him, can never reach up through the covenant-ink and drink from him directly. I cut him out to keep him safe.

That was the trap. That was the whole trap, laid for me, by me, with my own cleverness as the spring.

A binding that required the god's attention was a binding the god could *undo*. A binding that required nothing from him was a binding he could not touch without breaking it—and breaking it would release the Accumulation all at once, into a kingdom that had forgotten how to keep faith. My improvement made the prison permanent. My improvement made the god a stranger in his own house. He could see the cage he was locked out of, and he could see me inside it, and he could do nothing.

So he did the only thing left. He went silent.

Not abandoned. Not dead. Silent the way a man goes silent in a house where the walls have ears—because the Accumulation, once bound, had begun to listen through the covenant-ink, and anything the god said aloud would pass along the threads of every oath he held, straight into the thing's hearing. If he spoke, it would learn his voice, learn his chosen, learn the shape of the one hand in the world that could rewrite the binding.

He went silent to hide me.

Ten years of prayers thrown at a closed door, and the door was shut from the outside, by him, against the thing wearing the king's face—the thing I had built the lock for. The parasite in the crown was not some invader that had crept in while the kingdom slept. It was the Accumulation, grown patient, grown clever, wearing Aldric like a glove because the dawn-vow I wrote gave it lawful access to the king's mind every morning of his reign. I had written the lease. I had signed the god's name as witness. I had used the covenant-ink.

Every choice since has been a flight from this one. The Guild, the ledgers, the careful invisibility, the small precise life balancing accounts of impossible things—I built myself a new shrine out of double-entry bookkeeping and hid inside it, telling myself I was a person who merely recorded debts, never the person who authored the largest one in the kingdom's history.

The choir finished the verse. In the flooded chamber, the water around my ankles had gone still as glass, and in it I could see my reflection, and behind my reflection, faint as a watermark, lines of covenant-script glowing on my skin—the binding, written on me, because the last clause of any binding lives in the hand that wrote it. I had carried the prison's blueprint in my blood for ten years. That was why the map had appeared. That was why the courier had found me. The map was never leading me to the king's mind.

It was leading me home to the text.

"Petra." Sable's voice, close now, real, her grip hard enough to bruise. "Come back. Whatever you saw—we need you *here*."

I looked at her. Red hair plastered wet to her face, the brand on her wrist showing pale through the water, her eyes doing that thing they did when she was frightened and refusing to file it under fear.

"I wrote it," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's ledger entry. "The binding. The dawn-vow. The silence. All of it. I'm not the messenger, Sable. I'm the author."

She didn't flinch. I watched her decide not to flinch, and I understood in that moment why I had trusted her against every professional instinct I owned.

"Then you're the only one who can unwrite it," she said.

Above us, through fathoms of stone and water and singing, the thirteenth moon cleared the horizon, and the choir changed key—and somewhere far above, a king opened his mouth and began, at last, to scream.
