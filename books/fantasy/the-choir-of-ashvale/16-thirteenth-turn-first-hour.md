## 16. Thirteenth Turn, First Hour

Sable Vane had spent her whole life learning how to leave.

It was the first thing the Guild taught its property, though they never called it that. How to read a room for exits. How to keep a purse heavy enough to buy passage and light enough to run with. How to love nothing so much that leaving it would slow you down. She'd been eleven when an enforcer with kind eyes explained it to her over a stolen apple: *The ones who survive, little bird, are the ones who can walk away from anything.*

She had walked away from plenty. A hometown whose name she no longer owned. A brother who died at a salt door with his blood freezing black on the stones. Three employers, two identities, one marriage of six weeks that she still counted as her best accounting error. Sable Vane could leave anything. It was the one ledger entry she'd never once failed to balance.

So it was a particular insult, she thought, standing in the drowned dark beneath Ashvale's ruined cathedral with water to her knees and the thirteenth moon bleeding down through a crack in the world, that she could not seem to make her feet move toward the open door.

* * *

The escape route was real. That was the bitter joke of it. After everything—the raids, the lies, the enforcers at the gate, the wound in her side that Petra had stitched by candlelight with hands that shook only after the work was done—after all of that, the way out was simply *there*. The choir's song had shifted an hour past midnight, and where the song shifted, the undercrofts obeyed. A wall that had been wall since the founding of the kingdom now stood open like a mouth, breathing cold river air into a corridor that hadn't seen sky in three hundred years. Beyond it: stairs, then the old smugglers' walk along the Graywash, then the marsh road, then anywhere in the world that wasn't here.

Sable had found it twenty minutes ago. She'd stood in it. She'd felt the wind on her face and thought, with something close to relief, *good, there's a way out if we need one.*

Then she'd gone back to find Petra.

That was the problem, she realized now. That was the whole rotten problem, laid out as clean as a column of figures. Somewhere between the debtors' tavern and the gate where she'd held three enforcers off with a knife and a lie and a brand she'd spent fifteen years hiding, she had stopped being a person who left.

* * *

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone and earth, the city was screaming.

Not all of it. Not yet. But sworn oaths were fraying everywhere at once—Sable could hear it coming up through the floor like heat through iron. A dockhand's contract with his crew, sworn on salt, unraveling into argument and thrown tools. A marriage vow somewhere in the Merchant Quarter going brittle as old varnish; she'd heard glass break, then weeping, then nothing. The Guild itself was not immune. She could feel the oath that bound every ledger-keeper to the Guildmaster pulling loose in her chest, thread by thread, like a stitch being picked out of a wound. It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. It just felt like *less*, like rooms in a house going dark one by one.

Every promise ever made in Ashvale was anchored somewhere, and the anchor was failing.

And under it all, growing louder as the moon completed its turn, the choir sang.

They had heard it properly for the first time two days ago, descending past the cathedral's drowned nave: dozens of voices, maybe hundreds, singing the king's mind into order—or into captivity, the distinction having stopped mattering somewhere in the last century. It should have been ugly. It was not. It was the most beautiful thing Sable had ever heard, and she hated it for that, because beautiful things were always the most expensive, and the price of this one was written in Petra's face every time the song swelled. Because one voice in the choir belonged to a god who had gone silent ten years ago, and Petra knew it the way you know your own name even when someone else is wearing it.

Now the song was changing key. Sable didn't have the words for what she was hearing, but her body did: her teeth ached, the water in the corridor trembled in rings, and somewhere far above, a bell tower lost an argument with gravity.

"Petra." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Whatever you're doing down there, do it faster."

* * *

Petra was below. In the map's central chamber, in the heart of the thing, doing whatever a person does when they rewrite a binding in their own blood while a god sings through the walls. Sable's job was simpler and worse: hold the song steady. Keep the choir's rhythm unbroken while Petra worked, because if the song faltered—if the parasite noticed what was happening inside its own house—it would do to Petra what it had done to King Aldric, only faster, and without the courtesy of leaving anyone alive to scream politely.

So Sable stood at the junction where five corridors met, in water to her knees, with her knife drawn and her side throbbing and the moon completing its cycle overhead, and she kept time.

She'd discovered by accident that the song responded to her. Two nights ago, testing the map in the undercrofts, she'd tapped a rhythm against a stone—one-two-three, one-two-three, the cadence of a counting-song her mother had sung before her mother's name was taken—and the choir had bent toward it, the way grass bends toward light. The imprisoned voices wanted order. They had been singing order into a madman's skull for longer than anyone could remember, and they were starving for a beat to hold onto.

So she gave them one. Knife-hilt against stone, steady as a clerk's hand, hour after hour. One-two-three. One-two-three. The most important arithmetic in the kingdom, and no ledger would ever record it.

Her arms burned. The wound in her side had soaked through its bandage an hour ago. The moon above was doing something wrong—she could see it through the crack in the vaulting, swollen and copper-colored, hanging at the horizon like a coin refusing to drop—and she understood, with the cold clarity that had kept her alive her whole life, that dawn was not coming the way dawns came. The thirteenth turn was arriving early. The deadline wasn't approaching. It was *here*.

And in the king's palace on the hill, something began to eat.

* * *

She felt it before she heard it. A lurch, deep under the water, under the stone, under the bedrock—a swallow. The whole city flinched around her. Bells that hadn't rung in years went off all at once across Ashvale, wild and unscheduled, and the choir's song spiked into a register that made her bleed from one nostril, and she kept tapping, one-two-three, one-two-three, because stopping was death and she had decided, apparently, sometime in the last month without signing anything, that she was not done living.

Through the crack in the vaulting, the copper moon shuddered. And then, horribly, it *moved*—not drifting, not setting, but contracting, drawing inward like a pupil narrowing, and every oath in the city pulled tight around its failure.

The fraying became tearing. She heard it happen in waves: a judge's oath of office snapping somewhere near the courts, followed by a sound like a crowd realizing it could do anything. The Guild's own binding oath went out in her chest entirely, all threads cut, and Sable gasped—because what rushed into the space it left was not freedom. It was the memory of her brother's face, which the Guild's oath had apparently been sitting on for years, sharp and specific and unbearable, Tam's crooked grin and the wax seal he'd pressed into her hands—

No. Later. Grieve later. Count later. One-two-three.

The third swallow came, and this time the water around her knees glowed faintly, gold going gray, and she understood that the glow was the king. What remained of him. Light draining out of the man on the hill as the thing in the crown stopped pretending to share and started taking the whole meal, and the choir screamed—not sang, *screamed*—and Sable did the only thing she could think of, which was to tap harder, and shout the count aloud, and put her whole wrecked body into keeping the beat the way you'd hold a door shut against a flood.

"ONE. TWO. THREE. ONE. TWO. THREE."

The choir caught it. Bent back toward order. Held.

And that was when she saw the figure at the end of the corridor, and understood that the night had one more bill to present.

* * *

An enforcer. Alone. One of the Guildmaster's, tall, gray-cloaked, standing in the flooded passage with the calm of a man who has already won. Behind him, through the open wall, the smugglers' route breathed its cold clean air, and Sable measured the distance without meaning to—old habit, exit-reading, eleven years old again with an apple in her hand.

"You're bleeding out," the enforcer said. His voice carried flat over the water. "The route behind me goes to the marshes. By noon you're in Vellichor province, and none of this ever touched you. The Guildmaster's word on it. He doesn't want you dead, Vane. He wants you *gone*, and he wants Winterbourne's corpse, and those are two separate errands."

"And if I stay?"

"Then when the moon finishes turning, you die in a hole under a cathedral for a person who will not remember why you bothered." He tilted his head. "You've read the ledgers, Vane. You know what the unbinding costs. You've done the sums better than anyone. This is a bad investment."

He was right. That was the obscenity of it—he was *right*, by every rule she'd ever lived by. Every calculation she'd ever trusted said walk away. Love nothing so much that leaving it slows you down. The ones who survive are the ones who can go.

Sable looked down at her own hands, still tapping the count against her knife hilt. Steady. Steadier than they'd ever been.

"I used to be Guild property," she said. "Number and everything. Branded. You know what the first lesson was? They sat me down when I was eleven and told me the secret of survival." She smiled, and watched him take a half-step back, which was correct of him. "Walk away from anything. I was *very* good at it. Ask anyone. Ask my brother—oh, wait."

The enforcer's hand moved toward his weapon.

"But here's the thing about ledgers," Sable said, louder, over the choir, over the bells, over the sound of a kingdom's promises coming apart. "Every entry balances eventually. Every debt gets called. And I have been carrying one entry for fifteen years that I never wrote down, because I didn't have the words for it, because I was taught that wanting things was how property gets damaged." The moon contracted again. The water flashed white. She raised her voice until it tore. "I know exactly why I'm bothering. I know exactly what this costs. I'm staying because I love her, you gray-souled son of a filing cabinet, and I am DONE leaving—"

The enforcer lunged. Sable turned the lunge with her shoulder, opened his thigh with the knife, and put him down into the floodwater with a knee and an elbow and fifteen years of being worth exactly what the Guild paid for her, which had always been more than they knew.

He didn't get up quickly. Good. She stepped past him, took one long breath of the cold air from the open route—the smell of river and marsh and morning, the smell of *anywhere else*—and let it out.

Then she turned her back on the way out, waded deeper into the dark, toward the chamber where Petra was rewriting the world in blood, and kept the count.

One-two-three. One-two-three.

Above her, the thirteenth moon finished its turn, and the first hour of the new world began with a sound like a page being torn out of a very large book, and Sable Vane, who could leave anything, stayed.
