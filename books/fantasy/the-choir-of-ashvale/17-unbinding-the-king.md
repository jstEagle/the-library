## 17. Unbinding the King

**Petra**

The dreaming core was not a room. That was the first thing the map had never managed to say, because maps are made of rooms and this was made of a man.

Petra stood at the edge of it and understood why every chamber they'd crossed had cost a memory to open. They hadn't been paying tolls. They'd been paying *ink*. Every recollection surrendered in the undercrofts—the name of their first sergeant, the taste of their mother's bread, the color of the sky over a battlefield whose name they could no longer find—had drained down through the buried cathedral, through the drowned nave, into this place, where it pooled and thickened and became the floor on which King Aldric slept.

The king's mind was built out of what people remembered about him. And the thing wearing his crown had been eating the supply for years.

It was vast and dim and warm, like standing inside a closed mouth. Above them—though *above* meant nothing here—the choir sang on, Sable's voice threaded through forty stolen ones, holding the song steady the way you hold a rope over a drop. Petra could feel her up there in the drowned nave, feet braced against the flooded stone, blood from her gate-wound seeping into water that had been waiting ten years for exactly that flavor of sacrifice.

*Hold,* Petra thought at her, uselessly, the way you think at someone across a battlefield. *Just hold.*

Ahead, past the last threshold, something glowed like a coal wrapped in silk: the king's dreaming core, the small stubborn light Aldric had kept burning while everything else in him was devoured. Around it coiled the binding—Petra's own work, ten years old, written in an oath so old its language had been dead before Ashvale had a name. It looked like scar tissue. It looked like a cage built by someone who had loved the thing inside it and hated themselves for it.

The parasite's voice came from everywhere, patient as interest accruing.

*You've come to read your own handwriting.*

"I've come to correct it," Petra said aloud, because in this place speaking was writing, and they needed ink.

The map unrolled itself in their hands—no longer paper now, but skin, warm, the wax seal long since broken into thirteen pieces that floated around them like slow red moons. The final chamber was labeled in Petra's own hand, as it always had been:

*HERE THE KING. HERE THE DEBT. HERE THE HAND THAT WROTE IT.*

"Then let's settle accounts," Petra whispered, and drew the oath-knife across their left palm.

Blood-oath was the only currency the binding recognized. They had known that since the undercrofts; they had known it, probably, for ten years, in the place where taken memories go when they aren't destroyed but only *filed*. The cut hurt the way truth hurts—cleanly, and all at once—and the blood rose glowing, and the map drank it, and the first line of the old binding appeared beneath their feet like a vein of fire in black stone.

Petra knelt and began to rewrite.

* * *

**Sable**

The nave was drowning and Sable Vane was singing, which would have been funny if she'd had any breath to spare for laughter.

Forty voices. That was what the choir had been reduced to—forty prisoners folded into the cathedral's stones, singing the king's mind into order, or into captivity, depending on which ledger you believed. When she and Petra had descended past the flooded galleries, the song had faltered, because songs falter when their keepers see hope, and hope is destabilizing. So Sable had done the only thing she knew how to do with a failing account.

She'd covered the shortfall herself.

Her voice slid into the gap and held there, and the choir—those drowned, patient dead—leaned against her note like tired men leaning on a wall, and the song steadied. It was the hardest thing she had ever done and she had once talked her way out of a Guild debtors' pit with a broken wrist and a forged signature.

The water was at her knees. Her shoulder wound had soaked through its bandage twice. She could feel the song's structure now the way she felt ledgers—not the words, which were older than words, but the *arithmetic* underneath them. Each verse balanced a room in the king's mind. Each refrain locked a door. And one verse, near the end, was wrong—had been wrong for ten years—a rest where there should have been a note, a silence shaped exactly like a god.

She found herself singing toward that silence. Filling it. Not replacing it—nothing could replace it—but keeping its shape open, the way you keep a chair empty at a table for someone who might still come home.

Above her, through the drowned roof, the thirteenth moon was setting. Below her, somewhere in the dark, Petra was bleeding onto a map and rewriting a prison.

The water shivered. Far off, in the city above, something enormous shifted in its sleep, and every bell in Ashvale rang once without being touched.

"Hold," Sable told the choir, and her voice cracked on it, and the choir held anyway.

* * *

**Petra**

Three lines rewritten. The cost was immediate and itemized.

The first line took the memory of learning to write. Petra watched their own penmanship continue without them, hands moving in patterns their fingers no longer understood, and did not stop.

The second line took the face of the Guildmaster as he'd been the night he'd offered them everything—the kind eyes, the quiet offer, the trap dressed as mercy. Good riddance, some cold surviving part of them thought. One less face to carry.

The third line reached for something deeper, and Petra felt it coming like weather: the memory of the day the prayers stopped being answered. The shrine. The ash. The decade of disciplined, grief-shaped silence.

"No," Petra said—and then, kneeling in their own glowing blood, understood.

That was the payment the binding wanted. Of course it was. The god had gone silent to hide them; the silence was the lock's first pin; and to rewrite the lock, they had to give back the silence, had to return the very absence they'd been living inside for ten years. The binding didn't want their pain. It wanted the *container* of their pain.

Petra paid it.

The memory came back whole and terrible: a younger self at a shrine outside the palace undercrofts, knife in palm, swearing the sealing-oath while their god stood witness—and then the god turning, deliberately, folding itself shut like a book before an enemy reads it, going silent not as punishment, not as abandonment, but as *shelter*. Hiding Petra inside their own guilt. Making them too small and too sad for the crown's occupant to bother finding.

Ten years of unanswered prayer, and the answer had been the silence itself.

Petra wept without pausing, and wrote the fourth line, and the fifth, and the choir above them swelled as if the song could smell the ink.

* * *

**Sable**

The moon touched the horizon and the parasite stopped being subtle.

It came up through the water as pressure first—then as light, red and wrong, pouring down through the drowned roof in shafts that moved like searchlights. The choir screamed, forty voices bending into one, and Sable planted her boots and sang louder, and discovered that rage harmonizes beautifully.

She could feel it below her now, the crown's occupant, waking fully for the first time in centuries. It wasn't fighting the song. It was *eating* it—swallowing verses, digesting refrains, growing fat on the very music meant to contain it. The king's mind was fraying at the edges. Somewhere above, in the palace, she knew, oaths were starting to fray with it: marriages loosening, contracts unraveling, soldiers forgetting why they'd sworn.

Her own brand—the old property-mark on her wrist, the one she'd shown no one until three enforcers made showing it necessary—began to burn. Because it was an oath too. Everything in Ashvale was an oath. The whole city was a ledger, and the ink was failing.

And into that burning, that failing, that fraying dark, one word arrived.

Not spoken. Not sung. It came the way a debt comes due—everywhere at once, undeniable, addressed to no one and therefore to everyone. It came up through the water and through the stone and through the forty drowned throats of the choir, and Sable's voice broke around it and reformed after it, and she would spend the rest of her life trying to describe what it sounded like and settling, always, on the same inadequate phrase: *like a door opening after ten years.*

One word. A god's first word in a decade, spent not on thunder, not on judgment, but on instruction:

***Remember.***

The choir caught it and carried it downward, forty voices passing it hand to hand like a bucket in a fire-line, and Sable sang it too, weeping freely now, salt water and salt water, giving the word to the man below with the knife and the map and the oldest handwriting in the kingdom.

*Remember,* the cathedral sang. *Remember, remember, remember.*

* * *

**Petra**

The word hit the binding like a key hitting a lock that had forgotten it was a lock.

The seventh line—the last line—was already half-written in Petra's blood when the god's voice arrived, and the old scar-tissue script of the original binding lit from within, and Petra finally *read* it. Not translated. Remembered. Their own words, ten years younger, addressed to the thing beneath the palace:

*You will wear his shape and hunger on his hunger, and you will be bound by every vow sworn in this kingdom, until the hand that bound you unbinds you, and may whatever gods remain forgive the hand that binds you.*

Their god had remained. Had remained silent, hidden, folded shut—one god remaining, in hiding, guarding the one person who could undo it all.

And now the last line waited under Petra's knife-hand, blank, asking the only question left: *what replaces the binding?*

Because bindings don't get deleted. They get transferred. Every law of impossible things said so; every ledger Petra had ever balanced said so; the parasite itself, wearing the king's face somewhere below in the central chamber, had offered them the arithmetic plainly: *restore the god and the king dies with me; save the king and your god stays mute forever.* Two columns. Two totals. Pick one.

Petra looked at the third column, the one no honest accountant is supposed to add.

They pressed their bleeding palm flat against the blank line and spoke—not the dead language, not the formal oath-words, just the plain truth, which is the oldest ink there is:

"I take the binding into myself. My body for the prison. My years for the mortar. My name for the lock. The king goes free. The god goes free. I stay."

The blood flared white. The map dissolved into light and poured upward through Petra's chest like water finding its level, and every chamber they'd opened, every memory they'd paid, every room of the king's sleeping mind came home through them at once—too much, far too much, a kingdom's worth of remembering forced through one mortal set of doors—

And the god answered.

Not with thunder. With presence: vast and close and grieving and *grateful*, filling the cathedral the way dawn fills a valley, and its second word in ten years was not a command but a question, asked gently, already knowing the answer:

***Are you certain?***

"No," Petra said, laughing, crying, dissolving. "Do it anyway."

* * *

**Sable**

From the drowned nave, Sable watched the light go up.

It went up through the flooded roof in a single column, red moonlight and gold god-light braided together, and where it passed, the water stilled, and the choir's forty voices were released one by one—she heard them go, little exhalations of relief, forty debts forgiven—and the song ended, really ended, for the first time in ten years, and the silence afterward was not the god's silence. It was just quiet. Ordinary. The most expensive quiet she had ever heard.

Then the crown burned.

She felt it rather than saw it—felt the parasite's scream pass through the stone, through the water, through her brand, through every oath in Ashvale at once, a single vast note of fury and starvation cut off mid-howl. The bells rang again, all of them, and this time they kept ringing, wild and joyous and completely unscheduled, and somewhere above, she knew, a king was waking in his bed with tears on his face and nothing in his head but himself.

The water began to drain. Not flood—drain, the drowned galleries exhaling ten years of held breath, and Sable stood knee-deep, then ankle-deep, then dry, in a nave that was remembering how to be a nave.

"Petra." Her voice came out wrecked. She tried again. "Petra."

The light in the depths faded slowly, reluctantly, like a lamp being carried away down a long corridor. Where the dreaming core had been there was now only a small figure kneeling on bare stone, and even from here, even across the whole drowned length of the cathedral, Sable could see what the unbinding had taken: the way the figure knelt was wrong, emptied, a coat with no one in it, tipping slowly sideways.

She was moving before she decided to move, splashing through the shallows, sliding down the last stair on her wounded shoulder and not caring, because the ledger was open and the entry was unwritten and she had never in her life left an account unbalanced.

She caught Petra before the stone did.

Their eyes were open. That was the worst part. Their eyes were open and looking at her—looking *through* her—with the polite blankness of a stranger, and their lips moved, shaping a question they no longer had the words to ask.

"It's all right," Sable lied, holding them, rocking them, blood and drain-water and god-light between them. "I've got you. I keep good books. I'll write it all down. Everything. I swear it—I swear it on—"

On what? On whom? The god was free and gone; the oaths were fraying; the city above was already forgetting, she could feel it happening, the crisis dissolving out of a million minds like sugar in rain.

So she swore on the only thing left that was hers to give.

"On me," she finished. "I swear it on me."

Far above, through the clearing water and the ringing bells, the thirteenth moon set, and the sun of a kingdom that would never remember what it owed came up gray and ordinary over Ashvale.

In her arms, Petra Winterbourne slept the sleep of a paid debt—and did not know her own name.
