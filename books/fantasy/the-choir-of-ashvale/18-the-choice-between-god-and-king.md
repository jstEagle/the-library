## 18. The Choice Between God and King

The word *Remember* was still hanging in the air when the choir stopped singing.

Not faded. Stopped. Two hundred voices cut off at once, the way a blade cuts a rope, and the silence that followed was worse than any sound the undercroft had produced all night. It had weight. It pressed down on Petra's shoulders like a hand.

"Sable." Their voice came out cracked. "Sable, tell me you're still holding it."

A pause that lasted three of Petra's heartbeats. Then, from somewhere above and behind—up at the lip of the drowned nave where Sable stood knee-deep in black water with her wounded arm raised like a conductor's baton—"I'm here. I'm holding. Whatever you're doing down there, do it faster."

The king's dreaming core lay open before them.

It did not look like a chamber. It looked like a wound dressed as a garden. The map's final room resolved around them as they crossed its threshold: walls of pale membrane veined with gold, the gold pulsing in time with a heartbeat too slow to be human. Oaths grew here like ivy—thousands of them, tens of thousands, every vow ever sworn on Ashvale soil curling along the walls in ink-dark script, drinking light, bearing fruit that looked suspiciously like teeth. And at the center, rooted in the floor like an old tree in an old courtyard, sat the thing wearing Aldric's face.

It had stopped pretending to be the king. That was the worst part. In the throne room it had worn him like a glove—smiling his smile, signing his name, bowing his shadow half a second late. Here, in the room where he actually lived, it had let the glove fall. What remained was something older and hungrier wearing Aldric's features the way a coat hangs on a hook: correct in every line, empty in every eye.

"You've brought blood-ink," it said. Its voice was the king's voice, but slowed, the way a song slows when the singer forgets the words. "Clever. The Guild taught you well."

"The Guild built you," Petra said. They kept walking. Each step cost something—they could feel it now, the toll the rooms had been charging all along, except this room charged interest. With every stride, a memory loosened. The smell of their mother's kitchen went first. Then the name of the man who'd taught them to hold a sword. They kept walking. "And I helped. So let's skip the part where you tell me how reasonable you are."

"It *is* reasonable." The parasite spread its hands—Aldric's hands, scarred from thirty years of real wars it had never fought. Around it, the oaths on the walls brightened, feeding. "That is what no one understands. I am not a thief, Petra Winterbourne. I am a *bank*. Every promise your kingdom makes, someone must hold. Someone must keep the ledger of vows, or the vows rot. Your god held them for nine hundred years. Your god grew tired. I volunteered."

"You devoured him."

"I *inherited* him. There is a difference, and the difference is written in every contract your Guild has ever brokered." The thing tilted its head, and for one horrible moment the emptiness in its eyes filled with something almost like kindness. "You are bleeding memories into my floor right now. Shall I tell you which ones? No—you'd only want them back, and we both know how that negotiation ends."

Behind Petra, faintly, Sable's voice rose—not words, just sound, a low steady note she was feeding into the choir's absence, keeping the song's skeleton standing while the body of it lay dead. She couldn't know if it was working. She was doing it anyway. Petra filed that away, the way they filed everything, and turned back to the work.

The binding lay before them: the original script, the first binding, the one they had written ten years ago in a memory that had been taken from them as payment. They could read it now. Of course they could. You always recognize your own handwriting, even after a decade, even after it's been carved out of you and used to cage a god.

It was good work. That was the humiliating truth of it. Young Petra, twenty-two years old and full of oath-fire, had written a binding so clean, so elegant, that the god inside it had chosen to go silent rather than strain against it—because straining would have torn the kingdom apart along with him. The silence hadn't been punishment. It had been courtesy. A god, holding still, so the walls wouldn't fall on the people who built them.

"Here is your choice," the parasite said, and now it sounded genuinely weary, genuinely old, and Petra hated how much they understood it. "You stand at the root with blood-ink on your hands. Unbind me fully, restore your god, and I take the king with me—he has been my host so long that we share a pulse. Cut me out gently, leave him breathing, and your god stays in his prison forever, because the binding that holds me holds him, and you cannot amend one clause without voiding the other." It smiled with the king's mouth. "God or king. Choose. Everyone chooses. It is the oldest entry in every ledger."

Petra knelt at the root of the binding and opened their own forearm with a mercenary's knife, the way they'd been taught, the way they'd done once before in a life they couldn't remember.

Blood welled. Ink-dark. Oath-colored.

They thought about ledgers. That was the trick of it, they realized—the thing ten years of bookkeeping had been quietly teaching them without their consent. Every impossible trade the Guild brokered came down to the same question: *who pays?* And every honest ledger-keeper knew there was a third answer that nobody offered because nobody wanted it. Not the buyer pays. Not the seller pays. The *house* pays. The house eats the loss. The house absorbs the debt into its own foundations and stands anyway, creaking, until it falls.

The Guild had never once paid its own debts.

Petra intended to.

"There's a third path," they said.

The parasite's smile flickered. "There is no third path. I have eaten kings, priests, and one very stubborn archivist. There is never a third—"

"You're a creature of oaths," Petra said. "You said it yourself. A bank. A holder of vows. Fine. Then hear a new vow, sworn properly, in blood, by a bonded mercenary with standing to make it." They pressed their bleeding arm to the root of the binding, to the first line of their own handwriting, and began to speak.

They spoke slowly, because bindings punish haste. They spoke in the old formulae, the ones that made broken promises into wounds, because those were the only words with teeth enough for this. And as they spoke, the blood-ink crawled off their skin and into the script on the wall, line by line, clause by clause, amending.

*"I swear the house pays. I swear the debt comes home. Let the prison be flesh. Let the walls be bone. Let the keeper be kept. What was bound in stone I bind in myself; what was silent in a god goes silent in me; and when the thirteenth moon sets, let the crown burn and the king wake and the god walk free—for I will hold what he held, all of it, every vow, every year, every grief in every bottle, until my body fails or the world forgives me, whichever comes last."*

The room screamed.

Not the parasite—the *room*. The membrane walls rippled; the oath-ivy shuddered and began to pull itself free of the stone, roots tearing loose, thousands of vows suddenly homeless and panicking. The parasite lunged up from its roots, Aldric's face contorting through a dozen expressions that weren't his—grief, rage, and underneath both, unmistakably, fear.

"You don't know what you're swearing," it hissed. "Nine hundred years of vows. Do you understand the *weight*? Your god was a god, and it bent him near to breaking. You are meat. You are a woman with a knife and bad handwriting—"

"My handwriting," Petra said, "is excellent," and signed.

They dragged their palm through their own blood and pressed it flat against the final line, and the binding took.

* * *

There was no pain at first. That was the trap of it, and Petra knew it was a trap, and stepped in anyway.

What came instead was *arrival*. Nine hundred years of promises flooding into a body built for maybe sixty. A farmer's vow to his dying wife. A soldier's vow to a brother who never came home. A child's vow, sworn on a dare, to love the sea forever. They arrived not as memories but as *debts*, each one a small hot coal settling into Petra's ribs, into the marrow, into the spaces between cells. Petra's spine arched. Their teeth locked. Somewhere very far away Sable was shouting their name, and the shout sounded like it was coming up a long well.

Then the god left.

Petra felt it happen—a vast pressure lifting out of the walls, out of the deep places, nine centuries of held breath finally released. The silence that had defined their whole adult life broke open like ice in spring. For one instant, one shining terrible instant, Petra felt their god at close range: enormous, exhausted, gentle, *grateful*, a presence that had spent a decade holding still so the walls wouldn't fall, now unfolding joint by joint into freedom—

—and it looked at them. At the small mortal creature kneeling in its former prison, already filling up with the weight it had carried.

It said nothing. Gods, freed after nine hundred years, apparently don't need many words. But Petra felt the look the way you feel sunlight through a window: warmth, and distance, and the knowledge that the glass is between you.

Then it was gone, up through the membrane, up through the cathedral, up into whatever sky exists for things like it, and the room began to die.

The parasite died with it. That was the arithmetic, and Petra had known it would be: the binding transferred, the bank changed hands, and a bank cannot survive the insolvency of its vault. The thing wearing Aldric's face unraveled from the edges inward, oaths streaming off it like smoke, streaming *into* Petra, each one another coal, another debt, another weight. It looked at them while it came apart. It did not curse them. It looked almost relieved.

"Cruel," it whispered, in the king's voice, in a voice that was nearly its own. "And clean. The best trades usually are."

Then it was gone, and the walls came down, and the king began to wake.

* * *

Petra didn't remember falling. They remembered the water—black nave-water rising around them as the undercrofts gave up their architecture, chambers folding shut one by one, the map's geography closing like a book whose story was finished. They remembered trying to stand and discovering that their body now contained a cathedral's worth of obligation and had opinions about standing.

They remembered Sable reaching them. Red hair plastered dark with water, left arm soaked red to the elbow, her face doing something Petra had never seen it do in all their careful months of mutual suspicion. She hauled them upright, got their arm over her shoulders, and took their weight without complaint—which, from Sable Vane, was practically a marriage vow.

"The king?" she demanded.

"Waking." Petra's voice sounded like gravel in a bucket. "Free. He'll weep. Let him."

"And you?"

Petra tried to answer and found the answer had been repossessed. They reached for the Guild—found a shape where it should be, a hole shaped like ten years. Reached for their god—found silence, but a different silence now, an ordinary human silence, the kind everyone lives inside. Reached for themselves and found the shelves mostly bare, a few ledgers left behind like furniture in a sold house.

"Sable," they said carefully, "when we get out of here, remind me who I am. I appear to have traded it."

She laughed. It was a wet, awful, wonderful sound. "Remind *you*? Winterbourne, I lost my hometown's name to a closet three days ago. We'll remind each other." She adjusted her grip, and the water rose, and somewhere above them the thirteenth moon touched the horizon and began, at last, to set.

"Move," Sable said. "The whole city's about to forget us. I intend to be insulted in person."

Petra laughed—or tried to—and the undercrofts groaned, and the last thing they saw before the dark took them was the first light of a dawn with one fewer moon-turn in it, coming down through the cracks of a breaking cathedral, toward two people climbing out of the debt of the world.

Then the dark. Then nothing.

Then—much later, or perhaps no time at all—a voice reading aloud, slowly, patiently, from a page that had not existed yesterday:

*"Entry the first. Her name is Petra Winterbourne..."*
