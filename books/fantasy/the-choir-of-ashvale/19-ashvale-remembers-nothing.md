## 19. Ashvale Remembers Nothing

The crown burned the way a lie burns when it's finally said out loud: all at once, and brighter than it had any right to.

Sable felt it before she saw it. She was holding the song—Petra's blood-ink binding humming through her palms where she pressed them flat against the choir's stone—and the note she'd been carrying for what felt like a year and no time at all simply *changed*. It didn't stop. It *opened*, like a fist unclenching after decades, and every voice in the drowned nave below her came apart into something that wasn't captivity anymore.

Then the light came up through the floor.

Not firelight. Not moonlight. Something older than either, the color of a word being erased, and Sable threw her arm across her eyes as the central chamber above—or below; the undercrofts had stopped respecting direction hours ago—lit up like dawn trapped in a bottle.

"Petra," she said.

She'd been saying it for a while now, she realized. Saying it into the dark, saying it over the tremors that were shaking plaster dust from ceilings three districts away, saying it because someone had to keep count of the name while everything else came undone.

The chamber where she'd left them was filling with light and emptying of everything else.

She ran.

Her boots found the steps by memory alone—the map was ash in her pocket now, or would be soon, because maps of a mind being freed don't survive the freeing—and she took the last flight at a dead slide, shoulder against the wall, and burst into the central chamber with her knife already out out of pure habit.

There was nothing to fight.

That was the worst part. She'd braced for the parasite's last lunge, for the king's dreaming core to lash out, for *something* with teeth. Instead there was only Petra Winterbourne, lying on their back on the flagstones with their eyes closed and their chest rising in long, slow breaths, and above them, hanging in the air where the crown's occupant had worn its borrowed face, a shape made entirely of oaths was coming apart thread by thread.

And threaded through those threads—she could see them now, could see everything now, the song had opened her ears the way it had opened everything—were strands of gold going *into* Petra. Into their hands, their throat, the hollow behind their sternum.

They were drinking it down. All of it. Every vow the thing had hoarded, every promise ever sworn in Ashvale that the crown had fed on for a century—they were taking it into their body, because someone had to be the prison, and they hadn't even asked her first.

"You absolute—" Sable started, and then the memory went.

* * *

It went like weather.

One moment she was kneeling beside Petra, screaming at them for choosing martyrdom without consulting her, and the next she was kneeling beside Petra and couldn't remember why she was angry. There was an anger-shaped hole in her chest and no anger in it. There was a name on her lips and no reason for it.

Above the palace, visible through the chamber's high slit of a window, the thirteenth moon touched the horizon and began, impossibly, to rise backward. Unwinding. The cycle closing its own book.

And across the city, Sable felt memories die the way you feel rain coming—a pressure, a hush, a softness settling over everything.

She watched it happen from the one place in Ashvale that remembered watching.

The choir's song had done more than free the king. It had turned the whole crisis inside out, made it a dream the city had dreamed together, and dreams dissolve at dawn. The tremors stopped. The fraying oaths—she could feel them, thousands of them, marriages and debts and deathbed promises all unraveling since midnight—knotted themselves back together, smooth as if they'd never strained. Somewhere up there, a kingdom was waking up with a strange taste in its mouth and no idea why.

Somewhere up there, a king was waking weeping and free, and would never know what he'd wept about.

"Good," Sable said aloud, to no one, because her voice worked better than silence. "Good. Forget it. Forget all of it."

She looked down at Petra's face—slack, younger without the ledger-keeper's careful blankness, a face that had finally put down a weight it had carried for ten years—and understood, with the particular cruelty of people who keep books, exactly what the price had bought.

The god was mute forever. The king was free. And Petra Winterbourne, who had written the first binding with their own hand and carried the guilt of it through a decade of silent prayers, was paying the balance in the only coin left.

Themselves.

"No," Sable said. Then, louder, in case the universe kept ledgers too: "*No.* You don't get to do this. You promised me one truth. One. And I got it, I'm holding it, I'm not giving it back—so you don't get to leave before I collect the rest."

Petra's breathing didn't change.

But the gold threads did. They pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating, and Sable—who had spent her whole life reading what people wrote instead of what they said—read it anyway.

*Still here. Barely. Hurry.*

* * *

Getting an unconscious oath-prison out of a collapsing undercroft is not a skill they teach at the Guild.

Sable learned it anyway, fast, the way she learned everything: by refusing to fail at it in front of witnesses, even when the witness was a dying cathedral.

The undercrofts were coming down around them—not violently, not anymore, but with the tired dignity of a structure whose purpose had ended. Rooms that had shifted when unobserved were shifting now permanently, walls grinding into new positions like a sleeper turning over, and the map in Sable's pocket had gone soft and blank as wet paper. The buried places were forgetting themselves along with everyone else.

She dragged Petra through the drowned nave with their arm over her shoulders and her knife between her teeth because she needed both hands and refused to leave the blade behind—it was Guild property, technically, and spite was the one fuel that never ran dry. The water was knee-deep and rising as the old channels broke open, and every step she took, she talked.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out somewhere around the fourth collapsed gallery, wading through a century of the city's secrets while the city forgot them: memory needs a witness. That was the whole trick of it. A thing unwitnessed doesn't just fade—it never fully happened. And she had spent eleven years keeping the Guild's cruelest accounts, and she would be damned, damned *thoroughly*, before she let the best thing she'd ever stolen slip out of existence unwitnessed.

So she narrated.

"Your name is Petra Winterbourne. You keep terrible hours and worse handwriting when you think no one's grading it. You lied to me the first night we met and apologized for it with wine, which is the correct currency." A wall groaned and shed a century of mortar; she ducked and kept talking. "You hum when you balance accounts. Badly. Off-key, like the numbers offended you personally. You held my hand when I told you about my brother and you didn't say anything stupid about grief, which is how I knew—"

Her voice caught. She swallowed it and kept moving.

"—which is how I knew you were worth burning a career for."

The courier's name surfaced as she said it—her brother, the dead courier, the map pressed into bleeding hands at a salt door—and she held onto it with both fists. Some things the dissolution couldn't reach, she realized. Things paid for in blood-oath. Her truth to Petra, Petra's truth to her: those had been sworn, and sworn things had just been woven into a living prison, and prisons keep their contents.

Small mercies. She'd take them.

The gate stairs were gone. Of course they were. She stood at the bottom of where they should have been, holding half a mercenary upright, and laughed until it turned into something else, and then she climbed the rubble anyway, hand over hand, Petra's weight dragging at her ruined shoulder where an enforcer's blade had found her hours ago.

Halfway up, Petra stirred.

Not waking. Just—turning toward her voice, the way a plant turns toward light, and murmuring something. Sable bent close, dust in her eyelashes, water soaking through to the skin, and heard:

"...entry... unsigned..."

"Shut up and sleep," Sable said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle. "I'll sign for you. I've got excellent handwriting. Everyone says so."

* * *

Dawn found them in the ruins above, in the broken cathedral where Sable had once held a gate against three enforcers and gotten a brand-shaped scar and a wound for her trouble. She laid Petra down on a fallen slab of altar-stone, checked their pulse—steady, slow, a heart keeping prison-time now—and sat back on her heels to watch the sun come up over a city that no longer knew what it owed.

Below, in the palace, a king was awake. She'd felt him wake—a great shudder through the oath-threads, a sob that traveled through stone—and then quiet, the clean quiet of a mind with only one occupant. He would weep for a week and call it grief for his late queen. His physicians would agree. The city would agree. Ashvale was very good at agreeing.

The crown itself was slag on a throne-room floor, or would be by the time anyone thought to look. She'd felt it burn out through the soles of her feet.

And the enforcers? She'd felt them too, in the last hour—felt their purpose dissolve like sugar in rain. Whatever orders had driven them were orders about the map, the crisis, the thirteenth moon, and there was no longer any such thing. By noon, half of them wouldn't remember being sent. By evening, the Guildmaster himself would open his ledgers and find pages he couldn't account for, entries in his own hand describing transactions he had no memory of approving.

Let him choke on it.

Sable Vane, former Guild property, brand and all, sat in the ashes of a cathedral holding the hand of the person who had saved a kingdom by ceasing to be in it, and did the arithmetic.

Most of Petra was gone. The guild, the god, the decade of silent prayers, the guilt, the first binding, the name they'd written into a parasite's spine—all dissolved, unwitnessed, unpaid. What was left was a stranger with steady breathing and muscle-memory of a knife-grip, and a handful of sworn fragments that lived inside Sable now like coins sewn into a coat lining.

A name. A habit of humming off-key. Two truths, hers and theirs, knotted together past unwinding.

It wasn't enough. It would have to be enough to start.

"I'm going to write it all down," she told the unconscious form, because promises made aloud bind literally in this kingdom, and she wanted this one to have teeth. "Every day. Everything you lost and everything you were, and I'll read it to you each morning until some of it sticks, and if none of it sticks, then I'll read it to you anyway, because somebody in this city has to remember what you paid."

The sun cleared the rooftops. Light poured through the cathedral's broken ribs and lay across Petra's face like a hand.

Their fingers curled around Sable's.

Not much. Just a reflex, maybe. Just a drowning man gripping a rope without knowing why the rope was there.

Sable bowed her head over their joined hands and let herself cry for exactly as long as it took the light to cross the altar-stone, and then she wiped her face on her sleeve, stood, and became again the woman who got things done.

First: out of the cathedral before the city finished waking and started asking questions. Second: a safehouse, food, bandages for a shoulder that was complaining in increasingly creative language. Third—

Third was the hard part. Third was a lifetime.

She gathered Petra up—lighter than she expected, as if the dissolution had taken weight along with everything else—and carried them out through the shattered gate, down through streets where shopkeepers were sweeping away dust they couldn't explain and neighbors greeted each other with the vague unease of people sharing a dream no one can recall.

At the bottom of the cathedral hill, a beggar—old, blind, singing to himself in a cracked voice—caught her sleeve as she passed.

"Heard singing in the night," he said. "Down under the ground. Whole choir of them. Then it stopped, all at once, like a door closing." He smiled with too few teeth. "You hear it, mistress?"

Sable looked at him for a long moment.

"No," she said. "Must have been the wind."

He nodded, satisfied, and went back to his song, and Sable walked on with her burden through a city that remembered nothing, carrying the only memory of it that remained—except.

Except that as she reached the corner, the blind man's cracked voice drifted after her, threading through the morning air, and she stopped dead in the street.

He wasn't singing a beggar's song. He was singing the choir's melody. Note for note, the song she'd held steady through the longest night of her life—the song of the imprisoned, the song that had included, buried deep in its harmonies, a voice she now recognized as belonging to a god.

The blind man's clouded eyes turned toward her, unseeing and somehow seeing everything.

"One verse survived," he said, in a voice that was not entirely his own. "Songs are stubborn things, keeper. And your friend should know—the last word hasn't finished echoing yet."

Then he turned back to his begging bowl, humming, ordinary, blind.

Sable stood in the middle of the waking street, holding an unconscious stranger who used to be the most important person in the world, and felt hope arrive like a blade between the ribs: sudden, sharp, and impossible to ignore.

She adjusted her grip and walked faster.
