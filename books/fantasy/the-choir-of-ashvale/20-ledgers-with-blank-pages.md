## 20. Ledgers With Blank Pages

The first thing I know is the smell of bread.

Not the word for it—I don't have the word for it yet, not quite—but the warmth of it, the yeast-and-crust of it, drifting up a stairwell into a small room where I am lying in a bed that is not mine because I do not have one. I know this the way you know your own hands: absolutely, and without any story attached.

I open my eyes.

Ceiling beams. Smoke-darkened oak. A crack running through the third beam like a river on no map I've ever seen, because I have never seen a map. Have I? The thought arrives and then loses its footing. There was something about maps. There was something about—

There was nothing. There is a ceiling, and a crack in a beam, and bread.

I sit up too fast and the room tilts, and a woman is there before I finish tipping—red-haired, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink-stained fingers catching my shoulder with a grip that is gentle and absolutely immovable, the way a dock rope is gentle when it's the only thing between you and deep water.

"Slowly," she says. "You've been asleep for six days."

"Who—" My voice comes out sanded down to the grain. "Who are you?"

Something crosses her face. It's fast, and she's good at making things fast, but I catch it anyway: a flinch, quickly filed away. Like a bookkeeper marking an error in a column and choosing not to correct it yet.

"Sable," she says. "My name is Sable."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," she agrees. "But it's the answer you have today." She presses a cup into my hands. Water, faintly bitter with some herb. "Drink. Then eat. Then we'll see how much of you came back with you."

I drink. The water tastes like nothing, which is somehow the loudest thing in the room.

* * *

Here is what I have, when I take inventory the way a person takes inventory of a house after a fire:

I have language. All of it, or nearly—the words come quick and sharp, and some of them are strange ones. *Ledger.* *Oath.* *Undercroft.* Words that arrive wearing other words' clothes, that feel used, broken-in, like boots I've walked a thousand miles in and cannot remember buying.

I have skills. When Sable hands me a pen to test my grip, my hand does something impossible with it—a flourish, a correction mark, a tiny precise notation in a margin—and I stare at it like a bird that has landed on a stranger. I can write. I can write *well*. I can apparently read a column of numbers the way other people read faces.

I have a scar. A long one, from my left hip up across my ribs, old and silvered. I touch it and feel nothing, which frightens me more than pain would. Pain would be a story. This is just a fact with the story torn out.

And I have her. The red-haired woman who will not leave the doorway, who watches me eat with the careful attention of someone counting coins, who answers every question I ask with exactly as much truth as she thinks I can carry and not one drop more.

"What happened to me?" I ask, on the second day.

"You were hurt," she says. "Doing something important."

"What kind of important?"

"The kind that worked." And there—that flicker again, grief dressed up as patience. She turns away to stir the pot. "Eat your soup."

"I don't even know my own name."

She goes still over the pot. Stillness, I'm learning, is her tell. When Sable Vane stops moving, it's because moving would show something.

"Petra," she says finally, without turning around. "Your name is Petra Winterbourne."

Petra Winterbourne. I say it inside my own skull and it fits like a key fits a lock it was cut for: perfectly, and proving nothing. Anyone could have told me any name. But my chest does something when she says it—not recognition exactly. More like an echo finding the shape of the room that made it.

"Why do you look like that when you say it?"

"Like what?"

"Like it costs you money."

She laughs then, short and startled, and wipes her eyes with the back of her wrist while pretending it's steam from the pot. "You always did ask questions like that," she says. "Straight through the floorboards."

"*Did*," I repeat. "*Always.* So we knew each other. Before."

"Yes."

"How well?"

She sets down the spoon. Looks at me straight, and I give her credit—she doesn't flinch, doesn't soften it, gives me the whole weight of it in one unguarded breath.

"Well enough that losing you nearly killed me," she says. "Now eat your soup, Petra Winterbourne, before it goes cold, because I did not carry you out of a collapsing hell under the city to watch you die of stubbornness in my own kitchen."

I eat my soup.

* * *

On the fourth day she brings out the ledger.

It's a plain thing, cheaply bound, nothing like whatever instinct in my hands tells me fine ledgers should be—no gilding, no wax seals, just good paper stitched well. The cover says, in her handwriting, careful and deliberate: *PETRA WINTERBOURNE — RESTORATION ACCOUNT.*

"What is this?"

"Everything you lost," she says. "Or everything I could write down. Sit."

She sits across from me at the little table by the window and opens it, and reads aloud.

"Entry the first. Your name is Petra Winterbourne. You were a mercenary, sworn to a god whose name I am writing separately, because saying it aloud in this city still makes doors close." She glances up. "That's not superstition. That's observation. Don't test it."

"Entry the second. You kept accounts for the Ashvale Guild. The Guild traded in borrowed years, stolen names, bottled grief. You were the best ledger-keeper they had, which is not a compliment—it's a warning label. You knew exactly what everything cost, including yourself."

My skin prickles. Not memory—memory is gone, burned down to the studs. But something under memory, the way a tree stump remembers being a tree. "Keep reading."

"Entry the third. Ten years ago you helped seal something beneath the palace. You wrote its binding. In your own blood. You did it to protect people, and it worked, and it also broke you, and you spent ten years pretending it hadn't." Her voice stays level, professional, a clerk reading figures. Only her knuckles, white around the page edge, disagree. "Entry the fourth. The god you served went silent to hide you from the thing you'd bound. Its last word to you was *remember*. You are welcome to decide what that means. I've decided it means you were always meant to find your way back here, one entry at a time."

"Entry the fifth." She hesitates. For the first time since she opened the book, she looks afraid—not of me, I think, but of the sentence itself. "Entry the fifth. You chose to become the prison. Whatever wore the king's crown, whatever fed on oaths—you took it into your own body so the king could live free and the god could go home. And the price was—" Her voice catches, actually catches, and she steadies it with visible effort, the way you'd steady a wobbling stack of plates. "The price was most of you. The city forgot. The Guild forgot. As far as Ashvale knows, none of it ever happened, and the man on the throne wakes up weeping with gratitude he can't explain to anyone."

She closes the ledger gently, like closing a door on a sleeping child.

"That's five entries," I say. "Out of how many?"

"As many as it takes." She slides the book across the table to me. "Read them every morning. Read them until they stop sounding like someone else's life. And when you remember something—anything—tell me, and I'll write it down in your hand instead of mine. Because—" and here her composure cracks clean through, and what shows underneath is so raw I almost look away, "—because it should be your hand, Petra. It should always have been your hand."

I look down at the ledger. At her cramped, fierce, ink-stained script. At the account of a person I do not know, written by a woman who clearly loved him, or her, or whatever I was, with the kind of precision that only ever comes from terror of losing something.

"I don't remember you," I say quietly. "I want you to understand that. I don't remember anything about you. Not your face, not your voice, not—" I stop, because her expression is doing something complicated. "What?"

"Nothing," she says. "It's just—you said almost exactly that to me once before. Years ago. Word for word, nearly. Different circumstances." She smiles, and it's a real smile, tired and crooked and brave. "You'd just taken an oath that meant you couldn't lie to me anymore, and you hated it, and you told me all the ways it was going to ruin everything. And I said, *good*, because liars make terrible bookkeepers."

"Am I a good bookkeeper?"

"The best I ever saw." She stands, collects the soup bowls, and pauses at the doorway with her back to me. "And Petra? You don't have to trust me. I know how it looks from where you're sitting—a stranger with a book full of claims on your life. Trust the ledger instead. Check my work. That's what it's *for*."

After she's gone upstairs, I sit for a long time with the Restoration Account open on my knees, checking her arithmetic.

Every figure balances.

* * *

The mornings take on a shape.

Bread first—she bakes it herself, badly, and refuses all improvement. Then the ledger, read aloud, both of us at the table like merchants opening shop. Some days I remember fragments as she reads: the taste of salt on a door, the weight of a wax seal, a corridor of singing stone. They come as sensations without stories, sparks without fire, and I've learned to grab them fast, because they're gone within the hour if they aren't pinned to paper.

When one comes, she writes it down. Then she slides the pen across the table and makes me copy it in my own hand.

"Yours counts more than mine," she says. "Ink is just ink until intention gets behind it. You taught me that." A pause. "So I'm told. By you. Repeatedly."

Today, copying an entry about a god whose name must not be spoken, my pen slows halfway through a word I didn't choose. My hand writes *Ashvale* and then keeps going, adds a small hooked symbol after it, a notation I've never seen and instantly understand: *account closed, debt forgiven, do not reopen.*

"What's this mark?" I ask.

Sable leans over. Goes very still.

"That's the mark you invented," she says softly, "for debts that were paid by someone who couldn't afford to pay them. You called them *grace entries*. You said every honest ledger needed a column for them, or the numbers would lie." Her eyes come up to mine, bright and wet and fierce. "You put one after your own name. In the old Guild master ledger. The night before everything happened. I found it after. I never understood it until now."

I look down at the symbol drying under my pen. Grace entry. Debt forgiven. Do not reopen.

"It sounds like something a person writes," I say slowly, "when they already know what they're going to have to spend."

"Yes," she says. "It does."

We sit with that, the two of us and the morning light and the bad bread, and outside the window Ashvale goes about its business, a city that remembers nothing of what it owed, carrying on in the sunshine like a debtor who's had the whole account struck from the books.

"Does it make you angry?" I ask her. "That they all forgot? That nobody knows what it cost?"

She considers it honestly, the way she seems to consider everything—as a figure to be checked rather than a feeling to be felt.

"Some days," she says. "Then I remember that forgetting was part of the price *you* paid, not part of what they stole. You bought their ignorance along with everything else. Which means every fool in this city walking around happy and ungrateful is walking around inside your payment." She shrugs. "Hard to stay angry at people living inside your gift. Mostly I just want to bill them."

I laugh—really laugh, the first time, and she looks at me like I've handed her a purse of gold.

"There you are," she says quietly. "There's a bit of you."

* * *

It happens on the twelfth morning.

She reads the entries as usual—name, guild, god, binding, sacrifice—and I copy the new fragments from yesterday: salt, wax, singing stone. Routine. Ritual. Our two hands and one book.

Then she turns the page and stops.

Not the stillness of her tell. Something else. Something worse.

"Sable?"

"There's an entry here I didn't write," she says.

I lean over. Between entry nine and entry ten, in a hand I know—know the way you know your own reflection, sudden and total—is a line of script in dark brown ink that I recognize, with a lurch, as neither ink nor brown.

Blood. Dried blood. My blood, though I have no memory of spilling it.

It says: *Entry the tenth. The blanks are not empty. Check the ones you left on purpose.*

Below it, a list. Seven items. Six of them are ordinary enough—*the color of the door at the salt entrance; the tune you hummed when balancing; the name of your first commander*—things a person might genuinely have lost.

The seventh item is different. The seventh item says:

*The reason you chose the third path. You left it blank because you weren't ready to read it. You will be. Ask her about the night at the gate.*

I look up. Sable is white as paper, staring at the entry, and her hand has gone to her left wrist—to a place where, I notice for the first time, an old brand shows pale against her skin, half-hidden under her rolled sleeve.

"The night at the gate," I say. "What happened at the gate?"

Her jaw works. She looks at me—at me, not the ledger—and I watch her weigh it, watch the best liar in Ashvale stand at the exact border between protecting me and trusting me, and I watch her choose.

"You held a door," she says. "Against three enforcers. So the map wouldn't be taken. And I—" Her voice drops. "I took a blade meant for the book. And you held me afterward, and you said—" She stops. Starts again. "You said you finally understood why you'd built the prison. Because some things are worth being the wall for."

The room is very quiet. Somewhere below, the bad bread is burning, and neither of us moves to save it.

"I left myself a note," I say slowly, "in my own blood, telling myself to ask you about the moment I almost died. On purpose. Before I forgot." I turn the ledger toward her, tap the seventh item. "Which means the blanks aren't wounds, Sable. Some of them are *seeds*. I planted them. For exactly this."

She exhales shakily. "Then we'd better start digging," she says, "before whoever else helped write that book comes looking for their harvest."

Outside the window, high over Ashvale, a moon that should be waning gibbous hangs fat and full and thirteenth, and neither of us saw it rise.
