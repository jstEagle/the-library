## 22. A New Entry, In Our Own Hand

The salt door had been rehung on new hinges, and Petra noticed the difference every morning the way you notice a scar that finally stopped aching.

It swung quietly now. No groan of old iron, no scrape against the threshold. Sable had commissioned the work herself, standing over the carpenter with her arms crossed and her ledger open, as if even a door hinge might try to cheat her. The carpenter had charged double. Sable had paid it without blinking, which was how everyone in Ashvale learned the Guild under new management was not the Guild they remembered.

Petra stood in the counting room with the morning light coming through windows that had been washed for the first time in living memory, and balanced the books.

Not *the* books. Their book. The great ledgers of the old Guild — the ones that recorded borrowed years and stolen names and bottled grief, the ones written in a hand so careful it barely looked like handwriting at all — those were gone. Burned, most of them, in the weeks after the crown. What remained had been moved to the vault beneath the counting room floor, sealed behind salt and iron, marked with a single line in Sable's blunt script: *Paid in full. Do not reopen without cause.*

The new ledgers were thinner. Honest-thin. They fit in two hands, which was the joke Sable made every time she said it, and the rule she never broke: *ledgers kept in two hands.* Every entry witnessed. Every price named aloud before it was taken. Every impossible thing traded in the open, at fair value, with both parties walking away knowing exactly what they'd lost and what they'd gained.

It should not have worked. An economy of the impossible run like a fishmonger's stall. And yet.

Petra dipped the pen and read back through the week's entries, because reading them back was part of the ritual now, the way the nightly rite at the oath-shrine had once been. Some habits survived the god that shaped them.

*A year of good sleep, sold by a widow who could not bear to dream of her husband. Price paid: forty crowns and one honest funeral. Witnessed.*

*A name returned to a ghost — the miller's daughter, drowned thirty years past, whose name had been sold to a man who wanted to be forgotten by his creditors. The name went home. The creditors, mercifully, forgot him anyway. Witnessed.*

*One grief, unbottled and poured out at the grave it belonged to. No charge. Some things the Guild gives back free, and may the old masters rot for ever charging.*

That last line was Sable's. She wrote the editorial notes; Petra let her. It was, Petra had decided, a fair division of labor. Sable kept the accounts honest. Petra kept them kind.

There was a knock at the salt door — three raps, then two, the signal they'd agreed on for clients who came recommended and wanted discretion anyway. Discretion still had a market. Honesty did not mean shouting your business from the bell tower.

Petra opened the door on a boy of perhaps fifteen, mud to his knees, holding a plain wooden box with no seal and no mark.

"You're the ledger-keepers," he said. Not a question. "I'm not supposed to say who sent me."

"Then don't," Sable said from behind Petra, already reaching for the box. "We're very good at not knowing things. It's practically our trade."

The boy set the box in Petra's hands, looked at the coin Sable pressed into his palm — more than the errand was worth, because Petra had insisted the new Guild tip its couriers, having been one, briefly, on the worst night of their life — and fled back down the alley at a dead run.

Inside the box, wrapped in undyed wool, was a purse of gold. Heavy. Far too heavy. And beneath it, a card of good cream stock bearing a single line in a hand Petra almost recognized — a hand that had signed royal writs, a hand that had trembled, once, in a throne room while its shadow bowed a half-second late:

*For services rendered. The king remembers nothing, but he sleeps well, and he would like whoever is responsible to know that.*

Sable read it twice. Then she laughed — the real laugh, the one she spent like gold because it cost her something every time.

"Anonymous donation," she said. "From the crown. To the people who unmade the crown."

"He doesn't know what we did." Petra weighed the purse. It was an obscene amount. A decade of the old Guild's honest work wouldn't have matched it. "He just knows he woke up weeping and free, and something in him — some debt his mind won't name — wants it paid."

"Then we'll spend it honestly." Sable took the purse and carried it to the strongbox, and paused there, her hand flat on the lid. "Widows first. The ones who sold their years back when the prices were criminal. We know where the old rolls are."

"The old rolls are ash."

"Some of us have better memories than others." She said it lightly, but Petra heard the edge beneath it — the same edge that lived in everything Sable said about memory, since the night in the debtors' tavern when she'd admitted, in the space of one stolen truth, that she had lost the name of her hometown to a shifting room beneath the city and had never gotten it back. "I've been reconstructing them. Names, dates, amounts. It's slow work. I'm apparently the sort of person who finds slow work restful, which is information about myself I could have done without."

"You could hire someone."

"I could." She shut the strongbox. "But then someone else would know what I remember, and I've only recently learned the difference between keeping secrets and being kept by them."

Petra let that sit. It was the kind of sentence Sable produced maybe once a month, the way certain wells produce water — rarely, and worth waiting for.

Outside, Ashvale went about its morning. Bells from the restored cathedral, where the drowned nave had been pumped dry and reconsecrated to a god whose name no one could quite recall but whose last word was carved now above the altar in letters deep enough to outlast the stone: REMEMBER. Pilgrims came, sometimes. They stood beneath the word and felt something they couldn't explain, and left offerings they couldn't justify, and went home lighter. Petra had gone once, early on, and stood in the empty choir loft where a song had once held a kingdom's mind together, and had felt nothing at all — and had found, to their surprise, that the nothing was a kind of peace. Whatever had answered in that final hour, whatever had said *remember* with a voice like a struck bell, it had not asked to be worshipped afterward. It had asked to be remembered. The word above the altar was doing that work now, and it needed no keeper.

Mostly.

Some nights, Petra still climbed to the old oath-shrine. Not to pray — the habit of prayer had gone with everything else — but to sit, and to be quiet, in the place where they had once begged a silent god for ten years. The shrine was a ruin now. Moss on the stones. But the silence there had changed quality. It no longer felt like a door held shut. It felt like a room after the guest has gone home.

* * *

The trouble — because there is always trouble, and the new Guild had learned to treat trouble as simply another entry awaiting its price — arrived on a Tuesday, in the person of a woman with ink-stained fingers and a merchant's smile.

"I want to buy back a memory," she said, seated across the counting table with the composure of someone who had rehearsed this. "My own. I sold it eleven years ago. I don't know what it was. That's rather the point."

Sable and Petra exchanged the look — the one that took half a second and conveyed a full negotiation.

"Eleven years ago," Sable said, "the Guild wasn't in the business of selling memories honestly. If you sold one, you were robbed, and the robbery was called a trade."

"Then consider this a restitution claim."

"It is." Petra pulled the fresh ledger toward them. "And we pay restitution claims. But we can't return what we don't have. The old stores were destroyed. What we *can* do is tell you what category of memory you sold, what it likely contained, and what it's worth to you now to know that."

The woman's hands tightened on her bag. "You're telling me to choose between knowing and not knowing."

"I'm telling you that you get to choose. That's new. Under the old management, nobody got to choose anything."

She thought for a long time. Long enough that Sable poured her tea without asking, and she drank it without noticing, and the light moved across the counting table from one window to the next.

"Not knowing," she said finally. "If it was worth selling, it was probably worth losing. But—" and here her voice cracked, just slightly, the merchant's polish failing for one human moment— "write down that I asked. Write down that I came here and I chose. So that someday, if I change my mind, there's a record that the choice was mine."

Petra wrote it. *One woman, choosing not to know. Witnessed by two hands.* And when the woman left, lighter by nothing and heavier by nothing, carrying only the strange freedom of a decision, Sable watched the door swing shut on its quiet hinges and said:

"That's the whole trade now, isn't it. That's all we sell. The choice."

"It was always the whole trade," Petra said. "The old Guild just sold it to people who didn't know they were buying."

* * *

Autumn came to Ashvale the way it always did — sideways, smelling of woodsmoke and river rot — and with it came the anniversary, though no one else in the city knew it was an anniversary. The thirteenth moon had completed its turn a year past, and the sky had offered no special moon since, only the ordinary twelve, keeping ordinary time, as if the calendar itself had been unbound along with the king.

On the eve of it, Petra and Sable climbed to the cathedral roof, because Sable had decided months ago that the anniversary of nearly dying together ought to be observed with wine and a view, and because neither of them had anywhere better to be, which was its own kind of miracle. Two people who had built their lives on careful invisibility, spending an evening in plain sight of the whole city, on the highest roof in it.

"To the old Guild," Sable said, raising her cup. "May it rot."

"To the old Guild," Petra agreed, and drank.

"And to the new one. May it stay small enough to be honest."

They sat shoulder to shoulder above the lamplit sprawl, and below them the city murmured its thousand small oaths — promises between lovers, vows between partners, a mother swearing to a feverish child that morning would come. Somewhere in the weave of all that promising, Petra knew, the parasite had once fed. Somewhere in it, once, a binding had been written in blood-oath ink by a young mercenary who hadn't known what they were signing. The scar of that was still in Petra somewhere — a hollow where the memory had been, filled now not with the memory itself but with the knowledge of its shape, the way an amputee knows the weight of the missing hand.

"Do you ever miss it?" Sable asked. She didn't specify what. She never had to, anymore. That was the other thing the year had built: a shorthand, two liars' cipher repurposed for truth.

"The god?" Petra considered it honestly, because honesty was the discipline now, harder than any blade-drill they'd ever kept. "I miss being certain someone was listening. Even silent, even absent — the *listening* was the thing. Ten years of talking into that silence and calling it faith." They turned the cup in their hands. "But I don't miss who I had to be to survive it. The person who kept perfect books because the books were the only thing that would hold still."

"And now?"

"Now I keep imperfect books with a woman who argues with my margins." Petra smiled. "It's better."

Sable leaned her head against Petra's shoulder, which she did perhaps four times a year, each time as if daring the world to comment. Below them, the cathedral bell rang the hour, and the word above the altar held its vigil in the dark, and the city slept in a king's peace that no one remembered buying.

"One more year," Sable said. "Then we see if honest impossible things can survive a second winter."

"One year," Petra agreed. "Witnessed."

* * *

The last night of the accounting year, they closed the books together, as the rule required — two hands, two signatures, every column summed in the open. Sable ruled the final lines with her straightedge and blotted the ink, and then, instead of closing the ledger, she turned to a blank page and slid it across the table along with the pen.

"Tradition," she said.

"There is no tradition. We invented this Guild fourteen months ago."

"Then we invent a tradition. Every ledger ends with an entry the keepers write for themselves. The old Guild ended theirs with inventory. Ours ends with—" she waved the pen vaguely— "whatever's true."

Petra took the pen. The blank page waited, white as the first page had been white, a lifetime ago, in another life, in a hand so careful it barely looked like handwriting at all.

They thought about writing the summary of the year. The restitution claims, the widows repaid, the anonymous purse from a king who slept well. They thought about writing the god's word again, REMEMBER, as a kind of signature for the whole enterprise. They thought about writing Sable's name, or the word *witnessed*, or nothing at all.

Instead, they wrote:

*One name, returned to its owner. Not sold. Not traded. Not taken in payment for anything. Given freely, by the hand that kept it hidden for ten years, to the ledger that will keep it honestly from now on.*

*Petra Winterbourne.*

*Witnessed.*

They signed it. Then they turned the book toward Sable, who read it in silence, and whose eyes did something complicated that she would deny under torture, and who took up her own pen and wrote beneath it, in her blunt, argumentative script:

*Confirmed accurate. Paid in full.*

Sable closed the ledger. Its covers met with a soft sound, quieter than the old iron door had ever been, and outside the salt door Ashvale went on not remembering what it owed — and owing it anyway, in bread shared with strangers and oaths kept without witnesses and a bell tolling over a word carved in stone.

The hand holding the pen set it down.

Tomorrow there would be a knock at the door, and a client, and an impossible thing that needed trading honestly, and two hands to keep the account. Tomorrow the blanks in Petra's memory would still be blanks, and the mornings would still begin with Sable reading aloud from the book of everything lost, adding a line here and there as the reconstruction grew. Tomorrow the king would wake rested and unknowing, and the word above the altar would go on remembering for everyone.

But tonight the ledger was balanced, the entry was written, and for the first time in either of their lives, the name in the book belonged to the person who had chosen to put it there.

Petra banked the lamp. Sable locked the strongbox. Together they walked out through the salt door, which swung shut behind them on its quiet hinges, holding nothing but the dark and the honest pages — and opened, the next morning, onto a world that had no idea what it had been saved from, and two keepers who intended to keep it that way, one witnessed entry at a time.
