## 1. Salt on the Pass

The mule knew the way better than I did, which was just as well, because my head wasn't in the pass. It was back at the guildhall, in the room where Merrick used to sit, and where his chair now stood empty with a draft moving through it like something that hadn't been told yet.

Three weeks since we buried him. Less, if you counted by the way I still caught myself turning to speak to him when a plan went sideways, which was always. The Ravensworth guild had run Thornfell for six generations — salt south, tin and wool north, and things that never appeared on any manifest — and in all that time we'd lost exactly four people to the mountains. Merrick had sworn he wouldn't be the fifth. He kept his word. His heart gave out warm in his bed, which by smuggler's arithmetic was a fortune no one could steal.

Now it was mine to keep, this ragged thing. Last apprentice of a dying trade.

"Dom." Osk's hand touched my elbow, light, twice — *look up* — and I raised my eyes from the trail in time to see what he'd seen: cloud stacking itself over the eastern peaks like a debt coming due. First snow inside two days, maybe three. After that, Thornfell closed like a fist until thaw.

I signed back: *How far?*

*Two hours to the narrows,* he signed. *Then down.*

Down meant Hollowmere, the last village on the south side that still traded with us and didn't ask questions. Down meant salt delivered, coin collected, and then home to a hall with too few voices in it. We had seven left. Seven, when my grandmother's journals spoke of forty. Seven, and half of them were more scar than flesh.

Bramble had wanted to come. I'd told her no — someone had to keep the wards fresh on the hall, and besides, she walked like a woman arguing with the ground the whole way. Pellam had offered too, with that stiff knightly dignity he still wore like armor that didn't fit anymore. Also no. This run was simple. Salt, weather, home. I wanted one last job that stayed simple.

The gods, as Merrick used to say, hate a simple plan.

* * *

We made the narrows by late afternoon, the walls closing in until the sky was a gray ribbon overhead and the wind came through with an edge like a whetted knife. Osk went ahead — he always went ahead; the man read stone the way Bramble read weather — and came back signing *clear*, but with a hesitation at the end of it, fingers curling instead of dropping flat.

*What?*

*Someone.* He pointed up-trail, around the shoulder of rock where the narrows bent. *One person. Sitting. Waiting.*

Waiting. Nobody waited in the narrows. People hid in the narrows, or died in the narrows, but waiting meant they knew we were coming, and nobody knew we were coming. That was rather the entire point of us.

I put my hand on the knife at my hip and rounded the bend at a walk, because running tells people you're afraid, and the first lesson Merrick ever taught me was that fear is more expensive than blood.

She was sitting on a flat stone like a traveler in a painting, which should have been my second warning. No one sits in the Thornfell narrows unless they've given up or they're bait. She was wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate, hood back, and her hair was white — not gray, not silvered at the temples, but white as the snow coming behind us, though her face was young. Thirty, maybe. Younger than me, even, and I was thirty-one and felt every year of it pressed into my knees by the climb.

Her pack sat beside her, small and neat. Her hands were folded in her lap. And she was watching me with an expression I couldn't place at first, and then could, and wished I hadn't: it was relief. Pure, unguarded relief, the look of someone who has been praying and has just decided the prayer worked.

"You're Ravensworth," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a mule driver," I said. "Mules, salt. Very dull."

"The mule is lovely," she said, "but you walk like a Ravensworth. Weight on the balls of your feet. Eyes doing three jobs at once. My grandmother described it." She rose, unhurried, and the movement had a strange formality to it, like a rite performed so many times its meaning had worn smooth. "My name is Ysolde. I need passage through Thornfell. South side, Hollowmere, and then wherever the road goes after."

"We're closed for the season," I said. "Snow's coming. Come back at thaw."

"At thaw I'll be dead," she said, in the tone of someone reporting the price of bread. "Or worse. Please. I'll pay."

Everyone says they'll pay. Half of them can't, and the other half intend to pay in ways you don't want. I held out my hand anyway, palm up, because the gesture costs nothing and tells you everything. She reached into her cloak and set something in it.

Coins.

Five of them, heavy, and the moment they touched my skin I stopped thinking about the weather entirely. They were wrong. Wrong metal — darker than gold, darker than bronze, with a dull red undertone like iron left too long in a fire — and wrong shape, each one struck with a mountain peak on one face and something on the other I had to turn toward the failing light to read. A flame. A flame within a ring of small marks, tiny as stitching.

"No mint strikes these," I said. "No mint alive, anyway."

"No," Ysolde said quietly. "They don't."

They were warm. That was the thing that made the hair rise along my arms, under the cold. Coins in a pocket go to the temperature of your skin and stop. These were warmer than my hand, evenly, patiently warm, like stones pulled from a hearth hours ago — and beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something else. I almost said *humming*. I didn't say humming, because humming is a madwoman's word, and I am a smuggler, and smugglers deal in weight and measure and things you can bite to test.

But they sang. Barely. A single low note, felt more than heard, and every coin singing the same note, and my hand remembering the note before my mind did, some tune from a room I couldn't name.

Osk made a sound behind me. I turned. He was staring at the coins, and his face — Osk, who had watched soldiers burn our drop-houses and hadn't blinked — his face was doing something complicated and frightened.

He signed, slow: *My mother sang that.*

I looked at him. He never talked about his mother. He never talked about anything before the guild took him in, a deaf boy of nine abandoned at a shrine door because even the shrines hadn't wanted him.

*What?*

He shook his head. *Old song. Lullaby. She hummed it.* His hands hesitated. *I thought I made it up. After.*

The wind came down the narrows and carried the smell of snow with it, sharp and mineral, and the coins in my palm sang their one warm note against my skin, and somewhere above us the first flakes came down — early, early, days early — fat and slow, like ash.

Ysolde watched us both with those patient, terrible eyes.

"There's a shrine," she said, "in the deep part of the mountain. Older than the passes. Older than the kingdoms that named them. I have to reach it before the winter closes, and you are the last people alive who know the ways in." She paused. "Your grandmother wrote about it. In the journal with the burned cover. Page forty-one."

I hadn't shown that journal to anyone. I hadn't shown that journal to anyone *alive*.

The snow thickened. The mule shifted and blew out a breath that steamed. In my hand, five impossible coins sang their note, warm as a hearth, patient as a grave, and I heard myself say the words before I'd decided to say them, which is how the worst decisions of my life have always begun:

"How much do you know about my grandmother?"

* * *

We walked while she talked, because standing still in Thornfell in falling snow is how you become a story other smugglers tell. Osk took point with the mule, and I fell in beside Ysolde, close enough to grab her if she ran, far enough that I'd feel it if she drew steel.

She didn't talk like a pilgrim. Pilgrims talk in floods, all devotion and no detail. Ysolde talked like a ledger — precise, spare, each fact placed next to the last like stones in a wall. She'd come from the coast. She'd traveled alone. She'd been looking for the Ravensworths for eleven months, and had found, one by one, that the names in her records were dead, the halls cold, the trade routes grown over — until only one name remained, and a rumor of salt going south before winter.

"Your guild is dying," she said. It wasn't cruelty. It was inventory.

"I know what my guild is."

"Do you?" She glanced at me, and there was something in it that wasn't quite pity, and I liked it even less than pity. "You carry salt and stolen lace and call yourselves smugglers. Your grandmothers called themselves something else. So did their grandmothers. Six generations of Ravensworths, and every one of them kept a journal with a burned cover, and every one of them wrote page forty-one in a cipher the others could read, and every one of them died without telling their children why."

The trail dropped through a stand of black pines, snow sifting through the needles. My breath came out in clouds. "And you know why."

"I know what was kept," she said. "Not why. The why died with the priesthood. I'm what's left of the record-keeping, nothing more." She said it lightly, but her hands, I noticed, were clasped tight enough to whiten. "There was an order once. Keepers of a flame. When the kingdoms turned on the old gods, the keepers went underground — into trades that moved unseen. Smuggling was never the family business, Dominic Ravensworth. Smuggling was the *cover*. The cargo was always the same."

"And what cargo is that?"

She looked up, then — up past the pines, up the black shoulders of the mountain to where the storm was building, and her face did something I have no better word for than *homesickness*, though she'd never been here. Longing aimed at stone.

"A fire," she said. "A fire that's been dying for three hundred years, tended by people who forgot they were tending it. Until now, when it's nearly gone, and the last of the old records led me to you." She turned back to me, and for the first time her composure cracked — just a hairline, just enough to show the desperation underneath, the eleven months of dead ends. "I paid you in god-coins because they're all I have left. They were struck for the shrine. They want to go home. Can't you feel it?"

I could. That was the horror of it. All the way down the trail since the narrows, the coins in my inner pocket had been singing, and the song had changed — or I had — and now it didn't feel like a lullaby anymore. It felt like a pull. Like a hook set gently behind my sternum, reeling, reeling.

"Merrick," I said, before I could stop myself. "My mentor. Before he died, he — " I stopped. Started again. "He got strange, the last year. Kept going down to the cellar alone. Kept saying we'd let something starve. I thought it was grief-addled. He was old, and the guild was bleeding out, and old men say strange things."

Ysolde stopped walking.

"What did he say exactly?" she asked. "*Let something starve?*"

"He said — " and here my voice did something embarrassing, cracked on the word, because grief will ambush you on a mountainside as easily as anywhere — "he said, *we were keepers before we were thieves, girl, and we've forgotten which came first.*"

For a long moment Ysolde said nothing. Snow gathered on her white hair and didn't melt, and I remember noticing that, noticing it the way you notice a wrong note in a song — the coins were warm enough to melt snow at ten paces, and hers lay against her skin under her cloak, and the snow sat on her like it sat on the stones.

Then she smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face.

"He remembered," she said. "Oh, thank every silent god, he *remembered*, at the end." She started walking again, faster now, urgency finally breaking through the ledger-calm. "Then there may be time. Dominic — may I call you Dominic? — the storm is coming early, and the thing I serve does not have until thaw. Three hundred years it has waited, fading. It has perhaps one winter left. And when it goes out — "

She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together.

"When it goes out, what?" I asked.

She looked at me, and the snow fell between us, and above us the mountain went on being a mountain, indifferent, enormous, keeping whatever secret it had kept since before there were names for it.

"When it goes out," she said, "the world gets exactly what it chose. Nothing. Forever." 

Behind us, Osk made a soft sound, and when I turned, he was holding one of the coins I'd handed him to carry, and he was crying — silently, the way he did everything — and his free hand was moving, shaping the old lullaby in the air, note by note, the song his mother sang, the song no mint had struck in centuries.

The snow came down harder. And somewhere high above us, buried in the dark heart of Thornfell, I would have sworn on Merrick's grave that something answered.
