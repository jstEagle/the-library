## 2. The Coin That Hums

I didn't sleep.

That wasn't unusual — a smuggler who sleeps soundly on the eve of a run is either very good or very dead — but this was different. This was me sitting at Merrick's old desk with a candle burned down to a thumb's width and nine coins spread across the scarred wood in front of me like a fortune teller's cards.

They were warm.

Not body-warm, not pocket-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun has gone down, warm from somewhere inside themselves. I'd carried them in my coat all day, through snow and wind, and they hadn't cooled once. I'd taken them out expecting to feel cold metal, because that's what metal does, that's what metal has always done, and instead they'd lain against my palm like small sleeping animals.

And they hummed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the first thing Merrick ever taught me. *If you can't name what you're seeing, Dom, you'll sell it for a tenth of its worth or die holding it.* So: the coins did not sing, exactly. Singing implies melody, intention, a throat. What they did was vibrate at the edge of hearing, a tone so low and steady it might have been my own blood in my ears — except when I pressed one against the bone behind my jaw, the hum got *louder*, and it harmonized with itself, two notes a hair apart, beating against each other like a heart that couldn't decide its rhythm.

Nine coins. Nine voices, almost. When I laid them out in a row they were quiet as grave-goods. When I moved them closer together, the hum thickened.

I sat back and looked at them for a long time.

The old god-coins. Every smuggler knew the stories, the way every child knows ghost stories: coin struck before the Kingdoms, before the Inquisition, before the burning of the shrines. Silver that never tarnished. Faces on the obverse that weren't kings — something horned, something winged, something with too many eyes rendered lovingly by a mint-master who had clearly *seen* his subject. They turned up maybe once a decade, in a dead man's purse or a plowed field, and they always sold high and always brought bad luck, and every guild mother warned her apprentices against them the way you warn children against rivers.

Merrick had never warned me against anything. That should have told me something even then.

I turned one over. The face stared up at me — not horned, this one. Winged. A long muzzle open in what could have been rage or song, and around the rim, in lettering older than any alphabet I could read, a ring of characters like claw-marks arranged with terrible care.

My grandmother's journal was in the bottom drawer of Merrick's desk. It had been there since she died, and before that it had been in the bottom drawer of her own desk, and I had looked through it exactly twice in my life: once at fourteen, bored, and once at twenty-two, drunk. Both times it had seemed like the ramblings of an old woman who'd spent too many winters alone with ledgers. Columns of tolls and routes and bribes paid, and then — scattered through it, in a different ink, a different hand-pressure — fragments that read like fever.

I opened it now to no particular page and it fell open, because books do that, because hands remember what minds pretend to forget, to a page I had marked without remembering marking it.

*Asked Father why we always take the Thornfell road in winter when the Kestrel Pass is faster and drier. He said because the Kestrel men are honest. I said that's not a reason, that's a joke. He laughed and said, ask me again when you're chorister.*

*Chorister.* Underlined twice. In the margin, smaller, squeezed against the edge of the paper as if she'd been ashamed of it: *the Choir, the Choir, the Choir — he would never say more. Only that the Ravensworths were not founded to smuggle. The smuggling was the coat we put on over the other thing.*

I read it three times. The candle guttered. Outside, the wind came off the pass with that particular Thornfell moan, the one the old-timers swore was just rock and air and nothing else, and I sat in my mentor's chair in my dead guild's hall and felt the floor tilt under a life I had thought was built on bedrock.

Because here is what I knew about the Ravensworths, what every Ravensworth knew, what Merrick had fed me with my supper since I was six years old: we moved what needed moving and asked nothing about what it was. We ran salt and medicine and letters and sometimes people through passes no soldier could hold. We were thieves with a code, sinners with a schedule, and we had survived three hundred years of kingdoms and inquisitions precisely because we believed in nothing but the next job and the people beside us. That was the creed. That was the whole of it. *Nothing above the crew, nothing behind the debt.*

And my grandmother — sharp, flinty, ledger-perfect Gran, who taught me to count coin by touch in the dark — had written *chorister* in her margin like a girl writing a lover's name.

I picked up one of Ysolde's coins and held it to my ear like a seashell.

The hum resolved, just for a moment, into something with shape. Not words. But the *architecture* of words — phrases, cadences, a rise and fall like someone speaking through a wall, like a congregation heard from outside the church. And underneath it, so faint I could have invented it, another voice. Deeper. Slower. The kind of voice you feel in your sternum rather than hear.

The candle went out.

No draft. The shutters were latched; I checked them after, twice. The flame simply leaned sideways, bowed, and died, and in the dark the coin against my ear went suddenly, perfectly silent — and then, in the silence, I heard the rest of the room. The tick of the cooling stove. The wind. And beneath both, from everywhere and nowhere, from under the floor and behind the walls and down in the cellar where we kept the casks, a single sustained note like the last finger of an organ lifting off a pipe.

It stopped when I dropped the coin.

I lit the candle with hands that were steadier than I had any right to expect. Smuggler's hands. Hands that don't shake on the rope, don't shake at the checkpoint, don't shake when the patrol's lantern swings your way. I looked at those hands in the new flame and thought: *you're a Ravensworth, and you've been lying to yourself your whole life about what that means.*

* * *

Ysolde was awake, of course. Pilgrims on the eve of their pilgrimage sleep like cats, one ear open. She was sitting by the common-room fire with her cloak around her shoulders, watching the flames with an expression I recognized and hated on sight, because it was the expression of someone watching something end.

"The coins," I said. No preamble. Merrick would have approved of the bluntness and hated the tremor underneath it.

She didn't feign ignorance, which I'll grant her. Most people would have. "You heard them."

"I heard *something*. I want to know what."

"Sit down, Dominic Ravensworth."

The surname landed like a dropped anvil. I hadn't given it to her. Osk wouldn't have — Osk communicated in gestures and the occasional scrawled note, and besides, he'd known me since I was eight and still called me *little captain* in sign. Pellam was three valleys away. Which meant she'd asked, or deduced, or —

"You know the name," I said.

"I knew it before you were born." She stirred the fire with an iron poker, unhurried, and the light moved across her face and made her look briefly much older than her years, or perhaps exactly her years, worn honestly. "Your grandmother and I corresponded. Did she ever mention a correspondent in the south? No — of course not. She was careful. They all were, after the Purges. Careful, and lonely, and careful about the loneliness most of all."

"Corresponded about what?"

"About whether there was anyone left to carry it." She set the poker down. "Do you know what a chorister is, child?"

"A singer in a choir."

"That's what it became. That's the husk of it." She held out her hand, palm up, and I — fool that I am, curious fool, which is the most dangerous kind — put one of the coins in it. "Before the Kingdoms, before the Inquisition, before your creeds and your border wars, the world was loud. Not noisy — *loud*. Full of voices. The dragons sang, and the mountains answered, and everything that lived between them learned the song or got out of its way. The old gods weren't statues, Dominic. They were voices. Caerlum's voice was fire itself — the deep burn, the first warmth, the flame under the world. And the priesthood — the Choir — existed for one purpose only. To keep singing back. To keep the covenant in words, because a covenant unspoken is a covenant already broken."

"And the coins?"

"Communion tokens. Struck at the shrine, sung into being — literally sung, the metal shaped by voice, which is why they never cool and never corrode. Each one holds a fragment of the song. You could press one to your ear on the worst night of your life and hear that you were not alone in the dark." Her fingers closed around the coin, and her voice did something I didn't like at all. It frayed. "There were ten thousand of them once. This is the last I know of. Perhaps the last there are."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a beam settled. Ordinary sounds, and I clung to their ordinariness like a drowning woman clinging to driftwood.

"You're telling me," I said slowly, "that my family — smugglers, cutthroats, the people who taught me to forge a toll-stamp and lie to a customs man with a straight face — were *priests*."

"No." She shook her head, and for the first time she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "Worse. Priests can quit. Priests can be replaced. Your family were *keepers*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole tragedy of the last three hundred years. A priest serves. A keeper *holds*. Something was given into Ravensworth hands a long time ago, on terms no one alive remembers fully, and your line has been carrying it ever since — through the Purges, through the burnings, through three centuries of pretending to be nothing but thieves. They hid the covenant inside the smuggling the way you hide a blade in a bale of wool. The routes, the passes, the guildhall itself — ask yourself, child, why a smuggling guild needs a hall built on that particular ground. Ask yourself why the Thornfell road, always, in every winter, generation after generation, when better roads exist."

I thought of Gran's journal. *Ask me again when you're chorister.* I thought of Merrick taking the high road past the shrine ruins every year on the longest night, alone, and coming back smelling of woodsmoke, and when I'd asked him once, at fifteen, he'd smiled and said *guild business*, and I'd laughed, because what else does a guild do but business?

Gods. We'd been doing *business* the whole time.

"What was given to them?" My voice came out flat. "What are we keeping?"

Ysolde turned the coin over in her fingers, and the firelight caught the winged face, the open mouth, the ring of claw-mark letters, and I saw that she was looking at it the way you look at the face of someone you loved and buried.

"The flame," she said. "The last flame of Caerlum. The dragon-god's own fire, dying in the dark under Thornfell, tended by a family of smugglers who forgot — mostly, mostly they forgot — why they were tending it. Your grandmother didn't forget. I think your Merrick didn't either. And now the last keeper is you."

The word *last* went through me like a splinter working toward the heart.

"No," I said. "No, I'm a smuggler. I'm the last of a *smuggling* guild, which is a fancy way of saying I'm unemployed, and in spring I'll be running cloth to—"

"The shrine." She said it quietly, and it cut through everything I was building, brick by brick, to hide behind. "That's what I came to ask of you. Not passage south — I lied about that, or told a half-truth, which is the same coin clipped. I need you to take me to the dragon-fire shrine. The one your family has kept. The one under the mountain."

"There is no shrine."

"Then you have nothing to lose by looking." She rose, stiff-backed, and stood close enough that I could see the frost-melt still in the seams of her cloak, smell the road and the cold on her. "I watched the last priest of Anseth die in a ditch two winters ago, Dominic. I watched the Inquisition burn a chapel with four novices locked inside, and the kingdom called it order, and the gods said *nothing*, because there are so few of them left awake that even murder barely reaches them now. I am the remnant of a remnant of a remnant, and I am asking you — the last keeper of the last flame — to let me see it before I die. Before it dies. Because if it goes out unwitnessed, unmourned, unsung, then it didn't merely end. It was *erased*. And erasure is the only death the Inquisition cannot be forgiven for enabling."

I should have refused. Let the record show that every sane instinct I possessed voted refusal. Take the strange pilgrim to the border, refund her impossible coins, bar the door, drink until the humming stopped, and spend the rest of my life running cloth and telling myself the journal was an old woman's madness.

Instead I heard myself say: "Why you? Why does a remnant of a remnant need to see a fire?"

"Because I made a vow," Ysolde said, "to a god who may already be dead, and I intend to keep it anyway. That is the entire content of my faith, child. Persistence past evidence." She pressed the coin back into my hand and closed my fingers over it, and the hum rose against my palm like a pulse quickening. "And because the flame will need to be sung to, soon, one last time or one first time — I genuinely don't know which — and you cannot sing a song you've never heard."

"The coins don't have words."

"They have the *shape* of words. The rest is in the cellar."

I went very still. "What cellar?"

Her eyes were kind and pitiless. "You know exactly which cellar, Dominic. You've walked over it your whole life. The question is whether you've ever wondered why the third flagstone rings hollow, and if you haven't wondered — well. Then your grandmother's journal isn't the only thing in this hall that's been asleep."

The fire burned. The wind moaned off the pass. And in my closed fist, warm as a living thing, the last communion token of the dragon-god Caerlum hummed its two broken notes, over and over, patient as a heartbeat, like something that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more — but not, I understood suddenly, with absolute clarity, one day longer than it had to.

I blew out the candle, because I didn't need it anymore. The coin was glowing.
