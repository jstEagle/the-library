## 3. What the Guild Kept

Ysolde had spent forty years learning to read rooms, and the Ravensworth guildhall read like a lie told by someone who had almost forgotten the truth.

She stood in the cellar with a candle stub and let her eyes adjust to the dark. Above them, Dominic was arguing with Pellam about winter stores — she could hear the muffled rise and fall of it through the floorboards, the particular sharpness of a woman who grieved by organizing. Good. Let them count barrels and argue about salt pork. The cellar was where the real inventory lived.

The room itself was ordinary enough at first glance: casks stacked in rows gone soft with age, a rack of tools that hadn't been sharpened in years, the smell of earth and old tallow and, beneath it all, something else. Something Ysolde had crossed three hundred miles of war-torn country hoping to find.

Warmth.

Not the warmth of a hearth or a body. The warmth of a banked fire — deep, patient, buried. She pressed her palm flat against the coldest wall in the room and felt it anyway, faint as a pulse through wool. Her breath caught. After so long. After the shrines pulled down stone by stone and the priesthood scattered like ash on wind, after she had begun to believe the silence was permanent —

*Here.*

"You shouldn't be down here."

Dominic's voice from the stairs. Ysolde didn't turn. "Your cellar is warmer than your hearth," she said. "That's unusual in a building this old."

"It's a cellar."

"Mm." Ysolde lifted the candle and began to walk the walls slowly, deliberately, letting Dominic see exactly what she was doing. Deception would get her nowhere with this one; the girl had eyes like a hawk that had been cheated once and intended never to be cheated again. "Cellars are where people hide what they can't bear to look at. I've hidden a great many things in cellars myself."

"Smugglers keep goods in cellars." Dominic came down the last steps, boots quiet on stone. She'd taken to wearing her sword even indoors since they'd arrived — grief worn as a blade. "Brandy. Silk. Once, memorably, an entire choir of stolen bells."

"And yet you don't keep yours here." Ysolde stopped before the north wall. It looked like every other wall: fieldstone and mortar, sweating slightly in the damp. But the mortar there was newer. Not new — newer. Repointed within the last century, which meant someone had opened this wall and closed it again, and whatever they'd sealed inside had been worth the labor of two masons and a very good secret. "Where do you keep the brandy?"

"The cave under Thornfell. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that because you *want* everyone to know that." Ysolde ran her fingers along the mortar line, feeling for the seam. "A smuggler's first art is misdirection, child. Your guildhall sits above a road that three kingdoms have fought over, and your cellar holds nothing but empty casks and rusted tools. Either your family were the worst smugglers in history—"

"We were good enough to survive."

"—or this cellar isn't for smuggling at all."

The silence behind her had changed quality. Ysolde smiled without turning around. Forty years of reading rooms, and the best part had always been this: the moment the listener's breathing changed.

"There's nothing there," Dominic said.

"No," Ysolde agreed pleasantly. "There's nothing there at all. Which is why your grandmother sealed it so carefully."

Another silence, longer. When Dominic spoke again, her voice had dropped its armor a fraction. "You went through my grandmother's journal."

"You left it on the table. In a house where you invited me to sleep." Ysolde finally turned. Dominic stood at the foot of the stairs, arms crossed, candlelight carving shadows under her eyes. Younger than Ysolde had expected, when the stranger had first begged passage on the pass. Grief had aged her in patches, like frost on glass. "I read what was given to me. And what was given to me was a woman writing in circles around something she couldn't name. *The cellar work must continue. Maren asks why we bother and I cannot tell her. Some doors are not ours to open, only ours to keep.*"

"She was my grandmother's mother," Dominic said slowly. "Maren. That journal is — it's older than I thought."

"Journals are like that. They lie about their own age." Ysolde turned back to the wall. "Tell me, Dominic Ravensworth. What did your family actually do? Before the smuggling. Before the guild had a name."

"What kind of question is that?"

"The most important one you've never asked."

* * *

It took her another hour to find the seam, and she found it the way she found everything — by listening.

Not with ears. With the other sense, the one the priesthood had cultivated and the kingdoms had burned out of the world, the one that heard warmth and hum and the low sustained note beneath all things. Ysolde knelt on the cold flagstones with her palms against the wall and listened until the world narrowed to a single thread of sound.

There. Under the casks. A note so faint it was almost imagined — but not quite. A coin's-note. The same note the payment had sung in her palm when she'd pressed those old god-coins into Dominic's skeptical hand on the pass. The coins had been a key as much as a fee. She'd known it when she chose them; she hadn't known how the lock worked.

But the wall knew. She could feel it now, the way a tuning fork feels its twin across a room. Something behind the stone was answering the coins in her purse, faintly, desperately, like a voice calling down a well.

"The coins," she said aloud. Dominic had refused to leave — had planted herself on the stairs like a gargoyle with a grudge, watching Ysolde kneel and press her hands to stone and murmur to herself like a madwoman. "The ones I paid you with. Do you still have them?"

"Some. Why?"

"Bring them here. All of them. Touch them to the wall."

"I'm not going to touch my payment to a wall because a pilgrim tells me to."

"No," Ysolde said. "You're going to do it because you haven't slept properly since you took them, because you keep taking them out at night and holding them to your ear, and because you already know they're not money." She kept her voice gentle. Cruelty closed doors; this door needed opening. "You know it the way you know a storm is coming. In your teeth."

Dominic stared at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the little leather pouch, and Ysolde watched her weigh it in her hand — watched the girl's face do the thing faces did when a private suspicion was confirmed by someone else's certainty.

She crossed the cellar. She pressed a single coin to the mortar line.

The wall sang.

Not loudly. A thread of sound, rising through the stone like water through chalk — a chord, three notes braided together, warm and low and unmistakably *alive*. The candle flames in the room bent toward the wall as if in a draft, though there was no draft. The cold in the cellar retreated like a tide going out.

And in the mortar, glowing faintly gold where the coin touched it, a shape appeared: a handprint. Small. A child's handprint, pressed into wet plaster a lifetime ago, waiting.

Dominic snatched her hand back. The singing faded to almost nothing. "What — what is that?"

"A lock," Ysolde said. Her heart was hammering now, and she didn't trouble to hide it. Hope was a muscle she'd thought long atrophied; apparently it remembered. "A blood-lock. Old craft. Older than the kingdoms. It doesn't answer keys or force. It answers—" She stopped. Chose honesty, because the next step required it. "It answers kinship. Someone of the founding blood has to open it. That's why I couldn't. That's why I paid you in those coins and hoped — saints forgive me, I *hoped* — that the blood survived in you."

"In me." Dominic's voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices went just before they broke or drew steel. "What blood."

"Ravensworth blood. Or whatever your family was called before they called themselves Ravensworth." Ysolde rose, knees cracking, and faced her squarely. "Put your hand on the print, child."

"If you think I'm putting my hand on a singing wall in my own cellar because a stranger—"

"You're not a stranger to it." Ysolde nodded at the glowing print. "Look at the size of it. Look how small. Someone's daughter made that mark. Someone who played in this cellar while her parents worked the wall. This isn't a stranger's lock, Dominic. It's a family's. Yours."

* * *

In the end, it wasn't courage that moved her. Ysolde could see that clearly, and it broke her heart a little, the way true things did.

It was the journal. Dominic fetched it — climbed the stairs two at a time and came back cradling the battered book like a wounded bird — and sat on an upturned cask with the candle close, turning pages with fingers that trembled only slightly, stopping at passages Ysolde had marked in her own mind hours ago.

*"Maren asks why we bother and I cannot tell her."*

*"The choir must be kept though no one sings. That is the whole of the law and I will write it nowhere plainer than this, and then I will burn this page, and I never will, because I am a coward and a keeper, which may be the same thing."*

*"If you are reading this and you are not a Ravensworth, put it down. If you are reading this and you are, then I am sorry. We were meant to tell you when you were young enough to forgive us. There was never a young enough."*

Dominic read that last line four times. Ysolde counted.

"My grandmother read this," Dominic said at last. Her voice was very steady, which was worse than shaking. "She read it and she raised my father in this hall and she never said a word. He never said a word. I grew up thinking our whole inheritance was knowing which passes stayed clear in a snowfall." She closed the journal. Opened it again. Closed it. "Keepers of *what*?"

"Open the door," Ysolde said, "and I'll show you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. I know what should be behind that wall. I have prayed toward it for forty years. But I won't tell you what to hope for and then have it be otherwise. See it first. Then hate me, if hating me is what comes next."

For a moment Ysolde thought she'd pushed too far, too fast. Dominic stood, jaw tight, the journal clutched against her ribs — and Ysolde saw the exact moment the girl decided, saw it land in her shoulders like a weight accepted. Not bravery. Descent. The decision to stop standing on the surface of her own life.

She pressed her palm to the child-sized print.

The wall drank her touch. Gold spread outward from her fingers in veins, branching, reaching, and the chord swelled from a thread to a voice — three notes, then five, then more than Ysolde could count, harmonies stacking on harmonies until the cellar rang with it and the empty casks buzzed in sympathy and somewhere overhead Ysolde heard Pellam shout something startled and Bramble's voice answer, high and sharp, from the yard.

The mortar softened. Did not crumble — *unmade* itself, stones sliding apart along seams that had waited a century for exactly this hand, exactly this blood. The wall opened like a mouth.

Cold air breathed out. Cold, and clean, and underneath the cold, that banked-fire warmth, suddenly near, suddenly vast.

Ysolde lifted her candle and stepped past Dominic into the dark beyond the wall, and heard the girl follow, and heard her breath stop.

* * *

The chamber was small — perhaps twelve feet square, cut from living rock older than the guildhall above it. No dust. That was the first impossible thing. A sealed room for a hundred years should have lain under a shroud of grey, but the floor was clean, the air sweet, as if the room had been tended by hands that needed no light.

The second impossible thing stood in the center.

An altar. But no altar Ysolde had ever seen in the old engravings, none of the granite slabs and iron bowls the inquisitors loved to topple. This was a scale. A single scale, vast as a shield, curved like a sleeping animal's flank, its surface a deep ember-red shot through with veins of gold — and the veins *moved*, slowly, languidly, like embers breathing under ash. It rested on a bed of river stones, each stone carved with a word in a script Ysolde knew only from forbidden pages, and above it, suspended from the ceiling by three chains of woven silver, hung a lantern of black iron with no candle in it.

The lantern was warm. Ysolde could feel it from six feet away. Whatever had once burned inside it had been gone a long time — but the iron remembered.

"Oh," Dominic said. Just that. A single syllable with a whole life collapsing inside it.

"Do you know what you're looking at?" Ysolde whispered. The chamber held sound gently, reverently; shouting seemed obscene here.

"No." Dominic approached the scale the way one approaches a sleeping wolf. "Yes. I don't — I've seen scales before. On the old carvings in the pass shrines, before Voss's people chiseled them off. Dragon-scale." She looked back at Ysolde, and her face in the ember-light was stripped bare of everything — the smuggler's competence, the grief-hardened edge, all of it gone, leaving only a young woman standing in a room her family had lied about for generations. "This is real. This is a real dragon scale, in my cellar, under my grandmother's brandy casks."

"Not a trophy." Ysolde knelt beside the altar, not touching, though every nerve in her body screamed to lay hands on it. "Look at the stones. Read the words if you can't — I'll spare you the shame, I barely can myself. They're prayers. Each stone a prayer, laid down by a keeper, generation after generation. This isn't a shrine built *to* a dragon, child. It's a shrine built *for* one. A place where a flame could be kept alive. Where a promise could be kept." She looked up. "Your family weren't smugglers who happened to live near a holy site. They were keepers who became smugglers to survive. The passes, the contraband, the guild — all of it was cover. A century of hiding a covenant inside a criminal enterprise."

"That's—" Dominic laughed, one broken note, nothing like humor. "That's insane. That's — my father taught me knots. Knots and ledgers and which guards drink on duty. He never — there was never any *prayer* in this house. There wasn't even a shrine. We didn't — Ravensworths don't kneel."

"No," Ysolde said softly. "They tend. There's a difference, and it cost your family everything to learn it." She gestured at the black lantern. "Something burned in that. Something your grandmothers fed and guarded and passed down hand to hand, and something — a hundred years ago, give or take — went out. Or was taken. Or was carried away to be hidden somewhere safer than a valley full of soldiers." She rose, knees protesting, and met Dominic's eyes. "The journal will say. Somewhere in those pages, between the lines about salt routes and bribed sergeants, there will be a night your great-great-grandmother writes about in circles, the way she wrote about everything. Find it."

Dominic didn't move. She was staring at the scale, and the gold veins in it had begun — Ysolde's breath caught — the gold veins had begun to brighten. Faintly. Barely. The way coals wake when breath touches them.

"It's warm," Dominic said. Her hand hovered over the scale, not touching. "Why is it warm? You said the flame went out."

"Embers hold heat longer than hearts hold hope." Ysolde heard her own voice shake and let it. Forty years. Forty years of hiding in plain sight, of burning shrines mourned in secret, of believing she was the last fool alive who still prayed toward a mountain. "Or perhaps it isn't an ember at all. Perhaps, child, it's asleep. There's a difference, and it matters more than anything I have ever said to anyone."

Above them, footsteps on the cellar stairs — Pellam's heavy tread, Bramble's quick ones, voices calling Dominic's name, frightened by the singing, by the light seeping up through the floorboards.

Dominic didn't turn. She was leaning closer to the scale, close enough now that Ysolde could see her reflection in its polished curve, warped and small — and around the reflection, in the deep red of the scale's heart, something else. A flicker. A rhythm. Slow and vast and patient as geology.

Like breathing.

"Dominic," Ysolde said quietly. "Step back from—"

The scale pulsed. Once. Warmth rolled through the chamber like a wave off a summer road, and the black lantern above them chimed — a clear, sweet, impossible note from iron that held no bell — and in the depths of the ember-veins, Ysolde watched a spark kindle. Tiny. New. Undeniable.

It was looking at Dominic.

The footsteps on the stairs stopped dead. Bramble's voice, hushed: "*What in the nine names is that light?*"

Dominic's hand, moving without her permission, without her fear, settled flat against the scale.

And the voice that answered her — low, cracked, ancient, glad — spoke a name no one had spoken aloud in a hundred years:

"*Chorister.*"
