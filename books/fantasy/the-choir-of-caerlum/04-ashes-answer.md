## 4. Ashes Answer

The cellar door stayed open all night.

Dominic told herself she'd left it that way by accident, the way you leave a wound unbandaged because looking at it hurts less than touching it. But she knew the truth by the third time she climbed out of bed and stood at the top of the stairs, listening. The guildhall creaked its old familiar creaks — Bramble's still snoring through the floorboards two rooms over, the wind working at the loose shutter Osk kept promising to fix — and beneath it all, faint as a pulse under skin, something else was breathing down there in the dark.

She didn't sleep. She'd stopped pretending around midnight and taken to sitting by the cold hearth with her grandmother's journal open on her knees, reading pages she'd read a dozen times since Ysolde pried the truth out of the sealed door like a splinter from a palm.

*We do not smuggle. We tend.* Her grandmother's hand, cramped and hurried, ink gone brown with age. *Marta asks why we take no coin for the winter route. I tell her the road itself is the payment. She laughs. One day she will understand, or one day she won't, and either way the fire must be fed.*

*The fire.* As if that explained anything. As if a woman could write those words and then go back to salting herring and mending nets and never once say more.

Dominic closed the journal. Down in the cellar, the breathing went on.

* * *

She went down an hour before dawn, when the grey light through the high window was just strong enough to make the shapes honest. The hatchling-scale altar sat where it had sat for three hundred years, apparently — a broad disc of overlapping plates, each one the size of her spread hand, dull silver shot through with veins of faint rose-gold that caught the light wrong, the way embers catch it. Not metal. Not stone. Something between, something that had been alive once and perhaps wasn't finished being dead.

Ysolde had covered it again with the old tarpaulin before retiring, out of some priestly courtesy Dominic couldn't fathom. She pulled the cloth off now, and the scales seemed to warm the air above them, the way a hearthstone holds heat long after the fire dies.

"Right," she said to the empty cellar. "Let's have it out, then."

Her voice came back flat off the stone walls. Nothing else moved.

"You've been sitting under my family's floor my whole life." She crouched, laid her palm flat on the nearest scale — warm, faintly rough, like the hide of some enormous sleeping animal, which she supposed it technically was. "My grandmother knelt here. Her mother before her. Feeding you, whatever feeding means, and never telling their own daughters what they were kneeling to. Three hundred years of Ravensworths lying to their children about salt routes and toll schedules so they wouldn't have to say the word *god* out loud."

Still nothing. Of course nothing. Gods were stories the south told to frighten children and justify inquisitors. Gods were why Voss-types existed, why shrines got burned, why the guild had learned to hide everything it loved.

"So here's what I think." She stayed crouched, hand on the scale, talking the way she talked to skittish horses or green recruits — low, even, reasonable. "I think my family were fools. Devout, patient, careful fools who spent generations keeping a coal alive in a wet cellar for a god too dead or too proud to say thank you. And I think you owe them better than silence. And I think—"

Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway.

"I think you owe *me* an answer. Because I'm the last one. Do you understand that? There's no one after me. Whatever this is—" she tapped the scale twice, sharp, "—it ends with me unless someone tells me what I'm actually keeping. So. Caerlum. Dragon-god. Whatever you are. If you're listening — if you were ever listening — now would be the time."

Silence.

Then, quietly, in the tone of a woman conceding a point she'd already lost: "Please."

The word hung there. She hadn't meant to say it. Half-mockery, half-bargaining, and underneath both — she could admit it now, alone, in the dark, with her hand on something ancient and warm — underneath both, a child's plea. *Tell me I wasn't lied to. Tell me it meant something.*

The scale beneath her palm grew warmer.

Not gradually. Not the slow bleed of stored heat. It warmed the way a struck flint warms — sudden, purposeful, alive — and the rose-gold veins in the altar brightened all at once, every plate across the whole disc, until the cellar filled with a low amber glow like sunset through smoke.

Dominic didn't move. Some animal part of her brain screamed *run*; the rest of her, the part raised on Ravensworth discipline, held perfectly still and thought, absurdly: *so the stories were true, and I'm about to die in a root cellar.*

Then the voice came.

It did not come from the altar, exactly. It came from everywhere the warmth was — from the walls, from the floor, from the air in her lungs — and it sounded like coals settling in a banked fire, like the deep slow crackle of a hearth that has burned for years without ever being allowed to blaze. It was not loud. It did not need to be.

***Last chorister.***

Dominic's mouth went dry. "That's not—" She swallowed. "I don't sing."

***You sing. You have sung since the day you were born. Every Ravensworth has. You simply were not taught the words.***

"What words? What is this? What am I—"

***Look.***

And the cellar went away.

* * *

She stood on a mountainside she knew — Thornfell's high shoulder, the notch they called the Shepherd's Tooth — but wrong. The rock was bare of snow. The sky was the color of hammered bronze. And filling the pass below her, wingtip to wingtip, were dragons.

Hundreds. Thousands. They lay along the ridgelines like hills themselves, scales catching the bronze light, and the air rang with song — not melody as she understood it but something structural, harmonic, a chord sustained by a hundred vast throats, a sound she felt in her teeth and sternum and the backs of her eyes. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, and she understood, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that the sound was a covenant. A promise being renewed. Voices answering voices across the peaks: *we hold, we hold, we hold.*

***This was the Choir,*** said the coal-voice, gentle as ash falling. ***When the mountains were young and your people were few, we sang the seasons into their courses. Fire in winter's heart. Rain for the dry valleys. Your ancestors brought offerings; we brought the long weather. It was not worship. It was partnership.***

The vision shifted. The bronze sky curdled to grey. The dragons thinned — dozens, then a handful, the ridgelines emptying like a tide going out. And the song frayed. Voices dropped away mid-phrase, and the remaining singers strained to hold chords built for a hundred throats, and the harmony sagged, wavered—

***Your kings decided the covenant was a tax.*** No anger in the voice. Something worse: weariness, old and settled as sediment. ***They wanted the fire without the offering. The rain without the song. When we refused, they called it rebellion. They sent soldiers. Then they sent priests of the new quiet god, and the priests sent inquisitors, and the inquisitors were thorough.***

She saw it — brief, brutal flashes: a roost burning, eggs cracking in the heat like rotten fruit; a dragon the size of a cathedral standing over a canyon mouth while men with hooks and chains swarmed its flanks; a shrine like the one under her feet, tended by a line of keepers in grey robes, and soldiers dragging the keepers out, and one keeper — small, dark-haired, a face that made her chest seize with recognition three centuries too late — pressing a bundle of scales into a smuggler's arms and saying, *hide it, feed it, remember.*

***The Ravensworths remembered,*** the voice said. ***For three hundred years, your family fed the flame of Caerlum — my flame — a cupful at a time. Prayer. Tending. Blood spilled willingly on these scales, a drop at each solstice. It was never much. It was enough. Barely. Always barely.***

The vision turned one last time, and this time Dominic cried out.

A flame. Vast beyond reason, coiled in a mountain's heart, a bonfire the size of a village — and guttering. Dying. The way a candle dies in a draft, the light shrinking, the gold going to orange going to red going to a sullen purple ember-ring around darkness. She could feel it dying as though it were her own heartbeat slowing. Feel the cold creeping in at the edges of the world where the flame's reach no longer touched. Feel — distantly, horribly — the spring coming later each year to the valley towns, the fish moving deeper, the lambs coming smaller.

***This is what your family kept alive,*** Caerlum said. ***This is what you inherited. A god at his last coal. And the last chorister who might sing him back — or let the verse end.***

The cellar slammed back around her. Stone walls. Grey dawn-light. Her knees on cold flagstones and her hand still pressed to the altar, which was cooling now, the rose-gold veins dimming like a fire settling.

Dominic sat back hard on the floor and discovered she was shaking.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough for the dawn light to strengthen from grey to pale gold through the high window. Long enough for her tears to dry on her face without her noticing them start. She laughed once — a single cracked, disbelieving sound — and put her head back against the wall and stared at the ceiling beams.

Three hundred years. Salt routes and toll schedules and winters eating bark bread, and the whole time, the guild was a wick. Her grandmother's cramped handwriting wasn't madness or metaphor. *The fire must be fed.* Marta laughing, never understanding. Generations of women and men kneeling in secret on dragon scales, giving drops of blood to keep a dying god breathing, and passing the lie down like an heirloom because the truth would get everyone burned — literally, recently, by people like Serane Voss.

"We're not smugglers," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the empty cellar. "We never were. We're *clergy*."

The word tasted absurd. Her. Dominic Ravensworth, who'd learned to pick locks before letters, who'd bribed her first border guard at nine years old, who'd buried her mentor with a flask of cheap brandy and a smuggler's blessing — clergy. Last chorister. Keeper of a flame she hadn't known existed a week ago.

And yet her hand still remembered the warmth. And somewhere behind her sternum, faint but unmistakable, something was humming — the same note the coins had carried, the same note she'd felt through the altar. As if the vision had tuned her, the way you tune a string, and now she rang faintly at the covenant's frequency whether she wanted to or not.

***Chorister,*** the coal-voice murmured — softer now, almost fond, present in the room again though the altar had gone dark. ***Do not mistake what you saw for burden alone. The flame remembers every hand that fed it. Yours most of all. You are not the end of the verse, Dominic Ravensworth. You may yet be its turn.***

"The turn toward what?" she asked the dark. "More hiding? More cellars? More Ravensworths lying to their children?"

***Toward the last roost. Toward the fire itself. Toward a choice that only you can make.*** A pause, long as a held breath. ***But know this first, and carry it carefully: the enemy you fear is already on the road. The one who burns shrines. She hunts the resurgent faith, and faith is stirring in the valleys like sap in thawing wood. Prayers are being answered again, chorister. All across the north. And where prayers are answered, the quiet god sends his knives.***

Cold walked up Dominic's spine that had nothing to do with the cellar.

"Voss," she whispered.

***She does not yet know your name.*** The warmth gathered itself, drawing inward, banking down the way a fire banks for the night. ***Keep it that way. Feed the flame. Learn the first verse — it is written where your grandmother wrote everything that mattered. And when the stranger Ysolde wakes, ask her what she truly carries, for she is more than she seems, and the road south runs through her.***

"Wait—" Dominic lurched forward, hand reaching for the altar. "Wait, you can't just — what choice? What choice am I supposed to—"

***Sing,*** said the god, in a voice like the last coal settling into ash, ***and survive to sing again.***

The warmth went out. Not dead — she could feel that now, could feel the difference the way you feel the difference between an empty house and a sleeping one — banked, waiting, patient as it had been patient for three hundred years under the feet of people who didn't know what they were walking on.

Dominic knelt alone in the amber-dark afterglow, heart hammering, the hum still faintly ringing in her bones.

Above her, through the floorboards, she heard the guildhall wake: Bramble's footsteps, the clatter of the kettle, Osk's boots on the stairs. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of her family, who were not smugglers, who had never been smugglers, who were the last keepers of a dying god — and who had maybe a season, maybe less, before an inquisitor with a ledger full of burned shrines came up the valley asking questions.

Dominic rose on unsteady legs, took the journal from where she'd left it, and opened it to the page her grandmother had pressed flattest, the ink worn nearly away by thumbs:

*First verse, for the solstice feeding. Teach it to no one outside the blood.*

Beneath it, in characters older than any alphabet she knew — characters that matched, exactly, the markings she'd seen on Ysolde's god-coins — ran a line of song.

She began, very quietly, to learn it.
