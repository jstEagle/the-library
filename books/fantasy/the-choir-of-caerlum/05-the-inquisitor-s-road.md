## 5. The Inquisitor's Road

The shrine at Millbrook had been built into the roots of an ash tree, which was typical of them. They never raised temples anymore — that would be honest. Instead they hollowed out living things and called it devotion.

Serane Voss stood in the drizzle with her hands folded behind her back and watched her two soldiers work. Brother Aldric had the pry bar; Corporal Fen had the lantern shielded under his cloak to keep the oil dry. The tree was old, older than the town that had grown up around it, and its roots had swallowed the little altar whole so that only a carved face showed through the bark — round-eyed, open-mouthed, neither male nor female, wearing an expression Voss could only describe as *expectant*.

"Careful with the face," she said. "I want it intact for the record."

Aldric glanced back at her. He was a big man, patient, pious in the unremarkable way of most soldiers, and he had learned not to ask why an idol slated for burning needed to survive the process. "Yes, Inquisitor."

They worked it free in pieces. The heartwood came out in chunks that wept sap, and there, set into the hollow like a pearl in a rotten fruit, was the altar: a flat stone worn smooth by centuries of hands, ringed with script no one in Millbrook could read. Someone had left offerings recently. That was what interested Voss. Not the stone — stones were patient, stones would wait out any inquisition — but the *offerings*. A clay cup of milk, still fresh enough to have skinned over. A child's shoe, one only, laced shut. Three copper coins of no mint she recognized, green with age, stacked with a precision that suggested ritual rather than carelessness.

Fresh milk meant a living hand. A living hand meant a living faith.

"Burn the tree," she said. "Not yet. First I want the town assembled."

* * *

Millbrook held perhaps four hundred souls, a wool market, a mill whose wheel turned brown with the season's first silt, and a chapel of the Concord with a roof that leaked in exactly the way leaking roofs did when a parish had stopped believing its tithe mattered. Voss had the assembly called for the hour after noon, when the rain thinned to a grey mist and the whole valley smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke. She stood on the chapel steps with the idol's face propped on an easel beside her, and she let them look at it while she looked at them.

She had done this eleven times in three years. She knew how crowds behaved. They came angry or they came afraid, and either way they came curious, and curiosity was the seam you put your blade into. She catalogued faces as she waited: the miller with his arms crossed, defensive before anything had even been said. The reeve sweating through his chain of office. An old woman near the front who kept glancing at the idol with something that was not fear. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Voss marked her.

"People of Millbrook," she began, and pitched her voice to carry without shouting. Shouting was for men who needed volume because they lacked patience. "I am Serane Voss, Inquisitor of the Concord, Third Chair. I have come from the south, where last month I closed a shrine in Corren's Ford and took confession from a woman who had been leaving bread for the river-god for forty years. She is fine. She has been reassigned to a parish where she will learn what her devotion cost her neighbors." She paused. Let that settle. "You should understand what I am and am not here to do. I am not here to punish you. The Concord does not punish belief. Belief is air; you cannot arrest air. I am here to remove the objects and the practices by which belief becomes *obedience* — obedience to things that are not the Concord, not the crown, and not answerable to either."

She touched the carved face lightly, one finger beneath the round eyes.

"This is Caerlum. Or a piece of him. The mountain-god, the fire-that-sleeps, the Flame Under Thornfell — he has as many names as he has hamlets, because small gods are like small dogs; every household gives them their own name and believes theirs is special." A ripple of uneasy laughter, quickly smothered. Good. Laughter loosened people. "Three hundred years ago your ancestors made a bargain. They gave up these gods — burned them out, starved them out, forgot them on purpose — in exchange for peace. Real peace. The wars of the Godtorn Age ended not because anyone won but because everyone agreed to stop feeding the things that fed on the fighting. And now, in this generation, someone has started feeding again."

She lifted the clay cup of milk and held it high.

"Who brought this?"

Silence. Rain dripped off the chapel eaves. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I will ask differently," Voss said, still pleasant. "Who brought this *last*? Because the milk is fresh, friends, and fresh milk means a cow, and a cow means a household, and I have time. I have all winter. The passes close, the roads empty, and I will sit in your inn drinking your beer and asking this question until spring, if that is what it takes. But I would rather not. I would rather one of you do the arithmetic now, alone, in your own head, and step forward, and be dealt with gently, and let the rest of you go back to your lives."

Nobody moved. But Voss watched the crowd the way a fisherman watches water, and she saw the eddies. The miller uncrossed his arms and crossed them again. A boy of maybe twelve looked at the old woman near the front, then away, too fast. And the old woman herself — grey-braided, hands like knotted rope — closed her eyes for the space of one breath, the way people do when they are praying, and opened them again with her chin up.

Voss smiled at her.

"You. Grandmother. Come here."

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostler, and she was seventy-one years old, and she lied to Serane Voss with the calm of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and considered death a solved problem.

"The milk was mine," she admitted, in the chapel vestry, with Aldric taking notes in a fair clerk's hand. "I leave it for my dead. My mother did, and hers. It's remembrance, Inquisitor, not worship. There's a difference."

"There is," Voss agreed. "Remembrance is permitted. What I need to know is whether the difference holds. Tell me about the coins."

Maren's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Almost. "What coins?"

"Three coppers, unstamped, found on the altar. Old money, grandmother. Older than any kingdom currently printing it. Where does a wool-town widow get coin no mint has struck since the Godtorn Age?" Voss leaned forward. "And don't say you've always had them. Heirlooms are passed down openly, proudly, hung above mantels. Coins like these are passed down *quietly*, with instructions, and instructions mean doctrine, and doctrine means you are not remembering your dead. You are serving something."

The old woman met her eyes. Voss gave her credit for it; most didn't.

"My husband traded on the mountain road," Maren said. "Smugglers used to pay in odd coin sometimes. Old stock. It happens."

"It happens," Voss echoed. "Once, maybe. Three coins, matching, ritually placed — that isn't happenstance, that's a pattern, and patterns have sources." She sat back. "Here is what will happen now, Mistress Ostler. You will tell me nothing today, I can see that. So instead you will hear me. The Concord's writ covers idolatry, which is a sin of objects, and apostasy, which is a sin of souls. Objects I can burn all day and it costs me nothing but lamp oil. Souls are more expensive, and I spend them reluctantly — but I do spend them. If I find the source of those coins, and the source leads through your house, your family's remembrance will be reclassified as worship, and worship carries penalties. Do you understand the shape of what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you're threatening an old woman," Maren said.

"I'm informing one," Voss said. "There's a difference."

She dismissed her then, and watched through the vestry window as the old woman walked home through the mist, spine straight, past the blackened stump where the ash tree had stood an hour ago. The tree was burning now, Aldric seeing to it, and the smoke rose straight despite the damp, a grey column visible from every farm in the valley. That was deliberate. Shrines were private things; burnings were public ones. You did not destroy a god in front of witnesses because the god needed destroying. You did it because the witnesses needed to see that destruction was possible, that the thing they half-believed in could be reduced to smoke and wet charcoal by two men, a pry bar, and a jug of lamp oil.

Faith was mostly a rumor. You killed rumors with demonstrations.

* * *

The innkeeper's daughter told her about the Ravensworths.

It happened the way these things always happened: not through interrogation but through proximity. Voss had taken the corner room at the Drowned Lamb for three nights, and she made a practice of sitting in the common room each evening with a single cup of small beer she barely touched, doing nothing, being seen doing nothing. People grew uncomfortable around a motionless inquisitor the way horses grow uncomfortable around a fence post that wasn't there yesterday. They filled the silence. They always filled the silence.

On the second evening it was the innkeeper's daughter, a sharp-faced girl of nineteen named Tilly, who'd been refilling Voss's untouched cup out of pure nervousness.

"They're all gone, you know," the girl said, apropos of nothing, wringing her apron. "The Ravensworths. Everyone says the guild died with old Marta, but it's not true, is it? There's still one."

Voss did not look up from her cup. Looking up was pressure, and pressure made people stop talking. "One what?"

"A Ravensworth. The granddaughter. Dominic." Tilly pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed the name in her head. "Old Marta Ravensworth raised her after — well, after whatever happened to her parents, nobody talks about that. She ran with the guild since she was a girl. Carried loads through Thornfell when she was fourteen, they say. Now she keeps to the old guildhall up past the mill, comes down for salt and lamp oil, doesn't speak to anyone." The girl hesitated, then couldn't help herself: "People say the hall's empty. That it's just her rattling around in it. But she buys food for five, sometimes six. Flour by the sack. My father says she's mad, keeping a dead guild's accounts, but —"

"But?" Voss prompted softly.

Tilly bit her lip. "But flour by the sack isn't grief, is it? Grief eats little. Whoever's up there, she's *feeding* them."

Voss turned her cup slowly on the table, once, watching the beer swirl. "Where is this guildhall?"

"Up the Thornfell track, past the mill race. Half a day's walk. You can't miss it — it's got the old guild sign over the door, a wren carrying a key." The girl frowned suddenly, as if hearing her own words from outside. "Inquisitor, I didn't — smuggling's not illegal anymore, is it? The guild's been dissolved twenty years. Whatever she's doing up there—"

"Smuggling, no," Voss said. "The crown forgave the guild its debts when it dissolved. But harboring persons, trafficking in proscribed goods, practicing rites of the old covenant — those are very much alive in the law, and they travel together the way rats travel with grain." She finally looked up and gave the girl a mild, almost kind smile. "You've been helpful, Tilly. Forget you were helpful. That's an order, not a threat — though it functions as both."

The girl fled. Voss sat a while longer, finishing her beer at last, thinking.

A wren carrying a key. She knew that sigil. Every inquisitor did; it was in the third chapter of the *Codex of Severance*, in the list of marks proscribed alongside the broken-wheel of the river cults and the nine-pointed star of the lowland shrines. The wren-and-key had been the badge of the Choir's *wardens* — the lay order that had tended the dragon-shrines, kept the flame-tithes, carried the god-coins from roost to roost in the last century before the Severance. When the armies came, the wardens hadn't fought. They had *hidden things*. Relics, altars, eggs if the old accounts were to be believed, sealed behind doors keyed to bloodlines, and then they had scattered and become, within two generations, indistinguishable from smugglers, tinkers, hedge-witches, and other folk who lived by moving quietly along the edges of things.

The Concord had spent three hundred years assuming the wardens were extinct. Extinction was a comforting word. It meant you could stop looking.

But a guildhall outside a mountain town, stocked for more mouths than it held, run by the last of a keeper-bloodline, receiving old god-coins from somewhere — that was not extinction. That was dormancy. And dormancy, Voss knew from long experience, was the state in which dangerous things did their best work.

* * *

She wrote her report that night by candlelight, in the small precise hand the Third Chair demanded, and she read it over twice before sealing it.

*To the Office of the Third Chair, Bastion of the Concord, from S. Voss, Inquisitor. Shrine at Millbrook destroyed, 14th of Frostmonth. Idol recovered intact (face of Caerlum, ash-root construction) and forwarded with this letter. One (1) elderly practitioner interviewed and released under observation; assessment: custodial, not doctrinal — she tends the site but did not found it and cannot name its source. Source sought. Of greater weight: the Ravensworth line persists. One Dominica Ravensworth, approx. mid-twenties, resident of the former guildhall on the Thornfell track. Guildhall provisioned beyond apparent occupancy. Sigil of the wardens extant on premises. Recommend immediate survey of the hall and interrogation of the subject before winter closes the passes and evidence becomes inaccessible.*

She hesitated over the last line, quill hovering. Then she added:

*Note: the god-coins recovered from the Millbrook altar are warm to the touch. This should not be possible. Requesting the Chair's guidance on precedent.*

That was true, and she disliked how much it pleased her to write it. She had handled the coins herself that afternoon, alone, after Aldric bagged them. They had been warm the way a stone is warm after a day of sun — except there had been no sun, only rain, for six days. And when she'd held one to her ear, briefly, telling herself it was due diligence and nothing else, she had heard — or almost heard, or imagined she heard, and the distinction felt suddenly, urgently important — a sound like a note held very far away. Like a choir heard through a mountain.

She had put the coin down. She had washed her hands. She had prayed, properly, to the Concord's faceless unity, and felt the familiar clean nothing answer her, and had been grateful for it. That was the whole virtue of the Concord: it answered nothing. It asked everything and answered nothing, and a soul could build a life on that foundation the way a house builds on bedrock. These other gods — warm coins, singing stones, milk-drinking idols in trees — they *answered*. That was their corruption. Not the idolatry, not the doctrine. The answering. Once a person had been answered, they were ruined for bedrock forever. They spent the rest of their lives listening for the next reply, and listening was a wound that never closed, and wounded people did desperate things.

Voss had seen it in Corren's Ford. She would see it again.

She slept badly, which was unusual, and woke before dawn with the certainty already fully formed in her chest, the way her best certainties always arrived — not reasoned but *deposited*, whole, like coins in a cup.

The Ravensworth girl was not a smuggler. Smugglers were vermin; you trapped them and moved on. The Ravensworth girl was a warden, the last warden, sitting on whatever the wardens had spent three hundred years hiding — and somewhere north of Millbrook, in the cold throat of the Thornfell passes, there was a door that opened to her blood, and behind that door was the reason the coins stayed warm.

Voss dressed in the dark, buckled her sword, and went downstairs to wake her soldiers.

"We ride at first light," she told Aldric. "Up the Thornfell track. Bring the pry bar."

He blinked. "Another shrine, Inquisitor?"

"No," Serane Voss said, pulling on her gloves. "Something better. A door."
