## 6. Found Kin

The first thing I learned about running from an inquisitor is that you don't run. Running leaves tracks, and tracks leave stories, and stories get people hanged. So I walked — fast, with a pack on my back and Ysolde's humming coins sewn into my lining like a fever I couldn't sweat out — and I walked the old ways my mother had taught me before she taught me letters.

The shepherd's cut above Millbrook. The drover's ford where the water runs shallow over slate. The hollow oak at Windrow that's been dead since before the war and hollow since before that, with a cache inside that hadn't been touched in eleven years because everyone who knew it was dead or scattered.

Everyone who knew it was dead or scattered.

That was the thought that kept me walking when the cold got into my knees and the coins got into my dreams. The guild wasn't a guild anymore. It was a name on a burned ledger and four people too stubborn to admit they were the last of anything.

I needed them anyway.

* * *

Bramble was the closest, if you counted three days through thawing marsh as close, which after Thornfell I did. She lived in a turf-roofed cottage at the edge of the Fenn, growing things that had no business growing this far north, and she answered the door with a knife already in her hand because Bramble always answered doors that way.

She lowered it when she saw me. Mostly.

"Dom." Her eyes went over my shoulder to the empty track, then back to me, cataloguing. That was Bramble. She read people the way she read plants — by what was wilted, what was blooming out of season, what had been chewed. "You look like something a fox buried and dug up again."

"Good to see you too."

"It isn't. Come in before someone sees you."

Her cottage smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and, underneath it, the faint green rot of her workroom, where she grew the things she didn't sell to honest herbalists. She put water on without asking and sat me down and stood over me with her arms crossed until I told her all of it. Not everything — not the door, not the voice in the dark, not the word *chorister* still ringing in my skull like a struck bell — but Voss, and the shrine burned at Millbrook, and the old woman who paid in god-coins, and the way the inquisitor's road bent toward us like a compass needle finding north.

Bramble listened the way she did everything: completely, and with visible irritation about it.

"Serane Voss," she said at last. "I've heard the name. They say she burned the shrine at Harrowmere with the priest still inside."

"They say right. I saw what was left of Millbrook's shrine. It wasn't a shrine anymore. It was a stain."

"And you came to me because?"

"Because you're guild."

"I'm *retired* guild," Bramble said. "There's a difference. I grow cabbages. I sell poultices. I have not carried so much as a bolt of smuggled silk in six years, and I would very much like to keep not doing that until I die in this chair at a respectable age, which will not happen if Serane Voss finds me harboring the last Ravensworth."

"You're harboring me right now."

"Yes, and I'm furious about it." But she was already moving, pulling a satchel from under the floorboards, filling it with the quick, angry competence of a woman who had decided something and hated herself for how fast she'd decided it. "Where next?"

"Osk."

"Osk won't come. Osk never comes."

"He'll come," I said. "For this, he'll come."

* * *

We found Osk where Osk always was in the turning of the year: high pasture country, following the wild goats, trading meat and tracking-work to the hill farms in exchange for bread and being left alone. He saw us coming from half a mile off — of course he did; he'd have seen us from two miles if the fog hadn't been thick as wool — and he was waiting at the edge of his camp with his bow strung and his face carefully blank.

Osk was deaf. Had been since a fever at six years old, before the guild found him, before anyone found him. He spoke the way he did everything else: precisely, quietly, with a carefulness that came from knowing every word cost him effort other people got free. And he read lips better than any person I've ever known, better than hearing folk read voices, so you learned quickly to face him and speak plainly and never, ever mumble.

I faced him and spoke plainly.

"Inquisitor on the road. Coming up the Thornfell track. She knows about the door."

His eyes flicked to Bramble, then back to me. His hands moved as he signed — he'd taught me the guild's hand-talk years ago, in long winter evenings when there was nothing to smuggle and nowhere to be — *Knows how much?*

"That it exists. That it opens to my blood. Maybe more. She had someone talking. Pellam-shaped someone."

Something crossed Osk's face then, brief as a fish under ice. Pellam had been the one who pulled him out of the workhouse. Pellam had spent a whole winter teaching him snares, badly, because Pellam was bad at snares and good at stubbornness.

*He wouldn't,* Osk signed.

"People say that," I said. "People are usually wrong, and even when they're right, it doesn't matter, because Voss doesn't need him to talk. She just needs him to be seen with us."

Osk looked at the mountains behind his camp. He'd lived up here three years, alone by choice, closer to the sky than to people. I watched him weigh it — the silence he'd built against whatever we were asking him back into.

Then he began rolling his bedroll.

*I'll need my good boots,* he signed. *And someone has to carry the salt.*

"What salt?"

He pointed at a crate by the fire. Forty pounds of it, at least. Trade goods, I realized. He'd been planning to walk down to the valley markets in a week's time, same as every year, same small honest life.

He was bringing it anyway. Because if you were going to run from an inquisitor with your family, apparently you did it prepared to barter.

* * *

Pellam was the hard one.

Not because he was far — he was near, disgracefully near, drinking his knight's pension into ale in the river town of Corley's Bend, one bridge down from Millbrook. He was the hard one because of what I had to do when I found him.

He was in the taproom of the Drowned Gull, third cup deep, sitting with his back to the wall the way ex-soldiers sit, and when I walked in he lit up like a boy — actually lit up, big open grin, starting to rise — and I watched the grin die as he read my face.

"Dom." He sat back down slowly. "Dom, what's wrong."

"We need to talk. Outside."

"Whatever it is—"

"*Outside*, Pellam."

The yard behind the Gull was mud and rain barrels and the smell of the river. I turned on him and I didn't soften it, because I'd had three days of cold camps to practice not softening it.

"Voss has you. Or she had you. Don't lie to me, I'll know."

The color went out of his face like water out of a cracked jug. "How—"

"She's coming up the Thornfell track with soldiers and a pry bar, and she knows things only someone who'd sat at our fire could have told her. So either she's a witch or you talked. Which is it?"

"I—" He put a hand on the rain barrel to steady himself. Big man, Pellam, big hands, a face like a dropped plate that had been glued back together wrong. "It wasn't — Dom, it wasn't like that."

"Tell me what it was like."

And he did. Standing in the mud with the rain starting, he told me about the night in Millbrook when Voss's men took him quiet off the street, and the room with the brazier, and the questions that started gentle and stopped being gentle, and the moment — he couldn't look at me while he said it — the moment when they'd mentioned Bramble. Said they knew about the hedge-witch in the Fenn. Said names travel, sir, said it would be a shame if the list grew longer.

"They didn't hurt me," he said. "That's the filth of it. They didn't have to. They just — held her name up in front of me like a lantern, and I walked toward it. I told her about the guildhall. About the cellar. About—" his voice cracked, "—about you. That you were alive. That you'd come back to the passes."

The rain came harder. I let it.

"Pellam." I waited until he looked at me. "I'm not here to forgive you. I'm not sure I can yet, and I'm certainly not sure I should. I'm here because Voss is three days behind me and she thinks you're her lever, and the only chance any of us have is if she stops being able to predict us. Which means you come with us, and you carry what you did, and you look at it every day, and you don't get to set it down. Can you do that?"

He was crying now, this disgraced knight twice my age, crying in the rain like it was nothing, like men do when they think no one counts it.

"Aye," he said. "Aye, Dom. Whatever it costs."

"It'll cost," I said. "Get your sword."

* * *

We went to ground in the hollow oak at Windrow, because it was the only place left that felt like ours, and because I wanted walls around the argument I knew was coming, even if the walls were made of dead tree.

Osk built a smokeless fire, because Osk could build a smokeless fire in a hurricane. Bramble brewed something bitter that she claimed was tea. Pellam sat apart, which was correct, and nobody pretended otherwise. And I laid it out plain, the way you lay out cargo before dividing it.

"Here's where we stand. Voss is on the Thornfell track. She has maybe twenty soldiers, a pry bar, and a warrant that lets her burn anything she calls idolatrous, which so far has meant shrines, priests, and one granary that was probably just in the way. She knows the guildhall. She knows the cellar. She may or may not know about the door, but she'll find it, because pry bar. We cannot fight her. Twenty blades against four is not a battle, it's a scheduling note in her journal. So we run. South, past the war-torn valleys, somewhere she can't follow. Tonight."

Silence. Rain on the oak's dead crown.

Then Bramble said, "No."

"No?"

"No." She set down her cup. "You want to know why I retired, Dom? Why I really retired? Because in thirty years of smuggling I watched this guild lose everything worth having and keep calling itself a guild. Master Aldous died and we lost our route. The war came and we lost our buyers. One by one, everyone with sense took their coin and their scars and went to grow cabbages. And the whole time, the one thing we never lost—" she jabbed a finger at me, "—was each other. That was the entire guild, girl. That was the *cargo*. And now you're telling me to load it onto my back and run south and bury it in some fen where no one will ever find it, and call that survival. I did my running. I'm done."

"Bramble, she burns people."

"I heard you. I also heard you say she knows about the cellar. You know what's in the cellar, Dom. You've gone strange and quiet whenever anyone gets near the subject, but I've known you since you were nine and stealing my comfrey, and I know what hiding looks like. There's something down there worth an inquisitor's warrant. Something your grandmother's lot kept for three hundred years. And you want to run from it."

I said nothing. The coins hummed against my ribs.

Osk's hands moved, slow and deliberate. *Bramble's right.* Then, seeing my face, gentler: *Not about fighting. About staying together. Wherever we go, we go as the guild. If we scatter, we're done, and Voss wins without a sword drawn.*

"And you?" I turned to Pellam. Last voice. He'd been staring into the fire like it owed him money.

"I'll go wherever you go," he said. "Fight or flee. I've no standing to argue either way." A pause. "But since you asked. Voss doesn't stop. I saw her work, Dom, those two days in Millbrook before she let me go. She's not doing a job. She's on a pilgrimage of her own. Run south and she follows. Run across the sea and she builds a boat. The only question is whether she follows four scattered people or one family that's ready for her."

"So that's it," I said. "Three votes for standing. Against one vote for living."

"Against one vote for *running*," Bramble corrected. "Different thing."

I looked around the firelight at them — the witch with her knife-callused hands, the tracker signing to himself the way other people hummed, the knight sitting in his own shame like it was a wet coat he couldn't take off. My guild. Four people and a name on a burned ledger. Everything the world would say was nothing.

Everything I'd have burned a kingdom down to keep.

"Fine," I said. "We stay. But we stay smart. We don't defend the guildhall — it's a tomb and she knows it's a tomb. We take what matters out of the cellar tonight, we break the trail behind us, and we choose the ground. Osk, I need tunnels. Old ones. Smuggler's ones."

Osk grinned. It transformed his whole face, made him look fifteen again. *I know every hole in Thornfell. Some of them even have two ends.*

"Bramble, wards. Not the pretty kind. The kind that slow soldiers down and make noise doing it."

"The expensive kind," she said. "Naturally."

"Pellam." He straightened. "You're the only one of us who's worn a soldier's coat. When it comes to it, you tell us how they'll move, how they'll think, how she'll think. Every trick you know. Even the ones she used on you." I held his eyes. "*Especially* those."

He swallowed. Nodded. "Every one."

"Then we're agreed. We're the guild. All that's left of it, and enough." I reached into my coat and brought out one of Ysolde's coins, set it on the flat stone between us. In the firelight it seemed almost to glow, and if you listened — and I was beginning to learn that listening was a skill, not a metaphor — you could hear it. Faint. Patient. Like a choir holding its breath between verses.

Bramble leaned close. "Is that coin... warm?"

"It's a long story," I said. "And it starts with a door."

Which was when Osk's head came up, sharp as a hound's, and his hands cut the air fast and urgent:

*Riders. On the Windrow road. Many.*

* * *

We killed the fire and pressed ourselves against the oak's heartwood and looked out through the split in the bark, and there they were, torches strung along the valley road below like a rosary of embers. Too many to count in the dark. Too disciplined to be bandits. At their head, a rider in grey who sat her horse the way a blade sits in its sheath.

She shouldn't have been here. Three days behind me, I'd calculated. Three days of margin.

I'd forgotten the one thing Pellam had given her that mattered more than the cellar. Not the location.

The habit of thinking like us.

"She's not going to the guildhall," I breathed. Bramble's hand found my arm. "Look. She's riding past the turn. She's taking the high road." Toward Windrow. Toward the hollow oak. Toward the first place a Ravensworth would ever think to hide.

Osk's hands moved in the dark, steady as falling snow:

*Tunnels. Now.*
