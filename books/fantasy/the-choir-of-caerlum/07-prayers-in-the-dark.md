## 7. Prayers in the Dark

Osk felt the mountain before he heard it — before anything, because Osk had not heard anything since the fever took him at nine years old, the winter the guild found him frozen half to death in a culvert outside Marrowdale.

He felt it through his palms.

He had pressed one hand flat against the tunnel wall, as he always did at the mouth of a new passage, and the stone was talking to him. A low tremor, rhythmic, patient. Boots. Many boots. Somewhere above, on the old military road that switchbacked up Thornfell's eastern face, soldiers were marching in column, and every footfall was a small hammer blow that traveled down through rock and root and clay until it reached his hand like a knock on a door.

*Left to right,* the stone said. *Even spacing. Iron shod.* He had learned to read this language over thirty years of silence, and it never lied to him. Cavalry thumped wider and looser. Carts rolled with a grinding hum. Men on foot in marching order made this sound — this exact sound — and there were perhaps sixty of them, moving upslope toward the pass road's high shoulder, where a company could seal the whole eastern approach with a barricade and six crossbows.

They were closing the passes. Voss was closing the passes.

Osk pulled his hand back and turned to the others waiting in the dark behind him, their lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light, and he signed what he knew with the quick, blunt shorthand the guild had built for him over decades — a language of knuckles and forearms and flicked fingers that no outsider had ever bothered to learn, because why would they? Who taught a deaf man to be a smuggler?

The Ravensworths did. That was the answer. The Ravensworths had looked at a starving, silent boy pulled out of the snow and seen not a burden but an instrument, and then, harder and stranger, had seen a son.

---

He signed: *Sixty men. Above us. Moving to the shoulder. We go now or we go never.*

Dominic read his hands by the slit of lantern light the way she'd been doing since she was twelve years old, when she'd first followed him into these tunnels trailing her mentor like a curious cat. She signed back fast, sloppy with hurry but legible: *How long before they reach the shoulder?*

Osk counted against the wall again, palm flat, eyes closed. The rhythm told him what he needed. Marching men kept time whether they meant to or not; you could measure distance off it the way a sailor measures fathoms. *Half an hour. Maybe less if the officer pushes them.*

*And the tunnel?* Bramble asked, her hands stiff and cold and imprecise, because hedge-witches were not born smugglers and never quite learned to speak with their fingers cleanly. Osk had gotten good at reading bad hands. Half the valley mumbled when they signed, just like they mumbled when they spoke.

*Clear,* he signed. *I walked it three days ago. But the far gate is rusted shut and I'll need Pellam's shoulders on it.*

Pellam, standing at the rear with his sword still slung where a knight would sling it even after the king had stripped him of everything else, nodded without needing the signs translated. He had picked up more of Osk's language than anyone realized — enough for yes, no, wait, danger, and *follow me*, which covered most of what mattered underground.

Ysolde stood between them all, watching their hands move in the dark like moths, and Osk caught the look on her face — wonder, maybe, or recognition. Later, around the cold campfire in the waystation cavern, she would ask him how long he had been deaf, and he would tell her the truth: since the fever, since the snow, since before the guild. And she would sign back at him, clumsily, a single word she must have remembered from some dead liturgy of the old priesthood — a word for *listen* — and press her palm flat against the stone the way he did.

She had felt the mountain too. That was the moment Osk decided, privately, in the wordless ledger where he kept such decisions, that the strange pilgrim who paid in singing coins was telling the truth about something. You couldn't fake that. You either put your hand on the world and waited, or you didn't.

Most people didn't.

---

They moved.

Osk led, always, one hand trailing the left wall — the guild rule, older than anyone living, *left hand keeps the wall, right hand keeps your life* — counting paces in his head, feeling the tunnel's breath change against his face. Every passage had a breath. Air moved through Thornfell's bones like blood through a body, drawn by the temperature difference between the deep cold of the high caves and the thin warmth of the valley mouths, and a man who couldn't hear could still feel which way the wind of the mountain wanted him to go. When the air pushed against his cheekbones, the passage ran true to the surface. When it sucked past his ears, drawing inward, somewhere ahead the tunnel opened into a chamber large enough to breathe for itself.

Two hundred paces in, the air began to pull. Good. The Waystation Chamber was clear.

Behind him the little procession stumbled and cursed and kept up: Dominic with the pack straps cut short so nothing swung; Bramble clutching her satchel of jars and dried herbs like a mother carrying an infant; Pellam walking with his head ducked, a big man folding himself small out of habit, out of shame, out of the bone-deep conviction of disgraced men that they take up too much room in the world; and Ysolde, quiet, watchful, her god-coins wrapped in cloth at her breast, humming — Osk felt it through the floor when she walked, a faint buzz in the soles of his feet, like a struck tuning fork wrapped in wool.

The coins sang. He'd known since the night she paid them. Everyone thought he hadn't noticed, standing apart from the fire while the others argued about the payment, but he had felt the table vibrate under his forearm and had said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining how he knew, and there were things Osk kept in the dark on purpose. Not secrets, exactly. Seeds. Things you planted and waited to see if they grew.

The coins were still humming tonight, fainter now, and the direction of the hum had changed. He noticed that too. In the guildhall cellar they had buzzed strongest when Ysolde held them near the sealed door — the door only Ravensworth blood opened, the door Dominic had unlocked two nights ago with her grandmother's ring and a scream of rage at everything her family had hidden behind it. Now, walking south through the mountain's dark, the hum pulled at his feet like a current. Southward. Downward. Toward whatever shrine the old journal had called *the Choir's rest*.

The mountain was pulling them. Or the coins were. Or the god was. Osk didn't much care which name you gave it. He had spent his whole life listening to things other people couldn't hear, and he had learned that the difference between superstition and observation was mostly a matter of who was willing to put their hand flat on the stone and wait.

---

In the Waystation Chamber they stopped, and Osk let them stop, because the rule was that you rested where the air pooled warm and the walls showed no fresh tool-marks, and this chamber met both tests. It was an old place, older than the guild. Someone had hollowed it long before the first Ravensworth ran salt through these passes — someone who had carved a shallow basin into the floor at its center, and channels leading away from it like veins, and Osk had spent thirty years walking past those carvings without once wondering what they were for.

Ysolde wondered. Ysolde knelt at the basin and brushed the grit away with her sleeve and went very still, and Osk watched her face change in the lantern light the way water changes when a stone drops into it.

"This is a font," she said aloud, to Dominic, forgetting — or not caring — that Osk would catch only the shape of her lips and the shadow of her hands. "This whole chamber. It's not a smuggler's waystation. It's a shrine. The tunnels aren't smuggling routes. They're processional ways."

Dominic crouched beside her, and Osk watched the two women's hands move — Ysolde tracing the channels, Dominic signing questions, Ysolde answering in speech that Dominic translated with quick flicks of her fingers for the rest of them. The guild's language bending around a stranger, making room. It always did. That was the thing about the Ravensworth signs: they had never been a secret. Any soul the guild took in got taught in the first week, because a language you keep locked isn't a language, it's a leash.

Bramble sat down hard on a stone shelf and started unpacking food without being asked, because that was Bramble — worry turned outward into work. Pellam stood at the chamber's mouth with his hand on his sword hilt, watching the dark they'd come from, and Osk joined him there, because watching the way you'd come from was the other half of the guild rule nobody wrote down.

Pellam glanced at him. Signed, slowly, carefully, like a man handling borrowed words: *You feel them? Still?*

Osk pressed his palm to the wall. Listened with his skin.

The boots were gone. Not stopped — *gone*. The tremor had faded below the threshold of his reading, dissolved into the mountain's own slow pulse, the deep tidal groan of frost cracking and thawing in the high reaches. Either the soldiers had halted somewhere the stone didn't carry well, or they had turned off the road, or — and this was the possibility that sat in Osk's chest like a swallowed hook — they had found another entrance.

There were always other entrances. That was the whole doctrine of the guild. Every seal has a seam. Osk knew eleven ways into Thornfell that weren't on any map the crown possessed, and he had to assume that a woman like Serane Voss — methodical, patient, buying information the way other people bought bread — would find one eventually. Probably already had. Probably had a boy from Marrowdale showing her the culvert entrance right now, the same culvert the guild had pulled Osk out of thirty years ago, sold to her for coin or fear or both.

That thought arrived in his chest and stayed there, cold.

*Still moving,* he signed finally. *But I don't trust the count anymore. Too many echoes. This mountain is full of throats.*

Pellam nodded grimly and went to tell Dominic, and Osk stayed at the wall with his hand flat on the stone, and for a long moment there was nothing in the world but the pulse of the rock and the faint southward tug of the humming coins and the memory that rose in him now, as it always rose in this chamber, unbidden:

The night the guild took him in.

---

He had been nine. The fever had taken his hearing in autumn, and his mother — a washerwoman with four other mouths and no winter stores — had carried him to the shrine at Marrowdale's edge and left him there with a blanket and a heel of bread, which was not cruelty, whatever the town gossips said. It was arithmetic. The same terrible arithmetic Pellam would one day do at a river crossing, trading truth for a life. Osk understood that kind of math better than anyone alive, because he had been the number subtracted.

The shrine priest had fed him for six days and then told him gently that the shrine could feed him no longer, that winter was coming, that a silent boy was a hard charity to keep. On the seventh day a broad-shouldered woman with grey in her braid came to the shrine on guild business — paying, Osk learned later, for the priest's silence about a shipment — and saw the boy sitting alone in the corner, watching the world with his whole body because he couldn't watch it with his ears.

Her name was Maren Ravensworth. Dominic's grandmother.

She crouched in front of him, and instead of speaking — everyone spoke at him, loudly, uselessly — she held up her hand and tapped his wrist twice. Then she pointed at herself and tapped her own wrist twice. Then she pointed at him again and raised one eyebrow.

*Your name. Give it to me this way.*

No one had ever asked him for anything in his own language before. He didn't have a language yet, not really, just gestures. But he gave her the gesture he used for himself — a fist tapped against the chest, the sign he'd invented because *Osk* was a sound and sounds were gone from the world — and Maren Ravensworth looked at it, and repeated it exactly, and then taught it back to him three times until it was theirs.

Then she stood up, and told the priest she was taking the boy, and dared anyone in the shrine to object. Nobody objected. People rarely objected to Maren Ravensworth.

At the guildhall she set him in front of the whole assembled crew — twenty smugglers, scarred and profane and half-drunk — and announced that the boy was deaf and would be staying, and that any man who mocked him would answer to her. And then, that same night, she began inventing. She took his fist-to-chest sign and gave it to him permanently. She built the rest around it, week by week, from the raw material of the guild's existing hand-codes — the counting signs, the danger signs, the signals they'd always used across loud market squares — until within a year Osk wasn't the guild's charity. He was its eyes. Its ears, in the only way that mattered. The man who could stand in a storm on the pass and know, from the vibration in the rock under his boots, whether the patrol above had horses.

Maren was the one who first put his hand flat on the tunnel wall and said — signed — *the stone talks. Learn its voice.* Everyone thought she was humoring the boy. She wasn't. She had felt the marching columns through the rock herself, years before, and had never trusted her own dull human senses to read it well. She needed someone whose whole body was an ear.

So the guild gave him the mountain, and the mountain gave him back to himself.

And now the last Ravensworth was walking south through the dark with a pilgrim who paid in singing coins, and soldiers were sealing the passes above, and the guild that had made him was down to five souls and a burned secret, and Osk kept his hand on the stone and listened, because that was the only prayer he had ever known how to say.

It occurred to him — standing there in the carved shrine older than the guild, feeling the southward pull in his feet, feeling the mountain's pulse slow and deepen like a sleeping heart — that maybe the stone had been talking to all of them for three hundred years. Maybe the Ravensworths hadn't been smugglers who happened to find the tunnels. Maybe they had been listeners who happened to run salt to pay for the listening. Maybe Maren had known. Maybe that was the real thing behind the sealed door, behind the journal's careful evasions, behind the name *the Choir* — not a treasure, not a duty, but a conversation, ongoing, and the guild had been one side of it for generations, and the other side had finally, desperately, started shouting.

Osk pressed his palm harder against the rock.

*Talk to me,* he signed against the stone, knowing how it looked, knowing Bramble would laugh if she saw. *Tell me where they are. Tell me where the fire sleeps. Tell me anything.*

The stone pulsed. Slow. Deep. Patient as banked coals.

And then — faint, so faint he almost doubted the reading, almost blamed his own wishful blood — the pulse stuttered. Skipped. Resumed in a new rhythm, quicker, brighter, like a heart waking.

From the center of the chamber, Ysolde gasped aloud, and Osk turned to see her staring at the basin, at the coins in her hand glowing through the wrapping cloth with a light like the last coal in a dying fire catching wind.

She looked up and found his eyes, and signed — badly, urgently, the word she'd practiced on the walk, the word for *listen* —

—and Osk, hand still flat on the mountain's skin, felt the whole tunnel system inhale.

Far to the south, deeper than any guild map had ever charted, something vast turned over in its sleep. The tremor came up through his boots, through his knees, through the cage of his ribs, and it was not boots, not carts, not frost. It was a wingbeat, once, in the dark of the world.

Above them, faint through forty feet of rock, the soldiers' boots resumed their march.

Osk signed to Dominic, fast and certain: *We're out of time. Move.*
