## 8. The Bargain at Hollow Ford

Pellam had crossed Hollow Ford a hundred times in his life, and it had never once frightened him.

It was a mean little crossing, honestly. The river came down off the Thornfell shoulders wide and shallow, braided through gravel bars, and the old smugglers' road dipped to meet it where the water ran slowest. In summer you could walk across without wetting your knees. The guild had used it for decades because the king's surveyors had marked the deeper crossing two miles east, at the bridge, where the tollhouse sat and the soldiers drank. Nobody watched a ford that wasn't on any map worth printing.

Now Pellam stood on the north bank with Bramble's arm hooked through his and watched six riders come down the eastern trail at a canter, and understood that maps were not the only things that could be wrong about a place.

"They've been behind us since the shrine," Bramble said. Her voice was flat, which was worse than shaking. Bramble shook when she was angry. She went flat when she was afraid. "Since before the shrine. That scout on the ridge yesterday — I told myself it was a herder."

"It was a herder," Pellam said.

"It was a herder wearing soldier's boots."

He couldn't argue with that. He'd seen the boots too, and said nothing, because saying something would have meant admitting what he already knew in his gut: they were not going to outrun Serane Voss. Not with Osk's bad knee, not with Ysolde moving like a woman three times her age, not with Dominic carrying whatever she carried now — that look she'd had since the cellar, since the altar, since the voice in the dark that Pellam had not heard but had felt, the way you feel a storm coming through the soles of your feet.

The riders fanned out along the far bank. Six of them, chainmail under grey cloaks, crossbows slung but hands loose near them. And at their center, riding a black courser too fine for this country, a woman in a long coat the color of wet ash.

Pellam had met inquisitors before. He'd been a knight once; he'd stood at attention while men of the Inquisition read charges against villages, and he had told himself then, as he told himself for years after, that the law was the law and his oath was to the crown and not to the conscience. He knew the type. Cold eyes, patient voice, a certainty so complete it looked like calm from the outside.

Serane Voss dismounted at the water's edge and did not hurry. That was the first thing that scared him properly. She let her horse drink. She pulled off one glove, finger by finger. She looked across the ford at the four of them — five, with Ysolde hanging back among the alders — the way a merchant looks at goods she has already decided to buy.

"Pellam of House Aldery," she called, and his own name in her mouth was like a key turning in a lock he'd thought rusted shut. "Knight-banneret, retired. Or *degraded*, I should say. I read your file on the road north."

Bramble's grip tightened on his arm. "Don't answer her," she murmured.

"She already knows my name, Bramble."

"Then don't confirm anything else."

But Voss wasn't waiting for confirmation. She walked out onto the gravel bar as if the river were hers by deed, boots dry on the stones, and stopped where the water lapped at her heels. Close enough to talk without shouting. Far enough that a crossbow bolt would have to be very lucky or very deliberate.

"I'm going to make an offer," she said. "Not to all of you. To you." Her eyes found his and stayed there, and Pellam understood with sick clarity that she had done her reading. She knew exactly which of them would bend. "You wore the crown's colors for eleven years. You took the oaths before you ever heard of Ravensworths or shrines or whatever superstition is rotting this valley. Somewhere under the smuggler's grime there is still a man who believes in order."

"There is," Pellam said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "And he believes the Inquisition burning granary-shrines in a famine year is not order. It's arson with paperwork."

Something flickered across Voss's face. Not anger. Interest. "You think I enjoy the burning? I burned a shrine two days ago that held nothing but dried flowers and a child's carved dragon. You know what else that village held? Fever. Three dead this month. The old women say the god is punishing them for forgetting. The old women are wrong — it's bad water — but try telling them that while they're leaving offerings to a stone. Belief like that kills more surely than any plague. I am not destroying faith out of cruelty, Sir Pellam. I am performing surgery."

"And the people you cut?"

"Live longer than the ones who die of worship." She said it without heat, without irony, and that was the worst part. She believed it. He could see the whole architecture of it behind her eyes, load-bearing and sound, every beam squared: the gods were dead or never lived, the flame was a story, and stories that made people kneel to stones instead of digging wells were a disease, and she was the knife. A true believer. Those were always the dangerous ones. Liars could be bought.

"Here is the offer," Voss continued. "You will tell me where the guildhall is. Where the passage runs. Whether there is an altar — and I know there is an altar, Pellam, my sources are better than your tradecraft. Do this, and I take you into custody as a cooperating witness. The hedge-witch walks free. No pyre. The deaf boy walks free. The old woman—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—the old woman interests me, but she can be questioned gently."

"And Dominic?"

Voss smiled, and it was the smile of someone who has already won and is being generous about the pace. "Dominic Ravensworth interests me most of all. But interests are not sentences. Cooperate, and perhaps she becomes a witness too. Perhaps merely a name in a report. Refuse—" she turned, gestured lazily at the ridgelines, "—and I have forty men sealing every pass south of here by tomorrow night, and a writ that lets me burn whatever shelters you. There is no version of the next week in which you win, Sir Pellam. There is only a version in which fewer people burn."

Behind him, Bramble made a small sound. Not words. Just a sound, the kind a person makes when the arithmetic lands.

Because that was the thing. It was arithmetic. He could see it laid out plain as a ledger: refuse, and Voss took them all — took Bramble, who had pulled him out of a fever swamp when he was a disgraced drunk with a sword he didn't deserve; took Osk, who was twenty years old and deaf and had never harmed anyone but the king's tax rolls; took Ysolde, who was older than sin and half as strong. Or trade. Trade a building. Trade a tunnel. Trade stones and secrets, and keep the people.

*They're just buildings,* he told himself. *The guildhall is timber and slate. The tunnel is dirt. If Dominic's god is real, no fire touches it. If her god isn't real, then it's only stones anyway.*

It was a good argument. It was the kind of argument he'd have accepted from anyone else without a second thought.

"Say it," Voss said quietly. "Just say where."

"The hall sits above the old quarry road," Pellam heard himself say. "Half a mile past the cairn with the split top. There's a cellar door under the east wall. It doesn't open for soldiers. But the passage beyond it runs south under the ridge line, comes up in the hollow below Shepherd's Tor."

He said it all in a level voice, like reporting a patrol route, and some part of him stood apart and watched himself do it and felt nothing at all, which was its own kind of horror.

Voss put her glove back on, finger by finger, unhurried even now. "Wise," she said. "Truly. Most men need breaking first. You'll spare everyone a great deal of pain." She raised two fingers toward her riders. "Take the witch and the boy into custody. Gently. The old woman as well."

"You said they'd walk free."

"They walk free *of the pyre*," Voss said. "I never promised they'd walk free of me. You should listen more carefully, knight. It's a failing of your whole profession." She paused, and something almost like respect crossed her face. "Though I'll honor the substance. They'll be treated well. Better than they'd have treated themselves, blundering into winter with an inquisitor on their heels. Consider it mercy with supervision."

The riders splashed across. There was no fight to make — six crossbows, open ground, Osk's knee, Bramble's thin arms. Pellam stood on the bank and watched them bind Bramble's wrists with cord, gently, almost courteously, and Bramble looked at him over the rider's shoulder, and she didn't curse him. That was the unbearable part. She just looked at him with those flat, clear eyes and nodded once, slowly, as if to say: *I understand the sums. Do you?*

Then they were marched away east, and Voss mounted, and before she rode off she leaned down from the saddle and said, low, for him alone:

"When you find Dominic Ravensworth — and you will, because men like you always crawl back to the ones they've failed — remember that I kept my word to the letter. Remember that the next bargain costs more."

She rode east. The river kept talking over its stones, indifferent, and Pellam stood alone on the north bank until the riders were gone and the light started going copper in the west.

Then he turned and walked up into the alders, where Ysolde waited with her hood back and her ancient face unreadable, and beyond her, down the smuggler's track, Dominic was coming back at a run because Osk had signed to her from the rise — *soldiers, taken, gone* — and her face when she saw Pellam standing there empty-handed was the last thing in the world he wanted to see.

*

He told it all. That was the only penance he could think of, so he did it completely, standing in the lee of a shepherd's ruin while the wind came off the high snowfields and Osk watched with his sharp dark eyes and Ysolde sat on a fallen lintel like a woman attending a play she'd seen before.

He told them about the file Voss had read, the degradation, the oath-breaking that had ended his knighthood — though they'd known that part, mostly, or guessed. He told them about the fever swamp and Bramble hauling him back from death with nettle tea and insults. He told them the exact words of the bargain, every clause, because he would not soften a single one. And when he got to the end — *the hall sits above the old quarry road* — his voice finally cracked like ice underfoot.

"I gave her the hall," he said. "I gave her the tunnel. I traded stones for Bramble's life and I'd do it again, that's the filthiest part, I'd do it again right now, and I'll burn for it either way. Because either the god is real and I sold a holy place, or the god isn't real and I sold *you* for nothing but a witch who'd have cursed me gently on the pyre." His hands were shaking now, finally, when it was too late to matter. "I spent eleven years learning that survival is arithmetic. Bodies weighed against bodies. It's how you stay sane in a war. You do the sums and you live with the total. But this—" he looked at Dominic, and made himself hold her gaze, because looking away would have been the last cowardice, "—this total has your family's name in it. Your grandmother's hall. So judge me. Whatever you decide, I'll carry it. I just won't lie about what I did."

The wind moved through the alders. Somewhere above, a bird called once and quit.

Dominic hadn't spoken through any of it. She stood with her arms folded tight, and Pellam watched her work through it — watched the rage come up in her face and get met, head-on, by something else. By the memory, maybe, of every time he'd stood between the guild and trouble. By the simple fact that Bramble was alive because of what he'd done, and Bramble was theirs.

"You did the sums wrong," she said at last, and her voice was quiet and terrible. "Not because you traded. Because you thought the hall was the thing you were trading." She stepped closer. "Voss thinks it's a building. Let her. She'll kick in a cellar door that won't open for her, and she'll find a passage, and she'll think she's won something. But the altar doesn't live in the walls, Pellam. It never did. My grandmother's journal says the Ravensworths kept the flame *in themselves*. The hall was just where we slept." She exhaled, long and slow, and some of the fury went out of her with the breath. "So yes. You sold her timber and dirt. And you saved Bramble. And I am so angry at you I could put you through that wall, and I am also not going to, because if I'd been standing in your boots I don't know — I *don't know* — that I'd have chosen different."

"That's more mercy than I—" Pellam started.

"It's not mercy," Dominic said. "It's arithmetic. Yours was wrong. Mine says we have until tomorrow night before forty men seal the passes, and Voss has Bramble, and Bramble will tell her everything she knows the moment it matters, because Bramble is smart enough to know resistance is just pain with extra steps. Which means whatever advantage the hall gave us is already spent. All we have left is speed." She turned to Osk, and her hands moved as she spoke, matching the signs to the words so he'd catch every piece of it. *South. Tonight. The tunnel comes up at Shepherd's Tor — if Voss hasn't found the far end yet.* Then to Ysolde: "How far to the roost?"

Ysolde's eyes had been closed through the whole confession. Now they opened, and they were bright, and sad, and very old. "Farther than you have strength for," she said, "and nearer than Voss can imagine. The mountain opens for keepers, child. It does not open for knives."

"Then we walk tonight," Dominic said. "And Pellam—" she looked back at him, and what was in her face now wasn't forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but it was trust of a harder and more valuable kind, "—you walk at the rear. Where I can see you. Not because I doubt you."

"No," Pellam said. "Because you should."

He fell in behind them as the last light died, and the four of them went south along the river in the blue dark, toward the tor, toward the tunnel, toward the mountain's heart. Behind them, far off to the northeast, a smudge of orange rose against the stars where the valley town lay.

Pellam watched it for a long moment before following.

Fires, this early in the season. Too many fires, and all in one quarter of the sky.

Voss had said forty men by tomorrow night.

She had lied about that too. She'd brought more.
