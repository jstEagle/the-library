## 9. Songs Older than Kingdoms

Ysolde had not slept in a bed with walls around it in forty years.

The guildhall's guest room was narrow and smelled of tallow and old wool, and she lay awake in it listening to the mountain breathe. That was how she thought of it now — the breathing. She had felt it first on the pass, when Dominic had stopped to check the wind and Ysolde had pressed her palm flat against the rock face without thinking, the way her teachers had taught her when she was nine years old and the world still had gods in it. The stone had been warm. Not sun-warm. *Heart*-warm.

She had not touched living stone in three centuries' worth of dead years. Her hands had shaken for an hour afterward, and Dominic had noticed, because of course she had — the girl missed nothing, which was either a blessing or a curse depending on what you were trying to hide from her.

Ysolde had been hiding things from people for most of her life. It was strange, at her age, to feel the weight of them shifting.

* * *

She found Dominic in the cellar before dawn, as she had known she would. The girl kept smuggler's hours even now that there was little left to smuggle; grief had made her nocturnal, and the cellar had become the place she went to be near the door she could open and did not understand.

Ysolde stood in the shadows of the stair and watched her by the light of a single candle. Dominic had her hand flat against the sealed door again — the one only Ravensworth blood opened — and her eyes were closed, and her lips were moving. Not words. Something between a prayer and an argument.

"You'll wear a groove in it," Ysolde said softly.

Dominic started, then didn't turn, then turned all at once, the way she did everything — fast, guarded, half-expecting a blow. "Can't sleep either?"

"I'm old. Sleep and I parted ways decades ago." Ysolde came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the wall. Her knees announced each step. "But I knew you'd be here."

"Because I'm predictable."

"Because you're your grandmother's granddaughter." Ysolde lowered herself onto the upturned crate that served as the cellar's only chair. "Maren Ravensworth used to come down here too, in the last years. I never met her — don't look at me like that, girl, I know how it sounds — but I read her journal, or what survives of it. She came down here to argue with a door."

Dominic's jaw tightened. "You've been reading more than the journal I gave you."

"I have." There was no point denying it. Ysolde had gone through the guildhall's records two nights ago while the others slept, and she was not going to pretend otherwise. Bramble had caught her at it and, after a long look, had simply handed her a lamp and gone back to bed. The hedge-witch understood something about old women and their hungers. "I needed to know if this place was what I hoped it was. Or feared."

"And?"

Ysolde looked at the door. At the scale set into its center — dull silver-grey, broad as a shield, veined with lines that caught the candlelight like water catching moon. A hatchling's scale, the records said. Carried down from the mountain four hundred years ago by a woman named Ravensworth who had been neither smuggler nor keeper but something older than both.

"And I think it's time you and I stopped circling each other," Ysolde said. "You've given me passage, shelter, patience. You've opened a door for me that opens for no one else alive. You've earned the truth, and I am too old and too tired to keep carrying it alone."

Dominic set down the candle. Folded her arms. Waited. She had the particular stillness of someone who had learned young that the person talking was usually the person lying, and Ysolde respected it even as it made her task harder.

"Ask me what I was," Ysolde said.

"What were you?"

"A priestess." The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh — relief and pain in the same motion. "Not a very good one, at the end. But yes. Sworn, anointed, trained from childhood in the songs. The Choir of Caerlum wasn't a name I invented for your benefit, Dominic. It was the name of the thing itself. The dragons sang. We learned the song. We carried it between the roosts and the shrines, we tended the flames, we taught the verses to anyone whose blood could hold them. And then—"

She stopped. Her throat closed on the next part, the way it always did, the way it had for forty years.

Dominic waited. She was good at waiting. Smugglers were.

"And then the kingdoms decided the gods were competitors," Ysolde finished. "That's the clean version. The true version is worse and longer and involves a great many men in fine robes deciding that fire should belong to whoever held the crown, and a great many priests — my predecessors, my teachers — dying badly in places with names no one remembers. The Purges, they called them afterward. Cleansing, they called it while it happened. Three hundred years ago, give or take. Every shrine burned. Every song forbidden. Every priest who wouldn't recant went into the ground or into exile, and those of us who fled learned to be quiet. Quiet for a lifetime. Quiet until the song forgot itself."

"But it didn't," Dominic said. "Forget."

"No." Ysolde smiled, and the smile was bitter and grateful at once. "It didn't. Because of families like yours."

* * *

She told it plainly, because she was done with ornament. The Ravensworths had been choristers once — not the only ones, but among the last, and then simply the last. When the fires came, they had done what keepers do: they had hidden the flame inside ordinary life. They became smugglers. It was brilliant, in its way. Who suspects a smuggler? Men who move contraband through mountain passes are invisible by profession, and so the Ravensworths moved the one cargo that mattered — embers, scales, songs, the secret of the shrine — under cover of salt and brandy and untaxed silk. For generations. While the world above forgot the gods entirely, the guild below kept a coal alive in a clay pot and passed it hand to hand, mother to daughter, singing the verses under their breath on the passes where no one listened.

"The journal," Ysolde said. "Your grandmother's. You told me it was mostly accounts. Tolls paid, routes run, debts owed. Did you notice the margins?"

Dominic had brought the journal down with her — of course she had; she slept with it nearer than her knife these days. She set it on the crate between them, open to a page Ysolde had marked. In the margin beside a record of winter tolls, in Maren Ravensworth's cramped hand: *kept the third verse warm tonight. she hums when the frost comes.*

"She hums," Dominic repeated. "The flame hums."

"The flame sings. The coins I paid you — you heard it. Faintly. Everyone with the blood hears it faintly; most people hear nothing at all and call such coins cursed or counterfeit." Ysolde leaned forward. "Dominic. What did you think the guild was *for*? All those generations, running the same passes, taking the same risks, for shrinking profits, in a trade that was dying? Your mentor bled the guild dry keeping it alive past any sense. Why would he do that?"

Dominic was silent for a long moment. "He never said. He'd just say — *the route matters, Dom. Some routes matter.*" Her voice roughened on the memory. "I thought it was sentiment. An old man's habit."

"It was a covenant. He knew. Maren told him, or his mother told him, back and back and back — each generation told the next just enough. Keep the door. Keep the scale. Keep the flame in the high shrine fed, quietly, without worship, because worship would bring the king's men down on you. And wait." Ysolde spread her hands. "Wait for the day the world changed enough that the song could be sung aloud again. Or wait for the day the last keeper died and the flame went out and it finally, finally ended."

"And which day is it?"

"That," Ysolde said, "is why I came to find you."

* * *

She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the sealed door. She did not touch it — it answered to Ravensworth blood, not hers; she had tested nothing here except her own courage — but she stood close enough that the warmth of it reached her face. Living stone. A sleeping altar behind it, the records said, and a hatchling's scale, and beneath the floor, if the oldest entries were true, a channel cut to carry heat up from somewhere deep.

"The ancient war," Ysolde said. "You'll have heard of it. Every kingdom has its version. The dragons were monsters. The dragons were tyrants. The heroes slew them and freed mankind. It's in every chronicle, embroidered on every tapestry, carved over every gate." She turned. "It is a lie, Dominic. It is the founding lie of every crown on this continent, and I have spent forty years walking toward the place where I could say so out loud."

"Then tell me what actually happened."

"We broke our word." Simple. Flat. Ysolde let it sit there. "There was a covenant — the First Verse, we called it. Man and dragon, sworn at the mountain's heart before either had kingdoms or crowns. They gave us fire — real fire, the kind that warms, the kind that teaches, the kind that lets a small soft species survive winters it has no business surviving. We gave them song. Memory. Witness. Dragons live long, Dominic, longer than mountains almost, and a long life without witness curdles into something terrible. We were their rememberers. They were our hearth. That was the bargain, and it held for a thousand years, and it might have held forever."

"What broke it?"

"Greed. What always breaks covenants." Ysolde's mouth twisted. "A king — the histories disagree on which; they've all claimed the honor since — decided that if fire could be given, it could be taken. That a dragon's flame, harnessed, would win him the whole continent. He didn't want to slay the dragons, whatever the ballads say. He wanted to *milk* them. And when the priesthood refused to teach him the binding-verses — and we refused, I promise you, at terrible cost — he found other priests. There are always other priests. Men who would sing anything for a bishopric." She shook her head. "They took fire by force. And the dragons —"

She stopped again. This part she had never said aloud, not in forty years of walking, and it came out of her now in a voice gone thin.

"The dragons didn't fight back. That's the part no one believes. They could have burned every army, every city, every crown off the map. Instead they withdrew. Roost by roost, they went silent and went up — into the high places, the far places — and they stopped singing. Not because they were defeated. Because they were *grieved*. They had given us their fire in trust, and we had turned it into a weapon, and they concluded — reasonably, I sometimes think — that the experiment of loving mankind had failed." She looked at Dominic. "The war wasn't a war. It was a divorce. And everything since — the cold creeping down from the peaks, the harvests failing, the sickness in the lowland flocks, all the miseries your inquisitors blame on lingering idolatry — that isn't the gods punishing us. It's the hearth going out. We are living in a house whose fire was stolen, and everyone is freezing, and no one will admit why."

Dominic had gone very still. Ysolde had seen soldiers go still like that, receiving news that reorganizes the entire battlefield.

"The vision," Dominic said at last. "At the altar. Caerlum showed me the flame guttering. Said I was the last chorister. Said—" she swallowed "—said the choice was coming. Restore or extinguish."

"Yes."

"You knew. Before you ever hired me on the pass. You knew what I was."

"I hoped," Ysolde corrected gently. "There's a difference, and I've had forty years to learn it. I knew the Ravensworth line survived — the guild's name surfaces in old records every few decades, always deniable, always half-erased. I knew if any blood remained that could open doors like this one, it was yours. So I came north with god-coins and a story about a pilgrimage, and I put myself in the hands of the last smuggler on the Thornfell passes, and I prayed — properly prayed, for the first time in forty years — that I hadn't come all this way to find a woman who would laugh at me and sell me to the nearest magistrate."

"I considered it," Dominic said. Dry. Almost smiling.

"I know. Bramble told me. She also told me you didn't, and that you sat up all night with the coins humming in your fist." Ysolde let that land. "You're not your grandmother, girl. You're not Maren, keeping the secret small and safe and buried. You asked the door questions. You prayed at the altar half-mocking and got an answer anyway, which means the god judged your mockery honest and your heart open. That's more vocation than most priests ever manage."

"So what is it you want from me?" Dominic's voice hardened, the smuggler surfacing, the negotiator. "Say it plain. You want me to carry something south. I can see it in you. You've been working up to it for days."

Ysolde nodded. No more circling.

"There is a last roost," she said. "South, past the war-torn valleys, past the burned shrines, in a canyon the maps stopped naming two hundred years ago. One dragon remains that I know of — Vaelith, eldest of the Choir, the one who sang the First Verse. She is dying. Old beyond old, alone beyond bearing, holding the last ember of the covenant in a body that is giving out. If she dies without a successor-flame lit — without a keeper to receive the song — then it ends. Truly ends. Not suppressed. *Ended*. The cold keeps coming down off the peaks forever, and no one living will ever know it was a wound and not a weather."

"And you want me to carry the flame-secret there. The shrine. The scale. Whatever's behind this door."

"I want you to carry *yourself* there," Ysolde said. "The rest follows. The flame-secret is not a parcel, Dominic. It's a voice. Yours."

* * *

Dominic walked away from her then — not far, just to the far wall, just to put stone at her back, and Ysolde recognized that too. People who had been lied to by their own history needed walls at their backs while they reassembled themselves.

"My family weren't smugglers," Dominic said to the wall. "We were priests in hiding. For four hundred years. And nobody — not my mentor, not my grandmother's journal, not one damned scrap — nobody thought I deserved to know."

"They thought they were protecting you."

"They thought I was a tool to be kept sharp and ignorant." She turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and magnificent. "Well. Maybe I was. Maybe that's exactly what I was. And maybe the difference between a tool and a priestess is just whether somebody finally tells you the truth and asks instead of orders." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "So I'm asking. Is this an ask, Ysolde? Or is this the covenant talking? Because I've had enough of being ordered by dead people."

"It's an ask," Ysolde said. "The covenant doesn't compel. That was the whole of it — the whole First Verse, underneath all the music. Fire given freely or not at all. The kings never understood that. The inquisitors never will. It's the one law older than kingdoms, and it's the reason I'm standing in a cellar begging instead of commanding." She spread her empty hands. "I am asking, Dominic Ravensworth. Last chorister. Carry the song south. Save the last dragon. Unbreak what we broke — or try. And I won't pretend it costs nothing. I've watched what it cost your family for four hundred years of pretending to be something smaller than they were."

The cellar was quiet. Above them, faintly, through floors of old timber, Ysolde could hear Bramble moving, Osk's silent footsteps, Pellam's uneven tread — the ragged remnant of a guild that had been a congregation all along without knowing it.

And beneath all of it, so faint she might have imagined it, the door was humming.

Dominic heard it too. Ysolde watched her hear it — watched her head tilt, watched her breath catch, watched the fury drain out of her face and leave something rawer underneath.

"It's louder tonight," Dominic whispered.

"It knows you're close to answering," Ysolde said. "And Dominic — there's something else. The reason I chose now, the reason I couldn't wait another season. On my way north I passed soldiers on the roads. White cloaks, grey hoods, asking after shrines and singers. An inquisitor's company, hunting resurgent idolatry. They were a valley behind me and moving faster than an old woman walks." She met the girl's eyes steadily. "Whatever you decide — decide soon. Because I don't think we're the only ones who've heard the song starting up again. And some people have built their whole lives on making sure it stays dead."

Dominic looked at the door for a long moment. Then she blew out the candle, and in the dark, in the warm dark, Ysolde heard her say — barely aloud, the way you speak to something you're not yet ready to believe in:

"All right. South, then. All of us."

The door hummed like a struck bell.
