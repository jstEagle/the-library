## 10. Voss Closes the Net

The soldier who brought the news was shaking, and Serane Voss found that instructive. Men shook for two reasons: because they had seen something holy, or because they had lied to someone holier than themselves.

"Say it again," she said, without looking up from her ledger. The inn at Hollow Ford smelled of tallow and wet wool and fear, which was as it should be. Fear kept accounts honest.

"The knight, m'lady. Pellam of—of the Thornfell garrison, before it was disbanded. He's been drinking in the common room since dusk. He says he wants to confess to someone with authority."

"And you have authority?"

The soldier went pale. "I didn't think—I only thought you'd want—"

"I do want." She closed the ledger. "That is why I am here, and you are there. Bring him."

He came in wet from the rain, this broken knight, and Voss watched him the way she watched everything: cataloguing. He had the look of a man whose armor had been his skin for so long that losing it had left him flayed. He sat when told to sit. His hands were steady until they weren't.

"Inquisitor," he said. "I have sinned against the Crown's peace."

"Everyone has," Voss said. "Be specific."

"There are others. Survivors of the Ravensworth guild. A witch, a tracker, a woman called Dominic Ravensworth herself. They're gathering at the old guildhall above the valley. They mean to go south."

Voss turned her chair toward the fire. It was a small fire, inadequate, like every fire in this damp country. "Why south?"

Pellam's jaw worked. "I don't know."

"You know something. You simply don't know that you know it." She let the silence do its work. Silence always did. "You traded your friends' road for a witch's life tonight. That was the arithmetic, yes? Her pulse against their route. Don't be ashamed of it. Shame is just pride wearing sackcloth. But understand what you've purchased: you've bought her an hour, perhaps two, and sold me everything else. So now tell me about the hall. Its doors. Its cellars. What the Ravensworths kept below."

"Kept?" He frowned, genuinely. "Nothing. Grain, casks, the smuggling tunnels—"

"Then you can say so under oath, and if I find otherwise, you'll hang beside them. Think carefully, knight. Is there anything below that hall that a man might call sacred?"

And there — there it was. Not in his words but in his stillness, the particular paralysis of a man standing on a floor he has suddenly realized is thinner than he thought. Voss smiled, and it felt on her face like weather changing.

"No," Pellam said.

"Yes," she said pleasantly. "Thank you. You've been very helpful. Sergeant! Our guest will be escorted to comfortable quarters and given nothing further to drink."

* * *

They moved within the hour, thirty picked men through rain-slicked pines, and Voss rode at the front because she had learned long ago that inquisitors who commanded from behind were believed less than inquisitors who arrived first. The valley climbed around them, black trees dripping, and somewhere above, the guildhall waited with its thin floors and its buried secrets.

She did not hate the old gods. That was the thing no one understood, the thing her superiors in the Chantry would have found distasteful if they'd bothered to wonder. Hatred required belief. You could not hate a thing you knew to be dead; you could only hate the people who refused to bury it. And the people — ah, the people were the whole disease. Every burned shrine, every smashed idol, three hundred years of it, and still the peasantry whispered to stones, still they left bread on windowsills for things that had never once answered. Still they taught their children the old names like heirlooms.

Because the gods had abandoned them, and abandonment, Voss knew, was the wound that never closed. She had prayed as a child. Earnestly, terribly, kneeling until her knees bled on chapel stone, asking the silent sky for one sign, one word, anything — while her mother coughed her life out in the next room. Nothing came. Nothing ever came. And when she grew and read the Chantry's doctrine she understood at last that the silence was not neglect. It was mercy of a kind: the old powers were real, and real things demanded payment, and the covenant-breakers of antiquity had paid in blood and harvests and firstborn sons until humanity rose up and ended the account forever.

The plague in the southern valleys. The blight on the wheat. The famines that followed the wars like gulls follow ships. The Chantry blamed bad seasons. Voss had read the old records, the ones with pages missing, and she knew better. The old gods were not dead. They were sleeping, and sleeping things dreamed, and dreams leaked. End the sleepers entirely — burn the last shrines, find the last keepers, snuff the last flame — and the world would finally, truly belong to the people in it.

That was not cruelty. That was surgery.

"Halt," she said, as the hall's lights showed through the trees. "No torches until my signal. We take the yard quietly. Anyone runs, take them alive. Dead witnesses convince no one."

The guildhall was larger than she expected, and older, built of stone gone silver with lichen, squatting under the mountain's shoulder like a toad under a wall. Smoke from its chimney. A light in an upper window. Somewhere inside, the last Ravensworth was deciding how to spend her final hours of ignorance.

Voss gave the signal.

* * *

It went wrong immediately, of course. It always did. The witch — Bramble, the knight had named her, a hedge-witch with grey braids and eyes like a sparrowhawk — must have had wards strung through the treeline, because the moment the first soldier crossed the yard's edge, every bell in the hall began ringing at once though no hand touched them, and a voice like wind through hollow reeds spoke a single word that made three men drop their weapons and sit down in the mud, weeping.

"Wards," Voss said, unimpressed. "Break them."

Breaking them cost her two soldiers' noses bleeding freely and one man unconscious, but the witch's craft was hedge-craft, root-magic, the kind that worked by consent and suggestion, and it had never met a mind like Voss's: a mind that had spent thirty years refusing to believe in anything that did not file reports. She walked through the yard's boundary herself and felt the wards slide off her like rain off oiled leather, and she heard the witch scream from inside — *they're here, they're here, Dom, run!* — and noted, with professional satisfaction, that the woman had chosen warning over flight. Loyalty. Useful. Predictable.

They took the hall in eleven minutes.

The tracker was not there — gone, presumably, ahead or scouting — nor the Ravensworth woman. But the witch they took in the kitchen, fighting like a wildcat with a paring knife until a sergeant broke her wrist, and after that she went quiet in a way Voss recognized: the quiet of someone recalculating.

Voss had her brought to the main room, sat in a chair, and left alone except for Voss herself. Then she waited, hands folded, while the witch's breathing slowed and her eyes did their furious arithmetic.

"You're the Inquisitor Voss," the witch said finally. Voice like gravel in honey. "They tell stories about you down-valley."

"They usually get the burning wrong. It was one shrine, and the priestess who tended it had been selling children to slavers under cover of incense. Ask anyone from Marrowdale." This was true, and Voss offered truths the way other people offered coins: precisely, and expecting change. "Where is Dominic Ravensworth?"

"Gone."

"When?"

"This morning."

"Liar. There's a horse in your stable still warm, and two more in the paddock, and your kettle is hot. You expected her back tonight." Voss let that sit. "Here is what will happen, witch. At dawn, my men begin taking this building apart. Stone by stone if necessary. I am told there is a door in the cellar that opens for Ravensworth blood alone. When we find it — and we will find it — I will need that blood. Do you understand me?"

The witch's face did something complicated. Grief and defiance and, underneath both, a terrible quick tenderness. "You'll not touch her."

"I'd prefer not to touch any of you. Blood is inefficient. Which is why I'm offering terms." Voss drew a sheet of paper toward her and dipped a pen, unhurried. "Dominic Ravensworth may walk away. Free passage south, no pursuit, my written word on it — and my word, whatever you've heard, is iron. In exchange, she surrenders to me whatever lies beneath that door. The altar, the relic, the flame — whatever the Ravensworths have been keeping warm all these centuries. She gives it up, and she goes free, and you go free, and the deaf tracker and the drunken knight go free. Everyone lives. The gods stay dead. The world stays sane."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then at dawn I start breaking fingers, beginning with yours, and I will continue alphabetically through everyone I catch, and I will still find that door, and I will still open it, and the only difference will be how much of it happens in front of her." Voss set down the pen. "Survival has a price, witch. Someone has always paid it. All I'm doing is naming the amount honestly, for once, instead of hiding it in taxes and tithes and pretty lies about glory. Write the note."

"I'll write nothing."

"Then I'll write it, and you'll mark it." Voss wrote, in a clear clerk's hand:

*Dominic Ravensworth. Your people are in my keeping. Come to the hall before dawn with what your family guarded, and all of them walk free — yourself included. Refuse, and I take it anyway, and pay for it in ways you will carry longer than grief. — S.V.*

She slid it across. "Your mark."

The witch looked at the paper for a long time. Then she spat on the floor, took the pen, and drew her mark — slow, deliberate, a curse in the shape of a letter.

"Sergeant," Voss said. "Put our guest somewhere defensible. And double the watch on the treeline. If the Ravensworth woman has any sense, she's watching us right now — and if she has any love, she's already coming."

* * *

Alone, later, Voss stood in the hall's main room and let herself look at it properly. Smugglers' ledgers on the shelves. A cold hearth. Boots by the door in three sizes, set neatly, waiting for owners who would come home to a trap.

On the mantel, someone had left a bowl of milk.

Just that. A bowl of milk, fresh, set out in an empty house. For a cat, she supposed. Except there was no cat, and the bowl was chipped in the shape of a crescent moon, and it was the kind of small stupid tender superstition that three hundred years of fire had failed to burn out of these people.

Voss picked it up. Considered it. Poured the milk slowly into the cold hearth, where it hissed and steamed among the ashes.

Outside, far off in the dark hills, something that was not thunder rolled twice across the valley, low and long, like a voice clearing its throat.

Voss stood very still, listening, her heart — for the first time in thirty years — uncertain.

Then she set the empty bowl down, upside down, and went to give her orders for dawn.
