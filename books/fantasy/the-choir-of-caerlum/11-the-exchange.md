## 11. The Exchange

The river at Hollow Ford ran high with snowmelt, brown and loud, and Dominic stood on the near bank with her hands empty and her heart full of arithmetic.

One life for one secret. That was the trade. Bramble for the altar's location — or so Voss believed. What the inquisitor didn't know, what Pellam's slip had never touched, was that the altar was already gone. They had spent two days hauling the hatchling-scale up through the smuggler's tunnels, wrapped in oilcloth and Osk's silence, and now it lay hidden in a collapsed mine shaft three miles north, under a cairn of rock that looked like every other cairn in Thornfell.

So the exchange was a lie wearing the shape of a bargain. Dominic had made enough of those to know how they went.

Voss stood on the far bank with a dozen soldiers and Bramble between them — small, gray-haired Bramble, wrists bound, chin up, mouth moving. Even at forty paces over rushing water, Dominic could read her lips. *Don't you dare.* The hedge-witch was telling her not to come.

"She's alive," Voss called across the water. Her voice carried strangely well, flat and clear as a blade laid on stone. "You have my word she stays that way until I have what I want."

"Your word," Dominic said. "From the woman who burned a shrine with a family inside it."

"They were warned." No heat in it. That was the worst thing about Serane Voss — there was never any heat. "Walk out where I can see you. Alone. Bring nothing."

Dominic stepped forward onto the shingle, arms wide, coat open to show she carried no steel. The wind off the river cut through her shirt. Behind her, half-hidden in the alder scrub, Osk waited with a crossbow he would not use unless everything failed, because everything failing was the only condition under which a bolt from hiding would buy anything at all.

He couldn't hear the negotiation. He'd told her once, drunk on winter mead, that deafness was a smuggler's gift — the world's noise meant nothing to him, and so neither did its lies. He read lips like other men read weather. He'd taught her half of what she knew about watching hands instead of mouths.

*Watch the sergeant,* he'd signed this morning, before they'd split. *Sergeants hate their officers. Watch his eyes when she gives the order.*

She watched. The sergeant stood behind Voss's left shoulder, a heavy man with a scarred jaw, and when Voss raised her hand to signal the release of the prisoner, his eyes flicked — just once, just slightly — toward the treeline on the far bank.

There were more soldiers in the trees. Of course there were. Voss had never intended an exchange. She'd intended a net.

* * *

Later, Dominic would try to reconstruct the next ninety seconds and fail. Memory gave it back to her in pieces, sharp-edged and out of order: Bramble breaking free of the soldier holding her — the man must have loosened his grip, some flicker of conscience or simple surprise — and running for the water. Voss's hand dropping. The sergeant shouting. Crossbows coming off shoulders in the alder scrub, slow by half a heartbeat, because soldiers drilled for orders and orders came before sight.

And Osk.

Osk stepping out of the scrub with his crossbow already leveled, not at Voss, not at the sergeant — at the waterline, at the stones beside Bramble's path, driving them back from the bank with two bolts that cracked against rock like the world snapping its fingers. Buying seconds. He was always buying seconds; it was what trackers did, what smugglers did, what the Ravensworths had done for six generations. You never won. You bought time for someone else to get away.

Bramble hit the water. The current took her sideways and she fought it, gray head bobbing, and Dominic plunged in from the near bank without deciding to, the cold hitting her chest like a door slamming. The river dragged them together somewhere past the middle. She got a fist in Bramble's collar and turned downstream, feet scraping bottom, and the far bank came at them fast and ugly.

Behind them, Osk reloaded. Dominic heard it even over the water — the double click of the lever, a sound she'd known since she was nine years old, since the day the guild found him begging at the Thornfell market and he'd followed Pellam home like a stray dog and never left. *Click-clack. Click-clack.* The most familiar music in her life.

Then a different sound. A wet, heavy sound, and the clicking stopped.

She didn't see him fall. She saw the alder scrub go still, and she saw Voss walk down to the waterline unhurried, boots dry, and stand over something Dominic couldn't see from the water, and she knew.

They came out on the south bank coughing, Bramble half-drowned and furious, and Dominic turned and looked back across the brown water at the figure standing in the shallows where Osk had been. He was face-down. His crossbow floated beside him, turning slowly, the lever still cocked. He had gotten both bolts off. He had bought them the river, and the river had done the rest, and it hadn't been enough for him.

"No," Bramble said, when Dominic started to turn back. She grabbed Dominic's arm with both hands, and her grip was stronger than a drowned woman's had any right to be. "No. Look at me. There are twelve of them and they have crossbows and he is *gone*, girl. He is gone, and if you swim back there you make it worth nothing."

"He bought us the crossing," Dominic said. Her voice came out wrong, flattened, like someone else's. "We have to make it worth something."

"Then make it worth something *that way*." Bramble pointed north, up the valley, toward the cairn, toward the scale, toward everything Osk had died moving. "Not into their arrows."

* * *

They ran. Pellam met them at the old kiln above the ford, armorless, ash-pale, and when he understood what had happened he sat down on the cold brick like a man whose legs had been taken away as taxes. He had traded the guildhall's secret to save Bramble's life, and Bramble lived, and Osk was dead, and the arithmetic of it sat on him visibly, a debt no confession could service.

"I'll carry the scale," he said finally. "I'll carry it to the roost myself. On my knees if I have to."

"You'll carry it," Dominic agreed, "because you're strong and guilty, which is the same thing today." She said it without cruelty. There wasn't room in her for cruelty just then. There wasn't room for anything except the next hour.

Because the next hour had a shape, and the shape was this: Voss had Osk's body, and Voss had the guildhall, and Voss had all the time in the world. And the guildhall —

The guildhall was still standing.

It sat in its fold of the hills like an old dog too loyal to die, smoke-stained beams and crooked chimneys, the cellar below it hollowed out over generations into rooms the tax rolls had never heard of. Every wall had a name scratched into it. Every beam had a notch where a child — where *she*, age seven, knife in her fist, mentor laughing — had cut a mark for a run completed. The cellar held the sealed door, the empty alcove where the altar had slept for three hundred years, the dust of the Ravensworths going back past memory.

Voss would find it. Voss would strip it beam by beam looking for what was no longer there, and when she found nothing she would burn it anyway, because burning was what she did, and she would do it slowly, and she would make it a lesson.

Dominic stood at the kiln with the wind pulling at her and understood, with terrible clarity, that there was exactly one thing left to decide: who burned the guildhall first.

* * *

They went in at dusk, the four of them — Dominic, Pellam, Bramble, and Ysolde, who had come down from the high pass when the news reached her and who moved through the tunnels like water finding its level. The soldiers were in the hall itself, maybe twenty of them, cooking supper, loud, comfortable, certain. Voss had left a garrison and taken Osk's body and most of her column south toward the town, where there were more shrines to burn and more names to collect.

Twenty men who thought the war was over.

The smugglers' way in was the chimney flue behind the old drying loft, a climb Dominic had done a hundred times with a pack on her back, and she did it now with a firepot strapped across her chest like a child, like a bomb, like a heart.

That was the part she hadn't told the others. Not plainly. She'd said *we go back for the records, the coin, the names* — true words, necessary words — and let them believe that was the whole of it. It was Bramble who understood, watching her strap the pot on. Bramble, who had known her since she was eleven and thieving apples, who had stitched her wounds and cursed her luck and never once asked her to be anyone but what she was.

"You're going to burn it yourself," Bramble whispered in the dark of the flue, breath hot against Dominic's ear. "Aren't you."

"The alternative is letting *her* do it."

"There are names on those walls. Six generations of names."

"I know what's on the walls." Dominic's voice caught and steadied. "I put half of them there."

Bramble was quiet a moment. Then: "Then I'll ward the doors so it burns clean and quick, and no soldier dies in it, and no soldier stops it. And afterward, when you want to hate yourself for it, you come find me first. That's the price."

"That's a fair price."

"It's an outrageous price. Agree anyway."

Dominic agreed.

* * *

It went the way good smuggling went — which is to say, badly right up until the moment it went perfectly. Pellam took the two men at the stable door with a hay hook and a lifetime of regretting things quietly. Ysolde moved through the kitchen like a rumor, and the soldiers there drank wine that would remember none of this. Bramble chalked her wards on the outer doors, quick sigils that would hold a panicked man for ten heartbeats, long enough for him to choose the window instead of the flame.

And Dominic climbed the drying loft, crossed the beam with her own childhood notches under her palm, and poured lamp-oil down the length of the great hall like a woman watering a garden she loved.

The names looked up at her from the walls. *Ravensworth. Corvane. Ash. Little Marta who died at nine. Old Tam who swam the Ford in flood and never told anyone why.* Six generations of people who had kept a god's dying flame warm while the world forgot gods existed, and called themselves criminals to hide it, and lied to their children, and died poor, and had been — she understood it fully now, standing in the oil-smell dark — the last priesthood in the world, wearing a smuggler's face.

"We were never thieves," she said aloud, to the walls, to the names. "We were keepers. I'm sorry it took me so long."

She struck the flint.

The oil caught with a soft whump, and fire ran down the hall faster than grief, and the smoke went up the chimney in a black column visible, she knew, from the pass road, from the ford, from Voss's camp, from everywhere. A beacon. A funeral pyre. A message written in the only language left to her: *you are too late, and what you wanted is ash.*

The soldiers spilled out the windows and doors coughing and singed and alive, because Bramble's wards had held and because Dominic had counted them out of the building first, and they stood in the yard watching the guildhall burn and did not understand what they were watching. One of them wept openly, though he couldn't have said why. Fire does that. It takes things from people they didn't know they were keeping.

Voss would come. Voss would stand in the yard where Osk's body had been laid out under a cloak, and she would look at the flames, and she would understand that Dominic Ravensworth had chosen the fire rather than surrender one more inch of it to her — and that understanding would change her tactics, though not her faith. Zealots learn. They don't bend.

Dominic stood in the yard until the roof beams fell, until the heat pushed everyone back to the gate, until the hall that had raised her was a red skeleton against the stars. Pellam put a blanket around her shoulders. Bramble stood on her other side and did not say *I told you so*, and did not say *come find me*, because the hour for that was later, and Bramble kept her prices.

Ysolde alone spoke. The old priestess watched the fire with tears cutting clean lines through the soot on her face, and she said, softly, in the voice of someone reciting:

"*What is given to the flame is not lost. It is sent ahead.*"

"Is that scripture?" Dominic asked.

"It's a promise." Ysolde turned to look at her, and in the firelight her eyes were the exact color of banked coals. "The guildhall is sent ahead. Osk is sent ahead. And so are you, Dominic Ravensworth, whether you know it yet or not. South. To the roost. To the flame. Everything you've burned tonight is waiting for you there."

Above the collapsing roof, sparks rose thick and bright, streaming north on the wind — away from the town, away from Voss, away from everything that had ever hurt them — up the valley toward the high passes, toward the mountain's heart, like a choir of small lights finally going home.

Dominic watched them go until her eyes burned, and then she shouldered her pack, and turned south, and led her dead and her living into the dark.
