## 12. Grief Is a Long Road

Bramble counted the living the way she counted her herbs: twice in the morning, twice at night, and once more whenever the wind changed.

Three. That was what remained of the Ravensworth guild. Three out of the forty-odd souls who had ever sworn over that scarred table in the guildhall — and one of the three was Ysolde, who had never sworn anything, who had simply arrived with a purse of singing coins and stayed until the fire took her holding the gate at Hollow Ford.

Bramble walked at the back of their little line because that was where a hedge-witch belonged. Ahead of her went Pellam, broad as a door and moving like one off its hinges, and ahead of him Dominic, who had not spoken more than twenty words since the guildhall burned, and who set a pace through the thawing valley as though grief were a river she could outrun.

The valleys were waking up around them. Snowmelt ran loud in every gully, and the alder thickets along the streambeds had gone the color of old honey, and somewhere above them on the south-facing slopes the first crocuses were pushing up through the last gray drifts. It should have been beautiful. Bramble supposed it was beautiful. She noticed it the way you notice weather in someone else's country — cataloguing it, filing it away, feeling nothing much about any of it.

Her hands hurt. They had been hurting for two days, ever since Hollow Ford, ever since she'd knelt in the mud beside Osk and done everything a hedge-witch knows how to do and watched all of it fail. Her hands remembered the work even when her mind refused to. She kept flexing them as she walked, working the ache out, and each flex brought back the weight of him, the terrible lightness of him at the end, as if he'd already been mostly gone before the arrow found him.

*Deaf tracker*, they'd called him, when they'd pulled him half-frozen out of a poacher's snare line eleven years ago. He hadn't been able to hear Bramble's thanks then, so she'd pressed a sprig of dried mint into his palm, and he'd smelled it, and smiled, and that had been the whole of his induction into the guild. No oaths. Just mint, and a place by the fire.

She wondered if anyone would press a sprig of anything into *her* palm, when her time came. Then she decided that was a morbid thought for a morning walk, and put it away, and failed to put it away, and carried it anyway.

They made camp that first night under an overhang of limestone where a shepherd had once kept lambs — there was still straw in the corner, still a ring of blackened stones. Pellam gathered wood without being asked. Dominic stood at the mouth of the overhang looking north, toward the pass they'd come down, toward the smoke that wasn't there anymore because there was nothing left to make it.

Bramble got the kettle going. Tea was the first thing. Tea was always the first thing; you could not mend a person who hadn't drunk something warm, and you could not mend a person at all, but you could start with tea and work outward from there.

"Dominic." She said it the way she said most things, plainly, the way you'd say *rain coming*. "Sit down and let me look at that arm."

"There's nothing wrong with my arm."

"There's a hole in your sleeve and I've been watching blood seep through it since midday. Sit down."

Dominic sat. That was the thing about her that Bramble had learned across years of shared fires and shared danger: she argued with everyone about everything except the people who loved her, and then only when she had the strength to spare. She didn't have the strength now. She lowered herself onto the straw with the careful stiffness of someone whose body had finally sent its bill, and held out her arm, and looked away while Bramble peeled back the sleeve.

The wound was a graze — a crossbow bolt had kissed the outside of her forearm during the escape, opening a long shallow furrow. It was dirty. Of course it was dirty. Everything about the last three days had been dirty.

"You let this go a day and a half," Bramble said, cleaning it with spirits from her kit. Dominic didn't flinch, which was its own kind of information. "Infection sets in and we're carrying you through the thaw-mud, and I'll tell you now, Pellam and I together couldn't lift you over a stile."

"Then leave me," Dominic said. Flat. Not dramatic. The worst possible way to say it.

"No," Bramble said, and kept working.

"I mean it. If it comes to choosing—"

"It won't come to choosing. Hold still." She threaded the needle. Her hands had stopped hurting, which happened sometimes when the work was real; her hands knew what they were for better than she did. "You want to talk about choosing? Fine. Let's talk about it. You chose to burn the guildhall rather than let Voss have it. You chose to send Osk up the wall path because he was the only one fast enough and quiet enough, and he agreed to go, and he went, because that's what Osk did — he did the hard quiet things nobody thanked him for. Those were choices. They were made in about four seconds apiece, in the dark, with soldiers shooting at us. And now you're going to spend the rest of this road re-judging them by lantern-light with all the time in the world, and I'm telling you that's not grief, Dominic. That's just cruelty with better manners."

Dominic looked at her then. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which meant she'd run out of tears somewhere on the road and her body had given up supplying them.

"He died for me," she said.

"He died *with* you. There's a difference, and it matters." Bramble tied off the last stitch and snipped the thread. "Osk spent eleven years in this guild. Do you know what he told me once, when we were waiting out a storm in the Hollows and he thought I was asleep? He wrote it in the dirt, actually, because talking tired him. He wrote: *before the guild, I was a hole shaped like a person.*" Her voice caught. She let it catch. There was no point pretending otherwise in front of these two; they'd earned her real voice. "You didn't take anything from him, girl. You gave him eleven years of being full-shaped. Most people don't get that in a whole life."

Dominic's jaw worked. She turned her face away, toward the dark mouth of the valley, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped.

Pellam came back with an armload of wood and built the fire up without a word, because Pellam understood, in the wordless way of big men who had once been knights and had learned to read a room like a battlefield, that some silences needed feeding and not breaking.

* * *

They walked south for six days.

The thaw deepened around them. Rivers that had been ice in winter came up brown and muscular out of their banks, and the low meadows flooded silver, and twice they had to detour miles around water that in another season would have been a ford. The war had passed through here too — they saw it in the burned-out tithe barn outside Marleford, in the shrine by the crossroads with its alcove smashed and its stones scattered, in the way the farmsteads stood empty with doors swinging. Voss's kind of work, or work in Voss's style, done by men who'd never met her and didn't need to. The world had plenty of teachers of that lesson.

Bramble foraged as they went. It steadied her, the old litany: nettle for the soup, coltsfoot for coughs, yarrow always yarrow everywhere if you knew where to kneel. She taught the other two as they walked, not because they needed teaching but because words about plants were easier than words about anything else, and because a moving mouth doesn't grind on the same stone all day.

On the third day she caught Dominic standing at the ruined crossroads shrine, picking up the scattered stones one by one and setting them back in the alcove. Not rebuilding it — there wasn't enough left to rebuild. Just... ordering it. Making it neat. Bramble stood back among the alders and watched her do it and felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a boat coming about.

Because that was it, wasn't it. That was the whole shape of the girl. Give Dominic Ravensworth a broken thing and she could not walk past it. A broken shrine. A broken deaf boy pulled out of a snare line. A broken disgraced knight drinking himself to death in a dockside tavern, whom she'd hired on the spot because he had honest hands. A broken guild, inherited at nineteen from a dead mentor, already bleeding members to the war and the inquisitions, already a name and a debt and nothing else — and she'd carried it five more years because no one else would.

And now the guild was ash and the boy was dead and the knight was walking ten paces ahead carrying guilt like a millstone, and the broken thing left in the road was Dominic herself, and nobody had taught her what to do with that, because she'd always been the one holding the needle.

Bramble came forward and knelt beside her and picked up a stone and set it in the alcove.

Together, without speaking, they finished it.

* * *

On the fifth night, Pellam broke.

Bramble heard it coming the way she heard weather coming — a change in the pressure of a person. He'd been quieter than usual, which for Pellam was saying something, and he'd taken the second watch every night without being asked, and he'd stopped eating more than half of what she gave him. Guilt has a smell. Hedge-witches know it. It smells like a man who keeps apologizing with labor.

He waited until Dominic was asleep — truly asleep, finally, the first real sleep any of them had managed — and then he sat down across the fire from Bramble and put his head in his enormous hands.

"I keep seeing it," he said.

"I know."

"At the ford. When they had Bramble—" He stopped. Started again. "When they had *you*. Knife at your throat, and Serane Voss asking her questions in that voice, that reasonable voice, like she was discussing grain prices. And she said, tell me where the tunnel lets out or we open her here, and I believed her. Gods forgive me, I believed her completely. So I told her. One sentence. One sentence and Osk is dead and the hall is ash."

Bramble poured him tea and pushed it into his hands and made him hold it.

"Pellam. Look at me." He looked. His eyes were wet and wrecked. "I was there. I had the knife at my throat, remember? My view of the arithmetic was better than yours. Here's what it was: talk, and maybe they live; don't talk, and I die for certain and they probably die after, because Voss had forty men and we had four. You did the sums right. Any soldier worth his spurs does those sums in a heartbeat and doesn't lose sleep."

"But Osk—"

"Osk would have done the same sums for you. For any of us. Faster." She leaned forward. "Here's what you're actually doing, knight. You're not grieving. Grief says *I miss him*. What you're saying is *I am bad*, over and over, because being bad feels more useful than being sad. Being bad comes with a penance. You can starve yourself, stand extra watches, march till your feet bleed. Sadness just hurts and asks for nothing. You'd rather do penance than hurt. We all would. But penance is just pride wearing a hair shirt, and I'll thank you to take it off, because it's making you useless and thin and I need you eating."

A long silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere down the valley a fox screamed, high and lonely, and fell silent.

"What do I do instead?" he asked, and it was the smallest she'd ever heard him.

"You carry him," Bramble said. "That's all anybody gets. You carry him south, and you carry him to this roost Ysolde told us about, and you carry him the rest of your life, and some days it will be heavy and some days it will be almost nothing, and neither one means anything about how much you loved him. That's the whole rite. There isn't a better one. Believe me, I've looked."

Pellam drank his tea. After a while he ate the bread she handed him, all of it. Before he took his watch he paused at the edge of the firelight and said, "Ysolde used to say the old priests had rites for everything. Birth, death, planting, war." His voice roughened. "Maybe when we get where we're going, there'll be one for this."

"Maybe," Bramble said. "And if there isn't, we'll make one up. That's what hedge-witches are for."

* * *

On the sixth day, the valley narrowed, and the world changed.

It happened gradually, the way important things do. First the farms stopped altogether. Then the road gave out into a track, and the track into a game trail, and the alders gave way to pines, dark and enormous, trees that had never known an axe. The air cooled and sweetened. Snow lingered in the shadows in ribbons that never melted, summer or winter. And beneath it all — Bramble felt it before she heard it, in her back teeth, in the soles of her feet — a hum. Faint. Rhythmic. Like a song heard through a mountain's sleep.

Dominic stopped on the trail. She had her hand pressed flat against a pine trunk, and her eyes were closed, and the coins in her pack — Ysolde's coins, the ones that sang — were audible now even to Bramble, a thin bright chiming rising to meet the deeper note under the earth.

"Do you feel that?" Dominic whispered.

"I feel it," Bramble said. "Been feeling it since noon. Mountain's humming, girl. Or something in it."

"The roost." Dominic opened her eyes, and Bramble saw something in them she hadn't seen since the guildhall burned — not hope exactly, hope was too small a word, but the *shape* where hope had been torn out and might grow back. "Ysolde said the canyon opens past the ridge. She said—" Dominic's voice caught on the name, the way it still caught, the way it would for years. "She said the keeper would know the way by the song."

"Then let's hope you're the keeper," Bramble said, "because I've been humming wrong notes my whole life."

But as they climbed toward the ridge, Bramble hung back again, at the rear, where a hedge-witch belonged, and she looked at the three of them strung out along the trail — the knight straightening his spine as though approaching a king, the heir of smugglers walking toward a god with blood on her sleeve and stitches in her arm — and she thought about Osk, and about Ysolde, and about the strange arithmetic by which a snared boy and a stranger with singing coins had become family, and how family apparently didn't end when it burned.

The hum grew louder. Beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, Bramble heard something else — a second voice in the song, answering the first, from somewhere far ahead in the dark of the canyon.

Something down there was still alive. And something down there was waiting for them.
