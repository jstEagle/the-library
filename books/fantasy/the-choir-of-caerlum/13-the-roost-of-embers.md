## 13. The Roost of Embers

Three days south of Hollow Ford, the land stopped pretending to be kind.

The thaw that had carried them through the valleys gave way to a high, dry country of black stone and wind-scoured pine, where the snow lay in patches like old scars and nothing grew taller than a man's knee. Dominic led them along a smuggler's route so old it had no name — just a line in her grandmother's journal, annotated in three different hands across two hundred years: *the low road, past the split crag, when the mountain allows.*

The mountain allowed. She felt it the way you feel a door unlatch — not opening, exactly. Just no longer closed.

Bramble noticed first. The hedge-witch had gone quiet for an hour, her usual muttering of wards reduced to a low hum, and then she said, without looking up from the trail: "It's listening."

"Don't say things like that," Pellam said.

"It's a compliment," Bramble said. "Most places don't bother."

Osk would have signed something dry about it — *the mountain has better manners than you* — and Dominic's chest did its familiar stutter at the thought of him, the grief arriving on schedule now, less a wound than weather. He'd died so they could carry this. Whatever *this* was. Ysolde's secret, wrapped in oilcloth against Bramble's back: a shard of scale the size of a dinner plate, dull as river slate, warm as a sleeping child.

They walked. The country climbed. And on the third evening, as the light went copper and long, Dominic crested a rise and saw the canyon.

She had expected — she didn't know what she'd expected. A cave. A shrine. Something built, something with walls and intention. What she saw was a wound in the earth a half-mile wide, sheer-walled, running down into shadow, and filling that shadow from rim to rim —

Eggs.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one tall as a man or taller, each one stone. They stood in rows, in drifts, in fallen towers, packed into the canyon floor like a city of the dead built by something that thought in geological time. Petrified. Gray and gold-veined, some cracked, some whole, all of them silent in the way that only very old silence is silent.

"Saints' teeth," Pellam breathed beside her. The disgraced knight had his hand on his sword hilt out of pure reflex, and then, slowly, deliberately, took it off again. "This is a graveyard."

"No," Ysolde said. Her voice had changed since the ford — steadier, sadder, like a woman walking back into a house she'd been exiled from. "It's a nursery. Or it was. Look at the arrangement. Eggs don't fall in rows, knight. Someone *placed* them."

Dominic looked, and Ysolde was right. There was order here, deep and strange — spirals, avenues, a geometry that hurt to follow for too long, as if the minds that made it counted differently than people did. At the canyon's heart, where the rows converged, there was a hollow, and in the hollow, something moved.

Not much. But it moved.

"Down," Dominic said. "Slowly. We're guests until we're certain we're not."

* * *

The descent took the last hour of light. The trail was old steps cut into the canyon wall, worn smooth, sized wrong — too deep, too far apart, made for legs longer than any human leg. Dominic kept her grandmother's journal open in her mind, turning pages that weren't there. *The Ravensworths served the Choir.* *The flame must be tended.* *When the last fire gutters, the last keeper carries it home.* Home. This was what the old woman had meant. Not a guildhall with a sealed cellar door. This.

The air changed as they went down. Warmer. Dry, mineral, faintly sulfurous, and underneath it something else — a smell Dominic knew from her childhood, from the cellar beneath the guildhall, from the night she'd pressed her palm to the altar-door and it had known her blood. Hot stone and old smoke. The smell of a banked fire.

At the bottom, among the petrified eggs, the warmth was nearly comfortable. Bramble pulled off a glove and pressed her bare hand to one of the great stones and snatched it back, startled. "Warm. It's *warm*. These eggs have been dead for three hundred years and they're still warm."

"Not dead," Ysolde said quietly. "Waiting. There's a difference, witch. You of all people should know it."

Dominic didn't answer. She was watching the hollow ahead, where the last row of eggs opened onto a floor of black glass — fused sand, she realized, vitrified by heat beyond anything she could imagine — and lying on that glass, folded up small around herself like a hill trying to be a stone, was the dragon.

Vaelith.

Ysolde had spoken the name once, on the road, and refused to speak it again, as if names spent like coin. Now Dominic saw her and understood why. The dragon was vast — even diminished, even starved down to scaffolding and skin, her wings folded like rotten sails, her neck laid along the ground — she was vast in a way that made the canyon make sense. The canyon wasn't big. Vaelith was simply shaped for something bigger, and the world had shrunk around her.

Her scales were the gray of ash. Her eyes were closed. And rising and falling under those scales was breath — slow, so slow Dominic counted forty of her own before the dragon's ribs lifted once — but breath. Barely alive. A hearth-fire down to its last coal, one sneeze of ash from gone.

Ysolde walked forward alone, and knelt, and did something with her hands — a gesture, old and formal, fingers interlaced in a pattern Dominic had never seen — and spoke words in a language that sounded like stones agreeing with each other.

Nothing happened.

Ysolde knelt there a long time. Then her shoulders shook once, and she rose, and turned back to them, and her face was terrible with age all of a sudden, all her composure burned away. "She sleeps too deep. Three hundred years of listening to nothing." Her voice cracked. "I was afraid of this. I came hoping — I'm the last of my order, and I came hoping she'd know me, and she doesn't. She may be past knowing anyone."

"She knows someone," Bramble said.

Everyone looked at her. The hedge-witch was standing with her head cocked, listening to something, the way she listened to storms before they broke.

"There's a song," she said. "Under everything. Under the rock. Been getting louder since we crossed the ridge. I thought it was the place." She looked at Dominic, and her eyes were wide and frightened and certain all at once. "It's not the place. It's her. She's singing in her sleep, Dom. Has been the whole way down. And it got louder the moment you stepped off the last stair."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a petrified egg the size of a chapel, close enough to the dragon to see the steam of her breath ghosting in the cold air, far enough — Pellam insisted, and Dominic agreed — that if she woke badly, they might run. Supper was hard bread and dried apples and water from a spring that ran warm out of the canyon wall. Nobody talked much. The place didn't allow it. The silence here wasn't empty; it was full, the way a held note is full, and speaking into it felt like talking during a mass.

Dominic couldn't sleep. She sat up with her back against the egg-stone, watching the dark shape of the dragon, feeling the warmth come off the black glass floor through her boots. Her grandmother's journal lay open on her knees — the real one, the paper one, the thing she'd carried since Thornfell — and she read the same line over and over:

*The Choir does not ask for voices. It asks for keepers. A keeper holds the song when no one else will listen.*

"What are you afraid of?" Pellam asked quietly. He'd taken the watch; he sat across the little fire with his sword across his knees, sharpening it though it didn't need sharpening. It was how he prayed, Dominic had learned. To something. She'd never asked what.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "That's the worst part. I've been afraid of specific things my whole life. The excise men. Winter closing early. Mentor dying — that one came true." She turned the page. "This is just... standing at the edge of something and knowing it's bigger than me and not knowing whether it wants to eat me or claim me."

"Could be both," Pellam said.

"Could be both," she agreed.

Across the fire, Bramble slept sitting up, which she claimed was a warding technique and everyone else claimed was nonsense. Ysolde slept actually, deeply, for the first time since Dominic had met her — the sleep of a woman who had finally arrived somewhere, even if the arrival was a graveyard.

And Dominic sat, and watched, and waited, because waiting was the one skill the guild had given her that mattered tonight.

Sometime past midnight, the dragon opened one eye.

There was no dramatics to it. No roar, no stirring of the vast body. Just an eye, opening, amber and ancient and slit-pupiled, catching the light of their small fire from eighty yards away, and fixing on Dominic with an attention that felt like being weighed on scales older than kingdoms.

Dominic froze. Every instinct the guild had ever taught her said *don't move, don't run, don't meet the eyes of something that can kill you* — and every instinct was wrong, because the eye wasn't hunting. It was *reading*. She felt read. Turned over like a coin, tested between finger and thumb, bitten for gold.

Then the voice came. Not through the air. Through the stone, through the floor, up through her boots and spine and teeth — a sound like a landslide deciding to be gentle.

**WHO CARRIES THE SONG?**

Dominic's mouth was dry. "I — my name is Dominic Ravensworth. My family kept—"

**I DID NOT ASK YOUR NAME.** The word landed like a dropped boulder. **NAMES ARE WHAT HUMANS HIDE BEHIND. I ASKED WHO CARRIES THE SONG.**

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know the song."

Something moved behind the amber eye. Not anger. Something worse. Recognition.

**NO,** Vaelith said, and the grief in it was enormous, room-filling, canyon-filling. **YOU DO NOT KNOW IT. BUT YOU HEARD IT WHEN YOU WERE SMALL ENOUGH TO STILL HEAR SUCH THINGS. YOUR GRANDMOTHER SANG IT OVER YOU. I CAN TASTE IT IN YOU LIKE SMOKE IN WOOL. SHE HELD THE LINE WHEN HER GENERATION FORGOT. AND NOW THE LINE HAS COME DOWN TO A SMUGGLER SITTING ON A STONE EGG IN THE DARK, AND THE LAST DRAGON IS DYING OF QUIET.**

"I'm sorry," Dominic said, and meant it more than she'd ever meant anything, and heard how small it was.

**SORRY,** Vaelith said. **YES. YOUR KIND INVENTED THAT WORD, I BELIEVE. VERY USEFUL. VERY CHEAP.** The eye blinked, slow as a season changing. **I AM GOING TO GIVE YOU SOMETHING, LITTLE KEEPER. NOT A TEST. TESTS ARE FOR THOSE WHO MIGHT FAIL. THIS IS A MEASURE. HOLD WHAT I GIVE YOU, AND WE WILL KNOW WHAT YOU ARE.**

And the dragon sang.

* * *

It was not like anything. Dominic would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it, and she was a novelist, description was supposed to be her trade, and she failed anyway. It began below hearing, in the stone, in her bones, a vibration like the canyon clearing its throat. Then it rose — layered, braided, a chord made of a hundred voices that were somehow one voice, and every voice was the memory of a dragon. She could *hear* them: the ones who had flown, the ones who had burned, the ones whose bodies were these eggs, the ones who had chosen silence three hundred years ago and meant it and regretted it and meant it again. The song was their whole history sung at once — covenant and betrayal, flight and fire, the long despairing quiet — and it did not stop or resolve. It simply went on, verse after verse, and each verse was heavier than the last.

*Hold it,* the dragon had said. So Dominic held it.

It was like holding water in cupped hands while standing in a flood. The verses piled up. Her skull ached. Her heart tried to keep pace with a rhythm no human heart was built for and stumbled, and kept stumbling, and she gripped the journal against her chest and dug her nails into her own palms and *held*, because her grandmother had held, because Osk had died so someone could stand here and hold, because if she let go now she would never forgive herself and worse, the dragons would be right about them forever.

Verse after verse. She lost count. Time went strange; the fire burned down; Pellam was on his feet with his sword drawn and Bramble was awake and gripping her shoulder, saying her name, saying it louder, and Dominic heard all of it from very far away, underwater, inside the song.

And then — she didn't know how, she would never be able to explain it — she found the place where the next verse should go, the empty space in the chord, the rest in the music, the gap left three hundred years ago when the human voices dropped out. And she sang into it.

Not well. Not loudly. Her voice cracked and wavered and she had no words, none, she didn't know the language — she sang the shape of it, the *willingness* of it, one human throat doing its small best to fill a gap the size of a civilization. One note. Off-key. Held.

The song stopped.

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind had quit. Even the canyon seemed to lean in.

Vaelith's eye was open, both eyes now, fixed on her, and the amber in them had shifted — warmed — toward something like gold, toward something like dawn.

**OH,** the dragon said, very softly, and the softness was the loudest thing Dominic had ever heard. **OH, LITTLE ONE. THERE YOU ARE.**

Dominic was crying. She hadn't noticed starting. "I don't know what I did."

**YOU ANSWERED. THREE HUNDRED YEARS, AND SOMEONE FINALLY ANSWERED.** The great head shifted, scales whispering against the glass floor like a mountain turning over in bed. **IT IS NOT ENOUGH. UNDERSTAND THAT FIRST. ONE VOICE DOES NOT UNMAKE A SILENCE. MY FIRE IS ALMOST OUT. MY BODY IS ALMOST DONE. AND THE WORLD ABOVE US HAS FORGOTTEN US SO COMPLETELY THAT ITS KINGS SEND SOLDIERS TO BURN OUR SHRINES RATHER THAN KNEEL AT THEM.** The eye narrowed. **AND ONE OF THOSE SOLDIERS IS COMING HERE. I HAVE FELT HER BOOTS ON THE ROAD FOR SIX DAYS. A WOMAN WITH A STRONG FAITH AND A WEAK IMAGINATION, BRINGING FIRE TO PUT OUT FIRE.**

Pellam went rigid. "Voss. She followed us?"

**SHE WAS ALWAYS FOLLOWING YOU. YOU SIMPLY KEPT MISTAKING THE DIRECTION OF THE CHASE.** Vaelith's gaze returned to Dominic, and gentled, and aged, all at once. **SO. HERE IS WHERE WE FIND OURSELVES, KEEPER. YOU HAVE HELD THE SONG. YOU HAVE ANSWERED IT. AND NOW YOU MUST DECIDE WHAT AN ANSWER IS WORTH, BECAUSE I AM TOO OLD TO TEACH YOU SLOWLY, AND SHE IS TOO CLOSE TO LET US TRY.**

The dragon settled her chin back onto the black glass. The amber eyes stayed open, stayed on Dominic, patient as geology.

**REST IF YOU CAN,** Vaelith said. **TOMORROW I TEACH YOU THE VERSES. ALL OF THEM. INCLUDING THE LAST ONE.**

**AND THE LAST ONE,** she added, almost as an afterthought, almost kindly, **IS THE ONE THAT COSTS.**
