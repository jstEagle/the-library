## 14. A Secret Too Heavy

Pellam had spent twenty years learning how to stand still while other people talked. It was a knight's skill, mostly — the art of holding a wall of your body between a threat and someone softer than you, and doing it without letting your face tell anyone what you were thinking. He'd been good at it once. He was good at it now, standing in the mouth of Vaelith's canyon with his arms crossed and his sword wrapped in oilcloth because a dragon was sleeping forty paces away and even he wasn't fool enough to bring steel into her presence.

He was good at it right up until the moment the dragon spoke to *him*.

**YOU. THE BROKEN ONE.**

The voice arrived inside his skull like a hand closing around his heart. Not painful. Worse — gentle. Vaelith's attention settled on him the way snow settles on a branch, soft and total and heavy enough to snap things.

"I'm not broken," Pellam said, before he could stop himself.

**NO,** the dragon agreed. **THAT IS THE LIE YOU TELL. COME. WALK WITH ME IN YOUR MIND A WHILE. I HAVE QUESTIONS, AND DOMINIC SLEEPS, AND I AM OLD AND BORED AND CRUEL IN MY OLD AGE.**

"I don't answer to dragons."

**YOU ANSWER TO HER, THOUGH. THAT IS ENOUGH.**

And then the canyon was gone.

* * *

Not gone, exactly. His body still stood at the canyon mouth; some distant part of him felt the cold stone under his boots, heard Bramble's snores from the fire-pit, smelled the mineral stink of petrified eggs. But another part of him walked in a place that was all ember-light and long shadows, and Vaelith was there — not the vast dying shape on the black glass but something smaller, older, a dragon the size of a warhorse with wings folded like a mourner's hands.

**I will show you what Dominic cannot yet see,** she said, and now her voice was ordinary-sized, almost tired. **Because you are the one who carries shields for other people, and a shield should know what is coming.**

"I don't want your secrets."

**No one ever does. That has never once stopped me.**

She turned, and the ember-light bent around her, and Pellam saw the altar.

It stood at the far end of the canyon, past the field of stone eggs — a bowl of black glass the size of a hearth, ringed by seven pillars carved with verses in no alphabet he knew. The glass was cold. He knew it was cold the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without proof. Cold as a dead man's chest.

**Three hundred years ago,** Vaelith said, **the last keeper lit this flame with herself. Her name was Ravensworth too. Do you understand what I am telling you?**

"No."

**Yes you do. You are slow, knight, but you are not stupid. The rite does not take blood the way a wound takes blood. It takes a life *given*. Freely. Willingly. With the whole weight of the giver behind it. And it must be a life of the line — the blood that made the covenant, the blood that broke it. No other fuel burns hot enough.**

Pellam's mouth was very dry. "Dominic."

**Dominic.** The dragon said the name the way you'd set down something fragile. **Tomorrow I teach her the last verse, and the verse itself will tell her, because the song is honest even when its singers are cowards. She will know within a day. And then she will do it, because she is the kind of person who walks into fires for found family, and you love her for exactly that, and it will kill her.**

"There has to be another way."

**There is not. There never was. That is what a covenant means — the debt comes due in the coin agreed upon.** Vaelith's amber eyes held him. **But.**

"But?"

**The rite requires a life of the line,*given freely.* It does not require that the giver be the last of the line. Only that the giving be true.** The dragon's tail moved through the shadow like a slow thought. **You are not of her blood, broken one. Your offering would gutter and fail. I am sorry. I show you this not to torment you but because I have watched men like you for three hundred years, and I know what you will try, and I would rather you tried it knowing.**

"Then why—"

**Because knowing, you may find the thing I cannot see. Three hundred years I have slept beside a cold altar, and I have missed something once or twice. Perhaps love counts for more than lineage. Perhaps the god is kinder than the law. I do not know. But you deserve to look for the crack yourself rather than have me tell you there is none.**

The dream-canyon dimmed. The last thing Pellam saw was the altar, cold and patient, waiting for a Ravensworth.

Then he was standing at the canyon mouth with frost on his shoulders and dawn maybe an hour off, and Bramble was shaking him by the arm saying his name for the third time.

* * *

"Pellam. *Pellam.* You went somewhere. Your eyes went somewhere." Bramble's face was grey in the coals' light, hedge-witch sharp despite the hour. "What did she say to you?"

"Nothing."

"Liar. Dragons don't pull people out of their skins for nothing." She gripped his wrist harder. "Is it Dominic? Is it about tomorrow?"

"It's about tomorrow," he said, and it was even true, and it told her nothing, and he watched her decide — visibly, the way she decided everything — that she'd get it out of him later or get it out of the dragon, whichever bit first.

He sat by the fire and did not sleep.

That was the arithmetic, then. That was the sum Voss had taught him at Hollow Ford and the world kept rechecking: everything costs, someone always pays, and the only choice you ever really get is who. He'd paid once already in the worst currency a man can spend — another person's safety — and he'd sworn, kneeling in the mud with Osk's blood still on the story of it, that he would never again buy a thing with someone else's life.

And here was the bill come round again. Dominic. Freely given. The only coin the altar accepted.

Unless.

He turned it over and over through the dead hours. *A life of the line.* Fine. But lines were not made of blood alone — Ysolde had said so, back before the gate, before the arrows: *a guild is a line, a vow is a line, we are all of us joined by what we carry.* And what had the guild carried, all those generations? The secret. The tending. The Ravensworths had been keepers, yes, but they hadn't been keepers *alone*, had they? Someone had hauled the coal. Someone had stood watch. Someone had died in the passes keeping the road open for the shrine they weren't allowed to name.

Osk would have done it. Osk, who owed the guild everything and paid it daily without being asked. If Osk were alive—

But Osk was dead, and that was the whole lesson of Osk, wasn't it. The substitute always costs more than the original.

Still. Still. He was a knight. Sworn, once, in a real church with real words, before he'd disgraced every one of them. *My life for theirs.* He'd meant it then. He'd never stopped meaning it. Maybe the god didn't care about blood. Maybe the god cared about the giving — and nobody alive gave the way a man gives who has nothing left he wants to keep.

"You're going to do something stupid," Bramble said quietly, from across the fire. She wasn't asleep either. Neither of them had mentioned it.

"Yes," Pellam said.

"Good," she said. "Tell me first, this time. I'm tired of burying people who acted alone."

He looked at her across the coals and, for the second time in his life, lied to a friend. "Nothing stupid," he said. "Go to sleep, Bramble."

* * *

Morning came up gold and merciless, and Vaelith taught.

Pellam stood where he'd stood the day before, at the canyon mouth, and watched Dominic learn the choir-song. She learned fast — faster than the dragon expected, faster than Pellam liked, because every verse she mastered was a step closer to the last one. By midday her voice had found harmonics that made the petrified eggs hum faintly in sympathy, a sound like a struck bell remembered rather than heard. By late afternoon Vaelith had her singing the verses of waking, of tending, of the long silence, and each one landed in Pellam's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Bramble worked wards at the canyon's narrow gate, glancing back at him every few minutes. Osk's absence stood among them like a fourth companion. Somewhere south, Voss was marching with fresh soldiers and a faith like a whetstone.

In the evening, Dominic came to him. She was grey-faced and shining-eyed, the way she got after hard roads, and she sat down next to him on the ledge overlooking the egg-field without asking, the way family sits.

"She says the last verse needs a keeper's whole self," Dominic said. "Not just the words. Everything you are, poured into the flame." She laughed, short and unsteady. "I keep thinking it means I'll lose my voice. Or my memories. Bramble thinks it's a metaphor. Do you think it's a metaphor, Pellam?"

He looked at her — at the smuggler's calluses, the guild-tattoo half-faded on her wrist, the stubborn line of her jaw that was the closest thing he had left to a country — and he said:

"I think you should rest. Big day tomorrow."

She bumped her shoulder against his. "You're a terrible liar for a man who used to serve a liege."

"Worse every year."

She went down to the fire. Pellam stayed on the ledge until full dark, and then he climbed, alone, past the egg-field, to the seven pillars and the cold black bowl of the altar.

Up close it hummed. Faintly. The way the coins had hummed, Ysolde's coins, the first note of a song waiting for its singer. He put his palm flat on the glass and it was cold, cold as Hollow Ford, cold as the river he'd bought Bramble's life with.

"All right," he said quietly, to the god inside the mountain, to the dragon behind him, to whatever kept the books. "I know what it costs. I know it isn't mine to pay. But I've read contracts before, old man, and there's always a clause. There's always a door somebody forgot to lock."

The glass under his hand warmed. One degree. Maybe less. Maybe only his blood lying to him.

"So here's my offer," Pellam said. "Not instead of her. *Before* her. Tomorrow, when she sings the last verse, I step into the circle first. I give what I have — the oath, the guilt, the twenty years of standing between. If the line is vows and not veins, then I'm the most Ravensworth man here, because I chose them. Every one of them. Freely."

The hum deepened. Somewhere below, faint as memory, the choir-song turned over in its sleep.

And high above the canyon rim, on the road south, torches bloomed — one, then ten, then a hundred — a river of fire descending through the dark toward the roost, and the sound that came with them was hymns.

Voss had found them.

Pellam took his hand off the altar and reached for his sword.
