## 15. Voss Ascendant

The reinforcements came up from Corvane in three columns, two hundred men in slate-grey, and Serane Voss watched them crest the pass at dawn with the particular satisfaction of a woman watching a debt get paid.

Not to her. To the world.

She stood on the ridge above Hollow Ford with her hands folded behind her back and let the cold work its way through her coat. Below, the columns wound along the frozen river like a dark vein, banners snapping — not the gaudy heraldry of the crown but the plain grey field of the Inquisition's Office of Inquiry, marked only with the closed eye. She had requested no trumpets. Trumpets were for parades. This was surgery.

"Two hundred and six, Prelate," said Lieutenant Cassel, riding up through the frost-crusted grass. "Plus your original detachment. The quartermaster wants to know how long we're provisioning for."

"Until it's done."

"That's not an answer I can feed two hundred men on, Prelate."

Voss turned and looked at him. Cassel was thirty, competent, and had the particular weariness of a soldier who had spent too many winters burning things that screamed. She did not hold it against him. Fear was honest. What she could not abide was the other thing — the smirk, the shrug, the *gods are stories, Prelate* — and Cassel had never once given her that. He gave her his fear instead, and she accepted it as a form of respect.

"Provision for forty days," she said. "We won't need twenty."

"And if the dragon—"

"There is no *if*, Lieutenant." She said it without heat, which was how she said most important things. "There is one dying animal in a hole in the mountains, and a handful of smugglers who have confused sentiment for faith. We are going to put it down before it dies on its own and rots into something worse."

Cassel saluted and rode down to make the quartermaster miserable. Voss stayed on the ridge a moment longer, looking north, toward Thornfell, toward the high country where the passes narrowed to knife-cuts and the old road ran black under the ice.

Somewhere up there was a girl named Ravensworth.

* * *

People misunderstood her. That was fine; she had stopped needing to be understood around the time she'd stopped needing much of anything.

They thought she hated the old gods out of ambition, or grief, or some wound the Church had dressed for her once. The ambitious ones assumed she wanted a bishopric. The grieving ones assumed she'd lost children to a fever and blamed the shrines where the desperate had prayed instead of the physicians who'd failed. Both stories were comfortable. Both were wrong.

Serane Voss believed because she had done the arithmetic.

She had been born in the Fenmark lowlands, third daughter of a eel-fisher, in a village that kept its old shrine even after the Edict of Severance, because the reeve's mother remembered when the marsh-god had supposedly kept the dikes whole. Every spring they left eels on the altar stone. Every autumn the dikes held or didn't, according to weather and engineering and nothing else. And every few years the marsh-fever came, as fevers do in wet country, and the villagers carried their children to the shrine and laid them on the altar stone beside the rotting offerings, and prayed to a god who had never once answered, while the fever burned through them like fire through dry reeds.

Voss had buried two sisters by her tenth year. Her father had kept leaving eels.

That was the whole of it. No revelation, no burning bush, no voice in the night. Just a child standing in a muddy churchyard doing sums: *the god receives the offering; the child dies anyway; therefore the god either does not exist, or exists and does not care, and both of those are the same problem.* And then the second sum, the one nobody in the village would do because it cost too much: *every hour spent praying is an hour not spent digging drainage; every coin spent on offerings is a coin not spent on physicians; every hope pinned to the altar stone is a hope not spent on the living.*

The old gods weren't evil. That was the terrible part. They were simply *dead weight* — a debt the living paid to the dead, generation after generation, in blood and grain and children, for nothing. A mortgage held by corpses.

And now, this year, the corpse had started twitching.

She had seen the reports. Everyone in the Office had. Shrines warming where they'd been cold for centuries. Coins surfacing from hoards that hummed when touched. In the eastern valleys, a well that had run sweet again overnight; in the south, a child said to have spoken a word of the Old Tongue in her sleep. Small things. Tremors. But tremors were how avalanches introduced themselves, and Voss had read enough history to know what happened last time the gods woke: the Sundering Wars, sixty years of fire, a third of the continent unmade, and at the end of it the Edict and the Office and three hundred years of hard-won quiet.

The quiet was the achievement. Not of kings — kings had signed whatever was put in front of them — but of the people who had done the actual work of putting the old world down. Her predecessors. Her order. People who had understood that mercy sometimes looks like a hammer.

And now the plague.

It had come up the river valleys in late autumn, the grey flux, killing in four days, and it moved fastest through exactly the villages that still kept shrines — because the sick went to the altars first, and the altars were warm now, warmer than they should be, and warmth bred contagion as surely as anything. She had walked through one such village outside Corvane. Forty-one dead in a hamlet of ninety. The survivors had been found huddled around the shrine, still praying, too weak to flee, and the look they gave her when she came with soldiers and lime was not gratitude. It was the look of people whose god had finally, *finally* begun to stir — and who could not understand why the woman in grey had come to snuff it out.

She had ordered the shrine burned anyway. She had stood and watched it burn, and she had felt no triumph, only the flat clean feeling of a surgeon closing.

Because here was the truth she carried, the truth that made the marches bearable: the gods waking was not salvation. It was infection. Whatever was stirring under Caerlum's mountain was reaching out through the old shrines like roots through rotten soil, and everything it touched warmed, and everything that warmed drew the desperate in, and the desperate died of the flux in circles around the very altars they thought were saving them. Coincidence? Perhaps. She was honest enough to allow perhaps. But she had learned long ago that when you could not afford to be wrong, you acted on the crueler reading, and asked forgiveness of no one, because the dead could not grant it and the living wouldn't.

Destroy the last dragon, and the tremors stop. Cut the root, kill the warmth, end the flux's favorite breeding ground and the idolaters' favorite hope in the same stroke. Two hundred men, one mountain, one dying animal.

Arithmetic. She was good at arithmetic.

* * *

They made camp the second night in the ruins of a waystation below the guildhall turn, and Voss walked the perimeter out of habit more than need, boots crunching on old cinders.

The guildhall itself was visible on its bluff a mile off, a black skeleton against the stars. Her men had burned it eleven days ago — after the prisoner exchange, after the tracker Osk had died in the mud buying the Ravensworth girl her escape, after the girl herself had put the torch to her own inheritance rather than let the Office have it. Voss had watched that fire from across the valley. Most commanders would have called it madness, destroying your own stronghold. Voss had called it what it was: a funeral. The girl had known the guild was already dead and had chosen to bury it herself rather than let strangers dig up the grave.

There was something almost admirable in it. Voss noted the admiration and set it aside like a tool she would not be using.

"Prelate." Brother Anselm, her chaplain-clerk, found her at the perimeter with a lantern and a ledger. Young, earnest, the sort of face that still flinched at executions. "The interrogation reports. From the ford. You wanted them summarized."

"The knight."

"Pellam, yes." Anselm turned pages. "He confirmed the girl's route south. The Ember Canyon, the petrified roost. He also claims—" Anselm hesitated.

"Claims are inventory, Brother. Read them."

"He claims the rite requires a Ravensworth life. Freely given." Anselm lowered the ledger. "Prelate, if that's true, the girl means to die up there. Doesn't that change the calculus? If she offers herself—"

"Then we arrive before she finishes, and we save her the trouble." Voss took the ledger and skimmed it by lantern-light. "A death rite is still a rite, Anselm. Interruptible like any other. And note what he told us and what he didn't — he traded Bramble's life for ours, and he kept the rest back until we had him alone. Guilt makes men talkative in stages. Expect there's more he's sitting on."

"You don't think he lied?"

"I think frightened men lie in the direction of their hopes. His hope is that we'll believe he's bought absolution cheaply." She handed the ledger back. "But the canyon detail rings true. Smugglers know the ground, and the ground doesn't lie. We march on the canyon."

Anselm lingered. He always lingered; it was his one flaw as a clerk. "May I ask something, Prelate? You've never spoken of why. Your conviction. The others say—"

"The others speculate," Voss said. "It keeps them warm."

"They say you hate the gods."

Voss looked north, where the peaks stood up against the stars like broken teeth, and considered, briefly, giving him the true answer. It was not a secret. It was just heavy, and most people wanted a lighter thing to carry alongside her.

"I don't hate them," she said. "Hate requires belief that they matter. What I hate is what people do in their name — the eels on the altar stones while the dikes fail, the children on the shrine slabs while the fever spreads. Three hundred years ago my order cut that tumor out of the world, and the world healed, Anselm. It *healed*. Kingdoms rose, roads were built, the granaries filled. And now a handful of smugglers and a dying lizard want to tear the scar open because the scar itches." She turned to him. "I am not marching to murder a god, Brother. I am marching to keep a promise my order made to every child since the Edict: *no one will ever again die on an altar for nothing.* That's all. Go sleep. Dawn is early."

Anselm went. Voss stayed a while longer, watching the stars wheel over Thornfell, and permitted herself, in the privacy of her own skull, the one indulgence she allowed: the memory of her sisters' faces. Maren, who'd liked to catch frogs. Little Sef, who'd died asking whether the marsh-god was angry with her.

*No,* Voss thought, the answer she'd been composing for thirty years. *He isn't angry with you. He isn't anything. And I am going to make sure no one ever kneels to nothing again.*

* * *

On the fourth day the snow began, fat and windless, and the column slowed to a crawl through the high defiles. On the fifth, the reports started.

Scouts came back talking. At first it was small: a warmth in the rock where no warmth should be, springs running unfrozen in the shadow of the pass. Then less small. Corporal Denna — a solid woman, twelve years' service, not prone to fancies — reported hearing singing in the narrows above the tree line. Not voices, she insisted. More like... resonance. Stone humming. Half her squad heard it; half heard nothing; the half that heard nothing now wouldn't camp near the half that had.

By evening of the sixth day, three soldiers had deserted. Voss hanged one of the caught pair — publicly, swiftly, by the book — and pardoned the other, who wept and confessed that the song had sounded like his dead mother's lullaby. She pardoned him precisely because of that. Fear you could hang; longing you had to manage.

She gathered her officers around the map table in the command tent, lantern swinging, snow hissing against the canvas.

"Listen to me," she said, and they did; they always did, because she never wasted volume. "What you're hearing in the high country is real. I'm not going to insult you by calling it wind. Something up there is waking, and it wants you afraid, because afraid men desert, and deserters die in the snow, and every man we lose makes the thing in that canyon stronger. That is not superstition. That is tactics."

Silence. Good faces, mostly. Cassel's jaw tight. Denna staring at the map like it might sing too.

"The creature at the heart of this canyon is the last of its kind," Voss went on. "Old, wounded, dying. Everything else you've heard — the humming coins, the warm shrines, the singing stone — is the sound of a fire guttering in a drafty house. Loud, yes. Frightening, yes. But a guttering flame is still just a flame, and flames go out. Our task is to close the window." She put one finger on the canyon, a green thread of a valley stitched between two white shoulders of mountain. "We advance at first light. Skirmish line through the narrows, pike squares behind, archers on the rims. We do not charge, we do not scatter, we do not pray. We walk in like weather, and we end three hundred years of unfinished business."

"Prelate." Cassel, quietly. "If it's not dying. If it rises."

Voss met his eyes and gave him the only honesty she had ever offered anyone freely.

"Then you will see why the Edict was written," she said. "Sleep. First light."

* * *

She woke before the watch changed and stepped out of the tent into the blue-black hour, breath smoking, and looked south toward the canyon.

The sky above the far ridgeline was wrong.

Not storm-cloud — she knew storm-cloud the way she knew her own hands. This was a faint glow, deep red, banked-coal red, pulsing slow beneath the horizon of the peaks like a heartbeat under skin. Steady. Patient. Almost tender.

Around her, the camp slept. Somewhere a sentry coughed. The snow had stopped, and the silence was enormous, and in that silence — faint, so faint she might have invented it, though she knew she hadn't, because Denna had heard it first and Denna did not invent — the stone under her boots was singing.

One note. Low. Rising.

Voss stood very still and did the arithmetic one more time, the sum she had carried since the fen churchyard, and found, for the first time in thirty years, that the numbers would not sit still on the page.

Then she straightened her coat, woke her officers, and gave the order to march.
