## 16. The Siege of the Canyon

**Bramble**

The wards went out at dawn.

Not all at once — that would have been a mercy. They went the way an old woman goes: first the fingers, then the eyes, then the heart. Bramble felt each thread snap as Voss's engineers drove their iron stakes into the canyon mouth, one after another, and she felt them because she had tied every knot herself, kneeling in the frost for three nights running, whispering the old words into knotted cord and river-stone.

The seventh stake went in and the eastern ward died with a sound like a plucked string breaking inside her own ribs.

"They're coming through the low path," Osk signed from the rocks above her, quick and clean. He'd taken to signing even when he didn't need to — said it kept his hands warm. His face said what his hands didn't bother with: *too many. Far too many.*

"How many?"

He held up both fists, opened them, closed them again. Twice. Then a third time, and this time he left one hand half-curled. Somewhere north of two hundred and fifty. Against four living souls, one dying dragon, and a canyon full of stone eggs that had been dead longer than any kingdom had been alive.

Bramble spat over the edge of the ledge and began untying her apron strings. Inside the apron were eleven charms she had not wanted to use, because using them meant burning things she'd sworn to keep.

"Then we make them pay for every yard," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. Old women learned that trick late or never. She supposed she'd finally learned it.

Below, in the canyon's throat, Ysolde stood at the gate — the old gate, dragon-bone and bronze, older than the word for gate — with her white priestess robes pinned up like a soldier's skirt and a spear she'd taken off a dead man two valleys back. She looked small against it. She looked like a candle set before a cathedral door.

She looked, Bramble thought, exactly like what she was: the last of something, standing where the last of things always stand.

* * *

**Voss**

Serane Voss watched the canyon from the ridge and felt nothing so vulgar as hatred. Hatred was for amateurs. What she felt was closer to grief — the clean, disciplined grief of a surgeon who must cut away a limb to save the body.

Three hundred years ago, the old gods had gone silent, and the world had not ended. That was the whole of her theology, and it needed no more words than that. The dragons had abandoned their covenant; the shrines had rotted; the people had learned to pray to nothing and live anyway. Every famine since, every plague, every winter that took the weak — these were not punishments from absent gods. They were simply weather. And weather could be endured by those strong enough to endure it.

But now the silence was breaking. She had seen the shrine at Millbrook burn green instead of black. She had heard the well-water sing. And she had read the interrogation transcripts from the valley town, cross-referenced them with three centuries of Inquisition archives, and arrived at a name that should have been ash: *Ravensworth.*

A keeper alive. A flame tended in secret while honest folk starved believing heaven empty. It was not cruelty that brought her here. It was arithmetic.

"Signal the third company," she said. "Iron stakes through the western shelf. If the witch re-ties her knots, I want the ground under her feet staked too."

Her captain hesitated. "And the prisoner, Inquisitor? The hedge-witch we took at the guildhall—"

"Bramble Thornhollow stays in the fourth rank," Voss said. "Unharmed. If the Ravensworth girl surrenders, the witch walks free. That is the offer, and it will be made loudly enough to be heard over whatever they're singing down there."

"And if she doesn't surrender?"

Voss turned back to the canyon. The morning light came down its throat like water into a well, and somewhere at the bottom, faint as memory, she could hear it — a sound she refused to call singing. A sound she filed, in the ledger of her certainty, under *wind.*

"Then we do our work," she said. "All of it."

* * *

**Pellam**

Pellam had failed at everything he'd ever loved, and so he recognized the shape of this morning immediately: it was shaped like a last chance.

He stood in the altar chamber behind the roost, where the petrified eggs rose in ranks like a congregation struck dumb mid-hymn, and he looked at the hollow in the floor where the fire was meant to go, and he did the terrible arithmetic one more time.

*A Ravensworth life, freely given.* Vaelith had told Dominic on the second night, in the choir-song's oldest verse, and Pellam had been awake behind the rockfall when it was sung. Dominic did not know he knew. Bramble suspected — Bramble suspected everything, it was her profession — but she'd said nothing either.

Which meant the secret sat in Pellam's chest like a swallowed coal, and there was only one thing to do with a coal like that.

"You're thinking very loudly," said Vaelith.

The ancient dragon lay along the far wall, wings folded to skeletons, scales gone the grey of long-dead coals. Only her eyes still burned, twin embers banked so low they were nearly dark. Her voice came less as sound than as heat against the skin.

"I'm thinking about the rite," Pellam said. "The words. I've been listening."

"Have you." The embers brightened a fraction. "And you believe you could hold them. A knight who broke his oath, carrying a god's last verse."

"A knight who broke his oath," Pellam agreed, "carrying nothing else worth losing."

For a long moment the chamber was silent except for the distant crackle of Bramble's failing wards, and then Vaelith laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"Oh, little broken one. Do you know why the covenant asks for a Ravensworth?" Her tail moved, slow as a continent shifting. "It does not ask for blood. It never asked for blood. It asks for someone who has already given everything once, and can bear to give the rest. Your friend Dominic has spent her whole life being spent. You have only ever spent others. That is not the same coin."

Pellam's jaw tightened. "I traded information to save Bramble's life. I know what I am."

"Yes," Vaelith said gently, terribly. "You know what you were. That is the trap, child. Men who know what they were reach for grand deaths to avoid small lives. Go and stand at the gate instead. Standing is harder."

Above them, the first horn sounded, and the wards went out entirely, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

**Ysolde**

They came through the smoke in ordered ranks, and Ysolde thought: *three hundred years of silence, and still they fear us enough to bring two hundred men.*

She planted her feet at the gate and let the old words rise in her throat — not the choir-song, which was not hers to give, but the gate-verse, the door-verse, the verse the priesthood had used when kings came with armies to demand the gods take sides. It was a short verse. It had only ever needed to be spoken once.

The bronze in the gate woke under her palms. Dragon-bone has no business resonating; bone is dead things, and resonance is a property of the living. But the gate sang anyway, a low note that climbed until the men in the front rank stopped walking, and their officer shouted, and the arrows came.

Ysolde had trained for this in a century when training meant something. She turned the first volley with a word that cost her a year of her life — she felt it leave, the way you feel a tooth go — and the second volley with a gesture that cost her another. The third volley she did not turn.

The first arrow took her through the meat of the shoulder. The second grazed her skull and filled her left ear with ringing, which was almost funny; Osk would have made some joke about sharing his condition. The third—

The third never landed, because Dominic came down the canyon wall like a landslide with a purpose, and the smuggler's knives were in both her hands, and the front rank of the third company learned in one bad minute why the passes had belonged to the Ravensworths for six generations.

"Ysolde!" Dominic got an arm under her, dragged her back against the gate. Blood was sheeting down the priestess's side, hot and wrong. "You're done. Bramble—"

"Bramble is holding the west shelf alone." Ysolde gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "And you are needed at the altar, because Vaelith is dying faster than your enemy is advancing, and if the flame goes out before the army breaks, none of the rest matters. Listen to me, last chorister. The gate will hold while I hold it. That is the whole of my office. Let me hold it."

"I am not leaving you to die on your feet."

"No," Ysolde agreed, smiling through the blood. "You're leaving me to die *standing*, which is the only way any of us gets to. Go. Sing."

Dominic held her gaze for one breath, two — and then did the hardest thing Ysolde had ever watched anyone do, which was obey. She ran, back toward the roost, toward the hollow in the world's floor, and Ysolde turned back to the smoke and the ranks and put both hands flat on the singing bronze.

The gate-verse again. Once more. It only ever needed to be spoken once, but she had time for perhaps two more, and she intended to spend them extravagantly.

*Come,* she told the army, kindly. *I have been waiting three hundred years to be useful.*

* * *

**Osk**

Osk had fought deaf his whole life, and he had learned that the world without sound was simpler: no orders to mishear, no horns, no lies. Just motion. Just pattern. Two hundred men moving through a canyon made patterns a tracker could read like weather.

He read this one and did not like it.

The main push was a feint — too neat, too costly, men thrown at Ysolde's gate like coins into a wishing well. The real blade was the western shelf, twenty picked soldiers climbing single file toward Bramble's position, and behind them, unhurried, grey-cloaked, a woman with a white stole and a ledger-book manner: Voss herself.

*Bramble,* he signed, though she couldn't see him, and then he was moving along the high traverse, fast and silent, doing the thing the guild had taken him in to do.

They had found him at fourteen, half-starved, thrown out of three villages for being cursed — deafness read as God's fingerprint in those valleys, back when God had a name people liked. The Ravensworths hadn't cured him or pitied him. They'd handed him a slate and taught him signs and said, *you'll hear everything we can't.* First kindness of his life. Maybe the only one he'd needed.

So: Bramble. Then, if the gods were generous, the flanking company. Then, if they were extravagant, Voss.

He caught the flankers at the narrows, where the shelf pinched to two men wide, and he fought the way he did everything — watching, waiting, striking between the beats other people needed sound to find. Three went over the edge screaming into the gorge. A fourth put a sword through his thigh, and Osk killed him for it without anger, the way you close a door.

Then the fifth man did something no duelist would: he simply fired his crossbow from four feet away, point-blank, no art to it, and Osk learned in the last half-second of his life that the bolt's *thunk* into his chest was the loudest thing he had ever heard.

Sound, at the end. After all that silence. He might have laughed.

He fell at the narrows' mouth, and his body blocked it — wedged in the pinch, arm locked around a boulder, dead weight become fortification — and the flanking company stopped, and argued, and lost ten minutes they did not have.

Ten minutes. At the end, that was what the guild had bought him: ten minutes, spent exactly where they were needed most.

*You'll hear everything we can't,* they'd told him.

At the very last, drifting up through the stone, he heard the choir-song begin — Dominic's voice, cracked and terrified and true — and Osk thought: *there it is,* and was content.

* * *

**Bramble**

Bramble knew Osk was dead the moment the flanking attack stalled. There was no other hand in the world that could have stalled it.

She did not cry. There wasn't time, and besides, she'd buried a husband and two children and a guild, and she had learned that grief keeps perfectly well. It ferments. You can open it later.

What she did was burn her charms — all eleven, the ones she'd sworn to keep — and the western shelf became a place where iron stakes melted, where ropes frayed mid-climb, where twenty picked soldiers discovered that a hedge-witch with nothing left to protect except her own refusal was a category of problem their training had not covered.

It worked for nine minutes. Wards built on fury always do.

When the tenth minute came, they rushed her together, and Bramble went down swinging a charred oak staff, and the last thing she saw before the pommel of a sword met her temple was the sky over the canyon filling with Voss's signal-smoke — three columns, black, meaning *the altar is taken, the witch is captured, press to the roost.*

Meaning: *we have her. Come and trade.*

* * *

**Pellam**

He heard the fighting stop in pieces. The gate-verse faltered — Ysolde's voice going quiet mid-word, like a lamp running out of oil — and then the army's boots came down the canyon throat unopposed, and Pellam understood that everyone who had stood between the enemy and the altar chamber was now standing nowhere.

Everyone except him.

He was at the hollow before he fully decided to be, which is how the worst decisions arrive: already made. The hollow waited, cold, the size of a man curled small. Above it, Vaelith's breathing had slowed to one long pull per minute, and her ember-eyes had dimmed to almost nothing.

"The words," Pellam said aloud, to the chamber, to the eggs, to whatever listened. "I know the words."

"You do not," said Vaelith, barely audible. "You know sounds."

"I know enough."

"Child—"

But the chamber door burst open, and it was not Dominic. It was soldiers, six of them, and behind them a lieutenant reading from a paper: *"In the name of the Inquisition, the witch Bramble Thornhollow is held—"* and Pellam did not hear the rest, because he had already stepped between the paper and the hollow, sword drawn, an oathless knight making his last stand over an empty hole in the ground.

He fought the way desperate men fight, which is to say well and briefly. He killed two. He wounded a third. Then a spear took him through the chest from the side, from the angle he couldn't watch, and Pellam fell against the hollow's rim with his arms spread across it like a man shielding a child.

His last coherent thought was not of shame, or Bramble, or God. It was simple bookkeeping: *the secret dies with me. She'll never know the price. Good.*

Then boots, many boots, and a cold voice saying, "Leave him. Seal the chamber. And find me the Ravensworth."

* * *

**Dominic**

Dominic came out of the roost tunnels into a canyon that had changed hands.

Ysolde lay at the gate with her hands still on the bronze, and no one had dared move her — superstition, maybe, or something better. Osk's body clogged the narrows like a keystone. Bramble was gone, taken. And at the center of it all, ringed by soldiers with drawn bows, stood Serane Voss with a scroll in one hand and Bramble's life in the other, looking up at the ledge where Dominic stood with soot on her face and the choir-song still unfinished in her throat.

"Last chorister," Voss called, and her voice carried the way conviction carries, level and clear. "Here is the whole of my offer, and I will make it once. The witch for the altar. Walk down, tell me where the flame sleeps, and she goes free, and you go free, and this ends today with one grave fewer."

Behind Voss, through the sealed door of the altar chamber, faint as a heartbeat, Pellam's blood was seeping under the stones.

And below it all, deeper than stone, Dominic felt the flame gutter — felt it the way you feel your own pulse stumble — one breath from dark, with the final verse unsung, and the only voice left to sing it hers.

She looked at Bramble, bruised and bound. At Ysolde, still. At Osk, holding the narrows forever.

Then Dominic Ravensworth stepped off the ledge — not toward Voss, but toward the sealed door, walking slowly enough to be followed, fast enough to be feared — and began, in front of two hundred armed strangers, to sing.
