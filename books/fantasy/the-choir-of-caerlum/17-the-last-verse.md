## 17. The Last Verse

Vaelith was dying the way a city dies — not all at once, but district by district, light by light.

Dominic saw it in the days after the siege's first assault. The ancient dragon lay in the heart of the roost canyon, beneath the petrified eggs that rose around her like a forest of stone fruit, and each morning some part of her had gone dark. The glow along her spine dimmed from ember to ash. The great eyes, which had held Dominic's reflection like a mirror holds a face it will outlast, clouded at the edges. Her breathing had slowed to something geological — one long tide in, one long tide out, and between them a silence you could stand inside.

Bramble did what she could. She was no dragon-healer; nobody alive was. She packed Vaelith's cracked scales with poultices of comfrey and mountain honey and muttered wards over them that glowed faintly and faded faster each time. Pellam kept watch on the canyon rim and counted Voss's fires at night, and the count grew. Ysolde was dead. Osk was dead. The gate of the roost was a ruin of shattered wardstone and burned rope, and everyone who remained knew the next assault would come before the week turned.

So there was no time for what Vaelith wanted to give her. Which was, of course, exactly why Vaelith insisted on giving it.

"Come," the dragon said on the fourth morning, when the light through the canyon mouth was still gray. "Sit where I can see you. My eyes are going, but they are not gone."

Dominic climbed down the fallen slab she'd been using as a stair and settled herself on the warm sand near the dragon's foreleg. The leg alone was bigger than the guildhall had been. She'd stopped flinching at the scale of things somewhere on the road south, but sitting this close still made her feel like an ant invited to dinner.

"You should be resting," she said.

"I have been resting for three hundred years." A sound moved through Vaelith's chest, deep enough that Dominic felt it in her back teeth. It might have been a laugh. "Rest is how we got here, little chorister. I am done with it."

"You said that last time. Then you slept for two days."

"And woke with less than I had." The dragon's head shifted, grinding stone against stone, until one dim eye hung above Dominic like a dying sun. "The verse, Dominic Ravensworth. You have the first eleven. You have held them without breaking, which is more than I hoped when you staggered into my canyon smelling of smoke and grief. But the song has twelve verses, and the twelfth is the door. Without it, everything you carry is a key with no lock."

Dominic pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. "Then teach me."

"I intend to. But first—" the eye narrowed, or seemed to, "—I must tell you why the song stopped. Because the twelfth verse is not just words. It is a question, and if you do not understand the question, you will answer it wrong, and answering it wrong means the fire goes out forever, everywhere, in every shrine and every egg and every hidden hearth your family ever tended." A pause, one tidal breath. "Do you understand? There is no second attempt. There is no other keeper waiting behind you. You are the last verse's only reader."

"No pressure," Dominic murmured.

"I do not know that word."

"It means you're being very reassuring."

The sound again, deeper this time. Almost certainly a laugh. "Good. Fear keeps the hands steady. Now listen, and do not interrupt, because I will only say this once and my voice is leaving me the way the light leaves the sky — slowly, and then all at once."

* * *

"The covenant was not broken by us."

Vaelith began there, and Dominic, who had spent three weeks hearing versions of history from Ysolde — the war, the betrayal, the dragons withdrawing — felt the ground tilt under the sentence.

"We let humans believe it was their sin," the dragon went on. "It was kinder that way. Kinder, and easier, and it gave your ancestors something to do with their guilt: tend our shrines, keep our flames, sing our song down the generations. The Ravensworths were not smugglers who secretly served gods. They were priests whose god asked them to pretend to be criminals so the world would stop looking for them. Your grandmother's journal — you told me of it. Every lie in it was a mercy. Remember that, later, when you decide what to do with the truth."

Dominic's throat was tight. "Broken by whom, then?"

"By us." The word came out like a stone dropping down a well. "We broke it. We chose silence."

The canyon was very quiet. Somewhere above, Pellam's boots scraped on the rim as he walked his circuit. Below, water dripped somewhere in the dark of the egg-forest, patient as a clock.

"There was a plague," Vaelith said. "You know this much from Ysolde, I think. The gray sweating sickness, the one that emptied three kingdoms in a decade. What you do not know is that we could have ended it. Not eased it — ended it. Dragonfire does not only burn. It cleanses. It burns away rot in living flesh and leaves the living flesh whole. One flight across the infected lands. One pass of the choir, all twelve verses sung in harmony, and the sickness would have died and the dead would have stopped dying."

Dominic stared at her. "Then why—"

"Because the last time we flew to heal, two hundred years before, the kingdoms met us with ballistae and fear. We healed a river valley of blight and they burned the roost at Carrowmere in thanks. Four hundred eggs. Hatchlings still soft-shelled." The old eye did not blink. Dragons' eyes don't, Dominic had learned. That made it worse, somehow — nothing to break the stare, nowhere to hide. "And the kings came to us afterward, hat in hand, and asked us to fly again for the plague. And we looked at the smoke still rising from Carrowmere, and we said no."

"That's not breaking a covenant," Dominic said slowly. "That's refusing to be used."

"It is both. Those are not different things, little chorister. That is the lesson." Vaelith's breath stirred the sand around Dominic's boots, warm and smelling of iron and old incense. "A covenant is not a contract. A contract ends when the other party fails. A covenant is a promise you keep *because* the other party failed — because keeping it when keeping it costs everything is the only proof it was ever real. We had a covenant with humankind: your worship for our fire, your tending for our protection. And when you betrayed us, we discovered we had never actually made a covenant at all. We had made a contract. So we took our fire home and we went silent, and we called it justice."

"And it wasn't."

"It was despair wearing justice's clothes." For the first time since Dominic had known her, the dragon's voice cracked — a fissure running through the vast tone, brief and terrible. "Three hundred years I have told myself we were right. Three hundred years my brothers and sisters died one by one, unwoken, unsung, and I told myself the world deserved its silence. And every year the silence got easier to keep, and every year fewer humans remembered the song, and every year I understood a little better that we had not punished the world. We had punished ourselves. Justice that starves the judge along with the judged is not justice. It is just a longer way of dying."

Dominic thought of the guildhall burning. Of Osk falling in the square with Voss's crossbow bolt in his throat, covering her escape with the only thing he had left, which was his body. Of the arithmetic Pellam had done at Hollow Ford, trading truth for Bramble's life, and the shame he carried like a stone in his belly ever since. Everyone in her ragged, murdered, magnificent family had been betrayed by someone, and had betrayed someone, usually in the same week.

"And now?" she asked. "Now they've sent an inquisitor to finish it. Voss wants you dead. She thinks killing the last dragon ends the famine and the fever. She'll burn this canyon to the bedrock and call it mercy." Her hands had closed into fists without her noticing. "They broke it again, Vaelith. They're breaking it right now. Why give them the fire?"

"Ah." The eye brightened — or perhaps that was only the death-light shifting. "There it is. The question behind the verse. I wondered how long it would take you."

* * *

Vaelith taught her the twelfth verse the way you teach someone to swim by holding their head under — no gentleness, no easing in, because there wasn't time and because the song didn't want gentleness. It wanted truth.

She sang it first, alone, and Dominic heard why the whole thing needed twelve parts. The eleventh verses she'd learned were harmonies, interlocking lines that leaned on each other like smugglers leaning on a wall. The twelfth stood apart. It was a single line, low and plain, almost speech, and it repeated a phrase that turned on itself like a key turning:

*What was given freely, take freely. What was taken, give back. What was broken, mend — not because it earned mending, but because mending is what the fire is for.*

"Say it back," Vaelith said.

Dominic said it back. Got the cadence wrong. Said it again. The third time, something in her chest caught — a warmth behind the sternum, the same warmth she'd felt at the altar in the guildhall cellar, the voice like banked coals that had named her *last chorister*. The line wanted to be sung by someone who meant it, and it could tell the difference. Songs always could.

"Again."

"Vaelith—"

"Again. Until it lives in you deeper than your name. Because here is what the verse does, Dominic Ravensworth, and you must hear this with everything you have left." The dragon drew herself up, and even dying, even dimmed, the movement filled the canyon like weather. "The twelfth verse is the rite. Not a prayer beside the rite. Not a preparation for it. When you stand at the altar in the mountain's heart — and you will stand there, soon, sooner than any of us wish — you will sing the twelve verses together, and the twelfth will ask the fire a question, and the fire will answer, and the answer depends entirely on whether you mean it."

"Mean what?"

"*Mending is what the fire is for.*" Each word fell separately, like coins on stone. "Not punishment. Not judgment. Not a reward for good behavior or a wage for faithful service. The fire does not check ledgers. The fire burns away rot and leaves the living flesh whole. That is all it has ever done. That is all it is. And the question the twelfth verse asks — the question *you* will answer, not with words but with whatever is truest in you — is whether you can give that to a world that has never once deserved it."

Dominic sat very still.

She thought about the valley towns they'd walked through on the road south. The shrine-burnings. The informers. The woman in Millbrook who'd spat at Bramble's feet and then, an hour later, left bread outside their camp and run away before anyone could thank her. Both of those women were real. Both of them were the world.

She thought about Voss. Serane Voss, who burned shrines and broke people and genuinely, catastrophically believed she was saving children from fever and fields from blight — and who was not entirely wrong that the world was sick, only wrong about the cure. Voss would march on this canyon within days. Voss might well kill them all. If Dominic rekindled the flame, the first thing it might do was fall into Voss's hands, or a king's, or a mob's, and be made into a weapon, because that was what humans did with power. It was practically a tradition.

"They'll misuse it," Dominic said. "Give me one reason to believe they won't."

"I cannot." No hesitation. "They will. Some of them. Soon, probably. Fire in human hands has always been a wager, and the house always takes its cut." The great head lowered until the dying eye was level with hers, close enough that Dominic could see her own small reflection swimming in the clouded gold. "But hear me, last chorister. We did not withhold the fire because humans misused it. Humans misused it constantly, for ten thousand years, and we flew anyway. We withheld it because we were hurt. Hurt is not a moral position. It is a wound. And I am dying of it, and so are all my kind, and the world is dying of it too — the famine, the fevers, the cold creeping down from the passes — all of it is the long interest on a debt of silence." A breath, long and tidal. "Mercy is not a payment the world earns, child. Mercy is the fire deciding, one last time, to be what it is instead of what its pain made it."

"And if I can't mean it?" Dominic's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "If I stand at the altar and some part of me is still counting — Osk, Ysolde, the hall, my name, everything they took — if the ledger's still open in me? What happens?"

"Then the verse rings false," Vaelith said simply, "and the fire goes out, and the silence becomes permanent, and in a thousand years no one will remember there was ever a song at all." The eye held her. "I will not tell you to set the ledger down. I could not set down mine, and look at me. I will only tell you what I learned too late: justice is what you owe yourself. Mercy is what you owe everyone else. A keeper carries both, and spends them in the right places, and that is the entire discipline. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

They worked until the light went amber and then gone. Twelve verses, over and over, Vaelith's voice thinning with each pass until by dusk she was shaping the words more than singing them, and Dominic was carrying the melody alone, the dragon's lips moving silently alongside like a parent mouthing a lullaby they no longer had breath for.

On the last repetition, something happened that Dominic would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly. The petrified eggs around the canyon — hundreds of them, stone for three centuries — hummed. Faintly. Once. Like coins in a pocket remembering they were money.

Vaelith heard it too. The old eye widened, and for a moment the glow along her spine flickered up, one bright pulse, a heartbeat remembered rather than felt.

"You felt it," Dominic whispered.

"I felt *you*." Wonder cracked the dragon's voice wide open. "Oh, little chorister. Oh, you impossible, stubborn, grieving girl. Do you know what you just did? The eggs are stone. Stone does not sing. Unless—" and here the wonder curdled into something urgent, almost fierce, "—unless the song has found a keeper who can hold all twelve verses true. Which means the rite might work. Which means—"

She stopped. The glow guttered. The tide of her breathing hitched, caught, resumed.

"Which means what?" Dominic was on her feet.

"Which means you must go tonight." Vaelith's voice dropped to a rasp. "To the altar. Tomorrow will be too late — I can feel it, the last light going out, and besides, look to the rim."

Dominic looked. On the canyon's eastern edge, where the dark had just finished swallowing the sky, fires were kindling. One. Three. Seven. A constellation of enemy camps, more than Pellam's count had shown at dusk, more than there should have been. Reinforcements. Voss, ascendant, closing the net with everything the crown had given her.

"She comes at dawn," Vaelith said. "Perhaps sooner. You cannot win a battle. You cannot outrun an army carrying what you carry. There is only one door left, and it is the twelfth verse, and it opens inward." The dying eye found hers and held it, and in it Dominic saw reflected every hard thing she'd ever survived and one harder thing she hadn't yet. "Go to the altar, Dominic Ravensworth. Sing the song. Answer the question. And whatever the fire asks of you — and it will ask, it always asks —"

The breath caught again. Longer, this time.

"—remember that the fire has never once wanted a death. Only a keeper."

Dominic stood in the dark at the edge of the egg-forest, the twelve verses burning in her chest like swallowed coals, and above her the enemy fires multiplied, and behind her the last dragon in the world breathed slower, and slower, and slower.

She turned toward the mountain's heart and started to climb.
