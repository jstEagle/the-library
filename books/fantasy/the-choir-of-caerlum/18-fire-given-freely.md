## 18. Fire Given Freely

The canyon had gone quiet in the way that only places of slaughter go quiet — not silence, but the held breath after it.

Dominic climbed the last stretch of the roost's inner path on legs that did not feel like hers. Behind her, smoke still rose from the shattered gate where Ysolde had fallen. Somewhere below, Bramble knelt over Pellam, her hands green with ward-light that was guttering faster than she could pour it out. And somewhere above, on the canyon rim, Serane Voss was regrouping an army that had just watched a knight burn to ash trying to steal a god's fire.

*Steal*. That was the word that kept snagging in Dominic's mind as she climbed. Pellam had tried to *steal* it. He had learned the truth she'd been too slow to see — that the flame wanted a Ravensworth, that it wanted a life — and instead of telling her, instead of letting her choose, he had walked up this same path with his sword drawn and offered himself like a man paying another man's debt.

He had thought he was protecting her. That was the worst of it. That was the thing she would carry, if she carried anything at all out of this canyon: that the people she loved kept deciding her sacrifices for her.

The altar sat in a hollow at the canyon's head, ringed by petrified eggs taller than she was. The hatchling-scale that Ysolde had found in the guildhall cellar lay in its cradle of black stone, and it was singing — a thin, cracked thread of the choir-song, the way a dying man hums a tune he half remembers. The flame it guarded was not a flame at all, not anymore. It was an ember the size of a fist, sunk in ash, pulsing so slowly that between one beat and the next Dominic was certain it had gone out.

She knelt in the ash. It was warm. Everything here was warm, the way a body is warm.

"All right," she said. Her voice came out scraped. She tried again. "I'm here. I'm the last one. So tell me what it costs."

The ember pulsed. The song in the scale bent, and the voice that came out of it was the voice from her first prayer — banked coals, deep hearth, the smell of woodsmoke on a winter night — but thinner now, worn almost through.

*Little chorister.*

"That's not an answer."

*It is the only answer there is. The fire is covenant, and covenant is given, not taken. Your knight learned this. The fire does not want his death. The fire has never wanted death. It wanted what the first Ravensworth gave freely, three hundred years ago, and what every Ravensworth after her renewed in secret: themselves. Their name. Their place. Their belonging.*

Dominic stared at the ember. "I don't understand."

*You understand better than you pretend. You have spent your whole life belonging to something you were never allowed to name. The guild gave you a name and a trade and a family, and it taught you to hide all three. Now the world is asking you to choose: keep the name and let the fire die, or give the name and let it live.*

"And if I give it?" Her throat was tight. "If I give it — what's left? Who am I? What do I *do*?"

*You do what keepers have always done,* the voice said, and there was something in it that might have been gentleness. *You carry the fire to the places that have forgotten it.*

* * *

She thought about refusing.

She thought about it the way you think about jumping from a great height — not seriously, not as a plan, but because the mind insists on measuring the fall. She thought about the guildhall burning behind her two nights ago, the way she had lit it herself, the way the roof beams had gone up like the ribs of some vast dying animal. She thought about Osk's hands, quick and sure, the last time she'd seen them alive — signing *go* to her across the smoke. She thought about Ysolde's old god-coins, warm in her pocket still, and the way Ysolde had smiled when Dominic finally sang the first verse back to her, as if she'd been waiting three hundred years to hear it.

She thought about Voss. Voss, who believed with her whole terrible heart that the world was better off with the gods dead. Voss, who had burned shrines and broken people and called it mercy, and who was, right now, at this very moment, marching down into the canyon to finish what her soldiers had started.

If Dominic walked away, the ember would gutter. Maybe tonight. Maybe in a year. And Vaelith — Vaelith, who had held the choir alone in the dark for three centuries, who had tested Dominic with a song only a true keeper could hold and then, dying, taught her the final verse anyway, trusting her with it — Vaelith would be the last dragon, and the last dragon would be a story, and the story would end the way Voss wanted it to end. Clean. Silent. Dead.

And if she stayed — if she gave —

She didn't know what she was. That was the truth under all of it. Ravensworth. Smuggler. Orphan taken in by a guild that turned out to be a priesthood in hiding. She had built herself out of other people's secrets, and now the last secret was being offered to her like a knife, handle first.

*Little chorister,* the voice said. *The rite does not ask for your death. It never did. It asks for your name. Names are how the world holds you. Give the world back its hold on you, and the fire will know what you are.*

"And what am I?"

*You are the one who came when called. That has always been enough.*

Dominic laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "You know what my grandmother wrote in her journal? The last page. She wrote, *the guild is a door, not a house.* I thought she meant the smuggling routes. I thought she meant the passes." She pressed her palm flat against the warm ash. "She meant you."

*She meant us.*

* * *

She stood. Her legs held. That surprised her.

Below, in the canyon, she could hear the army moving — the creak of leather, the ring of steel, the low murmur of a few hundred people trying to be quiet and failing. Voss would come up this path soon. Maybe she was coming already.

Dominic looked down at the hollow once more. Bramble was still bent over Pellam. She couldn't tell from here whether he was breathing.

"Wait," she said to the ember, and climbed down.

Bramble looked up when Dominic's shadow fell across her. Her face was grey with exhaustion, streaked with soot and something darker. "He's dying," she said, flat, no room in her voice for comfort. "The fire took what it wanted and left the rest. I can't — Dominic, I can't stop it."

"I know."

"He did it to save you."

"I know." Dominic knelt. Pellam's eyes were open, fixed on the petrified eggs above, and when he saw her they moved, slow as a man turning over in deep water. His lips shaped her name. No sound came out.

She took his hand. It was cold.

"You idiot," she said, and her voice broke on it. "You absolute — you should have told me. You should have *told* me."

His mouth moved again. She leaned close. What came out was barely a breath: "Couldn't. Let. You. Pay."

"You didn't pay it either," she whispered. "It never wanted blood, Pellam. It wanted — " She stopped. Swallowed. Looked at his face, at the knight's honor and the smuggler's guilt and the terrible arithmetic all mixed together there, and finished: "It wanted everything. That's all. Just everything."

Something moved in his eyes that might have been relief. His fingers tightened on hers, once, and then his gaze went past her, up, to the eggs, and stayed there, and did not come back.

Bramble made a sound low in her throat and bent her head. Dominic held his hand until it was only a hand.

Then she stood, because the army was coming, and because the dead do not need you the way the living do.

* * *

The soldiers reached the hollow as the sun went down behind the western rim, and the canyon filled with long blue shadow.

They came slowly, warily — they had seen what happened to the last man who touched the altar, and whatever Voss had told them, fear moves faster than orders. They fanned out across the ash with torches and crossbows, and at their center, walking with the unhurried certainty of a woman who has never once doubted her own righteousness, came Serane Voss.

She stopped ten paces from Dominic and looked at her the way she had looked at her through the whole long chase — the way a surgeon looks at a wound. Not with hatred. With diagnosis.

"Ravensworth," she said. "Or whatever you are now. The guild is ash. Your knight is dead. Your witch is spent." Her eyes went to the altar hollow, to the faint pulse of light behind Dominic. "You have nowhere left to hide it. Give me the shrine, and I'll let the witch and the old woman walk. You have my word."

"Your word," Dominic said. It came out steadier than she felt. "The word of the woman who burned a shrine with a family inside it in Marrowdale. I heard about that."

Voss's face did not change. "Idolatry is a fire, Ravensworth. You do not put out a fire by asking it politely. You starve it. You smother it. You do what must be done so that a thousand others live." And the terrible thing, the thing Dominic would remember long after, was that she believed it. Every word. You could hear it in her voice, plain as a bell. She wasn't lying. She had never once lied to herself, and that was what made her more dangerous than any liar Dominic had ever smuggled past.

"You think the gods are a plague," Dominic said.

"I know they are. I've seen what worship does to the weak. I've buried what it leaves behind." Voss's hand rested on her sword, easy, certain. "The world is finally learning to stand on its own feet, and you would drag it back to its knees. For what? For an ember? For a dying lizard in a hole in the ground?"

Behind Dominic, the ember pulsed. Once. Twice. And the song in the hatchling-scale changed — not louder, but *deeper*, the way the ground hums before an earthquake, and Voss's soldiers shifted, and someone's crossbow wavered.

Dominic turned her back on the inquisitor. It was the most dangerous thing she had ever done, and it felt like the easiest.

"Last chance, Ravensworth."

"No," Dominic said. "That's yours."

And she knelt at the altar, in front of God and army, and began the rite.

* * *

She did not know the words. That was the strange part. Ysolde had taught her the verses of the choir-song, and Vaelith had taught her the final one, but the rite was older than songs, older than words, and it came up out of her like water from a well she hadn't known she'd dug — in the language her grandmother's journal was written in, in the cadence her mentor used to use counting cargo in the dark, in the rhythm of Osk's hands, of Bramble's charms, of Pellam's prayers to a knight's god he'd stopped believing in years ago.

She gave it her name first. *I am Dominic Ravensworth,* she said, or thought, or sang — there was no difference anymore — *daughter of no one, taken in by no one, smuggler of nothing, keeper of the last flame.* And she felt the name lift out of her the way heat lifts off a stone at dusk, and the world's hold on her loosened, and it did not hurt. That was the shocking thing. It felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying.

She gave it the guild next. Every name on every ledger her grandmother had kept. Every route through Thornfell, every hidden door, every coin that never passed a mint. *The Ravensworth guild is dissolved,* she said. *Its debts are paid. Its covenant is renewed. Let the door close and the house stand open.*

And then she gave it the past — and here she faltered, because the past was Osk, and Osk was dead, and the past was Pellam, and Pellam was dead, and the past was a mentor buried in an unmarked grave and a childhood spent learning which footsteps carry and which ones don't, and she did not want to give that up, she did not want to hand her dead to a fire, even a holy one, even a warm one —

*Little chorister.* The voice was inside her now, or she was inside it; there was no border left. *You do not give them to the fire. You give the fire to them. That is what a keeper is. That is what the covenant always was.*

Dominic understood, then — all at once, the way you understand a thing you've known your whole life and never said aloud. The guild had never been smugglers who secretly kept a god. They had been keepers who smuggled, because the world had made hiding the only way to keep anything safe. Three hundred years of hidden names and secret doors and coins no mint had struck — all of it, all of it had been one long act of faith, passed hand to hand in the dark, waiting for someone to come along who would give it back.

*We broke our word,* the voice said, and it was Vaelith's voice now, vast and tired and old, and it was coming from everywhere, from the eggs, from the stone, from the ash under her knees. *Three hundred years ago we broke our word to you, and you broke yours to us, and both of us have been dying of it ever since. Let it end here. Let it end with fire given freely.*

"I give it freely," Dominic said.

The ember flared.

Not caught — *caught* is too small a word for it. It stood up out of its ash like a thing that had been sleeping, and it was not the size of a fist anymore, it was the size of a heart, and then the size of a head, and the light came off it in waves that turned the blue shadow of the canyon to gold, and the hatchling-scale sang — not the cracked dying thread anymore but the full choir, all the verses, the ones Ysolde taught her and the ones no one had sung in three hundred years, and the sound went up the canyon walls and came back down changed, harmonized, *answered* —

And the eggs moved.

Not all of them. Not all at once. But here and there across the hollow, in the petrified shells that had stood dead for centuries, something *stirred* — a shiver of stone becoming scale, of scale becoming wing — and the first crack ran up the side of the nearest egg with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Voss's soldiers broke. Not all of them — some stood, frozen, torches shaking — but enough. Enough that when the light swept the hollow again, the line was ragged, and men were dropping crossbows, and one of them was on his knees in the ash with his helmet off, weeping, and no one had told him to.

Voss did not kneel. Voss drew her sword and stared into the light with an expression Dominic would spend years trying to name — not fear, not hatred, something older and sadder than either — and behind her, above her, the canyon rim began to glow.

The heat came first. Then the wind, hot and enormous, smelling of lightning and hot stone. Then the shadow, crossing the hollow like an eclipse — a shadow with wings.

Vaelith came down out of the high dark like the answer to a prayer three centuries overdue, and she was not dying anymore. The scales that had hung grey and loose on her frame burned now, ember-red to gold at the edges, and her eyes were furnaces, and when she landed the whole canyon rang like a struck bell, and she was *young* — not hatchling-young, not the young of years, but the young of purpose, the young of a fire that had been banked for three hundred years and had just remembered what it was for.

She raised her head and looked at the army, at the torches, at Voss with her sword drawn, at the kneeling soldier, at the eggs cracking open one by one along the hollow — and she opened her jaws, and did not breathe fire.

She sang.

The note that came out of her was the final verse, the one she'd taught Dominic with her last strength, and it was not a note of rage. It was a note of *judgment* — the kind that comes not from anger but from grief finally allowed to speak — and it rolled down the canyon and over the army and through them, and every soldier who heard it heard, in that note, every prayer they had ever whispered and every prayer they had ever refused to hear.

Dominic knelt in the ash with the fire standing at her back and her name gone out of the world, and she was no one now, no one at all, and she had never in her life felt so completely, so terrifyingly, *held*.

* * *

Afterward — after the soldiers had fled or knelt, after Voss had lowered her sword by inches and stood staring at the dragon as if staring at the end of everything she understood — Bramble came up the path on legs that shook, and stopped at the edge of the altar hollow, and looked at Dominic kneeling in the ash with the flame behind her.

"You did it," Bramble said. Her voice was wrecked. "You actually — Dominic, the eggs are *hatching*."

"I know."

"Vaelith — she's — " Bramble's voice cracked. "Pellam. Did you — "

"I know," Dominic said, and Bramble understood, and came and knelt beside her in the warm ash, and put her arms around her, and for a long time neither of them said anything, because the fire was singing and the eggs were opening and there were no words in any language, old or new, that were equal to it.

Then Bramble pulled back and looked at her, really looked, and frowned.

"What do I call you?" she said. "If not Ravensworth?"

Dominic opened her mouth. Closed it.

And from the altar, from the flame, from the song that was no longer only Vaelith's but was rising now from a dozen throats in a dozen shells, the answer came — warm, and deep, and new:

*That,* said the fire, *is the next question.*

And down in the canyon, where the army had knelt and broken and fled, Serane Voss stood alone in the ash with her sword point-down in the ground, staring up at the young dragon with the look of a woman whose whole world had just ended — and, for the first time in her life, no idea what to build in its place.
