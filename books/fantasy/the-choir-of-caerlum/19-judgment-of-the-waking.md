## 19. Judgment of the Waking

I had forgotten what it was to have a body that obeyed me.

For three hundred years I had been a thought inside a dying ember, a voice with no throat, watching through scales gone grey as grave-stone while the world above my canyon forgot my name. Now fire moved in me again — young fire, Dominic's fire, given freely at the altar — and the first thing it did was hurt. The second thing it did was make me *vast*.

I rose from the roost on wings that had not opened since before the kingdoms of men had names, and the sound of them was like a mountain deciding to walk.

Below me, the canyon floor was chaos. Voss's soldiers — two hundred of them, maybe more, rank upon rank of pikes and torches and the white-and-iron banners of the Inquisition — had come to kill a corpse and found instead a god. They were still processing this. Men are slow, bless them. It gives you time to think.

And I had much to think about.

Because here is what three centuries of dying taught me, what the silence taught me, what the long dark of the ember taught me: I knew exactly how to end this. One breath. That is all it would take. Fire does not negotiate; fire does not weigh; fire simply *is*, and everything within its reach becomes a story someone else tells later. Two hundred men. One breath. The arithmetic of dragons has always been simple.

The old me — the me of the ancient war, the me who had burned cities when men broke covenant — would already be breathing.

But I had spent three hundred years listening to prayers instead.

* * *

They came up the canyon road in good order, I will grant them that. Even terror disciplines some men. Their captain — a scarred woman with a voice like a whip-crack — got them into square formation on the flats below the roost gate, shields up, pikes out, the old anti-dragon doctrine that hadn't been needed since my kind went to sleep. It was almost touching. Like watching children build a sandcastle against the tide.

At their center, on a white horse that wanted very badly to be somewhere else, sat Inquisitor Serane Voss.

I knew her face before she spoke. Not from memory — from the flame. Dominic's fire ran in my veins now, and Dominic's memories came with it, unbidden: a shrine burning in a valley town, a hedge-witch's charms confiscated and fed to a bonfire, an old woman made to kneel in the ash and recant gods she still loved. Voss had done all of it herself. Personally. With the same hands now holding her horse's reins.

She looked up at me, and do you know what I saw in her face?

Not fear. That came later, for some of them. What I saw first was *certainty*, bright and terrible as a drawn blade. She looked at a living dragon — the last of the elder line, risen from death's antechamber on wings of renewed fire — and her eyes said: *this is the plague. This is the famine. This is every dead child and blighted field, and I am the knife that ends it.*

I have been hated before. Hated is easy. Hated I could work with.

Being hated by someone who believed she was saving the world — that was the wound I did not know how to answer.

So I did not answer with fire.

I landed.

* * *

The impact cracked the canyon floor. Stone jumped. Somewhere in the square formation, a man lost control of his bladder, and I heard it — heard everything, now, with young ears — the small wet shame-sound of it, his neighbor's bitten-off laugh, the captain's snarled order to hold. Pikes leveled at me, a forest of iron points aimed at scales that had turned aside ballistae when those pikes' great-great-grandfathers were ore in the ground.

Voss rode forward alone. Of course she did. Zealots always do; they cannot resist the theater of it, and neither, if I am honest, can I.

"Creature," she called up, and her voice did not shake. I will say that for her. "In the name of the Concord and the Crown Inquisitorial, I—"

"*Serane Voss.*"

My voice is not a voice anymore. It is weather. When I speak, men feel it in their teeth, in the fluid of their eyes, in the old animal places beneath thinking. Her horse screamed and bolted and she let it go, standing in the stirrups, one hand on her sword.

"You know my name."

"I know your prayers." I lowered my head until we were eye to eye, close enough that my breath stirred the white trim of her collar. "You pray every night. Did you know that? You think prayer is a weapon you aim at your god. But prayer goes *out*, little inquisitor. It goes everywhere. Into stone. Into ember. Into sleeping things. And some of us were too tired to sleep deeply enough to miss it."

Her jaw tightened. "Then you know what I prayed for. An end to the blight. An end to the famines that followed the shrines reopening—"

"The shrines reopened *last month*." Fire flickered along my jaws as I laughed, and half the front rank flinched back a step. "Your wheat failed in the spring. Your wells soured in winter. Your plagues walked through your towns while my kind was ash and memory and no one alive remembered how to spell our names. You did not suffer because the gods returned, Serane Voss. You suffered because the world has been running on broken machinery for three hundred years, and you are looking at the machine and calling it the malfunction."

"Lies." But her voice had changed key. Slightly. A quarter-tone. "The old faiths demanded blood. The histories—"

"The histories were written by the victors of a war whose cause they burned." I straightened, slowly, letting my shadow fall across the whole formation, two hundred men suddenly small as beetles under a boot that had not yet decided to fall. "You want truth? You came armed for a slaughter. Very well. I will give you truth, and you may keep your pikes where they are, because truth is the only weapon I intend to use today. Whether it cuts you or not is between you and yourself."

* * *

I showed them.

This is a thing dragons can do, when we are strong — when there is fire enough in us to spend on something other than destruction. The old priesthood called it *the Mirroring*. Ysolde would have known the proper rites for it; Ysolde was ash on the canyon gate by then, having held it open with her body so that a hatchling-scale altar could be carried to safety, and I grieved her even as I worked, grief folded into the working like salt into bread.

I reached into the flame — Dominic's gift, freely given, still singing in my chest — and I pulled the veil off the sky.

Every soldier in that canyon saw the same thing at once. I made sure of it. No one would be spared by position or blindness; the vision stood in the air above us like a second sunset, and it could not be looked away from.

I showed them the covenant. Not the corrupted version in their sanctioned histories — the true one, older than any kingdom. Humans and dragons, in the years after the Sundering, swearing mutual keeping: dragonfire to warm the valleys and burn the blight from the fields, human song to feed the flames, for fire that is loved burns longer than fire that is feared. I showed them the choir — thousands of voices, generations of them, keeper families passing the verses down like heirlooms, the Ravensworths among them, smuggling not goods but *song*, carrying embers from shrine to shrine in sealed lanterns while kings fought over borders that would not outlive their grandsons.

Then I showed them the breaking.

Not as the histories told it — dragons turning proud, demanding worship, devouring the unfaithful. I showed them the truth: kings who grew tired of sharing authority with anything they could not tax. Priests of the new Concord whispering that the old gods were demons in scale. And then the purges — oh, the purges. I showed them the roosts burning. I showed them eggs, cracked open by men chanting holy words, the unborn dragons inside them still warm, still dreaming. I showed them the last great flight south, my own wings among them, wounded, carrying what few of us remained away from a world that had decided love was heresy.

And I showed them what happened after. The slow dimming. The shrines going cold one by one as the keeper lines died or hid or forgot. The winters deepening. The blights taking root in ground that no longer had fire under it. Three hundred years of a world slowly starving itself of its own warmth, and calling the hunger piety.

The vision ended. The canyon was very quiet.

Some of them were weeping. Some were staring at their pikes as though the iron had suddenly become a question rather than an answer. One young soldier — I marked him, a farm-boy's hands, chapped knuckles — dropped his weapon entirely and sat down in the dirt, and his sergeant did not correct him, because the sergeant was crying too.

But Serane Voss stood in the center of it all with her sword drawn and her spine straight, and she said:

"Clever. Illusion is the oldest trick of devils."

* * *

There it is, I thought. There is the wall.

I could have shown her more. I could have poured truth into her until she drowned in it. But I had lived long enough to learn what Dominic was only beginning to understand: that certainty is not ignorance. Certainty is a fortress, and it is built, always, over a wound. You do not breach it by shouting over the walls. You find the wound.

"You lost someone," I said.

Her sword-arm twitched. Just barely. "What?"

"In the town with the shrine. The one you burned. There was a girl — eight years old, fevered — and her mother begged you to let the hedge-witch treat her, and you refused, because the witch's craft was idolatry, and the girl died in the night." I watched her face with the patience of mountains. "You tell yourself the child would have died anyway. Perhaps she would have. But you did not burn that shrine because of doctrine, Serane Voss. Doctrine is the armor. You burned it because if the old ways had power — real power, healing power — then your refusal killed that child for nothing. And you could not live in a world where it was for nothing. So you made the world into one where the old ways were lies, and the death was tragedy, and tragedy needs no forgiveness."

"Stop." Her voice cracked on the word. Actually cracked. Around her, soldiers were looking at their inquisitor with expressions I had not seen on human faces in a very long time — the expression of people watching a statue discover it is made of clay.

"I do not say this to shame you," I said, and I softened my voice to something almost gentle, which for a dragon is a strange and dangerous instrument. "I say it because I know your wound. I carried its twin for three hundred years. If the humans were right to break covenant — if we deserved the purges, if the silence was justice — then every year of my dying was *deserved*, and I could rest. Despair is just grief wearing armor, little inquisitor. I wore mine until a smuggler's daughter put her hand on an altar and offered me her name. And you—" I inclined my head, the smallest courtesy, "—you are being offered yours right now. Not by me. By the truth. It is standing in front of you, and it is asking whether you would rather be right or be whole."

For a long moment — ten heartbeats, twenty — Serane Voss stood absolutely still.

Then she looked down at her sword.

I did not make her drop it. Understand that. Whatever the survivors' accounts claim, whatever the songs will eventually say — I did not compel her, did not bend her mind, did not touch her will. She looked at the blade she had carried through twenty years of purges, the blade that had opened shrines and, once, in a valley town, closed a fevered child's chance at dawn — and she saw it clearly for perhaps the first time.

The sword fell from her hand. The sound of it striking stone rang through the canyon like a bell.

She did not kneel. I want that recorded too, wherever my words survive. She did not kneel, did not weep, did not recant in the fashion of the penitents she had spent her life manufacturing. She simply stood there, empty-handed, in the ruins of the only story she had ever had, breathing. It was, I thought, the bravest thing anyone had done in that canyon all day — braver than Ysolde at the gate, though Ysolde's death was the finer song. Dying for belief is common. Standing upright inside the death of belief is rare as dragonflight.

Around her, the army came apart — gently, mostly. A third of them knelt, weapons laid flat before them, the old gesture of covenant-oath, learned somehow from grandmothers and forbidden lullabies. More simply turned and walked, dropping pikes as they went, streaming out of the canyon toward a world they would have to relearn. A hard core — perhaps thirty, officers mostly, men whose careers were nailed to the Inquisition's mast — kept formation and kept their blades, but they kept them low, and their captain kept glancing at Voss for orders that did not come.

Voss finally spoke. Her voice was scraped raw, quiet, almost private.

"What happens to us now?"

"That," I said, "depends entirely on what you do next. Fire does not hold grudges, Serane Voss. But it remembers everything." I lifted my head and let my gaze travel across the kneeling, the fleeing, the stubborn few. "You came here to end a plague of idols. The idols are awake, and the plague was never ours. Go home. Tell your kingdoms what you saw. Tell them the covenant stands open — not to crowns, not to armies, but to any hand willing to tend a flame without owning it. And tell them the price of the last three hundred years, so that no king ever again mistakes silence for consent."

I spread my wings. The wind of them knocked the nearest officers from their feet.

"And go home by the low road," I added, almost as an afterthought, "because the high passes belong to my kin again, and they are *young*, and they have not yet learned restraint."

That got through to them where nothing else had. The remaining thirty broke.

* * *

Afterward, when the canyon had emptied of everything but ash and abandoned weapons and one white inquisitor still standing alone in the middle of it, I felt her before I saw her — Dominic coming down from the roost gate at a run, Bramble hobbling behind her, Pellam limping worse, all of them alive, all of them stunned, none of them quite believing the absence of corpses.

Dominic stopped a few paces from Voss. The woman who had hunted her across half a kingdom. The woman who had taken Bramble hostage, burned the valley shrine, hanged the guild's name in effigy. I watched my chorister look at her enemy — broken, disarmed, hollowed out — and I watched the vengeance rise in her like a tide.

I did not intervene. This lesson was hers.

"You should be dead," Dominic said quietly. "By every law of mine, every grief I carry — Osk, Ysolde, the hall, all of it — you should be ash."

"Yes," Voss said. It was not defiance. It was agreement. That was somehow worse.

"But he wouldn't let you be." Dominic glanced up at me, and there was accusation in it, and gratitude, and exhaustion, all braided together. "Why? After everything — why show mercy?"

"Because mercy is not the opposite of judgment," I rumbled. "It is the heaviest form of it. Death would have been easy for her — martyrs are cheap, and the Inquisition mints them by the dozen. Living with the truth, unarmed, in a world that will soon know exactly what she served — *that* is a sentence worthy of the crimes." I settled onto my haunches, and the canyon stones sang under my weight. "Besides. I did not wake to make the world smaller, little chorister. I woke because you asked me to. Remember what you asked for."

Dominic was silent a moment. Then, slowly, she held out her hand — not in friendship, not yet, but in the oldest smuggler's gesture there is: *passage granted, terms pending.*

Voss looked at the hand for a long time. Then, with the careful movements of a woman learning her body again, she took it.

Above the canyon, far to the north, something answered the flame in my chest — a distant pulse, a second heartbeat, then a third. Not echoes. Answers. Other peaks. Other fires, catching.

Other keepers, waking.

And one of those pulses, faint but unmistakable, came from a direction that made my old heart clench: the drowned shrine-vaults beneath Caerlum's own mountain, where the guild's founders had hidden a flame no journal had ever mentioned — a flame that was now, unmistakably, *calling a Ravensworth home.*
