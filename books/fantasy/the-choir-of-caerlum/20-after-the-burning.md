## 20. After the Burning

The valley smelled of rain and char for six days after the dragons woke.

Bramble noticed it first because smell was her trade — hedge-witchery was mostly knowing what things were supposed to smell like and being honest when they didn't. The canyon had smelled of blood and scorched iron and the sweetish reek of burned wards. Now the rains came down off the peaks and washed it all into the river, and what rose up in its place was something she hadn't smelled since she was a girl: cold stone and hot resin, like a temple that had just been lit.

Like incense with nowhere to burn.

She stood in the mouth of the roost-canyon on that sixth morning, hands still bandaged from where her wards had failed her, and watched the pilgrims come.

They came on foot, at first. A shepherd from the high pastures who'd heard the sky sing. Then two women from Hollow Ford carrying bread wrapped in cloth — real bread, hoarded through the war winter, given away freely to strangers. By noon there were forty people in the canyon, kneeling in the mud among the petrified eggs, not praying so much as *listening*, the way you'd listen for a sound you half-remembered from childhood. Vaelith lay coiled in the deep of the canyon, young again and vast, her scales throwing back the grey light like hammered bronze, and she did not speak to any of them. She only breathed. That was enough. Bramble had seen grown men weep at less.

"You should eat."

Pellam set a bowl in her hands without asking. Oat porridge, scorched at the bottom the way he always scorched it, because a disgraced knight could hold a shield line against thirty soldiers and still couldn't work a cookfire. She ate it anyway. It tasted like being alive, which was more than she'd expected of anything this week.

"How is she?" Bramble asked, nodding toward the ledge where Dominic sat.

Pellam followed her gaze. Dominic sat cross-legged at the canyon's rim with her back straight and her hands open on her knees, and she had been sitting that way, more or less, since the fire caught. Not meditating exactly. Bramble wasn't sure there was a word for it. Listening with her whole body. The ember-glow behind her eyes had faded to almost nothing now, but sometimes, when the wind moved, Bramble would catch it again — a banked-coal shimmer, patient as a hearth.

"She sleeps two hours a night," Pellam said. "She talks to the dragon. Or the dragon talks to her. I can't tell which, and I've stopped trying." He paused. "She asked about you. Your hands."

"My hands are fine."

"They're not."

"No," Bramble admitted. "They're not." The wards she'd laid across the canyon gate had been the best work of her life, and they had lasted eleven minutes against Voss's siege-casters, and the backlash had gone up her arms like white fire. Her fingers still wouldn't close properly. She flexed them inside the bandages, feeling the wrongness of the tendons. "Wards fail. Wards always fail. I knew that when I laid them. Knowing it doesn't make it hurt less."

Pellam said nothing, which was one of the things she'd come to love about him. He'd learned, somewhere in the long ugly years between his knighthood and his disgrace, that silence offered to a witch was worth more than comfort.

Below them, a child — no older than seven, mud to her knees — pressed her palm flat against one of the petrified eggs. Bramble felt it before she saw it: a thread of warmth moving through the ground, gentle as a pulse under a wrist. The egg did not hatch. It would never hatch; it had been stone for three hundred years. But it warmed under the child's hand anyway, the way a sleeping dog leans into a touch, and the child laughed, startled and delighted, and the sound carried up the canyon walls and every person in the roost turned toward it as if toward sunlight.

"There's going to be trouble," Pellam said quietly.

"I know."

"Kingdoms don't like things waking up that they didn't wake themselves."

"I know that too." Bramble looked south, down the valley, where the road unspooled toward the lowlands and everything the lowlands contained. "But look at them, Pellam. Look at what they're doing. Nobody told them to come. Nobody paid them. They walked up a war-road into a dragon's canyon because their grandmothers' grandmothers remembered something, and now they remember it too." She shook her head slowly. "You can't cast that down. Well. You can try. People have tried worse."

They buried Ysolde and Osk on the ninth day, together, on the shelf of green stone above the canyon gate where the morning sun reached first.

There was no priest to officiate, because there had been no priests for three hundred years, and the one priest the world now had was standing at the graveside with dirt on her boots and grief in her jaw, saying she didn't know the words yet. So Bramble made the words. That was what hedge-witches were for — births and deaths and the small ceremonies in between, the ones too humble for temples.

Ysolde went into the ground wrapped in her own traveling cloak, the old priesthood's sigil — a flame within a circle — stitched at the hem in thread gone silver with age. Bramble had mended that stitching twice on the road south, working by firelight while Ysolde slept, and had never once asked her why a remnant of a broken order kept walking toward a dying god with such terrible patience. Now she never would. That was the thing about death nobody warned you about, Bramble thought, shoveling the first earth. Not the dying. All the questions you'd been saving.

Osk they buried beside her, and this was harder, because Osk had been theirs.

He had no family to claim him — the guild had been his family, the way it had been hers, the way it collected people the way driftwood collects on a shore. Bramble laid his tracking-spear in the grave with him, and his gloves, and — after a long argument with herself — the little carved wooden bird he'd kept in his pocket for twenty years and never explained to anyone. Deaf as a stone since the fever took his hearing at nine years old, and he'd read the world through his feet better than any man Bramble had ever known. He had died covering their escape at the exchange, holding a tunnel mouth against soldiers, and he had died without fear, Pellam said, because Osk had never once in his life seemed to understand that fear applied to him.

Dominic spoke over the graves. Not the old liturgy — she said plainly she didn't have it — but the guild's words, the smuggler's words, the ones Ravensworth apprentices said over dead friends when there was no time and no safety:

*"The road remembers your feet. The door remembers your hand. What you carried, we carry. Where you went, we follow."*

Her voice cracked on the last line. Bramble took her hand — her bandaged, clumsy hand — and held it, and Dominic held back hard enough to hurt, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing else, and because both of them had learned in the last month that some silences are full to bursting.

Vaelith came to the burial.

Bramble hadn't known a dragon could move that quietly. One moment the canyon was empty of her; the next she was there above the graves, her head lowered, her breath stirring the loose earth, her great eye — an eye like a well of amber with a candle burning at the bottom of it — regarding the two mounds of fresh-turned soil. And then the dragon sang.

It was not loud. That was the astonishing part. It was barely louder than a hum, a vibration Bramble felt in her teeth and her breastbone and the damaged tendons of her hands, and it was the choir-song — or a fragment of it, a single line, the same line Dominic had carried down the length of the valley in her chest. A mourning verse, Ysolde might have called it, if Ysolde were alive to name it. The song said, in the wordless way songs say things: *you were ours, and you are not lost, and the road does go on.*

When it ended, the forty pilgrims in the canyon were on their knees, and Bramble was crying without any memory of having started, and Dominic stood very straight with tears running openly down her face and made no move to hide them.

"Three hundred years," Dominic said afterward, quietly, to Bramble alone. "Three hundred years of silence, and the first thing she sings is a funeral. For us. For *people*." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "We broke the covenant, Bramble. We walked out on them. And the first thing they do when they wake is grieve our dead."

"That's not weakness," Bramble said. "That's the whole point of them. That's what we forgot."

The envoys arrived on the twelfth day, and they were exactly as nervous as Bramble had predicted, which was a small satisfaction.

There were three of them, because apparently three kingdoms had managed to get riders into the valley within a fortnight, and none of them trusted the others enough to let them come alone. There was a Karthenian in good mail with a herald's sash and the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he hated. There was a Merrowan woman in scholar's robes who kept touching things and writing notes until Pellam gently removed her from the petrified eggs. And there was a priest of the Concordance — the new faith, the tidy one, the one built on the ashes of the old — who wore his god's symbol like a man wearing armor indoors.

They requested audience with "the dragon's representative." Vaelith declined to provide one, on the grounds that she was a dragon and not a petition-counter. They requested audience with "the local authority." There wasn't one. Eventually, through a process Bramble watched with something close to delight, everyone simply agreed that the scarred woman with the burned-out eyes and the ember-light behind them was the closest thing available, and Dominic received them on the green shelf above the graves, with Bramble at her shoulder and Pellam standing behind them both looking like exactly what he'd once been.

The Karthenian opened with flattery. The Merrowan opened with questions. The Concordant priest opened with scripture.

"The Church teaches," he said, "that the old gods were false gods, and their worship a corruption, and their passing a mercy—"

"Your Church teaches that," Dominic said. Not unkindly. That was the frightening thing about her these days, Bramble thought — she'd stopped raising her voice entirely. "And your Church taught it for three hundred years during which the mountains went dark and the winters got longer and nobody's prayers went anywhere. You're welcome to keep teaching it. But I'd think carefully before teaching it *here*." She gestured, one open hand, at the canyon: the eggs, the pilgrims, the distant gleam of bronze scales in the deep. "Because the god you're calling false is lying right there, and she can hear you, and she has already forgiven you once today."

The priest went pale. The Karthenian went thoughtful, which was worse. The Merrowan wrote something down.

What they settled on — after three days of talk that Bramble mostly slept through, warding her ruined hands and letting Dominic do the impossible thing she was apparently born to do — was a kind of truce. The kingdoms would recognize the roost as sacred ground, inviolate. In exchange, the dragons would answer no call to war, take no side, crown no king. Dominic dictated the terms herself, in plain speech, refusing every title the envoys tried to hang on her.

"And what are you, then?" the Karthenian asked finally, genuinely baffled, pen hovering. "For the record. We must write *something*."

Dominic considered for a long moment.

"A keeper," she said. "That's all. Write keeper."

*

On the seventeenth night, Bramble found her packing.

Not much packing. Dominic had never owned much — a smuggler's habits died hard — but she was folding her spare shirt with the particular care of someone leaving, and her grandmother's journal was already in the satchel, and Bramble felt the knowledge land in her chest like a dropped stone before a single word was spoken.

"No," Bramble said.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say you have to go north. To Caerlum. To the drowned vaults under the mountain." Bramble's voice came out rougher than she intended. "I've watched you sit with that pull in you for two weeks, Dom. You think I can't see it? You reach north every time you stop thinking. Like a compass needle. Like a wound."

Dominic sat down on the ledge beside her. Below, the canyon glowed faintly with cookfires and votive candles — actual candles, actual prayers, offered up by people who three weeks ago would have called it idolatry and meant it. The world was changing shape around them, fast as a landslide, and here was one more piece of it coming loose.

"The flame under Caerlum is calling a Ravensworth," Dominic said quietly. "Me. I'm the last one. If I don't answer—"

"It'll wait."

"It's waited three hundred years. That's rather the opposite of my point."

Bramble looked at her hands. The bandages came off tomorrow, or the day after; the fingers were healing crooked, and she'd have to relearn her knotwork, her sigils, the fine quick gestures that wards were made of. Everything was relearning now. Everything was crooked and new.

"I buried two of us this month," she said. "I'm not going to pretend I have a clever argument. I just — the guild is gone, Dom. The hall's ash, the name's burned, the roads are closed. You're the only thing left that I—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Don't go alone."

Dominic was quiet a moment. Then she smiled — the old smile, the smuggler's smile, wry and warm and slightly crooked, the first one Bramble had seen since the fire.

"Pellam already volunteered. He's been oiling his armor for three days and pretending it's habit." She bumped her shoulder against Bramble's. "And I wasn't going to ask you, because of your hands. But I'm not going to tell you no either. I learned that lesson watching Ysolde walk toward a dying god. You don't tell keepers no. You walk with them."

"Then we walk," Bramble said.

Far below, in the deep of the canyon, Vaelith raised her head, and the choir-song moved through the stone — soft, brief, and this time not mourning. This time it sounded, to Bramble's witch's ear, remarkably like a blessing granted to travelers.

North, then. Toward the drowned vaults. Toward whatever her grandmother's journal had never dared to write down.

Toward home, though none of them had ever been there.
