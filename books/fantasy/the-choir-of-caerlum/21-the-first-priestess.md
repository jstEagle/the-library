## 21. The First Priestess

The envoys arrived on the ninth day after the burning, when the ash in the canyon had settled into something like snow and the survivors had stopped flinching at every wingbeat overhead.

There were three of them. A herald from the Crown of Vellmark in a coat so heavy with embroidery he could barely bend to dismount. A churchman from the Concordat of the Nine, gray-robed and careful, who kept looking up at the sky as if expecting Vaelith to land on him personally. And a woman in traveling leathers with no insignia at all, which marked her, Dominic thought, as the only dangerous one of the three.

They came up the canyon trail on foot because their horses refused it — had refused it since the morning the dragons woke, every mount in three valleys screaming and hauling at its tether until someone thought to simply let them go. The animals remembered what people had spent three centuries forgetting. That was a lesson, Dominic reflected, that the kingdoms might have taken to heart before sending men in silk.

She met them at the mouth of the roost, where Ysolde's cairn stood and Osk's beside it, two mounds of river stone under a sky that still smelled faintly of dragonfire. Bramble stood at her shoulder. Pellam leaned on his new crutch, his burned leg wrapped in Bramble's green-scented bandages, and said nothing, which for Pellam was a kind of discipline.

The herald unrolled a scroll with a flourish that would have been impressive anywhere but a canyon full of petrified eggs.

"By grace of the Regent's Council of Vellmark," he read, "and in recognition of services rendered unto the realm in the matter of the Restoration—"

"The Restoration," Dominic repeated.

The churchman cleared his throat. "The return of the gods is a matter of considerable theological interest," he said delicately. "The Concordat would prefer to manage that interest rather than be managed by it."

"You want to put me in a collar."

"We want to give you a cathedral," the herald said, wounded.

* * *

They had been offering things for nine days, actually. Not all of them in person. The first offer had come written, sealed with the Vellmark crest: a pardon for every crime ever laid against the Ravensworth guild, retroactive and total, plus a stipend, plus a house in the capital with a view of the harbor. The second had come from the Concordat: reinstatement of the old office of Keeper-General, a title extinct since the Breaking, with vestments and authority over all shrines restored or yet to be restored. The third had been verbal, delivered by a very nervous captain of militia: the valley towns, collectively, wished to make her a freeholder of Thornfell, with lands and toll-rights and a seat at the winter council.

Bramble had laughed herself sick over the house in the capital. "You'd last four days," she'd said. "Five if they gave you a cellar."

Pellam hadn't laughed. Pellam had sat by the fire turning his cup in his hands and finally said, "Take the pardon, at least. Take it for the guild. For the names."

And that was the thing, wasn't it. The guild's name was already gone. She had fed it to the fire herself, willingly, gladly, in the moment when the rite asked what she was and she answered *Ravensworth* and meant it like a knife laid on an altar. There was no parchment that could hand it back. The Ravensworths had been keepers pretending to be thieves for three hundred years, and now the pretense was over, and the name had gone where all true things eventually go — into the flame.

"I need to say this once," she told the envoys now, "so we can all stop wasting each other's time."

The herald lowered his scroll.

"Vaelith didn't wake because I'm important," Dominic said. "Vaelith woke because the covenant was kept. That's all. Someone carried the fire when carrying it cost everything, and someone offered what the rite required, freely, and the god answered. If you build a cathedral around that, you'll get a building. You won't get a god. Gods don't live in buildings. They never did. That's the whole reason your Concordat exists — because people got tired of walking uphill."

The churchman's jaw tightened. "Madam, the common folk are frightened. They need structure. They need—"

"They need shrines," Dominic said. "Little ones. On hillsides. With a bowl for embers and room enough to kneel. Your grandmother knew how to tend one even if you don't. Start there."

"And you?" It was the woman in the plain leathers who spoke, the first time she'd opened her mouth. Her eyes were sharp and unhurried. "What will you do? The realms will not leave a vacuum. Someone will claim the office. If not you, then someone worse."

Dominic looked past her, up the canyon, to where the light came off Vaelith's resting-place like heat off summer stone. The old dragon slept most hours now, healing, growing young in ways that hurt to watch — scales smoothing, wings filling out, the vast ribs rising and falling with breaths that shook loose small avalanches of petrified dust. Sometimes at dusk she sang. Just a few notes. Testing the range of a voice three centuries unused. Every living thing in the valley went quiet to listen, including, once, the militia captain who'd come to deliver the freeholder offer; he'd stood in the road with tears running into his beard and no idea why.

"There is no office," Dominic said. "That's the point. The covenant doesn't need an administrator. It needs keepers. People who belong to no crown and no guild and no Concordat, so that nobody can ever again do what my family did — hide the truth inside an institution until the institution eats it." She turned back to the envoys. "I'm going to walk. I'm going to carry embers to every dead shrine between here and the sea, and light them, and teach whoever shows up how to keep them lit. And when I die, someone else will pick it up, because that's how a choir works. No soloists."

"A beggar-priestess," the herald said, with the particular horror of a man imagining his own wardrobe.

"Worse," Dominic said. "A smuggler-priestess. I know every back trail in three mountain ranges, I can move cargo through a war, and I've spent my whole life learning how to keep secrets from people exactly like you." She smiled, and saw the woman in leathers smile back, unwillingly. "The faith will travel fine."

* * *

They left the next morning, taking their offers with them. But the woman in plain leathers lingered at the cairns, and when Dominic came down at dawn to sit with Ysolde and Osk the way she did every day, the woman was there, standing bareheaded in the cold.

"My name is Maren Corvane," she said. "I serve no crown either. Or rather — I serve several, briefly, and lie to all of them. I find things. People, mostly." She studied the cairns. "Ysolde was my aunt."

Dominic went very still.

"She left the priesthood before the last purge. Married a cartwright. Told no one what she'd been." Maren touched the top stone of Ysolde's cairn, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "When the rumors started coming south — coins that hummed, a shrine that answered — I knew whose work it was. I came north to find her." Her voice caught, just barely, and smoothed itself flat. "I was three days too late for the gate."

"I'm sorry."

"So am I." Maren turned. Under the gray dawn light her face was ordinary and her eyes were not. "So here is what I know, priestess. Within the year, every throne from here to the coast will have sent someone to find you. Some will come with titles. Some will come with soldiers. A few will come with both, wearing each other's clothes." She held out a folded paper. "This is a list of names. People in six courts who owe me favors. When the wolves come, send word, and I'll slow them down. Consider it a debt owed to my aunt, paid to her choirmaster."

Dominic took the paper. "Why help?"

Maren looked up the canyon, to where the morning light was beginning to burn gold off the high walls, and for a moment her composure slipped entirely, and underneath it was just a tired woman who had walked a long way to stand at her aunt's grave.

"Because I heard her sing," she said. "At the gate. Before the end. I was on the ridge above, and I heard it, and I have not slept properly since." She pulled her hood up. "Keep walking, priestess. Light your fires. And when they ask who sent you — tell them nobody did. That's the safest thing anyone can be."

She was gone down the trail before the sun cleared the pass.

* * *

They left a week later. There was no ceremony. Vaelith would have made one, probably — the old dragon had developed, to everyone's alarm, a taste for drama — but Dominic asked her not to, and Vaelith had grumbled smoke and agreed, because the covenant ran both ways and a keeper's wishes weighed real weight now.

Instead, on the last morning, the dragon lowered her head — the head that could have taken a farmhouse in her jaws — down to the level of the trail, and breathed across Dominic's hands, slow and warm, and in the breath was a coal. Small. Bright. Wrapped in a curl of scale-thin membrane that pulsed like a heartbeat.

"Ember-mother," Vaelith said. Her voice was better now, deeper and younger, but she still spoke slowly, savoring words like a miser savoring coin. "First of the new choir. Carry this. Where it is welcomed, it will grow. Where it is refused—" the great eye blinked, ancient amusement in the depths of it, "—it will keep you warm regardless."

"Is that a blessing or a joke?"

"Yes," said Vaelith.

Bramble came up the trail with her pack already shouldered, her hedge-witch's staff in hand, her gray hair tied back for travel. She had not hesitated for a single hour when Dominic told them she was leaving. "Well, obviously," Bramble had said. "You think I'm staying here with the lizards? Someone has to keep you fed and patched, and you're hopeless with wounds, you always bind them too tight." Behind her came Pellam, limping, stubborn, his knight's oath long since broken and something better forged in its place. He'd argued harder — not against going, but about whether he deserved to. Dominic had ended it the way she ended most arguments now, by looking at him until he stopped.

"You gave your confession," she'd told him. "It was accepted. That's the entire theology. Come on."

Osk stayed. Osk and Ysolde both stayed, under their stones, at the mouth of the roost where the wind came through singing faintly off the canyon walls — and if you stood there at dusk, really stood and listened, you could almost hear voices under the wind, harmonizing with the peaks. Bramble swore it was acoustics. Bramble also left a sprig of rowan on each cairn every morning, so draw your own conclusions.

Dominic set the ember carefully in the iron lantern at her belt — guild-forged, smuggler-dark, the last piece of Ravensworth craft she owned — and felt it settle against her hip like a second heart.

"Where first?" Pellam asked.

Dominic looked south, down the long valley, toward the lowlands where the war had passed through and left its usual harvest of burned villages and bitter survivors. Somewhere down there was a shrine her grandmother's journal had mentioned, a hilltop chapel with a cold hearth, abandoned two hundred years. Somewhere beyond it were a hundred more. Beyond those, the coast. Beyond that, who knew.

"Downhill," she said. "It's always downhill. Then uphill again. That's the whole job."

* * *

They crossed the first pass at evening, the three of them, small on the white flank of the world. Below, the valleys lay in blue shadow, and here and there a woodsmoke thread rose from some village that did not yet know what was coming down the mountain toward it — not conquest, not plague, just a limping woman with a lantern and two ragged companions and a story older than any kingdom that had ever tried to own it.

Dominic paused at the crest and looked back once. The canyon glowed faintly in the distance, the way a hearth glows through a shuttered window. Vaelith's song reached her even here, thin with distance, a few notes held against the dark.

And then — she stopped. Because under the dragon's voice, far away and faint, another note rose. From the east. From a peak she had no name for. Answering.

Then another, from the south. Then a third, wavering, uncertain, unmistakably human — someone, somewhere, singing back without knowing why.

Bramble heard it too. So did Pellam. None of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the mountains weren't already saying better.

Dominic turned south, touched the lantern at her hip, and started down.

The choir was larger than she'd known. It was time to go find the rest of it.
