## 22. Choir Without End

A year, and Dominic had learned to measure distance in verses.

The pass above Caerlum's shoulder — the locals called it the Widow's Tooth now, though it had been something older before the dragons woke and names started shifting like weather — was three verses high. She'd counted them on the climb, matching her footfalls to the song that never quite stopped anymore. It came up through the stone itself these days, faint as breath on a mirror, and every shrine she'd relit along the way made it a little louder.

She crested the saddle at dawn because Bramble insisted dawn was when the wards were thinnest and Pellam insisted dawn was when his knees were quietest, and both of them were liars who just liked watching the light come down the valley. So did she. That was the thing about family nobody warned you: you inherited their habits like debts.

The valley below was green. A year ago it had been ash and mud and the black scars of Voss's fires, and before that it had been war-torn for a generation, and before that it had simply been forgotten. Now there were terraces on the south slopes, and smoke from chimneys that hadn't smoked in twenty years, and — she squinted — someone had built a shrine at the river bend. A small one, rough-hewn, with a stone basin that caught the first light and held it like water.

"That's the fourth this month," Pellam said, coming up beside her. He carried his sword slung across his back out of habit rather than need; the blade hadn't been drawn since the canyon, and she suspected it never would be again. He seemed lighter without it. Or maybe he'd just finally put down the weight of Hollow Ford. "People are building faster than we can bless them."

"We're not blessing anything," Dominic said. "We're lighting lamps."

"You know what I mean."

She did. That was the trouble with Pellam — he always knew what she meant, even when she wished he wouldn't. A year ago he'd tried to give his life for hers at an altar in the mountain's heart, and she'd dragged him back from the rite half-dead and furious, and they'd fought about it for two hundred miles until Bramble threatened to ward their bedrolls to opposite ends of camp. Now he walked beside her with the calm of a man who had been forgiven something enormous and had decided to spend the rest of his life earning it anyway, whether or not anyone was keeping count.

Bramble came up last, as always, leaning on her staff and complaining about the altitude, the cold, the state of her boots, and the general moral character of mountains. She was seventy-one years old and had survived a hostage-taking, a siege, and a year of sleeping rough, and she complained about all of it daily with the vigor of a woman who intended to keep surviving indefinitely out of spite.

"Your lantern's guttering," she said.

Dominic looked down. The ember-lantern at her hip — the one that held a splinter of Caerlum's flame, the first fire she'd carried out of the mountain — was indeed burning low. Not dying. Ember-fire didn't die; it waited. But it wanted tending, and tending meant finding a shrine, and finding a shrine meant another valley, another village, another knot of frightened hopeful faces asking her if the gods would forgive them.

"They will," she always told them. "That was never the question. The question is whether you'll forgive yourselves."

It went over better than she expected. People were hungry for permission in a way that broke her heart a little every time.

* * *

They ate breakfast on the summit with the whole world spread out below them, hard bread and hard cheese and tea Bramble brewed from pine needles because she refused to buy proper leaves while "the price of everything east of Thornfell remains an insult." And it was there, chewing and shivering and watching the sun clear the eastern peaks, that Dominic heard it.

Not with her ears. She'd learned the difference over the year. Ears heard wind and birdsong and Pellam humming off-key. The choir came through the sternum, through the teeth, through the old altar-scar on her palm where the flame had marked her.

A note rose from the peak due north — a real peak, a named one, Mount Sarelle, where she'd lit a shrine in a shepherd's hut eight months ago. The shepherd's daughter had been singing it ever since. Dominic could pick her voice out of the choir now, bright and slightly flat, the way a fourteen-year-old's voice is.

Then one from the west, from the coast road, from a lighthouse keeper who had never met Dominic but had dreamed the verse and woken up singing.

Then — and this was new, this made her set down her tea — one from far to the southeast, beyond the map's edge, beyond anywhere she'd walked. Thin. Distant. But there.

And then another. And another. A dozen peaks, maybe more, each one carrying its own small flame, each flame carrying the song, the voices overlapping and weaving until the whole horizon seemed to hum.

"More," Bramble said quietly. She'd gone still, staff across her knees, her weathered face turned toward the sound she couldn't properly hear but could feel, the way she felt wards. "There are more keepers than we thought."

"There were always more keepers than we thought," Dominic said. "We just thought we were the only ones left standing."

Pellam was smiling. He didn't smile easily, even now, but when the choir swelled like this he couldn't help it. "Ysolde would have—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Ysolde said the covenant was never broken. Only forgotten."

"Ysolde said a lot of things," Dominic said, "dying holding a gate so the rest of us could live."

The grief came the way it always did now — not a knife, not anymore, but a weight she'd learned to carry on the strong side. Osk too. Osk, who had died in the snow outside the guildhall covering their escape, deaf to the last, feeling the choir through his feet the way he'd felt everything. They'd buried him and Ysolde together in the valley below Caerlum, under a cairn that pilgrims had already covered in offerings: coins, bread, carved birds, a child's mitten. The dead kept better company than the living sometimes.

"The southeastern one," Bramble said. "That's new since midwinter."

"I heard."

"It's weak. Whoever's tending that flame is doing it alone, and badly."

"I heard that too."

Bramble looked at her over the steam of her tea. "You're going."

"I'm going."

"And we're coming," Pellam said, in the tone of a man stating that rivers ran downhill.

Dominic didn't argue. She'd stopped arguing about it around the third month, when she'd realized that exile wasn't exile if you brought your family with you. It was just a long road with good company.

* * *

They descended into the valley by the pilgrim road, which was really just a goat track that people had started walking in procession, and by afternoon they reached the river-shrine Dominic had spotted from the ridge. A woman was there — young, sunburned, kneeling with her hands in the basin, whispering the verse wrong. Not badly wrong. Just wrong enough that the flame she'd coaxed from a tinderbox wasn't catching the way it should.

Dominic knelt beside her without a word and cupped her hands around the ember. Breathed on it. Sang the correction — two notes, no more. The flame stood up straight and gold, and the woman gasped like she'd been underwater.

"I didn't know who to ask," she said. "There's no one— my grandmother used to sing it but she died and I only remembered half and I thought maybe the gods didn't want—"

"They want," Dominic said. "They've always wanted. That was the whole tragedy — everyone assumed silence meant refusal."

The woman stared at her. "You're her. The priestess. The one from the stories."

"The stories are exaggerated."

"They say you burned your own name to wake a dragon."

Dominic touched the scar on her palm. "The stories got that part right."

What the stories mostly got wrong was what it cost, and what it bought. She had given the rite her name — Ravensworth, smuggler, last chorister, all of it — and the flame had accepted identity instead of blood, and she had walked out of the mountain as no one at all, which turned out to be exactly what the covenant required. A keeper who belonged to no crown, no guild, no house. Free to be given entirely.

She'd expected to feel hollowed. Instead she felt, for the first time in her life, unencumbered. The guild was dissolved — its name struck, its hall burned by her own hand, its ledger closed. But its people were here, walking beside her, arguing about tea prices and boot leather, and it had taken her a shamefully long time to understand that the guild had never been the walls or the name or the routes through Thornfell. It had been Osk teaching her to read tracks. Bramble's terrible soup. Pellam standing between her and a firing line. Ysolde's coins, warm and humming, spent on nothing more than passage and trust.

Survival was never the prize. She'd thought it was, back when survival meant one more run through the passes, one more winter, one more job. She'd grieved the guild like a corpse. But belonging — belonging was the thing that had been worth everything, and belonging didn't burn.

* * *

They camped that night in the shrine-woman's barn, at her insistence, and after supper Dominic sat outside under a sky crowded with stars and took out her grandmother's journal one more time.

The pages were soft as cloth now from handling. Her grandmother — the last Ravensworth keeper before the long silence — had written in a cramped hand, half cipher, half prayer, and Dominic had spent a year learning to read between the lines. Tonight she found a passage she'd skimmed a dozen times and never understood:

*The Choir is not one voice. It was never one voice. We kept the mountain because ours was the loudest, not the only. If you are reading this and the fire has failed, do not despair. Count the dark peaks. Sing anyway. Someone is always singing back.*

Someone is always singing back.

She'd heard it today. A dozen answers, and one of them thin and struggling and alone, somewhere past the edge of every map she owned.

"Dominic." Pellam settled onto the step beside her, offering a cup of Bramble's pine-needle tea like a peace treaty. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one you had before Thornfell. Before the cellar door. Before all of it." He sipped. "The look of a woman about to walk toward something dangerous because it needs walking toward."

"It's not dangerous. It's a valley and a bad singer."

"Everything's dangerous. That's why we go in pairs." He nodded toward the barn, where Bramble's snores were already rattling the boards. "Three, actually. She'll say she's coming for the wards and then complain the entire way."

"She always does."

"She always will." Pellam was quiet a moment. Then: "Do you ever miss it? The name. Being Ravensworth."

Dominic considered it honestly, because he deserved honesty. "I miss my grandmother. I miss Osk. I don't miss the name. Names are what other people call you so they can stop looking." She turned the journal's page to the sketch in the margin — her grandmother's drawing of a dozen peaks, each one marked with a tiny flame. "I'm not nobody, Pellam. I'm just not anybody's. There's a difference."

He laughed softly. "Say that to the kingdoms sending envoys. Three crowns have offered you land and a title. The High Temple wants to make you a bishop of something they invented last spring."

"The covenant doesn't fit inside a title."

"No," he agreed. "But it fits in a lantern. And a barn full of strangers singing badly. And a goat track full of pilgrims." He bumped her shoulder with his. "You built a church out of walking, Dom. Don't let the ones in palaces tell you it isn't one."

* * *

They left at first light, as they always did, three figures on the south road with a guttering lantern and a journal full of dark peaks.

At the top of the first rise Dominic stopped and did what she did every morning now: she sang the opening verse into the wind. Her voice wasn't remarkable. It didn't need to be. The covenant had never been about talent.

From the north, the shepherd's girl answered, bright and flat.

From the west, the lighthouse keeper, steady as surf.

From a dozen peaks, the weave and swell of voices she'd never meet, tending flames she might never see, bound to her by nothing but a song and a promise older than any kingdom.

And from the southeast — faint, wavering, alone — the voice that needed her most.

Dominic smiled, touched the lantern, and turned her face down the long road.

Behind her, the mountains sang. Ahead of her, someone was waiting to learn the next verse.

Beside her — always beside her — her family kept pace, complaining cheerfully about the boots.

The choir, she thought, had never once needed walls.

Neither did they.
