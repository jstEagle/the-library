## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink froze first.

Edmund Reyes had learned to read the valley's moods through his materials — the way the lamp oil thickened in autumn, the way the vellum drank moisture from the air in spring, and above all the way the ink behaved when the cold came down off the pass like something with intent. Tonight it was freezing at the nib, a skin of black forming across the well of his horn every forty breaths, so that he had to pause mid-word, hold the horn over the brazier's coals, and wait for the world to become writable again.

He was copying the temple's grain ledgers for the third year running. Barley in, barley out. Tithes rendered to the abbey storehouse against winter. The debts of forty-one families, entered in a hand so small and even that Abbot Corvane had once held a page to the light and said, *You write like a man afraid of being heard.* Edmund had laughed, because it was expected, and because the abbot had meant nothing by it. He had not laughed on the inside, where a colder voice had answered: *You are right to be afraid.*

The scriptorium of Highmoor occupied the temple's eastern cloister, a long room of whitewashed stone with windows tall enough to catch the last grey light off the mountains. Beyond those windows lay the terraced fields, the orchards going bare, the slate roofs of the village huddled below the abbey walls like chicks under a hen. And beyond all of it, closing the valley at its northern throat, the pass — a notch of pale sky between two shoulders of rock, the only road in or out of Highmoor, and the reason Edmund had never seen anything else.

Twenty-six years old, born in this valley, bound to this desk. He knew the shape of the world chiefly as other people described it: peddlers who came up the pass twice a year with salt and iron and news already a season stale; pilgrims who climbed the switchbacks to touch the shrine of Saint Aldhelm the Silent; the abbot's own memories, which he dispensed sparingly, like a man pouring wine he could not afford. The world beyond the pass was a country made of talk. Edmund had built his whole life inside a sentence someone else had begun.

He set down his pen and flexed his fingers. They ached in the cold, and beneath the ache there was the other thing — the thing that was not ache at all but attention, a faint electric hum in the bones of his hands, as though his fingers were tuning forks struck by some sound too low for ears. It came most often in the evenings. He had learned, over twenty years of practice, to treat it the way one treats an itch in company: acknowledged, never scratched.

Tonight it was worse than usual. Tonight the hum had a direction.

It pulled faintly toward the pass.

Edmund sat very still and listened to the fire tick in the brazier, and told himself what he always told himself: *There is nothing in you. There is nothing in anyone here. The valley sees to that.* Every child in Highmoor learned the catechism early — *the valley keeps us, the valley keeps us whole* — though no one explained what it kept them from, or how, or why the shrine at the valley's mouth bore, instead of a saint's image, a plain slab of grey stone worn smooth by centuries of touching hands. Pilgrims called it the Quiet Stone. They said prayers to it. The priests let them believe it was holy.

Edmund had touched it once, at seven, on a festival day, pressed his palm flat against its cold surface while his mother held his shoulder. And he remembered — he had never forgotten, though he had spent nineteen years trying — the sensation of something in him going quiet. Not peaceful. *Quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when a dangerous guest finally leaves it. His mother's grip had tightened. She had smiled at the priest. She had never spoken of it again, and she had died three winters later still smiling, still silent, and Edmund had carried the memory of that silence the way other men carry scars: proof of a wound he could not show anyone.

*You are a scribe,* he told himself. *You copy ledgers. You owe the temple eleven years' labor for your mother's care, and you will pay it, and you will die in this valley at a respectable age, and the hum in your hands is a trick of tired blood.*

The lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick and went back to the barley.

* * *

Morning brought frost and a stranger.

Edmund saw her first from the cloister walk, where he was carrying a fresh pot of ink from the storeroom — a duty he'd claimed years ago because it got him outside, into the thin gold light, for ten minutes a day. The pass road descended toward the gate in a long S-curve, and on it, small with distance, a single figure climbed. Not a peddler; peddlers came with mules. Not a pilgrim either, or not the usual kind — pilgrims came in twos and threes, in families, singing the climbing hymns to keep their courage up on the exposed stretches. This figure came alone, on foot, moving with a stride that ate ground rather than admired it, and wearing a traveler's grey cloak with the hood back, which no pilgrim did, because the wind at the top of the pass could take the skin off your face.

Brother Anselm was already at the gate when Edmund drifted near, ostensibly to return the empty ink pot to the outer storeroom, actually to look. Anselm was the temple's porter, sixty years old, built like the doorframe he leaned against, and he watched the stranger come with an expression Edmund had never seen him wear. It took Edmund a moment to name it. Wariness. Old wariness, dusted off from somewhere deep.

"She'll want bread and a bed," Anselm said, not looking at him. "They always do."

"Is the inn not open?"

"The inn is open." Anselm's jaw worked. "But she asked me, through the grate, whether the temple keeps records of births. Whether we register everyone born in the valley, and everyone who comes to stay." He turned then, and his eyes were flat and troubled. "That's a strange question for a pilgrim, boy."

"It's a strange question," Edmund agreed. His heart had begun doing something slow and heavy, like a bell rung underwater.

The stranger reached the gate as they spoke. Up close she was younger than her stride had suggested — thirty, perhaps, weathered rather than aged, with short dark hair going grey at the temples and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever been permitted to relax. Her cloak was good wool, travel-worn. Her boots were army boots; Edmund knew them from the militia musters, the double-stitched sole, the iron eyelets. She looked at Anselm, then at Edmund, and her gaze snagged on his hands — on the ink stains, he assumed, though it felt oddly like being read — before returning to the porter.

"Serah Vane," she said. "Pilgrim. I've walked from Callowmere. I ask shelter under Saint Aldhelm's rule."

"Saint Aldhelm turns no one away," Anselm rumbled, which was true and also, Edmund thought, not entirely an answer. "You'll have bread and the pilgrims' loft. Vespers is at dusk; attendance is voluntary."

"And the records?" Serah Vane said. "I'm given to understand the temple keeps rolls. Births, deaths, debts. I have family questions. People who came through this valley, long ago. I'd hoped to consult your registers."

Anselm glanced at Edmund — a glance with weight in it, a glance that said *you're the scribe, this is yours* — and Edmund heard himself say, "The registers are open to any pilgrim who can read, sister. Though I should tell you our records only reach back a hundred years. Before that, the older books were lost in the fire."

There had been no fire. There had never been a fire. The lie came out of him smooth and complete, wearing the same even hand as his ledger script, and he did not know where it had come from, only that some instinct older than his fear had risen to meet this woman and had decided, without consulting him, to hide the door.

Something flickered across Serah Vane's face. Not suspicion. Recognition — the look of a person finding a marked trail in country they thought trackless.

"A hundred years," she repeated softly. "That's a pity. My family came through ninety years ago."

"Then you may be in luck after all," Edmund said.

She looked at him for a long moment. The wind came down the pass behind her and stirred the hem of her cloak, and somewhere above them a crow argued with the morning. "Thank you," she said at last. "Bread and the loft will do fine. And perhaps — later — a word with whoever knows these books best."

"That would be me," said Edmund Reyes, debt-bound scribe of Highmoor, keeper of barley accounts, man with no history and no gift and no future beyond the pass. "I know them best."

* * *

She came to the scriptorium at midday, when the other copyists had gone to the refectory, and Edmund understood immediately that she had chosen the hour deliberately. She moved through the room the way water moves through cracks — quietly, thoroughly, noting the shelves, the locked cabinet where the sealed registers lived, the window with its view of the pass road. She had taken off her cloak. Underneath she wore a plain wool tunic belted with leather, and the belt, Edmund noted, was the kind that could carry a sword, though it carried none now.

"You lied to me this morning," she said, without preamble. "About the fire."

Edmund kept his eyes on his page. "Did I?"

"There's no scorch mark on the abbey. No replacement stonework anywhere. A fire big enough to eat a hundred years of records leaves a scar." She stopped across the desk from him, and he made himself look up. Her eyes were the color of wet slate. "Why does a scribe lie to protect his own archives?"

"I don't know what you mean." But his hand, resting on the desk, had begun to hum — that low electric attention, stronger than it had ever been, as though the bones themselves were leaning toward her. He curled his fingers closed. *Stop. Be nothing. Be ink.*

"May I?" She gestured at the open ledger.

"It's grain accounts."

"I know barley from wheat. I grew up on a farm before—" She stopped herself. Something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Before I joined service."

"What service?"

"The crown's." She said it flatly, watching him. Testing the word the way a man tests ice.

Edmund felt the word land in the room and sit there between them, heavy and cold. The crown was a story in Highmoor, a face on old coins, a name in the harvest prayers — *and grant health to the king, long may he reign*. No tax collector had climbed the pass in Edmund's lifetime. No soldier. No edict. The valley kept itself, and the crown, whatever the crown was, kept its distance.

"The crown is far away," he said carefully.

"The crown is closer than you think." She drew out the bench opposite and sat, uninvited, and lowered her voice to something meant for him alone. "Scribe. I'm going to ask you three questions, and I want you to answer honestly, because I think your honesty may matter more than you know. First: has anyone from the capital ever come here? Hunters, inquisitors, men with instruments — anyone asking about gifts. About magic."

"No one comes here asking about anything. The valley—"

"—keeps you. Yes. I've heard the phrase." Her mouth twisted. "Second question. Have you ever felt it yourself? Even once. Even as a child. Something in you that shouldn't be there. A sound in your hands. A warmth behind your eyes. Anything."

The scriptorium was very quiet. The brazier ticked. Down in the cloister, Brother Peverel was sweeping, the rhythmic whisper of the broom the only sound in the world. Edmund became aware that he had stopped breathing, and made himself start again.

"Why would you ask me that?"

"Because when I said the word *fire* this morning, you didn't flinch like a liar. You flinched like a man guarding something." She leaned forward. "Third question, and then I'll leave you to your barley. What's under the shrine at the valley mouth? Under the Quiet Stone. Because I've stood on it, scribe, and I've felt what stands under it, and it isn't a saint."

The hum in his hands rose to a whine he could almost hear. The lamp flame between them stretched suddenly tall and thin, leaning toward her like a listener, and Edmund clamped down on — on what? On nothing, on the nothing that was all he was — and the flame settled, and Serah Vane's eyes went to it and stayed there, and when she looked back at his face her expression had changed entirely. The wariness was gone. In its place was something rawer. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"There it is," she whispered.

"There's nothing," Edmund said. His voice came out cracked. "This is Highmoor. Nothing follows anyone in here. Everyone says so. The valley—"

"The valley suppresses. Yes. I know exactly what it suppresses, and how, and why, and I promise you the answer will not comfort you." She rose, pulling her cloak from the chair-back, and for a moment she was every inch the soldier again, hard and certain and terribly awake. "I didn't come here as a pilgrim, Edmund Reyes. I came because this valley is on a list, and the list is real, and the people who wrote it are coming. Not today. Not this month. But they are coming, and when they arrive they will not ask about barley."

His heart was slamming. "Who's coming?"

"Hunters." She said the word the way other people said *plague*. "For the gifted. All of them. Everywhere." She paused at the end of the scriptorium, silhouetted against the tall window and the pale notch of the pass beyond, and the light made her face unreadable. "You felt something just now. Don't bother denying it — I watched the flame move. So here is my advice, freely given, from one stranger to another: tonight, when the abbey sleeps, you're going to remember every book in that locked cabinet. And then you're going to decide which side of that door you want to be standing on when it opens."

She left. The broom whispered in the cloister. The lamp burned steady and innocent, as lamps do, as though nothing in the world had ever leaned anywhere.

Edmund sat for a long time with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the hum in his bones pull northward, toward the pass, toward the wide and terrible world, like a tide answering a moon he had spent his whole life pretending not to see.

Then he opened the ledger, dipped his pen, and wrote, in his smallest and most even hand, beside the entry for the temple's winter stores, four words no ledger had ever held:

*She knows what I am.*

Outside, far up in the notch of the pass, so small it might have been a bird, a second figure had appeared on the road — descending now, walking fast, and behind it, faint on the wind, came a sound Edmund had never heard in the valley before and would not mistake for anything else as long as he lived.

Dogs. Many dogs. Coming down.
