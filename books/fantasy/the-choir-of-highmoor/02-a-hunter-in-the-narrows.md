## 2. A Hunter in the Narrows

The pass into Highmoor was a wound in the world, and Serah Vane had spent three days bleeding through it.

She climbed with her hood up and her head down, boots finding the switchbacks by memory of the survey maps she'd once drawn for the crown — maps that showed the valley as a blank, a smudge of nothing where the hunt's instruments read silence. *No resonance. No gift-signs. Nothing worth the crown's attention.* She had signed off on those maps herself, four years ago, in a neat clerk's hand she no longer recognized as her own.

Now she was climbing back into the blank to hide inside it.

The narrows tightened above her, walls of grey stone pressing close enough that she could have touched both sides at once. Wind funneled through with a low, keening note, and Serah flinched from it before she understood why. It was only wind. Only stone singing its dumb, tuneless song. But after Callowmere — after what she'd seen done to the weaver's daughter in the holding cells, after the sound the girl's gift had made as it was pulled out of her like a nail from green wood — every high thin noise sounded like an instrument being tuned.

*You built this,* she told herself, climbing. *Every rung of it. The ledgers. The routes. The questions.*

She had been the crown's finest hunt-captain because she understood the hunted better than they understood themselves. She knew how a gifted child's mother lied — badly, always, love making liars of careful women. She knew which neighbors reported and which stayed silent, and how long silence held before fear or coin pried it open. She had designed the interview protocols, the ledger cross-references, the quiet system of informants that turned every village against its own bloodlines. The purge had been her architecture before it was anyone's order.

And then, eleven days ago, in the archive beneath the Inquisition's tower, she had found the annex.

Not the hunt itself — she'd known the hunt, approved the hunt, believed in it down to her marrow: dangerous gifts must be found, catalogued, contained. That creed had carried her through fifteen years of pyres and paperwork without a single sleepless night. What she'd found was the *purpose*. A second set of orders, sealed in the Lord Inquisitor's cipher, routing captured gifted not to the containment prisons but to the capital. To the palace. To something below the palace, the orders said, that *fed*.

She had read the word twice before her mind let her hold it. *Fed.*

Eleven days ago, Serah Vane had stopped believing in her own work. Four days ago, she'd learned what happened to hunt-captains who asked about the annex: Captain Dole of the eastern circuit, whose resignation letter arrived the same morning his house burned with him in it. So she had taken her contingency purse, her forged pilgrim's papers, and the one thing she trusted more than any living person — her own memory of every road, garrison, and blind spot in the realm — and she had run for the valley that forgot.

Because if there was one place in all the kingdoms where the thing below the palace could not reach, it was here. And if she was going to decide what to do with what she knew, she would do it where nothing could listen.

* * *

Highmoor announced itself not with walls but with a change in the air.

Serah felt it as she came down off the last ridge: a softness, a muting, like stepping from a bright room into wool. The sounds of her own body — heartbeat, breath, the small grind of tired joints — seemed suddenly the loudest things in the world. She stopped on the pilgrim road and stood very still, listening to the silence the way a deaf woman might press her palm to a bell.

So it was true. The old stories, the survey blanks, the reason the hunt had never troubled this valley. Magic did not follow past the pass. Whatever ran beneath the skin of the gifted simply... quieted here. Went to sleep.

Her hand drifted, without her permission, to her own sternum.

Nothing. There had never been anything there — she'd been tested at seven, at thirteen, at her commissioning, three times pressed to the resonance plates and three times pronounced clean. Clean as slate. She had built her life on that cleanness; it was what let her look the hunted in the eye.

But lately — since the annex, since the flight, since a night in a shepherd's hut when she'd dreamed of fire answering her anger and woken with scorch marks on the blanket that she had explained away twice before running out of explanations — she had begun to wonder if the tests measured what she thought they measured. If clean was just another word for *asleep*.

Here, at least, it didn't matter. Here even a waking gift would lie down like a dog at the door. She adjusted her pack, arranged her face into the mild, footsore patience of a pilgrim, and walked down into Highmoor as the evening bells began.

* * *

The temple was older than the kingdom, grey stone gone silver with lichen, and it smelled of tallow smoke and cold water. Pilgrims were few this late in the season — a farmwife lighting candles for a sick son, two brothers of the road asleep in their cloaks by the hearth — and the brothers of the temple moved among them with the unhurried gravity of men who had never once needed to hurry.

Serah knelt at the votive rail and let the ritual wash over her. She had sat through a hundred interrogations wearing less convincing calm than she wore now. Kneel. Bow the head. Let them see piety, and piety is all they'll ever look for.

It was while she was rising, coins pressed into the offering box, that she saw the scribe.

He worked at a table near the scriptorium's open door, copying ledgers by the last of the daylight, and he was doing something with his hands that caught her hunter's eye the way a wrong note catches a musician's. Not fidgeting — she knew fidgeting, had catalogued it across a thousand interviews. This was *discipline*. His fingers moved along the edge of each page as he finished it, a slow deliberate pressure, thumb to forefinger, the way a man might steady himself. Or the way a man might hold something down.

She had seen that gesture exactly once before, years ago, in a village called Marrow's End. A cooper's son, sixteen, awaiting transport to the capital. He'd sat with his hands flat on the table through six hours of questioning, pressing down, pressing down, as though the words trying to climb out of his throat might otherwise shake the room apart. He'd never said a word about any gift. He hadn't needed to. His hands had confessed everything.

Serah crossed the nave slowly, telling herself she was looking because looking was habit, because a hunt-captain's eyes didn't retire just because the hunt had rotted out from under her. The scribe was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the kind of face that made itself easy to overlook — until you noticed the eyes, which were watching her watch him with a wariness far too old for his years.

"Pilgrim," he said, when she paused by the scriptorium door. Polite. Flat. The voice of a man performing small talk.

"You're working late," she said. "Even for a temple."

"The ledgers don't copy themselves." A small shrug. "Debt-work. I'm bound to the temple until my account is settled."

"Bound." She let the word sit. "That's a hard bargain, in a place like this."

"It's a safe bargain," he said, and there it was again — the emphasis laid so carefully over the word *safe*, the way a man lays a cloth over a birdcage. "In a place like this," he echoed her, and almost smiled. Almost. "There's nowhere safer in the world. Everyone says so."

"Everyone's right," Serah said. "I've traveled a long way to find somewhere everyone's right about."

Something flickered behind his eyes then — not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow fluent speaker of a language most people didn't know existed: the language of people who choose their words like footsteps on ice. She watched him file her away, mark her, add her to whatever private ledger he kept behind those careful eyes.

"I'm Serah," she offered. "Pilgrim. Bound for nowhere in particular."

"Edmund." He didn't offer a surname, and the omission was its own confession — bound men gave their debts, not their names. Then, quieter, almost against his will: "You should know the brothers ask pilgrims to leave by week's end. Winter provisioning. Nothing personal."

"Of course." She smiled her pilgrim's smile. "I'll be gone before the snow."

They looked at each other a moment longer than strangers should. Somewhere in the nave a candle guttered, and neither of them glanced toward it, and that too was a kind of recognition — two people who had both learned that sudden movements invited questions.

* * *

She took a cell in the pilgrims' wing and lay awake listening to the valley forget her.

That was the sensation, precisely: forgetting. Back beyond the pass, her body had been a lit window, every emotion a shadow crossing it — and now, here, the shutters were closed. She tested the edges of herself the way she'd once tested prisoners' stories, probing for the give, the seam. Anger, and nothing answered. Fear, and the dark stayed dark. It should have been a comfort. It was the closest thing to safety she had known in fifteen years.

Instead she lay staring at the ceiling and grieved for it, the way you grieve for a limb you're about to have cut off to save your life.

*You're not certain,* she reminded herself. *The scorch marks could have been the brazier. The dreams are dreams. You were tested three times—*

Three times, by instruments designed and calibrated by the Inquisition. By *her* office. By methods she had personally refined to catch the obvious and miss the buried — because the buried ones were harder paperwork, and she had been younger then, and efficient.

She turned the thought over and could not put it down. If the tests could miss a gift buried deep enough — suppressed young, hidden well, generations deep in a bloodline trained to concealment — then the tests had missed people. Had missed *her*, possibly. And if the tests had missed people, then the hunt she had built, the great righteous net of it, had been full of holes from the first day — and the annex meant the ones it *did* catch were being fed to something under the palace.

Guilt was an old companion; she knew its weight like her own pack. But guilt sharpened into something else that night, lying in the silence of Highmoor, and the something else had edges. Resolve. The difference between drowning and swimming toward shore.

*Find out what it is,* she told the dark. *Find out what feeds. And then decide whether you burn the machine you built, or die trying.*

Sleep, when it finally took her, was dreamless. The valley saw to that.

* * *

Morning brought frost on the cloister flags and the sight of Edmund Reyes at the well, hauling water with the same careful hands.

Serah joined him under the pretense of filling her flask, and they performed the small theater of pilgrim-and-scribe, weather-and-roads. But her eyes kept returning to his fingers wrapped around the rope, and on the third bucket he noticed her noticing, and the performance faltered.

"You look at my hands," he said quietly. Not accusing. Weary, almost. Like a man who has been watched before.

"Habit," she said. "I was — I worked records, before. For the crown." True, and truer than he could know. "Ink leaves its marks. So does other work."

"What other work?"

"The kind that teaches you to read people." She held his gaze and made a decision she would spend the rest of her life either justifying or regretting. "The kind that taught me what it means when a man presses his hands flat to keep something still. I saw a boy do it once, in Marrow's End. They took him east. I signed the order myself."

The well rope creaked. Somewhere above, a bird crossed the pale sky and vanished behind the temple roof.

"Why tell me that?" Edmund said at last. His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Why tell a stranger?"

"Because you already know what I'm talking about." She kept her voice as low as his. "Because you've been doing it your whole life, whatever *it* is, and you've never once said it aloud, and I'm the first person in this valley who would even understand the words." She paused. "And because I think something is coming here that you need to understand before it arrives. The hunt is changing. It's not containment anymore. I've seen the orders. What they take east doesn't come back, and it isn't prison waiting for them. It's worse."

His knuckles had gone white on the rope. "You're mistaken," he said, in the voice of a man reciting scripture he no longer believed. "This is Highmoor. Magic can't follow past the pass. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows," she agreed softly. "And the crown knows it too, Edmund. Do you truly believe a hunt that has reached every corner of this realm will simply skip the one valley it was always most curious about? Blank spots on a map don't stay blank forever. They become questions. And questions become rolls — lists of names, every soul born outside these walls, every debt-bound scribe who came here carrying something he hoped the mountains would smother."

The bucket, forgotten, slipped down the well shaft with a splash that sounded enormous in the morning quiet. Neither of them moved to retrieve it.

"I don't know what you mean," Edmund whispered.

"No," Serah said. "But your hands do. And when the soldiers come — and they will come, weeks, maybe days — you'll have to decide which part of you answers the door." She shouldered her pack, leaving him the last word unspoken between them, and walked back toward the pilgrims' wing with her heart hammering against ribs that felt, for the first time in days, entirely, dangerously ordinary.

Behind her, she heard him stand a long moment at the empty well.

And far to the north, though neither of them could know it yet, riders were already asking at the mountain villages for the road through the narrows — and carrying, in a locked dispatch case, a list of names with one line freshly added:

*Highmoor temple. Rolls required. All souls.*
