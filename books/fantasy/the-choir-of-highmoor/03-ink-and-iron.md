## 3. Ink and Iron

The bells woke him before dawn, and they were ringing wrong.

Edmund Reyes had copied the temple's bell schedule eleven times in his years of service — once for the abbot's records, twice for the guesthouse, and eight times because Brother Anselm's handwriting decayed each winter like everything else at Highmoor. He knew the pattern the way he knew his own pulse: matins at first light, a single stroke; lauds an hour later, three strokes with a pause between each; then silence until terce. The bells of Highmoor did not hurry. Nothing in Highmoor hurried. That was the whole point of the valley.

This morning the bells were running together, one into the next, no rhythm at all — a sound like panic given bronze.

He sat up in his cell above the scriptorium, heart already quickening, and told himself what he always told himself: *it is nothing. It is weather. It is a rope gone slack.* The valley taught you to explain things away. A tremor in the floor was settling stone. A flicker of light at the treeline was foxfire. The ache behind your eyes when you stood too near the salt mines was bad air, nothing more. For twenty-six years Edmund had lived inside a fortress of reasonable explanations, and he had built its walls himself, brick by brick, because the alternative — the thing coiled somewhere beneath his breastbone that stirred when he was frightened or furious or, once, unbearably happy — could not be allowed to exist.

He dressed in the dark, took up his ink-stained satchel, and went down into the cold.

The courtyard was full of brothers moving without purpose, which was how fear looked on men who had taken vows of order. Brother Anselm stood by the well wringing his hands as though trying to wash them without water. Two novices were arguing in whispers about whether to open the gate. And down the valley road, still half a mile off but closing, came riders.

Not pilgrims. Pilgrims came on foot, bent under bundles, singing the low walking songs of the lowlands. These rode in column, four abreast, and their armor caught the first grey light and threw it back dull as pewter. At their head flew no temple banner, no merchant's pennant, but a square of black cloth bearing a device Edmund had only ever seen pressed into wax on documents that passed through the scriptorium sealed: a circle bisected by a line. The crown's hunt-mark.

His hands went cold around the satchel strap.

*They cannot be here for me,* he thought, and then, immediately, honestly: *they are here for everyone.*

"Edmund." Abbot Cael's voice, quiet, at his shoulder. The old man had come out without his cowl, and in the pre-dawn grey his face looked carved from something older than the temple stones. "The rolls. All of them. Every ledger of souls, every register of births, every record of who came to us from beyond the pass and when. Bring them to the chapter house. Now."

"The rolls—" Edmund's mouth was dry. "Abbot, the rolls are confidential. Sanctuary—"

"Was never ours to give against the crown," Cael said, "only against men who feared it less than we do. Go."

Edmund went. His legs carried him through the cloister walk while his mind ran ahead of them, cataloguing, and it was in the middle of this cataloguing that he remembered Wren.

The girl slept in the infirmary wing, as she had for three years, since the winter a tinker's cart had left her at the gate half-frozen with no name she could remember and no history anyone could find. She was twelve now, sharp-tongued and quick-fingered, the kind of orphan temples collect the way rivers collect leaves. Three weeks ago, in the refectory, a lamp had guttered out above her head — not blown out, not starved of oil, simply *gone*, snuffed in a single instant while every other flame in the room burned steady. Wren had been laughing at something. Her eyes had gone wide and silvered for half a heartbeat, the way water goes bright before it freezes.

Edmund had seen it. He suspected Brother Anselm had seen it. He was fairly sure Wren herself had felt it, because she had not laughed again all evening, and afterward she had begun sitting nearer the door in every room.

A child born outside the valley. A child whose blood carried whatever it was that the black banner hunted. If the soldiers asked for the rolls, and the rolls said *Wren, foundling, origin beyond the pass*, then—

Then there would be a pyre, or worse. Edmund had spent years copying the crown's edicts. He knew what language like *"purification of unlicensed resonance"* meant when it came down out of the capital wearing formal seals.

He reached the archive, lit a lamp with hands that were almost steady, and pulled the registers.

* * *

The chapter house smelled of beeswax and old stone. The brothers had arranged benches in rows like a classroom, and the hunt-soldiers stood among them the way iron stands among wood — not hostile yet, not friendly, simply present, taking up more space than their bodies occupied. There were eight of them. Their captain was a broad man with a face like a closed door, and beside him, taking notes in a small precise hand, stood a clerk in civilian grey whose eyes never stopped moving across the assembled monks, counting, weighing.

"By authority of the royal commission," the captain read, from a document heavy with seals, "we require production of all rolls and registers of persons residing within this precinct, with particular attention to any soul born outside the valley of Highmoor. Compliance is mandatory. Concealment is treason."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Abbot Cael inclined his head with the weary grace of a man accepting rain. "Our records will be produced. Our scribe will attend you, as our records are kept in his hand."

And so Edmund found himself standing at a table in the corner of the chapter house, the registers stacked before him, the clerk seated opposite with the patient menace of a man prepared to sit all day, while the captain prowled the room behind them both. Edmund opened the first ledger. He had written most of it himself. He knew its contents the way a man knows his own reflection — and that knowledge, he realized with a lurch, was the whole danger. The clerk would ask questions. Edmund's answers would have to match a book written in his own hand, and somewhere in that book was a line reading:

*Wren — foundling, female, approx. age nine upon arrival. Discovered at north gate, midwinter. Origin: unknown, presumed beyond the pass.*

*Presumed beyond the pass.* Five words that might as well have been a death sentence written in his own careful script.

The clerk worked methodically, page by page, and Edmund turned pages for him, and the morning crawled, and with every turn Edmund felt the noose drawing closer to Wren's name. He had hours to think, riding the slow wagon of the clerk's thoroughness, and by the time they reached the infirmary register — third of five volumes — he had stopped thinking like a scribe and started thinking like a forger. Which was, he admitted to himself with a chill, not such a long distance. He had spent his life making copies so faithful that no one could tell them from originals. Faithfulness was just another word for control of the pen.

There was a way. He saw it clearly, the way he sometimes saw solutions to knotted passages of transcription: not boldly, not dramatically, but in the small spaces where truth gets tired. Foundlings' origins were frequently amended in the temple's own records — a tinker's testimony added years later, a parish name supplied by a traveling priest, corrections entered in different inks as information arrived late. The rolls themselves were full of such emendations. One more would look like nothing. A later hand, a plausible source, a date two years after her arrival: *Origin per testimony of Brother Anselm: child of Callowmere lowlands, parish of St. Erren, left with tinker's caravan.*

It was a lie. It would be written in Anselm's name. It would hang Anselm if it unraveled — gentle, half-deaf old Anselm, who fed sparrows off the windowsill and had never in his life told a lie larger than a compliment.

Edmund sat with that for a long moment, the pen hovering, the lamp hissing softly, the clerk's quill scratching on the far side of the table. Fear, he discovered, has a taste — copper and candle smoke — and underneath the fear was something else, something that felt disgracefully like clarity. He thought of Wren's wide silvered eyes in the refectory. He thought of the pyres the edicts described. He thought: *I am already damned by my blood if they ever test me. Let me at least be damned for something I chose.*

He waited for the clerk to look away — the man glanced toward the captain each time the captain's boots crossed the room — and in one of those glances-away, Edmund dipped his pen, turned back two pages in the infirmary register to Wren's entry, and wrote the amendment. Small. Tidy. In an ink mixed slightly browner than his usual, aged with a breath of lamp smoke. A correction, nothing more. The work of four seconds.

His heart slammed so hard he could hear it. He turned the page forward again and presented the register as though handing over a sleeping infant.

The clerk read. The clerk read it twice. And Edmund understood, in a rising tide of cold, that the amendment was *good* — good enough that it might save Wren, and good enough that if anyone ever held it to the light, really held it, the four seconds of his life it represented would become a rope.

Then a woman spoke from the doorway.

"That entry matches the testimony I gathered on the road."

Every head turned. She stood in the chapter house entrance still wearing travel dust, a plain grey cloak over leathers, her hair bound back practically, her posture the posture of someone who had spent years being obeyed and had recently learned to hide it. Edmund had seen her once before, three days ago, asking questions at the guesthouse — the stranger who asked what no pilgrim should ask. Serah Vane, the guest-brother had called her, though Edmund doubted it was the name she'd been born with.

"You, madam?" The clerk's tone sharpened. "You know this foundling?"

"I traveled with a caravan out of Callowmere last autumn," she said, level, unhurried, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "The tinker Maren Ostrey. She spoke of leaving a child at the Highmoor gate some winters past — a girl, sick with fever, whose people came from the St. Erren parishes. I noted it because I keep notes." She touched a leather folio at her belt. "Your record agrees with mine. That is usually how truth behaves."

It was beautiful. It was seamless. It was also, Edmund realized with a vertigo that reached all the way down through the floor of the world, entirely invented — invented in the space of a breath, delivered with the flat certainty of sworn testimony, by a woman he had spoken to exactly once. She was lying to the crown's hunt with the same fluency other people used for greeting each other in markets. And she was lying *for him*. Or for Wren. Or for something larger that he could not yet see the shape of.

The clerk made a note. "Name of the tinker?"

"Maren Ostrey," Serah said. "She winters in Callowmere, the Weavers' Quarter. You're welcome to verify." A pause, measured to the width of a hair. "Though I'd caution against rousing the free cities' guilds over a foundling's parish. They guard their registries jealously, and your commission has enough work without new arguments."

Something flickered across the clerk's face — the look of a man doing arithmetic about how much trouble he wanted. The captain, drawn by the conversation, studied Serah with narrowed eyes, and Edmund watched the two of them regard each other across the chapter house like duelists measuring ground. Then the captain nodded, curtly, and the clerk wrote *corroborated* beside the entry, and moved on to the next volume.

The interview lasted another hour. It felt like a year. When it ended, the soldiers withdrew to the guesthouse to continue their search of the precinct on the morrow, and the brothers dispersed, and Edmund stood among the shelved records holding a ledger he no longer needed, unable to move.

Serah Vane appeared at his elbow.

"St. Erren," she said quietly, for his ears alone. "Parish registers burned in the Callowmere fire six years ago. No one can check it. I checked." Her eyes, pale grey and ungentle, held his. "You write a very convincing older hand, scribe. Where did a debt-bound copyist learn forgery?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't." She glanced toward the doorway where the soldiers had gone, then back. "That child in the infirmary. Something happened in the refectory three weeks ago. A lamp. You saw it. So did I."

The words went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. *So did I.* Not guessed. Seen. This stranger had watched the same impossible half-second he had watched, had drawn the same conclusion, had walked into a room full of hunt-soldiers today and lied to their faces on the strength of it.

"Why?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Why cover it? Why cover *me*? You don't know me. Whatever you think I am—"

"I know exactly what you are," Serah said, and there was no cruelty in it, only a terrible gentleness, the gentleness of a surgeon delivering a diagnosis. "You're a man who held his breath for four seconds today and changed a child's fate with a pen. I've spent ten years hunting people like you, scribe. I know the difference between the ones who burn and the ones who hide. You hid so well you nearly disappeared. But today you didn't." She stepped back, adjusting her cloak, becoming again the anonymous traveler. "They'll be back tomorrow to finish the precinct search. And someday, maybe soon, someone will hold that register to the light. When that day comes, your lie and mine are the same lie. Think about what that makes us."

She walked out into the afternoon light, and Edmund stood alone in the archive among the true and false records of every soul in Highmoor, listening to his own heartbeat.

*Complicity*, he thought. The word arrived in his mind with the weight of a key turning in a lock. He had spent his life believing safety was a place — the valley, the walls, the silence. But safety was not a place. Safety was a story, and stories could be edited, and he had just learned that his hand was steady enough to edit anything.

In the infirmary, Wren was mending a shirt sleeve by lamplight, tongue between her teeth. She looked up when he came in.

"You've got ink on your fingers," she said. "More than usual."

"It was a long morning."

"Were they asking about me?"

The question landed without warning, straight and unchildlike, the way her questions always were. Edmund considered lying to her and found, examining the impulse, that he was finished with lies for the day — except, of course, the one drying on the page downstairs, the one that now belonged to three people instead of one.

"They were asking about everyone," he said. "I told them the truth about you."

Her shoulders eased a fraction. She went back to her stitching. And Edmund stood in the doorway watching lamplight move on her hair, feeling the shape of the crime settle over him — not like a burden, he realized, but like a garment. Like something fitted to him. Like something he might, against every instinct of his hidden life, choose to keep wearing.

Outside, the bells rang vespers, correct at last, orderly, indifferent. Tomorrow the soldiers would finish their search. Somewhere in the guesthouse, a woman who lied like breathing was deciding what to do next, and Edmund understood, with the certainty of a man reading his own handwriting, that whatever she decided, he was already part of it.

The ink was dry. There was no unwriting it now.
