## 4. The Debt Called Due

The abbot sent for him before dawn.

Not a novice, not a note slipped beneath his door as was the custom for corrections and complaints — a summons carried by Brother Anselm himself, an old man with a lamp held high and an expression like a man walking to his own funeral. Edmund dressed in the dark, fingers finding each buckle by memory, and followed Anselm through corridors that smelled of cold stone and tallow. The temple at that hour was a body holding its breath.

The abbot's study sat above the scriptorium, a small room crowded with forty years of correspondence, and Father Odo had never once summoned Edmund to it in eleven years of debt. Eleven years of copying ledgers, of mending bindings, of transcribing the liturgy into fresh hands — and always the work came to him, or he went to the desk assigned to him, and the abbot remained above, a voice behind a door.

Now the door stood open, and Odo was waiting, standing rather than seated, which frightened Edmund more than anything else could have. The old man's hands were folded. His eyes, when they lifted, were not unkind, and that was the worst of it. Unkindness Edmund could have fought.

"You forged the roll," Odo said.

Not a question. Edmund's mouth opened, closed. He thought of the ink still tacky on the ledger two nights past, of Wren's name struck and rewritten, of Serah Vane's steady hand covering his own error while the hunt-captain's clerk watched. He thought of lying, and understood even as he reached for the lie that it would be an insult to them both.

"Yes," he said.

"Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

Something flickered across the abbot's face — not anger. Recognition, perhaps. *He knows what I am,* Edmund thought, and the knowledge arrived whole, without surprise, the way one knows the shape of one's own hands. *He has always known.*

"The soldiers leave today," Odo said. "Their captain is satisfied. The rolls are correct. The orphan Wren does not exist in this valley, and no one born outside these walls will be surrendered." He paused. "You did a dangerous thing, Edmund. A good thing, perhaps. But the captain's satisfaction is not the same as the crown's, and the crown sends its questions slowly, the way water finds the crack in a wall. When they come back — and they will come back — they will bring someone who reads ledgers the way you do. Someone who will see what was done to that page, and ask who held the pen."

Edmund said nothing. His heart was a slow, heavy drum.

"And there is the other matter." Odo turned away from him then, toward the narrow window, where the first grey light was beginning to distinguish the terraced fields from the mist that filled the valley floor. "There has always been the other matter. You have waited eleven years for me to say it aloud, I think. Or feared I would."

"Don't," Edmund said. His voice cracked on the word, and he hated himself for it.

"The valley suppresses what you carry. That is why your parents brought you here, though they did not survive to see you settled. That is why I accepted your debt on terms no sane creditor would offer — eleven years of labor for a sum any journeyman scribe could clear in three. I kept you working, kept you tired, kept you *here*, because here the thing inside you sleeps. And because the alternative—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The alternative was the pyres they are building beyond the pass. I told myself it was mercy. Some mornings I even believed it."

"How long?" Edmund asked. The question came out flat, stripped of everything. "How long have you known?"

"Since you were nine years old, and the bell in the south tower rang without being struck, every time you wept." Odo's reflection watched him from the dark glass. "I should have sent you away then, when it was only a boy's grief and a bell's sympathy. Instead I taught you to be afraid of yourself, and called it discipline. I will answer for that. But not today. Today there is only this: the hunt-captain who left yesterday evening — she was seen. One of the brothers spoke with her on the road, thinking her a pilgrim bound for the shrine at Vell. She was asking after the salt mines under the pass. And she was seen speaking with *you*."

"They'll think she was questioning me about the rolls—"

"They will think whatever their masters tell them to think, and their masters have been told that a disgraced hunt-captain fled south with something worth having." Odo faced him fully now. "So. The debt, Edmund. I am calling it due."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through everything he understood. "Due. It isn't due for another year. The terms—"

"The terms were always mine to set, and I set them so that I could spend them when spending them mattered. Here is how it goes." The abbot's voice was steady, almost gentle, and Edmund wanted to strike him for it. "You can remain. I will surrender you myself within the month — a scribe of foreign birth, a man whose family name appears in the crown's own registers of gifted bloodlines. I will trade you for the temple's safety, and I will live out my days telling myself I had no choice, and some mornings I will even believe that too. Or you can be gone before the next bell. Gone, and never return, and the temple will mourn Brother Edmund the copyist, dead of a fever, buried in consecrated ground. There is a grave already dug. I had it dug last night."

"You've been planning this." Not grief now. Something colder, harder, rising through it. "How long? How long have you had a grave with my name on it?"

"Since the soldiers crossed the pass." Odo's composure finally frayed, just at the edges. "Do you imagine I wanted this? Do you imagine I have slept? I have spent eleven years keeping you safe by keeping you small, boy, and now safety requires me to erase you, and I find I cannot do both, and I am *tired*, Edmund. I am an old man who made a cage and called it a sanctuary, and the door must open sometime. Let it open while you can still walk through it."

Edmund stood very still. Outside the window the mist was lifting off the fields, and somewhere below a novice was sweeping the cloister steps, the rasp of the broom absurdly ordinary against the roar in his skull. Eleven years. He had given this place eleven years of his life, and it had given him walls, and he had mistaken the walls for home because he had never been shown anything else.

"You could have told me," he said finally. "Any time in eleven years. You could have told me what I was, and let me choose."

"And you would have chosen to stay." The abbot smiled, and it was a terrible thing, full of love and ruin. "That is why I couldn't. Now go. The mine entrance is behind the salt storehouse; the key is with the ledger of stores, third shelf, wrapped in oilcloth. Brother Anselm will look away for the length of a bell. And Edmund—" He hesitated, and for a moment he was not an abbot but simply an old man, hollowed out. "Whatever waits for you beyond the pass, do not let them make you small again. Not the crown. Not the gift. Not anyone."

Edmund walked to the door. Stopped with his hand on the frame.

"The fever grave," he said, without turning. "Put Wren's name on it too. If anyone ever asks."

Then he went down through the sleeping temple, past the scriptorium where his life's work sat in neat, anonymous rows, and did not look back.

* * *

Serah was waiting at the salt storehouse, and she had heard everything in his face before he said a word.

"Debt called due?"

"He gave me a choice between surrender and exile." Edmund found the key exactly where Odo had said, the oilcloth stiff with age. "He dressed it up as liberation."

"It usually is," Serah said quietly. "No one escapes until they're pushed. Come on."

The mine entrance was a low arch cut into the hillside behind the storehouse, half-hidden by stacked timber, breathing cold air that tasted of stone and brine. Inside, the dark was absolute until Serah lit a lantern, and then it became a corridor of pale, glittering walls — salt, ancient and crystalline, veined with seams of grey. The tunnel sloped downward and westward, toward the pass, and the silence in it was different from the valley's silence. The valley forgot. The mine simply *kept* things, held them in its mineral patience, and Edmund felt the weight of all the years of salt pressed around him like a second debt.

They walked. For a long time neither spoke. The lantern threw their shadows huge and wavering along the glittering walls, two figures moving through the heart of the mountain, and Edmund counted his footsteps because counting was easier than feeling.

"She knew," he said at last. "My mother. She knew what I was, and she carried me up a mountain and left me with a man who built a cage and called it kindness, and then she died for it, probably, and I've spent my whole life being grateful for the cage."

Serah's boots crunched on the salt grit. "My father put a sword in my hand at six years old because it was the only inheritance he had. I spent twenty years grateful for that too." She glanced at him sidelong. "It doesn't stop being true that they loved us. It just stops being the whole truth."

"Wren," Edmund said. "Where is—"

"Already inside. Two bends ahead, sitting very still and pretending not to be terrified, and doing a poor job of it." Something warmed in Serah's voice. "She wouldn't stay in the guesthouse. Said if we were leaving, she was leaving, and she'd follow the smell of the salt if she had to. She packed her own bag. It took her an hour. She packed a candle stub, a wooden bird she carved herself, and a book she can't read yet."

"A book?"

"Your hand was on the spine. She said it was yours, so it was hers now."

Edmund laughed — a short, broken sound that surprised him out of nowhere, the first laugh in days, maybe the first honest one in years. Of course. The psalter he'd copied for practice, the one with the mistakes he'd been too fond of to correct. He'd left it on the guesthouse windowsill like a fool, and a child had claimed it like treasure.

Ahead, around the second bend, a small shape detached itself from the shadows and ran to him, and Edmund caught Wren up before he entirely decided to, her arms around his neck, her heartbeat quick as a bird's against his chest.

"I knew you'd come," she whispered. "Brother Anselm told me to wait and I waited, mostly."

"Mostly?"

"There was a rat. I negotiated with it."

Serah snorted. Edmund held on a moment longer than necessary, then set the girl down and took stock of her — the too-large pack, the wooden bird clutched in one fist, the fierce chin. An orphan the temple had fed and schooled and never once claimed, and who had chosen them anyway, in the space of two terrible days, with the whole certainty of a child who has nothing left to lose by choosing.

"The tunnel meets the old pilgrim road on the far side of the pass," Serah said, crouching to adjust the lantern. "Three hours' walk, maybe four with short legs. Beyond the boundary stone, the valley stops... doing what it does. Whatever's asleep in you—" she looked at Edmund, and did not finish, and did not need to.

"What happens then?" Wren asked. "When we're outside?"

"Then," Edmund said, "we find out what I am."

* * *

The last hour of the tunnel, the air changed. Warmer, tasting of pine and distance, and the walls gave way to rough-hewn rock where the salt had run out generations ago. They climbed. Wren rode Serah's shoulders for the steepest stretch, solemn as a queen reviewing troops, and Edmund carried the lantern and tried not to think about what waited on the other side of a line drawn in the earth by men who had been dead for centuries.

The boundary stone was unremarkable. That was the cruelty of it — a waist-high marker crusted with lichen, standing in the mouth of the tunnel where it opened onto a ledge above the pilgrim road, morning light pouring down the mountainside in gold bars. On one side, Highmoor. On the other, everything.

Serah set Wren down. Checked the road with a hunter's patience — empty, empty, empty — and then turned to Edmund.

"Once we cross," she said, "there's no going back to sleep. Whatever your gift is, it wakes, and it wakes *loud* — I've seen suppressed gifts wake, in recruits, in prisoners. It's like a scream after years of holding your breath. We need to be ready. We need to be far from anything that matters before it happens."

"And if I flatten the mountainside?"

"Then we apologize to the mountainside." Her mouth twitched, but her eyes didn't. "Edmund. Listen to me. Whatever comes out of you — I've done this before. Not the waking. The surviving it. Trust me in those first minutes, and don't fight it, because fighting it makes it worse. Can you do that?"

He looked at her — the disgraced hunt-captain with a stolen pilgrim's cloak and a dead woman's guilt riding her shoulders, offering to stand beside a stranger's catastrophe because it was hers too now, bought with a forged ledger and shared crime. He thought of the abbot's terrible loving smile. *Do not let them make you small again.*

"No," Edmund said honestly. "But I'll try."

Wren took his left hand. Serah took his right. It should have felt childish, a chain of children crossing a stream, and instead it was the only thing keeping him on his feet.

They stepped over the stone together.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The wind moved. A hawk circled far above the pass. Edmund breathed, and the breath went all the way down for the first time in his life, deep past the floor of his lungs, into some cellar of himself that had been sealed since childhood—

—and the world *rang*.

It began in his chest, a single pure tone like a struck bell, and spread outward through the rock and the air and the light itself. The lichen on the boundary stone shivered. The hawk's cry bent sideways, warped into something harmonized, harmonizing. Every loose pebble on the ledge rose a finger's width and hung there, trembling, and Edmund felt — not heard, *felt* — the resonance climbing, his grief and his terror and eleven years of swallowed sound all finding their frequency at once, and the mountain answering.

"Edmund." Serah's grip tightened. "Don't fight it. Don't—"

The tone broke upward into a chord, and the chord into something vast and wordless, and far down the pilgrim road, a mile distant, a patrol of the usurper's outriders reined in hard as their horses screamed and bolted. On the mountainside, snow sloughed from the high slopes in soft white avalanches. And Edmund stood at the center of it with his eyes wide open, watching the air itself shimmer around his hands like heat over a forge, understanding at last — with wonder and horror in equal measure, indivisible —

that the valley had never been keeping the world out.

It had been keeping *him* in.

And something far away, beyond the pass, beyond the rivers, in a palace where a stolen king sat on a stolen throne — something with instruments tuned to minds like his — had just heard the bell ring.
