## 6. The Road of Ashes

Serah Vane had walked this road before, but never in this direction, and never with ash on the wind.

The first village was called Merrow's Ford. She knew it from the rolls — a river settlement, forty-one households, two licensed millers, one registered hedge-healer named Aldous Crane who had been catalogued in her own hand as *low-risk, minor gift, untrained*. She had written that assessment seven years ago, when she still believed the ledgers were protection. A list of names so the crown could watch over its gifted subjects like a shepherd counting sheep.

She stood now at the edge of the ford and counted something else.

Burned rafters. A well choked with black water. The meeting hall's stone chimney standing alone against the sky like a headstone, and beneath it, half-buried in drifted ash, a child's shoe.

"They were gone by autumn," Edmund said quietly behind her. He had stopped reading the road the way she read it — she could see him trying not to, see his eyes slide off the ruin and come back to her face instead, checking. "The refugees we met at the pass said the villages emptied ahead of the soldiers. People ran."

"Some ran." Serah crouched at the edge of the square and turned over a scrap of charred wood with the toe of her boot. Paper had been nailed there once; she could see the ghost of the nail and the pale rectangle where the weather hadn't reached. "And some were listed."

Edmund came closer. He didn't ask what she meant. She almost wished he would, because then she could keep talking instead of doing what she actually did next, which was walk to the village well, haul up the bucket, and find the dead man floating in it.

He'd been dead for months. The cold water had kept him from rotting entirely, and the rope around his wrists told the rest: he'd been weighted down to hide him, badly, by someone in a hurry. His left hand was missing two fingers.

"Informants," Serah said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, flat and professional, the hunt-captain's voice she'd spent a year trying to bury. "The crown pays them by the name. When a village is cleared, the informants are cleared after — they know too much about how the lists were made." She looked at the ruined houses, at the shoe, at the chimney standing sentinel over nothing. "This wasn't panic. This was procedure."

"How do you know?"

"Because I wrote the procedure."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a tithe barn three miles east, because Serah refused to sleep under any roof that had been burned with people inside it, and there were few enough of those left unburned. Edmund built a small fire — small, always small now, since the thing that had sung back over the capital — and Wren slept curled against his side like a burr, the orphan they'd carried out of Highmoor through the salt mines, who woke at every sound and went back to sleep only when Serah walked the perimeter twice.

It was on the second circuit that she found the notice board.

It stood at the crossroads near the barn, a government post driven into the earth, and someone had nailed the proclamations up in neat rows the way her own clerks had been trained to do. She should have walked past. Instead she lifted her lantern.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN**, the top sheet began, **all persons harboring knowledge of the gift-bearing are commanded to surrender such knowledge within three days...**

Below it, a warrant. A death warrant for the village of Merrow's Ford, itemized. *Aldous Crane, hedge-healer, gift of mending, unregistered practice.* *Marta Crane, daughter, age nine, manifestation observed.* Nine years old. *Sentence: purification by fire, per Statute Eleven.*

And at the bottom, in the strong right-slanting hand that had signed four hundred such documents across eleven years:

*Capt. S. Vane, Office of the Hunt.*

Her signature. Her actual hand, inked in the old confidence, the letters formed without tremor, the way you sign your name when you believe the paper is justice.

She stood there a long time. Long enough that the lantern oil burned low and the flame began to gutter. She did not hear Edmund come up beside her, but suddenly he was there, and he was looking at the warrant too, and he did not look away from it, and worse — he did not look away from *her*.

"You want me to say something," she said.

"No."

"Everyone wants me to say something. That I didn't know. That I was deceived. That the words meant something different when I wrote them." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "I knew. Edmund. I sat in rooms with the inquisitors while they refined the statutes. I trained the first cohort myself. I taught them how to read a household for signs — how a child who hums while working might be resonant, how a woman whose bread never burns might be mending it without knowing. I gave them the questions to ask. And when the answers started coming back wrong, when the pyres started going up faster than the trials could be held—" She stopped. Breathed. "I told myself the numbers were exaggerated. Enemy propaganda from the free cities. I was very good at telling myself things."

"I know," Edmund said.

"You don't—"

"My whole life was a lie someone told themselves," he said. "The abbot told himself he was protecting me. My parents told themselves the valley was mercy. I told myself the silence in my chest was peace." He looked at the warrant again, at her name, and then at her, steady. "You're the only person I've ever met who took the lie apart herself. From inside. Do you know how rare that is? Most people die inside their lies and call it loyalty."

Serah found she couldn't answer. She tore the warrant from the post, folded it, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where the weight of it sat like a stone she intended to carry the rest of the way.

"We burn nothing else of mine," she said. "No more villages. Whatever it costs."

"The free cities will try you anyway."

"Yes," she said. "And I'll let them."

* * *

The second village had no name left on its signpost; the board had been used for kindling. But Serah knew where they were by the bend of the river and the shape of the hills, and knowing made her sick, because this was Duncarrow, and Duncarrow appeared in her files as the site of the largest single seizure in the western provinces: nineteen souls taken in one night, transported east in wagons toward the capital.

The village wasn't burned. It was *emptied* — doors standing open, loaves of bread gone to green mold on tables, a cradle rocking gently in a draft as though someone had just stepped out. The hunt didn't burn villages that surrendered their gifted quietly. Burning was for resistance. This was the other kind of efficiency, the kind Serah had once praised in her reports: *orderly removal, minimal disruption to loyal subjects.*

Minimal disruption. She walked down the main street of Duncarrow and read the disruption everywhere. In the toys in the lane. In the ledger she found in the reeve's house, open on his desk, where he had recorded the seizure himself in a shaking hand: *They took the Hendersons' boy. He is six. Mrs. Alder followed the wagon for two miles until the soldiers turned her back. No one has spoken above a whisper since.*

Edmund found her sitting on the reeve's step with the ledger in her lap when the light went gray.

"There's a map," she said. Her thumb moved along the page. "In my office in the capital, there's a wall map of the whole realm, and every gifted bloodline is marked on it. Pins. Red for confirmed, white for suspected, black for..." She closed the ledger. "Black for harvested. I built that map over eleven years. Every pin was placed by my hand or my order. And somewhere on it, there's a valley called Highmoor with no pins at all, because I could never understand why the richest resonance readings in the realm all went silent in one place." She looked up at him. "Until I met you."

"So the valley wasn't an accident."

"The valley was the oldest entry in the archive. Suppression stone laid down generations ago. Your family line, Edmund — Reyes — it's in the deep records. Marked for containment before your grandfather was born." She watched him take that, watched his hands go still the way they went still when he was copying something difficult. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me and not from them. And because if we reach Callowmere, the council will ask me exactly what I know about how the hunt works, and I intend to give them everything."

"Even the parts that hang you."

"Especially those."

Wren appeared between them then, silent as the child was, holding out a heel of bread divided three ways. It was such a small, ordinary gesture in the middle of all that ash that Serah felt her throat close. The girl had lost everything twice over — once to the temple, once to the hunt — and still she divided what she had into thirds.

"Eat," Wren said. It was not a suggestion. "Both of you. You get strange faces when you talk about the capital."

* * *

On the fourth day they crossed the border stones into the marchlands, where the purge had run ahead of them like a fever, and here at last they began to meet the living.

Refugees, mostly, moving west in straggling lines: carters with their goods strapped high, grandmothers carrying portraits, children driving geese. They traveled light and spoke little, and when Serah passed them on the road, hood up, she caught the fragments of their talk the way she'd been trained to catch them.

"—took the miller's whole family, no trial, just the red hoods—"

"—my cousin says in Callowmere they don't ask questions at the gate—"

"—the king's gone mad, my brother serves in the palace guard, he says the king hasn't been seen at mass in a year, he says the edicts come out at night—"

That last one stopped her. She drew Edmund off the road behind a fallen wall and repeated it to him in a low voice.

"Haven't been seen at mass in a year."

"You said the letters bore two hands."

"And the archivist's cipher — the true king begging for rescue in his own code, hidden in official correspondence." Serah pulled her hood lower. A column of hunt cavalry was visible on the eastern road, a mile off, moving south. "But listen to it from the other side. A king who vanishes from public life. Edicts issued in his name that even his own guard finds strange. A purge that harvests the gifted and ships them east, toward the palace, not the prisons." She felt the shape of it settle over her, cold and complete. "Whatever sits on that throne isn't issuing orders anymore. Something is using the crown the way I used my seal — to make horror look like law."

Edmund was quiet for a long moment. On the wind, faintly, from the direction of the capital, there came a sound neither of them could properly hear and both of them felt — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the birdsong. Wren pressed her hands over her ears and looked north.

"It knows we're coming," Edmund said. Not a question.

"It knows *you're* coming," Serah corrected. "Which means we have maybe two weeks before it stops sending soldiers and starts sending something worse." She checked the sun, marked the road west, and made the decision she'd been circling for days. "We stop hiding our pace. Callowmere by the week's end, whatever the roads cost us."

They walked. Behind them the road of ashes stretched back toward everything she had built and burned, and ahead of them the refugee columns thickened, all of them flowing toward the same walled city and the same fragile promise: that somewhere in the world there was still a door that would open.

Near dusk, a boy running the refugee line pressed a printed broadsheet into Edmund's hand — news from the west, sold for a copper. Edmund read it by the last light and his face changed, and he handed it to her without a word.

**CALLOWMERE COUNCIL REAFFIRMS NEUTRALITY**, the headline read. **CITY TO REFUSE ENTRY TO ALL PERSONS FLEEING "THE SOUTHERN TROUBLE." GATES TO BE CLOSED AT WEEK'S END.**

Serah read it twice. Then she looked at the columns of refugees around them — thousands of people walking steadily toward gates that were about to shut in their faces — and she began, very quietly, to laugh, because the alternative was screaming.

"Well," she said, folding the paper into her coat beside the warrant, where the two documents lay together like accusation and sentence. "We'd better be fast."
