## 7. The Free City of Callowmere

The road to Callowmere smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and it was full of people.

Edmund had never seen so many people in his life. Highmoor's temple held perhaps two hundred souls on the holiest days; the market town at the valley's mouth might swell to five hundred when the salt caravans came through. But here, on the last mile of the pilgrim road, there were more people than he could count, and they were all moving in the same direction — a slow river of carts and donkeys and families walking with everything they owned bundled on their backs, funneling toward the gates of a city that rose out of the morning haze like something built by giants who had lost interest halfway through.

"That's not a wall," Edmund said. "That's a cliff with doors in it."

"Callowmere was carved, not built," Serah said beside him. She had her hood up and her shoulders down, doing her best to look like every other tired traveler, and she was very good at it — good enough that Edmund sometimes forgot what she was. "The first settlers found the rock already hollowed by an underground river. They just had to put windows in it."

Windows there were: hundreds of them, glittering in tiers up the pale stone face, catching the light like scales on a fish. Above them all, the council tower jutted from the crown of the cliff, flying no banner at all. That absence, Edmund would learn, was deliberate. Callowmere flew nothing because Callowmere belonged to no one.

"Neutrality," Serah said, reading his gaze. "They're proud of it. Four hundred years without raising an army or taking a side."

"And how has that gone for them?"

She didn't answer, which was answer enough.

They joined the river of refugees. No one asked questions at the gate — that was the point of Callowmere, apparently, the one civic virtue its council still believed in: ask nothing, record nothing, owe nothing. A bored gate-warden waved them through with two fingers, barely glancing up from his breakfast. And then the city swallowed them.

Edmund had imagined cities before. He had copied descriptions of them into temple ledgers, trade inventories from Vellisport, cargo manifests from the coast, and he had thought he understood what *many* meant. He had been wrong. Callowmere's streets were canyons of noise — hawkers crying the price of eels, a bell somewhere ringing the wrong hour, children running between cart wheels, a woman arguing passionately with a fishmonger about the moral character of mackerel. The smell hit him in layers: bread, tar, horse dung, spice, the green reek of the canal that bisected the lower city. His head turned constantly, greedily, drinking in details the way a man dying of thirst drinks water.

He caught himself grinning like a fool and made himself stop.

*You are a fugitive,* he reminded himself. *Fugitives do not gawk.*

But some part of him — the part that had spent twenty-six years copying other people's accounts of the world — refused to be sensible. He had crossed the pass. He had walked out of the valley that forgot. Every street here was proof that he existed outside of it.

Serah led them down toward the canal district, where the rents were cheap and the neighbors asked fewer questions, to a lodging house called the Drowned Bell that smelled of tallow and old rain. The landlady, a broad woman with forearms like a blacksmith's, took their coin without counting it and gave them a room with two beds and a window overlooking the water.

"The refugee quarter is east of the canal," Serah said, once the door was shut. "If we're looking for people like you—"

"People like me."

"You know what I mean." She sat on the edge of her bed, unlacing her boots with the efficient movements of someone who never fully relaxed. "The gifted. The ones who ran. There'll be word among them. There's always word."

"How would you know?"

"Because I built the network that hunted them." She said it flatly, without flinching, the way she said most true things. "Refugees talk to each other, Edmund. It's the only weapon they have left. Someone in this city knows where they gather, and we find that person tonight."

* * *

They found her by accident, and by bread.

The eastern quarter was a country unto itself — streets where three languages fought for air, doorways hung with charms against evil that were really just regional folkways wearing superstition as a coat, and everywhere, everywhere, the particular silence of people listening for footsteps behind them. Edmund felt it like weather. These were people who had learned to walk quietly in their own heads.

The bakery stood on a corner where two alleys met, and its line stretched out the door. Refugees queued for day-old loaves sold at charity prices, and Edmund joined the queue because his stomach overruled his caution, and because Serah had gone ahead to scout and he was supposed to stay unremarkable, and nothing was more unremarkable than a hungry man waiting for bread.

It happened while he was waiting. A boy in front of him — maybe ten, holding his little sister's hand — dropped his coin, and it rolled toward the gutter, and Edmund lunged and caught it without thinking.

And the world *sang.*

It was only a flicker — half a heartbeat of resonance rippling out from his skin like a ring in water — but the boy went rigid. The sister's eyes went wide. And three people further down the queue turned their heads in perfect unison, the way birds turn, and looked directly at him.

Not at the coin. At *him.*

One was an old woman with a shawl and a face like a walnut shell. One was a young man with burn scars down one arm. The third was a girl of perhaps sixteen, carrying a lute case, whose eyes were the color of river ice and who did not look away even when Edmund looked back.

The boy took his coin, whispered thanks, and fled. The queue closed around Edmund like water around a stone. And when he reached the counter, the baker — a heavyset man with flour to his elbows — slid him two loaves instead of one and said, in a voice pitched for no one else:

"Back door. After the evening bell. If you want to hear the choir."

Then he turned to the next customer and began arguing cheerfully about the price of rye as if nothing had happened.

* * *

"They knew," Edmund told Serah, back at the Drowned Bell, pacing the narrow floor. "Three seconds of contact and they knew. How? What did I—" He stopped, hands half-raised, staring at them as if they belonged to someone else. "I didn't do anything. I just *felt* it start and pushed it down, the way I've always—"

"That's how they knew." Serah hadn't moved from the window. Below, the canal lay dark, lamplight shivering on the water. "Suppression isn't silence, Edmund. It's pressure. Anyone sensitive enough can feel the shape of what's being held back. You're not hiding your gift. You're humming it."

"I've been careful my whole life."

"I know." Something moved across her face — guilt, quickly banked. "Careful wasn't enough for any of them either. That's rather the point of the purge."

He wanted to be angry at her for saying it so plainly. He couldn't manage it. She was right, and they both knew she was right, and there was a strange comfort in that — in being seen so completely by someone who had spent years seeing people exactly like him through crossbow sights.

"After the evening bell," he said.

"I'm coming with you."

"They'll smell the hunt on you."

"Probably." She finally turned. Her face in the lamplight was tired and resolute, the face of a woman who had decided long ago that she would carry her sins into every room she entered. "But if there are gifted people gathering in this city, then sooner or later the hunt finds them too, and they will need someone who knows exactly how the hunt works. Better they learn now that I'm with you than discover it later and assume the worst."

"And if they decide the worst anyway?"

"Then I'll have earned it." She picked up her cloak. "Come on. Bells wait for no one."

* * *

The back door of the bakery opened onto a cellar, and the cellar opened onto Callowmere's bones — old quarry tunnels from the first cutting, now candlelit and crowded, where the sound of the city above came through the stone as a distant ocean murmur.

There were perhaps sixty people in the great chamber. Edmund stopped on the last step and stared.

He had spent his whole life believing he was singular. The abbot had let him believe it — had perhaps needed him to believe it. *A tremor,* the old man had called it, once, in the single conversation they ever had about it. *A flaw in you, boy, like a weak heart. Keep it quiet and it will keep you safe.* Edmund had built his entire self around that sentence. He was the only one. He was alone with it. He was a locked room with one occupant.

Sixty people. And these were only the ones in Callowmere.

A woman was heating a kettle with her bare hands, steam curling off her palms, while children watched with the boredom of the familiar. A man sat in the corner with his eyes closed, and around him floated a slow orbit of pebbles, turning like tiny moons. Two brothers were playing a game with dice that always landed the way the thrower wished, and cheating each other cheerfully. An old soldier taught a circle of teenagers how to ground themselves — breathing exercises, focus drills — and his voice carried the flat patience of someone who had done this in worse places.

And underneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, Edmund heard it: the resonance. Dozens of small frequencies, each distinct, none quite in tune with the others, filling the chamber like light fills a fogged window. Not harmony. Not yet. But *near* it. So near it made his teeth ache.

"There you are." The girl with the lute case materialized at his elbow — the ice-eyed one from the bread line. Up close she was sharper-featured, with ink stains on her fingers and the manner of someone much older wearing a young face badly. "Baker said you'd come. I'm Maren. I keep the list."

"The list?"

"Everyone. Who you are, what you can do, who's looking for you." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Don't make that face. The list keeps people alive. When the inquisitors took the group in Marrowfield, it was because nobody kept records. Nobody knew who was missing until it was too late to run."

"You keep records of the hunted," Serah said from behind Edmund, "so the hunters can't erase them."

Maren's gaze slid past Edmund and sharpened. "And who's this?"

"A friend," Edmund said.

Maren looked at Serah for a long moment — at the sword-callused hands, the way she stood with her weight balanced, the flat watchfulness she could never entirely shed. Whatever Maren concluded, she filed it away visibly. "Everyone's somebody's friend," she said. "Come meet Old Sable. She runs things, such as things get run."

Old Sable turned out to be the walnut-faced woman from the bread line, seated on a crate like a throne, mending a child's coat with quick stitches. She looked up at Edmund with eyes gone milk-pale with age, and yet he had the unsettling certainty that she saw him more clearly than anyone ever had.

"Hold out your hands," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm old and rude and I haven't time for manners. Hold them out."

He held them out. She didn't touch him. She simply leaned close, the way a musician leans toward an instrument, and listened — actually listened, head tilted, lips slightly parted. After a moment her brow creased.

"Well," she said softly. "Well, well. Where did they grow *you*, boy?"

"A valley. Highmoor."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — recognition, and beneath it, grief. "Suppression stone under the whole valley floor. We wondered if any of the hidden ones survived in there." She took his hands in hers, and her grip was strong as a vise. "Do you know what you are?"

"A scribe with a flaw."

"You're a *resonant,*" she said. "And not a small one. Child, when you caught that coin today, half my people felt it in their sleep. You've been living in a valley built to smother you, and you've still got enough voice to carry through stone." She released him and returned to her stitching, as though she hadn't just dismantled twenty-six years of his self-understanding. "Sit. Listen. Learn the others' names. That's the first lesson — the gift answers to the person, so know the person first."

Edmund sat. And for the next four hours, in a candlelit cave beneath a neutral city, he did the thing he had never dared do in his life: he let the pressure ease.

Just a little. Just a crack in the dam. But the resonance rose in him like blood into a numb limb, painful and sweet, and the chamber answered — the kettle-woman glanced over and smiled, the dice-brothers paused mid-cheat, the pebble-orbiter opened his eyes. For one impossible moment, sixty scattered frequencies bent slightly toward his, the way grass bends toward wind.

*Oh,* Edmund thought, dizzy. *Oh, this is what it's for.*

Around him, unnoticed, the ordinary business of the hunted continued: Maren updating her list, mothers feeding children, Old Sable assigning watch rotations. A choir tuning up in a burning house.

* * *

It was near midnight when the runner came down the tunnel stairs — a gaunt man still wearing the mud of the south road, and wearing terror the way other men wore cloaks.

The chamber went silent in stages, like a wave rolling outward from the door.

"Vellisport's fallen," the runner said. His voice cracked on the second word. "No — not fallen. *Opened.* The garrison let the imperial army cross the bridge without a fight. The council bought terms, they say. Terms." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "And the army didn't stop there. They've taken the fords at Bren. All three fords. The border rivers are behind them now."

The silence changed texture. Edmund felt it shift — fear going from abstract to immediate, the way cold goes from air to water.

Old Sable rose from her crate. "How far?"

"Ten days to Callowmere walls, if they march hard. Five, if they ride."

"And the council?"

"Says neutrality holds. Says the empire has no quarrel with free cities that don't meddle." The runner spat. "Half the quarter's packing already. The other half says running is meddling."

Maren was already writing, pen scratching fast, her face bloodless. Serah had gone very still beside Edmund, and when he glanced at her, he saw her doing arithmetic behind her eyes — roads, distances, garrisons, the machinery of armies — and he understood suddenly that she had stood in rooms like this one before, on the other side of the news.

"The purge doesn't stop at borders," Serah said quietly, to the room. Not accusing. Just stating. "You should all know that. Neutrality is a word. Resonance is a resource. The thing commanding that army does not recognize the difference."

Heads turned. Suspicion moved through the chamber like a draft. Old Sable's blind eyes fixed on Serah with terrible attention.

"And how would you know what commands it, soldier?" the old woman asked.

Serah held her gaze. "Because I served it," she said, "before I knew what it was. Ask me anything. I'd rather you hate me informed than trust me ignorant."

For a long moment, nobody breathed. Then Old Sable snorted, folded her mending, and said, "Tomorrow, then. You'll tell us everything, soldier, and we'll decide what you're worth." She turned to the room. "Nobody packs tonight. Panic is a luxury for people with somewhere better to be. Sleep. Watch in pairs. And you—" her blind eyes found Edmund with unnerving precision, "—come here at dawn. If the empire is coming, boy, you'd best learn to sing properly before it arrives."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the bells of Callowmere rang midnight over a city that still believed its walls and its words would save it.

Edmund stood in the candlelight among the choir of the hunted, his gift humming loose in his chest for the first time in his life, and counted ten days on his fingers like a scribe.

Five, if they rode.
