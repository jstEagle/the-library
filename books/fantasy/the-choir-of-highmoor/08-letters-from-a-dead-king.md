## 8. Letters From a Dead King

The archivist's house smelled of tallow and rot, and both smells were old enough to have become furniture.

Edmund had expected Callowmere's underworld to be dramatic — whispered passwords in wine-cellars, couriers vanishing through trapdoors. Instead Serah led him up three crooked flights of stairs above a dyer's shop, where the fumes from below had turned the plaster a bruised violet, and knocked four times on a door painted with a fish that had lost most of its paint and all of its dignity.

"He won't like us," she said quietly. "He liked me even less when I was hunting for the crown. But he hates the usurper more than he hates me, and hatred is the only currency that never devalues."

The door opened on a chain, then wider, and Edmund found himself looking at a man who seemed to be assembled entirely of angles and parchment dust. Master Corvane had been First Archivist of the Royal Chancery once — Serah had told him this on the walk over, in the flat voice she used for facts that hurt — which meant he had been keeper of every edict, every decree, every royal signature that had passed through the capital's hands. Now he was a skeleton wrapped in a dressing gown, propped against a cane, with eyes that burned like coals raked down to their last heat.

"The hunt-captain," Corvane rasped. "And her scribe." His gaze moved over Edmund with the particular attention of a man who had spent forty years cataloguing things. "You copy documents."

"I do."

"Then you'll know what I mean when I say that a hand is a face. Sit down. I don't have the strength to stand through what I'm about to show you, and you don't have the sense to sit without being told."

* * *

The room behind the door was smaller than it should have been, walls swallowed by shelves, and the shelves swallowed by paper. Bundles of letters tied with string. Ledgers swollen with damp. A writing desk under the window, its surface scarred by decades of pen-knives, and on it — laid out with the care of relics — perhaps two dozen sheets of vellum.

Corvane lowered himself into a chair by degrees, like a building settling. "I served the crown for thirty-one years," he said. "I authenticated the king's hand on more documents than I can number. When Aldric took the throne at nineteen, his signature was already distinctive — a heavy downstroke on the A, a habit of crossing his final letters twice. Nervous young man. Conscientious. He crossed everything twice, as if to say *I meant it, I meant it.*" A dry sound that might have been laughter. "You learn to love them, in a way. Their little habits. Their humanity, leaking onto the page."

He gestured at the desk with one trembling finger.

"Four years ago, the hand changed."

Edmund rose and went to the papers. The first was an edict — he recognized the format instantly, having copied a hundred provincial copies of such things in Highmoor's ledgers — ordering the registration of all persons manifesting "irregular resonance." The seal was intact. The language was correct. The signature at the bottom read *Aldric R.*

But.

"It's not him," Edmund said, before he could stop himself.

Corvane's coal-bright eyes sharpened. "Go on."

Edmund touched the edge of the vellum, not the ink. "The downstroke is there, but it's drawn, not written. Whoever made this understood the shape of the king's hand but not its weight. And the double-crossing—" He turned the page toward the light. "Here it's doubled, but the second stroke goes the wrong direction. A right-handed forger imitating a leftward cross. And the spacing—" He stopped, suddenly aware he was lecturing a master of the craft in his own house.

"No," Corvane said softly. "Continue. It has been a long time since anyone in this city looked at ink the way I look at ink."

"The spacing between words is too even. The king writes fast — see how the ascenders lean? This hand is careful. Controlled. It's the hand of someone who is being *precise*, not someone who is thinking." Edmund set the sheet down. "This is a copy of a man, not a man."

For a long moment the only sound was the dyer's shop below, the slow slosh of vats, someone singing off-key about a girl from the river country.

"Thirty-one years," Corvane said again, quieter. "And it took a debt-scribe from a valley of no account thirty seconds to see what I spent two years convincing myself I was imagining." He closed his eyes. "I told myself grief. I told myself age, illness, the burden of rule. Kings change. Hands change. And then—"

He reached beneath the desk, into a drawer that clicked open with the sound of a lock long broken, and drew out a bundle tied not with string but with a strip of leather, worn soft as cloth.

"And then these began reaching me. Smuggled out of the palace in the lining of laundry baskets, sewn into book bindings, once inside a wheel of cheese. Letters bearing the king's cipher — his private mark, known only to perhaps six living people. Written in a hand that *is* his. And saying—"

His voice failed. Not from weakness, Edmund realized. From something older than weakness.

Serah took the bundle. She untied the leather strip and spread the letters across the desk, and Edmund leaned over them, and the world tilted very slightly on its axis.

They were short. They were desperate. And they were written in a code that was almost — but not quite — the standard chancery cipher, because a standard cipher could be forced out of a prisoner along with his fingernails. This one was warped, each substitution shifted by a pattern, and Edmund felt his mind seize on it the way a starving man seizes on bread.

"It's keyed," he murmured. "To something personal. The substitutions rotate." He pulled the desk lamp closer. "If I had the key phrase—"

"*Highmoor keeps what the crown forgets,*" Corvane said. "It was the king's joke, when he was young. About your valley. About all the valleys like it."

Edmund's pen was moving before the sentence finished. The rotation resolved itself in his head like a lock giving way — the first letter shifted by one, the second by two, wrapping around, the whole mechanism driven by the nine words of the key phrase repeating underneath the text like a heartbeat. He had spent ten years copying other people's records. He had never once decoded anything that mattered.

The first letter opened itself to him:

*I am held. I am lucid perhaps one hour in six. It reads my thoughts when I resist, so I write only in sleep-shapes, only what it cannot parse. Do not come for me. Come for the proof. The edicts are mine in shape only. Every gifted soul taken by the hunt is brought below. It feeds. Gods forgive me, it feeds, and I can feel them screaming through its mouth.*

The lamp flame bent in a draft that wasn't there. Or perhaps it was; the room had gone cold, or Edmund had.

He read the second letter. Then the third. His hands were steady — ten years of ledger-work had taught his hands steadiness as a religion — but somewhere behind his sternum, something was shaking hard enough to crack.

*It came from outside the realm. It wore the chamberlain first, I think, or the chamberlain wore it — I cannot untangle which. By the time I understood, it had my voice, my seal, my sleep. It does not want the throne. Thrones are tools. It wants the gift. All of it. Every thread of it in every bloodline. The hunt is not a purge. The hunt is a harvest, and I am the sickle it signs its name with.*

"Say it aloud," Corvane said. His eyes were open again, fixed on Edmund. "I've read them a hundred times. I need to hear them in another voice. I need to know I haven't gone mad alone."

Edmund looked at Serah. She stood by the window with her arms folded, and her face — her careful, disciplined, hunt-captain's face — had gone the color of the violet plaster.

"The king is alive," Edmund said. "Conscious. A prisoner inside his own body. The thing wearing the crown isn't a tyrant. It's—" He searched for the word and hated every word he found. "It's a parasite. And the purge we've been running from — the pyres, the rolls, the hunters — they're not killing the gifted. The ones taken alive are being brought to the palace. To feed it."

Silence. Below them, the singer had stopped. Somewhere a cart rattled past, and the whole fragile house shivered with it.

"That's why the suppression stone," Serah said at last, very evenly, the way she spoke when she was holding herself together with rivets and prayer. "Highmoor. The valley. The cells under the temple. I always assumed the crown wanted the gift caged because it feared it." Her laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "It doesn't fear it. It's *farming* it. Suppression keeps the stock alive. Docile. You don't slaughter the herd, you pen it."

"You designed the pens," Corvane said. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic.

"I know what I designed." Serah's voice didn't waver, but her knuckles did, white against her sleeves. "I built the rolls. I mapped the bloodlines. I trained the inquisitors who—" She stopped. Started again. "Every name in my registers. Every family marked for suppression. That's not a census anymore. That's a larder."

Edmund thought of the tremors he had hidden for twenty years. Of the abbot's knowing silence. Of a valley that forgot, that erased, that kept its people soft and silent and *safe* — and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that Highmoor had never been sanctuary. It had been storage.

*Including Edmund's line,* said a thought he refused to finish yet.

"How many letters?" he asked instead, because the alternative was to sit down and not get up.

"Eleven survive. There were more. The courier routes were dying even before I fled the capital — it hunts the channels now, the way it hunts everything." Corvane pushed himself forward in his chair, and the effort cost him visibly; his breath went thin and rattling. "Which brings me to the reason you're standing in my house instead of some safer fool's."

He beckoned, and Edmund came, and the old man gripped his wrist with fingers like dry twigs and a grip like iron wire.

"I am dying. Not slowly, either — the cough you hear is the polite end of what started last winter. I have spent two years sitting on this proof like a dragon on nothing, because proof without a voice is just paper, and everyone I might have trusted is dead, exiled, or turned." His coal-eyes bored into Edmund's. "You read the cipher in minutes. You saw the forgery in seconds. And you came here with *her*, which means either you're the bravest man in Callowmere or the biggest fool, and I've learned the two are often the same man wearing different coats."

"What do you want?"

"I want this to outlive me." He released Edmund's wrist and gestured at the letters, the forged edicts, the whole paper continent of his life's final work. "Copies. In your hand, not mine — mine will be watched the moment I stop breathing. Three sets, three directions, three chances. And the originals go with someone who can use them. Someone the free cities might actually listen to, if she stands in front of a war council and says *I built the machine that did this, and here is its blueprint, and here is the proof of what it's for.*" A terrible smile. "Guilt makes excellent testimony. No one doubts a confession."

"They'll arrest her," Edmund said.

"Probably."

"They might hang her."

"Almost certainly, eventually, unless she's useful enough first to make hanging inconvenient." Corvane shrugged, a small dry motion. "That's politics. I deal in documents. Documents are more honest."

* * *

They worked through the night.

The dyer's vats below went quiet, then noisy again with the false dawn of the night-shift, and still Edmund wrote. He copied the eleven letters twice over, then a third partial set, his pen moving with the fluency of ten thousand hours, while beside him Serah read the king's words aloud in a low voice, again and again, as if repetition could grind the horror into something she could carry.

By the fourth letter she had stopped flinching. By the seventh she had started taking notes — dates, names, the routes by which prisoners moved, the structure of the palace's lower levels as the king described it in his sleep-shaped cipher. By the eleventh she was drawing maps on the back of Corvane's spare paper, quick and precise, a fortress assembling itself under her pen.

"There's a service stair," she said, tapping the sketch. "If the king's descriptions are accurate — and they'd have to be, he lived in that palace his whole life — the old servant ways run from the river gate down to the underlevels. Past the cells." Her pen paused. "Past wherever *it* keeps itself."

"You're planning to go there." Edmund didn't look up from his copying. "To the capital. Into the palace."

"I'm planning to finish what I started. Yes."

"With what army?"

"With whatever the free cities give me when I put these letters in front of them and tell them the truth: that neutrality is a door, and doors open from both sides." She set down the pen. "And with you, if you'll come. And if you won't, I'll still come, but I'd rather—"

She stopped. Edmund looked up. In the lamplight her face was stripped of everything tactical, and what remained underneath was so bare it hurt to witness.

"I'd rather not do it without you," she finished. "That's all. That's the whole speech."

Edmund looked down at the letter in front of him, at the king's cramped, coerced, courageous hand. *Come for the proof.* Well. Here was the proof, and here was the woman who had helped build the cage, asking him to help break it.

"I've been hiding since before I could walk," he said. "In a valley built to bury what I am. I copied other people's numbers for ten years and called it a life." He dipped his pen. "Yes. I'll come."

Something crossed Serah's face that she immediately filed away somewhere deep, but he'd seen it, and they both knew he'd seen it, and neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of saying.

* * *

Master Corvane died three days later, in his chair, with the last copy still warm under his hand.

They buried him in the dyers' yard because nowhere else would take a disgraced archivist, and Serah spoke over the grave — a few flat sentences about service and truth that would have sounded like a military report if her voice hadn't cracked on the word *truth*. Edmund left a copy of the decoded letters in the coffin, tucked beneath the old man's folded hands. Let him keep the originals of his life's work. He'd earned them.

They left Callowmere two days after that, before the usurper's agents — already sniffing through the refugee quarters, already asking questions about a dead man's visitors — could close the net. Wren carried the second copy-set sewn into the lining of a coat two sizes too big. Edmund carried the third against his own chest, bound tight, feeling it there with every step like a second heartbeat.

The road north was choked with others heading the same direction — not refugees now, but something harder-eyed. Farmers with scythes. Free-city militia in mismatched colors. Word was moving ahead of them faster than any courier: armies massing on the border rivers, the usurper's banners advancing, the free cities' councils meeting in emergency session with their precious neutrality melting around them like spring ice.

At the first crossroads inn, Serah spread her maps on a table among three allied captains and said the words Edmund had been waiting for, the words that changed everything:

"The king is alive. I can prove it. And I can get to him."

The room went silent. Outside, far to the south, thunder rolled across a clear sky — or something that sounded like thunder, low and rhythmic and wrong, like a vast resonance gathering itself at the edge of hearing.

Edmund's scarred gift stirred in answer, unbidden, the way a tuning fork answers a distant bell.

It knew that sound. And now, so did he.
