## 9. Serah's Confession

The fire in the ruined mill had burned down to a red heart by the time Serah Vane decided she could not carry it another mile.

She had told herself she would tell him at Callowmere. She had told herself she would tell him on the road, somewhere flat and open, where he could walk away from her if he chose and not be trapped by walls or weather. She had told herself a hundred things in the eleven days since they'd fled Highmoor, and every one of them had been a lie dressed up as prudence.

Now there was no road left to defer to. The mill was the only shelter for miles, the rain was coming down like a judgment, Wren was asleep against a sack of rotting grain with Edmund's cloak over her small shoulders, and Edmund sat across the coals from her, mending a strap of his satchel with the same careful, patient hands that had forged temple ledgers for six years without ever once being caught.

Those hands. That was how she'd known, that first day in the Narrows, before he'd said ten words to her. Scribes learned economy; forgers learned nerve; but only someone who had spent his whole life holding something down moved like that — every gesture deliberate, every motion a small act of suppression. She had seen it in a hundred gifted people she'd catalogued. She had just never seen it in someone she —

She stopped the thought there, as she had stopped it a hundred times.

"Edmund." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "There's something you need to hear from me before we reach the city. Before you trust me with anything else."

He looked up. The firelight did unkind things to his face — hollowed his cheeks, deepened the shadows under his eyes — but his gaze was steady. It had been steady since the salt mines, since the moment his gift had woken outside the valley and flattened four hunt-soldiers like wheat under a scythe, and he had looked at her afterward not with blame but with a question: *what am I?*

She was about to answer a different question entirely. One he hadn't asked yet because he didn't know it existed.

"I wasn't fleeing the purge," she said. "Not at first. I was auditing it."

His hands went still on the strap.

"The crown's hunts have run for nine years," she continued, because if she stopped now she would never start again. "You know that much. What you don't know is how they work. There are three arms of it. The soldiers who make the arrests — they're mostly conscripts who believe what they're told. The pyres and the prisons — those are logistics, ugly but simple. And then there's the third arm. The quiet one. The one that decides *who*."

"And that was you."

"That was me." The words tasted like ash and river water. "I built the third arm. I trained its officers. We called them inquisitors, though the title on their commissions is *Assessors of Resonant Threat*. I wrote their curriculum. I taught them how to read a person's gift from the way they hold a cup, the way they flinch at bells, the way they dream out loud. I taught them interrogation methods tuned to each class of resonance so the subject couldn't shield themselves. I taught them everything I knew, Edmund, and I knew more than anyone alive, because I designed the classification system myself when I was twenty-three years old and very proud of my precision."

Wren stirred in her sleep. Serah waited until the child settled, until the rain filled the silence again.

"Why?" Edmund asked quietly. Not accusatory. Worse than accusatory — genuinely asking, as if she might still have an answer that would save them both.

"Because I believed it," she said. "That's the part you have to understand. I wasn't cynical. I wasn't ambitious — well, I was ambitious, but that came later, after the belief had already done its work. When I joined the hunt I was nineteen and I had watched a resonance-mad woman burn down half of Marrowfield with everyone inside it. My father's shop was one of the ones inside it. I saw what an untrained gift could do, and I thought: *this is preventable*. Someone should find these people early. Someone should teach them, or contain them, or — and this is the lie I told myself for seven years — protect them from themselves."

She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. A posture she despised in prisoners. Here she was making it her own.

"So I mapped them," she went on. "Every gifted bloodline in the realm. Nine generations back where the records allowed. Village by village, family by family. Names, locations, strength of line, whether the gift ran hot or cold, whether it skipped generations. I built the great ledger myself — the *Vane Register*, they ended up calling it, which is the kind of joke the gods make when they want to be cruel. Fourteen thousand names. Maybe fifteen by the end."

Edmund had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock — she knew that stillness, had catalogued that too. This was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he dreaded.

"You said fourteen thousand," he said carefully. "How many were taken?"

She made herself meet his eyes. "All of them, eventually. Or nearly. The register wasn't a defense tool, Edmund. I found that out three years in, when I tried to divert a family from collection and discovered my own classifications were being used to prioritize harvest quotas. By then it was too late to burn it. Every copy was sealed in the palace archive under the usurper's — " she caught herself, corrected, "— under the Lord Protector's seal. I could no more destroy it than I could reach into the past and unsay my own name."

"*Usurper,*" Edmund repeated softly. "You said it plainly. You've been circling that word since the mines."

"Because I haven't had proof. Only pattern." She leaned forward, toward the coals, needing the heat on her face. "A king doesn't change overnight. Aldric IV was — I served him directly for two years before the change. He was cautious, pious, mediocre in the way most kings are mediocre. Then in one winter he became something else. Ruthless, tireless, wrong in ways that were almost right — every decree reasonable on its face, every policy aimed like a blade. His handwriting changed. His cipher changed. And the hunt changed from containment to *collection*, and nobody around him seemed to notice or dare notice, and I noticed, and I did nothing, because noticing felt like madness and madness gets hunt-captains hanged."

"And so you ran."

"And so I ran." She laughed, short and bitter. "Nine years of building the machine, and my grand act of conscience was to desert. I told myself I'd find proof from the outside. I told myself a lot of things."

The rain hammered the mill's broken roof. Somewhere in the dark, water dripped into a barrel with a slow, patient rhythm, like a clock counting down something she didn't want to name.

"There's more," she said. "And this is the part I've been choking on since Highmoor, and you deserve it plain, so I'm going to say it plain."

Edmund set down the strap. He folded his hands in his lap — those careful hands — and waited.

"The Vane Register isn't just a list of living gifts. It tracks lines. Suppressed lines. Generations ago, before the modern hunt, there were older purges — quieter ones, conducted not by soldiers but by agreements, payments, relocations. Families whose gifts were considered too strong to manage were *moved* — placed in regions where the gift couldn't wake. Dead valleys. Null zones. Places where magic cannot follow." Her voice dropped. "Highmoor is one of the oldest null zones in the realm. It isn't an accident of geography, Edmund. It was made. Carved out centuries ago, stone by stone, the way the palace underlevels were lined with suppression rock. And the families settled there weren't pilgrims. They were stock. Stored. Kept alive and kept silent, generation after generation, because someone — some office, some order older than the crown itself — wanted those bloodlines preserved even while suppressing them."

The fire cracked. A log collapsed into embers, sending up a brief constellation of sparks.

"My family," Edmund said. Just that. Two words, and she heard everything underneath them.

"Reyes," she said. "Line 41 in the Register. Marked in the oldest stratum of the record — the entries that predate the modern hunt by two hundred years. Original notation reads: *strong harmonic line; do not extinguish; maintain in null.* Do you understand what that means? Your ancestors weren't hidden in Highmoor to be saved from the hunt. They were hidden *by* it. Preserved. Like seed corn. Like a wine cellar. Your whole life — your debt, your ledgers, your valley that forgets — none of it was chance. It was maintenance."

He didn't move. For a long moment she thought he hadn't heard her, and then she saw his face in the ember-light and understood that he had heard her perfectly, and that some part of him had always known. Had felt the shape of the cage his whole life without ever seeing its bars. The tremors he'd suppressed. The longing for the pass he'd never been allowed through. The abbot who watched him too closely. All of it clicking into place behind his eyes, piece by piece, a lock turning open onto nothing good.

"You knew," he said finally. His voice was level. That was worse than shouting. "In the Narrows. The first day. You looked at my hands and you knew what I was, and you knew what that meant, and you said nothing."

"Yes."

"You let me trust you. You let me forge records for you. You let me burn my life down and follow you out of the only home I've ever had — and you knew, the entire time, that you were the one who wrote my family into the book that caged us."

"I wrote the *classification*," she said. "The suppression orders predate me by centuries. But yes. I maintained the system. I trained the people who would have collected you if the valley's null field had ever failed. If you want to hate me for it, hate me accurately — that's all I ask. Hate me for exactly what I did."

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't give me permission. Don't make it tidy." And now the level voice broke, just slightly, and she saw the fury underneath at last, and she was glad of it, because fury was honest in a way that composure never was. "You want me to hate you cleanly so you can bear it. I won't do you that kindness. I don't know what I feel. I've known you eleven days and you're the first person who ever looked at what I am without flinching, and you're also the architect of the machine that buried me alive before I was born, and both of those things are true at once and I can't — " He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Fourteen thousand names, Serah. I keep trying to make that number into a shape I can hold and it won't hold. It just keeps being fourteen thousand."

"I know," she whispered. "I count them every night. I've counted them every night for three years. It doesn't get smaller."

Wren shifted again, murmured something in her sleep — a fragment of a song, maybe, or a name — and curled tighter under the borrowed cloak. Both of them fell silent, watching the child breathe, as if her breath were the only neutral ground left between them.

It was Edmund who spoke next, and when he did, the fury had burned down to something harder and stranger.

"The palace," he said. "You said the register copies are sealed in the archive. And you said the underlevels are lined with suppression stone — the same stone as the valley. How do you know that?"

"Because I mapped them." She blinked. "What?"

"The palace underlevels. In my second year as hunt-captain, after the king changed, I was given access no captain should have had — the Lord Protector's own authorization, which I accepted without questioning because questioning was already dangerous by then. I surveyed the servant ways, the old cells, the sealed corridors beneath the throne floor. I drew them. Every passage, every door, every stretch of black stone that deadened my instruments when I walked it with a resonance-gauge." She stared into the coals. "I told myself it was threat assessment. I told myself a lot of things. But I remember thinking, even then, standing in that corridor of dead stone below the throne room, feeling my own gift go mute like a struck bell — I remember thinking, *this is a cage built for something enormous.*"

Edmund lowered his hands from his eyes. She watched him arrive at it, watch the thought take shape in him the way a figure takes shape out of fog.

"If the king changed in one winter," he said slowly, "and the hunt turned from containment to harvest — "

"Then whatever sits on that throne may not be the man who wore it before," she finished. "And if it feeds on gifts — and the harvest patterns suggest feeding, Edmund, the quotas, the priority rankings, the way captives are transported to the capital instead of executed locally — then it needs a supply. A steady supply. Of exactly the kind of bloodlines my register catalogued. Exactly the kind stored in places like Highmoor."

The rain seemed suddenly louder, filling the ruined mill, drowning the world outside it.

"So the valley," Edmund said. "My valley. My whole life. Was a larder."

"I don't know," she said. "I suspect. There's a difference, and I won't blur it to make myself look better or worse. But I know the palace's bones, Edmund. I know them better than anyone alive, because I drew them. And if there's proof of what wears the crown — proof we could put in front of the free cities, proof that would turn their cowardly neutrality into war — it's in the archive I helped build, down the corridors I mapped, behind doors I can open."

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she began composing her farewell in her head — the words she'd say at dawn when he took Wren south and walked away from her, which would be right, which would be deserved, which would leave her alone with fourteen thousand names and no one left to count them for.

Then he said: "Show me."

She looked up. "What?"

"Show me the maps. Tomorrow, on the road. Everything you remember — every corridor, every door, every gauge-reading. If you're going to be the one who knows the palace's bones, then I want to know them too." His jaw tightened. "Don't mistake this for forgiveness. It isn't. I don't know if it ever will be. But you're the only person in the realm who can get us into that archive, and I'm apparently the only person alive who can sing its frequency, and Wren has nowhere else to go, and the thing wearing the king's face is harvesting children out of valleys like mine. So." He exhaled, long and unsteady. "We go north. Together. And you tell me everything else you've been carrying, starting tonight, because I am done learning the shape of your lies after the fact."

"It isn't a lie if I tell it true," she said.

"No," he agreed. "But you waited eleven days. So start now, and don't stop."

And so she started. She talked until the fire died and the rain thinned toward the grey hour before dawn — talked about the inquisitors' training yards at Vellmark, about the resonance-tuning chambers beneath the assize halls, about the Lord Protector's voice and how it never varied in pitch, not once in nine years, not in grief or rage or triumph, as if the throat saying the words were an instrument played by something else. Edmund listened without interruption, asking only sharp, precise questions, a scribe's questions, a forger's questions, and somewhere in the fourth hour Wren woke and crawled between them without a word and fell asleep again with her head against Serah's knee, and neither of them acknowledged it, and neither of them moved.

When the light came, grey and merciless through the broken roof, Edmund stood, shouldered his satchel, and offered her his hand up off the mill floor. She took it. His grip was warm and did not linger.

"One more thing," he said, as they stepped out into the wet morning, Wren trotting between them with her chin set in that fierce way she had. "This register of yours. Line 41. Reyes. Is there anything else in the entry? Anything about what the gift actually *does*?"

Serah hesitated. Then she gave him the last truth she had been hoarding, the one she'd promised herself she'd never speak aloud.

"The original notation calls it a *keystone* line," she said. "Harmonic resonance of sufficient depth to attune other lines. To harmonize them. The old orders used a phrase I didn't understand until the night outside Highmoor, when you flattened four soldiers without touching them." She met his eyes. "They called it a voice that could teach the choir to sing."

Edmund went white. Down the muddy road ahead, the pass opened northward — toward Callowmere, toward the cities, toward the capital and the thing that sat upon its throne, waiting, perhaps, for exactly this.

"Then we'd better hurry," he said. "Before it learns we're coming."

* * *

They walked north through a country that grew emptier by the mile.
