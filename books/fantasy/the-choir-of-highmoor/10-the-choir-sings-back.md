## 10. The Choir Sings Back

The bells of Callowmere rang wrong.

Edmund had learned the city's rhythms in the three weeks since their arrival — the dawn bell that woke the fishmongers on the Salt Quay, the noon bell that scattered pigeons from the council spire, the evening bell that meant the taverns opened their shutters and the lamplighters began their rounds. He had copied ledgers in a refugee house on Tanner's Row, teaching letters to children who flinched at loud voices, and he had come to think of the bells as the city's heartbeat: steady, indifferent, alive.

This morning they rang ragged. Off-beat. One bell trailing a half-second behind the others, as though the ringer's hands had begun to shake.

He was standing in the courtyard of the House of Lanterns — the tumbledown hospice where the gifted refugees had gathered, because hunted things always find each other — when he felt it. Not heard. *Felt.* A pressure behind his eyes, a wrongness in his teeth, like the moment before lightning strikes when the air itself turns traitor.

Wren felt it too. The orphan had been braiding grass stems by the well, and now her hands went still and her head came up like a deer scenting smoke.

"Edmund," she said. "The air is *singing*."

It was singing. A note so low it lived below hearing, felt in the chest rather than the ear, and threaded through it — faint, obscene — a pattern he recognized. He had spent twenty-six years suppressing a gift that spoke to him in resonance, in harmonics, in the secret music beneath the world's noise. And this was that language, but spoken with a mouth full of broken glass.

"Get inside," he said. "Everyone inside. Now."

* * *

They came out of the eastern sky.

Not soldiers. Not yet. First came the instruments — great bronze shapes carried on the backs of six wagons each, shaped like nothing so much as enormous tuning forks crossed with cathedral bells, their surfaces etched with sigils that hurt to look at directly. Edmund had seen suppression stone all his life; Highmoor's valley was carved from it. But this was different. This was suppression stone's dark twin — not silence, but *amplification*. Resonance weapons, tuned and aimed.

Serah stood beside him on the hospice roof, and he watched her face do something he had never seen it do: go blank with recognition, then fill again with horror.

"I designed the prototypes," she said quietly. "Three years ago. Small ones. For disabling wards." Her knuckles were white on the parapet. "These are the size of chapels."

"How many people can they—"

"They were never tested on people." She said it flatly, the way one says a thing one has repeated to oneself many times without believing it. "But I did the mathematics, Edmund. At full resonance, tuned to a gifted mind—" She didn't finish. Below them, in the courtyard, forty-odd refugees were looking up at the sky, at the wagons cresting the eastern ridge, and most of them didn't understand what they were seeing.

Edmund understood.

The first instrument sang.

There was no explosion. No fire. The world simply *bent*. Sound became visible for half a heartbeat — a ring of pale distortion spreading from the lead wagon like a stone dropped in still water — and then it passed through the House of Lanterns, through the courtyard, through the people standing in it.

A woman named Maren, who could coax warmth from cold iron, screamed and collapsed, clutching her head. A boy of perhaps fourteen whose gift was small — he could make flowers open early — fell to his knees and vomited. An old man who had hidden his talent for sixty years simply folded sideways, like laundry slipping off a line, and did not get up.

Inside Edmund's skull, every nerve lit at once.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was being *played*. The weapon found the frequency of his gift — the same frequency the parasite on the throne used, he realized distantly, the same song, its song — and dragged it out of him by force, hauling his suppressed magic up into the light like a fish on a hook. He tasted copper. His vision doubled, trebled. Through the ringing he heard Wren crying his name, heard Serah shouting orders to no one, heard the second wagon beginning to charge.

And underneath all of it, he heard the others.

Forty frightened minds, forty scattered gifts, each screaming on its own frequency — Maren's iron-warmth shrieking high, the flower-boy's green magic keening low, a dozen others between them, discordant, panicking, tearing at themselves. Alone, each of them would be crushed the way the old man had been crushed. He could feel it happening already, the weapon's note sliding against theirs, finding the cracks, widening them.

Unless.

The thought arrived whole, the way his gift always arrived — unbidden, dangerous, certain. They didn't have to be forty separate screams. Screams could be made to *agree*.

"Serah!" His voice came out shredded. "The courtyard — get everyone into the courtyard, close together, and *keep them there*."

"What are you going to do?"

"Something stupid."

* * *

He went down among them on legs that barely obeyed him, and he knelt in the center of the panicked crowd, and he did the thing he had spent his entire life refusing to do.

He opened.

Not wide — he couldn't afford wide, not here, not with the valley's training worn thin and twenty years of dammed-up power pressing against the breach. But enough. Enough to let his gift rise through him like water filling a cracked vessel, enough to reach outward along the frequencies he could suddenly see laid over the world like threads of colored light.

*Maren.* He found her frequency first — hot orange, iron-smell, the taste of a forge. Her mind was a clenched fist around her own gift, strangling it in terror. *Maren, listen to me. Don't fight it. Give it to me.*

*I can't—* her voice came back raw, wordless, more feeling than speech — *it's pulling it OUT of me—*

*I know. Let it. I'll hold it.*

She was dying anyway. That was the arithmetic of it, the terrible clarity the moment gave him. Alone she would burn out in seconds. Together—

Her gift poured into him like molten metal down a channel, and he nearly blacked out from the heat of it, and then he caught it, shaped it, and *held* it — and turned, and offered it onward, a thread of orange light seeking the next mind.

The flower-boy next. Green-gold, springtime, impossibly fragile. Then a stonemason whose gift was patience made solid. Then a girl who could hear lies, a ferryman who could calm water, an old woman whose only talent was making bread rise perfectly, and Edmund took even hers, because even hers was a frequency, and every frequency could be made to sing together.

It was like conducting an orchestra of drowning people.

Each gift fought him at first — panic does that, fear makes everything dissonant — and each time he had to reach deeper, spend more of himself, wrap his own resonance around theirs like a hand closing over a struck bell. He could feel himself fraying. Blood ran warm from his nose, from his ears. Somewhere very far away Serah was shouting his name, and Wren's small fierce presence pressed against his side, her own tiny flicker of gift adding itself without being asked, a child's candle held up against a storm.

*Together,* he pushed at them, at all of them, words dissolving into pure harmony. *Not alone. Never alone. Sing WITH me—*

The third wagon fired.

The note came down on them like a falling cathedral, and Edmund did the only thing left to do. He stopped holding the choir and let it *go* — threw every gathered gift, every borrowed frequency, every scrap of his own buried inheritance upward and outward into a single shape.

A shield.

Not stone. Not steel. A chord — vast, layered, forty voices strong — a wall of agreed sound meeting a weapon built to break solitary minds. The two notes met above the House of Lanterns, and the air between them turned white, and the whole city of Callowmere heard a sound that no one who survived it would ever quite be able to describe: not thunder, not music, but the moment a scream becomes a hymn.

The resonance weapon shattered. All three of them. Bronze cracked like eggshells, sigils flaring and dying, and the shockwave of their breaking rolled back across the eastern ridge and took the wagons and their crews with it. Horses screamed. Men fled. And under the shield's fading dome, forty people knelt in a courtyard, breathing, alive, weeping without knowing why.

Edmund fell forward onto his hands.

* * *

He woke in a bed that smelled of lavender and blood, with Serah sitting beside him and gray light in the window, which meant either evening or dawn and he genuinely could not have said which.

"You've been unconscious for two days," she said, before he could speak. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Your nose bled for six hours. You stopped breathing once, briefly, and Wren screamed the whole hospice awake. The physician wanted to bleed you. I told her if she touched you with a leech I'd break her arm."

"That sounds like you."

"Don't joke." Her hand closed over his, hard. "Edmund. Do you understand what you did?"

"Made a lot of noise."

"You *harmonized* forty gifts under fire. In real time. People train decades and can't link two minds safely." She leaned closer. "The inquisitors' instruments recorded it. The survivors said the crews were carrying recording-crystals — standard practice, I know, I *instituted* standard practice — and when the wagons broke, the crystals survived. Whatever you sang, they have it. Your signature. Your exact resonance."

The meaning of it settled over him slowly, cold as valley water.

"They'll know me."

"They knew you before — a scribe, a fugitive, a name on a list. Now they know your *frequency*. There is exactly one mind in this realm that can do what you did, and it just announced itself to every listening instrument the usurper owns." She paused, and he watched her choose honesty over comfort, because that was who she was. "You're not hiding anymore, Edmund. You're not even a fugitive. You're the prize."

He lay back against the pillow and looked at the ceiling's water stains and thought about the moment the shield formed — the terrible beauty of it, forty terrified strangers becoming one voice. It had been the finest thing he had ever done. It had also, he understood now, been the loudest possible way to say *here I am*.

"Wren?" he asked.

"Asleep. She wouldn't leave your side until she dropped. She added herself to your choir, you know. Did you feel it?"

"I felt everything."

"Good." Serah's jaw tightened. "Because the council summoned us an hour ago. All of us — you, me, Wren, the survivors. There are riders coming up the river road flying no colors anyone recognizes, and the council wants to know why their neutrality didn't protect them." She rose, and in the doorway she stopped, her back to him.

"And Edmund — the crystals weren't the only things listening. When you sang, every gifted person within fifty miles felt it. Some of them are already walking toward Callowmere. Refugees who felt the shield go up and thought—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—thought someone was finally answering."

* * *

That night, Edmund climbed to the hospice roof because he couldn't sleep, and found Wren already there, wrapped in a blanket, staring east.

"They'll come back," she said. It wasn't a question. Children who had survived temples and hunts and pyres didn't ask hopeful questions.

"Yes."

"Will you be able to do it again? The singing?"

He considered lying to her, and decided she'd earned better. "I don't know. It cost more than I had. Something's... changed. I can feel my gift differently now. Like a door that won't fully close anymore."

Wren nodded solemnly, the way children nod at truths adults try to soften. Then she pointed east, past the walls, past the dark fields.

"Look."

On the horizon, at the edge of sight, small lights were moving. Not riders — too slow, too scattered, too many. Travelers. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe, strung out along the river road and the southern hills, converging on Callowmere like moths toward a lamp. People who had felt a shield of harmony rise against the usurper's weapons, and had risen from their hiding places, and had started walking.

Edmund stood beside the child in the dark, his gift humming restlessly beneath his skin like a plucked string that refused to fall silent, and understood that whatever he had done in that courtyard, it had not ended anything.

It had begun something. And both sides had heard it.

Behind him, down in the courtyard, Serah's voice called his name — sharp, urgent — and he turned to see her standing in the doorway with a message in her hand, her face lit pale by the lantern behind her.

"Edmund," she said. "You need to read this. It's from the capital."
