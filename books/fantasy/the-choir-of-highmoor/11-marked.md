## 11. Marked

The notice was nailed to the well-post in the village of Harrow's Bend, and Serah Vane read her own handwriting on it.

Not her hand, precisely — the inquisitors had copied her field reports into their proclamations for years, and she knew the cadence of her own intelligence the way a mother knows a child's voice in a crowd. *The fugitives travel with a child of approximately ten years. The man is a scribe, unremarkable, brown-haired, answers to Edmund Reyes. The woman is armed, dangerous, and formerly of His Majesty's Hunt. She is to be taken alive if possible.*

*If possible.* That was Aldric Voss's phrasing. Voss had been her second-in-command once, back when she still believed the hunt had a purpose beyond harvest. He'd learned everything she could teach him, including how to write a warrant that sounded like mercy while promising a pyre.

Below the text, rendered in careful woodcut: three faces. Hers, drawn from an old service portrait, younger and harder than she felt now. Edmund's, sketched from some temple record — they'd gotten his jaw wrong, made him look meek. And Wren.

They'd gotten Wren right. Of course they had. A child's face sold a proclamation; it made every farmer in the realm a hunter with a clean conscience.

"They used my ledger drawing," Wren said beside her. Not upset. Curious, almost, tilting her head at the image the way she might study a beetle. "I didn't know they kept one."

Serah's stomach turned. "The intake clerks sketch everyone who passes through a hunt station. It's standard." *It was standard because I ordered it standard,* she did not say. "I'm sorry, Wren."

"Why? You didn't draw it."

"I built the machine that drew it."

Wren considered this with the grave attention she gave everything, then shrugged. "Then we should steal it off the post. So fewer people see it."

And before Serah could answer — before she could explain that tearing down one notice in one dying village changed nothing, that there were four hundred more between here and the capital — the girl had already scrambled up the well-post, wrenched the nails free with her belt knife, and folded the paper into her coat like contraband sweets.

Edmund came around the corner of the chandler's shop carrying bread he'd traded for with the last of their coin, and stopped when he saw them. He looked tired. They all looked tired. Eleven days since Callowmere, since the choir, since his gift had reached out through forty terrified minds and knit them into something that had turned resonance weapons meant to kill into a shield that sang. Eleven days since the enemy's instruments had found him the way a lighthouse finds a ship.

"You're smiling," he said to Wren.

"I'm famous," Wren said.

"You're *marked*," Serah said, and heard how harsh it came out, and couldn't soften it, because softness would be a lie and they had no budget for lies anymore. "All three of us. Which means the plan changes. Again."

---

They gathered in the loft of the abandoned tithe barn at the edge of Harrow's Bend, where the roof still held and the smell of old grain masked the smell of fear-sweat. Below them, the southern refugees made their preparations — nineteen souls from the Callowmere choir who had chosen flight over fight, led by a one-eyed dyer named Maren who had once harmonized a burning house into standing still long enough for her children to climb out.

Serah spread her map on the floorboards and weighted the corners with stones. The map was hers from her captaincy, annotated in her own cipher, and looking at it was like reading her own confession in geographic form. Every road she'd mapped for the hunt's supply trains. Every village she'd flagged as harboring gifted bloodlines. Every safehouse — and she knew, with sick certainty, that Voss would have burned or booby-trapped every one of them by now.

"The southern route is done," she said. "Voss will expect us south. The free cities are the obvious refuge, so he'll blanket the roads with checkpoints and let the notices do the hunting. Every innkeeper, every toll-keeper, every child with a copper reward in their eyes." She moved her finger north along the river. "So we go the way no hunted thing goes. North. Toward the capital."

Silence. Maren's single eye found hers across the loft.

"Toward the throne," Maren said. "You're mad."

"I'm realistic. There's proof in that city — real proof, decoded letters, two-hand edicts — that the king is a prisoner and the purge is a harvest. We can't rally the free cities with pamphlets. They need the true king's voice, or they'll sit behind their walls until the usurper eats them one at a time. And the only people who can reach that voice are the three of us, because we're the only ones the instruments *can't easily find*—" she caught herself, corrected, "—couldn't easily find. Until Callowmere."

"Until you," Edmund said quietly. Not accusatory. Just precise, the way he was precise about everything, ink-stained fingers resting on the map's edge. "It found me. Whatever they use to listen — it heard me sing."

"Yes." She wouldn't flinch from it. He deserved better than flinching. "Which means we have days, not weeks, before they triangulate your resonance signature and send someone like me after us. Someone good. So we move tonight, we move small, and we stop being refugees." She looked at each of them in turn. "Refugees flee. We're going to have to become something else. Travelers. Pilgrims. A scribe taking his ward north to family, with a widowed aunt as escort. Boring. Forgettable. The most dangerous thing we can be is forgettable."

"And when boring fails?" Maren asked.

"Then we're armed, dangerous, and formerly of His Majesty's Hunt," Serah said. "At least the notice got that part right."

---

The parting came at dusk, and it was worse than any battle she'd stood in.

Maren embraced Edmund first, then Wren, holding the girl a long moment. "Keep them honest," the dyer told her.

"I keep them *alive*," Wren said. "Honesty is Edmund's job."

There was scattered, exhausted laughter at that — the kind laughter gets when it's doing the work of weeping. Nineteen people melted south into the barley fields in ones and twos, and Serah watched them go and made herself memorize each face, because she had sent enough people to their deaths to know the ritual of remembering mattered.

Then it was three. Three sets of footprints on the north road, and the whole weight of a kingdom's hatred pressing down on them like weather.

They walked until full dark, then off the road into a stand of black pines, and made a cold camp because firelight carried. Wren took first watch without being asked — she always took first watch, claimed she slept badly anyway, though Serah suspected the truth was simpler and harder: the girl had learned in whatever orphanage or gutter or temple corridor had raised her that sleep was when bad things came, and watching was how you fought them.

Serah sat against a pine trunk with her map on her knees, working by feel more than sight, planning the infiltration she'd sworn for eleven days she wasn't ready to plan.

"The servant ways won't work anymore," Edmund said from the darkness beside her. He'd gone quiet so often lately, listening to something inside himself, but tonight his voice was steady. "You said yourself Voss has your files. If you mapped those passages, he's sealed them."

"He sealed the ones I mapped." Serah traced the capital's ring walls by touch. "But I didn't map all of them. I mapped what the hunt needed — approaches, exits, choke points for containment. There are older ways underneath. Pre-dynasty. The archivist's letters mentioned cistern galleries under the palace hill that predate the current foundations. Nobody seals what nobody remembers."

"And if somebody remembers?"

"Then we die underground, and honestly, Edmund, at this point I've made peace with choosing my own ground."

He was quiet a moment. In the dark she heard him turn that over, weigh it, set it down. "Wren shouldn't come into the city."

"She'll never agree to stay behind."

"That's not what I asked."

Serah closed her eyes. "No. She shouldn't. But she will come, because the alternative is leaving her alone in a countryside papered with her face, and I won't do that. So instead we do what we should have done from the start." She opened her eyes, found his outline against the faint star-glow through the branches. "We stop hiding what she is. What all of us are. You said the choir worked because you taught them — thirty minutes of teaching, terrified people, resonance weapons screaming at them, and they held. Imagine what Wren could be with months. With years."

"She's ten."

"I was twelve when the Hunt found me and started training. The difference is they trained me to hunt my own kind." Her voice came out flat and cold and she let it. "You trained her to shield. Keep doing that. Teach her everything. On the road, in the camps, in the gaps between walking. Make her strong enough that when I finally get her killed, it takes effort."

"That's a terrible way to say you love her."

"It's the only way I know how to say it."

---

She woke before dawn to the sound of Edmund's voice, low and rhythmic, and lay still beneath her cloak to listen.

He was teaching. He'd found some trick of it in the last week — not the temple's hushed suppression, not the hunt's clinical drills, but something older and warmer, a call-and-response that sounded half like a lullaby and half like a lesson. Wren sat cross-legged across the dead fire pit from him, eyes shut, hands open on her knees, and between them in the air hung a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, bending and reforming as the girl's gift stuttered and steadied.

"Again," Edmund murmured. "Don't push it. Ask it."

"It doesn't listen when I ask."

"It listens when you ask *correctly*. Everything does. Ink listens to the pen. Stone listens to the chisel. Your gift isn't a wild animal, Wren. It's a choir member who's been told to be quiet her whole life. Sing *with* it, not at it."

The shimmer steadied. Held. Formed, briefly, impossibly, into the shape of a small bird before collapsing into sparks that made Wren laugh — actually laugh, bright and startling in the grey dawn, the first true laugh Serah had heard from her since Callowmere.

"Do it again!"

"No. Once is a miracle, twice is showing off, and showing off is how hunts find you." But Edmund was smiling too, and Serah saw it plain: this was what he was made for. Not the ledgers. Not even the great unbinding song that waited for him somewhere ahead like a cliff edge. This. Teaching frightened children that the thing inside them was not a curse to be buried.

He caught her watching. The smile faded into something more careful. "How long were you awake?"

"Long enough." She sat up, began rolling the map. "That bird. Was that her idea or yours?"

"Hers. Entirely hers." He rubbed his eyes. "Serah — when this is over. If this is over. Someone has to build schools for this. Real ones. Not temples that bury children in silent valleys, not hunt academies that sharpen them into weapons. Something else."

"That sounds like a problem for people who survive the next month."

"It sounds like a reason to survive the next month."

She looked at him — this debt-bound scribe who had walked out of a valley designed to erase him and somehow arrived, eleven days later, at a vision of a better world, while she had spent fifteen years building the machinery of the worse one. She wanted to tell him he was naive. She wanted, more dangerously, to believe him.

"We move in an hour," she said instead. "There's a ferry crossing at Thorn's Ford. After that, we're in crown territory proper, and everything gets harder."

---

Thorn's Ford nearly killed them, and it wasn't even the soldiers.

It was the ferryman's daughter, maybe fourteen, who took one look at Wren's face and went white as milk. Serah saw it happen — saw recognition flicker behind the girl's eyes, saw her glance toward the proclamations nailed by the crossing toll-house, saw the arithmetic begin: *a copper reward, a new dress, father says we're poor—*

Serah's hand was on her knife before thought caught up. Then Wren stepped forward, straight up to the ferryman's daughter, close enough that Serah's whole body tensed to move.

"You've seen the pictures," Wren said. Not a question.

The girl nodded, frozen.

"I'm not what they say. Neither are they." Wren glanced back at Edmund, then at Serah, and something passed over her young face that was far too old. "But you already know that, don't you? Because you've got it too. The humming. Inside. I can hear it from here."

The ferryman's daughter's hand drifted to her own sternum, unconsciously, the way people touch a wound.

"My mum hums," she whispered. "She thinks nobody knows. She goes down to the river at night and the reeds all lean toward her."

"Reeds do that around our kind," Wren said gravely. "It means she's strong." She dug in her pocket and produced, of all things, the stolen proclamation, and pressed it into the girl's hands. "Burn this. And when soldiers ask if you've seen us — you haven't. Can you do that?"

The girl looked at the paper, at the reward price printed beneath Wren's likeness, and then folded it away like a promise. "Cross free," she said. "Papa won't mind."

On the far bank, walking north with the river at their backs and the capital's distant smudge of smoke on the horizon, Serah finally spoke.

"That was reckless."

"That was necessary," Wren said.

"That was *both*, which is what makes it terrifying." Serah crouched to the girl's level, which she almost never did, and made herself hold her eyes. "Listen to me. What you did back there — hearing her gift, knowing what it meant — that's going to save lives someday. But it also just put our fate in a fourteen-year-old stranger's hands, and someday it'll be the wrong stranger, and there won't be a second chance. Do you understand the difference between brave and lucky?"

"Lucky is when it works?"

"Lucky is when it works *and you planned for it not to*."

Wren absorbed this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to every lesson, then nodded once. "Next time I'll plan for it not to work."

"Good." Serah stood, and found Edmund watching her with that expression he wore sometimes — the one that suggested he could see straight through the drill-sergeant armor to whatever soft, ruined thing she kept underneath, and that he intended to wait patiently until it surfaced.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Just thinking you're a better teacher than you think you are."

"I'm not teaching her. I'm keeping her alive long enough for you to."

"Same thing," Edmund said, and shouldered his pack, and turned north.

Behind them, far to the south, thunder rolled across a clear sky — or something that sounded like thunder, low and resonant and wrong, a vibration Serah felt in her back teeth rather than heard with her ears. Edmund stopped. Went very still, head tilted, listening to the frequency no ordinary ear could catch.

"What is it?" she asked.

His face, when he turned back to her, had gone pale as the ferryman's daughter's.

"That was the instruments," he said. "They've found my note. And they're singing it back."
