## 12. The Palace Beneath the Palace

The capital of Veymar announced itself long before they saw it — a bruise of smoke on the northern horizon, growing all day as they walked the pilgrim road among carts and refugees who did not know they were walking toward the thing that hunted them. By dusk the walls rose out of the plain, black limestone veined with quartz that caught the last light and threw it back red, like a wound seen through skin.

Edmund had never seen a city. Highmoor's temple village held four hundred souls and a bell tower; Callowmere had been a revelation of noise. This was something else entirely. The walls ran farther than he could track, studded with towers, and above them all the palace climbed in pale tiers against the darkening sky, its spires needled with banners bearing the crowned stag of a king who was not free.

"Vaskarren," Serah said quietly, beside him at the edge of the road where the pilgrims' flow bent toward the western gate. "I used to think it was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen."

"And now?"

"Now I see how many doors it has." She pulled her hood lower. Her face had gone leaner on the road north, the softness burned off it, and there were new lines around her eyes that hadn't been there in Highmoor. "And how few of them we'll need."

Wren stood between them, small hands buried in the pockets of an oversized coat, watching the city with the flat, unblinking attention of a child who had learned that wonder was a luxury. The boy had not spoken since the river crossing three days ago, when the ferryman's questions had come too close and Edmund had felt the old tremor rise in his chest and forced it down, down, down, the way he'd done his whole life. Wren had watched him do it. Had reached up without a word and taken his hand.

He still hadn't let go.

"We don't go through the gates," Serah said. "Not tonight. There's a place east of the wall — an old tannery district, mostly abandoned since the river silted. We sleep there, and tomorrow night we go under."

"Under," Edmund repeated.

"The palace has two skins." She said it the way she said everything now — stripped, precise, a woman rationing her own words like a soldier rationing bread. "The one they show ambassadors and the one the servants use. I mapped the second one. Ten years ago, when I was twenty-two and thought I was building a map for the crown's protection." Her jaw tightened. "It will be changed. Some of it. But stone remembers its shape even when men forget it, and the oldest ways — the ones cut before the dynasty — those they don't bother to guard, because no one living remembers them exist."

"Except you."

"Except me." She looked at him then, and in the fading light her eyes were the color of wet slate. "You can still turn back. Take Wren south. The others made it to the coast by now, if the gods are kind."

"You knew I wouldn't."

"I knew." Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "I wanted you to say it anyway."

* * *

The tannery district smelled of old salt and dead industry, its vats long dry, its sheds collapsed into gray skeletons. They found shelter in the loft of a rendering house whose roof still held, and Edmund lay awake most of the night listening to the city breathe beyond the wall — the groan of gates, the far-off clang of a watch bell, once, terrifyingly close, the rhythmic tramp of a patrol passing along the rampart road.

Beside him, Wren slept curled against his side like a burr. The boy's gift flickered even in sleep — Edmund could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel warmth from a banked fire: a small bright pulse of perception, seeing-through, knowing-where. It had saved them twice on the road already. It would get the child killed if anyone with an inquisitor's tuning fork ever came near him.

Serah sat awake at the loft's broken window, sharpening a knife she would probably never use openly again, her whetstone whispering in the dark.

"Tell me about the suppression stone," Edmund said.

The whispering stopped.

"In Highmoor," he went on, "the abbot kept blocks of it in the reliquary vault. Gray stone, heavy, cold even in summer. When they carried me past it as a child — I remember this, I remember being six and screaming without knowing why — it felt like drowning. Like the air itself pressing on my chest." He stared at the rafters. "The whole valley is laced with it. Under the soil. In the foundations of every building. That's why magic cannot follow into Highmoor. That's why *I* couldn't—"

"Yes," Serah said softly.

"How much of it is there? Where does it come from?"

She set the knife down. For a long moment she didn't answer, and when she did, her voice had the careful flatness of someone stepping onto ice they already know is thin.

"When I trained the inquisitors — the first cohorts, before the purge went public — part of the curriculum was materials. Resonance doctrine, we called it. How gifts respond to different substances, frequencies, geometries." She turned toward him. "There is a quarry in the Greymarches. It produces a stone that deadens resonance completely. The crown has controlled it for two hundred years. Officially it's used for prison construction. Unofficially—" she paused, and he heard her choose the next words the way a surgeon chooses an instrument, "—unofficially, the palace itself sits on a foundation of it. Whole sublevels. Corridors of it. They call it the Quiet Deep. Even the guards won't go down there."

Edmund felt the cold arrive in his chest before his mind caught up to it.

"A palace built on suppression stone," he said. "A king who lives above a cage."

"A king who may be *in* the cage," Serah said. "Think about what we found in the archivist's letters. Two hands on every edict. A cipher begging under coercion. If the usurper needed to keep the true king contained — truly contained, not just imprisoned but silenced, unreachable by any gifted ally who might try to reach him — where would you build the cell?"

"Not in a dungeon," Edmund whispered. "Dungeons have visitors. Dungeons have escapes."

"You'd build it underneath everything," she finished. "Below the throne. Below the floors. Somewhere the king could sit in his own great hall and never know his body was standing on top of his own prison." She was quiet a moment. "Or somewhere the thing wearing him could keep him close. Keep him *handy*."

Outside, the watch bell rang twice more and fell silent. Wren stirred against Edmund's side and murmured something in his sleep — a name, maybe, or just a sound — and settled again.

"Tomorrow night," Edmund said. "We go down."

"Tomorrow night," Serah agreed. And then, because she was who she was, she added: "Sleep, Edmund. Whatever's down there, it will want us tired."

* * *

They entered the underlevels through a coal chute.

It was, Serah explained as she pried the rusted grate loose in the black hour before dawn, originally a delivery way for the palace kitchens in some earlier century, before the district around it burned and was rebuilt higher, burying the old service roads under fifteen feet of city. The chute descended at a steep angle into brickwork slick with condensation, and Edmund followed her down with Wren between them, one hand on the boy's shoulder, the other trailing the wall, breathing air that tasted of ash and deep stone.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a corridor tall enough to stand in, running straight into darkness both directions. Old lamp brackets lined the walls, their iron scrolled in a style Edmund recognized from the temple's oldest books — pre-dynastic work, five centuries dead.

"No one has lit these in a hundred years," Serah murmured, running her fingers over a bracket. "But see — the mortar's been patched. Here, and here. Someone maintains this level. Someone comes down."

"Guards?"

"Guards leave boot-prints and oil drips. This is different. This is *care*." She took out a small shuttered lantern and opened its slide a finger's width. Light fell yellow and weak across the bricks, and Edmund saw what she meant: the patches were neat, deliberate, recent. Whatever this passage was, it was not forgotten. It was *kept*.

They moved. Serah led with the map in her head, counting junctions, reading the architecture the way Edmund read manuscripts — for the hand beneath the hand, the revision beneath the text. Twice she stopped them at branch passages where newer brickwork sealed older openings, and twice she found the seam and confirmed her route still lived beneath the renovations. Wren, silent as ever, touched the walls as they went, and once he tugged Edmund's sleeve and pointed back the way they'd come, shaking his head — *that way is wrong* — and Edmund trusted it absolutely.

An hour in, the character of the stone changed.

Edmund felt it before he understood it. A pressure, faint at first, like the memory of water in the ears. Then stronger. Then unmistakable — the same drowning weight he had known his entire childhood, the pressure that lived in the soil of Highmoor, that had pressed his gift flat for thirty years until he'd almost convinced himself he had nothing to press.

He stopped walking.

Serah turned. "What is it?"

"It's here." His voice came out strange, flattened, as if the passage itself were muffling him. He pressed his palm against the wall and the cold of it sank through his skin into the bone. "In the stone. It's *laced* through the stone." He looked ahead, into the lantern's small circle of light, and saw what he should have seen ten minutes ago: the bricks here were not the red-brown of the upper levels. They were gray. Heavy-grained, dense, shot through with dull metallic flecks.

Greymarch stone.

Suppression stone.

"This whole section," he said. "This isn't a service corridor anymore. We're inside it."

Serah lifted the lantern higher. The gray stretched away in both directions, swallowing the light. "The Quiet Deep," she breathed. "I thought it was a story the guards told. I thought it was *smaller* than this."

They stood very still. Even Wren had gone rigid, his small hand fisted in Edmund's coat, and Edmund realized the boy was feeling it too — the flattening, the smothering — and worse, perhaps, because Wren had never learned to live inside that pressure the way Edmund had. Wren had only ever known open air.

"We can turn back," Serah said, but her voice said she already knew the answer.

"No." Edmund started forward. "If they built this to hold him, then this is the way to him. Everything dead ends except the thing they're protecting."

The gray corridors went on. They branched, doubled back, descended in shallow steps worn hollow at their centers by feet far older than the dynasty. And everywhere, the stone. Edmund walked through it feeling like a man wading chest-deep through freezing water, his gift crushed to a pinpoint, his breath short, and yet — and this was the terrible clarity of it — *familiar*. This was Highmoor. This was his childhood rendered in architecture. The valley of his whole life was a single room, and he had just stepped out of it into the rest of the house.

Then Wren stopped.

The boy planted himself, both feet, and pointed down a side passage they would otherwise have passed — a narrow corridor sloping gently downward, its gray walls unpatched, unmaintained, untouched. But Wren's face had gone white, and his lips moved without sound, and when Edmund knelt to him the boy grabbed his collar with both hands and dragged him close and whispered, for the first time in three days:

"He's crying."

Edmund's blood went cold. "Who's crying, Wren?"

The boy's eyes were enormous. He pointed down the slope, into the dark, where the suppression stone pressed thickest.

"The man under the palace," Wren whispered. "He's been crying for a long time. Nobody hears him." A pause, and then, smaller: "Except me. And now you."

Serah had her knife out, though there was nothing yet to fight. Edmund rose slowly, his heart hammering against the crushing weight of the stone, and looked down the corridor where the boy pointed.

Far below, past the limit of the lantern's reach, something glimmered — a line of light where no light should be, gold-white, trembling, like a seam of dawn split open in the floor of the world. And rising up through the gray silence, so faint he might have imagined it, Edmund heard it too: not crying, exactly. Not words.

A voice. Frayed, ancient, patient beyond all reason, singing to itself in the dark the way prisoners sing — half prayer, half defiance — a melody with no name that Edmund's bones recognized before his mind did.

Because it was the song his mother used to hum, in the kitchen, in the years before Highmoor.

The family song. The Reyes lullaby.

Edmund took one step down the slope, then another, the suppression stone grinding against his gift like a boot heel on a spark, and behind him Serah said his name — once, sharply, warning or blessing, he couldn't tell —

and the voice below stopped singing,

and said, in the silence it left behind, clear as a bell rung against his skull:

*"You came. After all this time — you came."*
