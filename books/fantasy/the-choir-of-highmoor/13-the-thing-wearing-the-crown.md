## 13. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The servant ways beneath the palace smelled of dust and old tallow, and Serah moved through them the way water moves through cracks — without hesitation, without sound, as though her body remembered every turning even after ten years away.

"Left here," she whispered, at a junction where three tunnels met. "The map called this the Weepers' Stair. The servants who built it named their passages. It was the only thing they were allowed to own."

Edmund kept his hand on the wall and followed. Behind him, Wren breathed in small, controlled sips, the way Edmund had taught them — breath held shallow when fear wanted to make it loud. The child had not spoken since they'd entered the underlevels, but their hand had found the hem of Edmund's coat and stayed there, gripping, a small anchor of warmth in the cold.

They had been in the dark for six hours. Twice they had frozen while boots passed above them, heard through grates as a muffled drumming, like rain on a roof. Once they had passed a door banded in iron, and from behind it came a sound Edmund would spend the rest of his life trying to forget: a humming. Not a voice. Not weeping. A sustained tone, thin and wavering, that rose and fell like something breathing through a reed.

Serah had stopped at that door too. She had put her ear against the iron for a long moment, and then she had stepped back with an expression Edmund had never seen on her face — not guilt, which he knew by now, but something older and more helpless than guilt.

*Suppression stone,* she'd mouthed. *Same as Highmoor.*

Now the tunnel began to slope upward, and the air changed. Warmer. And threaded through it — faint, so faint Edmund might have imagined it — a vibration he felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears.

He stopped walking.

Serah turned. "What?"

"You don't feel that?"

She went still. Listening with her whole body, the way she did everything. "No."

"It's—" He searched for words and found none that weren't dangerous. "It's like the valley. The silence of the valley. But wrong. Inverted. Like someone took the quiet and made it sing."

Wren's grip tightened on his coat.

Serah considered him for a moment, and he watched her weigh it — the risk of going forward against the risk of stopping, the mission against the man. He had learned to read those calculations on her face over the long road north, and he had learned, too, that she almost never let the calculation win. That was the thing about Serah Vane that no tribunal would ever quite believe: beneath all the discipline and the ledgers and the terrible arithmetic of her years as hunt-captain, she ran on something fiercer than strategy.

"We're close," she said. "The archive maps put the old chapel of the inner court two levels up. If there's anything to see, it'll be there." She paused. "You can wait here with Wren."

"No," Edmund said.

"I thought you'd say that." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but its shadow. "Then stay behind me. And Edmund — whatever you see, whatever you *feel* — you do not act. You watch. You learn. We leave with information or we don't leave at all."

He nodded, and they climbed.

* * *

The passage ended behind the wall of what had once been a chapel gallery — a narrow balcony of carved stone overlooking a space below, screened by wooden louvers gone gray with age. Serah eased one slat aside with two fingers, and Edmund looked through the gap into the place where the king of Vhalen worshipped.

Except no one had worshipped here in a long time. The pews had been dragged to the walls. The altar remained, but it had been draped in black cloth, and around it the floor had been cleared in a wide circle, and the circle was ringed with stones.

Edmund knew those stones. His whole body knew them before his mind caught up — a crawling familiarity, a homesickness for a prison. Suppression stone, cut and polished and set upright like standing graves, twelve of them, and between each pair of stones knelt a figure in gray chains.

Prisoners. Eight of them. Gifted ones — he could feel it now, could feel them the way you feel other bodies in a dark room, each one a small trembling heat. They knelt with their heads bowed and their hands bound, and they were silent, and their silence was the worst thing in the room, because it was not the silence of peace. It was the silence of people who had screamed themselves empty.

And on the altar's steps, in a chair that might once have held a bishop, sat the king.

Edmund had seen the king's face on coins and proclamations his whole life — the strong jaw, the heavy brow, the eyes that looked slightly past the viewer, as royal eyes always did. The face below him now was that face, and it was not. The jaw had gone slack. The skin hung as if the bones beneath had been borrowed from a larger man. And the eyes—

The eyes were open. They were open and they were moving, quick and darting, and they were *afraid*, and Edmund understood, with a lurch that nearly buckled him, that he was looking at the true king. Looking at him through the windows of his own skull, from somewhere very deep inside, where he had been screaming for years into a body that no longer answered him.

Because the thing that wore the crown was sitting slightly wrong in the chair. Its posture was almost human — it had studied humans, clearly, the way a scholar studies a dead language — but the weight of it settled differently, lower, coiled, and when it turned its head it did so in small precise increments, like a bird deciding whether a thing was food.

Beside the chair stood a figure in white and gold — an inquisitor, robed, holding a ledger. The usurper spoke to him, and its voice carried up to the gallery with terrible clarity: mild, unhurried, almost gentle.

"Begin with the girl."

Two guards hauled one of the kneeling prisoners to her feet — young, maybe sixteen, her wrists raw inside the chains. They walked her to the center of the circle, between the suppression stones, and Edmund felt what happened next before he saw it.

The stones *sang*.

Not aloud. Not in any air. They sang in the register where his gift lived — that deep, hidden frequency he had spent his whole life smothering, the tremor in his hands over temple ledgers, the hum he'd felt in the tunnel below. The suppression stones reached down into each prisoner and pulled, and the girl in the circle arched backward, and light came out of her.

Not metaphorical light. Not the glow of storybooks. It poured from her mouth and eyes and the seams of her clenched fists like smoke given a color, and it streamed — Edmund's stomach turned — it streamed *upward*, toward the throne, toward the slack-faced king, and the thing wearing him opened its mouth and drank.

The girl collapsed. Guards dragged her back to her place between the stones. Another prisoner was lifted, another vessel tipped and drained. The usurper sat through it with the patience of a farmer at harvest, and with each prisoner its body grew — not larger, exactly, but more *present*, the slack face filling, the shoulders squaring, the borrowed flesh plumping as though fed.

"That's why the hunt," Edmund breathed. It wasn't a question. It was the whole architecture of two years of horror assembling itself in his chest at once. "Not purging. Not cleansing. *Harvesting.* Every gifted person burned in a pyre, every bloodline mapped — they weren't destroying the gift. They were gathering it. Delivering it. Feeding it to—"

"To that." Serah's voice beside him was flat and dead, the voice she used when feeling would kill her. "I built the supply lines. I trained the men who fill them." Her hand on the louver had gone white-knuckled. "I told myself I was protecting the realm from wildfire. I was stocking a larder."

Below, the last prisoner was drained and dragged away. The circle stood empty. And the thing in the king's body rose, stretched — a luxuriant, uncoiling motion, like a serpent settling into new skin — and then it stopped.

Its head turned.

Toward the gallery.

Edmund's heart stopped with it.

For a long moment nothing happened. The usurper stood in the empty circle of stones, its newly-fed face tilted upward, nostrils flaring slightly — and Edmund realized, with a cold that started in his spine and spread outward, that it wasn't looking. It was *listening*. The way he listened. The way he had felt the prisoners' heat in the dark.

It was listening for resonance.

"Don't move," Serah whispered, so softly the words were barely breath. "Don't think loudly. Don't—"

But it was already too late, because Edmund had done the one thing he could not help doing. He had reached, instinctively, desperately, for the small trembling heats of the prisoners being dragged out through the far door — checking, the way you check for a heartbeat — and in reaching he had *touched* the frequency of the room, and the frequency had touched him back.

The usurper smiled.

It was the king's smile, worn like a mask two sizes too large, and when it spoke, it did not speak to the inquisitor or the guards. It spoke into the air of the chapel, into the stone, into the frequency where Edmund hid — and the voice arrived inside Edmund's skull as if it had always lived there.

*"There you are."*

Edmund's vision whited at the edges. Beside him, Serah had drawn a knife he hadn't seen her reach for; Wren had both fists in his coat. But the voice ignored them. It poured into him alone, warm as bathwater, patient as rot.

*"Do you know how long I have listened for a voice like yours? The others are instruments. Bells. You — you are a choir entire. Who taught you to hide so beautifully? Who buried you in that lovely silent valley?"*

Edmund tried to close himself off, to do what he had done his whole life — shrink, suppress, go small and gray and harmless. But the presence slid along the edges of him like fingers testing a lock, and it laughed, low and delighted, and the laugh vibrated in his molars.

*"You cannot hide from me in your own key, little song. I am the key. I am every note this realm has ever sung. And I am going to open you like a letter."*

"*Edmund.*" Serah's whisper, sharp as a slap. "*Come back.*"

He came back. Blinking, shaking, tasting copper. Below, the usurper had turned away, already bored, giving orders to the inquisitor in its mild harvested voice — but twice more, as they withdrew from the louvers and crept back down the Weepers' Stair, Edmund felt it brush against the edges of his mind, lightly, almost affectionately, the way a spider checks its web.

* * *

They did not speak until they were four levels down and three turnings away, in a disused cistern where the drip of water covered whispers. Serah lit no lamp. They sat in the dark, and Edmund discovered his hands were still trembling, and pressed them flat against the wet stone floor to stop them.

"It spoke to me," he said.

"I know. Your face—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully. "What did it say?"

"That it had been listening for me. That the others are bells and I'm a choir." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and cracked in the dripping dark. "It called my hiding beautiful. It said it buried me in a silent valley — Serah, it *knows*. It knows what Highmoor is. It knows what I am. It's known for—"

"For generations." Her voice was grim. "Your family line was marked for suppression before your grandfather was born. I read the file myself, years ago, and I never asked who wrote it, because the answer was always *the crown*, and questioning the crown was not a thing hunt-captains did." He heard her shift in the dark, heard the old shame grind in her voice like sand. "It wasn't the crown. It was that. It has been pruning the realm for a hundred years. Cutting the loud voices, keeping the garden quiet, and now it's harvesting everything it let grow."

"And the king?"

"The king is alive in there." She said it with certainty, and he loved her for it. "You saw his eyes. Whatever that thing is, it wears him, it doesn't replace him. There's a seam, Edmund. There has to be a seam."

"There is." And even as he said it, Edmund understood that he had known since the chapel, known in the marrow-deep way he knew the pitch of a room. "That's the horror of it. When it touched me, I felt its — its shape. Its frequency. It isn't some alien song. It sings in *my* register. The same resonance. The same gift, grown enormous and hollow and hungry." He stared into the blackness where her breathing was. "You can't kill a frequency, Serah. You can't put a sword in it. It would shed that body like a coat and pour into whoever stood closest — into me, maybe. Into Wren."

"So what do we do?"

"We do what you said. We find the seam." His voice steadied as the shape of it came clear, terrible and exact. "It's fused to him, but fusion has a note — the note where two things were joined. If someone could find that note, and sing it — sing *against* it, the way you break a glass with sound — the binding would shatter. Not the host. Not the king. Just the joining." He swallowed. "But it would mean getting close enough to touch its full attention. Close enough for it to pour itself into you while you worked. And it would mean someone whose gift sits in exactly that register."

The cistern dripped. Somewhere far above, muffled by many floors of stone, the palace went about its occupied evening — boots, doors, the ordinary machinery of a stolen kingdom.

"Edmund," Serah said quietly. "How many people in this world sing in that register?"

He closed his eyes. He thought of the valley that forgot, and the abbot who had always known, and the parents he had never been allowed to remember, and the trembling in his hands over twenty years of temple ledgers that had never once been a weakness.

"One," he said. "As far as I know, in all the world — one."

The water dripped. And in the dark, Serah reached across the wet stone and found his hand, and held it, and neither of them said the thing that all three of them were thinking:

The usurper already knew his name.
