## 14. Wren's Price

The mill outside Harrowgate had been empty for three years, and it still smelled of flour and rot in equal measure — a smell Serah Vane had come to associate with every safehouse she'd ever trusted too long.

She stood at the broken window with a spyglass she'd taken off a dead man whose name she made herself remember, watching the road that wound down from the market town. Wren was on that road somewhere. The girl had left before dawn with the last of their coin and a list written in Edmund's careful hand: salt pork, lamp oil, bandages, and — because Wren had insisted, and Edmund had not had the heart to refuse her — a paper of honey sweets from the confectioner on Tanner's Row who still traded with anyone who could pay.

"She'll be back by midday," Edmund said behind her. He was mending a strap on their pack, his hands moving in that patient, deliberate way of his, as if the world were a ledger he could set right line by line. "The roads were quiet when I scouted them yesterday."

"The roads are quiet until they aren't." Serah lowered the glass. "That's how I built it, Edmund. Quiet roads. Empty villages. People learn to stay indoors, and staying indoors looks like quiet."

He didn't answer. He never argued with her about the hunt anymore; he only went still, the way he did when a ledger didn't balance, and waited for her to finish saying what she needed to say. It was a kindness she hadn't earned and couldn't refuse.

Three weeks since Callowmere. Three weeks since the choir sang and Edmund's name went up on every board from the river towns to the capital — *the Resonant*, the broadsheets called him, in letters an inch high, with a woodcut likeness that flattered him shamefully. Three weeks since they'd split from the southern refugees and turned north with a child, a decoded cipher, and a plan that required them to be no one.

And for three weeks, Wren had been the reason any of it held together. She cooked. She kept watch with a seriousness that would have been funny if it weren't so necessary. She called Edmund *teacher* without irony and Serah *captain* with a great deal of it, and between the two of them she had stitched something neither adult had been able to stitch alone: a reason to keep moving that wasn't just fear wearing another coat.

Serah checked the sun. Past midday now.

*She's fine,* Serah told herself. *Tanner's Row is crowded. Crowds are slow.*

But her hands remembered what her mind was trying not to calculate: that Wren was eleven years old and marked in the rolls — Serah herself had signed the original entry, eight years ago, a bloodline notation in a neat clerk's hand, *Vane, verification* — and that the usurper's inquisitors paid informants by the head.

* * *

They came for her at dusk, though Serah didn't know it yet. What she knew first was the sound: hoofbeats on the north road, too many, too fast, and then a riderless pony trotting into the mill yard with its saddlebags torn open and a paper of honey sweets scattered across the mud like bright coins.

Edmund reached the door before she did. She watched him stop dead at the sight of it — watched something go out of his face and something else come in, something vast and barely leashed, so that the air around him seemed to tighten like a drumhead. The flour dust in the rafters began, very faintly, to hum.

"Edmund." She put her hand flat against his chest. Through his shirt she could feel his heartbeat, fast and wrong. "Edmund. Listen to my voice. Not the resonance. Me."

"They took her." His voice came out level, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "There were six of them at least. They took her into town."

"Then we get her back."

"With what?" He turned on her, and for one unguarded moment she saw the full architecture of his grief — the orphan ledger, the forged records, the abbot's betrayal, all of it stacked behind his eyes. "We're three people in a ruined mill. You taught me to count odds, Captain. Count them."

"I'm counting," Serah said quietly. "But you're not going to like my arithmetic."

* * *

The arithmetic was this: Harrowgate had a garrison of forty, a garrison commander named Pell who had once served under her and hated her for deserting, and a single holding cell beneath the tollhouse where they kept prisoners overnight before the wagons came. Wagons, plural — Serah had seen the schedule in a captured dispatch. Every gifted prisoner taken in the eastern marches went south to the capital in weekly convoys, and the convoy out of Harrowgate left at dawn.

"So we have one night," she said, crouched over a map scratched into the mill floor with a knife point. "One night, one cell, forty soldiers, and a commander who knows my face better than his own mother's."

"Thirty-nine," Edmund said. "You forget I can do things now."

"And that's exactly why we don't use them." She looked up at him. "The moment you sing, every instrument the usurper has within fifty miles turns toward us like a compass needle. We burned our cover in Callowmere. If we burn it again here, there's no second mistake. No next village. We die on this road, and the cipher dies with us, and the king stays on that throne with that thing inside him, and everyone we left in Callowmere died for nothing."

"Then leave me here." He said it simply, terribly, the way a man says a thing he has already decided. "I'll walk into the garrison at dawn and give myself up. They'll trade me for her — that's what this is, isn't it? That's why they took her and left the pony alive to tell us. It's not a capture. It's an offer."

"How do you know that?"

"Because it's what I would do." His mouth twisted. "It's what the thing in the palace would do. It thinks in trades, Serah. It fed on prisoners in cells under the palace — it doesn't want Wren, it wants leverage. And I am the most valuable piece on its board."

"No." The word came out of her harder than she meant it, and she heard in it, distantly, the hunt-captain's voice, the voice that had ended arguments in war rooms for a decade. She softened nothing anyway. "No. You walk into that garrison and you are in a suppression cell by nightfall, and by the next dawn you're in a wagon, and whatever you are — whatever you could become — gets poured down that thing's throat, and then it comes for the rest of us with your voice in its instruments. You don't get to make that trade. It's a bad trade."

"It's the only trade where nobody dies tonight."

"Somebody always dies tonight," Serah said. "That's the lesson of my entire life. The question is whether you choose who, or let them choose for you."

The silence stretched between them, taut as wire. Outside, the wind moved through the broken millwheel with a sound like slow breathing.

"You signed her name in a roll eight years ago," Edmund said at last, very quietly. "You told me that. In the confession you thought would end everything between us. Her bloodline, your hand, your signature."

Serah felt it land like a blow, because it was true, and because she knew what he was doing with it — not cruelty, never cruelty, but precision. He was showing her the shape of the debt.

"Yes," she said.

"Then help me pay mine back properly. Not with strategy. With her."

* * *

They went in over the millrace, because Serah had built the tollhouse's defenses herself — or ones like it, a dozen times, in a dozen towns, and the men who copied her designs always made the same mistake: they trusted the water. A millrace was noise, cover, and cold enough to kill a swimmer's nerve. Nobody watched it after dark.

She went first, Edmund behind her, both of them stripped to shirts with their gear wrapped in oilcloth above their heads. The water hit her like a fist, black and grinding, and for thirty seconds the world was roar and cold and the memory of every bad decision that had led her to swimming a frozen race at midnight to steal a child out of a cell she had designed the blueprint for.

Then gravel under her boots, and the shadow of the tollhouse wheeling overhead, and Edmund's hand finding her arm in the dark — steady, warm, human.

Inside, the plan was simple because simple plans survived contact. Serah took the two sentries at the stair with a garrote and a knee, fast and silent, and hated how easy it still was, how her body remembered this work the way Edmund's hands remembered script. Edmund stood at the cell door with his palm flat against the iron, listening — actually listening, in the way only he could — and after a moment his face changed.

"Two guards below. And her." A pause. "She's singing."

"What?"

"Under her breath. Some temple hymn, I think — the abbot's evening canticle." Something moved through his expression that was half terror and half fierce, blazing pride. "She's keeping her gift steady so they can't map it. Eleven years old, Serah. She learned it from watching me."

"Then let's not waste the lesson." Serah pulled the keys off the unconscious sergeant. "Cell first. Sentiments after."

The lock turned. The cell beyond was four feet of stone and straw, and Wren sat in the middle of it with her knees drawn up and her lips moving around a hymn, and when the door opened she looked up with eyes that were dry and furious and eleven years old all at once.

"Took you long enough," she whispered.

"We're leaving," Edmund said, and gathered her up, and for one moment — one whole breath — Serah allowed herself to believe the night might cost them nothing but cold.

* * *

It cost them more than that.

The alarm came from above — a sentry who should have been asleep, a lantern, a whistle — and then the tollhouse was full of boots and shouting, and Serah was fighting in a stairwell with no room to swing, taking a blade along her forearm, putting a man down hard against stone. Edmund got Wren behind him and did the thing they had sworn not to do.

He sang.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it — a thread of sound, almost gentle, and the stairwell filled with pressure like the moment before lightning. Torches guttered. Men staggered, clutching their ears, their weapons ringing on stone as their hands forgot them. Serah felt it pass through her too, found her heartbeat and steadied it, and understood in one vertiginous instant what the usurper's instruments must feel: a beacon lit in the dark, unmistakable, naming itself.

"Run," Edmund gasped, blood at his nose, his eyes unfocused. "Now. I can't hold it and run—"

So Serah ran, with Wren's wrist locked in her grip, out the water-gate and along the race and up into the hills while behind them the night lit with signal fires, one, then three, then seven, a chain of burning answers marching away south toward the capital. Edmund caught up at the tree line, gray-faced, staggering, and Wren broke from Serah and hit him at a dead sprint and held on, and he held her back, and Serah stood watch over them both with her bleeding arm pressed to her side and counted the fires.

Seven. By morning there would be companies on every road.

"We have the girl," she said, when Edmund finally looked at her. "We lost everything else. Name, route, timing, the northern approach — all of it's burned. From tonight we're not travelers, we're quarry, and the whole realm is the field."

Wren lifted her head from Edmund's shoulder. There was straw in her hair and a bruise coming up on her cheekbone, and her chin was set in a way that Serah recognized with a jolt — she'd seen that exact jaw on officers receiving impossible orders.

"Then we stop traveling," Wren said. "Teacher, you've got the cipher. Captain, you know the servant ways into the palace. Everyone keeps saying we need to get there before the army does." She wiped her nose with the back of her hand, fiercely, like a much smaller child asserting herself. "So let's get there."

Serah looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the chain of fires burning on the southern horizon, marking the road they could no longer safely take — and the darker country west of it, the old mining roads and dead forests that no army watched because no army believed anything but death lived there.

"The Graywaste," he said slowly. "Through the suppression fields."

"It will hurt you," Serah said. "Crossing ground soaked in that stone — it will be like drowning in silence. It might burn the gift out of you entirely, or worse."

"Wren's price was almost paid tonight," Edmund said. "I won't put her in a wagon's path again because I was afraid of quiet." He shouldered the pack, wincing. "West, then. Through the Graywaste. And Serah—" He paused, and in the firelight his face was older than she had ever seen it. "When we come out the other side, if there's anything left of me that can sing — we go straight at the throne. No more running. No more trades."

Above them, far to the south, the signal fires burned on like a rosary being counted, bead by bead, all the way to the capital.

And in the highest tower of that capital, Serah knew — with the certainty of a woman who had built the machinery now hunting them — something had just opened its eyes.
