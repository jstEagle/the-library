## 15. Armies at the River

The river had a name before the war — the Vell, slow and brown and unremarkable, famous only for the eels the Callowmere fishmongers sold on market days. Now it was a line drawn in water, and on its far bank the world was burning.

Edmund stood on the levee above the crossing town of Brastholm with the decoded letters in a leather case against his chest, watching smoke rise from the far shore in a dozen gray columns. The wind carried the smell of it: wet ash, charred thatch, something sweeter underneath that he refused to name. Three days ago the villages over there had been villages. Now they were fuel.

"They crossed at the fords last night," Serah said beside him. She had her spyglass up, though there was little to see beyond the smoke. "Two columns. Inquisitors riding with each one — I can feel them from here." She touched her sternum, where the resonance sat. Everyone who had survived Callowmere could feel it now, a faint wrongness on the air like a tooth aching in a jaw that wasn't theirs.

"How many?"

"Enough." She lowered the glass. Her face had gone thin since the choir; they all had. "Twenty thousand, maybe more, if the levies from the eastern marches answered their summons. And they will have. Fear answers faster than loyalty."

Wren stood at Edmund's other side, small and grim in a borrowed soldier's coat cut down to fit, a sword too heavy for her slung across her back because she refused to be parted from it. She hadn't spoken much since the rescue — since the trade Edmund had forced, since the moment they'd pulled her out of an inquisitor's wagon with rope burns on her wrists and a silence in her that hadn't fully lifted. But she'd insisted on coming north with them, and when Edmund had asked why, she'd looked at him as if he were simple.

"Because you came for me," she'd said. "Nobody comes for anybody, anymore. So I'm coming with you."

Now she tugged his sleeve. "They're waiting for you. The herald came again."

"I know."

"You're stalling."

"I'm memorizing the river," Edmund said. "In case it's the last time it looks like a river instead of a battlefield."

Wren snorted — the first sound close to laughter he'd heard from her in days — and Serah allowed herself half a smile before the weight settled back onto her shoulders. Then they turned together and walked up from the levee into Brastholm, toward the manor house where the free cities' war council was discovering, hour by hour, how difficult it was to agree on anything.

* * *

The council chamber had been a wool merchant's ballroom once. Its chandeliers still hung from the ceiling, draped now in canvas against the draft, so that the light fell strangely, in soft blinded columns. Around the long table sat the delegates of five cities — Callowmere, Vessany, Osterhale, the twin towns of the Larch, and grey old Dunmark whose delegate seemed to have been carved from the same stone as her city — along with generals, guildmasters, a river-captain who kept glancing at the window as though her boats might escape without her.

They had been arguing for two days. Edmund had listened to most of it from the gallery, and he had learned the shape of the argument the way he'd once learned the shape of a ledger: not by reading every line but by understanding what the sums wanted to be.

Vessany wanted to fortify the river and let the usurper's army break itself against the crossings. Osterhale wanted to retreat south and trade land for time. The Larch twins wanted to fight each other about tariffs even now. Dunmark's delegate, Maren Ostreth, wanted — as far as Edmund could tell — simply to disapprove of everything until the world ended and she could say she'd disapproved of it first.

And through all of it ran the question no one would say aloud, the question that had hollowed the room out: *what are we fighting for?* Not the cities — cities could bend. Not freedom — freedom was an abstraction that starved quickly. They needed a king. Or rather, they needed the thing a true king's authority provided: a reason for soldiers to stand on a cold riverbank and die on purpose.

"Master Reyes." The Callowmere delegate, Aldous Crane, spotted him in the gallery and beckoned with the weary courtesy of a drowning man hailing another drowning man. "Perhaps you can give us something useful. You've come from the capital. Tell us what's coming."

Every face turned upward. Edmund descended the stairs slowly, aware of the leather case against his chest, aware of Serah taking her place by the door with the watchful stillness she wore like armor. He set the case on the table and did not open it yet.

"What's coming," he said, "is twenty thousand soldiers who believe they serve their king. That's the whole problem. You cannot beat them with arguments, because they aren't fighting for an argument. They're fighting for a crown. Half of them are good men."

"Then what do you propose?" Maren Ostreth's voice was gravel. "We've heard proposals until the word has lost meaning."

Edmund opened the case.

He laid the letters out on the table one by one, in the order the dying archivist Corvane had given him, in the order that told the story best. The royal edicts bearing two hands — the flowing court hand of the king's secretaries, and beneath certain phrases, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, a second script that shook. The cipher, repeated in the margins of private letters, the same six symbols again and again until Edmund had understood they weren't code at all but a man spelling out his own captivity in the only way left to him. *I am held. I am held. I am held.*

"These are the king's hand," Edmund said. "Not the usurper's imitation of it — the king himself, writing under coercion, hiding messages inside edicts the usurper dictated. We decoded them in Callowmere. There are forty-one letters here spanning three years. The pattern is consistent. The man on the throne is alive, conscious, and prisoner in his own body, and the thing wearing him has been harvesting the gifted to keep itself fed."

He let the silence sit. He had learned that from Serah: the truth lands harder if you don't chase it.

It was a general from Vessany — Halvard, broad and sunburned, a soldier's face arranged around genuine distress — who finally spoke. "We heard rumors. Whispered things after Callowmere. But rumors are cheap, Master Reyes. How do we know these letters are what you say? Forgeries travel faster than armies."

"Because I can show you," Edmund said. "That's the other reason I've come."

He looked at Serah. She gave him the smallest nod.

"The king is still in there," Edmund said. "Still reaching out. And there is a way to reach back — a way to unbind him from the thing that holds him. It requires someone who can sing the parasite's own frequency back at it. Someone gifted, trained, and willing to walk into the throne room and likely not walk out." He paused. "I'm going to do it. But an unbinding alone won't win your war. What wins your war is this: the moment the king is freed, he can speak. His voice, his seal, his authority — broadcast from the capital itself, to every garrison and every levy column in the realm. And I promise you, General Halvard, most of those twenty thousand soldiers on the far side of your river do not want to be there. Give them a lawful king to follow home, and they'll lay down their arms without a single charge across your ford."

"And if you fail?" Ostreth asked.

"Then I die in the throne room," Edmund said, "and you'll have to hold the river yourselves. Which brings me to what I actually came to ask."

He spread a map across the letters — Serah's map, drawn from memory, the capital's approaches rendered in her precise hunt-captain's hand. "You need time. Not much. Ten days, perhaps twelve. Long enough for us to reach the palace through the underlevels and do the work. The usurper's army will move fast once it knows the king is in danger — which means it must not know. So you hold the Vell. Loudly. Every crossing, every ford, every bridge. Make them believe the war is here, at the river, so their eyes never turn north to the capital."

"Hold the Vell," Halvard repeated flatly. "Against twenty thousand and their inquisitors. With what? Five cities that can't agree on the price of bread?"

"With each other," Edmund said. "Which is the same problem, I know."

Laughter moved through the room — brief, bitter, real. And then, unexpectedly, Wren stepped forward from beside the door. She had been told to stay by the wall. She came to the table anyway, chin up, and Edmund saw several delegates startle at the sight of her: a child in a soldier's coat with rope scars at her wrists.

"I was in one of their wagons," Wren said. Her voice didn't shake. Edmund marveled at that, and hated that she'd had to learn it. "There were eleven of us in the wagon. Two made it out. Me, because Captain Vane and Master Reyes came. The others—" She stopped, steadied herself. "The soldiers guarding us talked. Most of them think they're serving the king. When they found out what the inquisitors did to us, some of them cried. One helped me get loose before the rescue even came. If you put the true king's voice in their ears, they'll turn. I know they will. I heard them want to."

The silence after she finished was different from the silences before it. Edmund watched it work on the delegates — watched Halvard look away, watched the river-captain stop glancing at the window, watched even Ostreth's carved face crack just slightly around the eyes.

It was Serah who sealed it. She stepped forward, and the room recognized her — of course it did; her face had been on recruitment posters and tribunal benches for fifteen years — and she did not flinch from that recognition.

"My name is Serah Vane," she said. "I built the hunt. I trained the inquisitors. I mapped the bloodlines they're using to choose their victims, and every death on those pyres passes through my signature. I am not asking your trust. I am telling you that everything I know says this plan is the only one that ends it — and that I will spend my life proving it, starting with the corridor beneath the palace." She met their eyes, one by one. "Hold the river. Buy us ten days. And then help me build the kind of realm where no one ever has to do what I did again."

Ostreth looked around the table. "All in favor of holding the Vell."

Five hands rose. Even the Larch twins, together, for the first time in recorded history.

* * *

They spent the evening preparing to leave, and Edmund spent it failing to sleep.

He sat in the chamber they'd been given — a wool merchant's guest room, absurdly comfortable, the bed turned down as though comfort were still a thing that applied — with the decoded letters spread around him, copying the cipher onto smaller sheets. If they were taken on the road, the originals had to survive. He wrote in his careful scribe's hand, the hand the temple had drilled into him, and thought about how strange it was that the small skill of a debt-bound copyist should end up carrying the fate of a kingdom.

A knock. Serah entered without waiting, which was its own kind of intimacy now. She carried two cups of wine and the expression she wore when she'd been rehearsing words and decided, at the last moment, to abandon the rehearsal.

"The vanguard leaves at dawn," she said. "Halvard's giving us four riders as far as the hills. After that we're on foot through the old ways."

"I know. I was there for the planning."

"You weren't there for this part." She handed him a cup and sat on the windowsill, looking out at the darkened town, the distant orange smear of the far shore. "Ten days. If we're late—"

"We won't be late."

"If we're late, they hold the river anyway, and they bleed, and they lose, and it will have been for nothing. Ten thousand people standing on a cold bank because we asked them to." She turned the cup in her hands. "I've sent armies to die before, Edmund. It never gets lighter. I only got better at carrying it."

Edmund set down his pen. He crossed to her and took the cup gently from her hands and set it aside, and held her hands instead — the sword-calloused, ink-stained, guilt-lined hands of a woman who had spent fifteen years being exactly what her country asked of her and was only now deciding what to ask of herself.

"Then we won't be late," he said.

She laughed — quiet, cracked, real — and leaned her forehead against his shoulder. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the river murmured in the dark, indifferent, patient, keeping its own counsel about what the morning would bring.

"When this is done," Serah said into his coat, "when the king is free and the war is over — whatever they decide to do with me at whatever tribunal they invent — I want you to remember that I chose this. All of it. The confession, the corridor, the rest. Nobody sent me. I want that to be the thing you keep."

"I'll keep all of it," Edmund said. "The choices and the woman who made them."

She pulled back and looked at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Then we'd better survive. I refuse to become a fond memory."

* * *

They rode out before dawn, four riders and a child, east along the river road while behind them the campfires of five cities' armies kindled like a second dawn along the western bank. At the top of the first hill Edmund turned in the saddle and looked back once: the river silver-gray in the early light, the bridges already crowding with soldiers, the great slow machinery of hope grinding into motion.

Wren rode beside him, upright despite her exhaustion, watching the same view. "Do you think it'll work?" she asked.

Edmund thought of the letters. *I am held.* Thought of a man alone inside his own body for three years, writing that message over and over into the dark, with no reason to believe anyone would ever read it — and writing it anyway.

"Yes," he said. "He held on. The least we can do is hurry."

They crested the hill and dropped down the far side, toward the capital, toward the corridor of suppression stone, toward the thing wearing the crown — and behind them, faint across the miles, Edmund heard the first horns of the allied army sound along the Vell, calling the free cities to the water's edge.
