## 16. The Suppression Stone

Serah Vane had stood before death often enough that she knew its face. It looked different every time — a charging boar in the Thornwood, a blade at her throat in the mutiny at Karst, the pyres she had lit with her own signature drying on the warrants. Death was a professional acquaintance. She had learned to look it in the eye and give orders anyway.

But watching Edmund Reyes sharpen a quill knife against a whetstone by candlelight, in the cellar of a safehouse three streets from the palace, she discovered there was a kind of dying she had no protocol for.

He wasn't sharpening it to fight. He was sharpening it to cut his own arm — a shallow line, just enough blood to anchor himself, the way Wren had taught him the choir's oldest trick. *When the song scatters, bleed and hold the note.* He worked the stone along the steel with the same patient rhythm he'd used copying ledgers, and that was what undid her: the ordinary hands. The scribe's hands. Hands that were about to walk into a corridor built to unmake everything he was.

"You're staring," he said without looking up.

"I'm memorizing," she said. "In case."

His hand stilled on the whetstone. "In case of what?"

"In case I need to describe you to someone someday, and all I can remember is the last thing." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out flat, the way truths did when they'd been sitting too long behind her teeth.

Edmund set down the knife. In the candlelight his face was thinner than it had been in Highmoor, hollowed by Callowmere and the corridor of screaming minds and the night Wren was taken and the night they got her back and the cost of both. But his eyes were the same — careful, watchful, always two thoughts deep.

"Then memorize this instead," he said, and held out his hand, palm up, the way he had in the salt mines under the pass when they were strangers sharing a crime. "Not the knife. Not the corridor. This."

She took his hand. His palm was warm and ink-stained even now; he could never quite scrub the last of it out of the creases. She thought: *I have mapped every gifted bloodline in the realm. I have carried death warrants in my saddlebag like letters of appointment. And the truest thing I have ever written is nothing. It is this.*

* * *

They had come into the capital through the bone-ways — Serah's forgotten servant tunnels, her old maps proving worth their weight in gold and guilt both — and spent two days in the cellar of a dyer's house owned by a woman who owed Serah a debt from another life. Two days of listening. The palace above them hummed like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Edmund felt it constantly now; since Callowmere, since the choir, his gift lay close under his skin like a second pulse, and the usurper's resonance soaked through the city's stones the way damp soaked through plaster.

Wren sat in the corner mending a strap on Serah's old cuirass, which no longer fit anyone but was going into the palace anyway, because Wren had declared it lucky and would not be argued with. Twelve years old, sharp-elbowed, fierce as a kestrel. She had been captured, caged, fed upon for six days, and had come out of it spitting. Now she watched Edmund prepare with the grim attention of a soldier checking a comrade's kit.

"If your hands shake," Wren said, "think about the temple bread. That dry stuff. Think about how much you hated it."

"That's your strategy for keeping me calm?"

"It works on me." She tied off the strap with her teeth. "Also, if you die, I'm taking your boots."

"Wren," Serah said.

"I'm helping." But her voice cracked on the word, just slightly, and she bent lower over the cuirass so neither of them could see her face.

Serah crossed the cellar and put a hand on the girl's shoulder. Wren let it stay there for exactly three seconds, then shrugged it off and kept working, which was Wren's way of saying *I love you* without having to survive saying it.

* * *

The plan was simple, which meant it was nearly certain to kill them.

The suppression stone corridor ran beneath the throne room — an old processional way from the palace's first founding, when the kings of the realm had feared their own gifted servants enough to build a throat of dead stone through which no gift could pass. Serah had walked it once, years ago, escorting a prisoner. She remembered the feel of it even now: like walking underwater, like a hand pressing on the back of her skull, like silence with weight. For her it had been merely unpleasant. For Edmund it would be a blade laid against the root of him.

Beyond the corridor lay the throne room, and on the throne, the thing wearing King Aldric's body. And inside that body, faint as a coal under ash, the true king — conscious, waiting, whispering through the seams for three years.

Edmund's task was to reach him and sing. To open himself so completely that the parasite would feel his whole self — every grief, every longing, every suppressed year of the valley — and in reaching for him, expose the seam where it gripped its host. Then Edmund would find the parasite's frequency, the one he alone had heard and recognized, and turn it against itself.

"The archivist's notes say the king's mind is still anchored at the coronation oath," Edmund had said, the night they planned it, tracing the old cipher by candlelight. "Kings swear themselves to the realm. If I can find that thread — the part of him bound to something larger than his fear — I can amplify it. Give him back enough of himself to push."

"And if you can't find the thread?" Wren had asked.

"Then I'll be standing in front of the most dangerous creature alive with my mind wide open," Edmund said, "and it will have me."

Nobody had suggested an alternative. There wasn't one.

* * *

Now the candles burned low, and Serah found she could not stand the quiet anymore.

She had confessed things to Edmund before — the ledgers, the bloodlines, the training yards where she'd taught inquisitors to break gifted children. She had confessed in fury and shame and the gray honesty of exhaustion. But she had never said the other thing. The thing underneath all the confessions, the one that had no tactical value and no absolution attached.

She sat down across from him and took both his hands, ink and all.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I stood in a corridor very much like yours. Suppression stone. Escorting a woman who could make plants grow by singing to them. They'd caught her growing wheat in a famine year without a license." Her voice stayed level. She made it stay level. "She begged me the whole way through. Not for her life. For her gift. She said losing it would be worse than dying, because at least dying ends. I told her to be quiet and keep walking. I reported that the transfer was completed without incident."

Edmund's thumb moved over her knuckles. He didn't speak. He had learned, somewhere in the miles between Highmoor and here, that Serah needed the silence held open for her, the way he needed ink held ready.

"I've been walking corridors ever since," Serah said. "Every order I followed was a corridor. Every name I wrote down. And tomorrow you're going to walk into the worst one ever built, and I will be standing at its mouth, and if you don't come back through it, I will spend the rest of my life on the far side of that stone knowing exactly what silence costs, because I sold it to people for twenty years."

"Serah—"

"Let me finish. I'm bad at this and I'll lose my nerve." She breathed. "You mean—" The word snagged. She tried again. "What you are to me is the only ledger I've ever wanted to burn clean. Not balanced. Clean. I don't want credit for the good I did you. I don't want the debt counted. I want you to walk through that stone and come back not because the realm needs you, though it does, and not because the king needs freeing, though he does — but because I am asking. Plainly. Without rank or warrant or strategy. I am asking you to live."

The candle guttered. In the corner, Wren had gone very still, pretending to work, failing completely.

Edmund turned her hands over in his, studying her palms as if they were a page he was copying, committing every line to memory.

"When I was a boy in Highmoor," he said, "I used to think the silence of the valley was a wall built against me specifically. That everyone knew what I was except me, and the whole world had agreed to keep it quiet." A ghost of a smile. "Turns out I was right. But here's what I know now that I didn't then. Silence isn't the absence of song. It's where songs wait. The valley was holding mine for me until I was strong enough to carry it." He lifted her hands, pressed his mouth briefly to her knuckles — a scribe's gesture, reverent toward the instrument of writing. "You are not a corridor, Serah. You're the opposite. You're the reason I can hear anything at all. So I'll make you the same promise, plainly, without rank or warrant: I will walk through that stone, and I will come back through it, because you asked. Because you asked *me*."

"And if the stone takes it?" she whispered. "If you come back hollow? If the thing that makes you *you* burns out in there and what walks out is only — the rest?"

"Then the rest of me still knows your name," he said. "And I'm told I have excellent handwriting."

She laughed — a broken, startled sound, half sob — and pulled him to her, and they held each other in the candlelight like two people standing on a ledge, which they were. Over his shoulder she saw Wren wipe her face furiously with her sleeve and go back to the cuirass, and Serah thought: *Whatever happens, this child gets the world we should have built. Whatever it costs.*

* * *

They slept in shifts. Serah took first watch and did not wake him when her shift ended early, because he was sleeping with his face finally unguarded, boyish, the debt-bound scribe from a silent valley, and she wanted one hour of that face before the palace took its toll.

Near dawn, Wren shook them both awake.

"They're moving," the girl said. "I heard the bells change pattern. That's the war-call sequence — I learned it off the inquisitors' runners." Her eyes were bright and scared and certain. "The allied army's reached the outer lines. It's starting today whether we go or not."

Serah was already lacing her boots. "Then we go now, while every eye turns to the river."

They climbed from the cellar into streets gray with smoke and pre-dawn, the capital holding its breath around them. At the mouth of the old processional stair, beneath a plaque worn blank by centuries, Serah stopped him one last time.

"The corridor presses on the mind," she said. "It will tell you that you are nothing. It will say it in your own voice. Don't argue with it — arguing gives it purchase. Just keep walking. Count your steps if you must. Scribes count well."

"How many steps?"

"I never counted. I was too busy being ashamed." She touched his cheek. "Count for both of us."

Edmund kissed her — brief, fierce, a signature pressed into wax — and turned to the stair. Wren squeezed Serah's hand hard enough to hurt and did not let go until the darkness below swallowed the last sound of his footsteps.

Then the silence of the suppression stone rose up to meet him, and Serah Vane stood at its mouth, counting.
