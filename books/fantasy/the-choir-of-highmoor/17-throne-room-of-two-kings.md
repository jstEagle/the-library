## 17. Throne Room of Two Kings

The corridor of suppression stone had taken something from him that he could not yet name.

Edmund walked out of it the way a man walks out of a river — heavier, wrung through, every joint aching with cold he could not shake. Behind him the passage sealed itself in silence; ahead, the throne room opened like the mouth of something vast and patient.

He had expected guards. He had expected inquisitors, wards, a hundred instruments of the usurper's will between him and the dais. Instead there was only emptiness — a hall built for a thousand courtiers, lit by a single line of windows high along the eastern wall, each pane stained the color of old wine. The light fell in long red bars across the flagstones, and dust drifted through them like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

At the far end, on a throne of black iron and pale stone, sat the king of Vhalen.

Edmund stopped. His gift, raw and bleeding from its passage through the suppression corridor, stirred against his ribs like a bird with a broken wing trying to fly.

*Come closer,* said a voice that did not come through his ears.

It came through the seams.

That was the only word for it. The king on the throne did not move — did not seem capable of movement, his body slumped in the posture of a man long past exhaustion, hands resting open on the armrests like things set down and never picked up again. His crown sat slightly askew, as if no one had dared adjust it in years. But beneath the stillness, beneath the skin, Edmund could feel *movement* — a slow churning pressure, like something vast turning over in shallow water.

And threaded through all of it, thin as wire, faint as breath through a cracked door:

*Please.*

Edmund's knees nearly buckled. Not from fear. From recognition.

He had spent his whole life listening to silence. In Highmoor, where magic could not follow, he had learned to hear the grain of things — the hum of temple stone, the frequency of salt in the mine walls, the particular quiet of a valley built to smother voices like his own. He knew what suppression sounded like. He had been raised inside it.

This was suppression. Writ in flesh. A man wrapped so tightly in another's will that only the thinnest thread of him remained, whispering through the cracks.

"I'm here," Edmund said aloud, and his voice sounded absurdly small in all that red-lit space. "Your Majesty. I'm here."

*Not Majesty.* The whisper frayed at the edges, like cloth worn to translucence. *Aldric. My name was Aldric. I have not heard it spoken in — I don't know how long. Time is strange in here. It is always the hour before dawn.*

"Aldric." He said it carefully, the way one sets down something fragile.

The whole hall shuddered. Not physically — nothing moved, no dust fell — but Edmund felt it in his gift, a tremor running through the resonance of the room like a bell struck once and left ringing. Hope. It was hope, and it was enormous, and it was terrible, because hope was exactly what the thing inside the king would notice first.

*You must be quick,* Aldric whispered. *It sleeps shallowly. When it wakes fully, you will not be able to reach me. It will stand between us wearing my face.* A pause, and then, with an urgency that scraped: *Do you have it? Do you have the song?*

"I have it." Serah had died a dozen small deaths letting him go alone up this last stretch of corridor; Wren had pressed a carved wooden bird into his hand and made him promise, voice fierce and cracking, that he would come back to teach her the rest. He carried them both in his chest alongside the song — the unbinding, the counter-resonance the dead archivist's cipher had described, the frequency that could cut a parasite loose from its host without killing either.

In theory.

*Then listen to me now, scribe, because there are things the letters could not tell you.* Aldric's whisper quickened. *It does not live in my body the way a worm lives in an apple. It lives in the song of me. Every thought I think, it harmonizes with. Every memory, it learns. It has been learning me for nine years. If you sing against it, it will sing back — and to sing back, it must know you.*

"I know."

*You do not.* For the first time, something like pity moved through the whisper. *To unbind me, you must open yourself completely. Not a shield. Not a wall. You must let it feel all of you — your grief, your love, the girl who waits below, the child they buried in that valley. Everything. Because the only way to pull a thing out of a song is to offer it a louder one, and then take the harmony away.*

Edmund stood very still. Dust turned in the red light. Somewhere far below, muffled by feet of stone, he could just barely sense the others — Serah holding the corridor's mouth, Wren beside her, their heartbeats small and stubborn as candle flames.

"And if it takes the louder song?" he asked quietly. "If it keeps me?"

*Then you will be a king's shadow in a stolen body, and I will be free, and someone will have to explain to that girl why her teacher lied about coming home.* The whisper almost smiled. Edmund could hear it — the ghost of humor, worn smooth by nine years of captivity but not destroyed. Whatever else the usurper had devoured, it had not managed to eat this man's spine.

"They'll be waiting," Edmund said. "Both of them. I intend to disappoint neither."

*Then come. Quickly. Before—*

The whisper cut off like a snuffed candle.

The temperature of the hall changed. Edmund felt it before he understood it — a pressure behind his eyes, a taste of copper at the back of his throat, the sensation of standing too close to a struck bell. Along the high windows, the red light seemed to thicken, as if the sun itself were being slowed.

The king's head lifted.

It moved wrong. Not the jerk of a puppet — worse, smoother than that, the deliberate grace of something that had practiced humanity for nine years and gotten very good at it. The body straightened on the throne, vertebra by vertebra, and the face — Aldric's face, hollow-cheeked and grey — arranged itself into an expression of mild, courteous interest.

"Ah," it said, in the king's voice, warm as a fireside. "The scribe from the silent valley."

Every instinct Edmund owned screamed at him to run. He made his feet stay.

"You've caused such trouble," the usurper continued, tilting its head. The crown caught the red light. "Callowmere. The choir. My poor instruments wept for a week after you sang at them — did you know that? They are tuned forks, little scribe, and you rang them until they cracked." It rose from the throne, unhurried, and Edmund saw how the body moved like a ship under full sail, all its terrible mass given direction. "I told them not to worry. I said: he will come to me eventually. They always do. The loud ones always want to see the source."

"I'm not here to see you," Edmund said.

"No." The usurper smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was the most obscene thing Edmund had ever witnessed — tenderness shaped by something that had never felt it. "You're here for the man underneath. How touching. How very like your family."

The words landed like a knife between his ribs. "You knew my parents."

"I *ate* your parents." It said it gently, the way one delivers a diagnosis. "Not their bodies — those were never interesting. Their songs. Your mother's gift was hearing, did you know? She could listen to a person's resonance and tell what they would become. She listened to me once, through three feet of suppression stone, and do you know what she said?" The usurper descended the steps of the dais, one measured tread at a time. "She said, *it is afraid of us.* She was right. We were so much smaller then. Hungrier. And she hid you in the one place in the world where we could not follow, and she spent the rest of her resonance doing it, and it bought you twenty-six years." It stopped ten paces away. Close enough that Edmund could smell the king's body — ink, cold metal, and beneath it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting slowly in a sealed room. "Twenty-six years, and here you stand anyway. Children always arrive at the table eventually. It's practically a law."

Edmund's gift was screaming. Not in terror — in *recognition*, and that was the horror of it. The usurper's resonance was built from the same harmonic structure as his own. The archivist had been right. It spoke his language. It was, in some awful sense, his kin — a voice that had learned to eat other voices.

"You're stalling," Edmund said.

"I am *savoring*," the usurper corrected. "Do you know how rare this is? Someone who can actually hear me? Everyone else hears the king. Even my inquisitors hear only the edicts. But you—" It leaned closer, and Aldric's grey eyes were not eyes at all anymore, they were apertures, deep places where light went and did not return. "You can hear both of us. The man and the thing. Tell me, scribe — can you tell where he ends and I begin? Are you certain the part begging you for rescue isn't simply the part of me that finds his despair delicious?"

"It's him."

"How do you know?"

Because Edmund had grown up in a valley designed to erase people, and he knew — he knew down to the marrow — what a suppressed voice looked like when it refused to stop singing. No parasite could counterfeit that. Hunger could imitate many things, but not endurance.

"Because he's still asking," Edmund said, "after nine years. Nothing that feeds asks. Only the starved do."

Something flickered across the stolen face — irritation, quickly mastered, and beneath it a hunger so vast that Edmund's vision swam. Then the usurper laughed, and the laugh filled the hall the way water fills a lung.

"Well then," it said. "Let us not waste the hour before dawn."

It reached for him.

There was no hand involved. The reaching happened entirely in resonance — a great unfolding of attention, the way a searchlight unfolds across a landscape, and Edmund understood in the final instant that he could not block it. Blocking was fighting, and fighting was losing; the usurper had eaten stronger singers than he would ever be. The archivist's cipher had been explicit, and Aldric's warning had been explicit, and Serah's voice in his memory — *you open, or you die closed* — had been explicitest of all.

So Edmund Reyes, scribe of Highmoor, debt-bound son of a silenced bloodline, did the hardest thing he had ever done.

He opened.

He let go of every wall he had ever built — the careful blankness of the scriptorium, the flat affect of a boy hiding tremors in his hands, the disciplined quiet of twenty-six years in a valley that punished resonance. He let it all fall. And the usurper poured in.

It was not pain. That was the trap of it. It was *welcome*. It moved through him like warm water through cold, finding everything, cataloguing everything, and Edmund felt himself being read the way he had spent his life reading ledgers — line by line, entry by entry, nothing omitted.

It found Highmoor. The smell of ink. The abbot's tired eyes. The ledger he had forged for Wren, and the exact shape of his fear as the hunt-soldiers' boots crossed the courtyard.

It found the road of ashes. Serah's signature on the death warrant. The moment he chose not to look away from her, and why — the precise architecture of that mercy, that he had loved her *because* she did not hide, and hated himself a little for loving anything in the middle of so much ash.

It found Callowmere. The choir. The near-death harmony, the taste of his own blood, the sound of two hundred frightened gifts locking together under his hands like fingers lacing.

It found Wren's wooden bird in his pocket, and the promise attached to it.

It found his parents — or the absence of them, the negative space they had left, the lullaby he could not remember and grieved anyway, the mother who had spent herself into silence to buy him a valley.

And it lingered there. Of course it lingered there. It pressed against that wound like a tongue against a broken tooth, and Edmund felt it preparing the offer — he could feel the shape of the temptation assembling itself, the false resurrection, the counterfeit comfort, *give me the song and I will give them back to you, I keep everything I eat, nothing I take is ever truly gone—*

"Yes," Edmund whispered. "I know. That's how I beat you."

The usurper went still.

"You keep everything you eat." His voice shook. He let it shake. He let the usurper feel the shaking, feel the truth of it, because the truth was the loudest thing he owned. "You're not a thief. You're a *hoard*. Nine years of a king, decades of gifted souls, my mother's hearing, my father's hands — it's all still in you, isn't it? All of it. Perfectly preserved. You can't help it. You don't destroy what you take. You *keep* it."

The pressure around him tightened, curious, wary.

"Which means," Edmund said, and now he began to sing — not with his throat, though his throat was moving, but with everything the usurper had just finished reading, every memory and grief and love laid out as notes, "everything you took is still *reachable*. And I am the only singer alive who knows your frequency from the inside. You just gave me the map yourself. Line by line. Entry by entry."

For the first time in nine years, the thing wearing the crown of Vhalen felt fear.

Edmond felt it — felt the great attention recoil, felt it scrabble for purchase in his mind, trying to close the door it had flung open, trying to un-read what had been read. Too late. The opening ran both directions, and that had always been the design, the terrible symmetry of the unbinding: to free the king, the scribe had to let the parasite feel his entire self.

And having felt it — having *learned* it —

Edmund sang the hoard open.

He reached past the usurper's surface, past the king's stolen voice, down into the dense archive of everything it had consumed, and he found them. Not bodies. Songs. His mother's hearing, clear as struck glass. His father's steady low tone. Two hundred voices from Callowmere. Hundreds more from villages whose names he'd read on warrants. And beneath them all, worn thin but unbroken, threaded through every seam of the prison that held him:

*Aldric.*

*I'm here,* Edmund sang to him, in the frequency of everything the usurper had just learned about Edmund Reyes. *Take my hand. We're going to make it regret keeping you.*

Deep in the stolen body, in the hour before dawn that never ended, the true king of Vhalen drew breath — his own breath, the first in nine years —

—and began to sing back.

* * *

The hall erupted.

Red light shattered from the windows. The black-iron throne screamed — actual metal shrieking, warping, as two resonances tore through it in opposite directions. The usurper convulsed on the dais, Aldric's body arching, its aperture-eyes blown wide, and Edmund felt it turn the whole monstrous weight of its attention on him, all its hunger, all its hoard, every devoured voice screaming at once—

He held the note.

His nose bled. His ears bled. Somewhere impossibly far below, he felt Serah's heartbeat spike and steadied himself against it like a man gripping a rail in a storm. The usurper offered him his mother's lullaby, note-perfect, and he wept and did not stop singing. It offered him Wren safe forever, Serah pardoned, the valley green, and he wept harder and did not stop singing, because he finally understood the last line of the cipher, the one the archivist had died transcribing:

*What it offers, it has already kept. Ask it to give one thing back. It cannot. It has never once given anything back.*

"Give her back," Edmund sang at it, through the blood, through the breaking. "Give me one voice. Just one. *Prove you can give.*"

The usurper could not.

And in the space of that impossibility — in the seam of it, the flaw, the single note the hoard could not counterfeit — Aldric's voice rose through the crack like dawn through a shuttered window, and the king took hold of his own song with both hands, and pulled.

The parasite came loose the way a tooth comes loose — horribly, inevitably, with a sound no one who heard it would ever describe twice.

The last thing Edmund saw before the world went white was the thing wearing the crown pouring out of the king's mouth in a column of darkness, turning on him with nine years of stolen songs, and screaming in his mother's voice:

**"YOU WILL CHOKE ON WHAT I KEPT OF YOU—"**

Then the light took everything, and somewhere beneath the throne room, a woman who loved him felt the shockwave come up through the stone, and started running.
