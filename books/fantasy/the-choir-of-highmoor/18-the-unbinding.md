## 18. The Unbinding

The song began as a single thread.

Edmund had spent his whole life learning to be quiet — quiet hands, quiet breath, a quiet mind that the valley's stone could press flat without ever hearing it cry out. Now, standing in the throne room of Two Kings with his gift uncoiling through him like something starved and finally fed, he understood that all those years of silence had not been emptiness. They had been tuning.

He let the first note go.

It left him the way grief leaves — not all at once but in a long, shuddering exhale, resonating through the suppression stone at his back, through the marble under his boots, through the air itself. The great hall answered. Every pillar hummed. The banners stirred though there was no wind, and somewhere high above, in the vaulted dark, dust fell like slow snow.

And on the throne, the thing wearing King Aldric's body *turned*.

* * *

It had no face Edmund could name. The king's face was still there — gray, sunken, the face of a man held underwater for years — but behind the eyes something else looked out, vast and patient and old beyond any cruelty Edmund had imagined. It did not rise. It did not need to. The pressure of its attention came across the hall like a hand closing around Edmund's skull.

***Little scribe,*** it said, and the words were not words. They arrived inside him, wearing his own voice like a stolen coat. ***You sing my song. Did you know that? I have been listening to you since Callowmere. Since the road. You have been calling to me your whole life, and you never knew.***

"I know what you are," Edmund said aloud. His voice sounded small in the humming hall, and he made himself stand straighter anyway. "I know what you've been eating."

***Eating.*** A ripple of amusement moved through the resonance, cold as water down his spine. ***Such small words you use. I am a gardener, Edmund Reyes. Your kind grows wild, chokes itself, burns its villages by accident. I prune. I harvest. And when the harvest is done, this realm will be quieter than your little valley — and far more grateful.***

Behind the parasite's voice, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, Edmund felt the other presence. The true king. Flickering at the seams of his own body, pressed thin as gold leaf, holding on the way a drowning man holds to a plank — not from strength but because letting go was unthinkable.

*I'm here,* Edmund sang toward that flicker. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

The usurper felt it too. And stopped pretending.

***Enough,*** it said, and the throne room *changed*.

* * *

The invasion did not feel like an attack. That was the horror of it. It felt like being remembered.

One moment Edmund stood in the hall; the next he stood in his mother's kitchen in a village he had been too young to leave, the smell of bread and lamp oil thick around him, and she was turning from the hearth with flour on her hands and his name in her mouth —

"Edmund."

— except it wasn't her voice. It was the usurper's, wearing her cadence, and the kitchen was wrong in ways he couldn't fix: the light came from nowhere, the shadows bent toward him instead of away, and his mother's eyes were kind in the way a trap is kind.

***They burned,*** the parasite said gently. ***Your father first. He tried to shield her with a working so crude it barely qualified as magic — a wall of noise, nothing more. I let him think it would hold. Hope makes the meat more tender. She lasted three days in the cells before she gave me your name. Not willingly. They never give it willingly. But everyone sings eventually, little scribe. Everyone.***

"You're lying." His voice cracked on the second word.

***Am I?*** The kitchen dissolved, and there was the cell — suppression stone, the same gray grain as Highmoor's cliffs, the same dead weight he had breathed since birth — and his mother sat against the wall with her wrists bound in iron and her lips moving, always moving, singing to herself the way prisoners do, and the song was a lullaby, and the lullaby was *his*, the one he half-remembered from a life before the valley —

Edmund screamed.

***Yes,*** the usurper murmured, wrapping the scream in silk. ***Grieve. Grief is a door. I have eaten through ten thousand doors like yours. Now open yours, and I will show you mercy. I will give you power such as your bloodline has never dreamed — your gift, unfettered, unashamed, loud enough to crack mountains. All you must do is stop fighting me. Stop singing. Let me in, and I will make the silence of Highmoor look like mercy.***

And the terrible thing — the thing Edmund would carry to his grave — was how much he wanted it. Power meant Serah safe. Power meant Wren safe. Power meant he never had to be small again, never had to copy ledgers in a valley built to bury him, never had to grieve parents he'd been stolen from before he could remember their faces.

His song faltered.

In the faltering, the parasite poured in.

* * *

Serah Vane had fought in a dozen corridors, and none of them had prepared her for this one.

The inquisitors came up the servant stairs in file, white-masked, resonance lances humming, and the narrow passage turned the fight into something ugly and close — blades and elbows and the shriek of tuned steel. Wren fought beside her like something feral, a child-sized storm with a stolen hunting knife, screaming wordless defiance that was almost a song itself.

"They keep coming!" Wren shouted, driving an inquisitor back with a wild slash. "How many do they *have*?"

"As many as we have minutes," Serah said, and put her sword through the man's mask-seam.

She could feel Edmund through the stone at her back — or feel the absence of him, a silence spreading where his presence should have been, like watching a fire gutter. *Hold on,* she thought, with a desperation that frightened her more than the masks did. *You stubborn, gentle fool. Hold.*

An inquisitor's lance found her shoulder and the world went white and wrong — the weapon didn't burn flesh, it burned *self*, scraping at the edges of who she was. She went down on one knee, tasted copper, saw Wren turn toward her with wide, furious eyes.

Then the child planted both feet, threw back her head, and *sang*.

It wasn't music. It was raw resonance, untrained, enormous — the sound of a gifted soul with no valley to suppress it and no reason left to hold anything back. The lance shattered in the inquisitor's hands. The man staggered, clutching his bleeding ears, and Wren hit him with the pommel of her knife until he stopped moving.

"Wren—" Serah coughed. "That was—"

"He's hurting Edmund." The girl's chin was trembling and her eyes were dry and fierce. "I can feel it. So get *up*."

Serah got up.

* * *

Inside, Edmund was drowning.

The usurper had filled every corner of him — his memories rearranged into a gallery of failures, his love for Serah dissected and displayed as weakness, his grief for his parents stretched taut and played like a string. It showed him Highmoor burning in a future that hadn't happened. It showed him Wren in a cell. It showed him a thousand years of quiet, promised like a lover promises sleep.

***Yield,*** it said, one final time, almost tenderly. ***You were mine from the beginning. Your bloodline was bred in my shadow. Why do you think the valley exists? Why do you think you survived? You are my harvest, Edmund Reyes. Come home.***

And in the deepest part of him, beneath the gallery and the grief and the drowning, Edmund found the one thing the parasite could not wear, could not counterfeit, could not eat:

The lullaby.

Not the false version in the false kitchen. The real one — buried so deep even the valley's stone had never fully silenced it, a melody his mother had sung into him before memory began, carried in the body the way rivers are carried in the land. The usurper had shown him the cell. Had shown him her lips moving. Had thought it was torture.

It had handed him the key.

Edmund stopped fighting the flood and started *singing*.

He sang the lullaby at full resonance, and it was not a defense — it was an invitation, the oldest trick in the oldest book: he opened himself completely, every wound, every want, every hidden thing, and the usurper plunged in after it, greedy, triumphant —

— and found the king waiting there too.

Because Edmund had not come alone. He had carried the flicker with him, the thin gold-leaf ghost of Aldric the True, and now, inside the shared country of Edmund's mind, the dying king rose up beside him, and Edmund reached out and *harmonized* them — his own raw gift braided with the king's fading strength, two voices becoming a chord, the chord becoming a choir of two, and the lullaby swelled into something the parasite had never once heard in all its long parasitic centuries:

Its own frequency, sung *back*.

***What—*** the usurper said, and for the first time, it sounded afraid. ***What are you—stop—STOP—***

Every note it had ever used to feed, Edmund took and inverted. Every door it had opened into him became a door he opened into *it*. He saw it then, whole and horrible — a hunger older than the crown, formless, endless, wrapped around a dying man's spine like ivy around a pillar — and he sang into its heart of its own devouring, showed it its own hunger reflected infinite, until the thing that had never known fear learned what fear was.

Outside, in the corridor, Serah felt the change come like a tide.

Inside, on the throne, King Aldric's body arched and shuddered, and from the seams of the stolen flesh the true king's voice rang out — cracked, human, *free* — joining the song, and the chord became a blade.

***NO—***

The usurper tore.

It came out of the king the way a splinter comes out of a wound — violently, shrieking, a column of darkness and discord that filled the throne room with a sound like the sky breaking. Edmund held the note. His nose bled freely; his vision split into white; somewhere very far away he could hear Wren screaming his name and Serah's boots hammering up the dais steps, and he held the note, held it past pain, past sense, past everything he was —

— and the usurper came apart.

It unraveled into its component frequencies, each one crying out in a different stolen voice — his mother's among them, a hundred mothers, a thousand harvested souls — and then, one by one, the voices went silent, released, *gone*, and the last thing the parasite did before it ended was look at Edmund with the king's ruined eyes and know, in its final instant, that it had been sung to death by the child of the woman it had broken.

Then there was nothing.

The throne room went still. The banners hung limp. Dust settled in the light from the high windows, and Edmund Reyes collapsed into Serah Vane's arms with his gift torn open and bleeding, hearing — even as consciousness left him — the faint, ragged, unmistakably human breathing of a king waking from a nine-year nightmare.

* * *

He woke to candlelight and the taste of iron.

For a moment he didn't know where he was, only that everything *hurt* — not his body, which was merely battered, but something deeper, some instrument in him that had been played far past its limits and now lay cracked and moaning. When he tried to reach for his gift, even a whisper of it, the effort sent a spike of agony through his skull, and he gasped.

"Easy." Serah's hand was cool on his forehead. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce with relief she was trying not to show. "Don't. Whatever you're trying to do — don't."

"The king," he rasped.

"Alive. Frail as a winter branch, but alive, and *himself*. He asked for you by name." Her thumb traced his brow, careful of the dried blood. "Wren hasn't left that chair since they brought you here. She'll deny she was worried."

From the chair in the corner, Wren said, wetly and furiously, "I wasn't."

Edmund tried to laugh and it hurt too much, so he just lay there, listening — to the candles, to the distant sounds of a palace stirring after long possession, to Serah's breathing. Outside the window, far off, horns were sounding. Not the usurper's horns. Something else. Something gathering.

"What's happening?" he whispered.

Serah went to the window and looked out, and when she turned back her face was grim and lit at once, dawn-colored.

"War," she said. "The usurper's army is at the river — leaderless now, but vast, and someone down there is giving orders. And the garrison..." She paused. "The garrison is asking for its king. And Edmund — the king is asking for *you*. He says there's a battle coming over these walls, and he cannot hold a sword, and neither can you right now."

She crossed back to him and took his hand, and her grip said everything her soldier's discipline wouldn't.

"So rest fast," she said. "Because whatever's left of the hunt is about to knock on these doors — and this time, we're the ones inside the walls."
