## 19. After the Song

Edmund woke to the smell of beeswax and blood.

The ceiling above him was vaulted stone, old work, older than the palace that had grown around it like ivy on a ruin. Candles burned in iron sconces along the walls, and their light moved when he breathed, or perhaps he moved when it did; he could no longer tell where his body ended and the room began. That was the first thing he understood about himself in the hours after the unbinding: the borders of him had been burned away, and everything leaked.

Sound came first. It always came first now. The drip of wax from a candle arrived in his skull as a struck bell. A rat moving in the wall was a drumroll. Somewhere below, men were shouting orders, and each voice broke over him like surf, one after another after another, until he curled onto his side and pressed his palms against his ears and understood that this would not help, because the sound was not coming through his ears at all. It was coming through whatever he was now.

"Easy." Wren's face swam into view above him, smudged with soot, one eye swollen half-shut. She pressed a cup to his lips. "Drink. Serah said you'd wake thirsty. She said you'd wake angry at the noise, too, but she said not to take it personal."

He drank. Water, wine, something bitter underneath. His throat felt as though he had swallowed the corridor of suppression stone whole and it was still dissolving in him, grain by grain.

"The king," he rasped.

Wren's expression did something complicated — pride and fear sharing the same small space. "Alive. Walking. Yelling at generals." She grinned despite her split lip. "You should hear him. He sounds like a king again."

* * *

They brought him to a window — carried him, because his legs would not yet agree to be legs — in a chamber high in the old keep where the shutters had been thrown open to the grey morning. Below, the city of Aldermere lay spread out in the smoke and drizzle: rooftops, bell towers, the great curve of the river, and beyond the river, on the far plain, the army.

Even damaged as he was, Edmund could feel them before he saw them. Forty thousand men camped in the mud outside the walls, and they rang in his awareness like a struck bowl — not their thoughts, thank all the quiet gods, not that, but their mass, their collective hum of fear and hunger and obedience-with-no-one-to-obey. For three days the usurper had driven them forward, and for three days the thing wearing the crown had been silent, and an army without its master's voice was a bell without a clapper. They had stopped advancing. They had started arguing.

"They don't know," Serah said. She stood at the window's edge, armor gone, dressed in a dead woman's wool cloak, her hands wrapped in linen where the corridor fighting had opened them. She looked as if she had not slept since the throne room. She probably hadn't. "Their officers are receiving orders from a king who no longer answers. Half of them think it's a test of loyalty. Half think he's dead and the other half is hiding it."

"And us?"

Serah glanced back at the bed where Edmund lay propped on pillows, trembling faintly with every peal of the bells ringing the alarm across the city. "We have a king who can barely stand," she said. "But he can stand."

* * *

Alderic IV came into the chamber leaning on a spear he had taken from a dead guardsman, and he came slowly, one step at a time, and every step cost him visibly. What the parasite had left of him was a frame of a man, flesh fallen away from the cheekbones, hair gone white at thirty-five, hands that shook until he closed them around the spearshaft and made them stop. But his eyes — Edmund had heard those eyes through the seams of the king's own mind, had sung against them in the dark — and the eyes were his own. No one else's. Human, exhausted, furious, alive.

"You're the scribe," the king said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. He lowered himself into a chair someone rushed forward, and he studied Edmund with the frank, unhurried gaze of a man taking inventory of what remained. "You sang me out of my own skull."

"Your Majesty—"

"Don't." A ghost of a smile. "I spent eleven years unable to say a single word anyone could hear. I have opinions about ceremony." He turned toward the window, toward the army on the plain, and the room went quiet around him. "Tell me what you feel. You're the only man in this city who can."

Edmund tried to refuse. Tried to explain that his gift was a wound now, that everything came through it sideways and bleeding. But the king waited, patient as stone, and so Edmund closed his eyes and listened.

"They're afraid," he said at last. "Not of us. Of the silence. They've been led by a voice in their heads for years — the officers have, the ones tuned deepest. And the voice stopped mid-word, three days ago, and no one will tell them why." He swallowed. "There's a division on the east flank that's already decided we killed him and they're glad. They haven't told anyone. They're waiting to see which way the wind goes."

The king nodded slowly, as if Edmund had confirmed a ledger entry. "Then the wind must blow," he said, "from my direction. Loudly."

* * *

What followed, Edmund watched from the window, because no one would let him descend and he lacked the strength to insist.

Alderic IV walked the walls of Aldermere in the late morning rain, on his own feet, refusing every arm offered except — at the stair towers — Serah Vane's. He wore no crown. He had ordered the usurper's crown locked in an iron box and sunk in the palace cistern, and he said, loudly enough for the garrison to hear, that he would not wear a thing that thing had worn. He showed them his wrists, where the suppression cuffs had scarred him in rings for eleven years. He spoke to the garrison in a voice that cracked and rallied and cracked again, and Edmund felt every word go out over the walls like a stone dropped into still water, felt the army on the plain begin to turn toward the sound the way flowers turn toward a sun they'd been told had died.

By noon, the eastern division had raised the old royal banner — the stag and the seven stars, not the sunburst of the usurper's regency. By midafternoon, two more had followed. The officers who stayed loyal to the thing in the iron box found themselves commanding regiments that no longer answered, and by evening the first of them were riding through the river gate under white cloth, surrendering their swords to a king who received them personally, standing, though his legs shook and Serah stood one pace behind him with her hand never far from her knife.

And then, near dusk, the hard core came.

They came out of the west in column — the inquisitors' own troops, the resonance-bearers, the ones whose minds had been tuned so deep into the usurper's frequency that his death had not freed them but broken them. Edmund felt them cross the river meadows and it was like feeling teeth. They had no leader anymore, so they had become their own weapon, a thousand minds locked into a single discordant chord, and they drove straight at the river gate screaming sounds that were not sounds.

The garrison met them with pikes.

That was the thing Edmund could not stop watching, could not look away from, though every clash on the wall below sent spikes through his skull and blood, once, from his nose. The garrison met them with pikes and halberds and crossbows and shields locked shoulder to shoulder — ordinary soldiers, unmarked, ungifted, men who had never felt magic in their lives except as something done to them. The inquisitors' chorus hit the shield wall and the front ranks staggered, bled from the ears, some fell. And the ranks behind stepped up into the places of the fallen and locked shields again, and the sergeants roared the old cadence-count, and the crossbows loosed in volleys, and the discordant chord broke itself against six hundred men who simply refused to stop being a wall.

Magic was never the point. Edmund understood it then, watching from the window with his ruined gift howling in him like a torn nerve, understanding it in his bones where the song still echoed. Eleven years of a king imprisoned. Generations of hunted children. An empire of fear built on resonance and suppression stone. And when the moment came, the wall held because men held it. Because a sergeant named something-or-other kept the count. Because ordinary courage was a frequency no parasite had ever learned to sing.

The inquisitor host broke at full dark. Those who could still walk threw down their weapons; those who couldn't were carried. Torches bloomed along the walls, and from somewhere below, ragged and off-key and utterly human, the garrison began to sing — a marching song, an old one, vulgar in the third verse — and the sound of it rolled across the plain to the surrendered camps, and thousands of voices took it up.

Edmund put his head in his arms and wept, and could not have said whether it was grief or relief, and suspected it was both, and knew that his gift would make even his weeping loud.

* * *

Serah found him there after midnight, when the singing had finally died and the candles had burned down to stubs. She sat on the edge of the bed without asking, the way she had learned to do, and for a while neither of them spoke. Her bandaged hands rested in her lap. Outside, the city bells had begun ringing something that was almost, but not quite, celebratory.

"It's worse at night," she said finally. Not a question.

"Everything's louder. I can hear the wounded in the field hospitals. All of them. At once." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I used to dream about this. About being able to hear everything. Do you know what I dreamed it would be like? Like music."

"And now?"

"Now it's like standing inside a bell when it strikes." He flexed his fingers and the candle flames in the room all leaned toward him, just slightly, just for a moment, and he forced them back down and the effort left him shaking. "I can't hold it in, Serah. Before Highmoor, before the valley — I didn't know what I was holding in. Now I know, and the knowing doesn't close the wound. Every mind in this city is a door and they're all open and I'm too tired to look away."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the voice she used for reports. "The king asked for you at dawn. There's a tribunal being formed — for the hunt, for the purges, for everyone who signed the rolls and lit the pyres." She paused. "My name will be at the top of the list. I'll be making sure of it myself."

"I know."

"You could lie for me. You could leave things out. No one would ever know — you forged temple records your whole life, Edmund, you could forge a testimony in your sleep."

He looked at her then, really looked, at the woman who had mapped gifted bloodlines for the thing wearing the crown, who had trained its inquisitors, who had also carried him out of a valley and held the corridor against ten armed men so a child could live. "No," he said gently. "I won't."

Something in her shoulders loosened, strangely, at the word — relief, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a person who has finally been seen all the way through and found worth seeing anyway. "Good," she whispered. "Thank you."

He reached for her hand, the bandaged one, and held it as carefully as he held everything now, because everything in him was too loud to hold any other way.

Outside, dawn was a long way off, and the bells would not stop, and somewhere in the palace a frail old-young king was already awake, drafting proclamations in his own hand for the first time in eleven years. And somewhere in Edmund's skull, beneath the bells and the wounded and the weeping city, another sound was gathering — faint, rhythmic, wrong.

A resonance. Far to the north, at the very edge of his hearing, where the mountains began.

Something had survived the unbinding. Something was singing back.
