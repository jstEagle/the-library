## 20. Judgments

The summons came on the ninth morning after the battle, delivered by a herald in the restored king's colors — white and river-blue, the old livery dug out of some forgotten armory and brushed clean of its long disgrace.

Serah Vane read it standing in the doorway of the rooms she'd been given in Callowmere's garrison quarter, though *given* was a generous word for what they were. A cell with a better view. A door that locked from the outside. She had not asked for anything else, and no one had offered.

The parchment was brief. The Crown, restored to its lawful sovereign, would convene a tribunal of judgment at Highmoor — at the temple itself, which struck her as either poetry or cruelty, she hadn't decided which — to hear the cases of all those who had served the usurper's hunt willingly, and to pronounce pardon or sentence upon them.

Her name headed the list.

Not among the pardoned. Among the judged.

She read it twice, then folded it along its original creases and set it on the narrow table by the window, where the light could reach it. Outside, Callowmere was still digging itself out of the terror — the resonance weapons had cracked walls, shattered windows, left whole streets ringing like struck bells for days afterward. She could hear hammers. She could hear children. She could hear, beneath it all, the faint harmonic hum that never quite left the city now, the residue of Edmund's choir woven into the stones themselves.

*You saved us,* people said when they saw her, because she had stood in the corridor with a sword and held it while he sang. They said it warmly. They said it without knowing.

They did not know about the ledgers. About the bloodline maps. About the eleven years of warrants bearing her signature, each one a small, neat act of arithmetic: one name, one village, one pyre.

The herald cleared his throat. "Captain Vane. The tribunal requires your answer."

"I'll come," she said. "Tell them I'll come freely."

He hesitated. "Freely is... noted. Most don't say it."

"Most have somewhere else to be." She almost smiled. "I don't."

* * *

Edmund found her packing that evening, which took very little time, because she owned almost nothing now — a sword, a spare shirt, the broken half of a resonance compass she'd pried from a dead inquisitor's hand at the river and kept for reasons she couldn't explain even to herself.

"You could run," he said from the doorway. He didn't step inside. He had learned, over these weeks, to give her thresholds. "Wren would help. Half the city owes you their lives. You could be in the free cities' southern reaches before anyone thought to look."

"And then what?" She folded the shirt. Set it down. "Every gifted child in this realm grows up knowing there's a woman who mapped their families for the pyres, and that she died comfortable in hiding somewhere south. That's not an ending, Edmund. That's just a longer corridor."

"It might be a life."

"It might." She looked at him then, really looked — at the faint scarring around his eyes where the unbinding had burned through him, at the way his hands were never quite still anymore, fingers moving against his thumb like a man quietly counting something only he could hear. His gift was still raw. Still bleeding, Wren called it, and Wren was right; Edmund flinched sometimes at nothing, at harmonics no one else could feel, and his control came and went like weather.

But he had come. That was the thing about Edmund Reyes that she had stopped being surprised by and started building her days around: he always came.

"Will you testify?" she asked. It wasn't really a question. She needed to hear the answer anyway.

"Yes." No hesitation. No softening. "All of it. What you built. What you trained. What your maps cost." He paused. "And what you did after. What you gave up to stop it. They'll need both, Serah, or the truth will be a weapon instead of a record."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of."

"I know." He finally crossed the threshold and sat on the edge of the cot, careful, as if the room itself were evidence. "I won't excuse you. I won't abandon you. Those are different things, and I can do both at once. I've had practice — holding two true things together without letting either one lie."

She sat down beside him. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved closer, and neither moved away.

"The tribunal sits at Highmoor," she said. "Your valley."

"My valley." His mouth twisted. "The abbot wrote to me. Did you know that? A letter, actual ink, carried over the pass. He says the temple will host the judgments because the suppression stone makes it neutral ground — no gift can sway the proceedings, no resonance can intimidate a witness." He laughed, short and dry. "The place they built to erase people like me is going to host the reckoning for everyone who tried. There's a sermon in that somewhere, and I'm glad I'll never have to write it."

"How do you feel? Going back?"

He was quiet for a long moment. "Like walking into a room I lived in for twenty years and finding out it was smaller than I remembered."

* * *

They traveled north with a column of witnesses and accused — forty-one souls the restored crown had named, ranging from inquisitors who had tuned the resonance weapons to clerks who had filed the harvest reports. Some went in chains. Some went in silence. One man wept the entire way, and no one told him to stop.

Wren walked beside Serah the whole journey, small and fierce and eleven years old, carrying a staff taller than herself that Edmund had taught her to ground through. The girl had appointed herself Serah's guard, which was absurd, and Serah had learned that arguing with Wren about absurdity was a battle no adult ever won.

"They're going to ask me to speak," Wren announced on the third day, apropos of nothing, kicking a stone off the road into the grass.

"Are they?" Serah said.

"You pulled me out of the temple cellars when the inquisitors came. Before Edmund even knew I was gone. I heard you tell the soldier you'd cut his throat if he touched me." Wren glanced up, all sharp edges and terrible honesty. "I'm going to tell them that."

"Wren—"

"Don't tell me not to. Edmund says testimony has to be true, all of it, or it's worthless. Well, that part's true too." She stomped ahead, staff thumping the road like a small, determined drum.

Serah looked at Edmund, walking a few paces back with his eyes closed, feeling for the road's harmonics the way other men felt for rain. He opened one eye.

"She's right," he said.

"I know she's right. That's what makes it unbearable."

* * *

Highmoor in autumn was a country of gold and smoke. The terraced fields stood harvested, the vineyards stripped, and the great temple rose out of the valley floor pale and patient as bone. Suppression stone, Edmund had once told her — the whole valley floored with it, laid down generations ago by people who feared the gift more than they loved their own children.

The tribunal convened in the temple's great hall, beneath windows that threw colored light across rows of benches. Three judges sat at the high table: a magistrate of the free cities, a general of the king's loyalist army, and — at Edmund's insistence, and over considerable objection — a representative chosen by the gifted survivors themselves, a gray-haired woman named Maren Ost who had spent six years in the cells beneath the palace and walked out of them thin as a reed and twice as hard.

The hall was open to any who wished to watch. It was full. Refugees, soldiers, villagers from the ash road, faces Serah recognized from villages she had emptied. They had come to look at her. She made herself let them.

On the first day, the tribunal read the charges aloud. It took two hours. Eleven years of service to the hunt. Design of the bloodline registry. Training of the inquisition's first three classes. Signatures on one hundred and sixty-two warrants resulting in execution or transportation. Complicity in the construction of the palace cells. Complicity in the harvest.

She stood for all of it. She did not sit. When the reading ended, the magistrate asked how she pleaded.

"Guilty," Serah said. "To all of it. I did every thing read into the record, and I did it well, and I knew enough, near the end, to know what it was for. Guilty."

A sound went through the hall — not gasp, exactly. Something lower. The sound of a room recalibrating.

Maren Ost leaned forward. "Captain Vane. Do you understand that guilt confessed does not equal guilt forgiven?"

"I understand that forgiveness isn't mine to ask for. Only judgment."

"Then we will proceed to judgment." Maren's voice didn't waver. "We begin with witnesses for the prosecution. And Captain—" the old woman's eyes were flint, "—we have many."

* * *

They came forward one by one, day after day, and Serah listened to all of it.

A woman from Thornmere whose husband's name had been on a warrant with Serah's seal. A former inquisitor, broken and precise, who described her training regimen in detail — *she taught us to think of the gift as a disease, she used that word, she made us write it fifty times* — until the magistrate asked him whether he had been compelled, and he said, "No. I believed her. That was the horror of it. She made belief feel like discipline."

A clerk who produced the original registry, and read Serah's marginal notes aloud: assessments of families, of children, of lineages marked for suppression going back four generations. Edmund's family name was in that book. Everyone in the hall knew it. The clerk read the entry anyway, because the record required it, and Serah watched Edmund sit in the witness gallery with his hands flat on his knees and not look away, and loved him for it in a way that felt like a wound.

On the sixth day, they called her own letters — correspondence with the usurper's court, discovered in the palace archives, in which she had proposed refinements to the harvest schedule. Her handwriting. Her cipher. Unimpeachable.

That night she did not sleep. In the morning, Edmund testified.

* * *

He stood in the well of the hall in plain clothes, no counsel, and the whole room leaned toward him the way rooms had learned to lean toward him since Callowmere — not because he demanded it, but because something in his damaged gift still resonated faintly, a tuning fork left humming, and even muffled by the suppression stone he carried a gravity that had nothing to do with magic.

"State your name and standing," said the magistrate.

"Edmund Reyes. Formerly a scribe bound to this temple. Currently..." he considered, "...a teacher."

"Master Reyes, you have a unique position in this matter. You traveled with Captain Vane for the better part of a year. You shared her dangers. By all accounts, you share her affections. The tribunal must ask: can you testify honestly?"

"No," Edmund said.

A stir. The magistrate frowned. "Explain."

"I can't testify honestly *about my feelings for her*, so I won't offer them. But I can testify about facts, and facts are what I've brought." He unfolded a paper — his own hand, they could see, scribe-neat. "I've divided it into two columns. What she did. What she undid. I'll read both, and I'll read neither one louder than the other."

And he did.

He read the registry, and her role in it, and the training of the inquisitors, and the hundred and sixty-two names, and he did not soften a single entry, did not round a single edge, and Serah sat in the dock and listened to the worst of herself spoken in the steadiest voice she had ever heard, and it was worse than the prosecution's version, because he understood it well enough to make it precise.

Then he turned the page.

"What she undid. She discovered the true purpose of the hunt — that it was a harvest feeding the parasite on the throne — and everything after that point was treason against it. She extracted me from Highmoor under threat of exposure. She forged transit papers. She killed seven inquisitors across five engagements, including two at the palace corridor, where she held the approach alone for the time it took me to complete the unbinding — wounded, at that, having taken a blade meant for me. She surrendered her rank, her name, and any possibility of pardon to do it. She rescued the prisoner Wren from the temple cellars before anyone else knew the child was taken. And when the choir shield failed at Callowmere's east gate, it was her counterattack that bought the seconds I needed to rebuild it."

He lowered the paper.

"I want the tribunal to understand something. I am not here to trade one column for the other. The dead cannot be paid with her courage. The woman from Thornmere's husband is not less dead because she held a corridor." His voice caught, just once, and he mastered it. "But if you weigh her, weigh all of her. That's all I ask. Not mercy. Completeness."

Maren Ost spoke without looking up from her notes. "Master Reyes. One question. Knowing everything in your first column — would you have walked that road with her anyway?"

Edmund was quiet for a moment. "Yes," he said. "And I think that's a fact about her, not about me. People are only worth walking roads with if the worst of them is true and they carry it themselves. She has never once put it down."

* * *

The deliberation lasted two days. Serah spent them in the temple's old guest cells — the same narrow rooms pilgrims had slept in for centuries, the same stone, the same smell of tallow and cold air. Wren sat with her the first evening and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of testimony, and fell asleep against her shoulder around midnight, and Serah did not move until her arm went numb.

On the second evening, Edmund came. He stood in the doorway again — thresholds, always thresholds — and she said, "Sit down, Edmund, you're not a visitor here, you're a siege."

He sat. For a while neither spoke. Down the corridor, another accused man was praying loudly, and the suppression stone ate the sound into a dull pulse, like a heart behind a wall.

"If they sentence me to death," Serah said, "I want you to know I won't appeal. And I want you to take Wren south. To your school. Don't argue."

"I'm not arguing."

"You're doing the thing where you're about to argue."

"I'm doing the thing where I'm deciding what's true," he said. "Here's what's true. If they kill you, I'll grieve you for the rest of my life, and I'll teach her, and I'll build the school, and none of it will be enough, and I'll do it anyway. Because that's what you taught me — that you carry the true thing even when it doesn't fix anything." He looked at her. "And here's the other true thing. I don't believe they'll kill you. Not because the judges are kind. Because Maren Ost spent six years in a cell and came out wanting records, not revenge. She knows the difference between justice and appetite. Most of the realm is still learning it."

"And if they're wrong about me?"

"Then they're wrong," he said simply. "And you'll serve the sentence they give instead, whatever it is, and that will also be a way of telling the truth."

She reached over and took his hand. His fingers were trembling faintly — the raw gift, always the raw gift — and she held them until they stilled.

* * *

On the ninth day, the tribunal pronounced judgment.

The hall was silent. Even the hammers of Callowmere seemed far away, though they were a week's ride distant. The three judges stood, and Maren Ost read the finding, her voice carrying to the last bench without effort.

"In the matter of Serah Vane, formerly hunt-captain of the crown: the tribunal finds the charges proven in full. We find no mitigation of the acts themselves, and we decline to accept confession as currency. The dead are not paid.

"We find, however, that the accused's subsequent service was material to the defeat of the usurper, that it was rendered at genuine and repeated risk of her life, and that it was rendered without condition, bargain, or expectation of pardon. We further find that her knowledge of the hunt's machinery proved essential to dismantling it, and continues to prove essential: the registry she built is the map by which the surviving hidden are being found and freed.

"Therefore, the sentence of death recorded against her name is commuted. Serah Vane is remanded to a term of service — ten years, under supervision of the free cities' council and the restored crown jointly — during which she shall labor to undo what she built: every name on the registry traced, every family notified, every hidden child brought into the light and given what she helped deny them. She shall hold no command. She shall bear the title of none of her former ranks. At the end of the term, the tribunal's successors shall judge whether the debt is discharged.

"This is not pardon," Maren finished. "Pardon is for the falsely accused. This is accounting. Let the realm learn the difference."

The hall exhaled. Somewhere in the benches, the woman from Thornmere began to cry, and no one told her to stop, and no one told her to start, and Serah stood in the dock and bowed her head to the judges and to the benches and to the woman crying, one bow for each, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to add to the record.

Afterward, in the emptying hall, Wren punched Serah in the arm hard enough to bruise. "Ten years," the girl said. "I'll be twenty-one. I'm keeping the staff."

"I'd expect nothing less."

Edmund came down the aisle slowly, and when he reached her he didn't embrace her — the hall was still watching, and the record was still open, and they had both learned what the record required. He just stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, facing the same direction.

"The abbot wants to see us," he said quietly. "Before we go. Both of us. He says—" and here Edmund smiled, a real one, rare as sun in the valley, "—he says there's a ledger with my name in it that needs settling, and he's too old to climb to the scriptorium alone."

Serah looked up at the colored windows, at the light falling through them onto stone that had been laid down to bury the gift and was now, instead, holding the weight of a new and harder kind of honesty.

"Then we'd better go settle it," she said. "Debts are the only inheritance I trust anymore."

They walked out together into the gold-and-smoke afternoon, toward the scriptorium, toward the valley that had forgotten Edmund and was learning, name by name, to remember everyone else.
