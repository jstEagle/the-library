## 21. The Return to Highmoor

The pass had not changed. That was the first cruelty of it.

Edmund had imagined a hundred versions of the valley in the year since he'd fled it — burning, drowned, renamed, erased — and it had done none of those things. It simply sat where it had always sat, cupped between the shoulders of the mountains like water in a stone hand, green and quiet and indifferent to everything that had happened beyond its rim. The terraced barley was gold at the edges. Smoke rose from the temple kitchens in a thin, unhurried line. Somewhere below, a bell rang the hour of terce, and the sound fell into the valley the way all sound fell here: softly, and then not at all.

He stopped on the last shelf of the road, where the path narrowed between two leaning pines, and let himself feel it — that familiar pressure against his chest, like a hand laid flat over his sternum. The suppression. The valley's great gift of silence, pressing down on everything that lived inside it.

A year ago it had felt like drowning. Now, with his own gift raw and half-mended beneath his skin, it felt almost like mercy.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're about to be sick but you're deciding whether to mention it." She shifted the strap of her pack and squinted past him down the switchbacks. "It's just a valley, Ed."

"It's not just a valley."

"I know." She said it simply, without argument, which was how Wren said most true things. She was eleven now by her own uncertain count, tall for it, with the orphan's habit of standing slightly too still, as if she'd learned early that stillness made people forget you were there. The scar from the inquisitor's resonance lash had faded to a pale thread along her jaw. She touched it sometimes when she thought no one saw. He always saw.

"Come on, then," he said, and started down.

* * *

The temple gates stood open, as they always had. Pilgrims came and went through them in every season, though few pilgrims ever truly arrived — they came to the valley to forget something, or to be forgotten, and the valley obliged, and then they left again lighter and emptier both. Edmund had spent nineteen years among them and had never once thought of himself as a pilgrim. He had been a debtor. A scribe. A body that copied ledgers in a room where nothing strange could reach him.

The gatekeeper looked up from her mending, saw him, and dropped her needle.

"Brother Reyes," she breathed, and then caught herself, because Brother Reyes had been struck from the rolls the day the abbot surrendered him — she would have written it herself, or watched it written. Her eyes went from him to Wren and back, wide as temple lamps. "We thought— after the battle, we heard the king was freed, and there were stories about a scribe who sang the usurper out of him, and Abbot Caelin said—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, and bowed instead of finishing.

Edmund felt the old reflexive shame rise and, for once, did not swallow it back down. "Is he well? The abbot?"

"Well enough. Older." The gatekeeper hesitated. "He'll want to see you. He's wanted to since the letters started coming."

"He could have written back."

"He wrote forty drafts." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but near kin to one. "None of them survived his own reading. Go up. You know the way."

He knew the way. His feet knew it better than he did, carrying him up the worn steps and through the cloister where he had spent so many years measuring ink by the spoonful, past the scriptorium window where Serah had once stood watching him work with an expression he hadn't understood then and understood completely now. The stones remembered him. That was the trouble with valleys that forgot everything else — they forgot everyone's sins except your own, and they kept yours polished bright for your return.

Wren walked beside him with her head turning constantly, cataloguing. "It's quiet," she whispered.

"That's the point of it."

"No, I mean—" She frowned, searching for the word. "It's quiet *inside*. Like the quiet is coming from under the ground. I can feel where it stops." She pointed ahead, at the cloister arch, invisible line drawn through empty air. "There. And there. It's like standing in a river and feeling the current."

Edmund stopped walking.

In nineteen years, he had never once felt the boundary of the suppression. It had simply been the shape of the world, the way gravity was the shape of the world. But Wren, standing in the same air, mapped it in three breaths.

"The abbot will want to meet you," he said, and heard how carefully he kept his voice level.

"Why?"

"Because you're exactly the kind of person this place was built for." He crouched to her height, and made himself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness; everyone in his life had lied to him in soft voices until the lies became walls. "They brought me here as a baby to bury what I am. If they'd known about you, they'd have done the same. I want you to see it, and understand it, and choose — really choose — whether you ever want to stand inside it again. Nobody chooses for you. Not me. Not anyone. Not anymore."

Wren considered this with the grave attention she gave to everything, then nodded once. "All right. But if they try to take my pack I'm keeping my knife."

"They won't try to take your pack."

"Good. Because I wasn't going to give them the knife either."

* * *

Abbot Caelin was in the garden behind the infirmary, kneeling in the dirt with his sleeves rolled, doing the work of a man a third his age because the brothers who should have been doing it were dead or scattered or simply gone since the war. He did not hear Edmund come in — or he heard him and chose not to show it, which with Caelin had always amounted to the same thing. His hair had gone fully white. His hands, when he set down the trowel and rose, shook more than Edmund remembered.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The valley held its silence around them like a held breath.

"You look terrible," Caelin said at last.

"You look older."

"I am older. That's what time does when you're not busy singing kings free of monsters." The abbot's voice was dry as the ledgers Edmund had once copied, but his eyes were wet, and he made no effort to hide it, which from Caelin was a confession of extraordinary depth. "I wrote you forty letters."

"So I'm told."

"Every one of them was a lie or an excuse, so I burned them." He wiped his hands on his robe, leaving brown streaks, and looked at Edmund the way he had looked at him across nineteen years of ledgers and silence and small unspoken mercies — appraising, unsentimental, and underneath it all, in the place he never let anyone see, afraid. "You've come to settle the debt."

Not a question. Edmund nodded anyway. "I want it settled properly. Written off in the rolls, witnessed, sealed. Not struck out quietly. Not forgiven in some corner where no one sees it. I want it *ended*, in ink, in front of witnesses, so that no abbot after you can call it due again. Mine or anyone else's."

Caelin studied him. "You've changed."

"I got out."

"Yes," the abbot said softly. "That would do it." He turned and began walking toward the chapter house, slow, one hand pressed briefly to the small of his back. "Come. Bring the girl. We'll do it before the full chapter, and I'll read the entry aloud myself, and then, if you can stomach it, you'll eat in the refectory like any other guest, and sleep in the pilgrims' wing, and in the morning you can tell me whether the world outside is as mad as the rumors say. Those are my terms."

"And mine," Edmund said, falling into step beside him, "is that you tell me why you did it. Why you called the debt when you did. You could have hidden me another year. You chose the moment the hunters reached the pass. I've had a year to wonder about that, and I want the truth, even if it's ugly."

Caelin didn't break stride. "It's ugly," he agreed. "I called the debt because if I hadn't, they'd have taken you, and they'd have taken the temple's charter with you, and every soul in this valley who'd earned their forgetting would have been dragged into the light and named. One debtor against four hundred refuges. I did the arithmetic." His jaw tightened. "And then I lay awake every night since doing it again, and getting a different answer, and doing it again, and getting the same one. That's what judgment is, Edmund. Not the arithmetic. The lying awake after."

Edmund walked beside him in silence for a while. The anger he had carried out of this valley — hot, righteous, carried like a torch through every dark mile of the road of ashes — was still there, but it had cooled into something he could hold without burning himself. He found, examining it, that he didn't want to put it down entirely. It was proof he'd mattered, even here. Even to himself.

"I forgive you," he said finally. "But I'm glad you lied awake."

"So am I," said the abbot. "It's the only penance that ever worked on me."

* * *

They settled the debt at noon, in the chapter house, before the assembled remnant of the brotherhood — thirty-one faces where there had once been sixty, several of them new, refugees of the purge who had washed up in the valley like everything else the mountains chose to keep. Caelin read the entry aloud in his flat ledger-voice: *the debt of Edmund Reyes, entered in the year of his arrival, is hereby discharged in full, the obligation transferred and satisfied by service rendered to the crown and realm, witnessed and sealed.* Then he dipped the quill, signed it with a hand that only trembled a little, and held it out to Edmund.

"Your copy," he said. "Burn it, frame it, feed it to the goats. It's yours. The first thing you've owned outright since you were born."

Edmund took it. The vellum was warm from the abbot's hands. Nineteen years of his life, discharged in eleven lines of script, and his eyes stung worse over those eleven lines than they had over anything the war had shown him.

Wren, standing beside him, took his sleeve and tugged it, and leaned up to whisper with devastating loudness: "You're crying."

"I'm not."

"You are. It's fine. I cried when we left Callowmere and you didn't say anything."

"That was different."

"It really wasn't," said Wren, and patted his arm twice, briskly, the way one comforted a horse.

* * *

That evening he walked alone to the salt mines beneath the pass — the route of his flight, the dark throat through which he and Serah had escaped while the hunt-soldiers' torches swept the ridgeline above. The entrance was collapsed now; the abbot had ordered it sealed after the war, partly for safety, partly, Edmund suspected, so the temple would stop dreaming about what had crawled out of it. He stood before the rubble with his palm flat against the cold stone and let himself remember it honestly: the terror, the dark, Serah's grip on his wrist, the moment the valley's silence had ended and his gift had woken like a struck bell and flattened six armed men without his consent or his understanding.

Wonder curdled into horror, he had written once, in a letter to Serah. That was the truest sentence he knew about magic, and he had learned it here, in this valley, at this mouth of stone.

But horror was not the whole of it. That was the other thing the year had taught him. The gift that woke in the dark had shielded Wren at Callowmere. Had harmonized a hundred terrified strangers into something that held. Had sung a parasite out of a king's skull at the cost of nearly everything he was. The same force. The same hands. The difference was never in the power. It was in what you chose, in the dark, when no one was making you choose.

He pressed his palm harder against the stone, once, like a farewell, and turned back toward the lights of the temple.

* * *

On his last morning, Caelin found him in the scriptorium, standing at his old desk. Someone had kept it. Kept his pens in their rack, his knife for scraping vellum, the little clay pot with his mark on it. Dust lay over everything like snow, undisturbed, and Edmund understood that the brothers had dusted around the desk for a year rather than touch it — grief expressed in the only grammar this valley permitted, which was omission.

"I never thanked you," Edmund said, without turning. "For the hiding. Whatever else it cost, you hid me, and it worked, and I'm alive because of it. Both things are true. I know that now. It took me leaving to know it."

Caelin came to stand beside him. Together they looked down at the desk, at the ghost of a life measured in copied lines.

"When you were a boy," the abbot said, "you used to press your hand flat on the table during thunderstorms. Did you know that? The brothers thought you feared the noise. But it wasn't fear. You were steadying yourself against the resonance. Holding yourself still, the way a man grips a rail in rough water. Nine years old, and already keeping your own secret so well you didn't know you were keeping it." He sighed. "I told myself for nineteen years that the valley was protecting you. Some nights I believed it. Most nights I knew we were only delaying you. There's a difference between a sanctuary and a cell, and the difference is whether the door locks from outside."

"And this one?"

"This one," Caelin said, "never locked at all. That was always the tragedy of it. You could have walked out any day of your life. You stayed because staying was easier than being what you are. That's not a prison, boy. That's just fear wearing a valley's shape." He put one weathered hand briefly on Edmund's shoulder — the first unprompted touch Edmund could remember from him in nineteen years. "Go home. Wherever that turns out to be. And send word sometimes. Not letters about kings. Letters about weather. Old men live on weather."

* * *

They left at dawn, Wren chattering about the marmots she'd befriended behind the stables, Edmund carrying his discharge in his breast pocket like a second heartbeat. At the tree line he stopped and looked back one last time.

The valley lay gold and green in the early light, smoke rising unhurried, bells ringing for prime. The silence pressed against his chest, gentle now, familiar — and he found that he could feel its edges, the way Wren had, the exact line where the hush ended and the world's noise began. He could stand inside it or step out of it. Either way, it would not change what he was. It had never changed what he was. It had only ever waited for him to decide.

Chosen rest, he thought. Imposed erasure. Same valley. Different man.

"Ed." Wren tugged his sleeve and pointed east, down the road toward Callowmere, where the low sun was gilding the dust. "Riders."

Three of them, still distant, moving at an easy canter — but even at this range Edmund's gift stirred against his ribs like a waking animal, reading what his eyes could not yet resolve. Two signatures he knew, steady and warm as banked coals: outriders of the free cities, guardians trained under Serah's new order, answerable to no crown.

The third signature was wrong. Faint, flickering, threaded through with something cold and hungry that he had hoped never to feel again in this lifetime — a resonance he knew the way you know a voice from a nightmare, a frequency that had once worn a king's face and spoken through his mouth.

Edmund went very still. Beside him, Wren's hand had already found her knife.

"Ed," she said quietly. "Tell me that's not what I think it is."

He watched the riders come, and the valley's silence pressed close around him, and far away in the capital a bell he could not hear was ringing for an assembly that should never have been called.

"It's not," he said. "It's worse."
