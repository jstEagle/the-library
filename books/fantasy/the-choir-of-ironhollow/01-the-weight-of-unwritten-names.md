## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Names

The ledgers of Ironhollow smelled of tallow, iron dust, and other people's regrets.

Xanthe Brightwater had learned to love that smell the way a prisoner learns to love the shape of their cell—because it was the only shape they had. Each morning before the guild's lamps were lit, they climbed the narrow stair to the scriptorium with its slanted windows overlooking the foundry district, where smoke rose in gray ribbons and the great hammers of Ironhollow beat out their patient rhythm like a second heartbeat beneath the city. There Xanthe sat at the long scarred table, dipped the quill, and wrote down what people had sold.

Not what they'd bought. The guild cared little for purchases; purchases were honest. It was the selling that required a scribe's precision.

*One memory, minor: the color of a daughter's eyes. Sold by Aldous Vane, cooper, against outstanding debt. Witnessed and recorded.*

*One shadow, whole and unfrayed. Sold by Maren Ostler, laundress. To be held in trust until redeemed or forfeit.*

*Three years of life, taken from the seller's middle so he would not feel them pass. Sold by Tam Reeve, night-soil man, who wept while he signed and thanked them after.*

Xanthe's hand was famous within the guild's walls—not that anyone inside those walls had a name worth fame. *The Brightwater hand*, Master Corvess called it when he bothered to call it anything. *Clean as a blade cut. Not a flourish wasted.* Xanthe took no pride in it. Pride required being someone, and Xanthe had spent eleven years learning not to be.

That was the nature of the debt-mark. It sat below the left wrist, a thin ring of ink gone silver with age, and it did its work quietly. People's eyes slid from Xanthe the way water slides off oiled cloth. Doors remained open around them without seeming to have been opened. In taverns, barkeeps reached past their coin to serve the next customer. Once, on the Foundry Bridge, a cart horse had shied from every soul on the cobbles except Xanthe, walking close enough to breathe on their neck, because even animals could not quite agree that they existed.

It was convenient. That was what Xanthe told themselves each morning, each year, each time the loneliness came back like weather. Convenience. Safety. A name kept off every ledger was a name no one could collect.

And the ledger never lied. That was the first law of Ironhollow, older than the guild itself: *what is written is owed, and what is owed is written*. Eleven years ago, a desperate child with a dead mother's funeral to pay had signed a contract in this very room, and the guild had written the child into its books not as a debtor but as an instrument—a scribe bound until the debt of two hundred crowns was served, which, at the wage of a bound clerk, meant never. The ink had dried, and Xanthe Brightwater had begun to disappear.

They told themselves they didn't mind. They were lying, but they had gotten very good at the telling.

* * *

On the last evening of the month of Ashes, the scriptorium was empty except for Xanthe and the lamps, because the guild's clerks kept merchant hours and the enforcers kept darker ones. Xanthe was closing out the day's entries, blotting the final line—a woman in the Tanners' Row who had traded her ability to grieve, poor fool, because grief had become more than she could carry—and reaching for the sand to dry the page when the door burst open.

Not opened. *Burst*, as though struck from outside by something falling.

A man came through it sideways, caught himself on the doorframe, and collapsed across the threshold like a puppet whose strings had been cut at the wrist. He wore courier's leathers, road-gray and rain-dark, and there was blood on him—not a little, not a wound's worth, but the soaking, spreading abundance of a body giving up its argument with staying alive.

Xanthe should have called for the watch. Should have called for Master Corvess. Should have done any of the ten prudent things available to a person whose survival depended on remaining unremarkable. Instead they were already moving, already kneeling, because eleven years of writing down other people's losses had taught their hands what to do even when their mind counseled stillness.

"Don't," the courier said. His voice was wet gravel. "Don't call them. Whatever you are—clerk, ghost—I've seen how they look through you. Don't give me to them."

"How—" Xanthe started, and stopped. *I've seen how they look through you.* No one saw Xanthe enough to look through them. The words went into them like a splinter working toward the heart.

The courier laughed, and the laugh cost him; his face went gray with it. "Guild city. Guild scribes. I made sure to die somewhere the guild keeps its pen." He fumbled at his chest, at the lacings of his jerkin, fingers too clumsy with cold and blood to manage what he wanted. "Help me. Help me or don't, but take it either way."

"Take what?"

"What I carried." His hand closed around Xanthe's wrist—the marked wrist—and squeezed, and Xanthe felt it, impossibly, *felt* it, pressure and warmth and the specific weight of another person's grip, the first deliberate touch they had known in years. "The king is already gone."

"I don't understand."

"No." Another laugh, weaker. "Nobody does. That's rather the point." He dragged himself up against the doorframe just far enough to work something free from inside his jerkin—a roll of oilskin, wrapped tight, stained dark along one edge where blood had soaked through. He pressed it into Xanthe's hands and folded their fingers over it, both of his hands around theirs, as if in blessing or in binding. "Map. To the king's true heart. Not the one beating in his chest—the one they keep. Beneath the palace. Under the under. You're a scribe; you read things. Read this."

"Give it to the guild, then. They'll pay your passage to whatever comes next—"

"The guild *made* it." For the first time his voice cracked into something like fury, and he pushed himself closer, close enough that Xanthe could see the blood crusted at his mouth, the pupils blown wide and black. "Eleven years you've written their trades. Have you never wondered where the years go? The memories? The shadows?" His grip tightened. "They go up the hill. They go under the crown. The king eats, scribe. Every day the king eats, and the guild sets the table, and I ran three hundred miles to put this in hands the crown can't see coming."

"You're dying," Xanthe said stupidly. "Let me fetch—"

"There's no fetching from this." He sagged, and Xanthe caught him, and for a moment his forehead rested against theirs, hot and damp. When he spoke again it was barely above breath. "Listen. The mark on your wrist. Your leash. Follow it backward someday. All the way back. It ends under the palace, same as everything else." His eyes found theirs and held, and Xanthe had the terrible, vertiginous sense of being *seen*—fully, precisely, the way a page is seen by candlelight—after years of being skimmed and passed over. "You're the only person in this city nobody's watching. That makes you the most dangerous thing in it. Take the map. Burn nothing. Trust no one who smiles. And when you meet her—the knight with the honest eyes—remember I said she'd matter."

"Who are you?" Xanthe whispered. "What's your name?"

He smiled then, blood on his teeth, and it was almost gentle. "Names are what got us all here, scribe. Keep yours close." And then, with the last of his breath, so quietly Xanthe had to lean in to catch it: "*The king is already gone.*"

He died between one heartbeat and the next, there in the doorway of the scriptorium, his hand still resting over Xanthe's folded fingers.

* * *

For a long time afterward, Xanthe knelt on the cold floor with a dead man's blood drying on their sleeves and an oilskin roll heavy as sin in their hands.

The sensible thing—the survivable thing—was to carry him to Master Corvess, present the map, record the delivery in the ledger in their clean bright hand, and let the machinery of Ironhollow swallow the whole affair as it swallowed everything. The guild would be pleased. The debt-marks would stay quiet. Xanthe would go back to being no one, safely, forever.

But the courier's words sat in them like coals. *Where do the years go?* Eleven years of entries scrolled behind Xanthe's eyes: the shadows in their hundreds, the memories by the sackful, the decades of life stripped from the desperate and the foolish. Xanthe had written every one and never once asked the question that now seemed so obvious it was obscene. Where does it all *go*?

Slowly, with hands that were not quite steady, Xanthe unwrapped the oilskin.

Inside lay a map—not drawn but *written*, dense with script in a hand older than the guild, older perhaps than the palace it described. The parchment was strange, warm to the touch, and the ink moved when Xanthe wasn't looking directly at it, letters rearranging themselves like sleepers turning over. At its center, rendered in lines that seemed to sink into the skin of the page, was a shape beneath the palace foundations: a chamber, a cage, and within the cage a small fierce symbol that Xanthe's eye refused to hold—a heart, maybe, or a flame, or a name.

And along the bottom margin, in the same shifting script, a single line that resolved only when Xanthe looked away and let it arrive sideways in their vision:

*What the crown wears, the crown consumes. What the crown consumes, the crown becomes.*

Below the left wrist, Xanthe's debt-mark had begun, very faintly, to itch.

From the stairwell below came the sound Xanthe had dreaded every working day of eleven years and never once heard at this hour: boots on iron stairs, measured and unhurried, ascending. More than one pair. Enforcers walked in threes.

There was nowhere in the scriptorium to hide a body. There was nowhere in Xanthe's life to hide a choice.

The boots came on, and Xanthe Brightwater—who had spent a lifetime making certain no one would ever come looking—folded the map into their shirt, stood up over the dead man, and waited to be seen.
