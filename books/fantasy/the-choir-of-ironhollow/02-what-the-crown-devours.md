## 2. What the Crown Devours

The rain came down like a verdict, and Xanthe ran through it with a dead man's map pressed against their ribs.

They had not run in years. Running required being seen, and Xanthe had spent seven careful years becoming the kind of person eyes slid past—a grey figure in the guild's grey corridors, a hand that wrote and a name that did not. But the courier had died in the scriptorium with his blood soaking into the ledger of traded shadows, and he had said *the king is already gone* loud enough for three clerks to hear, and now the enforcers were coming with their iron-tipped staves and their ledgers of collection.

So Xanthe ran, and discovered, somewhere between the guild's back gate and the drowned alleyways of Ironhollow's lower city, that being seen was not the same as being known. The enforcers' lanterns swept across them twice and passed over once before catching on the third sweep—because the map against Xanthe's chest was *loud*, somehow, loud in a way that made invisibility fail, the way a scream fails to be quiet no matter how small the mouth.

Xanthe ducked beneath the aqueduct where the poor washed their linens in water gone cold and grey, and waited for the lantern-light to pass, and did not let themselves think about the courier's face: young, ruined, already half-unwritten, his features blurring at the edges like ink touched by wet fingers. A man who had sold too much of himself. A man who had come to the one scribe who might understand what it meant to be mostly erased.

*The king is already gone.*

The map was drawn on skin. Xanthe had realized it in the first terrible moment of unfolding it—the grain of it, the fine blue tracery of veins gone pale, the way it flexed when warmed. Someone had flayed a forearm and written the palace's undercrofts upon it in a hand Xanthe almost recognized. Almost. That was the worst part. It was like reading a letter from a stranger who knew your childhood.

The undercrofts. Beneath the palace. And at their heart, marked with a symbol Xanthe knew only from forbidden margins—a circle with a crown inside it, and the crown pierced through by a stylus—

*A debt-mark*, Xanthe thought, and their own wrist, where the guild's sigil sat beneath the skin like a swallowed stone, began to ache.

It had never ached before.

* * *

Serah Vane had guarded the king for six years, and in six years she had never once seen him eat.

She told herself there were explanations. Kings dined privately; kings were strange; kings were tired after audiences that drained even the strongest men. She told herself the servants who carried covered trays up to the royal apartments at midnight were delivering wine, or sweetmeats, or any of the hundred things a king might want alone. She told herself the hollow look in his eyes was grief—for the old queen, dead these nine years—and not something worse.

But tonight, standing her watch outside the sealed door of the king's bedchamber, Serah had heard him talking. Not to himself. To someone who did not answer.

"You promised me more," the king's voice had said, thin and papery through the oak. "You promised I would forget her face last. You're taking it first. You're taking everything first."

And then a sound Serah had no name for—a sound like a page turning, if pages could sigh—and silence.

Her hand had gone to her sword hilt without her permission. Her oath sat on her shoulders like a second suit of armor: *to guard the king's body, the king's peace, the king's secrets.* Six years she had recited it. Six years she had not asked what the third clause cost.

When the relief knight arrived at dawn, she surrendered her post and walked down through the palace into the city, telling herself she was going to the shrine of Saint Aldric to pray. She did not go to the shrine. She went instead to the one place in Ironhollow where secrets were bought and sold openly, because a knight who suspected her king wore a stolen face needed to know what faces cost—and the Guild of Recorded Exchanges kept ledgers on everything.

She found the guildhouse in chaos. Enforcers in the streets, doors barred, a clerk weeping over a scorched desk. And in the scriptorium, crouched behind an overturned lectern like a rabbit that had learned to read, a grey-eyed scribe with blood on their cuffs and a piece of dried skin spread out before them like a holy relic.

"Step away from that," Serah said, and drew her sword, because duty was easier than doubt.

The scribe looked up. And Serah felt something shift in the air between them, some small catastrophic realignment, because the scribe looked at her—not past her, not through her, but *at* her, the way people looked at things they intended to remember—and said:

"You're the knight who guards the hollow king. He spoke your name in his sleep. Three nights ago. Through the wall." The scribe's voice was quiet, roughened by fear and disuse. "He said, 'Vane watches. Vane must not see.' So either you are his creature, or you are the crack in his armor, and I have about four minutes to find out which."

The lanterns of the enforcers were moving along the street outside. Serah stood with her sword bare in a room full of forbidden books, and understood, with the clarity of a blade coming free of a scabbard, that whatever she chose in the next four minutes would be the truest thing she had ever done.

"Four minutes," she said. "Start talking."

* * *

They deciphered the map in the crypt of Saint Aldric, by candlelight, among the bones of a saint who had supposedly died penniless and been buried by strangers—which felt, to Xanthe, appropriate.

The skin-map showed the palace's roots: the wine cellars, the old dungeons, the sealed well-shaft, and below all of them a chamber no architect's plan admitted to, drawn here in lines that seemed to move when unwatched. At its center, the crowned circle. Around it, in a spiraling script, words in Old Ironhollow tongue that Xanthe translated aloud, slowly, while Serah watched the crypt door with her sword across her knees.

"*The crown is hungry. The crown is fed. Nine lives a year, freely given or taken as debt.*"

Serah went very still. "Freely given," she repeated. "Or taken as debt."

"The guild," Xanthe said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Every debtor whose contract comes due. Every soul who trades away too much. They don't just record the exchanges, knight. They *route* them. All those memories, those years, those names—" Xanthe's hand rose without permission to their own wrist, where the debt-sigil burned now like a coal. "They go somewhere. They've always gone somewhere."

"To the crown," Serah said.

"To whoever wears the crown. Or—" Xanthe unfolded the map further, to its lowest margin, where a final line of script waited in a different hand, older, shakier. "*The wearer is also worn.*" They read it twice. "The king isn't feeding the crown, Serah. The king is the crown's meal too. Whatever sits on that throne, it's wearing him the way he wears the gold."

Serah was silent for a long moment. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, and Xanthe, who had spent a lifetime making themselves unlookable, found they could not stop looking.

"I heard him," she said finally. "Last night. Talking to it. Begging it to let him keep his wife's face until the end." Her jaw tightened. "I thought—I told myself I imagined it. Because the alternative is that I've spent six years guarding a cage and calling it a kingdom."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a crypt with a fugitive scribe, translating a map made of human skin, planning treason." She looked over, and something in her expression cracked—not weakness, but the specific fracture of a woman letting herself hope against every discipline she owned. "And it's the first time in six years I've felt like a knight."

Xanthe wanted to say something clever. What came out instead was quieter: "Your name. You gave it to me in the scriptorium. Serah Vane." They turned the syllables over like coins. "I don't give my name to anyone. It's the first thing the guild takes—it's how they bind you. Seven years, and no one in Ironhollow knows what I'm called."

"Then tell me," Serah said. "Not the guild. Me."

The candle flame bent. Somewhere above them, bells rang the hour, and beneath the bells Xanthe heard—or imagined they heard—the faint turning of a vast and patient page.

"Xanthe," they said. "Xanthe Brightwater."

And their wrist-sigil flared white-hot, and the map on the crypt floor began, softly, to bleed.

* * *

Serah bound the wound on Xanthe's wrist with strips of her own cloak, though there was no wound—only the sigil, raised now above the skin like a brand, glowing a dull angry red.

"It knows you have the map," she said. "It's pulling."

"I know." Xanthe's teeth were clenched. "It's not pain exactly. It's—*summons*. Like a hook behind my navel. Every debtor in this city is tied to that thing, and right now it's reeling mine in." They laughed, a short broken sound. "I spent seven years keeping my name off every ledger so nothing could find me. One word to you, and—"

"And you'd do it again," Serah said. It was not a question.

Xanthe looked at her—at the knight kneeling in the dust of a saint's crypt, wrapping a stranger's cursed wrist with steady hands, having thrown away six years of oaths in the space of four minutes—and felt the terrifying weight of being *seen*. Not glanced past. Seen. Held. It hurt worse than the sigil.

"There's a rite," Xanthe said, to keep from saying anything else. "In the map's margin. The old hand. A ritual to unmake the binding—to release what the crown holds. But it has to be performed *beneath the palace*, at the cage itself, and I don't know what it costs. Rites like this always cost."

"Then we go together," Serah said simply, tying off the bandage, "and we find out."

Above them, the bells stopped mid-peal. In the ringing silence that followed, both of them heard it clearly now, unmistakably, rising through the stones of Ironhollow like breath through a throat:

A page turning. A vast, patient, hungry page.

And far away, in the palace above the city, a door that had been sealed for nine years swung open, and something wearing the king's face began, unhurried, to descend.
