## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Meridian Guild's Warehouse of Traded Wonders smelled, on most days, of cedar shavings and old money. On the day the cart-horse spoke to her, it also smelled faintly of rain that hadn't fallen yet—a smell Freya Nystrom would later think of as the first small lie of a very long autumn.

She was at her desk by the sixth bell, as always, with her ink pot filled to the precise line she had scratched inside its rim years ago and her sleeves pinned back to the elbow so that no stray thread could smear a column. The warehouse stretched away from her in long aisles of shelving taller than two men, and on those shelves sat everything the Guild had ever bought, sold, or quietly confiscated: bottled thunder from the Stormreach coast; a wedding veil woven from moth-wing silk that made the wearer briefly believe herself loved; three jars of preserved starlight, price negotiable; a taxidermied griffin that was, according to the intake slip, "possibly still dreaming."

Freya did not deal in wonders. She dealt in the numbers beneath them.

This distinction mattered to her enormously, the way a wall matters to someone hiding behind it. The Guild's masters—Vey at their head, with his soft voice and his soft boots and his eyes like coins rubbed smooth—valued her precisely because she was unremarkable. A woman of no family name worth speaking, no gift worth registering, no ambitions worth watching. She was the person who knew what everything cost and appeared to care about nothing else. In eleven years she had built that invisibility the way other people build houses: plank by plank, carefully, checking each joint twice.

It was, she would have told you if you'd asked—and nobody asked—the best life in the world.

"Entry four hundred twelve," she murmured to herself, dipping her pen. "One (1) jar, preserved starlight, second shelf, aisle nine. Condition: dimming. Note: seller claims it was 'harvested honestly.' Flag for verification."

Her handwriting was famous within the Guild, or would have been if anyone famous had ever looked at it. Each numeral stood at attention. Each abbreviation matched the style guide she had copied out herself, in triplicate. When the auditors came through—and they came through every season, sharp-eyed and hungry for discrepancies—Freya's ledgers passed under their fingers like water over stone. Nothing to see here. Nothing to see.

There was, in fact, quite a lot to see. But none of it was in the ledgers.

* * *

Freya's gift had arrived, as such things do, without paperwork.

She had been seven years old, standing in her mother's garden, when the neighbor's goat had looked at her over the fence and said, with the weary authority of the very old, *"Your beans are planted too shallow, girl. They'll drown in the first rain."*

Freya had screamed. Her mother had come running. The goat, sensing trouble, had chewed thoughtfully and said nothing further, and by evening the whole household had agreed that the child had imagined it, because children imagine things, and goats do not talk, and the world keeps its categories tidy.

Freya learned quickly that tidiness was a thing people would defend with their lives. So she stopped mentioning the goat. She stopped mentioning the sparrows who gossiped about which chimneys had loose grain. She stopped mentioning the stable cat who had once, in the tone of a disappointed aunt, informed her that her braids were crooked. By fourteen she had perfected the art of hearing everything and saying nothing, and by twenty-two she had found the one profession in the world where silence was not merely tolerated but rewarded.

A bookkeeper hears everything. That is the whole trade. People talk over ledgers. Sellers confess to buyers. Buyers confess to sellers. And in a warehouse full of living cargo—horses, oxen, mules, the occasional caged songbird awaiting appraisal—the animals talked too, endlessly, and only Freya heard them.

She used the gift the way a miser uses a single coin: sparingly, secretly, and always at night.

* * *

The warehouse after dark was a different country. The day-stewards went home to their suppers, the great doors rolled shut, and the lamps along the aisles were banked to embers, and then the animals would begin.

They didn't know she could hear them. That was the rule she'd set for herself, carved deeper than any Guild regulation: *listen, never speak.* Speaking meant being known. Being known meant being useful to someone, and in Freya's experience, people who found you useful did not ask permission before they used you.

So she listened while she worked her evening rounds, lantern in hand, checking locks she had already checked. The draft horses in the south stalls complained about the quality of the oats ("chaff, all chaff, they think we can't tell"). The mules held committee meetings on the subject of the farrier, whom they had voted, unanimously and across three consecutive nights, to distrust. A mynah bird in the appraisal room recited fragments of sales contracts it had memorized, getting the numbers wrong, which offended Freya professionally.

And sometimes—this was the part she never admitted even to herself, the part she kept in the same locked drawer as her mother's face and the name of her childhood village—sometimes an animal said something kind. The old carthorse Bess, retired now to the end stall, had once watched Freya work late and observed to no one in particular: *"That one works like she's paying off a debt. I hope whoever she owes it to forgives her soon."*

Freya had gone home that night and cried into her supper, quietly, and told herself it was the onions.

* * *

On the evening the cart-horse spoke to her directly, she was counting crates of frost-salt in receiving bay three, and the horse was new—one of a team of four brought up from the lowland farms, big-boned and mud-colored, still spooked by the size of the building.

She hadn't intended to linger near him. But his distress carried, the way a bad smell carries, and she found herself drifting toward his stall under the respectable pretext of verifying the feed inventory.

He was pressed against the far wall, ears flat, staring at nothing—or at something that wasn't in the room, which was worse.

*"Too early,"* he was muttering. *"Too early, too early, we felt it coming in our teeth, the ground told us, the ground always tells us—"*

Freya stopped. Old habit made her glance at the door, the windows, the ember-lamps. Empty. Safe.

"You're new," she said softly, before she could stop herself. "What's got your tail twisted?"

The horse swung his head around so fast he banged it on a post. He stared at her the way men stare at a ghost, or a miracle, or an auditor.

*"You,"* he breathed. *"You heard me."*

"I hear everyone," Freya said, and immediately wished she hadn't. Eleven years of discipline, undone by one tired horse and a moment of pity. "It doesn't leave this stall. Do you understand? It doesn't leave this stall, or we both learn what the Guild does with irregular assets."

The horse's nostrils flared. He was working through it—she could see him working through it, the way animals did, testing the impossible against the evidence. Then, slowly, the fear in him rearranged itself into something else. Relief. Terrible, hungry relief.

*"Nobody's heard me in six days,"* he said. *"Six days of talking and talking and nothing answers."* He stepped closer, lowering his voice as though the walls themselves might be Guild employees. *"Listen. Listen, hearer. We came up the salt road, and the ground is wrong. The deep stones are waking. My dam told me stories—her dam told her—about the time before the Glass War, when the steppe sang all night. It's starting again. The thirteenth moon."*

"The thirteenth moon," Freya repeated flatly.

*"When the count comes round,"* the horse said, as though reciting something learned at a grandmother's knee, *"the wards grow thin, and the old voices wake, and somebody has to answer or the glass remembers what broke it."* His dark eye rolled toward her, wet and enormous. *"You're a hearer. You'll know what to do when it comes. Won't you?"*

Freya looked at him for a long moment. He was exhausted, road-worn, half-mad with six days of unanswered speech, and he was asking her—the way lost people always eventually asked her—for something she could not give.

"I'm a bookkeeper," she said gently. "I know what things cost. That's all I know."

*"That's not all,"* he whispered. *"You know it isn't."*

She checked the feed tally, wrote a number in her book that was true, and left. At the end of the aisle she paused, hating herself a little, and said, without turning around: "Eat your oats. They're not chaff. I checked."

Behind her, the horse made a sound that might have been laughter, or might have been weeping.

* * *

That night, in her narrow rooms above a chandler's shop on Tallow Street, Freya performed her usual rituals. She banked her fire. She wound the clock. She reviewed the day's entries one final time, because a ledger left unreviewed was a door left unlocked.

Then she sat by the window with her tea going cold and let herself think about the thirteenth moon, which was to say, she let herself not think about it, thoroughly and deliberately, the way a person presses down on a floorboard they've decided not to fix.

Old wives' rhymes. Stable-yard superstition. Horses were wonderful creatures, but they believed the wind had opinions and that black cats were civil servants of some malign bureaucracy. If there were truly wards failing across the Glass Steppe—if the old covenant were fraying after all these centuries—the Guild would know. The Guild knew everything. The Guild had records going back four hundred years, and Master Vey read them nightly, and if the world were ending, it would certainly appear somewhere in triplicate.

Besides. What would she do about it? Raise the alarm? On whose authority? *Forgive me, Master Vey, but a carthorse told me the moon is misbehaving.* She could hear the conversation now. She could see the polite, coin-smooth smile, and the little note that would be made afterward in some file she'd never be permitted to read: *Nystrom, F. — reports oddities. Watch.*

Invisibility had a price. Everything did; that was the first law of her profession, and she had never once seen it broken. The price of safety was silence. The price of silence was standing in a warm room while the cold pressed against the glass, telling yourself the cold was someone else's weather.

Freya drank her tea, closed her ledger, and slept.

* * *

Three days later, a storm came in off the salt flats—not snow, not rain, but salt, the white granular wind that scoured paint off doors and made the whole city taste of the sea's ghost. The Guild closed its loading bays. The streets emptied. And Freya, working late over the quarterly tallies, heard a sound she had never heard in eleven years of careful, invisible life.

Someone knocked on the warehouse door.

Small knocks. Fists, maybe, or something smaller than fists. Knocking, and knocking, patient as tide, while the salt storm howled and the lamps guttered and every animal in the building went silent at once—all of them, horses and mules and the dreaming griffin and the mynah bird—even the rats in the walls, even the mice, a silence so complete and so deliberate that it did not feel like absence at all.

It felt like listening.

Freya put down her pen. Her heart was doing something unprofessional. She told herself it was a stranded carter, a lost child, a trick of the storm, anything with a tidy entry in a tidy column. She picked up her lantern and walked down the long aisle between the shelves of bottled thunder and moth-wing veils and possibly-dreaming griffins, toward the door, toward the knocking, toward the thing that would end the life she had built plank by careful plank.

The cart-horse, in his stall, whispered as she passed:

*"Told you. Thirteenth moon."*

"Be quiet," Freya whispered back, and opened the door.
