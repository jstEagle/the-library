## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The salt storm came in off the Steppe the way unwelcome relatives come to dinner: loudly, without warning, and determined to get into everything.

Freya Nystrom heard it before she saw it—a low grinding hiss against the warehouse shutters, like a thousand tiny teeth worrying at the wood. She was three hours into her night ledger, which meant she had reached that particular state of mind where numbers stopped being numbers and became small obedient animals that did as they were told. Column by column she herded them: forty crates of frost-salt, six vials of stormglass shavings, one (1) preserved wyrm-scale, provenance disputed, value negotiable.

She liked this hour best. The Meridian Guild's Warehouse of Traded Wonders slept around her in creaking contentment, shelves groaning under things too strange for ordinary markets and too valuable for honest ones. Upstairs, the scribes snored. In the stable loft, the cart-horses dreamed whatever horses dream about, which Freya had once asked one about and received an answer so profoundly boring—*grass, mostly, and your face*—that she had never asked again.

That was the arrangement of her life, and it was a good one. By day, Freya Nystrom, Senior Intake Clerk, a woman of such reliable dullness that Guildmasters forgot her name and auditors waved her through inspections. By night, when the warehouse emptied, she would open the stable door a crack and listen to what the animals had to say.

Not much, usually. That was the secret nobody told you about having a gift: ninety percent of it was small talk. The rats complained about the cats. The cats complained about the rats, at greater length and with more literary flair. The old mule, Barnabus, held strong opinions on the proper storage of oats and had been delivering a single continuous lecture on the subject for four years.

It was companionship of a kind. It was also, if she was honest—and she tried very hard not to be, most days—the shape of a cage she had built herself and locked from the inside. Because gifts like hers got people burned in some provinces and indentured in others, and in the Meridian territories they got you *registered*, which was a polite word with teeth in it. So Freya had folded her gift up small, like a letter she never sent, and tucked it behind the ledgers where no one would think to look.

A woman who talks to animals is a curiosity. A woman who talks to nothing but ledgers is furniture. Furniture survived.

The knock came at the fourth bell before dawn, which was wrong in every way a knock could be wrong.

For one thing, the outer gate was barred and bolted; storms were when the Guild sealed itself like an oyster. For another thing, it wasn't really a knock. It was more of a *tap*. Three small sounds, spaced politely apart, as though whoever—or whatever—stood outside understood perfectly well that they were interrupting and hoped it wasn't too much trouble.

Freya set down her pen.

The horses were already awake. She could feel it without listening—a tautness in the air of the stable below, the particular silence of animals standing very still with their ears forward. Not afraid, exactly. *Attentive.* The way a congregation is attentive.

She took the lantern and went down the stairs, telling herself sensible things the whole way. It's the wind. It's a branch. Branches do not knock in threes, said a voice in her head that sounded suspiciously like Barnabus. *Neither do storms.*

The bar was heavy, and her arms remembered why she kept them strong—hauling ledger chests, she always told anyone who asked, and it was even true. She lifted it free, unhooked the chain, and pulled the door open onto a wall of white.

Salt wind hit her like a thrown blanket, stinging her cheeks with grains fine as sugar. The lantern flame bent sideways and hung on. And there, standing in the white roar on the Guild's front step, barefoot on ground cold enough to crack iron—

—was a child.

Perhaps seven years old. Perhaps eight. Wrapped in nothing but a shift the color of old milk, hair silvered with salt-frost, looking up at Freya with an expression of mild, patient interest, as though the storm were a curtain and Freya had finally gotten around to answering the door.

"By all the—" Freya grabbed a fistful of the child's shift and hauled her inside, kicking the door shut behind them and throwing the bar across it with a crash that woke half the building, probably. "Are you *mad*? Where are your—your *shoes*? Your coat? Your *parents*?"

The child looked down at her own bare feet, flexed her toes against the floorboards, and then looked back up as if to say: *yes, and?*

Her feet weren't even blue. Freya crouched, took one small foot in her hands, and found it warm. Warm as bread. Warm as a stone left in summer sun. On a night when the salt froze in the gutters and the horses stood hip-deep in straw for warmth, this child's feet were *warm*.

"Well," said Freya, to the room in general, "that's not right."

The child opened her mouth and spoke.

Freya had spent her life around language. She kept the Guild's intake records in four scripts and could curse fluently in two dead ones. She knew what human speech sounded like in every register from prayer to profanity, and she knew, with the certainty of long practice, that this was not it.

The syllables came out round and ringing, like stones dropped into deep water. There were clicks in them, and something that was almost birdsong, and a low hum that Freya felt in her sternum rather than heard with her ears. Above them, somewhere in the rafters, dust sifted down.

"I'm sorry," Freya said helplessly. "I don't—"

The child frowned, the frown of a teacher whose student has disappointed her but who is prepared, wearily, to try again. She reached out and gripped Freya's wrist.

And the words resolved.

Not into meaning. That was the thing Freya would struggle to explain later, to Sela Brann, to Ash herself, to the blank pages of her own memory. Language, when she used her gift, usually arrived as meaning wearing sound like a coat. This was different. This arrived as *feeling* wearing sound like a coat several sizes too large, and Freya's gift did its poor best to translate, stumbling, the way a man might carry water in his hands.

*Cold-wrong-outside,* came the first impression, and beneath it: *long-walking, alone-choosing, not-lost-not-lost-NOT-lost.* And beneath that, deeper, older, pressing up through the child's small hand like roots through pavement:

*Thirteenth moon. The door forgets. Someone must remember aloud.*

Then it was gone, and the child let go of her wrist, and Freya was kneeling on the warehouse floor with her heart going like a hammer and salt melting off her eyelashes, thinking: *what in the name of every ledger in this building was that.*

"You're hungry," she managed, because it was the only sentence in any language she trusted herself with just then. "You're hungry, and I have bread, and you are going to sit by the fire while I decide what to do with you."

The child considered this, then nodded once, gravely, as though accepting terms.

* * *

Getting a fire lit in the intake room took twenty minutes because Freya's hands would not stop shaking. The child sat on the clerk's stool—her stool, the good one with the cushion—and watched with the unnerving stillness of an owl. When the flames finally caught, she leaned toward them and made a soft sound of satisfaction in that ringing, water-deep tongue, and the fire *leaned back*.

Freya decided not to have seen that.

She cut bread. She found honey. She wrapped a wool blanket around the child's shoulders, and the child pulled it close with both fists and looked up at her, and something in Freya's chest went soft and dangerous.

"What do I call you?" she asked. Then, feeling foolish: "Do you have a name?"

The child touched her own collarbone and spoke—one word, brief and bright, a word like a struck bell. Freya's gift caught only the edge of it: something like *ash*, something like *what remains after*.

"Ash," Freya tried.

The child's whole face changed. It was like watching dawn happen on a small scale—surprise first, then delight, then a smile so wide and sudden it seemed to take even her by surprise. She said the word back, in Freya's accent, mangled and perfect: "*Ash.*"

"All right." Freya sat down heavily on the crate opposite. "Ash. Here is what happens now. At dawn, I report you to the Guildmaster, because that is the rule, and rules are how I have stayed alive and invisible for eleven years. The scribes will try to write down your language and fail, because I watched Master Corvin attempt to transcribe a parrot once and he nearly wept. Someone will feed you, someone will clothe you, and someone will ask where you came from, and you will need an answer that isn't *I walked out of a salt storm barefoot speaking the end of the world*."

Ash tilted her head. Then she pointed one small finger past Freya's shoulder, toward the shuttered window, and said something quiet.

Freya turned.

Through the gap in the shutters, wind-driven salt hissed against the glass. Beyond it, the paddocks. The stable. The long dark of the Guild grounds.

Silent.

Not storm-silent. Storms were loud places, full of horses stamping and dogs complaining and the mule delivering remarks. Freya had lived inside the animal noise of this place for eleven years the way other people lived inside the sound of a river—it was simply there, constant, comforting, easy to stop hearing.

It had stopped.

She opened her gift the way she hadn't in months, carefully, a crack, the way you check whether a stove is still hot. Nothing. No rat-gossip, no cat commentary, no Barnabus. The beasts of the Meridian Guild were awake—she could feel their attention like a held breath—but every mouth was closed.

They were listening.

To the child on the stool, licking honey off her fingers. Listening the way Freya imagined men listened in the years before a king returned from the dead. Listening the way you listen when something has been promised for a very long time and might, at last, be about to be said.

*The thirteenth moon,* the child's words had felt like, still pressed into her wrist like a brand. *Someone must remember aloud.*

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the fire popped. And in the paddocks and the lofts and the dark fields beyond, every beast of the Meridian Guild stood silent in the snow, ears forward, waiting for a child to finish her breakfast.

Freya looked at her ledger, still open on the desk. Forty crates of frost-salt. Six vials of stormglass. One preserved wyrm-scale, provenance disputed.

There was no column for this. There had never been a column for this.

"Oh," she said quietly, to no one, to the fire, to the listening dark. "Oh, I am going to be so much trouble."

Ash looked up at her, honey-bright, and smiled again—that same startled dawn of a smile—and answered in the old ringing tongue, and this time Freya didn't need the translation to understand.

Some things, you hear with your skin.
