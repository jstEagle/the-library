## 3. What the Rats Remember

The rats of the Meridian Guild warehouse were, in Freya's professional opinion, the most honest employees on the payroll.

They never padded an expense account. They never claimed a crate of saffron had arrived intact when half of it had molded in transit. They ate what they ate and left honest little evidence of it, and if you knew how to listen—and Freya had spent eleven years perfecting the art of pretending she didn't—they would tell you exactly what they knew.

The trouble was that knowing things was dangerous. Knowing things out loud was fatal. So for eleven years she had listened the way other people held their breath: quietly, briefly, with one eye always on the door.

Until now. Until the child.

* * *

It was past midnight, three nights after the salt storm had delivered a barefoot girl to the guild's doorstep like a parcel nobody had ordered. The warehouse slept around Freya in the particular way that old buildings sleep—creaking, settling, muttering to itself in timber. She sat at her desk with a single lamp turned low, ostensibly reviewing the intake manifest for a shipment of Mirrethi glass beads, actually doing nothing at all except listening to the child breathe two floors up, where she'd been tucked into the cot behind the drying room.

Ash—that was what Freya had taken to calling her privately, because the child answered to it with a small, startled pleasure, as though being named were a gift she hadn't expected—slept the way animals sleep: completely, instantly, one ear still cocked toward the world. Freya had checked on her four times already. This was excessive. Freya did not care.

The problem was not the child. The problem was the ledger.

Somewhere in the neat columns of Freya's handwriting there ought to have been an entry. *One child, age approx. seven, provenance unknown, condition: barefoot, mute by all human reckoning.* The Guild recorded everything. Salt storms. Dead canaries. A crate of pickled wyvern eggs that had exploded in the summer heat and cost three clerks their noses. If it crossed the threshold, it went in the book. That was the whole architecture of the place—every wonder traded through these halls was pinned down, priced, and filed until it stopped being wonderful and started being inventory.

And a child who spoke in syllables no scribe could transcribe? A child before whom the warehouse dogs had lain down flat and silent, not cowering but *listening*? A child whose arrival had coincided with every beast within a mile going quiet as held breath?

That was not an entry. That was a catastrophe wearing a small grey coat.

Freya had done nothing for three days. Nothing was a skill; she had a certificate in it, practically. But nothing was curdling into something worse, because every hour she delayed, the entry grew more conspicuous by its absence, and Guildmaster Vey read the manifests the way other men read scripture—with a magnifying glass and a suspicion of typos.

So tonight she would do the thing she had sworn for eleven years never to do. Tonight she would use the gift. Openly. Deliberately. In service of a lie.

She took a breath that tasted of lamp oil and old paper, and she went to find the rats.

* * *

The granary annex was the best place for it. Rats congregated wherever the grain sacks leaked, which was everywhere, which was why the annex smelled like ambition and mouse droppings in equal measure. Freya brought a heel of bread as a courtesy. One does not conduct an interview without refreshments; this was true of Guild negotiations and it was true of rat diplomacy, and honestly the rats had better manners than half the factors she dealt with.

There were six of them waiting when she lifted the lamp into the corner behind the rye sacks. Six fat, glossy, entirely unafraid rats, sitting in a loose semicircle like a committee that had been expecting her.

"You know," she said, before she said anything else, "I've been coming here for years."

She meant it as an apology, or maybe a confession. For eleven years she had heard them—their chatter about the quality of the barley, their complaints about the terrier in the counting house, their running commentary on the clerks (Rat opinion of Junior Clerk Ambrose: "smells of fear and ink; avoid"). And for eleven years she had said nothing back. Not one word. She had walked past their congresses with her eyes fixed politely on the middle distance, the way you pass a beggar you've decided not to see, and they had watched her do it, and they had remembered.

Beasts remember. That was the first thing her grandmother had taught her, back when Freya was allowed to have a grandmother out loud. Beasts forget kindness slower than people do, and insults slower still.

"They told me you'd come," said the eldest rat, a scarred female with a torn ear whom Freya thought of privately as the Provost. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "Three days ago. When the small one arrived. We said: the quiet woman will want to know things. We said: give her time. Shame takes longer to chew than grain."

Freya set down the bread. Her hands were not quite steady. "I'm here now."

"Mm," said the Provost, in the tone of a bookkeeper noting a late payment. Which stung, coming from a rat, and stung more because it was accurate.

"I need to know about the child," Freya said. "What she said. What it meant. Why the beasts went silent."

"Ah." The Provost exchanged a look with a younger rat—a quick, whiskered consultation, the kind of thing that looked like nothing and was everything. "You understand that what we know is old. Older than this building. Older than your Guild. It came to us the way such things come to rats: in fragments, from grandmothers, from the bellies of ships, from the mouths of dying beasts in cellars. We are not archives. We are *remnants*."

"I'm not asking for the whole cloth," Freya said. "Give me threads."

The younger rat—the one Freya privately called Tally, because he counted things compulsively, exits and crumbs and heartbeats—went first. He had been in the courtyard the night of the storm.

"She spoke," he said, "and the words were wrong. Not wrong like gibberish. Wrong like *old*. You know how a word can be shaped for a mouth bigger than yours? Her words were shaped for throats bigger than any throat now living. The dogs lay down because the words told them to lie down—not commanded. *Reminded.* Like hearing a song your mother sang before you could walk."

"The horses in the yard," Freya said slowly. "Old Bramble kept turning his head toward the door. All night."

"He was listening for the rest of the song," Tally said simply. "Everyone was. That's why the silence. Every beast in earshot stopped making noise so as not to lose it. Do you know how hard it is for rats to be quiet? We are a profession of noise. And we were quiet for two hours. My grandmother would have called it a miracle. I call it indigestion of the soul."

Freya almost laughed. Almost. Then the Provost spoke again, and the laugh died somewhere on its way out.

"There was a covenant," the old rat said. "Once. Between the speaking kinds and the walking kinds. Your people and ours made terms, long ago, when the Steppe was young and the moon was only one moon doing the work of many. The terms were simple: men would speak for beasts in their councils, beasts would speak for men in the wild places, and between the two voices there would be peace enough to build things. Roads. Granaries. *Warehouses,*" she added, with a flick of contempt that suggested warehouses were the least of it.

"And then?"

"And then your people got better at writing than at listening." The Provost's tail curled, slow, like smoke. "Words on paper don't need a beast's consent. Words on paper can own things. Your ancestors found they preferred owning to agreeing. The covenant thinned. The last speakers of the old tongue were driven out or forgotten—there was a war, at the end of it. The Glass War, the birds call it, because of what the fighting did to the Steppe. Burned salt and sand fused into mirror-fields a day's ride wide. Pretty, from a distance. From close up, it is full of bones that never got names."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "The Glass War. There's a mention of it in the deep archive. Two lines. 'Border conflict, resolution unclear.'"

"Resolution unclear," the Provost repeated, tasting each word like spoiled meat. "Yes. That is what it looks like from inside a ledger. From outside, it looks like this: the beasts withdrew their voice, and your people forgot there had ever been one, and the wards along the Steppe—the old agreements, carved in stone, sung into glass—were left without anyone to renew them. Agreements are not stones, clerk-girl. They are songs. Songs must be sung again or they stop."

"And the child's language—"

"—is the song," the Provost said. "Or the shape of it. Last spoken openly before the Glass War, by the covenant-speakers, the ones your Guild's founders priced out of existence. Where she learned it, we don't know. Who sent her, we don't know. But the beasts know what it is. The moment she opened her mouth, every animal older than a kitten felt it in the marrow: *the terms are coming due.*"

Freya sat very still. Somewhere above them, faint through floorboards, Ash turned over in her sleep.

"When?" Freya asked. "When do the terms come due?"

Tally counted something under his breath—he was always counting—and then said, reluctantly, the way you report bad arithmetic: "Thirteen. Everyone keeps saying thirteen. Thirteen moons. The thirteenth turn. We don't know what happens at the end of it. The fragments don't say. They only say that the last time the count reached thirteen and nobody sang, there was a war, and the Steppe turned to glass, and both sides lost so much that losing became the custom."

The lamp flickered. Or perhaps it didn't; perhaps it was Freya's vision doing something unhelpful. She thought of the cart-horse, weeks ago, whispering *the thirteenth moon* into her ear while she'd been balancing a column of figures, and how she had dismissed it as stable-gossip, as nonsense, as noise. She had been trained so well in not listening that she'd not-listened even to a warning.

"How long?" she asked. "How many moons since the count began?"

The rats conferred. It was a genuinely democratic conference; even the smallest of them, a juvenile with oversized ears, offered an opinion that appeared to be overruled on procedural grounds.

"We don't count moons," the Provost admitted finally. "We count winters, and litters, and the terrier's moods. But the geese say the count is far along. The geese fly over everything and gossip about all of it. The geese say: soon. The geese say: *you have less time than you think, and more than you fear.* Geese are showmen. But they're rarely wrong twice."

Freya looked at the bread, untouched, and pushed it toward them anyway. Payment for services rendered. The Provost inclined her head with the gravity of a magistrate accepting a bribe, which was essentially what it was.

"One more thing," Freya said, and her voice dropped, because this was the question underneath all the others. "The Guild. What will the Guild do when it understands what she is?"

The silence among the rats lasted four full seconds, which for rats is a geological era.

"What does any merchant do," the Provost said at last, "with the last of a thing? With the final specimen of a vanished trade good?" She did not say *rarity drives price.* She didn't have to. Freya had written that principle into a hundred valuation reports herself, in her own tidy hand, and the words rose up off the page in her memory and stood there, accusing.

"That's what I was afraid of," Freya said.

"That," agreed the Provost, "and worse. Because she isn't a pelt or a stone. She's a *voice*. And there are parties on both sides of the old war who would pay anything to control the singing—or to make sure it never happens. Your Guild has buyers you've never seen, clerk-girl. Entries in ledgers with no faces attached. We've watched the wagons come and go. Some of those wagons shouldn't know where this building is. But they do."

* * *

She climbed back up to the drying room afterward and stood in the doorway a long time, just watching the child sleep.

Ash had pulled the blanket into a nest around herself, the way Freya had seen fox kits do it, all edges tucked in and one fist out. In the lamplight her hair was the exact color of the ash she was named for, and her breathing made a sound too big for her body, a tide-sound, patient.

*The terms are coming due.*

Freya had spent eleven years building a life out of smallness. A desk. A lamp. A column of figures that balanced. A gift folded up so small and buried so deep that sometimes, on good days, she could almost believe it wasn't there—could almost believe she was exactly what the Guild saw: Nystrom, F., junior valuer, reliable, unremarkable, safe. Invisibility had a cost, yes. But it also had a warmth. Nobody expects anything of a shadow. Nobody burns a shadow.

And now here was a child who had been in the world four days and had already undone all of it, not by demanding anything, but simply by existing loudly in a language the world had agreed to forget.

Freya went downstairs to her desk. She lit a second lamp, because what she was about to do deserved light, and because she wanted to be able to see her own face in the inkwell's black mirror while she did it. She wanted witnesses, even if the witness was only herself.

The intake ledger lay open. Yesterday's entries ran down the right-hand page in her neatest script: *glass beads, Mirrethi, 3 crates, minor chipping, valued 14 crowns; dried lungwort, 2 bales, damp, valued 6 silver; correspondence, sealed, forwarded to Master Vey, no valuation.*

She dipped her pen.

Here was the treachery, laid out plain: the Guild's entire authority rested on the completeness of its records. A thing not entered was a thing that had never existed, and a thing erased was a thing the Guild could pretend it had never harbored—but an entry *falsified*, an entry made in the Guild's own trusted hand and bent to a private purpose, that was different. That was taking the architecture itself and hollowing it out. That was not hiding from the machine. That was becoming a flaw in the machine.

Her pen hovered. Guilt sat on her shoulder like the Provost, weighing things.

Eleven years of honesty, she thought. Eleven years of being the person whose numbers could be trusted absolutely, and it had bought her exactly this much: the certainty that when she lied, no one would think to check.

She wrote:

*Foundling effects, assorted: one wool cloak, worn; one wooden bird, carved, provenance unknown, valued 2 copper; one string of beads, glass, cracked, valued 1 copper. Items retained pending claim. No further notation required.*

A cloak. A toy. Trash, essentially. Nothing worth a second glance from a man with a magnifying glass. And nowhere in it, not one syllable, about a child.

She signed it. *F. Nystrom.* Her hand did not shake, which frightened her more than shaking would have. It was steady because it was practiced—eleven years of signatures, thousands of them, and the muscles did not care what the heart was doing.

Then she blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat there in the pool of lamplight feeling the strangest sensation: not triumph, not terror, but a kind of terrible *relief*, like a bone setting. As though some part of her had been waiting eleven years for permission to break, and had finally given it to herself.

*I'm sorry,* she told the Guild, silently, to the shelves and the columns and the ghost of every auditor yet to come. *I'm sorry. I'll pay it back. I don't know how yet. But I'll pay it back.*

Upstairs, faintly, Ash murmured in her sleep—in the old language, the shaped-for-larger-throats language, the song that had once held two peoples together and might, if anyone could learn it in time, again.

Freya blew out the lamps and started up the stairs to sit with her until morning.

She was halfway up when she noticed the dogs. Both of them, awake, standing at the foot of the stairs, looking up past her—at the drying room door. Silent. Listening.

And beneath the floorboards, soft as falling salt, the rats had begun, very quietly, to sing.
