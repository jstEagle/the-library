## 4. The Guildmaster's Kindness

Oskar Vey had three rules for reading a ledger, and he taught them to every apprentice who passed through the Meridian Guild's counting room, usually while standing behind them and breathing audibly, because fear improved penmanship.

Rule one: the numbers tell you what happened.

Rule two: the margins tell you who wanted it hidden.

Rule three: when a ledger lies to you, it is never the ink that is lying. It is the hand that held the pen.

He was thinking of rule three on a grey morning in early autumn, sitting in his office above the warehouse with the intake ledger for the month of the Salt Moon open before him, a cup of tea going cold at his elbow, and the particular, delicious quiet of a building full of people who did not yet know they were in trouble.

The discrepancy was beautiful. That was the first thing he'd noticed about it. He had been reading ledgers for forty years—first as a clerk's boy in the border towns, where the ledgers were written in blood as often as ink, and later here, in the great stone warehouse where the Guild traded in wonders the way other merchants traded in wool—and he could count on one hand the number of falsifications that had ever given him genuine aesthetic pleasure. This was one of them.

Item forty-one, page one hundred and twelve: *one (1) child, female, approx. seven years, recovered from salt storm at south gate. No kin. No papers. No fee.*

And then, in Freya Nystrom's small, immaculate hand, the margin note: *No trade value. Held pending inquiry.*

It was perfect. It was almost too perfect. The entry had all the right qualities of an honest record—dated correctly, witnessed correctly, filed under the correct subheading—and it was wrong in exactly one way, which was that Oskar Vey had personally read the night-watch report, and the night-watch report said nothing whatsoever about any child. Which meant the child had come in through a door no watchman watched, past men no ledger paid, into the care of a bookkeeper whose handwriting had not wavered by so much as a hair's breadth.

A frightened liar's hand shakes. A guilty one's goes very still. Freya Nystrom's hand had gone still as a frozen pond.

Vey turned the page back and read the entry again, slowly, the way he liked to read anything worth reading, and permitted himself a small smile over the rim of his cold tea.

*Held pending inquiry.* Whose inquiry? He hadn't ordered one. No auditor was scheduled until the turn after next. She had invented the inquiry herself, the way a child invents a parent's permission—a fiction built entirely out of the shape of authority, designed to be believed by anyone who didn't look too closely.

Which meant she expected someone to look closely eventually. Just not him. Not yet.

"Ah, Freya," he said to the empty office, tasting the name. "You clever, careful woman."

* * *

He knew more about his employees than they would ever have found comfortable, had they known it at all. This was not cruelty. It was housekeeping. A man who ran a warehouse of traded wonders—dragon-scale shingles, bottled thunder, a taxidermied wyrm that was, if you knew which seam to unpick, not nearly as dead as advertised—learned quickly that the most dangerous things in the building were never the inventory. They were the people. People wanted things. People hid things. And a person hiding a thing inside a warehouse full of hidden things was like a smuggler hiding contraband in a fog bank: safe from everyone except the man who owned the fog.

So Vey kept his own ledger. Not in ink. Ink could be seized, subpoenaed, burned. His ledger lived in his head, in the tidy vaults of a memory he had inherited from a mother who could recite the names of two hundred drowned sailors without drawing breath, and in it he kept entries far more valuable than anything in the warehouse below.

Entry: *Nystrom, Freya. Senior intake clerk. Nine years' service. Punctual to the point of pathology. Drinks her tea black, which he had always privately considered a small tragedy. Speaks to the cart-horses in the yard. Not chats—at them, the way lonely people talk to animals, which is common enough and means nothing. But the horses answer her. He had seen it twice from his window: the mare Juniper turning her head to follow Freya across the yard with an attention no horse gives a stranger; the old gelding Barley, deaf in one ear and sullen in all others, leaning his great head down to breathe against her shoulder as though sharing a confidence.*

And once—only once, three winters ago, on a night of white frost—he had looked down into the yard and seen Freya Nystrom standing among the horses with her arms slightly away from her body, her face lit by some private dawn, and every animal in the yard facing her, absolutely silent, absolutely attentive, like a congregation.

He had stood at that window for a long time. He had not reported it. Reporting it would have meant explaining how he came to know, and there were questions a guildmaster did not want asked even by friendly ears. Instead he had made an entry in the ledger behind his eyes, and waited, as he always waited, to see what the knowledge would be worth.

Nine years, and she had never once used it. Never traded on it, never slipped, never let it grow so much as a whisker of ambition. She kept it folded up small and hidden under her competence, the way poor families keep their one good dress wrapped in paper at the bottom of a chest. Vey had found this fascinating. Everyone wanted something. It was rule zero, the rule beneath the rules. So what, he had wondered for nine years, did Freya Nystrom want?

Now he thought he might finally have his answer, and it was asleep upstairs in the drying room, breathing in a language older than the Guild's charter.

* * *

He went down himself, which he rarely did. A guildmaster who walked his floors too often became furniture; a guildmaster who appeared once a season, unannounced, at odd hours, remained weather. He chose mid-morning, when the intake floor was at its busiest and its least suspicious, and he came down the iron stairs with his hands clasped behind his back and his face arranged into its customary expression of mild, paternal vagueness—the expression that had lulled three separate Guild inspectors, two rival factors, and one extremely persistent tax assessor into believing Oskar Vey was a harmless old man who loved his tea and his ledgers and nothing else.

The expression was doing excellent work already. He could feel it.

Freya was at her desk by the south windows, of course. Where else. She sat the way she did everything—precisely, quietly, as though taking up less space than allotted and grateful for the discount. Around her the morning commerce of wonders clattered and called: a factor arguing the price of a salamander-egg clutch, porters hauling a crate that ticked, an apprentice dropping a jar of preserved mermaid-scales and being forgiven by everyone but himself. Ordinary noise. Except—

Except that near the loading doors, the horses had gone quiet. All four of them, harnessed to a grain wagon that should have been rolling out five minutes ago, standing with their heads turned toward the drying-room stairwell. Toward upstairs. Toward the child.

And the rats—well. You had to know where to look. Along the shadowed seam where the floor met the wall, in the gaps between crates, the little dark shapes were sitting up on their haunches in broad daylight, in plain view, in front of witnesses, utterly indifferent to being seen. Listening upward. Vey had run this warehouse for twenty-two years and he had never once seen a rat forget itself like that.

*Interesting,* he thought, with the pleasant chill of a man watching the value of something rise. *Very interesting indeed.*

"Nystrom." He stopped at her desk and put one finger lightly on the open ledger, on item forty-one, and watched—not her face, which was admirably controlled, but her hands, which went still on the desk in that same frozen-pond way. "This entry."

"Yes, Guildmaster." Her voice was level. Good. "Recovered from the storm nine days ago. I've been holding her pending—"

"There was no inquiry ordered," he said pleasantly.

"No, sir. I—" A fraction of a pause. He could hear her constructing and discarding sentences behind her eyes, a whole bazaar of lies opening and closing their shutters. He gave her time. Kindness, he had learned, was mostly a matter of timing. "I intended to bring it to your attention," she said at last. "I should have done so immediately. I'm sorry."

"Mm." He pulled the visitor's stool from beside the desk and sat down on it, unhurried, settling himself like a man prepared to stay awhile, and watched her realize that he was prepared to stay awhile. "Let us not waste the morning pretending, Freya. You are many things—I have counted them over the years, the way one counts anything one values—but you are not careless. You do not forget to report a child. You hid her. Deliberately, competently, and in my own ledger, which I confess I found rather flattering. If you must commit treachery, do it in fine penmanship."

Color rose in her cheeks and, to her credit, stayed there without spreading into panic. "Guildmaster, I—"

"The child," he said, "who speaks to beasts."

The words dropped between them like a coin into a well, and he listened to the long silence of the fall.

He watched her go through it: the flicker of denial, the calculation of what he could possibly know, the swift inventory of exits. Her eyes went, just briefly, to the horses by the loading door—as if calling for help. As if they might give it. *Oh, Freya,* he thought, with something dangerously close to tenderness. *You have no idea what you've just told me.*

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Of course you don't." He smiled, warm as a hearth. "Here is what I mean. Nine days ago a child walks out of a salt storm barefoot, and every animal in this building loses its mind—politely, quietly, but loses it nonetheless. My dogs sit at stairs all night. My rats hold séance on the counting-room floor. My carthorses stand in harness like churchgoers. And the only member of my staff who has ever been able to make a horse weep takes the child into the drying room, writes *no trade value* in a ledger she knows I read, and prays to whatever gods bookkeepers pray to that the weather will keep."

He leaned forward, and lowered his voice, and let the warmth drain out of it just enough to show the steel underneath, the way you lift the cloth off a cage to show the bird is real.

"I am not angry, Freya. I want you to understand that clearly, because everything else depends on it. I am not angry, and I am not going to report you, and the child is not going anywhere. What I am—is interested."

* * *

They walked up to the drying room together, because he asked to see the child and saw no reason why she should refuse, and no reason she could offer that wouldn't make matters worse. She climbed the iron stairs ahead of him, straight-backed, and he noted with approval that she did not try to warn the child or spirit her away. Smart. Running now would confirm everything and forfeit everything. Whatever Freya Nystrom was planning—and she was planning something, her stillness had plans in it like ice has cracks—it would need time, and he intended to make sure time was exactly what he sold her.

The child was awake. She sat on the cot among the hanging herbs with her knees drawn up, and she did not flinch when the door opened, and she did not look at Vey at all. She looked at Freya—with an absolute, gravitational certainty, the way a compass needle looks north—and then she looked at him, and tilted her head, and said something.

It was not a word in any language Vey knew, and he knew more languages than he admitted to, including two that were supposed to be extinct. The sound was low and layered, a word with harmonics in it, a word shaped for a throat bigger than hers, and hearing it come out of a seven-year-old was like hearing a kitten produce the roar of a waterfall. The hair on his forearms stood up. Twenty-two years in a warehouse of wonders, and this was the first time in decades something had genuinely startled him.

"She does that," Freya said quietly. "I can't transcribe it. None of the scribes can. The symbols won't hold it."

"No," Vey murmured. "I imagine they wouldn't." He crouched down, slowly, to the child's level—an old man's knees complaining about it—and studied her. Salt-pale eyes. Unafraid. Watching him the way you'd watch weather decide. "What is your name, little one?"

She considered him for a long moment. Then she reached out and took a fold of Freya's sleeve in one small hand, and answered with another of those impossible syllables—but softer this time, and aimed, unmistakably, at Freya. And Freya—this was the part Vey would turn over for many nights afterward—Freya's breath caught. Not at the strangeness of it. At the meaning of it. Something in the child's voice had landed in her like an arrow finding a target only she could see, and for half a heartbeat her face was open, unguarded, lit from inside.

*Ah,* thought Oskar Vey. *There it is. There's what she wants.*

Everyone wanted something. He had simply been asking the wrong question for nine years. It wasn't what Freya Nystrom wanted. It was whom.

He rose, joints cracking, and clapped his hands together once with brisk good cheer, and the warmth came flooding back into his voice like water into a dry channel, and it was—this was the craft of it—entirely sincere. That was the trick his mother had taught him without knowing she was teaching it: the best lie is a true thing told at the wrong moment. Everything he was about to say, he meant.

"Well," he said. "Here is what will happen. The Guild cannot register what it cannot transcribe, and cannot house what it cannot register, and so officially, as of this morning, there is no child in this building. Unofficially—" he spread his hands, generous as a festival "—the child stays. In your care, Freya. Yours alone. You'll draw a second ration chit, and a stipend, and I'll see that the auditors' schedules develop a convenient blindness to this floor. You have kept secrets for this Guild for nine years without ever once asking for anything. Consider this the Guild keeping one for you."

Freya stared at him. He watched hope and suspicion wrestle behind her eyes, and he made sure hope was winning on the surface, because hope made people grateful, and gratitude made them slow.

"Why?" she said. Simply. Bravely. He almost applauded.

"Because I have worked with gifted people my whole life," he said, "and the world eats them, Freya. The temples want to own them. The armies want to spend them. The Guilds"—he smiled ruefully at himself—"want to invoice them. I have watched better protection than mine fail people like you and that child. Mine won't fail. Ask anyone in this district whether Oskar Vey breaks his word." They could ask. It was true. He had never broken his word. He had merely, on occasion, defined its terms late.

"And the price?" Freya said. Because she was a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers knew that nothing entered a ledger without a balancing column.

He laughed, delighted despite himself. "There it is. Yes. There's a price. There's always a price—you'd think poorly of me if there weren't, and I'd think poorly of you for trusting a merchant's kindness at par value." He moved to the door, and paused there with his hand on the frame, and gave her his gentlest smile, the one he saved for signatures.

"But not today. Today you say yes, and you take care of the child, and you sleep well. The price will come later, when I know what this is worth. That's not a threat, Freya—that's patience. The difference matters."

He went down the iron stairs whistling, an old border tune his mother had sung, and behind him he left the warmth hanging in the air like lamp-oil, waiting for a flame.

In the ledger behind his eyes, he opened a fresh page and wrote, in the ink of pure attention:

*Item: one child, speaking the old tongue. Item: one clerk, gifted, loyal to the child beyond sense—therefore predictable, therefore manageable, therefore mine. Price: to be named.*

He did not yet know what he would ask. That was honest. But he knew the shape of it, the way a man feels weather coming in his knee. Somewhere out on the Glass Steppe, under a moon that was beginning its thirteenth turn, there were buyers who paid fortunes for living relics of the old war—and Oskar Vey had spent twenty-two years learning that the most profitable trades were never the ones you planned, but the ones you recognized, early, while everyone else was still calling them kindness.

Below him, faint through the floorboards, the rats had begun again—soft, rhythmic, rising and falling.

Not singing, he realized, pausing on the stairs.

Counting.
