## 5. Thirteen Turns

The Meridian Guild's archive occupied the lowest floor of the warehouse, which was a joke the architects had presumably found funny and no one else ever had. Archives belonged at the bottom, they reasoned, because paper was heavy and gravity was free. The result was a room that flooded every spring thaw, smelled perpetually of damp vellum and mouse, and required anyone consulting it to descend three flights of stairs while carrying a lantern, a candle, a backup candle, and the guild's standing order of "a healthy suspicion of all staircases."

Freya Nystrom descended them now, past midnight, with two candles and her suspicion fully engaged.

She had told herself she wasn't going to do this. She had told herself it quite firmly, over supper, over washing up, over the long gray hour of lying awake listening to Ash breathe in the narrow cot beside hers. *You are a bookkeeper*, she had informed herself. *Bookkeepers do not sneak into archives at midnight chasing nursery rhymes whispered by cart-horses.*

But then Ash had slept, and in sleeping had spoken — not the untranslatable syllables this time, but something worse. Something Freya's gift had translated before her mind could stop it.

*Thirteen. Thirteen. The door forgets its hinges.*

So here she was, on the third staircase down, holding a candle like a coward holds a sword.

* * *

The archive's index was a masterpiece of the Guild's particular genius, which was to say it was organized by acquisition price rather than subject. Freya had spent four years quietly building her own cross-reference ledger — technically against regulations, practically essential, and hidden behind a false spine labeled *Municipal Tariff Codes, Vol. IX*, which no one in the history of the Guild had ever voluntarily opened.

She pulled it down, blew off the dust, and ran her finger down entries until she found what she wanted:

*Beast-speakers, historical. See: Executed, Miscellaneous.*

The Guild's honesty was at least consistent.

The actual texts were shelved in the far corner, in a section the catalog called *Folk Materials, Low Value*. Here sat the leavings of a profession the Guild had spent two centuries pretending never existed: charm-books, herbals with margin notes in shaking hands, song collections, and — Freya's fingers found it by memory, because she'd read it once as a girl and told herself she'd imagined it — a slim hand-bound volume titled *Rhymes for the Raising of Speakers*, author unknown, provenance unknown, purchased from a farm widow for the price of a chicken.

She opened it on the reading table and let the candlelight find the page she'd never forgotten, because some part of her had known, even at twelve years old, that she would need it someday.

It was a counting rhyme. Children's nonsense, mostly. But the last verses were not nonsense, and they had waited in the dark for someone to read them aloud:

*Twelve moons turn and the glass holds fast,*
*Twelve moons turn and the war stays past.*
*When the thirteenth moon comes full and white,*
*the wards go deaf and the wards go blind,*
*and what was spoken must be spoke again*
*by a voice that beasts know, tongue of men.*
*Speak it true or speak it never —*
*the Steppe forgets, and forgets forever.*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, moving her lips without sound, because saying it aloud felt like knocking on a door she wasn't sure she wanted opened.

*A voice that beasts know, tongue of men.* That was a beast-speaker. That was her, hiding in plain sight behind tariff codes and intake ledgers for six years. And *what was spoken must be spoke again* —

The covenant. The word the rats had used, or the shape of it, the feeling her gift had rendered as *covenant* the way you might render a mountain as *hill* because your vocabulary failed you. The promise between the peoples of the Steppe and its beasts, broken in the Glass War, buried so deep that even the rats only remembered it as a bad taste.

And when did the thirteenth moon come full?

Freya did what she always did when the world frightened her: she reached for a ledger. There was a blank one in the drawer beneath the false tariff codes — her private one, the one where she recorded things like *horse in bay 3 says the east road smells wrong* and *do NOT tell Marta about the pigeons' union demands*. She turned to a fresh page and wrote, in her smallest, neatest hand:

*Current moon: seventh turn, waxing. Full thirteenth moon therefore falls in approximately five weeks and three days.*

She looked at the number for a long time.

Five weeks. In five weeks, if a dead woman's nursery rhyme could be believed, the wards around the Glass Steppe — the wards that kept the old battlefields quiet, that kept whatever still stirred out there from remembering itself — would fail. Unless a beast-speaker stood up in front of gods-knew-who and renewed a covenant nobody alive remembered the terms of.

"Right," Freya said to the empty archive, because sarcasm was cheaper than screaming. "No pressure."

Somewhere above her, faint through the floorboards, a rat said something she chose not to translate.

* * *

Naming the child had taken her nine days, which was eight days longer than it should have, and the delay was entirely the Guild's fault.

The intake form demanded a name. It also demanded a surname, a place of origin, a parent or guardian, an age, and — in a field Freya had always found charmingly optimistic — *distinguishing marks*. The child had offered none of these things. The child had offered, in response to every question, a look of mild interest, as though Freya were a weather phenomenon of moderate note.

So Freya had improvised, as bookkeepers do. Age: seven, based on teeth. Origin: *north, presumably*, based on the child's habit of facing north whenever the door opened. Guardian: herself, written with a steadiness she hadn't felt. Distinguishing marks: *none visible; speaks a language that makes horses sit down, which is arguably distinguishable enough.*

For the name, she'd gone to the ash bucket.

It was a small domestic thing. The child had been crouched beside the hearth in Freya's room on the sixth evening, drawing shapes in the cold ash with one finger — spirals, mostly, and once something that looked disturbingly like a map of the Steppe's heart, though Freya had told herself firmly it was a snail. When Freya asked, yet again, *what is your name?*, the child had looked up, looked at the ash, looked at Freya, and patted the hearthstone twice with both hands.

An invitation. Or a translation. With this child it was hard to say where one ended and the other began.

"Ash," Freya had said slowly, testing it. "Ash?"

The child had smiled — the first full smile, wide and gap-toothed and transformative, the kind of smile that rearranged a room — and repeated it. Not in the trade-tongue. In the other language, the old one, the syllables settling into Freya's chest like stones dropped into still water. But the meaning came through her gift clear as bell-glass: *yes. This name fits. I will wear it.*

And so the intake ledger now read *Ash Nystrom, age 7, ward of F. Nystrom*, and Freya had signed it with a flourish she immediately regretted, because flourishes were memorable, and memorable was the one thing she could not afford to be.

"You realize," she told Ash that night, brushing out her hair by candlelight, "that I have committed fraud. On official Guild documents. For you." Ash watched her reflection in the polished tin mirror with grave attention. "Six years I've been invisible. Perfectly invisible. And in nine days you've gotten me falsifying records and — and *smiling at auditors*, which I've never once done, because smiling invites conversation—"

Ash tugged her sleeve and pointed at the mirror. At Freya's face in it.

"What?"

Ash pointed again, then pressed one small hand flat over Freya's sternum, and said a single syllable in the old language. Freya's gift translated it before she could refuse: *there you are.*

Freya put down the brush. "I was always there," she said, too quickly. "I was right here the whole time. Being invisible is a highly skilled profession, I'll have you know."

Ash gave her the look of a child who has heard a grown-up say something silly and has decided, generously, not to pursue it.

* * *

The dreams started on the eleventh night.

Freya had assumed, insofar as she'd thought about it at all, that Ash's language would be learned the way languages are properly learned: through repetition, flashcards, and the slow grinding humiliation of being corrected by a seven-year-old. Ash had other methods.

In the dream, Freya stood on the Glass Steppe under a sky with too many stars, and the ground hummed under her boots like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Ash was there, but taller — or Freya was smaller; dreams are dishonest about scale — and Ash pointed at the horizon, where a herd of something vast moved like weather.

"*Sennu*," Ash said.

And Freya understood it — not the way you understand a memorized word, but the way you understand *hunger* or *home*. *Sennu* meant the herd, yes, but also the walking-together, the shared direction, the agreement between many bodies to be one body. The word contained the whole idea the way an egg contains a bird.

"I can't hold that," Freya protested, in the dream, in the way one protests in dreams. "That's not a word, that's a — a philosophy with consonants—"

"*Sennu*," Ash repeated patiently, and made her say it back, and laughed when she got it wrong, and made her say it again, and the third time the word fit in her mouth like it had always lived there.

She woke with the taste of it still present, and lay staring at the ceiling, and thought: *this is how the covenant worked, isn't it. Not treaties. Not signatures. Just — enough people speaking each other's languages that the border stopped mattering.*

Then she thought: *I am learning a dead language from a child in my sleep, and my first reaction is philological,* and decided that perhaps she'd earned the right to a small hysteria later, scheduled between breakfast and the morning inventory.

By day, Ash learned the warehouse. This was its own education. The child walked the aisles of traded wonders — bottled storms, taxidermied impossibilities, crates marked DO NOT OPEN IN HUMIDITY — and touched nothing, but everything leaned toward Ash anyway. The caged larks went silent in respect. The resident cat, a professional mouser named Duchess who had ignored every human in the building for a decade, followed Ash from shelf to shelf like a furry shadow with opinions.

"They're listening to you," Freya murmured on the twelfth day, watching a crate of dormant fire-salts — genuinely dormant, inert, safe — emit a faint warm glow as Ash passed. "Ash, I need you to understand that glowing crates get reported. Reported crates get audited. Audited crates get *me* interrogated, and I'm a terrible liar, I once confessed to eating Marta's lunch simply because she looked at me accusingly."

Ash considered this. Then pointed at the crate, then at Freya, and said something brief in the old tongue.

*It isn't glowing because of me,* the gift translated. *It's glowing because you're near, and it remembers what speakers sound like.*

Freya stared at the crate. The crate glowed at her, apologetically.

"Wonderful," she said. "Now the merchandise recognizes me. I'd like to formally state, for the record, that my life was simpler two weeks ago."

Ash grinned the gap-toothed grin, and even Freya's dread had to step aside for a moment to let it pass.

* * *

On the thirteenth night, the dream changed.

No Steppe this time. No herd. Instead Freya stood in a great hall made of glass — not shattered glass, not broken, but *grown* glass, walls like frozen waterfalls, light coming through them green and gold — and she understood without being told that this was the covenant hall, that this was where it had been spoken, ages ago, by voices that filled the whole space like weather.

And she saw them. Figures at the center, blurred as figures in fog: human shapes and beast shapes, standing in a ring, and the ring was turning, slowly, like a wheel, like a moon, and every voice in the ring was speaking at once and the speaking was the wall, the speaking *was* the glass—

Then the ring broke. She didn't see how. One moment whole, the next scattered, voices tearing apart mid-word, and the walls of glass went gray, and went quiet, and somewhere very far away something enormous lay down to sleep with its grief.

Freya woke with wet cheeks and Ash's hand already on her shoulder.

"It broke," she whispered. "The covenant. I saw it break. Why show me that? Why show me the worst part first?"

Ash's face in the dark was solemn and very old for a moment — old the way the Steppe is old — and then the child said a phrase in the old language, soft, and Freya's gift carried it across:

*Because you have to know what you're renewing. A promise you don't remember is just a noise.*

Freya lay back down. The candle had burned to a coin of light. Five weeks, minus two days. Somewhere out in the dark, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward fullness at the same patient rate it had climbed for centuries, indifferent to ledgers, to deadlines, to a bookkeeper who had built her entire life on the principle that if she stayed small and quiet and accurate, nothing large would ever require anything of her.

"I used to be good at not being needed," she told the ceiling.

From the cot beside her, Ash made a small sound that needed no translation at all. It meant: *too late.*

* * *

Morning brought the schedule, and the schedule brought the end of denial.

Freya sat at her desk with her private ledger open, doing what she did best: arithmetic. On the left column, the tasks. On the right column, the days remaining. Learn the covenant words: unknown number, unknown difficulty, taught by a child, via dreams, at a rate of roughly one philosophy per night. Find out *where* the renewal must be spoken — the rhyme said the Steppe, the rats had hinted at the heart, the dream had shown a glass hall that might be memory or metaphor. Determine whether she could leave the Guild undetected. Determine what happened to Ash if she couldn't.

Determine, in short, everything, in thirty-three days, while maintaining flawless attendance records.

She was halfway through drafting a plausible illness — something respectable and slow, a lung complaint, nothing that invited visitors — when the knock came at her office door, and Marta's head appeared around it wearing the expression of a woman delivering news she'd rehearsed.

"Freya. You've got a visitor."

"It's half past seven. I don't have visitors. Nobody has visitors before eight; it's in the bylaws."

"Not a visitor exactly." Marta lowered her voice with relish. "Rider came in overnight from the capital. It's the Guild's audit division. They're doing a surprise inspection of the whole branch — gifts, registrations, irregular personnel." She paused for effect, having no idea how devastating the effect was. "There's an auditor setting up in the front office *right now*. Name of Brann. Sela Brann. They say she's never missed a discrepancy in eleven years."

Freya's pen stopped moving. In the silence, she could feel every falsified line in the intake ledger glowing like fire-salts, could hear the phantom chime of *irregular personnel*, could see with terrible clarity the entry in her own handwriting: *Ash Nystrom, ward of F. Nystrom*, signed with a flourish.

Thirty-three days, she had calculated. She had budgeted for wards, for distance, for the limits of her own gift.

She had not budgeted for the clock to arrive *today*.

"Tell her," Freya said, rising and smoothing her apron with hands that almost obeyed her, "that I'll be right out."

Beside the desk, unnoticed by Marta, the little brazier's cold ashes shifted — and Ash, who had been playing silently on the floor for an hour, stood up and looked toward the front office with an expression Freya had never seen on the child's face before.

It looked, very much, like recognition.
