## 7. The Auditor's Question

Sela Brann had been auditing the Meridian Guild for eleven days before anyone lied to her.

She knew this because she kept a tally. Not in a ledger—she'd left her official ledger locked in her travel chest at the Saltgate Inn, where it could do no one any good—but in a small notebook bound in grey leather that she carried in her coat pocket, the kind of notebook that looked like it might contain recipes or addresses and therefore invited no curiosity whatsoever. In it she recorded, in a hand so small it was nearly a code: *Day 1 — warehouse foreman Doss, evasive re: shipment manifests, but evasive like a man hiding laziness, not secrets. Day 3 — clerk Pell, honest to the point of tedium. Day 5 — nobody worth noting.*

And on the eleventh day, in a neat column beside the entry for the intake office: *Freya Nystrom. Lied twice. Both times about the child.*

Sela did not write down what she thought of the lies. That was the discipline of her trade. You recorded the fact; you kept the interpretation in your head, where it could ripen like winter fruit instead of spoiling on the page.

The Meridian Guild's coastal annex was not, by any stretch, an impressive building. It was a long stone warehouse with offices bolted onto its landward side like afterthoughts, smelling permanently of brine, lamp oil, and the particular sweetness of dried sea-lavender that the clerks burned to cover the first two smells. Sela had interviewed forty-one staff members in a borrowed room on the second floor—a room with one window, one table, two chairs, and a water pitcher that squeaked when poured, which she had come to think of as her co-investigator. People found the squeak unnerving. They talked faster to fill the silence after it.

She was three days from finishing the annex and moving upriver to the main house, and Guildmaster Vey's letter of instruction sat folded in her breast pocket like a stone she couldn't quite digest. *Inquire into irregularities of intake,* he had written, in his round, warm, terrible handwriting. *Particularly regarding persons or creatures of unusual provenance. Discretion expected.*

*Persons or creatures of unusual provenance.* Sela had read that phrase perhaps two hundred times since receiving it, and each reading revealed another layer of grease beneath the words. She had been an auditor for nine years. She knew what it meant when a guildmaster used language that soft. It meant there was something in his warehouse he wanted found—or something he wanted confirmed as found, whether or not it existed—and he wanted it done by someone deniable enough to disown afterward.

So she did what she always did with instructions that smelled wrong: she followed them precisely, and watched everything they weren't asking her to watch.

* * *

The child had arrived during the salt storm—that much everyone agreed on, though no two accounts agreed on anything else.

"A cart brought her," said the night porter, a broad woman named Hesk whose hands were the size of dinner plates. "Or walked. Or the storm dropped her, for all I know. I opened the door because something knocked, and there she was."

"Something knocked?"

"Knocked." Hesk demonstrated on the interview table, three polite raps. "Not banged. Not scratched. Knocked. Like a merchant." She frowned at her own knuckles as if they'd betrayed her. "Barefoot, mind you. In a salt storm. Bare feet don't survive a salt storm, Auditor. I've pulled worse men than me out of whiteouts missing fingers."

"And then?"

"Then she looked at me like I was the strange one." Hesk laughed, but it came out crooked. "Little thing, maybe seven years old, standing in all that howling white like it was a summer fair. And she made this—" Hesk stopped, hands hovering uselessly. "A sound. Not crying. Not words I know. But my dog, who has bitten four people in his life, went and lay down at her feet. Just lay down. Like a rug."

"Where is the child now?"

Hesk's face did something complicated. "Guildmaster Vey assigned her to Freya Nystrom's care. Intake office. She's got a cot behind the ledgers, far as I know."

"You sound uncertain."

"I sound," said Hesk carefully, "like a woman who knows which questions get answered in this building and which ones get you reassigned to the herring quota." She stood, apologized for nothing, and left.

Sela wrote: *Porter Hesk. Honest. Frightened. Of whom?*

* * *

Freya Nystrom came into the interview room on the twelfth day, and Sela understood within thirty seconds why the whole annex spoke of her the way they did—with a fondness that bordered on ownership, the way a village speaks of its well.

She was a narrow, quiet woman with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had spent a decade making herself small enough to fit between other people's attention. She poured the water without being asked. The pitcher squeaked. She didn't flinch; she smiled at it, faintly, as if it had told her a joke.

"Thank you for coming, Mistress Nystrom."

"You're welcome, Auditor Brann. Though I should say I've heard you've already spoken to everyone else twice, so if my answers differ from theirs, it's only because everyone else remembers better than I do."

It was delivered dryly, almost shyly, and Sela felt the hook of it go in anyway—the little invitation to laugh together, auditor and audited, on the same side of the table. *Good,* she thought. *Very good. Now let's see how deep the charm goes.*

"Let's begin simply. When did the child arrive?"

"The night of the salt storm. Eleven days ago now. The porter will have told you."

"She did. What was your first impression of her?"

Freya considered this with the seriousness of a woman balancing a column of figures. "That she was cold," she said. "That she needed soup more than she needed questions. So I gave her soup."

"And the language she speaks?"

"I don't speak it."

"No," Sela agreed pleasantly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point. Can you describe it?"

Another pause. Sela watched Freya's hands—resting flat on the table, still as paper—and noticed that the stillness was effortful. There was a tension in the wrists, the particular discipline of hands that wanted very much to be doing something else.

"It sounds old," Freya said at last. "I'm sorry, that's not useful. It sounds like... like a word you half-remember from childhood, stretched out over a whole conversation. You keep feeling you're about to understand it, and then you don't." She met Sela's eyes. "I'm a bookkeeper, Auditor. I transcribe things for a living. If I could write down what she says, I would have written it down."

That was true. Sela was fairly sure it was true. It was also, she noted, an answer to a question she hadn't asked.

"Here is what puzzles me, Mistress Nystrom." Sela turned her grey notebook to a fresh page, unhurried. "The intake ledger for the eleventh. Show me the entry."

Something moved behind Freya's eyes—not fear exactly. More like a door closing gently in a house where all the doors had been open.

"There isn't one," Freya said.

"There isn't an entry for a child taken into the Guild's care? A child assigned quarters, assigned a guardian, fed from Guild stores for eleven days?" Sela let the pitcher squeak as she refilled her own cup. "Mistress Nystrom, I have read your ledgers. All of them. Three years of them, back at the main house, before I ever came here. You record the number of nails in a crate of nails. You once noted, in the margin, that a consignment of glass beads was 'four short and likely to remain so, as the carrier wept.' You are the most meticulous bookkeeper I have encountered in nine years of auditing. A missing entry is not an oversight. It is a decision."

The silence that followed had texture. Sela let it sit between them like a stone on the table.

"I was going to enter her," Freya said finally, quietly. "I drafted the entry. Then the Guildmaster instructed me to hold it pending his review. You'd have to ask him why."

*Ah.* Sela wrote nothing, which was itself a kind of note. Because that, too, was probably true—and it meant either that Vey was telling his own staff to bury things, or that Freya Nystrom was quick enough to build her lies out of true bricks, which was worse and better in roughly equal measure.

"Then I shall ask him," Sela said, and closed her notebook. "One last question. Off the record, if you like—it changes nothing I file either way." She leaned back. "The beasts. The caravans through the eastern pass haven't been attacked in five months. Before that, we lost a convoy every season, sometimes more. Every driver I've interviewed says the same thing: the elk walk alongside the wagons now. The wolves watch them pass. Nobody can tell me why. Have you noticed anything, in your work here, that might explain it?"

And there—there—Sela saw it. Not guilt. Something stranger. Freya Nystrom looked at her with an expression of such naked, startled longing that Sela felt it in her own chest, like a hand pressed briefly against a window from the other side.

"No," Freya said. "Nothing."

"Thank you," said Sela, meaning it, and watched the bookkeeper leave with the careful, contained steps of a woman carrying something breakable.

She wrote one line in the grey notebook: *She knows why the caravans are safe. And she is dying to tell someone.*

* * *

That evening Sela did something slightly against procedure, which was to say she did something she did most evenings: she went walking without announcing it.

The annex backed onto the stock pens where the Guild kept the draft animals awaiting spring contracts. She told herself it was routine inspection—feed stores, fencing, the usual—and mostly believed it. The evening was cold and enormous, the sky doing that pale gold-to-violet thing that the coast managed even in the worst months, and the pens smelled of hay and horse and honest animal patience.

A bay cart-horse near the fence lifted its head as she passed.

Sela stopped. She was not a fanciful woman. Her mother had been a fishwife and her father a customs officer, and between the two of them she had inherited a horror of nonsense and a nose for evasion, in that order. But the horse was looking at her with an attention that had weight to it, ears forward, and when she took another step it stepped with her, matching her along the fence line like a chaperone.

"You're not mine," she told it. "Whatever you think you're guarding, I'm not after it."

The horse blew out a breath that fogged in the cold and did not move away.

Sela stood there longer than she meant to, hands in her pockets, looking at the darkening windows of the intake office where a light burned late. Somewhere beyond the pens, out on the flats, she could hear the elk herds moving—those vast quiet migrations that had recently begun passing close to the roads, close to the caravans, close enough to touch, and never touching, never raiding, never doing any of the thousand destructive things that beasts were supposed to do, according to every report she'd filed and every grave she'd stood over.

Her brother had died in a border raid eight years ago. Joren, twenty-six, a caravan guard with bad knees and a good singing voice, killed along with eleven others when elk spooked by something—no one ever established what—had torn through the eastern pass. She had received his effects in a Guild crate, itemized. She had appreciated the itemization. It was the only part of the whole business that had made sense.

And now the beasts had stopped. Five months of quiet. And a barefoot child had walked out of a salt storm speaking a language older than any kingdom, and the Guildmaster wanted it found and catalogued and priced, and the bookkeeper who kept the most honest ledgers in the province had lied to her twice with true bricks, and a cart-horse was escorting her along a fence like she mattered.

"I'm not your enemy," she said to the horse, and was embarrassed to hear how much she meant it.

The horse snorted, which could have meant anything, and wandered off.

* * *

Back in her rented room at the Saltgate Inn, Sela spread her notes across the bed and did what she always did at the end of an audit: she laid the facts out like a hand of cards and asked herself which game was being played.

Fact: the child's arrival was unrecorded, on instruction or by omission, depending on whose story held.

Fact: Vey's letter wanted "persons or creatures of unusual provenance" located, discreetly, by her.

Fact: the beasts had gone peaceful, and everyone in this building knew more about it than they were saying, and the person who knew most was also the person least capable of lying convincingly, which meant she was either innocent or the best liar in the province, and Sela's professional judgment—nine years, forty-one audits, two convictions—said innocent.

Fact: when Sela had asked about the caravans, Freya Nystrom had looked at her like a drowning woman looks at a rope, and then had said *no.*

Sela sat on the edge of the bed, elbows on knees, and thought about Joren's itemized crate. She thought about the word *provenance*, which was what the Guild called it when a living thing became inventory. She thought about a child who knocked politely on doors in salt storms, and dogs that lay down like rugs, and elk that walked beside wagons full of the same species that had spent a century shooting at them.

There were two ways this audit could go. She could file what Vey wanted filed, collect her fee, and go home to a city that paid her to count other people's sins without naming her own. Or she could find out what actually walked out of that storm—and why every animal within ten miles seemed to have appointed itself its guardian.

She blew out the lamp, and in the dark she made the decision she suspected she'd been making since day one, since the first evasive foreman, since the letter with its greasy warm handwriting.

Tomorrow she would stop interviewing staff and start watching the intake office. Quietly. After hours.

Because somewhere in that building of ledgers and lamp oil, a bookkeeper was sitting on the biggest entry of the century—and Sela Brann had just decided she wanted to read it.
