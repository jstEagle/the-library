## 8. The Price Named

The tea was wrong.

Freya noticed it before Vey said a single word, because Guildmaster Oskar Vey had drunk the same bitter redleaf from the same chipped clay pot for as long as she had kept his ledgers, and today he had sent down to the kitchens for honey and the good cups—the ones with the gilt rims that only came out for visiting merchants and funerals. That was the first entry in the ledger of her dread. The second was that he'd asked her to sit. In eleven years, Oskar Vey had never once asked her to sit. He simply talked at her while she stood with her book open, absorbing instructions the way the warehouse walls absorbed salt.

"Sit," he said again, gently, when she hesitated on the threshold of his office. "Please, Freya. You've earned a chair."

*You've earned a chair.* She sat. The chair was deeper than she expected and she sank into it like a coin into a well.

The office smelled of ink and lamp oil and, beneath that, the faint mineral breath of the steppe that clung to everything that came off the caravans—salt and glass-dust and something older, like the inside of a cave. Rain ticked against the window. Not true rain; a salt storm had been building all afternoon, the sky gone the color of dirty pewter, and the drops left white rings on the sill where they dried. Outside, the warehouse yard had gone quiet. Even the cart-horses stood still in their traces, heads low, ears turned toward the guildhouse as if listening through the walls.

Freya did not let herself wonder what they were listening *for*. She had gotten very good, over eleven years, at not wondering.

"You've been with me a long time," Vey said. He poured the tea himself, which was the third entry. His hands were steady. He had beautiful hands for an old man, long-fingered and clean, the hands of someone who had never once loaded a cart or mucked a stable, though he could price a shipment of frost-orchids to the copper without blinking. "Eleven years. Do you know what I paid for you?"

"I—" Freya's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "Paid for me, Guildmaster?"

"Your apprenticeship. Your board. Your silence." He smiled, and it was a warm smile, genuinely warm, and that was somehow worse than if it had been cold. "Every scribe in this building thinks you're the dullest creature ever to hold a quill. Diligent. Punctual. No ambitions, no vices, no questions. Do you know how valuable that is? A person who asks no questions is worth ten who answer them."

"I try to be thorough," Freya said, which was the sort of thing the dull version of herself would say, and she watched him enjoy hearing it.

"You are thorough. That's rather the problem." He settled back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. He was a broad man going soft, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that made strangers tell him their troubles in taverns. "Three weeks ago, the intake ledger shows a child admitted during the salt storm. Unregistered. Unnamed. And then—" he tapped the arm of his chair, once, like a man tapping a barrel to hear if it's full, "—the entries stop. No disposition recorded. No transfer. No fee collected. The Meridian Guild does not lose children, Freya. We are many things, but we are not careless."

The tea tasted like nothing at all. She drank it anyway, because her hands wanted occupation.

"There was a clerical error," she said. "I meant to correct it."

"Mm." He gazed out the window at the graying yard. "And the rats."

Freya went very carefully still.

"The rats," Vey repeated, pleasantly. "My night clerk swears the vermin have been behaving strangely. Marching about in lines. Sitting in the corners of the counting room like little gray judges. He wanted to buy cats. I told him not to." He turned back to her, and his eyes were mild and blue and bottomless. "Because I know what it means when the small things start organizing, Freya. I knew a woman once, years ago, before your time. A laundress here in Meridian. Wonderful with horses. Horses would cross a flooded ford for her that they'd drown any other rider attempting. And one day the Guild's auditors came for her, and she ran, and they caught her at the river, and do you know what happened?"

Freya's voice came out very small. "No, Guildmaster."

"Nothing," said Vey. "Absolutely nothing. Because I bought her contract first. Paid the auditors' fine, took her debt onto the Guild's books, and sent her east with a pension and a warning." He lifted his cup to her. "I have always found that gifts make better chains than irons. Cheaper, too."

The storm thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, a horse snorted—a long, deliberate sound—and Freya heard, threaded under it, so faint she might have imagined it: *soon, soon, soon.* She kept her face smooth. Eleven years of practice. Her heart was hammering like a clerk late with the quarterly totals.

"You know what I am," she said quietly.

"I know you come in some mornings smelling of the stables when there's no reason for you to be in the stables. I know the mule with the cracked hoof walked sound two days after you spent an hour 'checking the feed stores.' I know that when the auditor Sela Brann interviewed the staff, you lied to her twice, and you are a terrible liar, Freya, you blink like a startled owl." He said it all without malice, almost fondly, the way one reads aloud a list of items received in good condition. "And I know there is a child sleeping in the loft above the feed stores who speaks a language that makes old stones hum."

The cup rattled against its saucer. Freya set it down with both hands.

"Ash has done nothing," she said. "Ash is just a child. Lost. Mute except for—that. Whatever it is. If the Guild wants compensation for the keep, I'll work it off, I'll—"

"Freya." He said her name the way he entered figures into a ledger: precisely, finally. "Do you know why the border wars have lasted thirty years?"

She blinked. "Because the beasts broke the covenant. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what is profitable to know." Vey rose and went to the great map on his wall, the one of the Meridian territories, the steppe marked in blank white beyond the last surveyed line, the way cartographers mark places they'd rather you didn't look at too closely. "War is the finest market there is. Armies need grain, iron, mules, salt. Caravans need guards, tolls, insurance against raids. Every season the fighting continues, the Guild's margins improve by a factor I could show you to four decimal places, if you'd like. I have the figures." He glanced back at her. "Of course you'd like. You're thorough."

He let that hang in the air between them, delicate as a knife balanced on its point.

"Three months ago," he went on, "the raids stopped. Not slowed—stopped. The beasts withdrew from the caravan roads entirely. My factors in the east report wolves escorting lost couriers to safety. Elk clearing roads of snowfall. It is costing me a fortune, Freya. Peace, unmanaged peace, is ruinous." He tapped the blank white space on the map. "And then a child walks out of a salt storm speaking the language that built those wards out there, the language both sides swore on and then broke, and every remnant faction of the old war would empty their treasuries for a living relic of the covenant. There are buyers already asking. There have been buyers asking since before the child arrived—I receive letters, Freya, I receive *so many letters*—and now, at last, I have inventory."

The word landed in Freya's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. *Inventory.*

"No," she said.

Vey sighed, the sigh of a patient teacher. "You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear the terms. Ash is not goods. Ash is not a relic. Ash is a frightened child who doesn't remember where they came from, and I will not—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the way his eyes sharpened with interest at the crack, cataloguing it. She lowered her voice. "I will not deliver a child to slavers."

"Not slavers. Collectors." He returned to his desk and drew a folded paper from a drawer, laying it flat but keeping his hand upon it, a merchant's instinct even now. "The buyers are exiles, Freya. Descendants of families driven off the steppe when the wards woke and the war began. They were promised, thirty years ago, that the covenant could be renewed and their homelands restored. Their grandparents died waiting. Now they believe a child who speaks the old tongue can call the wardstones awake, and they will pay—this is the offer on the table—one hundred thousand crowns. Plus title to three caravan routes. Plus, and I confess this is the part I value most, exclusive Guild brokerage over any renewed covenant. Think of it. Every prayer, every ward-renewal, every treaty spoken in that language, priced and licensed through Meridian. Forever."

"That's madness."

"That's *business*," said Vey, with real cheer. "The terms are simple. You will take the child to the Steppe's edge—to the old meeting-stone at Grayford Crossing, where the exiles' representatives will receive them. You are known to the beasts along the road, apparently, so travel should be safe, which is convenient for everyone. You'll have a Guild escort, provisions, papers. When the handoff is complete, your debts are cleared. Your position becomes partnership. And the child—"

"Is sold."

"—is delivered to people who will clothe and feed and cherish it far better than a warehouse loft can," Vey said smoothly, "and who will give it a purpose, a homeland, a destiny. Honestly, Freya, among all the fates available to a nameless orphan in this hard world, being adored by wealthy zealots is hardly the worst. Some parents pay to secure less."

"And if I refuse?"

He looked at her then with genuine, unhurried kindness, and Freya understood, finally and completely, that Oskar Vey had loved her in his way for eleven years the way a man loves a lock he trusts: for keeping shut.

"If you refuse," he said, "I regretfully report an unregistered gifted employee and an undocumented child to the Crown assessors. The employee faces the gift-tithe courts—you've seen what confiscation does to people, they leave with hollow eyes and no more talent than a spoon. The child goes to the assessors too, and assessors sell to whoever pays, and I promise you the exiles bid higher and gentler than the alternatives." He refilled her cup, unasked. "So you see, my dear, refusing doesn't save Ash. Refusing only removes you from the story. Whereas agreeing—" he slid the paper across the desk toward her, "—makes you a partner, protects the child's comfort on the journey, and lets us all be rich and reasonably kind together. I've taken the liberty of drafting the contract. Take it home. Read it tonight. You're thorough; you'll want to check my figures."

Freya looked at the paper. She did not touch it yet. Through the window, the storm had begun to proper fall, fat salt-heavy drops hissing where they struck the yard stones, and the horses stood motionless in it, not seeking shelter, all of them facing the guildhouse, all of them waiting.

*Soon,* the wind said, or the horse, or her own blood.

"May I ask one question, Guildmaster?" she said. "Not about the terms. Just—for myself."

"Ask anything. Honesty costs me nothing today."

"When the laundress—the woman with the horses. When you saved her. Did you ever wonder whether you were saving her, or just... filing her somewhere you could find her later?"

Something flickered across Vey's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning in dark water. For half a heartbeat he looked tired, and old, and like a man who had once been someone else entirely.

"I wondered," he said quietly. "For about a year. Then I stopped, because the answer didn't change the arithmetic." He straightened his cuffs. "Read the contract, Freya. We'll sign tomorrow after the morning count. And Freya—" she had reached the door, her hand on the cold iron latch, "—the child stays in the loft until then. I've posted men on the stairs. For everyone's protection, you understand. These are uncertain times."

* * *

She read the contract by candlelight in her narrow room above the counting house, with Ash curled asleep on her bed because the men on the stairs had not thought to search a bookkeeper's own quarters, and Freya had simply walked the child out past them with a stack of ledgers in her arms and a bored expression, and Ash hidden beneath the topmost volume like a misfiled entry.

Ash slept the way animals sleep: totally, instantly, with one ear still working. Even dreaming, the child's fingers moved sometimes, tracing shapes on the blanket—little hooked glyphs, the old script, rehearsing something. Learning it, maybe. Or teaching it. To whom, Ash wouldn't say, though Freya suspected the dreams themselves were the classroom, and that lately her own sleep had grown crowded with syllables that weren't hers.

She read the contract twice. It was exactly as generous as he'd promised. That was the trap's whole architecture: every clause kind, every term fair, the whole document built like a comfortable chair you sink into and cannot rise from. Clause nine gave her guardianship of the child for the duration of transport. Clause twelve guaranteed Ash's humane treatment "in perpetuity," which sounded lovely until you noticed that perpetuity belonged to the buyers, not to Ash. Clause fifteen transferred all rights of negotiation over "covenant-related utterances" to the Meridian Guild.

They were going to license prayers. They were going to put a meter on miracles.

Freya set the pages down and looked at the sleeping child for a long time. *A living relic.* *Inventory.* She thought about the ledger she'd falsified three weeks ago, her first crooked entry in eleven honest years, and how easy it had been, and how right. She thought about the rats sitting in the corners like little gray judges, and the horses standing in the salt rain saying *soon*, and the terrible growing certainty that everything alive around her had already decided what came next, and was only waiting for her to catch up.

Loyalty, she realized, dies quietly. There's no thunderclap. You just pour the tea, and drink it, and set down the cup, and understand that you will never drink from this pot again.

She took a fresh sheet of paper. At the top, in her neat bookkeeper's hand, she wrote: *Provisions for journey — actual.* And beneath it, the true list: not the Guild's route to Grayford Crossing, but the eastern paths, the ones the elk kept clear, the ones the maps left white. Dried fish. A firebow. The heavy cloak from the storeroom that no one had inventoried since the war began. She calculated distances, water sources, moon-phases. The thirteenth moon was coming; the archives rhyme said so, and the beasts said so, and Ash's sleeping fingers said so. Whatever waited at the Steppe's edge, it would not be Vey's buyers deciding the child's fate. It would be the steppe itself, and the old words, and whatever Freya was willing to spend to speak them.

She blew out the candle, lay down beside Ash, and closed her eyes.

In the morning she would sign Vey's contract with a steady hand and a startled owl's blink, and thank him for his generosity, and mean none of it. Tonight, in the dark, she felt the last thread of her loyalty to the Meridian Guild go slack—not cut, just quietly, completely unwound, like a knot finally admitting it had always been tied wrong.

Beside her, Ash stirred and murmured something in the old language, soft as snowfall.

Freya didn't need the translation anymore. She was beginning to dream in it.

But outside, on the stairs, a floorboard creaked—and held—creaked again, closer, and stopped just beyond her door, where someone stood listening in the dark.
