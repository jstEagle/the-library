## 9. Flight Across the White

The night they fled, Freya stole three things from the Meridian Guild: a ledger, a pen, and herself.

The ledger was easy. It was her own field book, the one she'd kept for eleven years under the pretense of "inventory notes," and it had never once contained inventory. It contained the names of every beast she had ever spoken to, the terms of every small bargain, and — as of three nights ago — a page headed *ASH* in her neatest hand, because if she was going to commit treason she was going to do it with proper documentation.

The pen was Ash's idea, insofar as Ash had ideas that could be called ideas. The child had simply appeared at Freya's elbow holding the guild's good quill — the goose-feathered one Oskar Vey used for signing contracts — and had looked up at her with an expression of such serene criminality that Freya took it without argument.

"You're going to get us both hanged," Freya whispered, tucking it into her coat.

Ash said something in the old language. Freya didn't know the words yet, but she knew the tone. Every language in the world had a tone that meant *good.*

Herself was harder. Herself had been hidden for so long that extracting her felt like prying a fossil out of stone. But here is what nobody tells you about invisibility: it only works while you stand still. And Freya Nystrom had finally, irrevocably, stopped standing still.

* * *

They left through the salt cellar.

Not the kitchen's salt cellar — though Freya had considered it, briefly, in a moment of panic-induced whimsy — but the great underground store where the Guild kept its traded wonders: barrels of grey steppe-salt that sang when struck, salt that remembered the shape of whatever touched it last. The tunnel beyond it ran west beneath the wall, built by some long-dead quartermaster who had apparently also been a smuggler, or possibly just a man who hated his superiors, which amounted to the same thing.

Freya had learned of the tunnel from the rats.

This was, she reflected as she lifted the trapdoor, a strange sentence to be true of her life. She had spent two evenings among the warehouse vermin, listening to their chittering gossip, and they had told her three things: that the tunnel existed; that the child spoke the tongue from before the Glass War; and that the Guildmaster's kindness was a purse waiting to be opened. Rats were not subtle creatures. They did not deal in metaphor. When a rat told you a man was a trap, you believed the rat.

"Up," she whispered to Ash. "Quietly."

Ash went up quietly the way rivers go downhill — naturally, completely, without any sense that quiet was an effort. The child moved through the dark like something the dark had personally invited. Freya followed, heart hammering, and emerged into the night behind the tannery, where the smell alone could have covered the escape of a marching band.

Above them, the sky was doing something obscene.

The salt storm had passed in the afternoon, scrubbing the air down to its bones, and now the stars came out in numbers that seemed less like astronomy and more like an accusation — *where have you been looking all this time?* And above the eastern horizon hung the thirteenth moon, swollen and pale as a fingernail clipping from some god's thumb, three-quarters full and counting down.

Freya looked away from it. Looking at it made her arithmetic itch. Twenty-six days. Give or take. Give or take being the part that terrified her, because the rhyme in the archives had been written by someone who thought moon-counting was obvious, and nothing was ever obvious, that was the entire problem with archives.

"Ash." She crouched. "We go north-west. Across the White. There are people at the Steppe's edge who want you, and none of them are kind, and there's a place past them — the heart — where I think..." She faltered. She had been about to say *where I think you can be safe,* which was a lie, because nowhere on the Glass Steppe was safe, that was rather its defining feature. "Where we need to be. That's all. We need to be there."

Ash studied her with those eyes that were too old for the face around them, then reached out and pressed one small finger against Freya's sternum, right over the hammering heart.

And spoke.

Freya didn't understand the word. Not yet. But feeling poured through it the way heat pours through iron — a word that meant something like *yes* and something like *together* and something like *I have walked farther than you and I am still walking.* Her eyes stung. She blamed the tannery.

"All right," she said gruffly. "All right. Come on, then."

* * *

By dawn they were three miles out, and by midmorning Freya had made her first mistake.

It was a small one. That was the horror of it — how small it was. They had stopped to drink at a seep spring, and Ash had asked her something — held up two fingers, tilted their head, the child's shorthand for *how far?* — and Freya had opened her mouth to say *four days to the edge, six if the flats are soft* and found that she did not know.

She knew she had known. The knowledge left a shape behind, the way a chair leaves a dent in carpet. Four days? Five? She had read the survey maps a hundred times. She had *filed* the survey maps. Distances were her profession; she could price a mile of silk in her sleep.

"Four days," she said, guessing, and heard the guess in her own voice, and hated it.

Ash looked at her a moment too long. Then the child pointed at the ledger bag on her hip, and then at her head, and made a gesture Freya would come to know well over the following weeks: fingers opening, like a book letting go of its pages.

"I'm fine," Freya said.

Ash repeated the gesture, slower.

"I said I'm fine."

Ash sat down on a warm stone, folded their hands, and waited with the patience of a creature who had once watched a glacier move, and Freya stood there in the middle of the beautiful lethal morning and admitted, silently, to no one, that she could not remember whether she had ever liked honey.

That was the thing she'd lost. Somewhere between the guild door and the seep spring, between one heartbeat and another, her opinion of honey had gone out of her like a candle. She searched for it and found only the absence, smooth-sided, clean. She might have loved honey. She might have loathed it. Eleven years of breakfasts, gone to wherever lost opinions go.

*Deep speech costs,* the old beast-speaker's marginalia had warned, in handwriting like spider legs. *You pay in what you keep.*

She hadn't yet spoken deep speech on this journey. She hadn't spoken anything at all. Which meant the cost had started earlier — back in the warehouse, perhaps, with the rats, with the cart-horse, with every whisper she'd ever let herself hear. The debt had simply been patient.

"Right," Freya said aloud, to the morning, to Ash, to the smooth-sided hole where honey used to live. "New rule. Everything important goes in the book. Everything. If I ever tell you I've remembered something, check the book first."

Ash nodded solemnly, and then — gently, terribly — patted the stone beside them, inviting her to sit and grieve for a moment before they walked on.

She sat. She gave it exactly ten seconds. Then they walked on.

* * *

The White earned its name by noon.

The salt flats began where the grass gave up, and they were not white the way paper is white or snow is white; they were white the way a bell is silent — a white made of absence, miles of crusted earth so bright it rang in the eyes. Heat shimmered off it in slow mirages. Here and there the crust had buckled into ridges and blades, glassy where ancient fires had fused the salt, and the wind moving across them made sounds like distant choirs warming up.

"It's beautiful," Freya said, and meant it, and immediately stepped into a patch of crust that collapsed to the knee, pitching her forward into salt that got in her mouth, her ears, and — she was certain — her soul.

Ash helped haul her out. The child's face performed no visible smile, but something around the eyes did, and Freya, spitting brine, decided to count it as laughter. A traveling companion who laughed at your misfortunes without sound was, she supposed, at least economical.

They walked. The sun climbed. The choir-wind sang. And in the late afternoon, when the light went gold and long, Freya saw the dust.

Not storm dust. Moving dust. A column of it, far to the south, undulating — and beneath it, resolving as it crested a rise, the herd.

Elk. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, flowing across the southern flats on their autumn road — antlers like bare winter trees, bodies like a river given fur. The migration. Of course. The rhyme had mentioned it, buried in a verse about timing: *when the herds walk the white road, the wards grow thin as breath.* She'd filed it under *later.* Later had arrived.

"They'll pass within half a mile of us," she breathed. "If we're seen as herd, we cross the flats in a day instead of four. If we're seen as wolves—" She didn't finish. Wolves near a migration got trampled, and rightly.

Ash tugged her sleeve and pointed at her mouth, then at the herd, then raised an eyebrow with devastating clarity: *well?*

Freya's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for falling off roofs.

She had not spoken openly — openly, standing up, in daylight, to anything larger than a rat — since she was nineteen years old. Eleven years of keeping her gift folded small enough to fit inside a ledger. And now the flats were empty in every direction, the Guild was a day's hard ride behind them, and a river of elk was coming, and the only person watching was a child who already knew.

"You'll think less of me if I'm bad at this," she warned.

Ash shook their head with great conviction.

"Liar," said Freya, and closed her eyes, and let herself unfold.

It was like unclenching a fist she'd forgotten she was making. The gift rose in her the way blood rises into a numb limb — painful, flooding, alive. She breathed the way the old woman had taught her, decades ago, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and secrets: *don't push the words out, Freya-lamb. Open the door and see who's already there.*

The herd noticed her before she said anything. She felt it happen — a thousand minds swinging toward her like a thousand weathervanes in one wind. The migration slowed. The front ranks turned. And then the whole golden river of them bent toward the two small figures on the white plain, and came.

"Oh," Freya said faintly. "Oh, that's — that's a lot of attention. Hello. Hello! Good evening. Lovely — antlers. All of you. Very... seasonal."

The lead cow stopped ten feet away. She was grey-muzzled and enormous, with one antler palm worn smooth as if the world had leaned on it for years, and she looked at Freya with the flat appraisal of a matriarch who has seen everything twice.

*Small-voice,* the elk said. Her mind-voice was dry as the flats themselves. *You smell of cages and ink. What does a cage-and-ink thing want of the People's road?*

"Passage," Freya said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she pushed through anyway. "My — my charge and I. We walk to the heart of the Steppe, and the flats will take us four days on foot, and there are men behind us who ride faster than we walk. Walk with your herd and we make it in one. I ask passage, and I offer payment."

*Payment.* The word moved through the herd like a shiver through water. The lead cow's ear flicked. *What has a cage-and-ink thing to pay?*

And here was the trap, laid by her own younger self. Because the old bargains ran on payment, real payment, and she had nothing — no salt, no silver, nothing but a stolen quill and a book of names — except the one thing she'd hoarded all her life without knowing it was currency.

"Stories," Freya said. "Memories. True ones, freely given. Your people value what lasts, and memories last longer than teeth."

A murmur ran the herd, mind-to-mind, a sound like wind deciding something. The lead cow chewed on the offer, literally and otherwise.

*One story,* she said at last. *One true memory, given whole, for carrying you one day along our road. This is fair. This is old law. Do you consent, small-voice, knowing that what is given cannot be taken back?*

Freya's mouth had gone dry. She thought of honey. She thought of the smooth-sided holes she was already carrying, and how many more she could afford, and understood with terrible clarity that the answer was *not many* — and that she was going to say yes anyway, because behind her were riders, and ahead of her was a deadline measured in moons, and a person who refuses to pay for what she needs ends up paying more.

"I consent," she said. "But — wait. Let me choose which one."

She knelt, right there on the burning white plain, and pulled out the ledger, and flipped to a page near the back. She had prepared for this. Three nights ago, sleepless, she had written down the memories she could bear to lose, ranked by pain, like a merchant pricing stock. *The taste of the guild's burnt coffee: cheap. The name of my first schoolteacher: moderate. My mother's laugh:* — and there her pen had stopped, and blotched, and she had turned the page.

She chose the winter she was nine, the one with the ice festival and the boy who'd dared her onto the frozen canal. A good memory. A whole one. She held it up in her mind, examined it one last time — the orange lanterns, the crack of the ice singing under her boots, the terrifying joy — and then she opened her hands, figuratively, and gave it.

It went out of her like warmth leaving a bath. One moment the winter was hers; the next it belonged to the herd, passing mind to mind down the long brown river of them, and she saw it flicker in a hundred pairs of dark eyes — the lanterns, the singing ice — and then it settled somewhere in the middle of the herd, in some young elk who would carry a human childhood for the rest of its life, and it was gone from her.

She knew she had lost it. She could not remember what it had felt like to have it. The difference between those two facts was the whole price, paid at once.

"Well," she said, when she could speak. "That's — yes. Fine. Bargain struck."

The lead cow lowered her great head and touched Freya's forehead with the smooth palm of her antler, lightly, the way you'd close a book you intended to open again.

*You pay like a queen and flinch like a thief,* the elk observed. *Walk in the middle of us, small-voice. The calves will ask you questions. Answer honestly; they can tell.*

* * *

Traveling inside a migrating herd was like living inside a weather system that loved you.

The elk flowed around Freya and Ash all through the golden afternoon and into the violet dusk, closing ranks whenever the ground grew treacherous, and the calves — as promised — asked questions. Endless questions. *Why do you walk on only two legs? Is the small one a calf? Why is your den made of cloth? Do you dream? What do you dream?* Freya answered until her voice wore thin, and discovered, to her astonishment, that she was enjoying herself. That was the exhilaration the old woman had tried to describe, all those years ago: not the power, but the *company.* Eleven years of talking to beasts in whispers and shadows, and it turned out the whole time there had been a world of company waiting, if only she'd stop hiding.

Ash rode pickaback on a yearling who had appointed himself, and drowsed to the rhythm of the long stride, and the herd carried them through the night at a pace no human legs could match.

Sela Brann, riding hard a day behind, would find their trail dissolve into ten thousand hoofprints fanning across the White, and would swear with impressive creativity, and turn her horse south on a hunch that would cost her two days. Freya never learned this. But the herd's outriders, ranging wide, noted the lone rider and her fine guild-issue boots, and mentioned it to the matriarch, who mentioned it to Freya, who allowed herself — for the first time since the guild door — one full hour of believing they might actually make it.

She spent the hour memorizing things. Deliberately, fiercely, like a woman packing a house she knew would burn: Ash's weight asleep against the yearling's neck. The smell of the herd, musk and dust and milk. The exact color of the thirteenth moon rising over the glass. She wrote them in the ledger by starlight, in case.

Near midnight, the matriarch walked beside her a while, silent, and then said:

*The small one dreams loud.*

Freya's blood cooled. "Loud?"

*Loud enough that the east hills heard. Loud enough that things older than hills turned over in their sleep and listened.* The elk's dark eye caught the moon. *Something is waking on the road ahead of you, small-voice. Something that knew the tongue the calf speaks when the world was young. My people will not pass the old wardstone village. You understand me? We will carry you to the edge of the place where the ground remembers, and there we leave you, and after that you walk alone.*

Freya looked down at Ash, asleep, dreaming loud enough to wake the world.

"How far," she said, "is the wardstone village?"

The matriarch told her.

It was much closer than four days.
