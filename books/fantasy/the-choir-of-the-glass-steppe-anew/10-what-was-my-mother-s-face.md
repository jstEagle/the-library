## 10. What Was My Mother's Face

The trouble with forgetting, Freya decided on the third morning out from the Guild's reach, was that it did not feel like anything at all.

She had expected loss to announce itself — a door slamming, a tooth pulled, some clean and terrible moment she could point to later and say *there, that is when it went*. Instead it was like snow falling on a path behind her while she walked. She only noticed the whiteness when she turned around.

They had made camp in a hollow of wind-carved stone, where the elk had left them at dawn with a courtesy that still unsettled her. The great grey bull had touched his nose to her forehead before going, and the touch had felt like a signature on a contract she hadn't finished reading. Ash sat nearby, cross-legged on a folded blanket, sorting pebbles by a system that made no sense to anyone but Ash, and humming.

Freya was trying to remember her mother's face.

This should have been easy. It was, in fact, the sort of thing a person did without trying — the way one breathed, or flinched from cold. Her mother's face had been *hers*, worn smooth into her by twenty years of looking. There had been a crease beside the left eye from squinting against harbor light. There had been flour, usually, somewhere near the jaw. There had been —

There had been nothing. Freya reached for the memory the way she'd reached for the ledger shelf in the dark of the warehouse, hand out, certain of its place, and closed her fingers on empty air.

"No," she said aloud, which was foolish, because saying it aloud made it more true.

Ash looked up. Pebbles scattered.

"Nothing," Freya said again, and heard her own voice go thin and strange. "I can't — I know she had a face. Obviously she had a face. I know she laughed at my father's jokes even when they were bad, *especially* when they were bad, I know she burned the fish every single Friday for eleven years and blamed the stove, I know all of that, I have all of that—" She was talking too fast now, the way people talked when they were walking backward off a cliff and hoping their feet would find ground. "But her face. Ash, I can't see her face."

Ash crossed the hollow in three quick steps and took Freya's hands. The child's palms were warm despite the steppe wind, always warm, as though the old language kept a small fire banked under the skin.

*Tell me,* said Ash's hands, or rather told Freya's hands, because that was how it worked now: not meaning arriving through the ears but feeling arriving through the skin, translated by whatever lived in her that she had spent nine years pretending wasn't there. *Tell me what you do see.*

"Friday," Freya whispered. "Fish burning. She's standing with her back to me, and she's waving the smoke out the window with a towel, and she's laughing, and Father is saying something terrible about how smoke adds flavor, and I'm — I'm small, I'm sitting on the counter, and I can hear everything. Not just them." Her throat tightened. "The gulls outside. The neighbor's dog. The fish itself, if you can believe it, a kind of offended bubbling. I could hear all of it. And she turned around to tell me to stop making faces at the herring—"

*And?*

"And I don't know what she looked like when she turned around."

The wind moved through the stones above them, a low organ note. Somewhere a lark was doing something showy and unnecessary, because larks had never once considered the feelings of grieving women.

*Sit down,* Ash signed against her wrist, gently bossy. *Breathe. I will make tea. Tea fixes most things and dignifies the rest.*

"You're seven years old."

*Eight,* Ash corrected, with dignity, and went to fuss with the kettle.

* * *

It was Sela Brann's fault, Freya thought later, uncharitably and then, upon reflection, accurately. Not the auditor herself — the auditor was two days' ride behind them and possibly an ally, possibly a trap, a question Freya had stopped being able to hold in her head for longer than an hour. No: it was Sela's *question*, asked in that flat, fair voice across the interview table back at the Guild, that had started all this.

*How long have you been able to speak to animals, Mistress Nystrom?*

Freya had lied, of course. Beautifully. Nine years of practice. But the lie had lodged in her like a splinter, and on the road, spending her gift openly — bargaining passage from elk, asking permission of wolves, thanking a river in words older than any kingdom — she had begun to wonder what else she'd been lying about. To whom. For how long.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Freya Nystrom had never once in her life been able to leave arithmetic alone:

Nine years of hiding the gift. Nine years of silence, of keeping it folded up small in a drawer of herself like contraband ledger paper. And in nine years of silence, she had lost — what? Nothing measurable. A dream here, a name there. Ordinary erosion. Everyone forgot things.

Then, in eleven days on the steppe, she had spoken deeply perhaps fourteen times. Deep speech — the real thing, the covenant-tongue, words given rather than used. And in eleven days she had lost: the name of her childhood street. The taste of her father's terrible plum wine. The year she'd first come to Meridian. And now her mother's face, which was not a small thing, which was not a drawer-item, which was load-bearing.

Fourteen conversations. Four losses she could count, and more she couldn't, because — and here was the truly elegant cruelty of it — you could not inventory what was already gone. A bookkeeper could only audit what remained on the shelves.

"I've been paying for it," she said to the fire that evening, "my whole life. I just didn't get the bill until now."

Ash handed her a tin cup of tea that tasted mostly of pine and optimism. *Explain the bill.*

"The gift isn't free. It never was. Every time I speak deep — really speak, give something of myself into the words — it takes memory to fuel it. Like a stove takes wood." She stared into the flames. "When I hid it, the burn was slow. Barely a candle. But out here, using it the way we need to use it—"

*You are spending yourself.*

"I am spending myself," Freya agreed, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Nine years I thought I was being careful. Frugal. Sensible Freya, who balanced every column. And the whole time I was just — deferring payment. With interest."

Ash set down a cup and regarded her with the grave, unhurried attention that was either very childish or very ancient, depending on the light. Then the child climbed into her lap as though lap-sitting were a right conferred by treaty, which, Freya was beginning to understand, it might actually be.

*Show me her,* Ash said. *Not the face. The Friday.*

So Freya held the memory up to the light of the fire and looked at it sideways, the way you looked at a watermark. Her mother, back turned, towel waving, smoke curling out the window into harbor light. The offended herring. Her father's voice mid-joke. Herself, small, on the counter, hearing everything.

"She turned around," Freya said. "I know she turned around. I know what happened next — she put flour on my nose. I can feel it, the flour, cold and soft. I just can't see—"

*Then don't look,* said Ash's hands against hers. *Listen.*

And Freya, who had spent her whole life listening to things other people couldn't hear, understood suddenly that she had never once listened to herself.

She closed her eyes. And there — beneath the missing face, beneath the white snowfall of what was gone — was the sound. The creak of the floorboard by the stove, third from the wall, that her mother's weight knew without looking. The particular rhythm of her laugh, two notes rising, caught short by the smoke, resumed. The way she said Freya's name — *Fray-ya*, the old spelling pronounced, the y doing honest work — half scold, half song.

"Oh," Freya said. The word came out wet. "Oh, she's still there. She's just — I was looking in the wrong room."

*Memories are not pictures,* Ash said, with the serene authority of someone reciting something learned before learning to walk. *That is a human mistake. You store your dead in sounds and smells and the weight of hands, same as we do. Pictures fade. Songs keep.* A pause, and then, gentler: *You have not lost her. You have lost the frame around her. She is still singing.*

Freya cried, quietly and thoroughly, into Ash's hair, which smelled of woodsmoke and salt and something green that had no business existing on the steppe. Ash permitted this with the patience of a monarch receiving tribute.

* * *

That night, the old language came to Freya in her sleep, as it had been doing since the second week — not as words exactly, but as weather. She dreamed in it the way you dreamed in rain: syllables falling soft and enormous, each one carrying a weight of meaning no dictionary could hold. In the dream she stood in a kitchen that was also a grassland that was also a harbor, and her mother was there, back turned, and the whole horizon was speaking.

*Stay,* said the horizon, in a voice like wind through glass.

*Come,* said the grassland, in her mother's two-note laugh.

*Remember,* said the kitchen floorboard, third from the wall.

She woke at grey dawn with tears already dry on her cheeks and Ash watching her from the far side of the dead fire, chin on knees, eyes knowing.

*I heard you dreaming,* Ash said. *You spoke beautifully. You argued with the moon.*

"Did I win?"

*No one wins against the moon. But you made it laugh.* Ash crawled over and pressed a small warm palm flat against Freya's sternum, over the heart, which was where the child delivered the important things. *Listen to me, Freya-of-the-ledgers. What is being taken from you is real, and it is wrong, and I am sorry. But hear this too: the tongue does not take randomly. It takes what you give. And you keep giving it the past because you think the past is what you are.*

"What else would I be?"

Ash's hand pressed firmer. *A choice,* the child said. *You are what you choose next. That is the only part of you no fire can burn, no buyer can buy, no guildmaster can price. Your mother's face is gone and I grieve it with you. But the woman who chose to falsify a ledger to hide a stranger's child — that woman is not a memory. She is happening right now. She is happening every day you keep walking.*

Freya looked at this impossible child — barefoot in frost, eight years old, exiled twice over, keeper of a language that had outlived the war that broke it — and felt something rearrange itself in her chest with an audible click, like a ledger closing square.

"When did you get wise?" she asked. "You're eight."

*Nine in the old counting,* Ash said serenely, and began packing camp with the brisk efficiency of someone who had, apparently, been managing adults for centuries.

* * *

They walked north through the morning, following the faint seam of green that marked the old covenant-road, the one the beasts remembered even when maps didn't. Freya had taken to carrying a small notebook now — a habit so deeply Guild that it survived everything, apparently, including the slow theft of her self. On its pages, in her neat bookkeeper's hand, she recorded what mattered:

*Mother: two-note laugh, rising, caught by smoke. Flour on the nose, Fridays. Floorboard third from wall. Called me Fray-ya, the y doing work. DO NOT LOSE THE LAUGH.*

*Father: plum wine, terrible, he knew it was terrible, served it anyway with pride.*

*Ash: warm hands. Bossy. Wise beyond all accounting. Laughs at larks.*

*Me: I hear things. I pay for it. I would pay again.*

She wrote the last line and then sat looking at it for a long moment, because it was true, and truth written in her own handwriting had a way of becoming binding.

It was Ash who stopped first, head tilting, the way the child did when hearing something on frequencies the rest of the world had agreed to ignore. The elk had left them yesterday; the wolves had ranged ahead; the land, which had been full of quiet company for days, had gone abruptly, attentively still.

"What is it?" Freya asked, hand going to the notebook as though paper might help.

Ash's fingers found her wrist. The feeling that arrived was not fear, exactly. It was the sensation of being watched by something that had been watching for a long time and had finally decided to stop being subtle.

*Someone is coming,* Ash said. *On a fast horse. Alone. She has been following our trail for two days, and last night she stopped pretending she hadn't found it.*

Freya's stomach dropped through the frozen ground and kept going. She thought of flat gray eyes and a voice that asked questions fairly and waited, patiently, for lies to run out of road.

"Sela," she said.

*Yes,* said Ash. And then, with the mild curiosity of a child observing weather: *She is calling your name at the wind, Freya. She has been doing it since dawn. Do you want to know what she is saying?*

Freya looked north, where the white steppe ran on forever, and at the small dark shape just now cresting the far ridge, moving fast.

"Yes," she said. "Tell me everything."
