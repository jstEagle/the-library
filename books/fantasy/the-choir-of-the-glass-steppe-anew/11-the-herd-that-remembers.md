## 11. The Herd That Remembers

Sela Brann had been chasing two fugitives and a rumor across the White Steppe for six days, and in that time she had learned three things.

First: the steppe was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. Second: her horse, a placid bay mare named Turnip who had never once shown initiative in eight years of service, had developed opinions. And third: she was not chasing them at all anymore. She was following them. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

"Left," said Turnip, when Sela reined toward the salt flats.

Sela stopped. She looked at her horse. Her horse looked back at her with one rolling brown eye, chewing, insufferable.

"You don't talk."

Turnip flicked an ear toward the north, where the wind came off the glass, and blew out a long breath through her nostrils. It was not words. Sela knew it wasn't words. But it had the shape of words, the way a footprint has the shape of a foot, and after six days alone on the white nothing, Sela's mind had begun filling in the shapes whether she wanted it to or not.

*They went left,* the breath said. *The elk went left. Everything went left.*

And everything had. That was the thing she could not put into her reports, because there would be no reports, because she had burned her authorization papers on the second night for warmth and had felt, watching them curl, like a woman burning a bridge behind her while still standing on it.

The caravans had stopped being attacked four months ago. That was why the Guild had sent her — not for the child, not at first. The border towns were frightened by the quiet. Merchants whispered that the beasts were massing. The Guildmaster had smiled his flat gray smile and said, *Go and find out why, Auditor Brann. Find out what they know.*

She had found ledgers instead. That was the trouble with auditors: you could send us anywhere, and we would find the numbers.

* * *

It had started three nights before she rode out of Meridian, in the deep archive beneath the counting house, where the old intake books went to be forgotten. Sela had gone down looking for caravan tolls from the border years — grain levies, escort fees, the ordinary arithmetic of fear. What she found was a second set of books.

Not hidden, exactly. Worse than hidden. *Filed.* Vey had hidden nothing; he had simply made sure no one ever asked. The records sat in plain order under a heading so boring that only an auditor would ever read it: *Steppe Survey Fees, Recurring.*

Recurring. For nineteen years.

She had sat on a crate of moth-eaten maps until the lamp burned low, cross-referencing, and the picture that assembled itself out of the columns was so clean, so tidy, so perfectly bookkept, that she laughed out loud in the dark and then had to sit with her head in her hands until the laughing stopped.

Every spring, the Guild sold route information to the border factions — which passes the beast herds would use, which villages were unwarded, when the caravans ran light. Every autumn, the Guild sold the same information back to the caravan masters — where the raids would fall, which escorts to hire, at what price. Both sides paid. Both sides bled. And every year the Guildmaster's ledger showed a modest, respectable profit line labeled, in Oskar Vey's own neat hand: *Conflict Stabilization Services.*

Stabilization. Nineteen years of border raids, burned granaries, orphaned children — Sela's own town had lost eleven people the winter she turned fourteen; she still remembered the names carved into the meetinghouse beam — and somewhere in a warm office above the archive, a man had entered all of it under *stabilization*, balanced the column, and gone home to his supper.

That was the night Sela Brann stopped being the Guild's auditor and became something else. She hadn't decided what yet. But she knew the direction of travel, because the child had gone north, and north was where the truth lived.

* * *

She caught sight of them on the sixth day, cresting a ridge of blue-white ice, and her heart did something complicated — relief and dread braided together, the way it always did when a chase ended and the harder part began.

There was Freya Nystrom, the ledger-keeper with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at you like she was totaling you up. Beside her, a small figure in an oversized coat, walking with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned the ground. And around them —

Sela pulled up short.

Around them, the herd.

Elk. Hundreds of them, pouring across the ridge in a slow gray river, antlers like winter branches against the pale sky, and the two small figures of woman and child moving in their midst as if this were the most natural thing in the world. As if the largest migration on the steppe had simply decided to include two pedestrians.

Turnip, traitorously, whinnied a greeting.

One elk near the front of the river turned its great head and regarded Sela with an expression she could only describe as *assessment*. Then it dipped its antlers slightly — a nod? a warning? — and the whole herd flowed on, carrying Freya and the child away over the ice like leaves on a stream.

"Oh, no you don't," Sela told them, and spurred Turnip along the flank of the herd, shouting into the wind. "Freya Nystrom! Guild business! I know you can hear me!"

Nothing. The herd flowed. Somewhere inside it, she was almost certain, someone was laughing.

* * *

She caught them at dusk, when the herd stopped to graze on lichen scraped from the underside of the ice shelf, and the two fugitives stood at the edge of the grazing ground looking tired and cornered and — in the child's case — faintly amused.

Freya had planted herself in front of Ash with her arms slightly out, which would have protected the child from precisely nothing, and said, "Auditor Brann," in the voice of a woman reading her own arrest warrant.

"Auditor Nystrom." Sela dismounted slowly, keeping her hands visible. Six days of wind had taught her that sudden movements on the steppe were read differently here — by the elk, by the land, by whatever else was listening. "You're a hard woman to catch."

"You caught me."

"I followed you. There's a difference." She took another step. An elk the size of a hay wagon chewed lichen ten feet away and watched her with one liquid eye, and Sela had the distinct feeling of being included in a conversation she couldn't hear. "I'd like to talk. Not arrest. Talk."

"People say that," Freya said. "Right before the arrest."

"Yes. Usually I'm the one saying it." Sela huffed a laugh despite herself. "You have no idea how strange this is from my side. I spent four years putting people in irons for less than what you've done this week. And now I've ridden six days through a frozen hell to do—" she gestured vaguely at the herd, the child, the entire impossible situation— "*this*. Whatever this is."

"What is this?" Freya asked. "Why are you here?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Sela looked at the child — Ash, the files called the child nothing at all, just *unregistered arrival, disposition pending* — standing there barefoot in the snow as if cold were a rumor from another country. She thought about the meetinghouse beam with eleven names on it. She thought about a ledger line that read *Conflict Stabilization Services* in a tidy hand.

She thought about the fact that she had burned her own papers, and what that made her.

"My family's farm was two miles from the border," she said. "When I was fourteen, the raiders came in the winter — beast-driven, everyone said. Beast-led. We lost eleven people. I knew all of them. Old Marta who taught me letters. The Ferro twins." She kept her voice level, auditor-flat, because if she didn't it would crack. "I joined the Guild because I wanted the world to make sense again. Numbers make sense. Columns balance or they don't. Nobody lies to a well-kept ledger."

Freya's face did something complicated at that.

"And then," Sela went on, "four months ago the attacks stop. Completely. And the Guild sends me to find out why, and I go looking through the records, and I find—" She reached into her coat and drew out a folded sheaf of copied pages, transcribed in her own careful hand, because she had not dared take the originals. "I find nineteen years of this."

She held them out. Freya took them the way a person takes a snake, and read, and Sela watched the color leave her face line by line as the columns did their work. Auditors and bookkeepers spoke the same language; it was the cruelest language in the world, because it could not lie.

"*Stabilization services*," Freya read aloud, very quietly.

"He sold both sides," Sela said. "Routes, timings, ward schedules. Kept the war alive at exactly the size that turned a profit. Your border raids — mine — the ones the beasts supposedly led—" She stopped. Started again. "Nystrom, the beasts haven't attacked a caravan in four months. Since the child arrived. Which means either they stopped, or they were never the ones doing it, or—" she spread her hands— "or the whole war is a product, and somebody's been managing supply."

The wind moved across the ice. Behind Freya, the child Ash tilted their head, listening to something in it, and then looked directly at Sela with eyes too old for the face, and said — not aloud, Sela would swear to her dying day it wasn't aloud, it arrived behind her ears like a memory of a voice:

*You are angry in the correct direction.*

Sela staggered half a step. Turnip snorted.

"It does that," Freya said, and there was, unbelievably, a thread of gentle humor in her exhaustion. "You get used to it. You don't, actually. But people say you do."

"The child just talked into my head."

"The child talks into everyone's head. The horses love it. The rats are ambivalent." Freya folded the copied pages with hands that were not quite steady and tucked them inside her coat, next to her heart, which Sela suspected was exactly where they'd stay. "These are copies."

"I burned my originals. For warmth." Sela shrugged. "Seemed symbolic at the time."

"And your Guild authorization?"

"Burned those too. Also for warmth. Also symbolic." She met Freya's eyes. "I'm not here for the Guild, Nystrom. The Guild is a building in Meridian full of men who balance columns. I used to think that was the same thing as the truth. It isn't. I've spent my whole life being loyal to the wrong version of that sentence."

Ash tugged Freya's sleeve and murmured something too soft for Sela to catch. Freya listened, nodded, and translated: "Ash says you smell like burnt paper and honest anger. Apparently that's a good combination."

"It's the only combination I have."

Another tug, another murmur. Freya's mouth twitched. "Ash also says you've been calling our names at the wind since dawn, and that your pronunciation is terrible."

"My pronunciation—" Sela threw up her hands. "I've been *shouting*. Across a glacier. At fugitives. Forgive me if my diction suffered."

Behind Freya, the child laughed — a real laugh, bright and startling, the first sound Sela had heard Ash make with an actual mouth, and it did something to the air, and several nearby elk lifted their heads as if a bell had rung somewhere pleasant.

* * *

They walked with the herd as the light went long and gold across the ice, and Sela told them the rest of it: the buyer contacts in Vey's private correspondence, the remnant factions of the old Glass War who paid for steppe secrets, the names of the hunters Vey had hired — three of them, professionals, already a week ahead of anyone's comfort. In return, Freya told her about the thirteenth moon, the failing wards, the covenant that needed a voice. Sela listened the way she read ledgers: completely, asking questions only where the column didn't balance.

"So let me summarize," she said finally, as the first stars pricked through the violet sky. "In a few weeks, every ward on the steppe fails unless someone speaks an ancient covenant in a dead language. The Guildmaster wants to sell the only person who speaks it to the people his own profiteering helped create. And the plan is to walk into the middle of the steppe, past hired killers, with a child, a bookkeeper, and a disgraced auditor, and renew a peace treaty older than any kingdom."

"That is roughly the plan," Freya said.

"We're missing several things."

"We're missing almost everything. But we have a herd." Freya gestured at the elk, who had settled around their little camp in a loose ring, backs to the wind, and Sela realized with a start that the animals had arranged themselves as a wall without anyone asking them to. "And as of tonight," Freya added, glancing at her sideways, "we have an auditor."

"Not an auditor." Sela unbuckled her Guild badge — brass, heavy, worn smooth by four years at her breast — and weighed it in her palm. She thought about throwing it onto the ice. Dramatic. Final. Very the sort of thing people did in ballads.

Instead she tucked it into her saddlebag, because brass was brass and they might need to trade it, and because she was done with gestures that cost nothing.

"An accountant," she said. "The worst kind. One who knows where all the bodies are buried, because she filed the burial expenses."

Ash said something soft and delighted. Freya translated, and her voice cracked halfway through on something between laughter and grief: "Ash says the herd remembers you now. Says you walk like you mean to arrive."

Sela looked out at the white dark, where three hired killers waited somewhere ahead of them, and a city of lies behind, and a moon thinning overhead toward its final turn.

"Good," she said. "Because I do."

Far off to the north, at the very edge of hearing, the ice sang — one long glass note, like a finger run round the rim of a cup — and every elk in the herd raised its head at once, and Ash stood up, barefoot in the snow, and said, aloud, in a voice like a small bell answering:

*"They're singing first."*
