## 12. Wardstone Village

The village announced itself three days before we reached it.

The elk told me first—or rather, the elk stopped telling me anything at all. For two hundred miles they had been a murmuring parliament around us, offering directions in that wordless way of theirs, pointing out frozen streams with a toss of antlers, complaining about the salt wind like old men complaining about weather. Then, on the morning the horizon grew a smudge of smoke, they went quiet and began walking in a wide, respectful arc, the way you might walk around a sleeping bear.

*What is it?* I asked, in the deep speech that now cost me something every time I used it. I had stopped asking for small things. I asked only when it mattered, and each question took its tithe—a face, a name, the taste of some particular winter. I was becoming a book with pages torn out, and I had learned to read carefully what remained.

The eldest elk answered with an image: stone, deep under the earth, and something coiled around the stone like ivy around a pillar. Then a feeling I could only translate as *old courtesy*. The beasts were not afraid of the village. They were *minding their manners*.

"They do that," Sela said, when I reported it. She walked with her hand resting on Ash's shoulder, which had become her habit, though Ash needed no guiding. The child navigated the white steppe better than either of us, barefoot in snow, head tilted as if listening to something under the ground. "Manners. My grandmother said the herds used to bow to Wardstone Village. Before my time."

"Your grandmother was from here?"

"Two valleys over." Sela's mouth did the thing it did when she mentioned family—closed briefly, like a ledger being shut. "She claimed the village sat on the back of a sleeping god. She also claimed the god snored, and that's why the ground hums at night."

"The ground does hum at night," I said.

"My grandmother was right about most things," Sela said, "which is why nobody liked her."

Ash looked up at us then and spoke—not the old language, but the handful of trade-tongue words the child had been hoarding like a magpie hoards buttons. "*Stone. Wake. Soon.*"

I felt those words settle into my chest like stones dropped into a well. *Soon.*

* * *

Wardstone Village was not what I expected, which by then should not have surprised me, but did anyway.

I had imagined something grim—black timber, shuttered windows, people who whispered. Instead we crested the last ridge and found a place that seemed to have grown rather than been built: houses of pale stone and turf roofs, arranged in no pattern I could read, spiraling outward from a central hill the way ripples spiral from a dropped pebble. Smoke rose cheerfully from a dozen chimneys. Goats regarded us from rooftops with the supreme indifference of goats everywhere. A woman was beating a rug off a balcony and paused to watch us come down the road, then resumed beating it, having apparently decided we were not worth interrupting her work.

"You're staring," Sela murmured.

"I'm trying to find the war," I said. "We've walked out of one. I'm looking for where it is here."

"There isn't one," Sela said slowly, as if testing the idea for rot and finding none. "Look at the fences. No watchtowers. No burned fields. Freya, this village hasn't fortified itself against anything in—" She stopped. "In however long the wards have held."

That was the thing about the Steppe's wards, the ones everyone said were failing, the ones the thirteenth moon would finish off. Where they still held, they didn't hold against armies. They held against *forgetting*. And inside them, apparently, people beat rugs and raised goats and let their fences lean, because there had been nothing to fear for so long that fear itself had gone dormant, like the stone under the hill.

An old man met us at the first gate. He was small and round and wrapped in so many layers of wool he resembled a friendly boulder himself, and he looked at Ash for a long moment before he looked at us.

"You'll be the ones the birds wrote ahead about," he said.

"The birds wrote," I repeated.

"We keep a rookery. They see everything on the white." He beamed at Ash, who stared back with the flat, unblinking assessment children reserve for new adults. "I'm Tammas. I'm the mayor, the miller, and the choir master, in descending order of competence. You'll want soup. Everyone wants soup. The question is whether you want soup before or after you meet her."

"*Her,*" I said.

"The Warden." He said it the way you'd say *the weather*—vast, unavoidable, best discussed practically. "She's been asking after your child since the rooks came through. Well. Asking is a strong word. She's been *humming* in a key that makes my teeth ache, which among our people counts as conversation." He leaned toward me conspiratorially. "Between us, I'd take the soup first. Meetings go worse hungry. That's not wisdom, that's just experience."

It was the first time in weeks anyone had offered me something without wanting a price named later, and I nearly wept over barley broth in a stranger's kitchen. It was good soup. There were dumplings in it. Ash ate four and fell asleep sitting up against Sela's arm, mid-dumpling, which Sela declared was the highest compliment a traveler can pay a cook, and Tammas wrote it down in a little book, because apparently everything in Wardstone Village got written down somewhere.

* * *

They took us up the hill at dusk.

The hill's crown was bare rock—no, not rock. Glass. Old glass, fused smooth, green-black and shot through with veins of light that pulsed slow as a sleeper's breath. Houses ringed its base, and doors opened as we passed, families stepping out quietly, not to gawk but to *witness*, the way you attend a birth or a funeral. Nobody spoke. Somewhere below, someone had begun to sing—a low, wordless line, and other voices joined it, until the whole village was humming, and I realized with a chill that the humming was not for us.

It was a lullaby. They sang the hill to sleep. Or perhaps they sang to keep it dreaming gently.

At the summit, the glass opened into a hollow, and in the hollow—

I don't know what I expected. Something monstrous, probably. What I saw was a shape like a whale made of granite, if a whale had decided to become a mountain and had mostly succeeded. Its flanks were grown over with centuries of moss and lichen; its eye, when it opened, was a pool of amber light the size of a cart wheel, and it was kind. That was the thing that undid me. After everything—the Guild's ledgers, Vey's tea, the hunters' crossbows—I had forgotten that enormous things could be gentle.

The Warden looked at Ash.

And Ash looked at the Warden, and spoke.

I will spend whatever words I have left trying to describe what happened next, and I will fail. The child's voice in the old language had always sounded like water over stones, like wind finding a crack in a wall. But here, above the wardstone, with the village humming below, the language landed. The glass lit beneath our feet. The veins of light in the Warden's hide flared gold. Every hair on my arms stood up, and Sela made a small sound beside me, and the elk far down in the valley all turned their heads at once, I learned later, facing the hill.

The Warden answered.

Not aloud. The voice arrived inside the skull, vast and slow, like geology learning to talk: ***Little speaker. Little flame. You carry the tongue true.*** The amber eye slid to me. ***But you—you are a strange knot. One who hears. One who has spent years not-hearing. Come closer, knot. Let me look at what you have tied yourself into.***

"I'm fine here," I said, to a mountain.

***Come closer,*** said the mountain, with infinite patience, ***or I will show your child what you looked like at six years old, stealing honey from a windowsill, and I assure you it is undignified.***

"That's not possible," I said. "You've never seen me."

***No,*** the Warden agreed, with what I swear was humor. ***But I know how to make a good guess, and your face is doing it now.***

Sela made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. I walked closer.

* * *

The Warden touched me with one thought, and the world folded.

I stood on the Steppe, but the Steppe of long ago—green to every horizon, rivers braiding silver through grass tall enough to hide a horse. And the plain was full. Not of armies. Of *voices*. Humans and beasts together, and between them figures I understood without being told were Speakers—men and women whose throats glowed faintly when they talked, carrying meaning both ways, translating not just words but *selves*. A woman argued amiably with a bear about the boundaries of a berry patch. A boy rode a wolf the way another boy might ride with an older brother. Elk pulled plows beside farmers, and I heard—this was the part that broke something open in me—I heard them *joking*. Beast-speech, human laughter, the same joke landing in both languages.

This was the covenant. Not a treaty signed and shelved. A *conversation*, kept going, generation after generation, the way you keep a fire.

Then the vision turned, as visions do.

I saw the drought come—years of it, grass browning, rivers thinning. I saw fear do what fear does. Human lords decided the beasts were eating grain that belonged to mouths. Beast elders decided humans were damming rivers that belonged to herds. Both were half-true, which is the most dangerous kind of true. I watched Speakers stand in market squares pleading for patience and get shouted down—by their own people. I watched a beast-matriarch counsel restraint and get challenged by younger bulls drunk on grievance. And I saw the night it ended: not one betrayal but a hundred small ones, compounding, each side certain it was only answering the other. The first spear. The first fire. The Speakers' throats cut or silenced—both sides again, each killing its own translators first, because a war cannot proceed while anyone is still explaining the enemy.

The last Speaker I saw was a woman on this very hill, holding a child, singing the covenant's terms into the wardstone even as the glass cracked around her. *Remember the terms. Remember the terms.* And the stone remembered. The stone held the words the way a bank holds a deposit no one has come to claim.

The vision released me, and I was on my knees on cold glass, and I was crying, and I couldn't have told you at whom.

Because here is what I had carried out of the archives, out of the rats' memories, out of every mile of the white: a story where humans broke the covenant and beasts were its wronged guardians. It was a clean story. Clean stories are comfortable. The truth the Warden poured into me was that the breaking had been a duet. Fear on both sides. Grievance on both sides. And on both sides, the ones who could have translated—the ones who *heard*—had stayed quiet. Had hidden behind ledgers, behind caution, behind the respectable silence of people who know something is wrong and calculate that speaking will cost them more than it costs others.

People like me.

***Now you see it,*** the Warden said, gentler than any judgment has a right to be. ***I do not show you this to shame you, hearer. Shame is a stone; it only sits. I show you because your child cannot renew the covenant alone. A child did not break it. Adults broke it, and adults must speak it mended. When the thirteenth moon comes, someone with a voice and a history must stand on the heart-stone and give the words weight. Ash carries the tongue. But the tongue needs a throat that knows what silence costs.***

"And mine does," I said. My voice came out rough. "Mine knows exactly what it costs."

***Yes,*** said the Warden. ***That is why I am sorry.***

Below us, the village hummed on. Ash climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do, and pressed one small hand flat over my heart, and said nothing at all, which in the old language, I was beginning to understand, is sometimes the longest sentence there is.

* * *

We stayed three nights in Wardstone Village, because Tammas insisted and because the Warden ordered rest the way weather orders rain. Ash slept twelve hours a night and ate everything not nailed down and taught the miller's daughter a beast-song about a vain crow, which spread through the schoolhouse in two days like gossip. Sela fixed a gate hinge, reorganized someone's grain ledger—she couldn't help herself—and sat with the rook-master for a whole evening, laughing at his stories about border towns she'd patrolled, and came away looking lighter, as if she'd set down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing.

On the third evening, the Warden called me up the hill alone.

***The heart-stone lies nine days east,*** it said. ***Nine days, if the weather holds and nothing hunts you. The moon will complete in eleven. You will need every hour, and I will tell you plainly: the road east passes the salt mirrors, and the salt mirrors are where men with contracts go to wait. Your Guildmaster's hunters are not behind you anymore, hearer. As of yesterday's rooks, they are ahead of you.***

My stomach dropped through the glass and kept falling. "How many?"

***Enough,*** said the Warden, which was the answer I feared most, because it meant the number was bad enough to be vague. ***Go at dawn. Take the valley route, not the flats. And hearer—one more thing.*** The amber eye dimmed, brightened, like a slow blink. ***When you stand before the heart-stone and the time comes to pay, do not try to pay less than everything. Partial payments bounce. I have seen it. It is very sad, and very loud.***

"Was that a joke?"

***Everything I say is a joke,*** said the Warden, closing its eye. ***That is what ten thousand years teaches you. Now go. Dawn comes whether you are ready or not, and in my experience, dawn prefers it that way.***

I walked down the hill past houses with their lamps lit, past the sound of the miller's daughter practicing the crow song badly and joyfully, and found Sela sharpening her knife by the fire with the grim economy of a woman who had already heard the news from the rooks.

"Dawn, then," she said.

"Dawn," I said. And above us, faint and patient, the ground began to hum.
