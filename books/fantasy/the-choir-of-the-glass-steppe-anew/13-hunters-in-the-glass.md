## 13. Hunters in the Glass

The salt flats began at dawn, and Freya knew they were being followed before she saw anyone, because the crickets told her.

*They ride heavy,* said a cricket somewhere beneath a crust of white, in the bored tone of a creature who had seen many riders and cared about none of them. *Six horses. Two of them are limping. One man keeps swearing at his boots.*

"Thank you," Freya murmured.

*Don't mention it. Literally. Don't tell anyone I talk to you. It's undignified.*

She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the salt, feeling the faint tremor of hooves still too far away to hear with human ears. Behind her, Sela was tightening the girth on their one remaining mule with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had decided that if doom was coming, it would find her organized.

"How far?" Sela asked.

"Half a day. Maybe less." Freya stood, brushing salt from her knees. "Six riders. Two lame horses. One man with bad boots."

Sela blinked. "You got all of that from—"

"The crickets are very chatty this time of year."

"I'm going to need you to stop saying things like that as though they're normal."

"They *are* normal. That's rather the whole tragedy of my life." Freya tried to make it a joke, and mostly succeeded, which surprised her. There had been a time—not so long ago, though already the edges of it were softening—when speaking aloud to anything with more than two legs would have made her sick with fear. Now it felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

What she did not say, what she could not quite bring herself to examine, was the small list of things that had gone missing since the elk crossing. The name of the village where she'd bought her boots. The taste of the tea Vey used to serve. Small things. Pebbles from the shore of herself. She kept telling herself the tide would bring them back.

Ash stood a little apart, barefoot as always on the salt, toes curled into the crust as though reading it. The child's head tilted, listening to something below even cricket-range.

"Ash?" Freya asked. "What do you hear?"

Ash's answer came in that old syllabic language that no longer frightened Freya but lived in her dreams now like a second heartbeat. She caught only fragments: *glass. Under. Old. Waiting.*

"The wardstone plain is close," Freya translated slowly. "And something under it is... awake?"

Ash nodded once, gravely, then pointed east—toward the heart of the Steppe—and then, with terrible simplicity, turned and pointed back west, toward the riders.

Toward them first.

* * *

Sela Brann had been a soldier before she was an auditor, and being an auditor had only taught her that most battles were lost in the paperwork beforehand. So while Freya argued with a mule and Ash communed with the ground, Sela did what she did best: she took inventory.

One mule. Three days of hardtack, two of which were arguably a form of punishment. A waterskin with a slow leak she'd been meaning to fix for a week. Her Guild-issue short sword, which she had technically stolen from the Guild, which meant that by the strictest accounting she was now a thief, a deserter, and a traitor, in ascending order of severity.

Her mother would have had opinions.

"Freya," she called. "If we run, they run us down on open salt. If we stand, six against three ends exactly how arithmetic says it ends. And if we hide—" she gestured at the horizon, which offered nothing whatsoever in the way of hiding, "—I welcome suggestions."

"There's glass," Freya said quietly.

Sela followed her gaze. Out past the white flats, the land changed. The salt there had fused long ago—in the war, the histories said, though Freya's face whenever the war came up suggested the histories were polite fictions—into sheets of pale mirrored glass, tilted and buckled like a frozen sea. Light went into it and came out strange. Travelers' tales said people had gotten lost out there for days, chasing their own reflections.

"It'll cut the horses' feet to ribbons," Sela said.

"And it'll cut ours. But we know where we're going and they don't, and out there, everything looks like everything else." Freya was already repacking the satchel, her bookkeeper's hands moving with quick economy. "Also, I have an idea about the reflections, but I want to check with someone first."

"With whom? There's nothing alive out there."

"That," said Freya, "is what everyone always assumes about the places I end up."

She knelt, laid both palms on the boundary where salt met glass, and listened. Sela watched her go still in that particular way she had—the way of a woman receiving a letter dictated by the entire world.

When Freya looked up, she was smiling, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"The glass remembers," she said. "It's been waiting a very long time for someone to ask it a favor."

* * *

Vey's hunters were not soldiers. They were something worse: professionals.

Their leader was a broad, gray-templed woman named Maret Kolb who had once, briefly, been in the same mercenary company as Sela Brann, and who thought of that period of her life the way one thinks of a mild illness. She rode at the front of her five companions across the blinding white flats and permitted herself the day's first honest emotion, which was irritation.

The tracker—a nervous young man whose name she refused to learn until he stopped flinching at loud noises—reined up beside her. "Sign's fresh. They're heading for the glass."

"Of course they are." Maret squinted at the shimmering expanse ahead, where the world doubled and tripled itself dishonestly. "The bookkeeper thinks she's clever."

"She *is* clever," said the tracker. "The ledgers alone—"

"I've read the ledgers. Cleverness on paper is still paper." But Maret said it without conviction, because she had also read the reports from Wardstone Village, where a stone guardian older than kingdoms had tested the woman and let her pass, and Maret Kolb did not believe in stone guardians and was deeply annoyed to be employed in a job that required it.

"The Guildmaster wants the child alive," said the big one, Doran, spitting into the salt. "Alive and unmarked. And the woman?"

"Alive," Maret said. "Mostly."

"And the auditor?"

Maret was quiet a moment. She had liked Sela Brann, once, over cards. "Brann made her choices."

Ahead, at the edge of the glass, something flickered. Shapes—three shapes, maybe four, moving among the tilted mirrors, here and gone, here and gone, reflected and re-reflected until there might have been thirty of them or three.

Doran laughed uneasily. "Which one's real?"

"All of them," Maret said grimly, and drew her blade. "That's the problem."

* * *

It worked beautifully for almost an hour.

The glass did exactly what Freya had promised: it multiplied them. Every step scattered ten ghosts; every ghost cast ten more. The hunters' horses screamed and balked at the mirror-fields, and twice a rider went down hard when his mount stepped on a sheet that gave way like rotten ice. From somewhere in the maze, Ash's voice rang out in the old language, and the glass itself seemed to answer—groaning, shifting, opening passages behind them and closing others, so that the hunters chased echoes through a labyrinth that rearranged itself around its guests.

Freya ran beside Ash, hand gripping the child's shoulder, laughing despite everything—a wild, breathless laugh she didn't recognize as her own.

"They can't pick us out! Ash, they can't—"

Then the glass sang a warning, and Freya, who had spent weeks learning to hear warnings in everything, heard this one too late.

A crossbow bolt came out of the glare—from a hunter who had simply stopped chasing ghosts and started shooting at *all* of them, letting probability do his work. It missed Freya by a hand's width and shattered a mirror-pillar beside her into a bloom of glittering shrapnel.

And the glass, wounded, forgot its manners.

The maze collapsed. Not everywhere—just enough. Just one corridor of clear sight, one straight line through the mirrors, and at the end of it, Maret Kolb on a horse that had finally found footing, and between Maret and the horizon, one small barefoot child.

"No!" Freya lunged, and the world performed its oldest trick: she arrived precisely one moment after history needed her.

Two hunters swept in from the flank where the glass had failed. Ash did not scream. That was the worst of it, Freya thought later, in the white silence afterward—that the child looked at her, and raised one small hand, and spoke three syllables of the old tongue with perfect calm, as though delivering instructions.

*Go. Heart. Finish.*

Then the hunters closed around the child like water closing over a stone, and Ash was gone, and the glass held nothing but light.

* * *

Sela fought the way she did everything: thoroughly, and while mentally itemizing.

She put Doran off his horse with a salt-crusted tent pole, which she felt was fitting. She disarmed a younger hunter by informing him, truthfully, that his bootlaces were undone, and taking advantage of the half-second of pure human nature that followed. She was doing reasonably well, all things considered, right up until the flat of a blade caught her temple and introduced her face to the salt.

She woke to silence, a mouth full of white grit, and the sound of Freya screaming a name into the empty mirrors.

Not her name.

* * *

They found each other by sound, the way the blind do. Sela, bleeding and swaying, followed the voice through the broken maze until the mirrors opened onto a shelf of salt where Freya knelt, hands pressed to the glass, calling and calling.

"Ash. *Ash.* They went north—no—east—the tracks—Sela, I can't read the tracks, the wind—"

"Freya."

"—I had the child's shoulder, I had it, one moment, one *moment*, and I—"

"*Freya.*"

Freya turned, and Sela saw it happen. Saw the recognition arrive, and falter, and arrive again, like a lamp guttering in wind.

"You're hurt," Freya said. "Your head. Sit down before you fall down."

"You're bleeding too." Sela touched her own scalp gingerly. "From where?"

Freya reached up—and stopped. Her fingers found the sticky matting above her ear, the gash she had not noticed, and her expression did something complicated and terrible. It was the look of a woman opening a ledger and finding a page torn out.

"Sela," she said carefully. "How long have we been traveling together?"

"...Nine days. Since the river."

"Nine days." Freya repeated it the way she used to repeat figures that wouldn't balance. "And before that. The village. The stone guardian. What did it show me? I remember it showed me something. I remember that I *know* it showed me something, the way you know a room you've slept in. But the room is—" Her breath hitched. "Sela, what year did my mother die?"

"What?"

"I should know that. I know I should know that. I can see her hands—I can see her hands kneading bread, I can see the scar on her thumb—but her *face*, and the year, and—" Freya's voice climbed, cracked, steadied itself with visible effort, the bookkeeper in her dragging the panic back into columns. "The blow. The bolt hit the pillar, the glass—it took a payment. Everything costs, Sela, I've known that since the elk, I just never thought it would take a whole—"

She stopped. Looked at her own hands as though checking them against an inventory.

"My name," she said quietly. "Is Freya Nystrom. I am a beast-speaker. I worked eleven years in the Meridian Guild warehouse. I falsified an intake ledger to hide a child who speaks the covenant-tongue. We are going to the heart of the Steppe because the thirteenth moon completes in—" she frowned, counted on her fingers, and Sela watched her count twice, "—in some number of days I will tell you as soon as I finish counting. Everything else is..." She made a small, helpless gesture at the endless white. "Everything else is missing."

Sela sat down heavily beside her on the salt. Somewhere far off, a cricket resumed its commentary, and for once had nothing useful to add.

"Then we rebuild," Sela said. "Page by page. That's what you're good at, isn't it? Ledgers?"

Something flickered at the corner of Freya's mouth—not quite a smile, but the foundation of one. "I was told I was insufferable about ledgers."

"You are. Were. Will be." Sela pulled the waterskin from her pack, watched it dribble through the crack she'd never fixed, and laughed—a short, ragged, honest sound. "We'll get her back, Freya. Vey wants the child delivered to buyers at the Steppe's edge. That means roads. Roads mean sign. Sign means I can track them, and I tracked men through the border wars for six years."

Freya stared north, where the glass gave way to the long pale grass, and where, hours ago, a child had spoken three calm syllables.

*Go. Heart. Finish.*

"Then we follow," Freya said. She rose, unsteady, and pressed her palm one last time to the broken mirror. Beneath her hand, faint as a pulse under ice, the glass whispered—and though most of her past lay somewhere behind her in the shards, the gift remained, patient as ever, translating:

*The child lives. The child sings. The ones who carry it grow afraid of what it teaches them.*

Freya lifted her hand. Her eyes, when she turned to Sela, were wet and clear and utterly certain.

"Well then," she said. "Let's give them something to be afraid of."
