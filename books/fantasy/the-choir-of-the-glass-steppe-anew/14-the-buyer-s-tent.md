## 14. The Buyer's Tent

The cage smells of old iron and older grief.

Ash knows this the way Ash knows most things now—not in words first, but in the body. The iron has been wept on. Many times. By many people. The rust carries salt that did not come from any sea.

*Grief-salt. Old. Layered like snow.*

The cage is not a cruel cage. That is the strange part, the part that keeps catching in Ash's chest like a burr. There is straw, clean and deep. There is a wool blanket that smells of lavender and someone's careful washing. There is a clay cup of water changed twice a day by hands that are always gentle and always trembling.

But it is a cage.

Outside the cage is a tent, and outside the tent is a camp, and beyond the camp the white steppe stretches away under a sky so wide it makes Ash's eyes ache to hold it. Somewhere out there, Freya is coming. Ash does not know how Ash knows this. It is simply a fact, solid as a stone held in the palm: *the one who hears will come.*

Until then, there is the tent full of impossible things.

* * *

The buyers keep their relics arranged like a market, which tells Ash something important before anyone says a word. These are people who have lost everything once already. People who have lost everything learn to organize what remains.

There are shelves along the tent walls, and on the shelves:

A bell made of horn, cracked down one side. When the wind moves the tent flap, the clapper stirs but cannot ring. *A bell that almost rings,* Ash thinks, and feels the ache of it in the teeth.

A child's shoe, very small, woven from grass gone silver with age. Someone has kept it oiled. Someone has kept it for a long time.

A jar of earth. Just earth—dark, rich, nothing like the white salt outside—but the jar is sealed with wax pressed with a thumbprint, and the thumbprint is worn nearly smooth from being touched.

And in the center of the tent, on a stand of black wood, a stone the size of a loaf of bread. Grey-green, veined with light that moves when no one is looking at it directly. The beasts' word for such stones lives in Ash's blood even though Ash has never seen one awake: *heartseed.* A splinter of a wardstone. A splinter of home.

At night, when the camp goes quiet, the heartseed hums.

It hums in the old language.

It hums things like *cold* and *long* and *where-are-the-hands-that-carved-us*, and Ash lies in the straw and hums back, small as a cricket, until the stone settles into something like sleep.

* * *

The buyers come to look at Ash the way people look at relics, too. That is the worst of it. They come in ones and twos, or in solemn family groups, and they stand at the bars of the cage, and they do not gawk—they *revere*. An old woman presses her knuckles to her lips. A man removes his hat and holds it against his chest like a wound.

They speak a dialect Ash half-understands through feeling rather than grammar. Words shaped around loss. Words like *before* and *taken* and *the green years*. Their language is the old tongue wearing human clothes, frayed at every seam, patched with border-speech and Guild-trade words. But underneath the patches, the bones are the bones of the language Ash breathes.

On the fourth day, the old woman comes alone.

She brings bread. She sets it through the bars on a wooden slide, and then she does not leave, as the others do. She sits down on a stool on the other side of the iron, folds her hands, and looks at Ash with eyes the color of weak tea.

"You're smaller than the stories," she says.

Ash considers this. "Stories make everything bigger," Ash answers—in her language, her real language, the one that makes the tent poles shiver faintly and the heartseed flare green.

The old woman does not flinch. Her breath catches, yes—the way a person catches when a door they thought locked swings open—but she does not flinch. She answers in the same tongue, halting, accented, each word set down carefully like a foot on ice.

"I learned from my grandmother. She learned from hers. We were told—" She stops. Starts again. "We were told if we kept the words alive, someone would come who could hear them."

"How long?"

"Nine generations."

Ash sits with that. Nine generations of grandmothers teaching children a dead language for a homeland none of them had ever seen, in case—*in case*—someday a barefoot child walked out of a salt storm and the words woke up.

"That seems," Ash says slowly, "like a very heavy thing to hand a child."

The old woman laughs. It startles them both. It is a rusty sound, a bell-cracked sound, and she wipes her eyes afterward and says, "Yes. Yes, it is. My grandmother cried when she taught me. I thought she was crying about the war. She was crying because I got the word for *river* wrong and she couldn't bear to correct me."

"What is the word for river?" Ash asks.

The old woman says it wrong.

Ash teaches it right. And the old woman says it again, correctly this time, and puts both hands over her face, and cries the way people cry when a bone that has been set crooked for nine generations finally, painfully, clicks true.

* * *

After that, they come more freely. Not all of them—some still only stare, and one man with a burn-scarred jaw refuses to enter the tent at all, watching Ash through the gap in the flap with eyes like a hawk that has been shot at too many times. But many come, and they bring their words like offerings, and Ash begins to understand what Vey sold them.

Not a relic. A promise.

"They believe you can give it back," says a young man named Torv, who brings Ash honey-cakes and sits cross-legged by the bars for hours. He is perhaps nineteen, all elbows and earnestness, with his grandmother's accent and his grandfather's anger. "The homeland. The green years. Before the breaking." He gestures at the shelves, the little shoe, the jar of earth. "Everything here is from before. We've spent everything since just keeping the *before* alive."

"And after?" Ash asks. "What happens after you get it back?"

Torv blinks. "After?"

"After you have the rivers and the grass and the wardstones singing. After. What do you *do*?"

He opens his mouth. Closes it. Looks genuinely troubled, the way a boy looks when asked what he'll do with his life and realizes he has only ever planned the getting. "I suppose... farm it," he offers weakly.

Ash laughs into the straw. Even caged, laughing feels good. "You've waited nine generations to farm."

"We're very patient farmers," Torv says with dignity, and then ruins it by grinning, and then ruins *that* by going serious again, leaning close to the bars. "Is it true? Can you do it? Open the wards?"

The question hangs there, and Ash feels its weight—not just Torv's hope but the whole camp's, pressing through the canvas walls like weather. Hundreds of people. Descendants of exiles. Families that fled east when the covenant broke, carrying bells that couldn't ring and shoes their great-great-grandmothers wore, believing for two hundred years that the world owed them a debt.

And here is the terrible thing, the thing Ash turns over in the dark hours while the heartseed hums its lonely hum:

*They are not wrong.*

The wards were broken. The land was taken—taken in fury, in fear, in the chaos of a war where both sides broke faith first, or second, or third; the guardian at Wardstone Village had shown Freya that much, and Freya had told Ash in dreams, in the old language, in pictures: *both sides, little spark. Both sides broke it. Both sides paid.*

These people's grandparents had paid with exile. The beasts had paid with silence. The steppe itself had paid with glass.

Nobody in this camp is evil. That would be easier. Evil can be fought. What these people are is *hungry*—hungry for a place in the world, hungry enough that a Guildmaster with soft eyes and soft voice could sell them a child and call it justice.

"They think you're a key," Torv says.

"I'm a person," Ash says.

"A key-person," Torv tries.

"A *person*," Ash insists, and flicks a honey-cake crumb at him through the bars, and he laughs, and somewhere outside a guard shifts uncomfortably, having heard laughter from the cage and not knowing what to do with it.

* * *

The scar-jawed man finally comes on the eighth night.

He does not bring bread or honey-cakes. He brings the little grass shoe, and he holds it in both hands, and he stands at the bars without speaking for so long that Ash begins to count his breaths to stay awake.

Finally: "My daughter's feet would have fit this."

Ash waits. Some words need room.

"She died in the crossing. Fever, on the marsh road, the winter my grandmother died too. Three funerals in one year." His voice is flat, sanded smooth by retelling. "I joined the border raids after that. I killed beasts. Good ones. Ones who were probably somebody's friend." He looks up, and his eyes are wet and furious. "So don't sing your little songs at me, child. Don't tell me the beasts suffered too. I know what suffering sounds like. I *made* some of it."

The tent is very quiet. The heartseed's glow dims, listening.

Ash could say many things. Ash has many words now, gathered from grandmothers and farmers and dream-teachings from Freya. But instead Ash does the simplest thing: reaches through the bars, palm open, and waits.

The man stares at the offered hand. His jaw works under the scar.

"The word for river," Ash says gently. "Your grandmother's grandmother would have known it. Do you want to learn it? Or do you want to stay angry at a child in a cage, because angry is easier than grieving?"

For a moment Ash thinks he will strike the bars, or spit, or walk out forever.

Instead he sits down heavily on the stool, sets the little shoe on his knee, and says, in a voice like a door opening on a house long shut: "Teach me the word for river."

Ash does.

And when he leaves, near midnight, he takes the shoe with him—but differently than he brought it. Lighter. As though for nine generations his family had been carrying a stone, and tonight someone had shown him it was a seed.

* * *

On the ninth day, Ash speaks to all of them.

It happens without planning, the way true things often do. The whole camp has gathered for evening meal, fires burning low against the vast cold dusk, and someone—it might be the old woman, it might be Torv, it might be everyone at once—asks the question again: *Can you open the wards? Can you give us back the green years?*

And Ash stands at the bars of the cage, which has been carried out to the fire's edge so the child can "breathe proper air," and Ash looks at all those faces—hungry faces, hopeful faces, the scar-jawed man standing at the back with his arms crossed and his eyes open—and Ash speaks in the old tongue, loud enough that the horses at the picket lines lift their heads and the heartseed in the tent flares like a green star.

"I am not a key," Ash says. "I am a door. And doors do not decide who walks through them—that is the work of the people on both sides of the threshold."

Murmurs. Confusion. The old woman translates in a low voice for those whose old tongue has worn thin.

"Your grandmothers kept the words alive. That was holy work, and I will not shame it. But hear me: the beasts also kept something alive. Their silence. Two hundred years of not attacking, not avenging, not finishing what the war started. You call it a truce. They call it mourning." Ash's voice cracks, and Ash lets it. "Both sides paid. Both sides kept faith in the dark. And when the thirteenth moon closes, the wards fail—not for you, not for them, for *everyone*. The choice is not whether the old land wakes. The choice is whether it wakes among friends or over graves."

Silence, except the fire.

Then the scar-jawed man steps forward, out of the shadows at the back, and says in careful, correct, newly-learned old speech: "What must we do?"

And Ash, who is eight years old and barefoot in a cage in the middle of a hostile camp, surrounded by desperate descendants of a broken covenant, with no army and no plan and only a certainty like a stone in the palm that *the one who hears is coming*—

Ash smiles.

"First," Ash says, "you could let me out of this cage. It's making a very poor impression."

Laughter ripples through the camp—startled, guilty, real. And in that laughter, something shifts, some small tectonic grace: for one moment they are not captors and captive, believers and relic. They are just tired people and a tired child, sharing a fire and a joke in a language two hundred years dead.

That night, the lock on the cage is left unchained for the first time. Not opened—*unchained*. A distinction the guards insist upon, red-faced. But Ash lies in the straw touching the loose hasp, understanding exactly what it means: *we are afraid of what you'll ask us to do next, but we are no longer sure we'll refuse.*

And far off across the white steppe, past the picket lines and the sentry fires, past the salt flats glittering under the waning twelfth moon, Ash hears—or feels, or simply knows—a heartbeat moving through the dark. Two-legged. Determined. Accompanied by the soft pad of a beast walking beside it, and another set of boots behind, and somewhere above them all, wheeling on the wind, a bird crying news across the miles in the old tongue:

*She's coming. The one who hears is coming. And she is bringing an auditor, a grudge, and absolutely no plan worth the name.*

Ash curls up in the unchained cage, hugs the lavender blanket, and grins into the dark.

"Hurry, Freya," Ash whispers. "I think I've almost got them talked around. But you'd better be here before they change their minds—or worse, before they start asking *me* to lead them."

Outside, the twelfth moon wanes a little thinner.

Inside the tent, the heartseed hums a note it has not hummed in two hundred years.

Hope.
