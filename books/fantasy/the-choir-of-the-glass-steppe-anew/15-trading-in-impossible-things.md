## 15. Trading in Impossible Things

The buyer's camp sat in a shallow bowl of the salt flats like a coin dropped in milk—dozens of tents ringed around a central pavilion of striped canvas, lanterns burning behind yellow glass, and everywhere the smell of money that had traveled too far from home. Sela and I lay on our bellies on the ridge above it, watching.

"There," I said. "Third row from the center. The tent with the iron stakes."

Sela squinted. "How can you possibly know that's where they'd keep a child?"

"Because I've priced tents." I kept my voice flat and professional, the way I'd kept it for eleven years behind the Meridian Guild's counters. "Iron stakes mean heavy goods. Heavy goods mean something the owner is afraid will walk away. And look—" I pointed with my chin. "Two guards at that tent, one at every other. Nobody posts double coverage on rugs."

"You're enjoying this," Sela said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It wasn't quite not one, either.

"I am *appalled*," I said, "by how much I'm enjoying this."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about spending eleven years as a bookkeeper for a guild that trades in wonders: you learn things. Not the things the masters think you learn—their histories, their politics, their careful lies—but the other things. The weight of a griffin feather versus a phoenix one. The going rate for a bottled storm in Highsummer versus midwinter. The precise markup applied to anything described in a catalog as *priceless*, which was always forty percent, because *priceless* meant the seller had no idea what he had and the Guild intended to keep it that way.

I had spent years recording impossible things in neat columns, and somewhere in those columns I had learned the grammar of greed. Greed has rules. Greed has seasons. Greed, most importantly, has *competitors*, and competitors are the softest spot on any merchant's body.

Which is why, when we'd planned this over cold rabbit and colder fear, I had told Sela: "We don't sneak Ash out. We make them want to sell her back."

"And if they won't?"

"Then I'll have badly mispriced something for the first time in my career, and we'll improvise."

Sela had looked at me for a long moment—the look of a woman recalculating a colleague—and said, "You're terrifying when you talk like that."

I had taken it as the compliment it was.

* * *

We walked in through the front. This was Sela's contribution to the plan; she argued that anyone sneaking deserved to be caught, while two women walking openly with a ledger under one arm and an auditor's seal under the other would be waved through on pure institutional reflex. She was right. The sentry at the camp's edge looked at Sela's seal, looked at my ledger, looked at the sky as if hoping for guidance, and stepped aside with the expression of a man who had decided this was above his pay grade and probably above several higher pay grades too.

The camp itself was stranger up close. These were not soldiers or slavers, whatever Vey had implied. They were exiles—I could see it now in the mended clothes worn with stubborn care, in the way families clustered, in the old border dialects drifting between the tents like smoke. A woman ground grain with a stone quern that must have been carried across half the world. Two men argued over a map so faded its lines were more memory than ink. Children—actual children, plural—chased each other between the tent rows, and one of them stopped to stare at me with the frank appraisal of the young, then held up a carved wooden horse for my inspection.

"It's very fine," I told him gravely.

He nodded once, satisfied, and ran off. Somewhere behind my ribs, something that had been clenched since the salt flats loosened one notch. People who let their children run loose did not usually intend to hurt the child in the iron-staked tent. It didn't excuse anything. But it changed the shape of the problem, and changing the shape of a problem is half of solving it. The other half is arithmetic.

The guards at Ash's tent were the problem with that theory. They stood with spears planted and faces arranged into official blankness, and when Sela announced herself—"Auditor Sela Brann, Meridian Guild, here regarding the valuation of recent acquisitions"—they did not move except to become slightly more blank.

"The buyer's not receiving," said the taller one.

"The buyer will receive me," Sela said, "or the buyer can explain to the Guild's arbitration office why he refused a standard audit during an active transaction. Your choice. I'll wait." She waited. She was extremely good at waiting; she had a face built for it, all patience and quiet disappointment, like a headmistress contemplating a boy who has eaten the school's entire beehive.

The guards exchanged a glance that contained an entire negotiation. Then the tall one ducked inside, and we were admitted.

* * *

The buyer's name was Corvane Halst, and he received us in a tent hung with relics the way other tents are hung with laundry. A cracked bell that had once rung over a beast-temple. A spearhead fused into glass. A child's shoe, bronze, three sizes too small for any living foot. He himself was a lean man gone gray at the temples, with the careful hands of someone who handled fragile valuables, and when his eyes found me they did the thing I'd seen a thousand merchants' eyes do: flicked to the ledger, weighed it, dismissed me, returned to Sela.

That was fine. Being dismissed was the best disguise I'd ever worn.

"Auditor Brann." He gestured to a stool. "I confess I didn't expect the Guild to send anyone. Master Vey led me to believe our arrangement would remain... discreet."

"Master Vey believes many convenient things," Sela said, sitting. "I'm here about the item in your third-row tent. Iron stakes. Double guard."

Halst's face did not change, which told me everything. "You're well informed."

"I'm an auditor. Being informed is the entire job." She leaned back. "Here's my concern. The Guild sold you information about this acquisition. We did not sell you clear title. If the Guild's interest in this asset is challenged—if another party claims prior claim—you could lose everything you've paid, plus the asset. My job tonight is to determine whether that risk exists."

"And does it?" His voice was still smooth, but his hands had gone still on the table, and still hands are louder than shouting.

"That depends entirely," I said, "on whether you're holding a relic or a person."

Both of them turned to look at me. Halst's eyebrows rose a fraction—an intruder speaking uninvited—and Sela's mouth twitched in a way I chose to interpret as pride.

"Your scribe has opinions," Halst said.

"My appraiser," I corrected. "Freya Nystrom. Eleven years with the Meridian Guild. And I have priced things like what you're holding, Master Halst, so let me save us both some time." I opened the ledger on the table, to a page I had prepared that afternoon in a hand indistinguishable from genuine Guild script, because it *was* my hand and it had written ten thousand genuine entries. "A mute child who speaks the covenant-tongue. Assuming she's a curiosity—a living oddity, a tent-piece—she's worth perhaps four hundred crowns to collectors, maybe six if she sings. You paid considerably more than that, because Vey told you she was more than that. He told you she was the key to the Steppe's wards. Am I close?"

Halst said nothing, which was a yes with better posture.

"Now. Here's the difficulty." I turned the ledger toward him and tapped a column. "If she's truly the key, then she isn't a relic. She's a *claim*. And claims require standing. Do you have standing, Master Halst? Because I've read the exile registries—all of them, back to the Glass War—and there are nine surviving bloodlines with documented covenant-descendance. Yours may be one of them. But the family currently encamped at the Steppe's edge with Guild backing also claims standing, and they have lawyers, and lawyers cost more than spears."

"We have been robbed of our homeland for three generations," Halst said quietly. "We did not cross the White to be lectured on paperwork."

"No," I agreed. "You crossed it to go home. Which is exactly what makes you vulnerable, because wanting something badly is the most expensive condition a buyer can have, and Vey knows it. He priced your homesickness to the copper." I let that sit. Around us the tent full of relics seemed to lean in and listen. "So. Let me offer you an audit finding instead of a lecture."

* * *

This was the part I had rehearsed until Sela threatened to gag me with my own quill. Because the truth was, I didn't need to trick these people. I needed to *trade* with them, honestly, for the first time in a chapter full of lies—and the only honest currency I had was the one thing Vey wanted and couldn't get cleanly: the mother wardstone.

Vey had sent hunters after us for it. Vey had sold Ash to these people knowing they'd want the same prize. Vey's whole architecture rested on being the only man holding the map. So we were going to hand the map out in front of him, publicly, in triplicate, with witnesses—and thereby make it worthless as leverage and priceless as peace. Sela had called it insane. Then she'd helped me draft the terms, because she was an auditor too, underneath the spear-work, and auditors cannot resist a well-drafted term.

"The child is not a key," I told Halst. "She's a door-hinge. A hinge opens nothing by itself. What you actually need is the mother wardstone—the original, the one the covenant was spoken over, before it was broken. And I can tell you where it is."

His breath stopped. I watched a man three generations hungry hear the word *home* spoken aloud in a trade tent, and I understood, suddenly and uncomfortably, that I was doing to him exactly what Vey had done: selling hope at markup. The difference, I told myself, was the terms. Terms matter. That was the whole religion of bookkeeping.

"In exchange for what?" Halst asked, and to his credit his voice stayed level.

"Three clauses." I held up fingers. "One: the child walks out of this camp tonight, unharmed, unfollowed, with us. Two: you burn nothing, take nothing further from the Steppe, and hold your people at the edge until the wards answer—or fail. Thirteen days. If the thirteenth moon completes and the Steppe stays dead, come back and call me a fraud; I'll refund your hope at full price. Three: when the covenant is renewed—if it is renewed—your people get their hearing before the assembled hosts. Standing. Real standing, witnessed, not bought."

"And what do you get?" His eyes narrowed. "No one gives away a wardstone's location."

"Peace," I said. "At cost. It's the only item in my inventory I've never managed to turn a profit on, and I'd like to finally move it."

Silence. Outside, the wind moved across the salt with a sound like pages turning. Halst looked at Sela. "Is this Guild business?"

"This is *better* than Guild business," Sela said. "This is the first honest transaction I've seen the Guild adjacent to in years. I'm witnessing it. Formally." She produced her seal and set it on the table with a click that sounded, in that hush, like a gavel.

Halst stared at the seal, then at me, then—this surprised me—at the ledger, open to my neat columns, and something in his face shifted. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man whose grandmother had kept accounts in exile, in a borrowed alphabet, because numbers were the one language nobody could confiscate.

"You love the numbers," he said. It wasn't a question.

"They've never lied to me," I said. "People lie to them constantly, but the numbers themselves are honest. It's the closest thing to a moral education I ever got."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped him. Then he stood, and I stood, and we shook hands over the ledger like two merchants concluding the strangest deal either of us would ever record.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

They brought Ash out under the yellow lanterns, and my heart did something complicated and painful, because she was thinner than I remembered and her wrists bore the red rings of bindings now removed, but her chin was up and her eyes found mine across the tent with the ferocious calm of someone who had spent her captivity teaching her captors words. Behind her, I noticed, two of Halst's people were rubbing their arms and muttering—not angry, bewildered. I'd have bet my last crown she'd been giving them lessons. The girl collected students the way rivers collect rain.

She reached me and gripped my wrist, thumb finding the pulse point, the way she always did, and the feeling came through the contact the way it always did: not words, but weather. *You came. I knew you'd come. You're late.*

"I know," I murmured. "Traffic on the salt flats was terrible."

Halst watched this exchange with an expression I recognized from the warehouse floor: a man realizing the item in his inventory had opinions about him. To his lasting credit, he knelt to her level.

"Little hinge," he said in the border dialect, and then, carefully, in the old tongue—a handful of syllables, mispronounced, learned presumably from her during her captivity—he said something I didn't fully understand but felt anyway, like warmth through a wall: *forgive the cage; the hunger was ours, not yours.*

Ash studied him. Then she touched his forehead with one finger, gently, the way you'd bless a slow student, and said one word of the old language that made him flinch and then, oddly, smile.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"She said," Halst translated, rising, "that I pronounce it 'like a man chewing gravel.' But that I will improve." He turned to his guards. "Strike the stakes. Fetch the lady's belongings—her drawings, her stones, all of it. And ready horses for three travelers as far as the ridge. After that, we hold here. Thirteen days." He looked at me. "And if you're lying about the wardstone, Mistress Nystrom, I will find you. I've crossed the White once already. I can do it again."

"If I'm lying," I said, "you'll be in excellent company. Half the Steppe wants a word with me."

* * *

We rode out under a sky salted with stars, Ash wrapped in my cloak against my back, her cheek against my shoulder blade, humming something in the old language that the night wind picked up and carried away southward. Sela rode point, scanning the dark out of habit.

"That went well," she said at last, suspiciously. "Too well. Nothing in my professional experience goes that well."

"Halst is desperate, not stupid, and desperation plus intelligence equals a man who can do arithmetic," I said. "Also, I gave him a genuinely good deal. Good deals close fast. It's the bad ones that take all night."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. "There's just one thing I keep turning over. Vey sold Halst the information about Ash. Which means Vey knows exactly where this camp is. Which means when Vey learns his prize walked out the front gate in front of witnesses—"

"He'll come himself," I finished. "Yes. I'm counting on it. He'll want to see the wardstone bid fail in person. Men like Vey never trust a catastrophe they haven't supervised."

Ahead of us, far off across the black plain, something answered Ash's humming—a low, vast note, rising out of the earth itself, like a struck bell heard through miles of glass. Ash's arms tightened around my waist. Sela reined in. Even the horses went still.

"That," Sela said quietly, "is new."

Ash spoke against my shoulder, and for once she used words I knew, ones she'd taught me weeks ago in a dream:

*Mother-stone. Waking early.*

Thirteen days, I had promised Halst. Looking at the horizon, where a faint glow was gathering like dawn arriving twelve hours ahead of schedule, I began to suspect the Steppe hadn't read my contract.

It was starting without us.
