## 16. Vey's Confession

Oskar Vey had always believed that a man could be measured by his tea.

Not the quality of it—any fool with coin could buy good tea—but by how he took it when the world was ending. His father had drunk his black and bitter on the morning the Glass War reached their village, and had died that afternoon with the taste still in his mouth. Vey himself took his with honey, three spoons, because he had decided long ago that if he was going to be complicit in terrible things, he would at least do them sweetly.

He was stirring the third spoon into his cup when the tent flap opened and Freya Nystrom walked in as though she owned it.

Behind her came Auditor Sela Brann, hand resting on her belt-knife with the casual menace of a woman who had already decided where she would put it. And behind *her*, filling the doorway like weather, stood half the camp's hired muscle—except they weren't stopping her. They were standing aside. Which meant someone had been paid, or persuaded, or both, and Vey suspected he knew which ledger those transactions lived in.

"Guildmaster," Freya said. "You're drinking my tea."

Vey looked down at his cup. "This is camp tea. It tastes like boiled bark and regret."

"I stocked this caravan myself. Item forty-one, crate six." She crossed the tent, lifted his pot, sniffed it, and set it back down with the air of a woman confirming a theft. "That's mine. You owe the Guild four coppers."

For one absurd moment, Vey wanted to laugh. This was the woman he'd chosen, years ago, out of a hundred gray-faced clerks—the one who could smell a miscounted crate from across a warehouse, who filed her grievances in triplicate, who had hidden a miracle child in an intake ledger under the heading *miscellaneous textiles* and thought no one would notice. He had never once regretted hiring her. That was going to make the evening considerably harder.

"Sela Brann," Sela said, introducing herself to no one in particular, taking up a position near the tent's single exit. "Formerly of the Meridian Guild. Currently of the truth."

"Ambitious career move," Vey said. "Sit down, both of you. The tea is terrible but there's plenty of it."

"We're not here for tea."

"No," Vey agreed. "You're here for the child. And you've brought my own guards with you, which tells me you've spent money I didn't authorize. I respect that. Sit."

Freya sat. She sat the way she did everything—precisely, spine straight, hands folded, a bookkeeper attending the audit of her life. But Vey saw what most people wouldn't have: her eyes went first to the corner of the tent, to the cage.

Ash sat inside it, cross-legged, watching them all with the unnerving patience of something that had waited through centuries. The child's eyes found Freya's and something passed between them—not words, exactly. Vey had spent three months studying that language secondhand, through reports and intercepted dreams, and he still couldn't say what it did. Only what it cost. Everyone near it started listening to things they shouldn't be able to hear.

"You drugged the guards' stew," Freya said. "The night you took him."

"I seasoned it," Vey corrected. "Drugging implies harm. I'm not in the business of harming children, Freya. I'm in the business of keeping them alive, which is a great deal more difficult."

"He's eight years old and he's in a cage."

"He's eight years old and he's the only living speaker of a covenant that ended a war," Vey said. "Do you know what people would have done to him if I hadn't taken him off the open market? Do you know what the border lords offered? What the beast-cults in the Salt Reaches offered?" He ticked them off on his fingers, an old habit from pricing lots. "Three separate buyers bid on the child before I ever left Meridian. One of them wanted him *hollowed*. There are rituals, Freya. Old ones. They'd have taken the language out of him like marrow from a bone and left the rest to sweep up."

Freya's hands had gone white where they were folded. "And your alternative was a cage."

"My alternative was a cage with a lock only I hold, in a camp I pay for, guarded by men whose families I also pay for." Vey set down his cup. "You'll forgive me if I don't apologize for the furniture."

* * *

Sela Brann had been quiet for a long time, which Vey recognized as a professional habit rather than a personal failing. Now she spoke.

"The records," she said. "I pulled eleven years of them. Caravan routes sold to raiders. Ward schedules leaked to the beast-factions. Border skirmishes timed to the quarter-day, when the tolls run highest." She let that sit. "You didn't profit *from* the war, Guildmaster. You kept it running. Like a shopkeeper keeping a lamp lit so the customers can find the door."

Vey looked at her for a while. She was younger than he'd expected, when the Guild had sent her sniffing after unregistered gifts, and sharper too—he'd known within a day that she'd find the thread if he let her pull it. He had let her, in the end. Perhaps that had been a confession of its own.

"You want the numbers," he said. "Fine. In the last eleven years, the border wars killed approximately nine thousand people. Do you know how many died in the twelve years *before* that, when the fighting was hot and honest?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Forty thousand. Every year of open war cost five times what my managed version cost. I didn't invent the conflict, Auditor. I inherited it, the way you inherit a fire in the walls. And I made a choice: I could put it out and watch it reignite worse, or I could control the burn."

"That's not control," Freya said quietly. "That's arson with a schedule."

"It is *stewardship*," Vey said, and heard his own voice crack on the word, which annoyed him enormously. He got up, walked to the tent's rear, and pulled aside the hanging map that covered the wall.

Beneath it was not a map. It was a painting—or what remained of one, smoke-stained, its edges charred round as old bread. A village. A river. A woman in a blue shawl holding the hand of a small boy.

"My mother painted it the year before the Glass War came north," Vey said. "She was a schoolteacher. She taught children their letters, including—" his mouth twisted— "including a few words of the old tongue, back when that was legal, back when the covenant still held and nobody had decided yet that a language could be a weapon. My father said she sang it better than the birds. The birds used to come sit on the fence and listen. That's not a story, Auditor. That's a fact. Beasts came and listened to my mother sing."

He touched the small boy in the painting. The paint had bubbled there; the face was gone.

"When the wards failed—when the thirteenth moon completed and *nobody spoke the renewal*, because the humans had stopped teaching it and the beasts had stopped trusting it—the Steppe opened like a door. And everything on either side of that door came through it with teeth." He turned around. "My mother died in the first week. My father drank himself dead by the third year. My sister—"

He stopped. Some ledgers, he thought, you keep balanced precisely so you never have to read them aloud.

"My sister was taken by the beast-hosts at Corren Ford. Not killed. *Taken*. They had reasons—they'd lost their own young to human hunters, and taking was their custom, tit for tat, older than any treaty. She came back seven years later. She came back speaking their tongue and weeping whenever she heard ours. She lived with us another twenty years and never once slept indoors." He smiled, and it was not a pleasant thing to see. "So yes. I know what the war costs. I have paid retail."

Freya's voice, when it came, was gentle in a way that was somehow worse than anger. "Oskar. The child isn't a lever. He's a person."

"He is a *key*," Vey said. "And keys don't get to choose which doors they open. Neither, if we're honest, do the people carrying them." He gestured at her, at the whole improbable shape of her—a clerk who talked to rats, who bargained with elk, who had burned her own memories like candle-tallow to buy passage across a steppe. "You think I don't know what you've spent to get here? Your gift eats you, Freya. I've watched the reports come in. You forgot your own surname two weeks ago. You're walking toward the heart of the Steppe with less and less of yourself in your pockets, spending it all to deliver a child to a stone that will ask for even more." He shook his head slowly. "And you call *me* the monster."

"The difference," Freya said, "is that I asked. Every time. The elk could have said no. Ash can say no. Can your buyers?"

Vey held her gaze for a long moment. Then he laughed—short, genuine, surprised out of him.

"There it is," he said. "There's the woman I hired. You know what your intake file said, the day you applied? *Candidate demonstrates exceptional attention to consent clauses.* I remember thinking: what an odd thing to highlight. Now I know why." He poured himself more tea. His hand was steady. He'd practiced. "No. My buyers cannot take no for an answer. That's rather the point of buyers."

"Then we're done talking."

"We're almost done talking." Vey set down the pot and looked at her, and let the last of the performance fall away, because she had earned the true thing and he was tired of carrying it alone. "Here is my confession, since you've come all this way. I am not trying to prevent the covenant's renewal, Freya. I am trying to *own* it. Whoever controls the renewal controls the next hundred years of peace—and whoever botches it publicly, in front of both armies, will destroy any chance of peace for another century. Do you understand? If a guildmaster does it, it's politics. If a bookkeeper and a mute child do it, it's a miracle, and miracles are followed by wars over who owns the miracle."

"So you're doing this for our own good," Sela said dryly.

"I'm doing it because I have watched well-meaning people break the world twice," Vey said, "and I have decided that this time, if it breaks, it will break on my signature." He spread his hands. "There. Confession complete. Hang me."

* * *

Nobody hung him. Instead, to Vey's considerable irritation, Freya did something far worse.

She stood, walked to the cage, and knelt in front of it, and began to speak to Ash in that low, careful voice she used for frightened animals—which, Vey reflected, was probably accurate, since the child had spent a week among hired killers and was handling it better than most adults would.

Vey couldn't understand the syllables. Nobody human could. But he watched the child's face change, watched the fear go out of it like frost off glass, and felt something in his chest shift that he had spent thirty years welding shut.

Then Ash looked past Freya—at Vey—and spoke directly to him.

It was only a few words. It sounded like wind through a wheat field, like his mother singing to birds on a fence. Vey did not understand a single syllable, and understood all of it anyway, the way you understand grief in any language.

*You lost someone too.*

Vey stood very still.

"Don't answer that," he said finally, roughly, and turned away to fuss with the tea things because a man needed somewhere to put his hands. "Don't you dare start translating me, child. I'm not one of your beasts."

But of course he was. They all were. That was the whole cursed point of the covenant, wasn't it—that every living thing was in the herd together, and the only question was whether you admitted it before the wolves came or after.

He gathered himself. When he turned back, he was Guildmaster Oskar Vey again, buttons fastened, ledger balanced.

"Here is what happens now," he said. "I'm releasing the child. To you. Unconditionally."

Freya stared at him. "Why?"

"Because I want you to reach the wardstone," Vey said simply. "I want you to stand in front of both armies with your borrowed courage and your eaten memories and your eight-year-old key, and I want the whole world to watch you try." He smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "And then I want you to fail. Publicly. Spectacularly. Because you *will* fail, Freya—you must see that. A clerk and a child cannot renew a covenant that armies and archmages broke. The moon completes in days. When it does, the wards fail, the Steppe opens, and everyone who bet on hope will learn what I learned at nine years old standing in a burning village." He raised his teacup to her, three spoons of honey and all. "And then, when the screaming starts, they will come looking for the man who planned for it. And I will be here. With my ledgers. Ready."

The tent was silent. Outside, the wind moved across the salt flats with a sound like pages turning.

Freya rose. She looked at him for a long moment—not with hatred, which he could have handled, but with something like pity, which he could not.

"You're right about one thing, Oskar," she said. "I've forgotten most of my life. My mother's face. My name. Half of everything I ever was." She picked up the cage key from his desk herself, because of course she knew exactly where he kept it—item fourteen, drawer two—and turned it over in her fingers. "But here's what I still have that you don't. I've forgotten *why* I was afraid. And you never will."

She unlocked the cage. Ash stepped out, took her hand, and paused at the tent flap to look back at Vey one last time.

The child said nothing at all. Somehow it was the loudest thing anyone had said all night.

When they were gone, Vey sat alone with his cold tea and his mother's burnt painting, and told himself the feeling in his chest was indigestion.

Then he opened his ledger to a fresh page, dipped his pen, and wrote the date. Under it, after a moment, he wrote: *Thirteenth moon completes in four days.*

And beneath that, in handwriting he barely recognized as his own, smaller than anything he'd written in thirty years:

*Let her be right. Please. Let her be right.*

Outside, far across the darkening Steppe, the horizon began to glow—not with sunrise, which was hours away, but with the first pale shimmer of wards waking along the glass, answering footsteps that were still a valley distant and coming on like a tide.
