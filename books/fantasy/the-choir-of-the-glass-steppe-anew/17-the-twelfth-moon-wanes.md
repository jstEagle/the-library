## 17. The Twelfth Moon Wanes

The moon was dying by inches.

Freya noticed it first in the way the light fell on the salt—thinner each night, more apologetic, as though the sky itself were running out of something. The twelfth moon hung low over the eastern horizon, a coin worn smooth by spending, and beneath it the steppe stretched white and endless and waiting.

She sat up in the lee of an overturned waystone, her ledger open across her knees, and wrote by moonlight:

*My name is Freya Nystrom.*

The ink shivered in the cold. She watched it dry with the particular attention of someone who no longer trusted her own head to hold anything for longer than a season holds snow.

*My name is Freya Nystrom,* she wrote again, because the first entry had been written eleven days ago and she wanted to be sure the hand matched. It did. Small mercy. Whoever she had been, at least she had consistent penmanship.

"You're doing the list again," Ash said, materializing out of the dark the way Ash always did—soundless, sudden, wrapped in a coat three sizes too large that Sela had won off a caravan guard in a card game neither of them would describe honestly.

"I'm doing *inventory*," Freya corrected. "There's a difference. Inventory is a respectable professional activity."

"What's on it?"

Freya looked down at the page. *My name is Freya Nystrom. I was a bookkeeper of the Meridian Guild for nine years. I am good with numbers. I like tea with too much honey. Sela is my friend. Ash is—*

Ash is what? She had been circling that one for days now, the way a tongue circles a missing tooth. The word *charge* felt cold. *Ward* felt like something you put around a castle. The old language had a word for it, of course—the child had taught her that much before the words began sliding away—but the old language lived in her dreams now, and dreams did not survive transcription.

"Ash is mine to get to the heart of the Steppe," she said finally. "That's what's on it."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Freya agreed. "But it's what I answered." She tapped the page. "Humor me. Read this back tomorrow morning. If I look at you like a stranger, read it back anyway. Especially the part about the honey."

Ash took the book with both hands, the way the child handled everything Freya gave her—as if paper were a living thing that deserved gentleness. "You won't forget me," Ash said. Not a question. A fact stated in the tone children use for facts about weather.

"Wouldn't bet the warehouse on it," Freya said lightly, and hated how easily the lightness came, nine years of practice rising up in her like water finding its level. Then, because the moon was thin and the night was honest: "I already forgot your arrival day. Did you know that? I went looking for it last week and found a hole where it should have been. I remember the salt storm. I remember your feet, bare on the frozen stones. But the day itself—" She shook her head. "Gone. Spent. Like everything else I've paid out."

Ash climbed under her arm without asking, the way small creatures do when they have decided a thing belongs to them. "You'll make new ones," the child said into her shoulder. "Days. That's what they're for."

*That's what they're for.* Freya pressed her lips to the top of that unbrushed head and said nothing, because there was nothing in any language, old or new, that could improve on it.

* * *

They walked at dawn, because the beasts had told them to.

That was the strange arithmetic of these last weeks: an army assembling itself out of every horizon, and all of it moving to a rhythm none of them had set. Elk herds flowing south like dark rivers. Wolves trotting at the flank of sheep they would ordinarily have eaten, with the strained politeness of relatives at a funeral. A bear the size of a granary who had taken up position two hundred paces behind them and followed at exactly that distance, day after day, with the patient air of a chaperone.

And beside the beasts, the exiles. Human ones. They came out of the border villages in ones and twos at first—a woman with a cart full of root vegetables, two brothers who mended bridles, an old man who claimed to remember his grandmother singing in a tongue he'd assumed was nonsense. Then dozens. Then hundreds. None of them carried weapons that Freya could see. All of them walked toward the Steppe's heart as though summoned by a bell only their bones could hear.

"They think we know what we're doing," Sela muttered, falling into step beside Freya. The auditor looked like she hadn't slept in a month, which was accurate, and like she'd enjoy sleeping even less if it meant missing whatever happened next, which was also accurate. "Three hundred people following a bookkeeper, a fugitive, and a child. If I were still wearing the Guild badge, I'd call that a public order crisis."

"If you were still wearing the Guild badge, you'd be arresting us."

"Details." Sela squinted at the horizon, where the bear had paused to scratch itself against a waystone with earth-shaking thoroughness. "The elk spoke to you again last night. What did they say?"

Freya turned her wrist over, where the memory of the bargain still tingled. Bargaining with the elk had cost her the name of her childhood village—she'd discovered the loss at breakfast, reaching for it and finding smooth blankness—and she'd made the trade anyway, gladly, because the elk had brought news. "They said the glass plain is humming. The wardstones are waking, one by one, west to east, like lamps being lit in a long dark house. And they said—" she hesitated, "—they said the thirteenth moon is not waiting for anyone. When it fills, it fills. The wards fail or they don't. There's no second sitting."

"So we're late or we're not."

"We're not late. We're *barely* on time. Which, in my professional experience, is the same thing if you walk fast and don't stop to admire the scenery."

Sela snorted. Ahead of them, Ash had fallen in beside a wolf—actually beside it, close enough to touch, though the child never touched unless invited—and was talking to it in low syllables that made the hair on Freya's arms stand up. Not because the sounds were frightening. Because they were *familiar*. Because some cellar of her mind recognized them the way a body recognizes home.

"What does she say to them?" Sela asked quietly. "All day, every day. What do they talk about?"

"The same things everyone talks about. Where the water is. Whether the weather will hold. Who is coming and from where." Freya watched the wolf dip its head to the child, gravely, as one colleague to another. "And lately, a great deal about us. About whether the one-who-hears can carry what has to be carried. The beasts are worried about me, Sela. Do you understand that? An entire ecosystem is concerned about my wellbeing. I've spent nine years trying to be invisible to one guildhall, and now I'm famous among the elk."

Sela laughed—short, surprised, real. Then her face settled back into the gravity that seemed to live there permanently now. "Can you?" she asked. "Carry it?"

Freya thought about the holes in her head. Her mother's face, gone since the deep speech with the stone-bound guardian. Her surname, rescued only because she'd written it down in time. The taste of the honey tea she supposedly loved—she'd written that she loved it, but the loving of it was somewhere else now, in whatever country the memories went to when she spent them.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know how to keep books. You write down what matters, you balance the columns, and you trust the record over the recollection. So that's what I'm doing. Every night. Everything that matters goes in the ledger." She patted the book at her hip. "If I arrive at that plain with nothing left but ink, then the ink will speak for me."

* * *

They crossed the river at noon—or what had been a river, and was now a seam of glass where the water ran beneath, visible and silver and utterly silent, like a vein under skin. The exiles oohed at it. The wolves drank through cracks in the surface without breaking stride. Ash knelt and put one palm flat against the glass and listened, and when she stood her face was strange.

"It remembers the war here," she said, in the trade-tongue she was learning with alarming speed. "Underneath. Everyone remembers underneath."

"Everyone remembers underneath," Freya repeated softly, and reached for her ledger.

*What was I doing when the war ended?* The question surfaced and sank, surfacing and sinking, and she realized with a lurch that she didn't know. Not the forgetting kind of didn't know—the *never knew* kind. She had been so busy hiding, all those years, that she had never once asked herself what side of anything she'd been on. She had kept books for a Guild that traded in wonders and lies, and she had told herself that keeping accounts clean was a neutral act, and now three hundred exiles were following her toward a reckoning she had helped bury simply by staying quiet about it.

"Sela," she said. "When this is done—if it's done, if there's an *after*—I want you to keep the ledgers. All of them. Mine, Vey's, the Guild's intake records, everything. Someone has to remember what the prices were."

Sela looked at her for a long moment. "You're planning to pay for everything," she said. It wasn't a question either. Auditors, Freya was learning, could smell a liquidation coming the way wolves smelled weather.

"I'm planning to *balance*," Freya said. "What happens after the balancing is above my pay grade."

* * *

Evening came down violet and enormous. They made camp in the shadow of a waystone taller than the guildhall, carved all over with script that predated every kingdom, and the exiles lit fires in careful rings while the beasts lay down beyond the firelight—an encircling host, patient as winter.

Freya sat apart with her ledger and did the night's accounting.

*My name is Freya Nystrom.* (Still true. Still hers. She checked twice.)

*I am walking to the heart of the Glass Steppe with a child named Ash, who arrived barefoot in a salt storm on a day I can no longer name, and who is braver than anyone I have ever audited.*

*Sela Brann is my friend. She lost family to the border raids Vey prolonged for profit, and she chose truth over institution, which is the rarest transaction I ever hope to witness.*

*Guildmaster Oskar Vey let us go because he wants us to fail in front of everyone. Remember this. Remember it especially if I forget it. He is not finished.*

*The covenant was broken by both sides. The guardian showed me. Humans lied and beasts withdrew, and the silence between them curdled into war, and the war curdled into glass. No one gets to be only the innocent party. Write that down. Underline it.*

*My mother's face—I don't have it anymore. But she sang while she worked, badly, and she believed that a debt honestly recorded was a debt half forgiven. I think I became a bookkeeper because of her. I think I became a coward in spite of her. Both things can be true. Underline that too.*

Her hand was cramping. The moon had cleared the waystones now, one night shy of some threshold only the sky understood, and its light lay on the page like a blessing or a summons.

Ash appeared at her elbow, as inevitable as dawn. "Read it to me," the child said. "The parts you want me to keep."

So Freya read. The name. The friend. The warning about Vey. The underlined truths. And at the end, because the night was honest and the moon was listening, she added one line aloud, speaking it slowly as she wrote it, pressing each letter down like a seal:

*Ash is the reason. Whatever else is lost, keep the reason.*

Ash studied the page upside down, lips moving, sounding out the trade-script she was only weeks into learning. Then the child looked up, and in the old language—the language that lived in Freya's dreams now, that rose in her like groundwater—said something short and certain.

Freya didn't need translation. Some words resolve not into meaning but into feeling, and this one resolved into *stay*, and *here*, and *mine*.

"Mine too," she said, in the trade-tongue, in the old tongue, in whatever tongue remained to her tomorrow. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we walk fast and don't admire the scenery."

But sleep did not come quickly, and when it came, it came strangely.

She dreamed of the plain at the Steppe's heart—not as she had seen it in the guardian's visions, but as it would be tomorrow, or the day after, when the thirteenth moon filled. She dreamed of two hosts facing each other across the glass, human and beast, breath steaming in the cold, every eye on a single small figure standing between them. She dreamed of the wards waking one by one like lamps in a long dark house, and of a voice beginning to speak—

—and in the dream, the voice was hers.

She woke before dawn with her heart hammering and her hand already searching for the ledger, and found Ash awake too, sitting upright, staring west, where the horizon had begun—very faintly, very far away—to glow. Not sunrise. Sunrise was hours off and came from the wrong direction.

The wardstones. Lighting up, west to east, like lamps in a long dark house, and the light was coming *toward* them, and beneath her palm the frozen ground hummed like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Ash turned to her, eyes wide, and spoke in the old language, and this time the feeling that resolved in Freya's chest needed no gift at all to translate.

*It's starting. Walk faster.*

Freya closed the ledger, tied it to her belt, and stood up into the humming dark.

"Wake Sela," she said. "We're out of time to be barely on time."
