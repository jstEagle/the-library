## 18. The Choir Assembles

Sela Brann had faced down a charging border bull-elk with nothing but a ledger and a bad attitude, and she had wept less than she did watching the horizon fill with beasts.

They came from every direction at once, the way weather comes—not as an army marches but as a truth arrives, undeniable and slightly ahead of schedule. Elk in rivers of antler. Wolves moving with the unhurried confidence of creatures who have never once been late. Birds in sheets so dense they made moving shadows on the salt. And stranger things besides: things Sela had names for only from her grandmother's warnings, and things she had no names for at all, which was somehow worse and more wonderful.

"They're not attacking," said Toma beside her, a young Guild outrider who had joined them at Wardstone Village and who had not stopped being astonished since. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because nobody told them to," Sela said. "That's rather the point."

She stood on a low ridge of glassed salt with Freya Nystrom on one side of her and Ash on the other, and below them the heart of the Glass Steppe opened like a held breath.

The wardstone plain hummed.

Sela felt it in her teeth first, then her boots, then somewhere behind her sternum where she kept the things she didn't say out loud. The plain was a vast shallow bowl of mirror-smooth glass, and scattered across it—half-buried, half-revealed—stood the wardstones themselves. Not the squat village-bound markers she'd grown up fearing. These were pillars, some taller than guildhalls, their surfaces clouded and veined with light that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. The hum came from all of them at once, out of sync, like a choir warming up in a hundred different keys.

"It's been doing that for three days," Freya said quietly. "The beasts hear it as a summons. I think the humans hear it as a threat."

"And what do you hear it as?"

Freya was quiet long enough that Sela turned to look at her. The woman who had fled the Meridian Guild three turns ago had been sharp-eyed, wry, careful with her words the way a bookkeeper is careful with sums. The woman standing here now was thinner around the edges in a way that had nothing to do with rations. She looked at the humming plain the way you look at a room you've just realized you grew up in.

"I don't know yet," Freya said. "I think—I think I used to know. Before." She touched her temple briefly, the gesture she'd developed for the missing year, the way other people touch a scar.

Ash tugged Sela's sleeve.

The child had changed too, over the turns of flight—not older exactly, but *more*, the way a word becomes more when you finally understand its second meaning. Ash's eyes went from the beast-hosts pouring down the northern slopes to the human army assembling on the southern rise, and back again, and something in the child's face made Sela's chest hurt.

"What is it, little one?" Sela asked. She'd started calling Ash that about a week after joining them, mostly because Ash had taken to calling her *the one who asks questions* in the old language, which was accurate but exhausting.

Ash spoke, and the old language came out in a rush, and Freya translated with the slow care of someone reading by candlelight: "*Both sides are singing the same wrong song.*"

"Wrong how?" Toma asked.

Freya listened to another burst of syllables, and her face did something complicated. "Wrong the way it's always been wrong. Each side hears the hum and thinks: *they are breaking the covenant.* Both armies are marching to defend a peace that neither has actually attacked."

Sela looked south, at the human lines.

She knew those lines. She'd served along them for six years after the border raids took her family—had stood watch on ridgelines very much like this one, staring at empty steppe and waiting for beasts that mostly never came, and when they did come, coming badly, starving, confused, shot before anyone thought to ask why. Her commanders had called it defense. It had always looked, from the ridge, like two peoples shouting at each other across a distance too far for words, each convinced the silence was malice.

Now the silence was ending, and both sides had brought their loudest voices.

***

The human army reached the southern rim of the bowl an hour past midday, and Sela got her first good look at what fear dressed up as duty actually wears.

It wasn't the Guild's regulars, thank every merciful ledger. Vey's hired hunters were there—she picked out their mismatched armor at the left flank, and her hand found her knife hilt without asking permission—but the bulk of the force was levies and volunteers, border-folk and steppe-townsfolk, farmers with heirloom spears and miners with hammers still bright from the forge. Banners she recognized from a childhood of market days. Somewhere in that mass were people she'd drunk with, traded with, arrested once or twice fairly and once unfairly.

At their center, on a white mule that was clearly beneath his dignity, rode Guildmaster Oskar Vey.

"He came himself," Toma breathed. "He never leaves Meridian."

"He wants witnesses," Sela said. Her mouth tasted like copper. "He wants the whole steppe to see the gifted child fail, and the wards fail with her, and then he wants to be standing there with his solution already in his pocket." She glanced at Freya. "You heard him say it."

"I heard him say it." Freya's voice was flat as the salt. "He wants us to reach the stones. He wants Ash to speak and falter. Because if the covenant renews itself through a child and a traitor and an auditor—" she nodded at Sela, without heat, "—then no one needs men like him anymore. But if it fails publicly, catastrophically, in front of ten thousand people, then everyone runs back to the strongman who promised to keep them safe. He isn't trying to stop us. He's betting on us."

"That's the most depressing thing I've ever heard," Toma said.

"You should read his ledgers sometime," Sela said. "We brought copies."

Below them, the armies settled into their facing positions with the terrible orderliness of a ceremony nobody had planned. Ten thousand humans on the south rim. On the north, the beast-hosts flowed into formation that wasn't formation—the elk ranks closing like water finding its level, the wolves spacing themselves with mathematical grace, the great pale shapes of the steppe-bears sitting down at the rear like grandparents at a wedding.

And between the two hosts, the glass plain stretched, humming, veined with light, studded with wardstones like the pipes of some buried organ.

Nobody moved.

"Well," Sela said, into the silence. "Someone has to go first."

"We go together," Freya said. "All four of us. Slowly. Hands visible. And Ash—" she crouched to the child's height, and Sela watched her choose her words with the tenderness of someone handling something breakable, "—you don't have to do anything until you're ready. Do you understand? Whatever happens down there, you are a person first and a covenant second. If your voice shakes, it shakes. We'll be beside you."

Ash looked up at her with an expression Sela couldn't read, and answered in the old tongue—one short sentence—and Freya flinched as though the words had physical weight.

"What did it say?" Sela asked.

Freya swallowed. "It said, *I remember when this was sung properly.*" She straightened. "Which is impossible, because Ash is seven years old, and the last time this was sung properly was before the Glass War. So either the language carries memories the way water carries silt, or—" she stopped.

"Or?" Toma prompted.

"Or the child has been dreaming the same dreams I have," Freya said, "and mine go back further than my own life. Which raises questions I would genuinely rather not answer while standing on top of an active wardstone." She managed something that was almost her old dry smile. "Later. Questions later. Walking now."

***

Walking down onto the glass plain was like walking onto a frozen lake that hummed.

Sela kept her hands wide of her body, palms out, the way you approach a horse that doesn't know you. The light under the surface moved faster now, responding to their footsteps, veins of gold and green chasing away from each bootfall like ripples in reverse. The hum climbed in pitch until it wasn't a sound anymore so much as a pressure, a hand laid flat against her breastbone.

Behind them, ten thousand humans held their breath. Ahead, ten thousand beasts did whatever the equivalent was—she could feel the attention of the whole northern host like sunlight through a magnifying lens.

Halfway to the nearest wardstone, a wolf broke from the host line.

Toma's hand went to his sword; Sela caught his wrist. "No," she said. "Watch."

The wolf crossed the glass at a trot, unbothered, and sat down twenty paces ahead of them, and waited. Then a second wolf joined it. Then an elk, folding its absurd legs beneath itself with the delicacy of furniture being arranged. Within minutes there was a loose crescent of beasts ringing the path ahead of them—not blocking it, Sela realized. *Escorting.* An honor guard, or an audience, or both.

"They know," she murmured. "Somehow they all know why we're here."

"Beasts talk," Freya said. "People just stopped listening, so people assume the conversation stopped too." She paused. "I used to be one of the people who assumed that. For about nine years. In case anyone's keeping score."

"I'm keeping score," Sela said. "You owe me approximately one confession per league traveled. You're behind."

That earned her a real smile, brief and crooked, gone as fast as it came. But Sela had learned to bank those. Over three turns of shared road and shared danger, she had collected Freya Nystrom's smiles the way a miser collects coins—carefully, and against hard times.

They reached the first wardstone as the sun leaned west.

Up close, the pillar was vast—a finger of cloudy glass wider than a wagon, its surface crawling with that slow internal light. Carvings covered it, worn nearly smooth: figures Sela slowly understood were beasts and humans interwoven, hand in paw, antler to shoulder, arranged in rings that spiraled upward toward a broken band near the top. The break was clean, deliberate, recent-looking despite its age. Someone had chiseled the covenant's crown off this stone, and every stone like it, a lifetime ago.

Ash walked forward alone.

Sela hadn't planned to let that happen. None of them had. But the child stepped out from between them with the calm of someone crossing their own courtyard, and none of them could move—the hum had thickened around their legs like deep water, holding them gently, and perhaps that was mercy, or perhaps the stone simply wanted a clear view.

Ash pressed one small palm flat against the glass.

The plain went silent.

Not quiet—*silent*, the way a room goes silent when someone says the true thing out loud at last. Every vein of light froze. Every beast on the northern rim lowered its head. On the southern rise, ten thousand humans, who could not possibly have heard anything at that distance, nonetheless leaned forward as one body.

And Ash began to speak.

Sela had heard the old language in fragments for weeks—over campfires, in dreams she wouldn't admit to having, murmured by Freya in her sleep. She had thought she knew its shape. She had been wrong. Spoken here, against the stone, with the whole steppe as resonance chamber, the language was not speech at all. It was architecture. Each syllable landed and *built* something—she could feel structures rising in the air, invisible, load-bearing, ancient words fitting into ancient grooves like a key that had been waiting in a lock since before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

The light in the stone woke. Gold climbed the carvings, filling the interwoven figures, and the broken crown of the pillar flickered—

—and guttered.

Ash's voice wavered. The child tried again, reaching higher in the phrase, and the light rose with it, trembling, and fell again. A third attempt. The syllables came slower now, fraying at the edges, and Sela saw the exact moment the child heard it too—that the song wanted a depth Ash's voice simply did not have. Not a failure of courage. A failure of years. The covenant had been broken by adults, in full voice, with the weight of nations behind the breaking, and it seemed the glass remembered that, and wanted its equal and opposite.

Ash stopped.

The silence afterward was the worst thing Sela had ever stood inside. It had edges. It had weight. Somewhere on the southern rise, she knew, Oskar Vey was watching his prophecy come true, and somewhere on the northern rim, ten thousand beasts were learning—or confirming—what humans did to promises, and the space between the two hosts was filling up with all the old arithmetic of blame, the arithmetic that had started a war once already, the arithmetic that said *they will break it, strike first, strike now—*

Ash turned around. The child's face was wet and furious and terrified, and looking not at the armies, not at the stone, but at her—at Sela—and at Freya.

And Ash said something in the old language, small and clear, and this time Sela didn't need a translation, because some words travel intact across any distance:

*Help me.*

The hum shuddered. The light in the stone pulsed once, twice, like a heart deciding.

And Freya Nystrom, former junior ledger-keeper of the Meridian Guild, the invisible woman, the one who had spent nine years teaching herself not to listen, stepped forward out of the deep-water hold of the wardstone's attention, rolling up her sleeves like a bookkeeper preparing for inventory day.

"No," Sela whispered, grabbing for her arm. "Freya—you don't know what it costs, you've seen what it costs, you've already lost—"

"I know exactly what it costs," Freya said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were shining. "I've been reading the price list for months. I'm the only one here who has." She looked down at Ash, and smiled that crooked, banked-smile smile, and said something in the old language that Sela would spend the rest of her life trying to translate, and failing, because she heard it only once.

Then Freya Nystrom turned to face the broken crown of the wardstone, filled her lungs with the humming air of the Glass Steppe, and began to speak.
