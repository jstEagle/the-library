## 19. Freya Speaks

The plain of wardstones hummed like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop.

Freya stood at the edge of the glass with Ash's hand in hers and a folded page against her heart, and she thought—absurdly, at the end of everything—that the humming sounded exactly like the Meridian Guild's counting room on inventory day. Ten thousand small voices adding themselves up toward one enormous total.

She had written it all down last night by firelight, while Sela kept watch and the beasts gathered on every horizon like weather deciding where to fall. Her own handwriting, careful as always, because some habits outlive the person who made them:

*My name is Freya Nystrom. I was born in a house with blue shutters. My mother smelled of cedar smoke and sang off-key on purpose to make me laugh. I have spent nine years hiding what I am because hiding felt safer than being seen. It was not. It never is.*

*If you are reading this and I am no longer reading it with you, know that I chose this. Know that I would choose it again.*

*Know that I loved a child who taught me that silence is also a language, and that mine was saying something terrible.*

She had read it three times until the words sat behind her eyes like lanterns. Then she had folded the page, tucked it into her coat, and slept two hours without dreaming—which was its own kind of warning, because for weeks her dreams had been full of the old tongue, Ash's voice pouring syllables into her sleep like water into a dry field.

Now the twelfth moon hung above the eastern glass, worn thin as a coin rubbed between worried fingers, and the thirteenth was already there beside it—a sliver so new it was barely more than an intention. Both armies could see it. Both armies knew what it meant.

To the west, the human host: pikes and salt-stained banners, the Guild's grey livery, Vey somewhere near the front on a white mule he had almost certainly overpaid for. To the east, the beastkind: elk with antlers like winter branches, wolves in ranks that made the eye slide off them, great slow shapes at the back that might have been bears or might have been hills that had decided, after long consideration, to attend.

And between them, the glass steppe itself, singing.

"Ash." Freya crouched to the child's level. The wind pulled at both their hair. "You don't have to do this alone. You were never going to have to do this alone."

Ash looked up at her with those eyes the color of deep river ice, and answered in the old language—the true one, the first one—and Freya understood it now without trying, the way you understand your own heartbeat. *The words are too big,* Ash said. *I am too small. A child cannot renew what grown people broke. The stone told me. The stone has been telling everyone for a thousand years and nobody listened.*

"I know," Freya said. "I've been not-listening professionally for most of my life. I'm rather good at it."

Ash's mouth twitched. Even here. Even now. That was the thing about children that no ledger could account for: they carried joy the way other people carried knives, casually, ready at any moment.

"So we'll do it together," Freya said. "But if the words need a voice that remembers what was broken—" she took a breath that hurt all the way down, "—then I'll give them mine."

Sela Brann stood a few paces off, arms crossed, sword sheathed, which from Sela was practically a declaration of faith. She had argued with Freya half the night. *There has to be another way. There's always another way; I've built a career on finding them.* And Freya had shown her the page, and Sela had read it twice, and then she had done the most Sela thing imaginable: she had taken out her own notebook and started drafting terms for the covenant, so that when Freya spoke, she would speak to something real, something written, something with clauses.

"Terms are ready," Sela said now, holding up the notebook. "Shared stewardship. Joint archive. Amnesty provisions. Beast signatories witnessed by—" she glanced at the enormous hill-that-was-probably-a-bear, "—that one, apparently, if the nodding means what I think it means."

"It does," Freya said. "He says your handwriting is very legible. He's impressed. He's seen a lot of human writing and most of it is complaints."

The bear-hill rumbled something that might have been laughter.

Then the moons touched.

It was not a sound. It was the absence of one—the humming of the plain cut off mid-note, like a choir master's fist coming down—and in that terrible silence every wardstone across the steppe went dark at once, a thousand lights snuffed in a single breath, and Freya felt the wards fail the way you feel a floor give way beneath you: in the stomach first, then everywhere.

The armies surged. Pikes dropped level. Wolves lowered their heads. Somewhere behind her a human officer screamed an order, and somewhere ahead an elk bellowed the old word for *war*, and Freya had perhaps ten heartbeats before the glass ran red.

She stepped forward.

Not walked. Stepped—out from behind Ash, out from behind Sela, out from behind nine years of ledgers and lowered eyes and the comfortable gray invisibility of a woman nobody bothered to remember. She walked onto the open glass between the hosts, alone, and the wind died, and ten thousand eyes found her, and for the first time in her life Freya Nystrom did not look away from a single one of them.

She opened her mouth, and she spoke the old language aloud.

---

Here is what no one had told her about the covenant: it was not a contract. It was not law, or oath, or magic in the way humans used the word. It was a *remembering*. The wards of the Glass Steppe were not walls—they were memory given mineral form, the land itself holding the record of a promise made when the world was young: *we will share this place, and neither kind will forget the other's face.*

And the price of speaking it renewed was not blood. It was not gold.

It was the remembering itself.

Freya understood this the moment the first word left her lips, because the word took something with it. She felt it go—a small bright thing plucked from inside her—and she knew, with bookkeeper's precision, exactly which entry had been deleted.

The name of the street she'd lived on as a girl.

Gone. Not faded—*gone*, the way a line is gone after you strike it through in good ink. In its place, the word rang out across the steppe, and the nearest wardstone flared back to life with a light like dawn deciding to be punctual.

The armies froze. Both of them. Ten thousand soldiers and beasts watching a thin woman in a travel-stained coat stand on the glass and pay out her life one memory at a time.

She kept speaking.

Her mother's hands, kneading bread on a winter morning—she gave them up and heard them ring from the stones, cedar-smoke and flour-dust translated into light. The blue shutters. The off-key song. Each memory tore free and became syllable and became radiance, and the dark wardstones caught one by one down the length of the plain like a fuse burning toward the heart of the world, and Freya walked forward as she spoke because the covenant required her to walk the line between the hosts, and because stopping was not in her nature anymore, if it ever had been.

*This is my mother's face,* said the language, though Freya could no longer see it herself. *I remember it. Take the remembering. Keep it. Let it hold the promise.*

A wolf at the front rank of the beast host whined. An old soldier dropped his pike and covered his mouth with both hands. On the human side, someone began, quietly, to weep—not from grief but from recognition, the way you weep at a funeral for someone else's mother because yours is suddenly unimaginably precious.

Freya reached the midpoint of the plain. The moons overhead ground together like millstones. The thirteenth moon swelled, hungry, nearly whole.

She was running out of childhood now. She could feel the edges of it going—the game she played with the neighbor's dog, the taste of the berries she wasn't supposed to pick, her father's laugh. She fed them all into the words without slowing, because she had learned arithmetic from the best guild in the world and she knew exactly how much she had left and exactly how much the covenant cost, and the numbers balanced only one way.

Then came the harder entries.

Nine years at the Meridian Guild. The smell of the warehouse—tallow and pepper and the musk of wonders in crates. Oskar Vey teaching her double-entry bookkeeping and calling her *the steadiest pair of hands I've ever employed*, and meaning it, and meaning, underneath it, *you are useful and usefulness is safety*. The cart-horse whispering about the thirteenth moon, and Freya turning back to her ledgers, and the specific texture of that cowardice, warm as a blanket, suffocating as one.

She gave it all. Every year of hiding. Every hour of not-listening. The guilt braided with protectiveness; the tea at which her loyalty to the Guild had died so quietly she hadn't noticed the body for days. The memories rang out over both armies, and she saw soldiers flinch—because these were *their* memories too, every one of them, the small daily surrenders that wars are actually made of—and the wardstones blazed higher, drinking truth like rain.

The thirteenth moon completed.

Mid-sentence. She felt it happen above her, the sliver rounding to full with a silent click she heard in her teeth, and the plain's humming returned—but changed now, deeper, expectant, a held breath. The covenant was listening. The covenant wanted the rest.

And the rest was—

Freya stumbled.

Because the next entry in the ledger of herself was Ash. Ash arriving barefoot in the salt storm. Ash gripping her wrist. Ash asleep against her shoulder on the elk's back, teaching her words through dreams, naming her *the one who hears*. The whole short fierce history of the only family she had chosen rather than inherited.

If she gave that—if she poured the child into the covenant along with everything else—then whoever finished this sentence would finish it as a stranger. Someone who did not know why the small figure at the edge of the glass was crying. Someone who had traded the love away to power the peace it bought.

She stood on the glass with the moons burning and both armies waiting, and she thought about her folded page, and about what she had written by firelight: *know that I chose this.*

But she had written that before she understood the final price. And a bookkeeper knows that a contract signed under false pretenses is void.

"No," Freya said—in the old tongue, aloud, to the covenant itself, to the listening land. "That one isn't for sale. Find another way or take nothing."

The plain went still. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

And then—from the edge of the human host, from the edge of the beast host, from everywhere at once—came answering voices. Sela, reading the terms aloud in her clear auditor's voice. The old bear-hill, rumbling the covenant's first lines. Elk and wolves and soldiers, hesitantly, badly, pronouncing syllables they didn't understand but had *witnessed*, and the witnessing was the point, had always been the point: a covenant renewed by one voice is a debt, but a covenant renewed by many is a promise.

They could not carry her memories. But they could carry the words.

Freya laughed—actually laughed, breathless and ragged, at the beautiful fraudulent arithmetic of it—and rejoined the sentence where she had left off, and this time she was not alone. The words rose from a hundred throats and a thousand wardstones, and what remained for her to give was only what she had offered from the start: her name.

"My name," she said, and felt it begin to lift out of her like a bird leaving a cold branch, "is Freya."

*Nystrom* went next, dissolving into light before it fully left her lips, and the last wardstone at the heart of the plain ignited, and the light rolled outward across the steppe in a wave that touched every soldier and every beast and every stone, and the glass itself sang—one vast chord, the choir assembled at last, every voice that had ever been silenced on this ground rising together.

When it faded, Freya Nystrom was standing on the Glass Steppe with no memory of her childhood, no memory of her mother's face, no memory of her surname, and no idea—none at all—why everyone was looking at her like that.

She looked down. A child was gripping her hand, crying and smiling at once, saying something in a language Freya somehow still understood, the way you understand warmth without remembering fire.

*You're safe,* the child was saying. *It's done. You're safe, and I'm staying with you, and I will tell you everything. Every day. As many times as it takes.*

"That's very kind," Freya said politely, to this stranger with the river-ice eyes. "Have we met?"

Behind them, across the whole length of the plain, ten thousand pikes began—slowly, one by one, like winter grass bending—to lower.

And a tall woman in an auditor's gray coat pushed through the host, notebook clutched to her chest, tears freezing on her cheeks, and stopped in front of Freya as if she'd run the entire way on legs that had forgotten how.

"You don't know me," the woman said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Freya admitted. "Should I?"

The woman pressed the notebook into her hands. "No," she said, and her voice cracked clean through. "But you wrote yourself a letter. And I promised to deliver it."

* * *

Freya turned the notebook over in her hands. On the cover, in handwriting that felt familiar the way a house feels familiar in a dream, someone had written:

*For Freya. Read slowly. You paid full price—you may as well collect everything you bought.*

She opened it. The first line said: *My name is Freya Nystrom.*

"Well," said Freya, to the child, to the woman, to the singing plain, to whatever patient part of herself was waiting in the ink. "I suppose we'd better start at the beginning."
