## 20. After the Thirteenth Moon

Sela Brann had spent eleven years as an auditor of the Meridian Guild, and in all that time she had never once seen an army stand down. She had seen armies retreat, which was standing down with the dignity removed. She had seen them surrender, which was standing down with paperwork. What she saw now, on the glass plain beneath a moon that had just finished its thirteenth turn, was neither.

It was people putting down their weapons because they no longer needed them.

The sound of it was what undid her. Not the clatter of dropped blades—though there was that, thousands of small bright notes ringing off the glass like a rainstorm made of bells—but the silence underneath. For years the border had hummed with the tension of two peoples waiting for the other to move first. Now the humming stopped, and in its place came something Sela had almost forgotten existed: the ordinary noise of an evening. Wind over salt. A horse stamping. Somewhere behind the human lines, a soldier laughing, high and startled, the way you laugh when you realize you are not about to die.

Beside her, Freya Nystrom swayed on her feet.

Sela caught her by the elbow without thinking—auditor's reflexes, honed by years of catching falling ledgers—and felt how light the woman had become. Not thin, exactly. *Emptied.* Like a warehouse after inventory, everything of value carried out through the front door and signed for.

"I'm fine," Freya said, which was the traditional lie of people who were not fine, delivered in a voice Sela did not recognize. It wasn't the careful, colorless voice of the guild's best bookkeeper. It wasn't the raw, cracking voice of a woman pouring her life into an ancient covenant. It was something new and unformed, a voice still deciding what it wanted to be.

"You're not," Sela said. "But you will be. That's the difference between tonight and every other night this year."

Freya looked at her then, and Sela braced for it—the look she'd been dreading since the wardstone plain began to blaze. The look of a stranger. Freya had given away her childhood, her mother's face, her own name. Surely there was nothing left in those grey eyes that knew Sela Brann from any other tall woman in a scorched auditor's coat.

"Your left bootlace is undone," Freya said. "It has been undone since the salt flats. I kept meaning to tell you."

Sela laughed before she could stop herself, a rough, graceless sound that scattered a nearby crow. "You gave up your entire past to a rock, and your first act as a person with no history is to critique my footwear."

"It seemed important," Freya said gravely. "Someone has to keep records."

* * *

The terms of the covenant, as far as anyone could determine, were three.

Sela learned them the way everyone did that night: not from a proclamation or a treaty scroll, but from Ash, who stood between the two armies with the thirteenth moon still fading above, and spoke in both tongues at once—a strange doubled music, human words braided through the old language, so that neither side heard a translation but a single voice saying one thing in two ways.

*The Steppe belongs to itself,* said the first term. *Those who walk it keep it.*

*What was broken is mended, not avenged,* said the second. *No debt is owed for the Glass War, by man or beast. The ledger closes.*

That one had taken longer. Sela watched faces on both sides of the glass as it settled over them—the exiles who had followed Vey's buyers across half the world hoping to reclaim stolen homelands, the beast-hosts who had gathered from every horizon with old grievances folded into their bones. She watched an old elk-mare lower her great antlered head before a human woman whose grandfather had died in the border raids, and she watched the woman weep, and she understood that the second term was the hardest mercy ever spoken aloud. Forgiveness with nothing asked in return. An audit in which every account was written off.

She hated it for approximately four seconds. Then she thought of her brother Tomas, dead eleven years in a raid that had accomplished nothing except making more grief to go around, and she thought: *he would have hated the war more than he hated them.* And she let the four seconds pass.

The third term was the simplest and the strangest: *A record shall be kept. What the covenant must not forget shall be written, and the writing shall be shared.*

Ash had looked directly at Sela when speaking that one. The child's eyes—dark, unhurried, older than kingdoms in the way that deep water is older than the ships on it—had found her in the crowd with terrible precision.

Later, sitting on a crate of abandoned army rations with a stub of charcoal and the back of a requisition form, Sela wrote out the three terms in her neat auditor's hand and stared at them.

*A record shall be kept.*

"Well," she said to the paper. "I suppose that's me told."

* * *

Oskar Vey surrendered his ledgers on the fourth morning after the moon completed.

He did it, characteristically, with excellent penmanship. Sela found him in his command tent—not a command tent anymore, she noted; the banners had come down—seated at a folding desk, the Meridian Guild's master ledgers stacked before him in perfect order, oldest volumes on the bottom, each one labeled in his small precise script. Beside them sat a document titled, in full, *A Complete Accounting of Transactions Concerning the Steppe, Its Wards, Its Inhabitants, and Its Secrets, Prepared Voluntarily by Oskar Vey, Guildmaster.*

"Voluntarily," Sela read aloud.

"I find the word strengthens a confession." Vey folded his hands. He looked smaller than he had in the guildhall, Sela thought. Or perhaps she had simply grown. "Auditor Brann. Or should I say Archivist Brann? The child announced your appointment last night. Rather publicly."

"She did." Ash had indeed, in front of several hundred soldiers, several thousand beasts, and one deeply embarrassed former bookkeeper, declared that the third term required a keeper of records, and that the keeper should be "the one who asks true questions." Sela had tried to decline. The elk-mare who had carried them across the white had rested her chin on Sela's shoulder until she stopped trying.

"There will be trials," Sela said. "You know that. What you sold—the caravan routes, the ward timings, the names of gifted children—there are families on both sides of the border who lost people because of your profit margins."

"Yes." He said it simply, without flinching, and somehow that was worse than denial. "I have read my own ledgers, Auditor. Every night for thirty years. I know exactly what each entry cost." He pushed the stacks toward her. "Take them. All of them. Every volume, every cipher key, every letter of instruction I sent to the buyers' camps. I will answer any question your archive puts to me, under seal, under oath, under whatever ritual makes your new institution legitimate."

"Why?" Sela asked. It was the question she'd carried up the hill to his tent, and now that she had him in front of her, it came out blunter than intended. "You could run. Half the buyers would shelter you. You could burn these and die rich."

Vey looked at the ledgers for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and very tired.

"Because I built my whole life on the belief that someone must control the covenant's renewal, or the war comes back. That belief made me do monstrous things in the name of prevention." He met her eyes. "And then a bookkeeper I barely noticed and a mute child I tried to sell walked into the heart of the Steppe and renewed it in an afternoon, with no army, no treasury, and no plan worth the name. Which means my belief was wrong. And a man who discovers his life's work was founded on an error owes the world an honest accounting of it. It's the only apology that isn't worthless."

Sela studied him. Eleven years of auditing had taught her to hear the difference between remorse performed and remorse paid, and this—this dry, ledger-shaped grief—was payment.

"The trials will still happen," she said.

"I should hope so. Justice that spares the powerful isn't justice; it's accounting fraud." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of the charming man he must once have been, before the war ate his family and he decided to become the war instead. "Though I'll note, for the record, that I released the child when I could have kept her. And that I let you three cross the flats alive when a word from me would have buried you."

"For the record," Sela agreed, "and weighed accordingly." She gathered the ledgers into her arms. They were heavy. They would be heavy for years. "Enjoy the wait, Guildmaster. The archive works slowly. We're thorough."

"That," said Oskar Vey, "is the only thing about you I was ever afraid of."

* * *

The trouble with Ash began, as most good troubles do, with a committee.

The exiles—the descendants of the war's displaced, the desperate folk who had pooled their ruined fortunes to buy a living relic of the old tongue—recovered quickly from the shock of the covenant. Quicker than Sela expected. Grief, she was learning, turns fast into hope if you give it somewhere to go, and the second term had given them nowhere to go except forward. But within two days they had organized themselves, because exile teaches organization the way winter teaches mending, and they came to the camp where Freya slept off the cost of her sacrifice, and they brought gifts, and they brought a proposal.

They wished to take Ash with them. As a teacher. As a treasure. As, though they dressed the word in silk, a relic—the last native speaker of the covenant tongue, raised among them, honored, protected, *kept.*

Sela arrived in the middle of it, carrying a kettle of broth, and stopped in the tent flap.

There must have been twenty exiles inside, dignified and earnest, and at the center of them stood Ash—nine years old, barefoot out of habit even indoors, wearing a coat three sizes too big because nobody had yet measured the child for anything—and the child's face wore an expression Sela recognized from a thousand intake interviews. The polite blankness of someone being discussed rather than heard.

"No," Ash said.

An elder—a silver-haired woman with a mapmaker's hands—began gently, "Little speaker, we only want—"

"No." Ash's voice didn't rise. It deepened, if anything, taking on that doubled resonance, the old language humming underneath the common words like a river under ice. "You want to own the language by owning me. The buyers bought relics. I was in a cage next to them. I counted." The child held up small fingers. "Forty-one relics. Two singing stones, one jar of storm, six eggs that never hatched. None of them chose to be there. I am not the forty-second."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a horse snorted.

"I will teach," Ash went on, softer now. "Anyone. Everyone. Beasts already speak it; humans can learn; the school will be here, on the Steppe, where the words live. But I stay with Freya." The child crossed the tent and took Freya's hand—the hand of a woman who no longer remembered her own mother's face, but who had, the night before, carefully written *ASH LIKES HONEY ON BREAD, DO NOT LET ANYONE TELL YOU OTHERWISE* on a scrap of paper and pinned it inside her coat. "She gave everything to say the words. I'm not going anywhere she isn't."

The elder looked at Freya. Freya, who had been following perhaps half the conversation, looked back with the frank, slightly anxious expression of a woman meeting a roomful of strangers—which, for her, they were.

"I'm told I used to be a bookkeeper," she offered. "I don't remember much of it. But apparently I was thorough."

A laugh went around the tent—startled, then genuine, breaking the tension like ice going out of a river. And the elder, wise enough to recognize a battle already lost, bowed to Ash, and then—to Sela's surprise—bowed to Freya too.

"The school will need a registrar," the woman said. "Someone thorough."

"Oh no," said Sela.

"Oh yes," said Ash.

* * *

Evening came down over the glass Steppe the color of cooled iron and rose ash, and Sela walked out past the edge of the camp to be alone with the largest administrative task of her life.

She had set up her archive in an abandoned guard post halfway between the human camp and the place where the beast-hosts had begun to drift apart, mission complete, back to their horizons. Already the first beasts had come to *her*, though—curious, formal, bringing things. A raven had deposited a shard of pre-war glass on her desk that morning with an air of filing it. An elk had stood watching her sort papers for an hour, then nudged open her ink pot and left a hoofprint on the margin of page one, which Sela had decided, after consideration, to preserve as the archive's founding seal.

Behind her, footsteps: Freya, wrapped in a blanket against the evening cold, moving slowly, conserving herself. And beside her, Ash, chattering in two languages at a badger who trotted alongside looking profoundly put upon.

"They've offered you a tent in the elders' row," Sela said, without turning. "With honors."

"I know." Freya came to stand beside her at the rail. Below, the glass plain caught the last light and threw it back in long rose-colored bands, beautiful the way the Steppe had always been beautiful—indifferently, magnificently, needing nothing from anyone. "I told them thank you. I told them I'd think about it." A pause. "I don't remember whether I was ever good at thinking about things. I seem to have been good at deciding them instantly and writing them down."

"That tracks with the historical record."

Freya smiled. Then, quieter: "I read your file today. Mine. From the guild archives—you brought everything, so it's all here. *Nystrom, F. Senior Reconciler. No irregularities noted.* Eleven years, and that's all anyone wrote about me." She turned the phrase over like a coin of doubtful minting. "I keep hoping something in these papers will feel like mine. Most of it doesn't. But there was one entry—an intake record, the day Ash arrived. My handwriting. I wrote *child, unregistered, speaks unknown tongue, assigned to F.N. pending review.* And underneath, in the margin, very small—" Freya's voice caught, just slightly. "Underneath I wrote, *will protect regardless of cost.*"

The wind moved over the glass. Far off, a herd sang to each other across the darkening plain, long low notes that the new covenant carried gently, like hands passing water.

"So I don't remember deciding," Freya said. "But I remember that I decided. And whoever I was—I'd trust her judgment."

Sela looked at her friend—at the stranger with the familiar kindness, the empty house with the lights still on—and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were hot. Eleven years of audits, of border raids, of a brother buried under a flag and a lie, and it took a bookkeeper with no past to finally make her cry.

"Well," Sela managed. "For what it's worth, the archive agrees with her. First entry in the new ledger, already drafted." She held up the page. At the top, in her neatest hand: *Herein recorded everything the covenant must never forget.* And below it, the first line: *Item the first. On the night the thirteenth moon closed, a woman with nothing left gave what remained, and it was enough.*

Freya read it twice. "It needs an accession number," she said at last, and Sela laughed, and the laugh went rolling out over the glass where the beasts picked it up and passed it along, note by note, all the way to the horizon.

Then Ash tugged Sela's sleeve and pointed east, where the dark was gathering.

"Archivist," the child said, with the gravity of a nine-year-old delivering a subpoena. "The ravens have brought you another filing. And I think—" the old language flickered under the words, listening to something on the wind, "—I think this one is a complaint. From the wardstone village. About the guardian."

"What about the guardian?"

Ash's eyes went wide and bright in the dusk.

"He says he's awake now. And he wants to know who's going to write down *his* side of the story."
