## 21. The Blank Page

The ledger had been waiting for me on the desk in the little stone house at the edge of Wardstone Village, and I did not know whose handwriting filled it.

That was the first thing I learned about myself that morning: I wrote a fine, even hand, slanted slightly to the right, the way scribes are taught to write when they expect someone else to read their work someday. The ink was good quality. The margins were generous. Whoever I had been, she had not been careless.

I sat down across from my own past like a merchant sitting down to inspect goods of uncertain provenance, and I opened the cover.

*If you are reading this,* the first page said, *then it worked.*

I remember laughing at that. A short, startled sound, like a bird startled off a fence. It worked. Whatever *it* was, some version of me had planned for this morning, had sat in this same chair with this same lamp and thought: *she will wake up with holes where her life used to be, and she will need instructions.* And then, being who she apparently was, she had written instructions. Numbered them. Cross-referenced them.

*Item one,* the page continued. *Your name is Freya Nystrom. You will want to check this, because you won't believe it. Check it anyway. It's true.*

I checked it against the second page, which confirmed it, and against the third, which added: *You gave up your surname once already, years ago, when you joined the Meridian Guild. You took it back for the covenant. It cost you more than you knew at the time. It is yours now. Keep it.*

My hands were shaking. Not from grief—not yet. From the strange vertigo of being handed yourself by a stranger who happened to have your fingers.

*Item two. Ash lives in the east room. Ash is not your child, though you may find you love the child as if—no, I won't presume. Ash will explain what we did. Let Ash explain. Don't read ahead out of pride. Pride was always your worst ledger-entry.*

I put the book down.

Through the window, the Glass Steppe went on being the Glass Steppe: pale grass gone silver at the tips, the great plain of fused sand glittering under a sky so wide it seemed boastful. Somewhere out there, men and women who had come to kill each other were instead arguing about water rights and grazing boundaries, which Sela assured me was what peace looked like up close—less a choir, more a very long committee meeting. Somewhere out there, beasts walked openly among them.

And here, in a small house that smelled of bread and lamp oil, a woman I did not know was learning herself from her own notes.

* * *

Ash found me an hour later, still at the table, three pages deep and crying without any particular drama about it—the way rain falls, steadily, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

"You're at the part about Mother," Ash said. Not a question. Ash had always known things by smell, by sound, by some sense older than either. Now the child spoke our tongue fluently and the old one too, and could tell from halfway across a room which page of a book a person had reached. "Everyone cries at the part about Mother."

"I don't know who Mother is," I said. "That's rather the point."

"No." Ash pulled a chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded on the backrest, chin down—a posture I apparently permitted, since the chair was worn smooth on the backrest in exactly that pattern. "You don't *remember* her. That's different. Read what she wrote about her. You'll see."

*She*, meaning me. Ash had settled into this habit within days of my waking: referring to Before-Freya in the third person, gently, as though speaking of a departed aunt who had left us her furniture. It should have hurt. Mostly it helped.

I read.

*Item nine. Your mother's name was Ingrid Nystrom. She had a laugh like a door hinge that never got oiled because she said oiled hinges made a house feel dishonest. She smelled of tallow and thyme. She died when you were eleven, of the winter fever, and you spoke to her bees afterward for two years because they were the only ones who would talk about her.*

I read it twice. Three times. The words sat on the page like facts in a ledger—accurate, no doubt, itemized, correct—and behind them, where a memory should have stood, there was only a clean white space, swept and ready, waiting for a tenant who was never coming home.

"I can't see her," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. Bookkeeper's voice. "I can't see her face. I know she never oiled hinges. I know she kept bees. I know—" I stopped, because the next thing I knew was that I didn't know anything else, and saying it aloud felt like reading a debt aloud in front of the debtor's family.

Ash reached across the table and put one small hand flat on the open page, over the word *bees*.

"The hive's still at the old guild warehouse," Ash said. "Sela had them brought out before they burned the records wing. They're in the village now, behind Torvald's smithy. They remember you." A pause, and then, with the terrible precision children reserve for the people they love most: "They asked about you yesterday. I told them you'd be along."

"What did they say?"

"That you used to bring them crushed apples in winter." Ash smiled, and it was an old smile on a young face, patient as stone. "They said you'd forgotten, but the apples weren't for them anyway. They were for you. So you'd have somewhere to stand that remembered you, even when you couldn't."

I am told—I have it on excellent authority, entered in my own hand—that I was once a woman who hid every feeling behind a column of figures. Apparently some habits survive the destruction of everything else, because I closed the ledger, squared its edges against the table, and said, "Well. Then I suppose I owe some bees some apples."

* * *

The ledger itself deserves a description, because it was a strange object even by the standards of a year that had contained a war ending mid-sentence.

It was not one book but a system, as befitted its author. The main volume held the items—name, history, people, debts, warnings. But tucked into its back cover were loose pages, dozens of them, each headed in that same right-slanting hand:

*THINGS THAT CANNOT BE REPLACED — READ WITH CARE*

*THINGS THAT CAN BE RELEARNED — READ WITHOUT FEAR*

*PEOPLE WHO WILL CLAIM TO KNOW YOU — TRUST THEM, THEN VERIFY*

Under the first heading, in smaller writing, almost apologetic:

*Your mother's face. The sound of the guild bell on your first day (you'll keep reaching for it; let yourself reach, it fades slower if you don't fight it). The taste of the salt-storm air the night Ash arrived—you'll want it, it was the beginning. Your own arrival day at the Guild. I'm sorry. I spent that one buying the words for "covenant" and "witness." It was worth it. I'd spend it again. That's not comfort, I know. Write it down anyway.*

I sat with that page a long while. Outside, someone was arguing cheerfully about fence posts in three languages at once, two of them human.

Then I turned to the second list, and here my predecessor's tone changed entirely—warmed, loosened, almost playful, as though writing these entries had been the first fun she'd allowed herself in years:

*Things that can be relearned: Bread-making (you were mediocre at it anyway; Ash is better; surrender). The Guild's entire pricing system (good riddance, though it may prove useful—see Item fourteen). How to whistle through your cupped hands (Torvald will teach you; he teaches everyone; resist politely for at least a week for form's sake). Which drawer the good pens live in (they live wherever Ash has moved them).*

And at the bottom of that page, alone, underlined twice:

*How to listen.*

*I don't know yet if the gift survived. I'm writing this the night before, and tomorrow I pour out everything, and after that either the beasts will still know me or they won't. If they do—go slowly. The old way of using it cost too much. Find the gentle way. There must be a gentle way; the covenant says so, and the covenant is the truest thing either of us ever wrote.*

*If they don't—if the silence is total—then hear this: you bought a peace with what you paid. Every beast between the mountains sleeps safer tonight because of you. A silence that holds a sleeping child is not an empty silence. Learn to live in it the way you once lived in ledgers: carefully, and finding the beauty in the columns.*

I closed the book again.

I had been avoiding the window for an hour, and I stopped avoiding it.

* * *

The horse was in the paddock behind the house, and it had been watching me all morning.

I noticed it the way you notice weather—with the back of the mind, without deciding to. A big grey mare, steppe-bred, coarse-maned, standing at the rail with her ears swiveled toward the house like two small attentive flags. When I finally stepped outside with the ledger under my arm, needing air and space and a horizon, her head came up.

Here is what I must make you understand: I did not know her. I had no memory of horses at all—of stables, of harness, of the particular smell of them. My knowledge of horses began and ended with the word *horse*, the way a man who has lost his sight retains the word *sunset*.

But she knew me.

She came along the rail at a walk, then a trot, and stopped in front of me with her breath steaming in the cold, and she looked at me with one dark, liquid eye, and she said—in the way horses say things, which is less words than weather, less speech than a change in pressure across the whole sky—

*Little-speaker. Little-speaker, you came back thin.*

And the world tilted, and righted, and I was crying again, and this time I let it be dramatic about it.

"You know me," I managed.

*We all know you,* she said, and her voice was gentler than any voice I had ever heard, or perhaps it was the same voice I'd always heard and the gentleness was new in *me*. *The deep-speech went out of you like a river going out with the tide. But the tide leaves the shape of the riverbed, little-speaker. You are carved for listening. Nothing that carves a person ever fully fills back in.*

She pushed her great blunt face against my chest, nearly knocking me over, and I put my hands on her neck—at once familiar, muscle-deep, my hands knowing exactly how to hold a horse even though the rest of me was a stranger in its own skin—and I laughed, wetly, stupidly, into her mane.

"I don't remember you," I told her. "I'm sorry. I don't remember you at all."

*Good,* she said.

I pulled back far enough to stare at her. "*Good?*"

*You remember everyone you ever met,* she said reasonably. *That is why you had nothing left for yourself. Start over with me. Do it properly this time. My name is Brindle-Weather, I am eleven years old, I dislike dogs and flattery, and I have carried you across the White Steppe twice, both times when you were too stubborn to admit you were lost.* She snorted, and warm air fogged my collar. *Now you know one thing about yourself that isn't written in a book: you are loved by a horse who thinks you are ridiculous. That is a rarer entry than any miracle. I've known kings. None of them had it.*

* * *

That evening I made my first new entry in the ledger.

I had found the good pens—they were, as predicted, exactly where Ash had moved them—and I sat at the desk with the lamp lit and the bread Ash had baked (mediocre; my predecessor was right) going cold beside me, and I opened to the first blank page after all the items, all the lists, all the careful instructions from the woman who had planned my mourning down to the margins.

For a long time I wrote nothing. Then:

*Item twenty-two. Today I learned that I am loved by a horse named Brindle-Weather, who thinks I am ridiculous. This was not in the ledger. This is the first thing I know about myself that I chose to learn, rather than inherited from the woman who was here before me.*

*I think she would approve. I think she would also tell me to stop writing about myself in the third person, and then do it herself for another six pages.*

Below that, after some thought:

*Item twenty-three. The gift remains. It is quieter. It does not take; it seems only to listen alongside me, the way a friend listens. I do not yet know what it costs, if it costs anything. I intend to find out slowly, and to write down the price of every entry, because I am still, whatever else I am, a bookkeeper—and someone, someday, will need honest figures.*

I set the pen down. Through the wall I could hear Ash humming in the east room—an old song in the old language, the one that had started everything, sung now the way children sing, casually, as if the world had always been safe enough for it.

And then I heard something else, and my pen stopped above the page entirely.

Hooves. Many hooves, coming up the valley road at a pace that meant news. And beneath the hoofbeats, threading through them, voices—human voices calling to each other in excitement, and among them, unmistakable even to my quieted gift, the bright layered chatter of beasts running alongside, unafraid, unhunted, *together*.

Brindle-Weather lifted her head in the paddock and whinnied once toward the road, and the answering call came back across the darkening steppe like a bell.

Ash appeared in the doorway, eyes wide, lantern in hand. "Freya. There are riders—and they're asking for you. They say—" the child paused, listening to something on the wind that I could not yet hear, though I was learning—"they say the beasts from beyond the mountains have sent envoys. To the school. They want the one who speaks to help teach them."

I looked down at the ledger. At the blank pages waiting, row upon row, whiter than the Steppe in winter.

"Well," I said, and picked the pen back up. "Then we'd better start a new volume."
