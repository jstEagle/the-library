## 22. The Choir of the Glass Steppe

The first bell of the morning was a heron.

That was the thing about the school on the glass steppe—no one had ever agreed on what a school bell should be, so we ended up with all of them. The heron flew the length of the wardstone plain at dawn and cried three times, which meant *rise*. The elk stamped twice at midmorning, which meant *the children are wandering off again*. And when the day truly ended, the wind itself came down from the northern ridges and sang through the standing stones until every human and beast within earshot went quiet, whether they wanted to or not.

I knew all three languages now—the human one with its stubborn little boxes of words, the beast-speech that lived in the body more than the mouth, and the old tongue underneath them both, the one I'd been born speaking, the one that had nearly broken the world before it mended it.

My name is Ash. A year ago I could not say that in any language humans understood. Now I said it forty times a day, usually while chasing someone's goat out of the writing hall.

* * *

The school had started, as most impossible things do, by accident.

In the first weeks after the thirteenth moon, when the armies were still camped on opposite sides of the plain and pretending they weren't staring at each other, the exiles' children had begun wandering over to the beasts' side of the glass. Not to fight. Children never fight first; adults have to teach them that. They wandered because the great stone-bound guardian at the heart of the Steppe hummed when it spoke, and humming is irresistible if you are seven years old.

A girl named Petra—her family had lost their farm in the border raids, and she had lost two front teeth to a well ladder—was the first to come back with a word. She marched into the human camp holding a beetle the size of her fist and announced, at full volume, that its name was *Hrrm-veln-of-the-Under-Roots* and that it was very old and slightly deaf and would appreciate being spoken to loudly.

Her mother fainted. The beetle, unbothered, accepted a slice of apple.

By spring there were tents on both sides of the old battle line with no sides at all, just benches, and a roof made of salvaged guild canvas, and a floor of glass that sang faintly underfoot when enough children ran across it. Sela Brann drew up the charter herself, in her auditor's hand, so neat it looked printed: *Here, nothing is taught that cannot also be learned, and nothing is learned that cannot also be taught.* She read it aloud to the first assembly and then added, dry as salt, "Which means yes, Master Hrrm-veln-of-the-Under-Roots may teach the beetling class. No, he will not be grading on a curve."

The beasts thought this was hilarious. Beast-humor runs long and slow, like a river deciding where to go, but it runs deep. The elk still tell the story of Sela's curve, and they still laugh, which sounds like an avalanche being polite.

* * *

I taught the dawn class, the small ones, because the old language comes easiest to the young and hardest to anyone who has already decided how words work. We sat in a circle on the warm glass—warm always, even in frost, the wards remembering their purpose—and I taught them the way Ash-the-mute-child had once tried to teach Freya Nystrom: not meanings first, but feelings. *This word is the color of your mother's voice calling you home. This one is the feeling of snow before it falls.*

The children got it faster than any adult ever had. Adults kept asking what things *meant*, as though meaning were a coin you could hold instead of a river you stood in.

Freya said that was because adults had ledgers in their heads already, full columns written down years ago, and no room for new entries. Freya said a lot of things like that. She was our record-keeper now, and she talked about everything as though it were accounting, which was either a joke or a wound, depending on the day, and often both.

* * *

I found her that morning where I always found her: at the long table under the eastern awning, with her ledger open and her pen moving, and a horse leaning over her shoulder reading upside down.

The horse's name was Bracken. He had been a cart-horse for the Meridian Guild for eleven years, and he was the one who had whispered the first warning about the thirteenth moon, back when Freya still pretended she couldn't hear him. He considered himself the founder of the entire peace. He mentioned this frequently.

"You've spelled my name wrong again," Bracken said.

"I've spelled it the way it's pronounced."

"It's pronounced with dignity."

"It's pronounced *Brak-en*, same as yesterday." Freya didn't look up. Her pen scratched on, steady as rain. "If you want dignity in the historical record, contribute something dignified."

"I warned everyone," Bracken said, with dignity. "Before anyone. Ask the ledger."

"The ledger says you asked for extra oats."

"That was *also* part of the warning."

I sat down across from her and watched her write. This was the thing I did most mornings now, and I wasn't sure either of us had ever agreed to it—it had simply become true, the way rivers become borders. I watched her write because I was the only one who knew what she was really doing.

Anyone else would see a record. Dates, names, decisions, the terms of the covenant copied out fair and complete. But I could feel, through the old language humming in the glass under the table, what each entry cost her.

She was writing down everything she had given away.

Every memory she had poured into the covenant renewal—her childhood kitchen, her mother's face, her own surname, whole years of her life—she had reconstructed secondhand, from other people's accounts and her own careful notes, and entered into the ledger like recovered property. *These things belonged to me. These things are gone. Let no one else pay this price without knowing it first.*

That was the new kind of ledger. Not a record of wonders traded, like the Meridian Guild's warehouse. A record of wonders *kept*—and of what keeping them had cost.

"Stop watching me grieve," Freya said mildly, without looking up. "It puts wrinkles in the prose."

"You're not grieving today."

"How would you know?"

"Because you argued with a horse about spelling. On the bad days you don't argue. You just write."

Her pen stopped. For a moment she was quiet, and the glass beneath us hummed its low patient note, and somewhere out on the plain the heron cried the midday call, which meant *eat something, all of you.*

"There's a blank page coming up," she said finally. "End of the year. I mark the anniversary of—" Her hand made a small circle that meant *everything, the speaking, the losing.* "And I don't know what to put on it. You can't ledger a blank page. Believe me, I've tried. I've drafted it four times."

"What did you draft?"

"*Page intentionally left empty,*" she said. "*See previous entries.* It reads like a coward wrote it."

I reached across and turned her ledger toward me, past a year of her steady handwriting, to the last written page. Then I took her pen.

On the next page, in the old language—in the script that looked less like letters than like weather, like the tracks wind leaves on water—I wrote a single line. Then I translated it underneath, in human letters, so the record would carry both tongues the way the plain now carried both peoples:

*What was given is not gone. What was given is here. Listen to the ground.*

Freya read it twice. Her eyes did the thing they did when she was refusing to cry on principle—went bright and narrow, like a woman auditing her own feelings and finding discrepancies.

"That's not standard bookkeeping," she said.

"No," I agreed. "It's better. It balances."

* * *

Afternoon brought the exchange classes, which were the heart of the school and also, reliably, the chaos.

Today it was humans teaching birds, and birds teaching humans, and both parties discovering that grammar is a matter of opinion. A flock of steppe larks was attempting to explain their song-counting system to a circle of exile farmers, who kept trying to convert it into numbers, and the larks kept politely explaining that songs are not counted, they are *weighed*, and the farmers kept saying *but how much does a song weigh*, and the head lark—a small brown tyrant named Sif—finally landed on the nose of the most stubborn farmer and sang directly into his face until he understood.

He understood, apparently, because he sat down abruptly and said, "Oh. Oh, it's heavy. Songs are *heavy*." And then he wept a little, the way people do when a door opens in a wall they'd stopped seeing.

Sela observed all this from the edge of the circle, arms crossed, making notes. She was the school's adjudicator now, keeper of disputes between species, and she was very good at it, because she had spent years learning to ask the question behind the question. When a wolf-clan and a shepherd couldn't agree on grazing rights, she didn't ask about the grass. She asked what each side was afraid of losing, and it turned out the wolves were afraid of losing the burial ground of their dead beneath the east ridge, and the shepherds were afraid of losing the path their grandmothers had walked, and once those two fears were sitting on the table together, the solution arranged itself in an afternoon: the path would bend, the graves would be fenced in respect, and the first lamb of every spring would be named by the wolves, which the wolves found deeply funny and the shepherds found oddly moving.

"Peace isn't a treaty," Sela told me once, watching the lamb-naming, which involved a great deal of solemn sniffing. "Treaties are paper. Peace is a chore. Somebody has to do it every single day, like hauling water. That's why I keep the schedule."

She did, too. There was an actual schedule, posted by the door, in her auditor's hand. *Monday: covenant recitation, all ages. Tuesday: grievance airing, bring your own grievances, do not bring extras. Wednesday: shared meal, wolves eat first, do not make it strange.*

Nobody made it strange. Mostly.

* * *

Evening came, and with it the thing the whole steppe waited for, though we pretended we were used to it.

The wind came down from the north ridges.

It moved across the glass in a long slow wave, and the standing stones caught it, and the plain began to sing—not words, not exactly, but the shape of words, the covenant renewed a year ago and renewed again every moment since, because that was the secret nobody had understood until Freya spoke it: the covenant was never a document. It was a voice, kept in use. A choir, not a monument. As long as someone spoke it, it held. As long as it was sung, it listened.

The children fell silent first, the way they always did, even the smallest ones who had been born after the war. The beasts lay down where they stood. The humans took off their hats, most of them, except old Marta who claimed the wind had heard her hatless and it hadn't improved anything.

And in the middle of it, as always, Freya stood at the edge of the singing with her ledger closed against her chest, listening with her whole body.

She couldn't remember the day she'd arrived at the guild door. She couldn't remember her mother's face, or her surname, or the taste of the tea she'd drunk the afternoon Guildmaster Vey named his price. Those pages were torn out of her forever, and no covenant, however renewed, gave them back. Some losses are permanent. She had written that line herself, in her own hand, and signed it.

But she could hear the wind carrying her voice—the voice she'd given away, still traveling, still working, woven into the plain's long note like a thread into cloth. Everything she'd surrendered was still *here*, doing its work, holding two peoples together, and I think that was the only arithmetic that had ever let her sleep.

"She's smiling," Bracken whispered to me, over her head. "Put it in the ledger."

"I'm not putting that in the ledger."

"History will want to know."

"History can ask me," Freya said, without opening her eyes. "I'm right here."

* * *

Later, when the singing had faded to the ordinary small noises of evening—pots, laughter, an elk complaining about the quality of the winter hay—we sat together on the eastern bench, the three of us, looking out over the plain. Campfires dotted the dark on both sides of where the battle lines used to be, and there was no telling anymore, from the fires alone, which were human camps and which were beast gatherings. That was the whole victory, right there. Indistinguishable firelight.

"Do you know what I figured out today?" Freya said. She had her ledger open on her knees, the blank anniversary page, my line written at the top of it. "I spent my whole life thinking invisibility was safety. Don't be seen, don't be known, don't be owed. And it worked. Nobody ever hurt me. Nobody ever *found* me." She turned the page gently, like it might bruise. "But nobody ever found me, Ash. Do you understand? All those years, and not one person in the world knew where I was. Including me."

I leaned my head against her shoulder, the way I had the first night, barefoot in a salt storm, when she was the only warm thing in a cold doorway. Some languages don't need translating.

"Now I'm visible," she went on. "Painfully. Half the steppe knows my handwriting. The other half knows my arguments with the horse. I've traded every scrap of privacy I ever hoarded." She paused. "Best bargain I ever made. Terrible rates. Would recommend."

Bracken snorted. Out on the dark plain, the glass caught starlight and threw it back in long silver roads, going everywhere, leading everywhere, and the wind moved over it all, gathering up the day's voices—human and beast and the old tongue underneath, three streams running into one river—and carried them out toward the horizon, where tomorrow was already waiting with its lessons unlearned and its grievances unscheduled and its lambs unnamed.

Peace, Sela liked to say, was a chore. Looking out at the singing dark, at the fires that could not be told apart, at the woman beside me writing tomorrow's entry in a language the world had almost lost and then almost lost again, I decided she was right, and that it was the best chore there ever was.

The steppe sang. We kept it.

And far to the south, past the edge of the firelight, something answered—a single low note from beyond the horizon, a note none of us knew, in a register the glass had never hummed before. Freya's pen stopped. Bracken's ears went forward.

The wind paused, listening too.

Then it carried the stranger's note onward, toward the school, toward the ledger, toward us.
