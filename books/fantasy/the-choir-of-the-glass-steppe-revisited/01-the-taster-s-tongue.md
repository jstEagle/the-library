## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew wanted killing.

Petra Villalobos stood at the queen's elbow with the ceremonial silver spoon—polished so often its handle had gone thin as a wafer—and let the first mouthful sit on their tongue the way a jeweler lets a stone sit in the light. Around them the great hall murmured and glittered, forty nobles pretending not to watch the taster work, which was itself a kind of performance, because of course they all watched. Watching the taster was half the pleasure of eating in this palace. It was theater. It was the crown saying: *see how much we are worth, that someone dies first so we may dine.*

Petra let three heartbeats pass. Two was customary. Three meant *wait*.

The stew was venison and dark ale, rosemary, a whisper of clove, and beneath it—so shyly folded into the fat that a lesser tongue would have called it merely *earthy*—the green, faintly sweet ghost of belladonna. Nightshade. Not enough to fell a horse. Enough to make the queen's hands tremble by the third course and her heart stumble by the ninth, and by then whoever had seasoned the pot would be two provinces away and the palace physicians would be murmuring about *the queen's delicate constitution*.

Clever. Patient. Almost respectful, in its way.

Petra swallowed—swallowing was important; a taster who spat was a taster who started riots—and turned to the head of the table and said, in the flat, uninflected voice they had spent five years sanding down to nothing at all:

"The venison course is off. The kitchen will prepare the capon."

A ripple went through the hall. Somewhere a fan stopped mid-flutter. The queen, a small sharp woman with ink-stained fingers she was never permitted to show at dinner, looked at Petra with the particular expression she reserved for bad news delivered competently—gratitude and resentment in equal measure, like twins in one crib.

"Off," she repeated.

"Off, Your Majesty."

The king did not look up from his plate. The king never looked up from his plate; it was widely understood that the king considered food a tax collected on his body. But the Lord Chamberlain's face had gone the color of the tablecloth, because a poisoning attempt in the queen's own stew meant an inquiry, and inquiries had a way of rolling uphill like barrels, and the Lord Chamberlain had spent thirty years standing exactly where the barrels stopped.

Petra bowed, set down the spoon, and walked out of the hall with the measured, unhurried gait of a person who had never once in their life been interesting. This, too, was craft. Panic was a scent; it carried. Petra had learned to be unscented the way other people learned to whistle.

* * *

The kitchens, by contrast, were honest. Kitchens were always honest—hot, loud, smelling of onions and ambition—and Petra loved them the way one loves a loud, blunt cousin who says out loud the things everyone else is thinking. The moment the door swung shut behind them, the noise hit like a warm wave, and Cook Odile looked up from a mountain of capons with the expression of a woman who had already heard.

"Nightshade," she said. Not a question. Odile had fed three queens and buried two kings and could read a room's trouble the way she read a rising dough.

"In the stew. Well-hidden. Whoever it was knows the palace larders."

"Then it's someone with palace hands." Odile said it lightly, the way you'd comment on weather, but her eyes did the real talking: *be careful, little shadow, be careful.* She flipped a capon with unnecessary violence. "Capon it is, then. Go stand in your corner and be decorative."

Petra's corner was a stool by the spice racks, where a taster could observe the whole kitchen while appearing to observe nothing. They sat. They folded their hands. And beneath the left sleeve of their uniform—grey wool, deliberately unremarkable, one of seven identical garments—a small stitched charm lay against the inside of their wrist, warm as a sleeping bird.

A knot-charm, hedge-made, stitched from three threads: red for luck, black for silence, blue for the finding of lost things. Forbidden twice over. Once by the Edict of Clean Hands, which made the sale of charms punishable by drowning. And once, privately, by Petra themselves, who had sworn—five years and one river ago—that they were finished.

The charm was not for sale. That was the distinction Petra clung to on the bad nights. Selling was the crime. *Keeping* was only sentiment.

It was also, they admitted, in the privacy of their own skull, a lie with excellent posture. Because last Tuesday they had sold a fever-knot to the laundress Marta, whose daughter was burning up in the tannery quarter, and the Tuesday before that a finding-charm to a guard whose brother had gone missing on the steppe road, and the Tuesday before that—well. Tuesdays were busy. Petra's prices were absurdly low and their customers were the invisible people of the palace: the scullions, the grooms, the women who scrubbed the floors the nobles walked on. The crown had hoarded magic so thoroughly that a stitched knot of thread was worth more to these people than a week's wages, and Petra, who tasted poison for the people who had outlawed mercy, could not seem to stop handing it out at a loss.

*You are a hypocrite with a spoon,* their mentor used to say, delighted, the one person who had ever found Petra's contradictions charming rather than dangerous.

Petra's hands went still in their lap.

Five years. Five years since the river.

* * *

Maren had been a hedge-witch the way an oak is a tree: entirely, unapologetically, with deep roots. She had lived in a leaning house by the tannery with forty cats she claimed were all named *Cat* and a garden that grew feverfew out of season and a workbench where she stitched charms for anyone who knocked, charging what they had and nothing when they had nothing. She had taken Petra in at nineteen—a starving, furious, half-literate refugee of a bad harvest—and taught them three things.

One: the taster's discipline, though she'd framed it as cooking. *A tongue is a muscle, little shadow. Feed it bitterness until bitterness is only information.*

Two: the stitching. *Magic isn't a shout, it's a whisper you put into things. The crown thinks it can own a whisper. The crown is a fool with a very large mouth.*

Three: when to run.

She had failed at the third. Or Petra had failed to learn it. The histories disagreed, and Petra had had five years to rehearse both versions.

They had come at dusk, the Inquisitor's men, four of them and the Inquisitor behind them on a grey horse—Casimir Vane, though Petra hadn't known his name then, only the grey coat and the grey eyes and the terrible unhurried way he had of dismounting, as though the law were a thing he carried in his pocket and could unfold at leisure. Maren had heard the hooves before Petra had. She had pressed a knot-charm into Petra's hand—red, black, blue—and shoved them out the back window into the nettles with a force no small old woman should have owned.

*Run,* she'd said. And then, because she was Maren: *and don't you dare come back until you've learned to be boring.*

Petra had run. From the reed bank two fields over, chest heaving, thorns in their palms, they had watched the torches, and heard the reading of the Edict—*for the practice and peddling of unlicensed craft, the penalty is the river*—and heard Maren say something back that made the soldiers laugh uneasily. She had gone into the water still talking. Petra had never learned what she said. They had spent five years not learning it, and the not-learning sat in their chest like a stone they had grown around, the way a tree grows around a fence post, until you couldn't tell anymore where the tree ended and the fence began.

The Inquisitor had looked at the reed bank. Petra was certain of it—certain in the way of dreams, which is to say unprovable. He had looked at the reeds where a nineteen-year-old crouched with a stolen charm burning in their fist, and he had turned away, and mounted, and ridden off, and Petra had never understood why, and had built five years of survival on top of that unexplained mercy like a house on a single unexamined stone.

Afterward: the palace. The tasting trials, where they starved a hundred applicants and fed them graduated doses of the common poisons until the survivors' tongues were maps of bitterness. Petra had passed on the fourth day, mostly because Maren's training had already done what the trials took a week to do. The crown believed it had made Petra's tongue. The crown was wrong, but the crown was used to being wrong; it simply never checked.

And the charm Maren had pressed into their hand—Petra had stitched it into their sleeve the first night in the servants' quarters and had never taken it out. Red for luck. Black for silence. Blue for the finding of lost things.

*What was lost?* Petra had asked her once, about the blue thread.

*Everything worth having,* Maren had said, and threaded her needle, and would say no more.

* * *

The capon went out on schedule. The queen ate, and did not die, and the hall's tension dissolved into the particular relief of rich people who have been reminded that the world contains danger and that they have purchased protection from it. Petra tasted each course—soup, sweetmeats, the little honeyed pears the queen loved—and found nothing but food, which was its own quiet satisfaction, the pleasure of a night with nothing in it.

It was nearly midnight when the kitchens began to empty. Odile pressed a heel of bread and hard cheese into Petra's hands on the way out—"you're all ribs and secrets, child, eat"—and Petra walked home through the servants' gate, past the sleeping gardens, past the high pale wall of the palace where, if you knew where to look, the old ward-stones sat in their sockets like teeth in a jaw. The border wards. Everyone knew the wards; nobody thought about them; they were the sky, they were the walls, they were the reason the steppe's wild things stayed on the steppe. They hummed, very faintly, if you pressed your palm to the stone—a hum Petra had always privately thought sounded less like machinery and more like a held note. Like a choir holding its breath.

Tonight, passing the wall, Petra noticed the hum had changed. Thinner. A singer going slightly flat and no one correcting them.

They stopped. Pressed their palm flat against cold stone. The note wavered under their hand like a candle in a draft, and something in Petra's chest—some animal inheritance from Maren, from the hedge, from every whispered charm—lifted its head and *listened*.

Above the palace, above the city, above the whole stitched and guarded world, the first stars should have been out.

Petra looked up.

The sky was black as the inside of a closed mouth.
