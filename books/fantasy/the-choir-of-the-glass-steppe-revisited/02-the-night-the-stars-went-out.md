## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Petra had always assumed the end of the world would be louder.

They stood in the middle of Tanner's Row with their palm still pressed to the wall, waiting for the sky to correct itself. The city went on around them—drunkards singing off-key three streets over, a cat arguing with a fishmonger's shutters, the ordinary machinery of a Tuesday night—and above it all, nothing. No stars. Not dimmed, not clouded. *Gone*, as if someone had reached up and pinched them out like candle flames.

Then the wall screamed.

There was no better word for it. The hum that lived in the border stones—the one Petra had been quietly eavesdropping on for five years—shivered up an octave and held there, a note of pure wrongness, and then every ward-stone from the river gate to the Salt Tower cracked at once. Not crumbled. *Cracked*, clean and bright, like a wineglass tapped by a ring, and the sound ran through the ground and up through Petra's boots and teeth, and somewhere in the dark beyond the walls something that had been held back for a hundred years took a long, unhurried breath.

The stars did not come back.

Petra walked home very fast, which was difficult, because so was everyone else.

* * *

By the time they reached the crooked lane behind the tanneries, the markets had woken up. Not the night markets—those were always awake—but the *charms*. And that was the problem.

Petra felt it before they saw it: a warmth against their sternum where the little stitched sigil lay hidden under their shirt, the one they wore themselves because a hedge-witch who didn't trust her own work deserved everything she got. It was glowing. Not faintly, not the shy ember-glimmer that a well-made charm produced when it worked. Glowing like a coal blown open, like a lantern with opinions, throwing green-gold light through the weave of their shirt for anyone to see.

They ducked into a doorway and yanked their collar shut and swore, softly and comprehensively, in the language Maren had taught them—the one with all the words for weather and none for forgiveness.

Down the lane, the market was lit up like a festival gone wrong. Every charm Petra had sold in the past five years was answering the night. The fever-knots in the apothecary's window burned steady green. The luck-braids hanging from the fishwife's awning pulsed in time with each other, a string of tiny heartbeats. Old Widow Sarr's protection-hitch, the one she'd bought for her grandson's cradle, glowed so brightly through the shutters of her house that the whole street could see the shape of it—a small, fierce knot of light in a dark window, like a held breath.

And people were coming out to look.

"Is it the plague?" someone asked. "It's the plague, isn't it. Light-plague."

"It's the wards," said someone else, in the flat voice people use for things too large to hold. "The wards broke. You heard them break. Everyone heard."

"The king will fix it," said a third voice, young and certain, and Petra closed their eyes. That was the sentence that would get people killed, eventually. But not tonight. Tonight the crowd only wanted to stand together in the strange light and be afraid in company, which was the oldest human technology there was, older than any charm.

A hand caught Petra's sleeve. Widow Sarr, wrapped in two shawls, her face lit from below by her own purchase.

"You made this," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low, and her grip was surprisingly strong for a woman of eighty-one years and four fingers.

"Widow Sarr—"

"I know your stitching. I've known since the winter fever." She looked at the glowing hitch in her window, then back at Petra, and her old eyes were dry and level. "Five years I've kept my mouth shut. So you'll tell me true: is my boy safe tonight?"

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Petra thought of the boy—Tam, seven now, gap-toothed, terrified of eels—and of the hitch stitched with salt-thread and patience, and of the fact that whatever had broken tonight, it hadn't broken *downward*. The charms weren't failing. They were shining like things called home.

"He's safe," Petra said. "Whatever this is—it isn't eating anything. It's waking things up."

"And is that good?"

Petra looked up at the starless sky, at the crowd lit green and gold by five years of quiet crimes, and found they honestly didn't know.

"Go inside, grandmother," they said gently. "Bar your door. If anyone official asks, you bought that hitch off a peddler who drowned."

"They hanged the last peddler," Sarr said. "But point taken." She squeezed their wrist once, hard, and shuffled home through the glow.

Petra made it three steps toward their own door before the bells started.

* * *

All night the bells rang, and all night the city talked itself into shapes. By dawn there were nine explanations and a riot forming at the palace gates, and Petra had slept perhaps an hour sitting upright in a chair with every charm they owned wrapped in wool and shoved under the floorboards, where they glowed anyway, a soft green pulse through the gaps in the planks like a heartbeat under a mattress.

They spent the morning doing what they knew how to do: being invisible. They reported to the kitchens, tasted the regent's breakfast (bland; the almond milk was off but not fatally), nodded at the right moments, laughed at the under-cook's joke about the chamberlain's wig, and let the panic wash around them like water around a stone. The other servants wanted to talk about the sky. Petra wanted to talk about the sky so badly their teeth ached. Instead they chopped onions and said "mm" in six different sympathetic registers.

It was nearly noon when the summons came—not for Petra, thank every listening ear, but for the Master of Wards, who went gray as flour and left without his hat. Petra watched him go and understood, with the cold clarity of five years' practice, that the crown had no more idea what had happened than the fishwives did. That should have been comforting. It was the opposite. If the people who built the cage didn't know how it broke, then nobody was coming to put the sky back, and the thing breathing beyond the broken wards was still breathing.

They walked home in the late afternoon through streets hung with light. Someone had started selling bread at triple price "against the dark times," which meant the city was going to be fine; profiteers were the surest sign of continued civilization. A child pressed her face to a window glowing with her mother's luck-charm and waved at the light like it was a relative.

Petra let themselves into their rooms, bolted the door, and stood in the middle of the floor, and finally let the fear arrive properly.

Because here was the truth they'd been stepping around since midnight: the wards breaking was terrible, yes, world-ending, possibly. But some traitorous cellar of their heart had heard that glass-crack note and felt—*relief*. Five years of tasting poison in small doses until their tongue learned treachery by flavor. Five years of selling hope in stitch-form to people who needed it, at prices that wouldn't feed them, in a city where the same act was punishable by drowning. Five years of watching Maren go into the river with her hands tied and her mouth full of the song she refused to stop singing, and keeping their own mouth shut, and getting very good at shut.

If hiding was over, then the exhausting part was over too. Whatever came next, they wouldn't have to be invisible anymore.

That relief lasted exactly as long as it took to hear footsteps on the stairs.

Not hurried ones. Unhurried footsteps were worse. Hurried footsteps belonged to neighbors and bailiffs; unhurried ones belonged to people who already knew the door would open.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Then a voice, level and courteous as a court summons:

"Taster Villalobos. Inquisitor Vane. Might we speak?"

Petra stood very still. Under the floorboards, forty small charms pulsed like a buried heart, and they could do nothing about it, and he would know it the moment he crossed the threshold, and they both knew he knew.

They opened the door.

Casimir Vane filled the frame the way law fills a room—not large, precisely, but *load-bearing*. Gray coat, gray eyes, the silver quill-and-scale of his office pinned at his collar, and no soldiers behind him, which was either mercy or theater. He looked at Petra the way he'd looked at them once before, five years ago across a drowning-dock, with those same unreadable eyes that had somehow, impossibly, found them innocent of the crime everyone knew they'd committed.

"Inquisitor," Petra said. Their voice came out steadier than they'd budgeted for. "Have you come about the sky?"

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite humor. An acknowledgment that humor had been considered and filed.

"No," he said. "I've come about the wall." He stepped inside without waiting to be asked, and paused, head tilting slightly, like a man hearing music through a floor. His gaze dropped to the boards beneath their feet, where the green pulse showed through the gaps, patient as sunrise.

"Well," Casimir Vane said quietly. "First question, then. How long have they been singing?"

He wasn't looking at the floor anymore. He was looking at Petra, and his hand—Petra noticed with a distant, floating calm—was nowhere near the irons at his belt.

"Because," the Inquisitor said, "from what I've spent the last twelve hours learning, Taster, the crown has been lying to me for thirty years. And you are the only person in this city who might know why."

Under the floorboards, the charms pulsed. Outside, the first bell of evening rang into a sky with no stars in it, and Petra closed the door behind the man who had once spared them, and wondered which of them was walking into the deeper water.
