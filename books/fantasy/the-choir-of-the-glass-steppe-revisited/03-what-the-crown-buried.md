## 3. What the Crown Buried

Casimir Vane had conducted four hundred and eleven interrogations in his eleven years as Inquisitor of the city of Vellamere, and he had never once served tea at one.

He served tea now. He was not entirely sure why.

The taster sat across the table from him in the small stone room beneath the Hall of Inquiry — a room designed, he had always felt, by someone who hated both architecture and people — and watched the steam rise off their cup with the wariness of someone who had spent five years assuming everything edible might be their last meal.

"It's chamomile," Casimir said. "I brewed it myself."

"I know what chamomile smells like," Petra Villalobos said. "That's not the same as knowing what's *in* it."

"Fair." He pushed the honey pot across anyway. "You're under no obligation to drink it. You're not even formally under arrest, which I realize is an unusual sentence to open an interrogation with."

"Is that what this is?"

"That depends entirely on your answers." He opened his notebook — leather-bound, brass-clasped, the one possession he had ever been sentimental about — and clicked his pen. "Let us begin with the simplest question. On the night the stars went out, every charm you have ever sold began to glow. Is that correct?"

Petra's jaw tightened. "If I say yes, am I confessing?"

"You confessed the moment you didn't deny it. You've been confessing since I walked into your shop and you said *oh, it's you* like a woman greeting a debt collector she'd been expecting for years." He turned to a fresh page. "So let us skip past the part where we pretend this is about charms. Tell me about the wards."

* * *

Here is what Casimir Vane knew before that night, and what he had built his entire life upon:

There were laws. The laws were written down. The laws were enforced by the Office of Inquiry, which answered to the crown, which answered to the gods, or possibly the other way around; the paperwork on that point had always been ambiguous. Magic was forbidden because magic was dangerous. The border wards were holy. The stars were eternal. And the man who enforced the law could sleep at night, provided he drank less than his predecessor, which was a low bar shaped like a grave.

He had spared Petra Villalobos five years ago. He told himself it was because the evidence was thin. It was thin. A drowned mentor, a burned shop, a single charm-stitch found in a sleeve — circumstantial, all of it. But he knew, in the private room behind his ribs where he kept the things he did not write in notebooks, that he had spared them because they had looked at him during the questioning with the flat, exhausted patience of someone who had already decided how to die, and something in Casimir had recoiled from being the instrument of that particular arithmetic.

He had told no one. Not reporting a suspected hedge-witch was itself a crime. His whole career was a crime he had committed once, quietly, and then buried under four hundred flawless convictions.

And now the stars were gone, and the woman who sold forbidden luck-knots was sitting in his interrogation room drinking his tea, and the only person in Vellamere who seemed to know anything was the one criminal he had ever failed to catch.

"The wards," Petra said slowly, "were not walls."

"No?"

"They were a *song*. A held note. The old choir sang the covenant, and the star-spirits answered, and the answer was the wards — a boundary made of agreement, not stone. The spirits kept the dark things out because we kept our half of the bargain." They turned the teacup without drinking. "Covenants are contracts, Inquisitor. What happens to a contract when one party stops paying?"

"It is voided."

"Mm." A thin smile. "Now ask yourself what the payment was."

* * *

Casimir did what he always did when a case refused to close: he went to the archives.

The Royal Archive occupied three floors beneath the eastern wing of the palace, and it was staffed by exactly one archivist, Brother Aldous, who was ninety-one years old, mostly deaf, and had survived six monarchs by the simple strategy of forgetting where everything was whenever anyone important asked. Casimir liked him enormously.

"Aldous. I need records from the reign of King Osric the Second."

"Osric." The old man squinted. "The grandfather. Bad knee, good singing voice, if the portraits flatter true."

"The grandfather. I need everything from the year the wards were raised. Construction records, treasury disbursements, correspondence."

"Correspondence's sealed."

"By whose order?"

"His Majesty's grandfather's order, funnily enough." Aldous shuffled toward a shelf with the unhurried gait of a man who had learned that urgency was a young person's problem. "Sealed for a hundred years. Unseals in..." he counted on his fingers, gave up, "...a while yet."

"Aldous."

"You'll want the third sub-cellar. Everything sealed goes in the third sub-cellar." He paused at the stair. "Terrible damp down there. Ruins the ink. Half those documents are probably illegible by now. Shame." And he went back to his desk, humming, having seen nothing whatsoever.

Casimir descended with a lamp and a growing coldness in his stomach that had nothing to do with the damp.

* * *

The third sub-cellar smelled of mildew and old iron. The shelves ran back farther than the lamp light reached, and the boxes were labeled in a clerk's hand so precise it looked printed: *WARD MAINTENANCE. WARD MAINTENANCE. WARD MAINTENANCE.*

He pulled the earliest box. Inside: requisition orders. Not for stone, not for timber, not for the silver and salt that ward-workings traditionally required.

For *chains*. Forge-chains, star-tempered, in quantities that made no sense for any border. For cages — the word appeared plainly, *cage, reinforced, thirty feet*, followed by a cost figure that could have fed a province for a winter. For a choir's worth of silence: dismissal papers for the entire Royal Choir, dated within a week of each other, each stamped with the same phrase in Osric's own hand.

*No longer required.*

Beneath the papers lay a ledger, water-swollen but legible, titled in the same clerk's careful letters:

*Disbursements — Containment of the Star-Fire, Year One of the New Arrangement.*

New arrangement. As if it were a trade policy. As if the sky were a supplier to be renegotiated with.

Casimir read until his lamp guttered. He read treasury figures that showed the crown siphoning the covenant's power — the star-fire that should have flowed freely between realm and heaven — into reservoirs beneath the palace foundation. He read a memorandum, unsigned but unmistakably royal, weighing the costs of the covenant against its benefits and concluding, in language of impeccable bureaucratic calm, that *the annual tithe of magic to the star-spirits represents an unacceptable transfer of sovereign resources to non-sovereign parties*, and recommending instead *the permanent retention of said resources, with the wards sustained by alternative means until such time as the spirits' cooperation becomes unnecessary.*

Alternative means. The wards had lasted sixty years on stolen fire, burning down the reserves like a merchant spending his grandchildren's inheritance. And when the reserves ran dry —

The stars went out. The wards shattered. Because there was nothing left to steal.

Casimir sat down on a crate of chains and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it at all. Four hundred and eleven interrogations. Every hedge-witch dragged to the drowning dock, every charm burned, every child warned away from the old songs — all of it in defense of a law whose first and greatest violator wore the crown. He had spent his life enforcing a contract breach. He had *drowned people* for the smaller version of the king's own crime.

He thought of Petra Villalobos's face when they'd said *what happens to a contract when one party stops paying*, and understood they had known. Of course they had known. The hedge-witches always knew; that was why the crown feared them. Not because their charms worked. Because their memories did.

* * *

He brought the ledger to the next conversation. He told himself it was evidence-gathering. He told himself a great many things over the following days, in the tone of a man rehearsing testimony he doesn't believe.

Petra came to the Hall of Inquiry voluntarily, which was its own small scandal — a wanted charm-seller walking in the front door, nodding to the guards like a regular. They looked tired. Everyone looked tired lately; the starless nights ground people down like river stones.

"You found it," they said, before he'd even sat down, eyes going straight to the ledger under his arm.

"How could you possibly—"

"You smell like the third sub-cellar. Mildew and iron. Everyone who comes up from there smells like that." They accepted tea this time. Progress, he supposed. "Well, Inquisitor? Say it out loud. I find people believe things better when they hear themselves say them."

Casimir set the ledger on the table between them. He chose his words the way he chose them in court — carefully, precisely, aware that whatever he said next would be entered into a record somewhere, even if the record was only his own conscience.

"The wards were powered by a covenant between this realm and the star-spirits. King Osric broke the covenant deliberately. He caged the star-fire beneath the palace and spent sixty years consuming it. The wards fell because the theft was complete." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Every execution I have ever carried out was the enforcement of a law broken first, and worst, by the throne."

Petra said nothing. They let the silence do its work, which was, he realized, exactly what a good interrogator would have done to him.

"I have a duty," he said at last. "Eleven years. I have a duty to the law."

"Do you?" Petra wrapped both hands around the teacup. "Or do you have a duty to the thing the law was supposed to protect? Because those stopped being the same thing sixty years ago, and somebody in this city has to decide which one they serve." They leaned forward. "You spared me once, Inquisitor. Five years ago, for reasons you've never said out loud. I've wondered ever since whether it was mercy or cowardice."

"And which do you think?"

"I think it doesn't matter anymore. I think you've just discovered the law itself is the crime, and now you get to find out what *you* are." They stood, drained the tea at last — chamomile, unpoisoned, he noted with distant professional satisfaction — and paused at the door. "They'll come for me soon. Properly, this time. Warrants signed, guards sent. When they do, you'll have to choose fast, and I suspect you already know which way you'll jump, and it terrifies you."

It did. It terrified him precisely as much as the ledger did, which was to say: completely, and with a strange, vertiginous relief underneath, like a tooth finally coming loose after years of aching.

"One more question," Casimir said. "The night the wards fell. Where will you be?"

Petra smiled — the first real one he'd seen from them, tired and crooked and utterly unrepentant.

"Gathering a choir," they said, and left him alone in the room with the ledger, the cold tea, and the long fall of everything he had believed, waiting to see whether he would report it — or follow.
