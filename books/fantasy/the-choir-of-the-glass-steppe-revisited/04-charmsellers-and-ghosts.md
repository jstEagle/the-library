## 4. Charmsellers and Ghosts

The knock came at the wrong hour, in the wrong rhythm, from the wrong hand.

Petra knew every knock in the Saltmarket. The widow Osk's three quick raps meant fever-charm, paid in onions. The cooper's son knocked twice and coughed, which meant he'd been caught again with someone's wife and needed something to make a husband forget. But this knock was official — measured, patient, spaced like a man who had all night and a warrant.

Petra blew out the candle, swept the charm-stitch off the table and into their sleeve, and went out through the window that had never once been locked, because a locked window is a confession.

They were three streets away and climbing when they heard the door give way below, followed by the particular silence of soldiers finding an empty room with a warm candle stub. Petra crouched in the shadow of a chimney and let themselves feel it: not fear exactly. Grief. Five years of careful invisibility, gone in one evening, sold for whatever the crown paid informants these days. It had been the cooper's son. Of course it had been the cooper's son. Petra had made his forgetting-charm twice, and both times had told him, gently, that charms don't fix what honesty could.

People never forgave you for being right about them.

* * *

Dawn found Petra on the roof of the tannery, which smelled like a crime against the nose but had the advantage of being a place no soldier would search voluntarily. From there they could see the city waking up under its starless sky, and it still looked wrong — like a face missing its eyes. People had hung lanterns higher than usual. Some had painted false stars on their shutters, little white daubs of hope, and Petra found this so heartbreaking and so human that they had to sit down on the hot leather roof for a while.

One starless night, and already people were making charms against the dark. That was the whole trouble with people, Petra thought. They couldn't not.

Which meant the old choir couldn't stay scattered. Which meant Petra — who had spent five years perfecting the art of belonging to no one — was going to have to go find people.

"I hate this," they informed the tannery roof. The roof declined to argue.

* * *

Maren Ostrova lived above her own disgrace, in a narrow room behind the Conservatory where she had once been first violinist to the court itself, before the night she played a song in public that no one had taught her and couldn't have learned anywhere alive.

Petra climbed her stairs and knocked the way you knock on a coffin lid: respectfully.

"Go away," said Maren through the door. "I'm dead. I've been dead for eleven years. Dead women don't answer doors."

"Dead women don't drink," Petra said, hearing the clink of a bottle inside. "I can hear you pouring."

A long pause. Then the door opened on a woman who might once have been beautiful in the way a storm is beautiful — all angles and weather. Her hair was gray and defiant. Her left hand, the one that had held the bow, was wrapped in bandages.

"You're the taster," she said. "The one Vane didn't drown."

"The same."

"He'll be sorry he didn't." She looked Petra over with the frank appraisal of someone who had survived court by reading faces. "You're here about the sky."

"I'm here about the song."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — hunger, or terror; with musicians it was often the same thing. "No."

"Maren—"

"No. You know what they did to my hand? Not cut it off. That would have been honest. No, they broke each finger separately, very slowly, and then a chirurgeon set them wrong, on purpose, so I'd remember every time I tried to hold anything." She raised the bandaged hand like a lantern. "Eleven years, and it still won't close properly. And you want me to play."

"I want you to sing," Petra said. "It doesn't need fingers. It needs a voice that remembers the verse. Yours is the only one left that heard the covenant whole."

Maren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, rusty as a hinge in a drowned house. "And what happens to me when we're caught? Because we will be caught. We're always caught. That's what the drowning pool is *for*."

Petra leaned against the doorframe, tired down to the marrow. "Look at the sky, Maren. There's no *caught* anymore. There's no law left standing between us and whatever comes through those broken wards. The crown broke the covenant to keep magic in a cage, and now the cage is open and empty and we're all standing in it. You can die hiding in this room drinking yourself toward your hand, or you can die singing. I know which one I'd pick."

"That's not fair."

"No," Petra agreed. "None of this is. Fairness died with the stars."

Maren stared at them for a long moment. Then she unstoppered the bottle, poured one more measure, drank it, and set the bottle down with the finality of a woman closing a coffin on her former self.

"The second verse," she said quietly. "That's my fragment. The rising line. My grandmother sang it to me and swore me to secrecy, and I hated her for it my whole life, and now—" She stopped. "Give me an hour. I'm not going anywhere looking like a corpse. If I'm going to be hanged — drowned, whatever — I'll look better than the Inquisitor when they do it."

* * *

The bone-carver worked in the fishmarket's shadow, and you found him by listening.

This was the joke everyone in the low city told: Old Sefa is deaf as a post, but he hears everything. He was a small, brown, wrinkled man with hands like driftwood, and he carved vertebrae and knucklebones and whistles, and he had been deaf since a fever at age six, and he had been carving bone-songs for forty years that nobody in authority had ever thought to forbid, because how do you suspect a deaf man of singing?

He saw Petra coming, of course. He always did. He read lips across a crowded room the way other men read broadsheets.

*You're running,* he signed, before Petra had even sat down at his stall. His sign-language was his own invention, half trade-gesture, half pure Sefa, and Petra had learned enough of it buying charm-bones from him over the years.

"The whole city knows I'm running. It has a knock now."

*The cooper's boy.* Sefa's signs were sharp with contempt. *I carved his mother's luck-bones. This is what luck gives.*

"I need the third verse, Sefa. The deep line. The one that goes in the bones."

Sefa went still, the way water goes still before it freezes. Around them the fishmarket roared, oblivious. When he signed again, his hands were slower, heavier.

*You know where that verse lives?*

"In the riverbed. With the others."

*With the drowned.* He looked at his own hands, the carver's hands, the hands that had spent a lifetime making the dead speak. *There is a price for asking the river for anything. You know this.*

"I know."

*I will pay it,* he signed, simply, the way another man might say *I'll get the door.* *It is what my hands are for.*

"Sefa—"

*Petra.* He smiled then, and it transformed his whole ancient face. *All my life I have carved songs for other people's dead. Let me go meet some of mine.*

* * *

It was Maren, waiting with Petra in the fishmarket's shadow while Sefa closed his stall, who said the thing that undid them both.

"There's a fourth voice," she said. "I heard it two nights ago, after the stars went out. A child, singing in the square where the fountain froze. Singing the *fourth* verse, Petra. The binding line. The one that was lost."

"That's impossible. The fourth verse died with the choir."

"I know what I heard." Maren's ruined hand flexed inside its bandages. "It wasn't learned. That's the horror of it. Nobody taught her. She just — opened her mouth and there it was, like the song had been waiting thirty years for a throat young enough to carry it."

They found her by following the sound.

She was maybe eight years old, sitting on the frozen fountain's rim in the gathering dusk, swinging her legs, singing a melody that made the hair on Petra's arms stand up and the charms stitched in their sleeve glow like embers through cloth. She had a round, serious face and flour on her cheek, as though she'd been pulled away from her family's bakery mid-chore by something larger than chores.

When she saw them staring, she stopped mid-phrase and regarded them with the flat, unimpressed gaze of a cat.

"You're the ones the song wants," she said. "It's been buzzing in my teeth for two days. Very rude of it. I have bread to deliver."

"What's your name?" Petra managed.

"Nell. What's yours?"

"Petra."

Nell considered this. "You sing wrong," she announced. "Not badly. Wrong. Like you're holding the song at arm's length because you're scared of it." She hopped off the fountain, landed lightly, and brushed off her skirt with the air of a duchess concluding an audience. "The song says you're supposed to lead us somewhere. It says it *very loudly*. Honestly, it's exhausting. Are we going?"

Petra looked at Maren, who was staring at the child with an expression between reverence and seasickness. They looked at Sefa, whose hands had begun, unconsciously, to move — signing along to a melody only he could hear, tears cutting clean lines through the flour dust on his cheeks.

Somewhere across the city, an Inquisitor was knocking on doors. Somewhere beyond the wards, something that used to be held back by starlight was noticing the dark. And here, on a frozen fountain, stood everything left of the oldest song in the world, looking at Petra like they were the answer.

"I never wanted followers," Petra said, mostly to themselves.

"Good," said Nell, taking their hand with the confidence of someone who had never once doubted a stranger. "Followers are heavy. Come on. The song says east."

And far to the east, past the walls, past the wards' shattered edge, the Glass Steppe waited under its starless sky — and in the riverbed between here and there, the drowned were already turning in their sleep, listening for footsteps.
