## 5. The Drowned Choir

*Petra*

The river called the Mourning had no fish. Everyone in Vell said so, and everyone in Vell was wrong about why — they blamed the current, the cold, the silt — because the true reason was the kind of thing you didn't say aloud near water that remembered.

Petra stood on its bank at dusk with four companions, a stolen lantern, and a sack of bread that Sefa the bone-carver had insisted on bringing, though none of them could explain to whom they might offer it.

"You're certain it's here," said Maren Ostell, once first violinist of the royal court, now a woman who played for coins in taverns and flinched when anyone hummed. She hugged her instrument case like a shield. "The verse."

"The drowned keep what they were singing when they went under," Petra said. "It's the one law even the crown couldn't repeal. They tried, actually. There's a statute." They heard their own voice go dry and bitter and stopped.

"Of course there's a statute," Maren murmured.

Beside her, the child — Nell, nine years old, all elbows and terrible confidence — peered into the darkening water with the frank appetite of someone who had not yet learned that rivers were frightening. "I can hear them already," she said. "They're humming. It's very out of tune."

"They've been dead a long time," Sefa said mildly. The old carver's hands were folded atop her walking stick; she was deaf as a stone and read lips like a scholar reads scripture, which meant she watched mouths the way other people watched doors. "Out of tune is honest. In tune is what you do for an audience."

"And what are we doing?" asked Tam, the fourth of them, a former palace guard whose contribution to the expedition so far had been carrying everything and asking this question every hour.

"We're asking a favor," Petra said, "from people we helped drown."

Nobody had anything to add to that, which was itself a kind of answer.

* * *

*Casimir*

Casimir Vane had followed them for two days, and he hated himself in new and inventive ways for each one.

He told himself it was surveillance. He told himself that an Inquisitor of the Third Chair did not need warrants to observe persons of interest, that observation was not complicity, that watching a crime was not the same as standing beside it. He told himself these things while crouched in reeds that smelled like rot and old grief, watching a hedge-witch, a court musician, a deaf grandmother, a child, and a deserter light candles on the bank of the Mourning River.

Five candles. He counted automatically — habit from a hundred interrogations — and then made himself stop counting, because counting was how you built a case, and some traitorous part of him had already stopped wanting a case.

The stars were still gone. Five nights now, a sky like a closed lid, and the city beneath it had gone strange and quiet in its panic. Casimir had spent those five nights reading the archives his office was never meant to open: ledgers of star-fire, requisitions signed by a king's grandfather, a covenant dissolved with the bureaucratic neatness of a man canceling a subscription. And beneath the ledgers, the execution rolls. Four hundred and eleven names. Drownings, all of them, in this river, over sixty years.

He had known, abstractly, that the purges happened. Every Inquisitor knew. What he had not known — what no one knew, because the knowledge had been drowned along with everyone who held it — was that the executed had been singing.

* * *

*Petra*

Sefa waded in first.

She went out to where the water reached her waist, unhurried, her skirts blooming around her like ink, and she set the bread on the surface. It floated. Of course it floated; bread floats; but it floated in a slow circle, deliberately, the way nothing bread does naturally, and Petra felt the hair rise on their arms.

Then Sefa began to sign.

She had explained it on the walk down: the dead can't hear, not properly, not through air. Sound dies at the surface. But water carries pressure, carries rhythm, and hands moving under water speak in pulses the drowned understand better than words. She signed the old greeting — the one carved on bones in markets before the purges, the one Petra sold stitched into sleeves without fully understanding — and the river went still.

Not calm. Still. The difference between a sleeping dog and a listening one.

Maren began to play.

She'd chosen something simple, a lullaby-adjacent fragment, the musical equivalent of knocking politely. Her bow shook for the first three notes and then steadied, and the sound came out rounder than it had any right to be, as if the river were leaning in.

And the river answered.

It started as a hum in the banks, a vibration in Petra's teeth. Then shapes gathered beneath the surface — pale, drifting, indistinct, faces like coins worn smooth. Not rising. Just... attending. Hundreds of them. More. The candlelight bent strangely over the water, and every reflection showed a sky full of stars that no longer existed overhead.

Nell gasped with delight, which was either very brave or very nine years old.

"They want to know who asks," Sefa signed, her hands breaking the surface just enough for Petra to read. "And what we'll pay."

Here it was, then. Petra stepped to the water's edge. Their mouth was dry. Five years of tasting poison had taught them every flavor of fear, and this one — the fear of speaking to the dead you'd failed — was the worst vintage yet.

"My name is Petra Villalobos," they said. "Royal taster. Charmseller. Hedge-witch, if you like. I'm the reason some of you were found out." A ripple moved across the river, neither angry nor kind. "I'm not here to apologize. Apologies are cheap and you've heard enough of them. I'm here because the covenant is broken, the stars are caged under the palace, and we need the first verse — the one you sang going under. The one they couldn't stop you from keeping."

The stillness stretched. Somewhere behind them, Tam whispered, "Is that good silence or bad silence?"

"Shh," said Nell, with enormous authority.

Then the water spoke back — not in words, in weight. Pressure against the shore, a slow pulse, and Sefa's hands flew as she translated, her face unreadable and then, suddenly, not.

"They say the verse isn't free," she signed. "It never was. It's a covenant. Everything is given for something." Her hands hesitated. "They ask what we'll give."

"I have coin—" Tam started.

"It doesn't take coin," Sefa said aloud, and her voice cracked oddly on it, the voice of a woman who rarely used it. She looked at the water for a long moment. Then she looked at Petra, and smiled, small and dry and entirely unafraid. "It takes memory. A song only lives if someone living carries it. They'll give us the verse — but one of us has to stay in the river's keeping until the covenant is sung again. A hostage. A promise with a heartbeat."

"No," Petra said immediately.

"Yes," said Sefa.

"Sefa—"

"Child." She waded toward shore, water streaming off her, and took Petra's face in her cool wet hands with the gentleness of someone who had raised six children and buried two. "I am seventy-one years old. I am deaf, my knees are liars, and I have been waiting sixty years for something worth trading my time for. You need me to reach the shrine — I know the old road-signs, I can read the ward-marks, and you'd be lost in a week without me. So I'll come. But the river holds my name until the singing's done, and if we fail, I stay." She shrugged. "Fair terms. Better than the crown ever offered them."

"That's not fair, that's horrifying," said Maren.

"Maren, dear, I bargained with a ghost once over a carving commission. This is much more reasonable."

The river pulsed again, patient, and Sefa turned back to it, knelt in the shallows, and pressed both palms flat to the surface. When she lifted them, a shape remained in the water-glass above her hands: lines of light, hanging like frost, the first verse written in the old notation — the one that wasn't notes exactly, but instructions for what a voice should *do*.

Nell looked at it and, casually, catastrophically, sang it.

Perfectly. First try. The whole river shuddered, and every drowned face turned toward her at once, and the child blinked and said, "What? I just... it was already in my head. It's always been in my head."

* * *

*Casimir*

Casimir should have lit the signal flare. That was procedure. Persons of interest engaged in unauthorized thaumaturgy: flare, withdraw, report, and by morning there would be forty soldiers on this bank and another roll of names being drawn up.

His hand was actually on the flare when the child sang.

He didn't have words for what it did to the air. It was as if the night had been holding its breath for sixty years and finally exhaled. The river glowed. The dead glowed. And Casimir Vane, who had put seventeen people into that river himself — each one guilty, each one proven, each one *singing*, though he hadn't known to listen — sat down hard in the reeds and discovered he was crying, quietly, in a manner entirely unbefitting his office.

The verse hung in the water-light. The old woman rose from the shallows, laughing, actually laughing, wringing out her skirts. The witch stood with their fists clenched, looking like a person who had just been handed both a gift and an invoice.

And Casimir closed his hand around the flare and did not light it.

He stayed in the reeds until they packed up and moved off along the bank, following the old woman's sure signs, the child humming the stolen verse under her breath like it belonged to her. Then he got up, brushed the mud from his coat, and walked the other way, toward the city, rehearsing the report he would give and would not mean.

*Subject observed. No unauthorized workings confirmed.*

The lie tasted like every poison he'd ever made Petra swallow for the crown.

Behind him, faint across the water, the drowned were humming — still out of tune, still honest — and for the first time in twenty years of service, Inquisitor Casimir Vane hummed along.
