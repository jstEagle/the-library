## 6. Crossing the Steppe

The Glass Steppe did not glitter the way the songs promised. The songs, Petra reflected somewhere past the third day of walking, had been written by people who crossed it in carriages.

Underfoot it was all cracked panes and salt-bright dust, a plain that had once been a sea and now remembered being one only in its posture — flat, patient, faintly hostile. Above them there was nothing at all. No stars. No moon worth the name. Just a ceiling of black so complete that Petra kept catching themselves looking up as if expecting to find a hole in it, some seam where the sky had been mended badly.

Five days out from the riverbed, and the company walked like a sentence someone had forgotten to finish.

Maren the bone-carver went first, reading the ground through her boots and her hands and whatever other sense deafness had sharpened in her. She signed her observations to Odile, who translated them aloud in a voice gone hoarse from wind: *Crack-line ahead. Ice under the crust. Walk where I walk or fall where I don't.*

Then came Tam, the disgraced court musician, who complained with the stamina of a man who had once performed four-hour operas. "I'm just saying," he said, for perhaps the ninth time that morning, "that when I imagined my redemption arc, there were fewer blisters involved."

"You imagined an audience," Petra said.

"I imagined *chairs*."

And then Sefa, the child, who did not complain at all, which frightened Petra more than anything else on this steppe. Sefa simply walked and hummed — the fragment of the covenant she carried, the song nobody had taught her, looping through her like breath. It had been pretty, at first. A small bright thing. Now, each day, it grew a note longer, a shade deeper, and the ground around her feet had begun to frost in patterns too deliberate to be weather.

Petra brought up the rear, because rearguard was where you put the person whose job was to notice things, and noticing things was the only trade they'd ever been any good at. Poison, mostly. But these days: the way Maren's shoulders sat wrong. The way the water skins sloshed lighter than arithmetic said they should. The way their own charms — the little stitched wards they'd sold by the hundreds back in the city — had started glowing in their pack at odd hours, warm as coals, humming a half-beat behind Sefa's song like apprentices trying to keep up with their mistress.

They weren't answering Petra anymore. That was the thing Petra hadn't said aloud yet. Five years of making charms sit up and obey, and now they answered *her*.

* * *

The supplies failed on the sixth day, and they failed honestly, which was almost worse.

There was no disaster. No raid, no spill, no theft. There was simply less than there had been, every morning, in the way of all journeys, until the morning when Odile turned the last sack inside out over the cookpot and produced what could charitably be called flour and uncharitably be called dust.

"Porridge," Odile announced, with the dignity of a woman presenting a state banquet.

"It's soup," said Tam.

"It's porridge-adjacent."

"It's *warm*," said Maren, signing fast, and then added something longer that made Odile laugh and refuse to translate.

"What did she say?"

"She said the drowned choir ate better than this, and they were dead."

Sefa ate her portion without complaint and then, quietly, put half of it back in Odile's bowl. Petra watched her do it. Watched the child's face while she did it — solemn, matter-of-fact, a small accountant of hunger already fluent in scarcity. Nobody had taught her that either. Petra thought of the city, of the markets where their charms had glowed and marked their buyers, of the queen's stew and the nightshade and the long disciplined invisibility of the palace kitchens, and felt the old question rise up in them like bile:

*Is it worth this?*

Is what worth this? The covenant. The singing. The idea that magic could belong to everyone instead of a throne. On paper — in the dark, around fires, in Casimir's careful treasonous voice — it had sounded clean. Out here, with a child giving away her supper and a musician's feet bleeding through his boots and a sky that had been stolen before any of them were born, it sounded like a bill someone else expected them to pay.

That night Petra took first watch, which meant no watch, because there was nothing to watch *for* except —

Except.

Near midnight, low against the eastern horizon, lights moved. Not stars. Stars didn't come back; Petra would have known. These were ground-lights, running lights, a loose constellation of pale fire loping along the distant glass, and Petra's stomach dropped five years in an instant, back to the night the wards shattered, because they knew what ran on stolen star-fire.

Ward-hounds.

They'd been bred beneath the palace, Casimir had told them, from the caged light itself — dogs made partly of law and partly of radiance, set to patrol the borders the covenant used to guard. When the covenant broke, the hounds hadn't been released. They'd been *aimed*. And somewhere behind them, someone royal had finally done the arithmetic on where a taster, a traitor-inquisitor, a bone-carver, a musician, and a singing child would be headed.

"Wake up," Petra said, and hated how loud their own voice sounded under a sky with nothing in it. "Everyone up. We have company."

* * *

They ran for two days.

Not well. Not heroically. They ran the way exhausted people run, in staggered bursts, Maren choosing the line, Odile carrying Sefa when the child's legs gave out, Tam carrying the instruments because he refused not to, and Petra carrying everything else plus the growing certainty that they had led these people into a killing field.

The hounds didn't close. That was the horror of it. They held distance — always visible, never arriving — pale shapes pacing them along the horizon like a deadline. Herding, Petra realized on the second night. Not hunting yet. *Steering.* Someone wanted them tired when they arrived. Someone wanted them desperate enough to deal.

On the third morning Sefa stopped mid-stride, tilted her head, and sang a note that none of them had heard from her before — high, clear, and old, old the way riverbeds are old.

Every charm in Petra's pack lit at once.

Not flickering. Blazing. The stitch-wards in their sleeve, the pocket-charms, the little knotted protections they'd carried since the city — all of them burning like captive dawn, and all of them turned, physically turned in the pack, toward the girl. Toward the east. Toward the shrine.

"Sefa," Petra said carefully, kneeling, "what did you just hear?"

"The rest of it." The child looked at them with those unnervingly calm eyes. "The middle part. It's under the ground here. It's been waiting so long it's *loud*."

Maren signed something rapid. Odile translated, voice cracking: "She says the song is leading us now. She says we should let it."

"And if it leads us into the hounds?" Tam asked.

"Then the hounds are standing on the song," Sefa said simply, "and the song doesn't like being stood on."

It should have sounded like a child's fancy. Instead, twenty paces ahead, the glass of the steppe hummed — audibly, faintly, a struck-note underfoot — exactly where her shadow fell.

Petra stood up slowly. Their hands were shaking, and not from cold.

This was the moment, they understood dimly, where leadership stopped being a thing you declined and became a thing you simply *were*, whether or not you'd consented. Everyone was looking at them. Even the charms were looking at Sefa, but everyone was looking at Petra, waiting for the word.

"We follow the song," Petra heard themselves say. "We follow it all the way to the shrine. And when we get there—" They stopped. Swallowed. The honest ending of that sentence was a thing they couldn't afford to say yet, because saying it would make it real: *when we get there, someone is going to have to pay for all of this, and I am increasingly afraid the price list has my name at the top.*

"When we get there," Petra finished, "we sing better than they do."

Tam laughed — actually laughed, ragged and delighted. "That's the plan? That's the whole plan?"

"It's the only instrument we brought."

* * *

They gained the shrine's ridge on the evening of the ninth day, and the hounds stopped.

All of them. At once. Like puppets whose hand had lifted. They stood in a crescent on the far slope, pale and patient and wrong, and did not pursue, and did not leave, and Petra understood with a cold settling clarity that the steering was over. Everything beyond this point happened inside. Whatever waited at the shrine had been arranged for their arrival, and the hounds were the walls of the room.

Below, in a shallow bowl of glass, the ruin showed itself: broken columns like snapped teeth, a ring of stones worn smooth by centuries of vanished voices, and above it — worst of all — a hole in the sky. An actual absence, a circle of black deeper than the night around it, where the shrine's patron star had been unpicked from heaven like a pearl cut from its setting.

Sefa's song rose to meet it, full and unbroken now, the whole middle verse pouring out of a child who had never been taught it.

And down in the ruins, campfires burned. Several of them. Neat, orderly, provisioned.

Someone had gotten there first.

Petra counted the fires, counted the banners, felt Maren's hand close hard around their wrist, and saw — unmistakable even at this distance, even in this lightless world — the silver-and-scarlet of a royal envoy's pavilion pitched dead center in the shrine, directly beneath the hole where the sky used to be.

"Well," said Tam, into the silence. "I don't suppose anyone packed a second plan."
