## 7. The Shrine Without Sky

Casimir Vane had spent twenty years learning to read rooms, and the shrine was the worst room he had ever entered.

It had been a cathedral once — or something older than that word, some word the founders had drowned along with their mages. The walls still stood in places, glassy black stone veined with faint silver, and above it all gaped the absence: a perfect circle of open sky where a dome should have been, cut clean as if the heavens themselves had taken a bite and left nothing behind. No stars filled it. No clouds crossed it. It was simply a hole in the world, and Casimir found he could not look at it for longer than a few breaths at a time.

The envoy's pavilion sat dead center beneath it, silver-and-scarlet, absurdly festive, like a birthday cake set out in a graveyard.

"Lord Envoy," said the captain of the shrine guard, bowing. "They've been spotted on the ridge. The taster, the child, the musician, the bone-carver. And—" a hesitation, delicate as a scalpel "—your Inquisitor."

"I am not *his*," Casimir said.

"No, my lord." The captain's face did the thing faces did around Casimir these days: the careful blankness of men who had heard rumors about which side of the law he was currently standing on. "Shall we bring them in gently?"

"Gently," Casimir said, "as in without breaking anything."

"As you say, my lord."

He stood at the edge of the camp and watched them come down the switchback path, and he hated how his chest tightened at the sight of them. Petra first, thin as a winter branch, walking like someone who had decided exhaustion was a country they now lived in. Maren the bone-carver behind them, deaf and unbothered, reading the ground with her feet. Tam the musician, who had once played for the king's grandmother and now looked like a man being marched to his own surprise party. And Sefa, the child, skipping. Actually skipping, at the end of the world, because children were built with a kind of courage adults kept misplacing.

Petra saw him and stopped.

"You came ahead of us," Petra said. Not an accusation. Worse — a question, asked carefully, the way you'd test a floorboard over a cellar.

"The envoy travels faster when she knows where she's going," Casimir said. "I didn't tell her. I want that understood. But I also couldn't stop her, and I thought—" He stopped himself. Honesty had become a habit lately, and habits were dangerous. "I thought you'd rather hear what she has to offer from me standing beside you than from across a room full of guards."

"That's either loyalty or strategy," Maren signed, and Tam translated it aloud with relish.

"It's usually both," Casimir admitted.

* * *

Envoy Seraphine Oduya received them beneath the hole in the sky, seated at a table laid with actual linen, actual wine, and small candied fruits arranged in a pattern Casimir recognized as the royal cipher. She was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the smile of a woman who had negotiated three treaties and two abdications and enjoyed all five.

"Petra Villalobos," she said warmly. "And companions. Sit, please, sit. You must be starving — the Steppe is not kind to travelers."

Nobody sat. Sefa stared at the candied fruit with naked longing until Maren put a steadying hand on her shoulder, at which point Sefa stared at the fruit with *disciplined* longing, which was somehow worse.

"I'll be brief," Oduya said, "because I respect you too much to insult you with ceremony. His Majesty is prepared to offer a full pardon. All charges — charmselling, hedge-craft, sedition, the unfortunate business at the riverbed. Beyond that: a title. Warden of the Reforged Covenant. A seat at court. Funding, protection, honors." She spread her hands. "In exchange, you sing the covenant back. Under crown authority. The wards restored, the borders sealed, the star-fire returned to its proper keeping beneath the palace."

"And the cage stays locked," Petra said quietly.

"The *vault* stays secure," Oduya corrected, with the smoothness of long practice. "Magic is dangerous, Warden-to-be. Unregulated magic drowned this city's history. The crown merely asks to remain the regulator."

Casimir watched Petra's face while she spoke, and it was like watching weather change over water. He saw the temptation land — really land, a physical thing, a blow. Five years of tasting other people's food and swallowing fear. A lifetime of hiding charms in sleeve-seams and selling them to desperate women through back doors. *A seat at court.* Safety. Legitimacy. The right to stop running.

He saw Petra want it.

That was the moment, he would think later, that he understood how betrayals actually worked. Not as sudden collapses but as slow thaws — good people, tired people, being offered warmth at exactly the moment they could no longer generate their own.

* * *

It was Tam who broke first.

The old musician had been quiet through the whole exchange, hands folded, eyes closed — listening, Casimir realized, the way he always listened, to the shape beneath the words. Now he opened his eyes and looked at the table, the wine, the linen, the candied fruit, and something in his face crumpled and reformed into a decision.

"I'm seventy-one years old," Tam said. "My hands shake unless I've had drink, my knees sound like the palace gates, and I have slept on wet glass for nine nights." He laughed, a small broken sound. "I have a fragment of the first verse in my head and no one left to leave it to. If I go home with a pardon and a pension, the verse survives. If I go with you—" he gestured at the starless hole above them, at the wind moaning through the ruined walls "—it might not."

"Tam," Petra started.

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't make me a villain, little taster. I'm just an old man choosing the warm room. Kings have built whole religions out of less." He turned to Oduya and bowed, shallow and bitter. "I accept your terms, my lady. I'll sing your covenant. God forgive me, I'll even make it beautiful."

Oduya's smile widened by a precise millimeter. One voice secured. Two remained.

Maren stepped forward before anyone could speak to her. She looked at Oduya for a long moment — the envoy had the grace to look uncertain, having clearly been briefed on how to address a deaf woman and having forgotten every word of it — and then Maren began to sign, fast and hard, and Tam, traitor though he now was, translated anyway, because some services transcended allegiance.

"She says: the vault is a throat. She says the last time the crown held a song, it drowned the singers. She says she has bargained with the dead in the riverbed and the dead were more honest than this table." Tam paused. "She says several things I will not repeat in front of the child."

Sefa, who had been following Maren's hands with the fluency of long friendship, nodded solemnly. "She also said a word I'm not allowed to say."

"*Thank* you, Sefa."

Maren finished with one flat, final gesture — both palms turning outward, pushing away — and stepped back beside Petra, arms folded, immovable as the Steppe itself.

Oduya's warmth cooled by degrees, the way a hearth cools when the door is opened. "Madam Bone-Carver. I admire conviction. But I should tell you that refusal has consequences. The crown cannot permit fragments of the covenant to remain... loose."

"They're not loose," Casimir said.

Every head turned to him.

"They're *hers*," he said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, because inside he felt like the shrine walls looked — cracked, silver-veined, holding. "That's the entire point everyone keeps missing. The song belongs to whoever carries it. That was true before your king's grandfather broke the covenant, and it will be true after. You can take the woman. You cannot take the verse. It's already in the child. It's already in the ground here. You are trying to arrest a melody, my lady, and I have seen what happens when the law attempts the impossible." He thought of the drowning docks. Of his own signature on forty-two warrants. "It drowns."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the ruins, wind moved through a broken arch and made a sound almost like a held note.

Oduya recovered beautifully. "Lord Vane. Your eloquence does you credit. It also constitutes, I believe, open confession of complicity." She sighed, genuinely regretful, which was the most frightening thing about her. "Captain. Take the bone-carver into custody. And place the Inquisitor under watch."

Guards moved. Maren did not resist — she went with her chin up and one last message flung over her shoulder in quick signs that made Tam cover his eyes and Sefa begin, very quietly, to cry.

Casimir let them take his sword. He kept his eyes on Petra the whole time, because someone had to witness this properly, and witnessing, he had finally learned, was the only part of justice he had ever been any good at.

* * *

They were held in separate corners of the camp — Maren bound and guarded, Casimir disarmed and merely watched, the guards around him uneasy, half-convinced he was a trap of some kind. Night came down starless and total. Beneath the hole in the sky, the dark had texture, weight, like standing underwater.

Petra came to him near midnight, slipping past the guard with the ease of five years' practice at being invisible. They sat down against the wall beside him without asking, shoulders almost touching.

"You knew she'd do that," Petra said. "Take Maren."

"I suspected. I hoped otherwise. Hope has a poor record in my line of work." He glanced sideways. "You're not blaming yourself. I can see you deciding whether to."

"Give me a minute. I'm between drafts."

Despite everything, Casimir huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then: "Tam surprised me."

"He surprised himself. That's the sad part. He'll play beautifully for them, too. That's the sadder part." Petra pulled their knees up. In the dark their face was mostly shadow and voice. "I wanted to say yes, Casimir. When she said pardon, when she said *title* — some animal part of me was already packing. Five years of poison and hiding, and she offered me a chair by the fire."

"I know. I watched you want it."

"Is that what you do now? Watch people want things?"

"Lately, yes. It's remarkably educational." He was quiet a moment. "But you said no."

"Not yet."

"You will."

"How do you know?"

"Because I watched your face when they took Maren," Casimir said. "And because I've spent twenty years watching powerful people explain why cages are necessary, and I have never once seen one of them volunteer to sit inside. Restoration means the same song, same vault, same drownings — just with your name carved over the door. You already know that. You've known since the riverbed. Tonight the knowing just got expensive."

Petra was silent for a long time. Overhead, the hole in the sky offered nothing back.

"There's another way," Petra said at last, so quietly Casimir had to lean in to catch it. "Not the old covenant. A new one. A song with no owner — no vault, no throne, no monopoly. Magic scattered instead of caged. Everyone's inheritance or no one's." A breath. "It's never been sung. It might not work. It might unmake everything, including me — the immunity goes into it, all of it, that's the price the old verses hint at. But if it works—"

"If it works, there is no more Warden of the Covenant," Casimir said slowly. "Because there is no covenant to ward. There's only... everyone."

"Now you understand why I haven't told the others yet. Tam would call it madness. Oduya would call it treason." Petra's voice hardened into something he'd never heard from them — not fear, not grief. Conviction, being born, which sounds like both. "I need one night, Casimir. One night where nobody reports to anybody, so the choir can learn it. Can you give me that?"

Casimir looked across the camp at the envoy's tent, where lamplight burned and dispatches waited for the dawn rider — dispatches that would summon a regiment before Petra's new song had learned to stand.

Twenty years ago he had sworn an oath to uphold the law. Six months ago he had discovered the law was the crime. Tonight, apparently, he was graduating.

"I can give you better than a night," Casimir Vane said, rising, rolling his sleeves, feeling lighter than he had since the stars went out. "I can give you a bonfire."
