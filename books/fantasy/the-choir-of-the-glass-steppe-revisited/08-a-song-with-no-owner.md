## 8. A Song With No Owner

The envoy's offer sat in the middle of our camp like a dropped knife.

*Pardon. Title. A place at the king's right hand.* All Petra Villalobos had to do was sing the old covenant back into the sky, with the crown's hands resting warm on their shoulders the whole time, and everything that had been taken would be returned—except, of course, to everyone it had been taken *from*.

I turned the parchment over twice, as if the back might say something different. It did not. It smelled of sealing wax and confidence.

"So," said Marisol, the bone-carver's absence still a bruise between us all. She was sharpening a knife she had no intention of using on anything but her nerves. "Do we write back, or do we burn it?"

"We don't burn it," I said. "We answer it."

That got me four faces at once. Even the child looked up from where she was teaching a beetle to walk in circles, which she insisted was not magic and which I chose not to investigate.

"The old covenant," I said, "was a song with an owner. One owner. The crown held the melody, the star-spirits held the harmony, and everyone else got to listen through a wall." I stood up, because my legs wanted to pace and it seemed rude to let them do it alone. "The envoy is offering us the wall back. Fresh mortar. Our names carved into it."

"And your alternative?" Tam Virel asked. The disgraced court musician had a way of asking questions that made them sound like accusations he'd already rehearsed. He'd been rehearsing accusations against the crown for thirty years; it was nice of him to finally aim one at me.

"My alternative is a song with no owner."

Silence. The good kind, mostly—the kind where people are thinking instead of fleeing. On the Glass Steppe under a starless sky, silence had teeth, but this one was only chewing.

"Magic for everyone," Marisol said slowly, testing the words like coins bitten for gold. "Or no one."

"Everyone," I said. "But honestly? If we can't manage everyone, no one is still better than *one man in a tall hat*. The covenant broke because it belonged to a throne. Thrones fall. Songs shouldn't."

Tam laughed—one short, startled bark, like a dog surprised by its own reflection. "You're proposing treason."

"I'm proposing *music*, Tam. Treason is just what they'll call it when it works."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then something in his face unclenched that I suspect hadn't unclenched since the day the palace dismissed him for playing a lament at a coronation. "Thirty years," he said, "I've been waiting for someone to say something stupid enough to be worth doing."

"Then you'll love the rehearsal schedule."

* * *

We rehearsed in secret, which on the Steppe meant: quietly, badly, and at odd hours, huddled in the lee of a glass ridge that hummed faintly when the wind crossed it wrong.

The child—I'd stopped calling her *the child* in my head around the time she started correcting my pitch—had the first verse whole. She said it lived behind her ribs, "like a swallowed bell," and when she sang it the ground remembered being younger. Tam had fragments of the descant from his years at court, where the choir had sung a hollowed-out version of the covenant every solstice without knowing what they were amputating. Marisol contributed the deep drone, learned from bones, which is exactly as unsettling as it sounds and twice as effective.

And me? I had the bridge. The part nobody had ever sung, because the old covenant never needed one—it went straight from throne to sky and left out the part where ordinary people hold the melody together. That gap was mine. Five years of tasting poison a drop at a time had taught me something no court mage knew: how a body learns to carry what should kill it. How small doses, shared widely, become tolerance instead of death.

"That's the whole theory, isn't it," Tam said one evening, watching me demonstrate by drinking tea Marisol had laced with something that would have felled a horse with opinions. "The new covenant. Poison in small portions. Magic in small portions. Nobody gets the whole cup."

"Nobody *hoards* the whole cup," I corrected. "Everyone gets a sip. Some people will misuse theirs. Some people will make terrible glowing charms and sell them in markets." I raised an eyebrow at him. "It happens."

"You were arrested for less."

"I was *nearly* arrested for less. There's a difference, and it's called running."

The child giggled. Then she sang the first verse, and the ridge hummed along, and for three minutes the Steppe sounded like a place where things could grow.

Then we heard the hounds, far off, and stopped sounding like anything at all.

* * *

Casimir came back two hours before dawn, smelling of smoke and decisions.

He'd been gone six days—"errands," he'd said, in the tone of a man who considered the word an insult to both of them—and now he stood at the edge of our firelight holding a leather dispatch case like it was a dead bird he'd been asked to identify.

"How many?" I asked.

"Three riders. They'll reach the shrine by tomorrow midday if the weather holds, and the weather has been very cooperative lately, which I resent." He crouched by the fire. In its light his face looked older than it had in the city, or perhaps just more honest; the Inquisitor's face had always been a kind of uniform, and he'd been out of uniform for weeks now. "There were dispatches. Envoy Corvane's reports. Your names. My name. Locations. An estimate of your numbers that flattered you, incidentally—he thinks there are twelve of you. I didn't correct him."

"There are five of us and a beetle."

"The beetle stays off the record." He set the case down between us. Inside, I could see the wax seals, the neat official hand, the machinery of a state folding itself around our necks. "These were supposed to go ahead of the riders. Confirmation of target. Authorization for the ward-hounds to be loosed at dawn."

Marisol's knife stopped moving. "Was."

"Was," Casimir agreed.

"You burned them."

"I burned them." He said it simply, the way he once read charges, and I realized this was the same voice—the law's voice—but pointed the other way for the first time. "All of them. And then I forged replacements saying the company had scattered north, which buys us confusion, and confusion buys us hours. But Corvane isn't a fool. When the riders find nothing at the shrine's north face and everything at its south, the confusion ends." He looked up at me across the fire. "One night, Petra. Possibly two if the wind lies well. After that, the crown arrives in force, and no amount of forged ink will matter."

"One night," I repeated.

"One night."

I should have felt afraid. I did feel afraid—it sat in my chest like a stone I'd grown used to carrying. But underneath it, absurdly, there was something lighter. Because here was the thing about hiding for five years: you start to believe the hiding is the shape of your life. And here was Casimir Vane, the man who once spared me, burning the crown's paper in a fire built from steppe-grass, and the look on his face wasn't fear at all.

It was relief. Mine, reflected.

"Then we sing at dawn," I said.

"At dawn," Tam echoed, and his voice cracked on it, and nobody pretended not to notice, because pretending had been outlawed somewhere back on the riverbed with the drowned.

* * *

We spent the last dark hour doing the thing choirs have always done before the impossible: tuning.

The child sang her swallowed bell. Tam laid his fragments over it like a man laying planks over a chasm, testing each one. Marisol's drone came up from the ground itself, patient as geology. And I walked among them, adjusting, listening, tasting the air the way I'd once tasted a queen's stew—hunting for the wrongness, the nightshade note, the thing that would poison the whole.

I found it near the end. The old covenant's final phrase still wanted to resolve upward, toward the sky, toward *ownership*—one voice rising above the rest. Tam's instinct pulled him there; thirty years of court training doesn't leave, it just waits.

"No," I said gently, and put my hand on his shoulder. "Not up and away. Down and *around*. The last note goes to everyone. It doesn't land on the throne. It lands on the ground, where people are."

"It'll sound unfinished," he warned.

"It *is* unfinished. That's the point. A finished song belongs to whoever ended it. This one stays open." I smiled. "Let the next singer finish it. And the next. Let it be a song that's always being handed to somebody new."

Tam stared at me. Then he laughed again—that same startled bark—and shook his head. "A song with no owner," he murmured. "They'll drown us for it."

"They'd have drowned us anyway," Marisol said. "At least this way we're in tune."

Even the child laughed at that, and the beetle, I swear, paused in its circle.

East of us, far past the glass ridges, the sky over the distant capital stayed black and starless, but I thought I felt something shift in it—a pressure changing, like a held breath deciding. The wards were dead. The cage beneath the palace was cracked. And in one night, maybe two, either we would give the magic back to the world, or the world would watch us fail trying.

I touched the charm-stitch in my sleeve, the little forbidden knot that had started all of this, and found it warm.

"Dawn," I said. "Places, everyone."

Far off, answering me, the first hound began to howl.
