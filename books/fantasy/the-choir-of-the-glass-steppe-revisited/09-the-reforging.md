## 9. The Reforging

**Petra**

Dawn came to the shrine without sky the way grief comes—gray, inevitable, and far too soon.

Petra stood at the center of the broken circle, barefoot on stone that still remembered singing, and tried not to think about how cold their feet were. Cold was good. Cold meant nerves. Nerves meant alive, and they intended to stay alive through at least the first verse.

"You're doing the thing," Maren said. The bone-carver sat cross-legged at the circle's edge, her deaf hands resting in her lap like sleeping birds. She read Petra's face the way other people read weather. "The thing where you look calm and your jaw is screaming."

"My jaw is fine."

"Your jaw has been fine for five years of eating poison. I don't trust it."

Petra almost laughed. Almost. Across the circle, Sef the child sat with her eyes closed, humming the fragment she'd never been taught, the one that had grown teeth and wings since the Steppe. And beside her—

Beside her, where Ilan should have been standing with his lute, there was only a folded coat and the envoy's men walking down from the ridge in the gray light, a hundred torches burning yellow against a black sky.

*He took the pardon,* Petra thought, and the thought had no edges anymore, just a smooth worn place where anger used to be. *Or he's buying us time. With Ilan you could never tell which until the bill came.*

The envoy's voice carried across the ruin, polished as palace silver: "Last offer, taster. Sing it under the crown's seal, and every one of you walks home."

Petra looked at Casimir Vane, who stood between the choir and the soldiers with his inquisitor's badge torn off and his hands very still. Casimir looked back, and gave the smallest nod—the nod of a man who had burned a satchel of dispatches in the night and discovered that fire suited him.

Petra turned to the circle. To Maren, whose hands were already beginning to move. To Sef, whose hum had become a thread of light.

"No," Petra said. "We sing it our way."

They began.

* * *

**Casimir**

Casimir Vane had spent twenty years learning what fear looked like, and he had never once seen it look like this.

It looked like a hundred soldiers going quiet. Not disciplined-quiet, not ordered-quiet—*caught*-quiet, the silence of men who had come expecting a hanging and found themselves at a funeral. The song rising out of the ruined shrine did not sound like music. It sounded like something remembering itself.

And it sounded wrong, too—that was the horror of it. The old covenant, the one his grandfather's grandfather had strangled in its sleep, had been a courtly thing, ordered and hierarchical, each voice assigned its station. He knew that now; he'd read the buried ledgers. But this new song had no hierarchy. It was a round with no lead voice, a knot with no end, and when Maren's hands carved the rhythm out of the air and Sef's thread of light wove through it and Petra's voice—gods, Petra's voice, steady as a kitchen bell—held the melody without owning it, Casimir understood why the crown had feared this more than any army.

You cannot arrest a song that belongs to everyone.

"Captain," the envoy said beside him, and her voice had lost its polish. "Stop them."

"With respect, Lady Envoy." Casimir did not look at her. "I'd rather not."

She drew her blade anyway. And that was when the ward-hounds came over the ridge—not the crown's hounds, not anymore. They had been bred from stolen star-fire, leashed with star-fire, starved on star-fire, and the song was offering them their own light back. Casimir watched the great pale shapes pour past the soldiers like floodwater past fence posts, watched them wheel once above the shrine, and then turn—

—and sit. All of them. A hundred hounds sitting in the ash like dogs waiting for supper, their throats glowing, their chains dissolving into sparks.

One soldier laughed. Then another. Then someone dropped a torch, and the fire went out, and nobody picked it up, because the sky overhead had begun—very faintly, very terribly—to flicker.

*Hold,* Casimir thought, to no one, to everyone. *Hold, hold—*

* * *

**Petra**

The song needed a price. That was the whole truth of covenants, the thing the crown had never understood: magic was not hoarded because it was scarce. It was hoarded because giving it away *cost*, and thrones do not pay.

Petra had known since the Steppe what their price would be. Five years of swallowing death in small doses, five years of building a fortress in their own blood—and the covenant wanted the fortress. Not their life, exactly. Their armor. Every immunity, every hard-won resistance, poured out into the song so that the magic would know what sacrifice tasted like and would ask it of no one else ever again.

*I'll be ordinary,* Petra thought, holding the note. *Just a person. Anyone can poison me now.*

It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But underneath the terror was something that felt suspiciously like relief—the same strange lightness from the night Casimir had knocked on their door. Five years of being invisible, untouchable, sealed. Five years of tasting everything and feeling nothing.

They opened the fortress and let the song in.

It went through them like river water through a drowned man's coat. Petra felt the nightshade-resistance go first, then the arsenic, then the slow accumulated calluses against a dozen lesser deaths, all of it streaming out of their blood and up into the gray air, and the song *swelled*, and Sef laughed—a bright, startled child's laugh—and the sky overhead cracked open like an egg.

Not all at once. One star. A single dim ember directly above the shrine, trembling, as if unsure it was welcome.

The envoy screamed an order. Petra never learned what it was, because that was the moment Ilan stepped out from behind the soldiers' line with his lute raised—not toward the choir, but between the envoy and Sef—and Petra saw the blade before they could even stop singing, because stopping was not possible anymore, the song had them by the blood.

The envoy killed him in front of everyone. One motion, almost casual, the way you swat a fly during prayers. Ilan fell with the pardon-scroll still in his pocket, unspent, and his last chord rang off the broken stones and went *up*, into the song, and became part of it.

The single star flared.

Then a second answered it. Then ten. Then the whole wounded sky stuttered alight, dim as banked coals, dim as memory—but *there*, and returning, and the ward-hounds threw back their heads and howled harmony, and Maren's hands were shaking, and Sef was crying and singing at the same time, and Petra Villalobos, royal food-taster, hedge-witch, ordinary mortal, felt the last of their immunity leave them and the first true starlight in five years fall on their upturned face.

Then the ground tilted, and the song let go, and Petra fell.

* * *

**Casimir**

He caught them. Later he could not say how he crossed the circle fast enough; he only knew that the taster weighed nothing in his arms, lighter than any person had a right to be, as if the song had taken more than poison-resistance—as if it had taken the density of secrets.

Their pulse was there. Slow, thready, human. Ordinary.

"Is it done?" Petra whispered. Their lips were gray. "Did it take?"

Casimir looked up. The stars hung low over the Steppe, dim and uneven, like a city relighting after a long war—one street at a time, no guarantees. Around the shrine, the soldiers had grounded their weapons or simply wandered off among the hounds, touching them like children at a fair. The envoy stood alone with her bloody sword, and no one, not even her own captain, would meet her eye.

"It took," Casimir said.

"Good." Petra's eyes drifted half-closed. "Don't let them reforge it wrong. Don't let anyone own it. Promise me you'll—" A frown, the particular frown of a taster detecting something faint and bitter. "Someone should teach it. Someone who reads law and knows exactly which laws deserve drowning."

"That is not subtle, taster."

"I'm dying-ish," Petra said. "I'm allowed to be unsubtle."

Maren arrived at a run and pushed Casimir aside with the gentle ruthlessness of a woman who had bargained with ghosts and found them better company than most living men. Her hands moved over Petra's body, reading them, and her face did something complicated.

"Heart's strong," she signed, then remembered herself and said it aloud, rough-voiced, for the others. "Heart's strong. Body's just been emptied out. Feed them. Everything. Starting with honey."

Sef curled up against Petra's side without asking anyone's permission, still humming, softer now—a lullaby version of the covenant, already changing it, already making it hers. That was the point of it, Casimir thought. That was the entire point. In a year the song would be unrecognizable. In ten, no one would remember it had ever belonged to a throne, because it never would again.

Above them, the stars burned low and stubborn, and one by one, across the dark curve of the world, more of them were coming home.

Casimir Vane, traitor, ex-inquisitor, future teacher of songs, sat down in the ash beside a sleeping hedge-witch and began, quietly, to count the stars—because someone ought to keep track, now, of everything the kingdom owed.
