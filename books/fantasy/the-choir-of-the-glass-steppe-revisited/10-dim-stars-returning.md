## 10. Dim Stars, Returning

The stew was almond and lamb, thickened with ground rice, and it contained nothing that would kill the queen.

Petra knew this the ordinary way now — by taste, by smell, by the slow accumulation of five years' habit that had once been backed by an immunity they no longer possessed. They swallowed a spoonful anyway. That was the job. That had always been the job, even when the job had come with a hidden margin of safety stitched into their blood.

The spoonful sat in their stomach like any other spoonful.

"Verdict?" asked Cook Maren, who had taken to hovering during tastings lately with the anxious energy of a woman whose kitchen had become famous for reasons she didn't fully understand.

"No nightshade," Petra said. "No henbane. No crushed glass, which I appreciate. The saffron's gone slightly off, though. Buy from the other stall."

Maren beamed as if she'd been handed a medal. "You can tell *that*?"

"I can tell that," Petra said, "because I am a completely unremarkable woman with a completely unremarkable tongue."

This was not entirely true. Nothing in Vellamare was entirely true anymore. But it was true enough, and these days true-enough felt like a luxury.

Six weeks since the shrine. Six weeks since the sky had cracked open its first reluctant star like a miser prying loose a single coin. Six weeks since Petra had woken on the cold glass of the steppe with Casimir Vane's coat draped over them and the bone-carver's granddaughter — Sefa, her name was Sefa, Petra had finally gotten her to say it aloud — sitting cross-legged at their side, humming the new covenant under her breath the way other children hummed about puddings.

They had died a little, out there. Everyone agreed on this, in the careful way people agreed on things too large to look at directly. The immunity that five years of the crown's poisons had built into Petra's body had been the price of admission, poured into the song like wine into dry earth. What remained was a taster who could be killed by a bad oyster like anyone else. Petra found, somewhat to their own surprise, that they did not miss it. Immunity had never once made them feel safe. It had only made them feel *used*, in a way they'd needed six weeks of weak tea and naps to articulate.

The queen's court, meanwhile, had shrunk. This was the polite word for it. Half the nobility had discovered urgent family business in provinces where magic was already being taught in barns and back rooms by anyone with a voice and the patience to practice. The king — the young one, the grandson of the covenant-breaker, who had inherited a theft he hadn't committed but had certainly profited from — had issued a proclamation three weeks ago. It was four pages long and could be summarized in one sentence: *magic belongs to everyone now, please stop rioting.*

"He signs his proclamations with a shaking hand," Casimir had reported, over the terrible coffee Petra still made because some habits were load-bearing. "I watched him do it. He looked, honestly, relieved."

"You looked relieved," Petra said.

"I resigned. Resignation is mostly relief with better tailoring."

He had resigned. Inquisitor Casimir Vane, terror of two decades of charmsellers, had stood up in the Hall of Inquiry and read out — from notes, in a flat voice, which somehow made it worse for everyone listening — a full accounting of what the crown had buried beneath the palace. The caged star-fire. The drowned choir. His own role in enforcing the law that had been the crime. Then he had set down his seal of office on the table like a man setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying, and walked out, and nobody had stopped him, partly because half the hall was crying and partly because the other half was afraid of him.

Now he taught songs in a converted granary in the Tanners' Quarter, to classes of forty that smelled faintly of hides and ambition. He was, by all reports, a strict teacher. He was also, by all reports, secretly delighted, in the way of a man who has spent his whole life learning rules and has finally been given permission to love them properly.

* * *

Petra's new shop opened on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were unlucky and therefore cheap.

It was not much of a shop. A front room with a counter, a back room with a stove, and a sign painted by Sefa — who had opinions and a brush and no fear whatsoever — that read:

**PETRA VILLALOBOS — FOOD-TASTER. CHARMS. TEA. NO REFUNDS ON FATE.**

"You can't write 'no refunds on fate,'" Petra had said. "People will think we're flippant."

"People will think we're *honest*," Sefa said, with the terrifying confidence of a twelve-year-old who remembered songs she was never taught and had recently begun teaching them to other children in the alley behind the granary, to Casimir's mingled pride and despair. "Also I already painted it."

The charms themselves were different now. That was the thing Petra kept trying to explain to old customers who came in expecting the old goods — the little stitched protections, the whispered wards, the contraband warmth of forbidden things. Those were gone. What remained was stranger and quieter: charms that worked because the person wearing them had learned the smallest piece of the song themselves. A knot tied with intent. A word sung into bread dough. Magic as a household skill, like darning, like preserving plums.

"It's less impressive," grumbled Old Wenna, first through the door on opening day, buying a hearth-charm out of loyalty more than need. "Before, I paid you and it just *worked*. Now I have to hum."

"Now you have to hum," Petra agreed, wrapping the charm in brown paper, "and it works, and it's yours, and no one can take it away from you or drown you for having it. I've done the arithmetic on that trade several times and it keeps coming out in our favor."

Wenna sniffed. Then she hummed the first phrase of the covenant — badly, off-key, with tremendous feeling — and the charm in its paper gave a small warm pulse against Petra's palm, and Wenna went pink with pleasure like a girl at her first dance.

"There," Petra said. "See? You're insufferable already."

* * *

Some evenings, when the shop was shut and the stove banked, Petra walked up past the last houses to the ridge where the city ended and the Glass Steppe began.

The stars were dim. That was the honest truth of it, the truth the ballads were already getting wrong. The covenant they had sung at the shrine — the new one, the ownerless one, the one that belonged to everyone and therefore to no throne — had not restored the old sky. The star-spirits did not return in glory. They returned the way trust returns after betrayal: slowly, partially, with visible scars. The stars burned low and amber, closer to the horizon than memory claimed, steady as banked coals. On clear nights you could see perhaps half of what the old maps promised. Children born after the Reforging would grow up thinking this was simply what the sky looked like, and they would be right, and it would be enough.

From the ridge, on a good evening, you could see the shrine itself, far out on the glass — a pale smudge where the company had stood, where the envoy's soldiers had come down at dawn, where the ward-hounds had turned mid-charge and knelt in the frost like confused dogs remembering an older master. Where the musician, dear foolish generous Halven, had died with the melody still in his mouth, so that the others had to carry it the rest of the way without him, each of them holding a fragment, none of them whole alone. Petra thought that was probably the point. It had taken them years to understand that the old covenant's flaw wasn't the cage beneath the palace. The flaw was the idea that one voice could hold a song that size.

The bone-carver, Yeva, had stayed out there. Her hands had paid the river's price at the drowned choir's riverbed, and she said the dead spoke more clearly to her on open glass than in crowded cities. She sent letters sometimes, dictated to travelers, always in the same style: brief, unsentimental, and somehow more moving than anything Halven had ever written. The latest one lay folded in Petra's pocket now. *Stars steadier this week. Dead quieter. Tell Sefa the third verse wants a child's voice, not a trained one. Tell Vane he still counts wrong on the fourth measure. Come visit before winter.*

Petra meant to go. They told everyone they meant to go. There was a part of them, small and tired and honest, that was afraid of the place where they had poured themselves out, the way a person is afraid of a room where they once wept.

But tonight the wind off the steppe smelled of frost and something green underneath it — spring, gathering its arguments — and the low stars held their line across the dark like a choir waiting for the downbeat, and Petra thought: soon. Not yet, but soon.

Behind them, down the hill, the city glowed with a thousand small lights that were no longer merely lights. Somewhere in the Tanners' Quarter, forty voices were murdering the fourth measure. Somewhere in the palace, a shaking-handed king was reading petitions from farming villages asking, politely, for rain-songs. Somewhere a former Inquisitor was grading homework.

Petra pulled their coat tighter, checked — out of five years of habit, and then, deliberately, out of choice — that the charm-stitch in their sleeve was just thread now, just cloth, just their own small craft and no one else's power, and started home.

There was stew to taste in the morning. Unremarkable work, for an unremarkable tongue.

They whistled the third verse on the way down the hill, and every lamp they passed flickered, very slightly, in time.
