## 1. Small Charms for Hard Winters

The fog came off the Graywater before dawn, as it always did, rolling up the cobbled throat of the smuggler's quarter like something that had been paid to arrive. Silas Whitlock stood in the doorway of the Saltcellar tavern with his collar turned up and his hands in his pockets, and he counted what he had left.

Three knots. One warding sigil drawn on a scrap of oiled leather. And a coin purse that made a sound like a dying man's last cough when he shook it.

He had woken before the bells, as he always did — twenty years of habit outliving its purpose — and for a moment, in the gray dark of his rented room above the chandler's shop, he had reached for the old place inside himself where the god used to live. It was a habit too, that reaching. Like a tongue returning to the socket of a missing tooth. There was nothing there. There had been nothing there for twenty years, and still he reached, and still the nothing answered him with the particular silence of an empty room that had once held music.

*Ashen Father,* he thought, not quite a prayer. *Old Man of the Hearth-Coal.* The names came easily; it was only the listening that failed.

Below him, the quarter was waking. Gulls arguing over fish heads on the breakwater. A woman beating a rug from a third-floor window with the rhythm of someone settling an old grudge. The smell of tar and brine and frying onions from Maren's cart, which was already lit, which meant Maren had slept badly again, which meant her husband's cough was worse.

Silas went down to meet the day.

* * *

Maren gave him a boiled egg and half a heel of bread without being asked, because she had decided years ago that he was too thin and that this opinion entitled her to act on it.

"You look like death's clerk," she said, sliding the egg across the counter. "All black clothes and ink-stained fingers, taking down names."

"Death's clerk would have better boots."

"Death's clerk would have eaten." She nodded at the egg until he cracked it. Her husband's cough rattled through the floorboards from the room behind the kitchen, long and wet and patient. "You'll be wanting payment for the charm you left under our door Tuesday," she added, in a voice that dared him to name a price.

"It wasn't a charm. It was a knot. Different trade entirely." He ate the egg. It was warm, and he was grateful in the way he was grateful for most things now: quietly, and without expecting the gratitude to be received by anyone. "And I don't take payment for luck-knots. Bad theology."

"You don't take payment for anything anymore. You used to charge the shipping lords a month's wages for a fair-wind working. Now you give away knots to dockwives like a priest at a poorhouse door."

"The shipping lords can afford priests."

She snorted, but her eyes stayed on him a moment longer than joking required. Maren had known him eleven years, since the winter he'd warded her cellar against flood-water and refused to explain how a mercenary came to know hearth-magic. She knew there was something wrong with him the way you know a clock has stopped — not by watching the hands, but by the silence where the ticking should be.

"How is Tomas?" Silas asked, to move her eyes elsewhere.

"Worse." She said it flatly, the way people say things they have said so often the words have worn smooth. "The physician says his lungs are full of wool. He sleeps sitting up. He dreams about drowning, though we live on a hill." Her hands kept working, wiping the counter that did not need wiping. "You said if it got worse—"

"I know what I said."

"He can't sleep, Silas. Three nights now. He just sits there listening to himself breathe like it's a debt collector at the door."

Silas put down the eggshell. He looked at his hands — broad hands, scarred hands, a swordsman's hands wearing a craftsman's calluses — and felt the familiar arithmetic begin. What do I have. What will it cost. What will be left after.

"I'll come tonight," he said. "After the docks clear."

* * *

The smuggler's quarter earned its name honestly and without shame. Half the cargo that came up the Graywater never saw a customs seal, and the other half saw three apiece. By midmorning the quay was a forest of masts and argument, and Silas moved through it the way water moves through a hull — slowly, finding every gap, going where he was needed rather than where he was wanted.

He sold a warding sigil to a barge-master named Osk for two coppers and a promise, the promise being that Osk would stop drinking on the night crossing past the Drowned Chapel, because the sigil was good but it was not a miracle, and miracles were not currently in stock anywhere in Veyra.

He re-tied a mooring-line blessing for the *Kestrel's Debt*, whose captain superstitiously required one every new moon despite having no magic in her and knowing Silas had barely any either. That one cost nothing. The captain paid him in coffee and gossip: grain prices rising in the capital, soldiers drilling on the east road, a herald's party expected within the week.

"Heralds," Silas said. "What are they heralding?"

"Who knows. New taxes, probably. Or the queen's got a new favorite and wants him paraded." The captain shrugged with her whole body, a sailor's shrug that disclaimed all interest in politics ashore. "They say the court's gone strange lately. All candles and whispering. My cousin works a kitchen in the palace district — she says the inquisitor's household buys more wax than the chapel does."

"Inquisitor," Silas said. "There hasn't been a crown inquisitor in thirty years."

"There's one now. Woman. They say she's very polite." The captain said *polite* the way sailors said *storm*, meaning: the kind of thing you survive by respecting it early.

Silas drank his coffee and filed it away with everything else he'd been filing away for months — the quiet edicts, the census questions about "unlicensed practices," the way certain clients had started paying in advance and avoiding eye contact. The realm was changing weather. He'd survived enough changes of weather to know the trick was not to pretend the sky was clear.

* * *

The dockworker found him at noon, or rather Silas found him being found — a big man named Corrag, cranesman, forty years of hauling rope written into shoulders like mooring posts, standing at the mouth of Tanner's Alley with the specific posture of a man who wants something and is ashamed of wanting it.

"Whitlock." Corrag said it quietly, glancing up and down the alley. People didn't shout for magicians anymore. Even small ones. Even ones who were mostly retired.

"Corrag. You look like a man who's lost an argument with the sea."

"My girl." Corrag's hands worked at each other, knuckles like walnuts. "Pella. She's signing onto a deepwater run. Whaler, out of Kell's Reach. Eight months." He said it as though reporting a death he hadn't yet decided to believe. "Her mother went deepwater. Never came back. And she's nineteen and she knows everything, the way they do, and I can't—" His jaw worked. "I heard you make luck-knots. For sailors. Real ones."

Silas looked at him for a moment. Then at the alley — the wet cobbles, the gull-shit and fish scales, the thin light coming down between the warehouses like something rationed.

"Walk with me," he said.

* * *

His workshop was a room above the chandler's shop, twelve feet by ten, smelling of tallow and hemp-ash and old smoke. There was a table, a chair, a shelf of cord and wire and bone needles, and a window that looked out on nothing but another wall, which Silas had always considered a professional advantage. Magicians who could see the sky tended to get ideas.

He sat Corrag down and took out his cord.

Now — here is what Silas Whitlock knew, and what no one in the quarter knew, and what he intended to carry to his grave with whatever else he was carrying: a luck-knot was supposed to be a conversation. You tied the knot, and as your fingers worked, you spoke — not aloud, not in words, but in that older language, the one the Ashen Father had breathed into hearth-coals and keel-timbers and the first rope ever twisted by human hands. You asked the cord to hold. You asked the sea to be reasonable. You asked, politely, in the grammar of small things, and the god listened, and the god inclined, and the knot came off your fingers warm.

That was how it had worked for the first fifteen years of his second life. Before the silence.

For twenty years now, Silas had tied knots alone.

He measured out the cord — grey wool spun with a single thread of red, the old pattern — and began, and reached, as he always reached, into the cold room where the music used to be.

Nothing. The nothing that was not emptiness exactly, but absence-with-a-shape, the way a footprint in snow is shaped like a foot. Twenty years ago he had stood in a burning temple in the hill country with his oath breaking apart in his mouth, and the god had simply — stopped. Not died, he'd thought then. Gods didn't die. Fallen silent. Withdrawn. Busy elsewhere. He had told himself every version of it there was to tell, and told them so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones, and none of them explained why the silence felt less like absence every year and more like theft.

But his hands remembered. That was the mercy of craft: the body keeps the liturgy even when the congregation is gone. His fingers moved through the turns and crossings, over and under, the pattern his teacher had drilled into him in a temple that no longer existed, in a country that had changed its name twice since.

And here was the other thing, the secret under the secret: sometimes — rarely, once in a hundred knots, on nights when the fog lay right and he was very tired and very honest — the cord warmed anyway. Just slightly. Just enough. As if somewhere very far away, something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed, briefly, of rope.

Not today. Today the cord stayed cord, cool and honest in his fingers, and Silas Whitlock tied a luck-knot the way a deaf man writes a song: correctly, beautifully, and entirely on faith.

It took him the better part of an hour. He talked while he worked, because Corrag needed talking to more than Pella needed knotting.

"Tell me about her."

"She's stubborn." Corrag said it with helpless pride. "Gets it from her mother. She reads — proper reading, charts and almanacs, not just chapbooks. She can take a latitude off the sun with a bit of glass and a spoon of tar. First mate material, the whaling master said so, and he doesn't say things." He stared at his own hands. "I told her no. I'm her father, I told her no, and she looked at me the way her mother used to look at me, and I understood I'd already lost, probably years ago, probably the day she was born."

"That's fatherhood," Silas said. "You lose slowly and then all at once."

"When did you lose yours?"

"Never had the posting." He turned the knot, tucked the tail, began the closing sequence. "I had something else. An oath. Same shape, different weight."

"What happened to it?"

Silas considered the question with the part of his mind that wasn't tying. Around them the afternoon light had gone the color of weak tea. Somewhere below, the chandler was stacking candles for the evening trade, and the sound was soft and rhythmic, like breathing.

"Its god went quiet," he said. "The rest is just paperwork."

Corrag had the grace not to press. Dockworkers understood unanswerable questions; their whole trade was built on them.

When the knot was done, Silas held it a moment in his closed fist. Old habit. In the old days he'd have felt the working settle against his palm like a bird choosing to stay. Now he felt wool, and his own pulse, and nothing else, and he performed the little ritual anyway — thumb pressed to the heart of the knot, a breath, a wordless word — because ritual was a raft, and you did not stop bailing just because the ocean had stopped answering.

"Pella," he said, and handed it over. "Grey wool, red thread. Tell her it's from her father, not from a magician. Luck-knots work better that way. The gods are sentimental about fathers."

"Does it work?" Corrag asked. He held it carefully, the way men hold small expensive things. "Truly. I'm not asking cheap. I'm asking because my daughter is going to the ice-fields and I cannot go with her, and if this is a piece of string, I'd rather know it's a piece of string and buy her a good knife instead."

It was, Silas thought, the most honest question anyone had asked him in years, and he owed it an honest answer, and the honest answer was: *I don't know. I haven't known for twenty years. The power that backed these things has gone wherever dead and silent gods go, and what I sell you now is craft without covenant, form without fire, a prayer mailed to an address that may be empty.*

What he said was: "Twenty-two years I've been tying these. Every sailor I've knotted for has come home except the ones the sea took for reasons no knot argues with. Storms, whales, rum. No luck-knot of mine has ever failed anyone who deserved the luck." He let that sit, and then, because Corrag deserved the rest of it too: "I tie them well. Whatever else is true, that's true. The pattern is old and correct, and patterns matter. Ask any sailor whether the shape of a rigging matters, and then ask him whether the shape of a prayer might."

Corrag looked at the knot a long time. Then he took out his purse, and Silas watched him count coins into his palm with the deliberation of a man paying a debt rather than buying goods, and something in Silas's chest — the hollow place, the tooth-socket place — ached in a way that was almost warmth.

"Two silvers," Corrag said. "It's not enough. Don't argue."

"I never argue with fathers. It's a losing trade." Silas took the coins. Two silvers was nearly a week of bread and rent. He did not mention that he'd have done it for nothing, because Corrag knew that, and paying anyway was Corrag's own kind of knot — a binding between them, a small covenant of two mortal men with no god to witness it, which might have been the oldest magic there was.

At the door, Corrag paused. "The heralds," he said. "Coming up the river. My brother-in-law saw the barge at Tarn's Ford yesterday. White and gold livery. Queen's colors." He hesitated. "They're saying it's about the gifted. Registration. Assessment. That word — assessment — nobody uses a word like that about anything good."

Silas stood very still for a moment. Outside, the fog was burning off, and the light through the little window fell across his table, across the shelf of cords and needles, across the small ordinary tools of a small ordinary trade.

"Thank you, Corrag," he said. "Give Pella the knot tonight. Tell her the red thread is for coming home."

* * *

Afterward he sat in the tea-colored light and did not move for a long time.

*Assessment.* He turned the word over. He thought of the census riders last spring, asking cheerful questions at every door. He thought of the hedge-witch in Mill Lane who'd closed her shutters in high summer and never reopened them. He thought of the client last month who'd paid double and wouldn't shake his hand.

Then he got up, took down the ledger from behind the loose brick — twenty years of names, every charm and knot and warding, every soul he'd served in this quarter, written in his own careful hand — and stood holding it, feeling the weight of it, which was the weight of everyone who would come looking for those names if the wrong person read them.

The chandler's stove downstairs was banked for the night. He fed the ledger to it page by page, and the fire, at least, received him graciously, and the names went up the chimney into a sky with no one in it.

By morning, the white-and-gold barge would be two miles closer.
