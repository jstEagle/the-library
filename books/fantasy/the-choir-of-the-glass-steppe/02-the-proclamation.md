## 2. The Proclamation

The bells woke the quarter before dawn did.

Silas Whitlock lay in his narrow room above the chandler's shop and listened to them — not the usual bells of Saltmere Row, which rang for fires and funerals and the odd wedding that could afford them, but the deep bronze voices from the Citadel on the hill, tolling in a pattern he had not heard since he was a boy: three strikes, a pause, three more. The King's Pattern. The sound that meant every ear in Veyra was commanded to listen.

He had known it was coming. You did not sell luck-knots to dockworkers for twenty years without learning to read the weather of a city, and this weather had been building all winter — the new proclamations nailed to the customs house, the gray-cloaked surveyors counting doors, the way old Marta had stopped meeting his eyes at the fish stall. But knowing a storm is coming is a different thing entirely from hearing the first thunder crack over your roof.

He dressed in the dark. He did not light a lamp. There was a time when he would have whispered a word to make the dark give up its shapes for him — a small charm, a kindness of light, the kind of thing his god had once handed out like a grandmother handing out bread. But the word died somewhere behind his teeth these days, the way it always did, arriving at his lips and finding no one home to answer it.

Twenty years of silence. He was still not used to it. He suspected he never would be.

* * *

By the time he reached the square outside the customs house, the crowd had already gathered thick as spawning fish. Dockworkers with their hands still smelling of tar. Fishwives with baskets set down at their feet, forgotten. Children hoisted onto shoulders. And above them all, on the customs house steps, three heralds in the crown's white-and-gold, their brass horns gleaming in the flat gray morning light.

Silas worked his way to the edge of the crowd, back against the wall of the ropewalk, where the shadows were deepest and a man could watch without being watched. Old habit. Older than the silence.

The middle herald unrolled a scroll with the ceremony of a man unveiling a relic. When he spoke, his voice carried unnaturally far — a minor enchantment woven into the horn at his belt, Silas guessed, one of the few magics the crown still permitted itself. The hypocrisy of it sat in his stomach like a swallowed hook.

"By decree of Her Majesty Queen Isolde Veyra, First of Her Name, Protector of the Realm, Keeper of the Turning Year—" the herald's voice rang off the shuttered windows "—let it be known throughout the dominions of the crown that the practice of unsanctioned sorcery is hereby declared an act of treason against the realm and its people."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Someone near Silas crossed themselves with the old sign, the one that predated the Quiet Church, then seemed to remember themselves and stopped halfway.

"Let it be further known," the herald continued, "that all persons bearing the gift — whether by blood, by bargain, by blessing, or by any other means — are required to present themselves for Soul Assessment at the nearest assize within thirty days of this proclamation. Those who comply shall be examined with mercy and, where found harmless, released to their lives."

*Where found harmless.* Silas turned the phrase over and found nothing inside it. A coin with no face.

"Those who conceal their gift, or who are discovered harboring the gifted, shall be judged complicit in the crime of hidden magic." The herald let the scroll drop another fold. "Know this, good people of Veyra: the crown does not hate the gifted. The crown seeks only to protect you. Magic unmastered has drowned your harvests. Magic unmastered has burned your granaries and soured your wells and turned neighbor against neighbor. Her Majesty asks only that what is hidden be brought into the light, weighed, and governed — for the safety of all."

Governed. Weighed. Silas had heard men use those same soft words about cattle before market day.

The third herald stepped forward, younger, with a voice less practiced and therefore somehow worse. "Soul Assessment will be conducted by the Office of the Crown Inquisitor, by appointment of Her Majesty. Inquisitor Vael extends the crown's assurance: assessment is not punishment. Assessment is understanding."

Understanding. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to cry, quietly, the way people cried at funerals they weren't supposed to attend.

Silas scanned the faces around him. Most wore the careful blankness of people deciding, right there in the square, what they would claim not to know for the rest of their lives. That was how it always went. Not torches first — forgetting first. Torches came later, once enough people had agreed to remember nothing.

But here and there he caught other looks. A young man by the well with ink-stained fingers — a scrivener, maybe, or something else — whose eyes had gone very still. An old woman who met her neighbor's gaze and deliberately looked away, and whose neighbor understood perfectly, because she looked away too. The gifted, marking each other in the dark the way sailors marked shoal water. Everyone pretending not to see. Everyone seeing.

The heralds rolled up their scrolls. The horns sounded once more, a bright ugly note, and the crowd began to dissolve — slowly, reluctantly, the way ice leaves a pond, no one wanting to be last and no one wanting to be first.

Silas stayed against the wall until the square was nearly empty.

That was when they brought out Tam Broadstairs.

* * *

They came for him quietly, which was somehow the loudest thing about it. Two gray-cloaks and a clerk with a ledger, walking down Cooper's Lane at a pace that suggested a delivery rather than an arrest. Tam was mending a net on his front step, the way he did every morning of his life, and Silas — passing on the far side of the lane with his collar up — saw the exact moment Tam understood.

It was in his hands first. They went still on the netting needle. Then he looked up at the gray-cloaks, and his face did something terrible and ordinary: it arranged itself into apology. Apology, as if he were the one causing trouble. As if forty years of hauling nets and raising four daughters and never once cheating on a weigh-ticket were suddenly beside the point, because twenty years ago he'd paid a mercenary two coppers for a knot of braided cord that kept his boat off the rocks.

"The edict says assessment," the clerk was saying, in the tone of a man reading a menu. "If you come willingly, it'll go easier."

"I don't have anything," Tam said. His voice cracked on the second word. "I'm just a fisherman. I've got nothing."

"Sir, we have a report."

"A report." Tam laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound Silas had heard in years. "From who? Who reported me?"

The clerk consulted his ledger with great dignity. "Reports are confidential."

One of the gray-cloaks took Tam's arm — not roughly, not yet, just a hand on the sleeve, the way you'd steady a drunk — and Tam pulled back, and for one heartbeat Silas thought the man might run, might bolt down Cooper's Lane and make them chase him and turn a quiet erasure into a story people would tell.

Then Tam looked past the gray-cloaks, down the lane, toward his house. Toward the window where his youngest daughter's face was pressed against the glass.

And he stopped pulling.

Silas stood ten paces away with his collar up and his heart going like a drum in a marching band, and he did nothing. That was the fact of it, stripped clean. He was armed. He was, in the old days, more than armed — there had been a time when Silas Whitlock could have put a blessing on that lane that would have made the gray-cloaks forget why they'd come, could have folded the morning fog around Tam and walked him out through it like a ghost through a wall. He had done things like that. He had been, once, someone worth the name they'd given him.

Now he was a man with a collar up and a purse full of charms that were mostly cord and mostly hope, and if he moved, Tam's daughters would lose a father anyway and gain a fugitive's shame besides, and Silas would join Tam in whatever room waited at the end of soul assessment.

So he did nothing. He watched them walk Tam Broadstairs down Cooper's Lane, past the fish stall where Marta suddenly became fascinated by her own scales, past the chapel with its boarded-up shrine-niches, past everything and everyone who had known Tam for forty years and now, collectively, knew nothing at all. Tam did not look back except once, at the window, at the small pale face there. He raised his hand — a little wave, a father's wave, *off to work now, mind the house* — and then the corner took him, and he was gone.

The daughter at the window kept waving long after.

Silas made himself keep walking. One foot, then the other, down the lane, past the empty step where the half-mended net lay coiled like something dead. He did not look at the window. If he looked at the window he would stop, and if he stopped—

His hand was shaking. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

Two coppers, Tam had paid him. Two coppers and a smoked herring, and Silas had braided the knot with what power he had left even then, eighteen years ago, and told himself the charm would hold because it had to, because his god's silence couldn't mean what it seemed to mean, because surely somewhere under all that quiet the old strength was still there, resting, waiting, like a bear in winter.

The knot had held. Tam's boat had come home through the Gray Gale when eleven others hadn't.

And now the crown was going to open Tam Broadstairs like a letter to see what was written inside him.

* * *

His rooms, when he reached them, felt like a stranger's.

He had lived above the chandler's shop for nine years. Long enough to accumulate the sediment of a life: the chipped blue cup, the map of the Steppe coast with its pins marking old contracts, the shelf of books bought in better years, the bundle of dried herbs that had long ago lost any virtue but smell. He stood in the middle of it all and looked at it the way a man looks at a campsite he must abandon before nightfall.

Then he knelt and dragged out the floorboard under the bed.

The ledgers came up wrapped in oilcloth, six of them, twenty years of them. Every client. Every charm sold, blessed, braided, whispered, or faked. Names, dates, prices, notes in his cramped hand — *M. Ostell, hearth-ward, weak, renewed twice; the ferryman's widow, grief-charm, charged nothing; T. Broadstairs, weather-knot, two coppers + herring.* Twenty years of small mercies recorded in ink, and every line of it now a rope to hang someone. Every name a door the gray-cloaks could knock on. Every note a confession signed in his own blood-warm handwriting.

He had told himself for weeks that he should burn them. He had told himself, too, that he couldn't — that the ledgers were the last proof the work had mattered, that somewhere in those pages was evidence that the silence hadn't hollowed him completely, that a man with no god left in him had still spent two decades standing between ordinary people and the ordinary cruelty of the world. Burning them felt like burning the only witness to his own soul.

Tam's little wave settled the argument. *Off to work now. Mind the house.*

He built the fire up hot in the chandler's old copper basin, the one used for melting wax, and fed the ledgers to it one page at a time, because burning them whole risked a legible scrap surviving in the ash, and Silas Whitlock did nothing halfway when other people's necks were on the line. The paper curled and blackened and lifted its ghosts. Names went up in smoke: M. Ostell, the ferryman's widow, T. Broadstairs, a hundred others, a whole hidden parish of the overlooked rising off the basin in gray ribbons and vanishing through the roof slates.

He saved the last ledger for last, and in it, the last entry, and he read it once more before it burned:

*Wren. Song-gift, wild, untrained. No charge. Debt acknowledged.*

Fifteen years old, that entry. Fifteen years since a child had sung a dying man back into his body on the marsh road, and he had written her name in the book of debts because he had nothing else to give her and knew, even then, that a debt like that doesn't close. It compounds. It sits in a man's chest gathering interest until the day it comes due.

The page caught. Her name went orange, then black, then up.

He sat back on his heels and watched the fire eat the last of it, and the cold that had been sitting in him since the bells finished its work. This was what remained: a man with no record, no name in any book worth burning, no god answering, and thirty days — fewer, really, because the clever ones would run early and the gray-cloaks knew that — before the pattern of his life closed over his head like water.

Run. The word arrived, sensible and cold. North, over the Steppe, or east on some grain ship, some place where the queen's writ thinned out. He had money enough for passage. He had skills enough to survive. He had, if he was honest, no one — the whole point of twenty careful years was that there was no one whose ruin his ruin would drag down.

Except.

Except a singer with a ribbon in her hair who performed Thursdays at the Lantern eel-house, who laughed too loud, who sang songs with old magic folded into the verses so casually that most listeners never felt the touch of it, who had once poured her own borrowed light into a stranger's failing body because it was the decent thing to do and she was fifteen and didn't know yet what decency cost.

Wren didn't know. She thought her gift was small — party tricks, pretty harmonies, a warmth in a cold room. She didn't know that what she carried was the kind of thing the crown's new office would classify, assess, and file away beneath a mountain, or wherever such things went, forever.

She wouldn't run. That was the trouble. Wren would stand in the assize line with her chin up because she had nothing to hide, because she was honest, because she believed — the way Tam had believed, the way everyone believed until the hand took their sleeve — that innocence was armor.

Silas rose. His knees cracked. In the basin, the last ash settled, fine and gray as winter.

Thirty days. He took down his coat, the good one with the oiled seams, and began, methodically, to pack.

Somewhere north of the city, he'd heard, there were others who hadn't surrendered — whispers in the smuggler's quarter, a name passed like contraband: the Hollow Choir. He didn't know if it was real. He knew that Tam Broadstairs was in a gray-cloak wagon, and a girl with a ribbon in her hair was singing on Thursdays in a room full of witnesses, and the bells would ring again soon, and next time they would come for someone whose name was written nowhere at all.

He blew out the lamp, though the morning had already done it for him, and went down the stairs into the smoke-smelling dark of the chandler's shop, and out.

Behind him, above the empty room, the ash of twenty years drifted up through the slates and scattered over Saltmere Row, falling on the honest and the hiding alike, soft as the first snow of a hard winter.
