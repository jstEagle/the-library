## 3. Wren's Voice

Fifteen years is a long time to carry a debt, and longer still to carry a voice.

Silas Whitlock had carried both since the winter he died on the road to Harrow Fen, and some nights — most nights, if he was honest, and honesty came cheap when the candles were low — he could still hear it: a child's voice, thin as a reed split by frost, singing him back out of the dark.

He had been thirty-one that year, though he felt older, because his god had already begun to go quiet in him. Not silent yet — not the terrible nothing that would come later, the hollowed chapel of his own chest — but quiet the way a hearth goes quiet when the coals are settling into ash. His charms still worked, mostly. His luck-knots still knotted. But there were mornings when he woke and reached for the warmth at the base of his skull where the god used to sit like a hand resting on his neck, and found only the memory of warmth, and had to pretend, even to himself, that pretending was enough.

He'd taken a contract that winter from a wool-factor in Corvess who wanted his rival's warehouse to burn cold instead of hot — a freezing charm, subtle work, the kind of job that paid well and hurt no one. The factor had paid half up front and Silas had ridden north through the Teeth with sleet coming down like thrown gravel, and somewhere past the second milestone his horse had gone lame, and somewhere past the third, the men had come out of the trees.

Brigands, or something wearing brigands' faces. Later he would learn they'd been hired by the same wool-factor, who wanted his charm-worker dead rather than paid in full and remembered. That knowledge came later, though, and did him no good later. What mattered then was the ice under the snow, the horse screaming, the first arrow taking him through the meat of his shoulder, and the second — the second taking him somewhere worse.

He remembered falling. He remembered the sky going the color of old pewter, and the sleet landing on his face without melting, which is how he knew he was dying, because dying men are cold all the way down to the surface of their eyes.

* * *

The dark after death, he learned that day, is not empty.

It has a sound. A low, grinding hush, like a millstone turning far away, like the world being slowly chewed. He drifted in it and understood, with the strange calm of the very badly wounded, that this was what waited for everyone: not fire, not judgment, just the millstone and the meal. He thought about the luck-knots he'd sold over the years — the dockworker's daughter in Veyra-port, the fisherman's boat, the widow's door-latch — small charms for hard winters, each one a little thread of borrowed divinity tied off in a mortal knot. He wondered who would untie them now. He wondered if they'd unravel all at once, everywhere, like a garment losing its seam, and whether anyone would notice that the world had come slightly unluckier.

He was sorry about that. He found, drifting, that he was sorry about a great many things, and that sorry was a poor coin to arrive with anywhere.

Then, through the millstone-hush, a voice.

It should not have reached him. Nothing reached that place; that was the whole nature of it. But the voice came anyway, thin and cracked and utterly untrained, a child's voice singing words in no language Silas knew — or perhaps in every language, worn down to their roots, the way a riverbed holds the shape of water long after the water has moved on. It sang a rising line, three notes climbing, then a fourth that bent wrong on purpose, and the wrongness went through him like a hook through cloth.

And the dark *leaned toward it.*

He felt the millstone slow. He felt the hush tilt, attentive, like a wolf lifting its head. And then the voice did something he had never heard magic do before and has never heard it do since: it *borrowed.* He felt it happen — felt the song reach out sideways, away from the singer, into everything around her, into the frozen ground and the standing pines and the dead man cooling in the snow, and take up a little of each. A grain of warmth from the earth. A green ghost from the pines. And from him —

From him it took the shape of his life. Not the life itself; he kept that, barely. It took the *pattern* of it, the tune his living had been humming all these years, and it held that pattern up against the dark like a lamp against a wall, and said, in the oldest grammar there is, *this is how he goes. Put him back the way he was.*

The world slammed shut around him like a book.

* * *

Pain woke him, which was fitting. Pain had been waking him for twenty years.

He was still in the snow. The sleet had stopped. Above him the pewter sky had torn open along one seam, and a thin, dishonest gold light came through it, the kind of light that makes promises January has no intention of keeping. His shoulder screamed. His ribs screamed. Everything else had the decency to merely ache.

A girl was sitting in the snow beside him, cross-legged, her hands pressed flat against his chest as if she were holding down a lid.

She was small — seven, maybe eight — wrapped in a coat too big for her, brown wool gone gray at the elbows, the sleeves rolled back four times. Her hair was dark and cut short and unevenly, someone's knife-work done in haste or anger. Her lips were blue. Her eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and she was still singing, barely, the last thread of the song running out of her like the last thread of anything runs out — reluctantly, and then all at once.

When it ended she swayed. Her hands slid off his chest. She looked at him, and her eyes focused slowly, like a lamp being turned up, and Silas watched a child realize what she had done.

"You were dead," she said. Her voice was wrecked, scraped raw by whatever the borrowing had cost. "I mean. You were going to be."

"I was," Silas agreed. Speaking hurt. He spoke anyway. "Thank you."

"For what?"

"Being better at my job than I was."

She frowned at that, working it over, decided it was either a joke or nonsense, and settled for neither. "Your blood got on my song," she said, with the grave precision children use for things that frighten them. "I didn't mean it to. I only meant to sing you warm until someone came. But you were so far down, and nobody was coming, and the song wanted more than I had, so I — " She stopped. Her small jaw set in a way he would come to know very well over the years, the way of a person deciding not to apologize. "I borrowed. Mother says you're never supposed to borrow. She says the song remembers what it takes."

"The song remembers," Silas repeated.

"And who it took it from." She looked at him then, straight on, and there was something in her face that made the hair on his arms rise under all that blood and frozen cloth — something old looking out of young eyes, the way dawn looks out of a keyhole. "So now it knows you. And you know it. That's what the binding is. It isn't a rope. It's more like — " she searched, eight years old and furious with the limits of language — "like two people who've heard each other's true name by accident, and can't unhear it."

Silas lay in the snow and felt, faintly, at the base of his skull, where the hand of his god used to rest: a second warmth. Small. Flickering. New.

His god, in those days, was still capable of jealousy. He felt it stir — a dim, offended shifting, an old dog raising its head at a stranger in the yard — and then, strangely, subside. Settle. As if it had looked at the girl in the oversized coat and the blue lips and the borrowed song, and found nothing there to resent. As if it recognized the work.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." She shrugged inside the enormous coat. "Mother says names are doors. We keep ours small."

"Your mother sounds like a woman I should thank properly. Where is she?"

Wren's face did something complicated. "Looking for food," she said, in the tone of a child who has answered that question before and learned exactly how much of it adults believe. "We're going south. There's work in the cities, she says. Singing work." A pause, and then, with the artless cruelty of the young: "There wasn't any here anymore. After the last purge."

The word landed in the snow between them and neither of them picked it up. Purges were a fact of the landscape in those years, like wolves or bad roads — you didn't discuss them, you simply adjusted your route. Silas had spent his whole career adjusting his route. He looked at the child who had pulled him out of the millstone-dark with a song stitched together from pine trees and frozen ground and the pattern of his own unwound life, and he understood that she was exactly the kind of person his contracts existed to protect, and exactly the kind of person his contracts never seemed to reach.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

"Can you?"

It was a fair question. He sat up, and the world performed its usual treacheries, and he rode out the wave of them with his teeth locked. "Badly," he said. "But yes."

"Then we'll both be bad at it together." She stood, knees cracking like an old woman's, and held out one small mittened hand with the imperiousness of a duchess. "Come on, then. You can't stay here. The men who shot you might come back for your boots."

"They might," Silas agreed, and took her hand.

Her grip was astonishingly strong for something so small. Or perhaps it wasn't strength at all. Perhaps it was only that the song remembered what it took, and had decided, in its patient, borrowed way, to hold on.

* * *

They walked south together for six days.

He learned things about her the way you learn things about weather — gradually, and mostly by being caught in it. She hummed when she was frightened and went silent when she was terrified, so that her silence became the thing he watched for, more than the road, more than the trees. She could tell when a fire had been built by someone angry. She said bread tasted different if the baker sang while kneading, and when he laughed at this she looked at him with such wounded dignity that he apologized, and meant it, and never entirely stopped believing it afterward.

On the third night, sheltering in a charcoal-burner's hut with the wind trying the door like a debt collector, she asked him what he did.

"Charms," he said. "Small ones. Luck, mostly. Protection, when people can afford it. Weather-work, if I'm desperate enough to risk it."

"Is that real magic? Charm-work?" She asked it without malice, the way children ask if you're really a knight or just dressed like one.

"It's real," Silas said. "It's just — secondhand. I don't make the luck. I ask for it. There's a — " he groped for it, the way he always groped for it, the way he had never once in twenty years managed to say the word *god* to a stranger without feeling like a beggar describing a palace he'd been expelled from — "there's someone I serve. Serve *with,* maybe. The charms are his voice, passed through me. Like a message shouted down a line of people. By the time it reaches the end, it's quieter. But it's still the message."

Wren considered this, chewing the heel of the loaf they'd bought with the last of his coin. "My songs aren't messages," she said finally. "They're more like — wells. Everyone's got a well in them, Mother says. Most people never draw from theirs. When I sing, I let people draw. Whatever they need. Warmth, or courage, or sleep, or — " she gestured at him with the bread, a small brown smear of accusation — "coming back from being dead."

"That's not a well," Silas said quietly. "That's a river."

"Mother says the same thing." Something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice — pride, and fear of the pride, in equal measure. "She says rivers get dammed. That's why we keep moving. That's why the name stays small."

He thought about that for a long time after she slept, curled in the charcoal-burner's corner like a comma, breathing in the tiny rhythm of the utterly exhausted. He thought about dams. He thought about the dim warmth at the base of his skull, and how it had leaned, that night in the hut, toward the sleeping child — not hungrily, but the way an old man leans toward a window with sun in it. He thought: *when she sings, I can almost hear him again.* And he thought, with the first cold thread of a dread that would take fifteen years to finish weaving itself: *and what happens to her, someday, when someone powerful hears a river and wants to build a dam.*

He made himself a promise in that hut, in the dark, with the wind worrying the door. He was not a man given to promises; oaths were currency in his trade and he'd learned to spend them carefully. But this one he cut deep and tied off like a knot, the kind that doesn't slip.

*Nothing dammed,* he told the darkness, and the god, and the sleeping girl, all three. *Not while I'm bought and breathing.*

* * *

In the morning the road forked at a shrine so old its god's name had weathered off the stone, leaving only the hollow where the word used to live. Wren stopped and looked at it, head tilted, listening to something Silas couldn't hear.

"There's a song in there," she said. "Under the stone. It's very old and very tired." She glanced up at him. "Do you want me to wake it?"

"No," Silas said, and was surprised by the force of his own voice. He softened it. "Some things sleep because they choose to. Let them."

She looked at him a moment longer — that old look out of young eyes, the keyhole-dawn — and then nodded, and took his hand, and they walked south together into the low gold morning, toward the cities, toward the singing work, toward everything that came after.

He would lose track of her within the year, the way you lose track of everyone in that trade — a safehouse emptied, a name changed, a river moving on. He would spend fifteen years hearing her voice in the space between sleep and waking, three notes climbing and a fourth bent wrong on purpose. He would tell himself the debt was paid in kindnesses scattered blind across the Steppe, every hidden magician sheltered, every hunted soul turned away from the main road. He would almost believe it.

And then, fifteen years later, in the smuggler's quarter of Veyra-port, with the heralds' drums rolling the Purge Edict down streets that had gone quiet as held breath, Silas Whitlock would hear a rumor in a dockside tavern — a singer, a girl grown now, a voice like a river, taken or soon to be taken — and the knot he had tied in a charcoal-burner's hut would pull tight around his throat, and he would know that the song had remembered after all.

It remembered what it took.

And it had come to collect.
