## 4. The Client Who Sold Him

The house on Lantern Row had a door like a held breath.

Silas had knocked on a hundred fine doors in his years of selling small mercies, and he knew the grammar of them — the brass knockers worn to dullness by servants' hands, the bells that rang in distant kitchens, the long waits that told you your business was being weighed by someone who counted money the way other people counted heartbeats. But this door was different. This door was new, lacquered the green of deep river water, and it opened before his knuckles touched the wood, as though the house had been listening for him all along.

"Master Whitlock." The woman who greeted him was perhaps fifty, silver at her temples in two precise wings, dressed in wool so dark it drank the lamplight. "I am Lady Corvane. You come recommended."

*Recommended.* In the smuggler's quarter, where he kept his stall between a fishmonger and a man who sold caged wrens — *wrens*, and he thought of another bird entirely every time he passed — recommendations were passed mouth to mouth like contraband. In this quarter they were written on cards with gilt edges.

He stepped inside. The hall smelled of beeswax and something underneath it, faint and wrong: scorched sugar, almost, or the ghost of a candle burned down to nothing. He had learned to trust that smell. It was the smell of magic recently spent, hastily spent, spent in fear.

"You'll take tea," Lady Corvane said. It was not a question.

"I'll hear what you need," Silas said. "Then maybe tea."

Something flickered across her face — irritation, quickly smoothed. She led him through rooms that grew quieter as they went, as though the house were swallowing sound, until they reached a study lined with books whose spines had never been cracked. A fire burned low. On the desk lay a letter, folded once, sealed with grey wax.

"My daughter," she said, without preamble, "is gifted."

Silas did not move. Twenty years of careful living had taught him stillness the way other men learned swordwork. Outside these walls, heralds were nailing the Purge Edict to church doors and market posts, reading it aloud in voices trained to carry across squares: *all persons bearing the gift shall present themselves for assessment, for the safety of realm and soul alike.* Assessment. He had watched them take the tinker's boy from the lane behind his stall three days ago, had stood among the neighbors who looked at their shoes while the boy screamed, and had done nothing, because doing anything was dying, and dying helped no one. That was the arithmetic he lived by now. It tasted like coins held too long in the mouth.

"And you want me to hide her," he said.

"I want you to *quiet* her." Lady Corvane sat behind the desk and laced her fingers. Her rings caught the firelight — good rings, old rings, the kind that outlive their wearers. "The girl sings. When she sings, things happen. Candles gutter. Milk sours or sweetens, we've never known which. Last month she sang her brother's fever down, and the whole household felt it, like a wind through a closed room." Her voice was level, rehearsed. "A servant talked. Servants always talk. If she is found, she goes to assessment, and I have heard what assessment means. Haven't you?"

He had. Everyone in the quarter had, though no one said it above a whisper. People went into the inquisitors' wagons pale and came out — if word could be believed, and word from the assessment houses was thin and strange — not screaming anymore. Not anything anymore. Quiet as emptied rooms.

"So you want a charm," Silas said slowly. "A binding. Something to smother the gift."

"To *veil* it. Until this passes. Until the crown comes to its senses, or falls, whichever comes first." She slid the sealed letter across the desk toward him. "My husband's family has served Veyra for six generations. There are names in that letter — names I would burn before letting them be read aloud. I am not asking you to save my soul, Master Whitlock. I am asking you to save my child from my own history."

It was well done. He gave her that. Even now, some part of him that had once been a priest's student — back when his god still answered, back when the world had a floor under it — wanted to believe her. The mother protecting the child. The oldest story there was.

But he had been a mercenary of charms too long to miss what was missing. No mention of payment. No haggling, which wealthy clients loved more than prayer. And that scorched-sugar smell, stronger here, near the desk, near the letter.

"What's in the letter?" he asked.

"Names."

"Whose hand wrote it?"

Her fingers tightened around each other, one white knuckle at a time. "Does it matter?"

"It matters," Silas said, "because a charm of veiling takes three days to weave and a week to settle, and during that week I will be in this house, and I don't work blind." He let the silence stretch. "And it matters because you haven't asked my price, my lady, and people who don't ask the price have already decided how they mean to pay."

For one heartbeat — he saw it clearly, the way he sometimes saw things he had no gift for seeing, just an old soldier's read of a face — Lady Corvane looked at him with something that was almost grief.

Then the doors opened.

They came in fast and quiet, four of them in the grey-and-silver of the Crown Inquisition, and a fifth behind them in black who did not touch anything, only stood in the doorway with his hands folded, watching Silas the way a man watches weather he has already decided to walk through. The room filled with the smell of cold iron and consecrated ash. Silas knew that ash. Every charm-bearer knew it; it was what they burned to scrub a room clean of working.

"Master Whitlock." The one in black spoke gently, reasonably, a voice for reading edicts. "By authority of the Purge Edict, you are summoned for soul assessment. You will come quietly. Resistance will be noted."

Silas was already moving.

Not toward the window — they'd expect the window, and the window was barred anyway, he'd marked the bars when he entered because he marked everything, always, because twenty years ago his god had gone silent mid-prayer and left him alone in a world that was suddenly, entirely, made of teeth. He went for the fire instead. His hand closed on the iron poker, and he swung it not at the men but at the desk lamp, and glass burst, and oil spread flame across the ledgers and letters in a bright rushing sheet.

Lady Corvane screamed — genuinely, he thought, or well enough that it didn't matter.

"The letter burns," Silas said, backing toward the servants' door he'd noticed behind the bookshelf, half-hidden by a drape, because rich houses always had a servants' door and rich houses always forgot it. "Your names burn with me. Or you call them off, and I go quietly, and you keep your history."

The man in black tilted his head. "Burn it," he said. "We already have copies. We always have copies."

Of course they did. Of course she'd given them copies. He saw the whole shape of it then, clean and terrible: the recommendation passed up through channels, the appointment made, the daughter — real or invented, who knew — the letter placed on the desk like bait on a hook, the whole room built around him like a snare built around a rabbit's heart. Lady Corvane had sold him. Not for gold. For her family's name, struck from some list, her household spared a visitation, her daughter's singing filed away as a rumor rather than evidence. She had bought her children's safety with his body, and the crown had made the transaction easy, and somewhere in a ledger in some grey office there was probably a line item for it.

She wouldn't meet his eyes. That was the worst of it. Not triumph. Not even shame, exactly. Just a woman looking very hard at the burning desk, at the flames eating the letter she'd written, as though the fire might absolve her of having written it.

"I'm sorry," she said, to the desk, to the fire, to no one.

"Yeah," Silas said. "Me too. About your curtains."

He pulled the drape down over the shelf, kicked the servants' door open, and ran.

* * *

The house's back passages were narrow and smelled of tallow and old bread, and for thirty seconds he thought he might actually make it. Then a kitchen maid stepped into his path with a carving knife held the way people hold knives who have never used them, arms stiff, eyes enormous, and he had a choice to make in the space of a stride, and twenty years ago the choice would have been easy because twenty years ago he could have put a sleep-charm on her with a breath and a word and she'd have dreamed gently against the wall while he passed.

Twenty years ago. Before the silence. Before the charms had begun to fade like ink in rain, each working costing more than the last, each answer from heaven a little thinner, until the last time he'd prayed — really prayed, on his knees, in the reeds by the Greyfen — there had been nothing back but wind and his own pulse, and he had understood, finally, in his bones, that whatever had listened to him was gone, and gone in a way that meant *taken*, though he hadn't known taken by what, not yet, not for years yet.

So he did it the mortal way. He turned his shoulder, took the knife along the meat of his forearm instead of the throat it was aimed at, shoved past her, and kept running. Pain arrived a half-second late, hot and bright, blood sheeting warm down his wrist and dripping off his fingertips, printing little red commas on the flagstones behind him. Good, some cold corner of his mind noted. Blood trails help them. Stop the bleeding or stop leaving it.

There was a scullery door. Beyond it, a yard, a wall, a drain grate he'd have sworn on anything holy — on anything that used to be holy — was too narrow for a man of his breadth. He went through it anyway. Stone peeled skin off his shoulders and hips. The world became a black throat of brick and running water, and he dropped into it, and the cold hit him like a confession.

The sewers under Lantern Row were old, older than the city's fine face, Roman-work in places, the bricks laid by hands that had believed in river gods nobody remembered. The water came to his thighs and moved with the slow patience of something that had nowhere to be. Above him, faint through the grates, he could hear the city going about its evening: cart wheels, a drunk singing, someone calling a child home. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who were not, tonight, being hunted through their own city's bowels.

He got his belt off and bound the forearm, cinching it with his teeth, tasting leather and blood. The cut was long but shallow — the maid had flinched, bless her terrified heart — but he'd torn himself on the grate and the cold was already in his joints, working its way toward the marrow, patient as debt. He needed heat. He needed dry ground. He needed, most of all, to be anywhere but here, because the inquisitors would know the sewers — of course they would, they'd mapped this city's veins the way physicians map a body — and somewhere behind him, faint but steady, he could hear boots on a maintenance walkway, echoing down through the brick in a rhythm too disciplined to be rats.

Silas pressed his back to the wall in the dark and did the thing he hated most.

He reached for his god.

It wasn't praying, exactly. Praying implied an expectation of answer. This was more like an old man talking to a photograph — the motion of speech without any hope on the far end. He had eleven charms left. He counted them the way a starving man counts coins: the knot of luck-braided hemp against his sternum; the salt-ring bead; the little clay whistle that had once called fog off a harbor; three knotted cords whose purposes he'd honestly half forgotten; the rest odds and ends, fragments, leavings. Each one was a thread tied to something vast that had once answered him, and each year the threads frayed further, and he did not know — this was the truth he carried like a stone behind his breastbone — he did not know whether the silence at the other end was absence, or death, or something worse: something *listening and unable to reply*, which he somehow could not bear to imagine, and imagined anyway, at night, in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next.

He drew on the luck-knot. Just a little. Enough.

Warmth bloomed in his chest, thin as the last coal in a banked fire, and spread down his arm, and the bleeding slowed, and the cold retreated a handspan from his heart. It cost him. He felt it cost him the way you feel a tooth come loose — a small permanent subtraction, a loosening he'd never get back. Ten charms after tonight. Maybe nine, if the knot frayed worse than he thought.

*Where are you,* he said into the dark, not really asking, the way you say it every time. *What happened to you. Who ate you.*

No answer. Wind in the grates. Water moving. Boots, still coming, unhurried now — they knew he was wounded, they knew the sewers funneled toward the river, they could afford patience. Patience was the inquisitors' whole art. He'd seen it in the man in black's folded hands. Men who believed themselves instruments of salvation never hurried.

Silas pushed off the wall and moved.

He knew these tunnels better than they'd credit a smuggler's-quarter charm-monger with knowing, because he'd spent the lean years learning them, sleeping in them some winters, and because a man who sells luck to dockworkers learns where the city keeps its shame. He took the fork toward Tanner's Run, waded upstream against the flow because downstream led to the river gates and the river gates would be watched, and he climbed, hand over hand, up a rusted ladder rung by rusted rung, breath sawing, arm screaming, until he surfaced through a coal hatch into the freezing open air of a chandler's yard, lay flat among the barrels breathing fog, and listened.

The boots faded below. Somewhere a bell rang the ninth hour. Overhead, the sky was the color of wet slate, and the first snow of the season was starting down, fat and slow, settling on the barrels, on his shoulders, on the blood drying brown on his sleeve — early snow, too early, the kind of snow that made old folk mutter about the seasons turning strange.

He should run farther. Cross the quarter, shed the coat, become someone else until morning. That was the smart play, the survivor's play, and he had survived twenty years by making it.

But his feet took him the other way. Through the chandler's yard, down the crooked lane, to the stall with the cages — empty now, the wrens sold off days ago — and past it, three streets on, to a garret above a dyer's shop where a light still burned behind shutters, and a voice, even through the wall, even muffled, was singing something small and looping and stubborn, a spinning song, a keep-the-dark-out song.

Wren.

Nineteen now. Fifteen years since a dying mercenary had lain in a ditch outside Harrow Fen and a child had sung him back from the edge with borrowed magic she didn't understand and couldn't repeat, and he had sworn — stupidly, absolutely, in the manner of men who owe their lives to children — that nothing would ever reach her. He'd kept the oath cheaply for fifteen years, keeping watch from a distance, selling her grandmother charms at cost, telling himself distance was protection.

Now the crown had made the oath expensive.

Silas stood in the falling snow beneath her window, bleeding, half-frozen, ten charms from nothing, and understood with perfect clarity that the hunt had stopped being a thing that happened to people like him in general. It had a face now. It had a name on a list, and the name was hers, and hers was the one he'd have traded every other name in Veyra to strike.

Above him, the song paused. A shutter cracked. One eye, dark and quick, peered down into the yard — and widened.

"Silas?" Wren's voice, sharp with alarm. "Saints' teeth, you're bleeding — get inside, get *inside*, they came by the shop today asking questions—"

He climbed the dyer's stair on legs that barely consulted him, and as she pulled him through the window into candlelight and warmth, pressing cloth against his arm, scolding him in a whisper, he looked past her shoulder at the little room — the herbs drying, the songbooks, the second cup already set out as if she'd known — and felt the last of his cleverness, his caution, his twenty years of careful arithmetic, quietly go up like the letter on Lady Corvane's desk.

Because on her table, half-hidden under sheet music, lay a summons. Grey wax. The seal of the Crown Inquisition.

They had already found her.
