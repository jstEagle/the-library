## 5. Inquisitor Vael

The rain came down on Veyra like a verdict, and Inquisitor Serane Vael stood at her window in the Tower of Assessment and watched it wash the streets clean.

Not clean, of course. Nothing in the lower city was ever truly clean. But the rain made a good show of it, silvering the slate roofs, filling the gutters with a sound like applause, and for a few minutes each evening the smugglers' quarter almost looked like a place worth saving.

That was the work, she had come to understand. Not the arrests, not the assessments, not the long grey ledgers with their columns of names — the work was this: standing at a window, believing.

"Three more processed, Inquisitor." Her clerk, Pellam, stood in the doorway with a tray of tea he was afraid to offer. He was always afraid to offer it. She had never once rebuked him for interrupting, which somehow frightened him more than rebuke would have. "A cooper's widow, a ferryman's boy, and a woman who reads fortunes at the Saltmarket. The fortune-teller wept."

"Did she?" Vael did not turn from the window. "Grief or relief?"

Pellam considered. "I think... relief, madam. She said her gift had been a debt all her life. That people came to her with their dead and their debts and their dying mothers, and she could never refuse them, and now—" He stopped, embarrassed by his own report.

"Now she can rest," Vael finished. "Yes. Write that in her file. *Assessed willingly; expressed gratitude.* It will matter later, when they ask whether we were gentle."

*When they ask.* As though history were a magistrate waiting patiently outside the door. Pellam wrote it anyway, because she asked, because everyone in the Tower did what she asked, and that obedience — so clean, so quiet — was one of the few comforts left in her day.

She took the tea at last and held the cup without drinking. Porcelain painted with wrens, a gift from the Queen's own hand two winters ago, back when the Edict had been a draft and Vael had been a scholar of canon law with ink-stained fingers and a reputation for asking uncomfortable questions about the old liturgies. The Queen had said: *You see the rot in our foundations, Serane. I need someone unafraid to look under the house.*

Vael had looked. God help her, she had looked.

* * *

The archive beneath the Tower was hers alone now; even the Queen's own archivists needed her seal to enter. She descended the spiral stair with a lamp, as she did most nights, past shelves of vellum that predated the dynasty, and stopped where she always stopped: at the iron cabinet marked with the sigil of the First Accord.

Inside lay the founding contracts of Veyra itself. Every schoolchild learned the pious version — how the first King Halvard had driven back the Long Dark through faith and steel, how the gods had blessed his line. What the schoolchildren did not learn was written here in a hand like frost forming: the seasons of the realm were not a blessing. They were a payment schedule.

Spring, summer, autumn, winter — four installments per year, rendered faithfully for nine hundred years, purchased with something the contract called only *the choir's maintenance*. The word *souls* appeared nowhere. It didn't need to. The first drafters had been lawyers too.

For years Vael had told herself the documents were allegory. Metaphor. The fever-dream of an early court that mistook astronomy for theology. But then she had been given access to the mountain's receiving-house below the Citadel — the plain brick room where the tithe was counted — and she had seen the ledger there balance against the weather. She had watched a winter arrive three days late and found, in the margin of the mountain's book, a note in the Receiver's elegant hand: *short by three; remit.*

Short by three souls. Three days late, the thaw.

After that, there was no more allegory. There was only arithmetic, and arithmetic was merciful in its way. It did not care what you felt. It cared only that the sum be paid.

Nine hundred years ago, the gods themselves had gone into the mountain — that much the murals in the deep places made plain, if you were permitted to see them. The old divinities, devoured whole, had bought the realm perhaps five centuries of borrowed time. When their substance ran thin, the Court beneath had turned to smaller coin. Human coin. And the crown, faced with the death of every harvest, every river, every child who needed spring to arrive — the crown had paid.

The Purge Edict was not cruelty. Vael held to that the way other people held to scripture. The Edict was triage. Every gifted person taken gently into assessment was a soul delivered to the Court *willingly recorded*, and willing souls burned slower, lasted longer, kept the seasons turning with less waste than the terrified ones the mountain used to take in the bad centuries — snatched from beds, dragged screaming, their fear souring everything. She had read the old Receiver's complaints about it. *Spoiled stock,* he had written, in 611. *Bitterness reduces yield.*

So no more snatching. No more screaming. Now there were summons on grey wax, and warm rooms, and clerks who offered tea, and a woman who read fortunes at the Saltmarket weeping with relief because someone had finally told her the truth: that her gift was not hers, had never been hers, that it was a fragment of stolen fire and the fire had to go home.

We are not monsters, Vael thought, climbing back toward the lamplit office. We are the only honest people in Veyra. Everyone else gets to pretend the seasons are free.

* * *

In the morning she rode down to the lower city herself, which her guards disliked and her work required. Paper assessments were one thing; you could falsify paper. But a soul had a smell, a pressure, a way of making the air around it slightly *too much* — colors a shade too vivid, shadows a half-step out of joint. Vael had trained herself to feel it the way a moneylender feels clipped coin between finger and thumb. You could not write that skill into a procedure. You had to walk the streets.

The smugglers' quarter received her the way it received all weather: with shutters closing and smiles going smooth. She walked it anyway, grey cloak, no insignia, Pellam trailing with the satchel of writs, and let the quarter lie to her with its whole heart.

At a chandler's stall she paused over a row of candles and felt it — faint, faded, like warmth rising off a stone hours after sunset. Something blessed had passed this way recently. Something worn down to its last reserves.

"The tallow's good this month," the chandler said. A big man, dockside hands, eyes carefully stupid.

"I'm sure it is." She smiled at him, and the smile was genuine, which unsettled him more than any glare. "You've had a visitor. Someone who sells... small services. Charms."

The chandler's face performed ignorance beautifully. "Can't say as we get many fine folk down here."

"No," Vael agreed pleasantly. "You don't." She set a coin on the stall — silver, far too much for candles — and watched his throat move. "When he comes again, and he will, because men like that cannot stop serving people, it's the whole disease of them — send word to the Tower. There'll be another coin. Two."

"And if I don't?"

She looked at him then, fully, with all the patience she possessed, and let him see that she was not angry, had never been angry, would never need to be angry, because the world itself was on her side and all she had to do was wait. "Then one morning my colleagues will come instead of me, and they are not patient, and they are not kind, and I will have to write down whatever they do in language that makes it sound better than it was. I would rather write something true and decent. Help me write something decent, chandler."

He took the coin. They always took the coin. Not from greed — from the same place the fortune-teller's tears had come. Relief. Someone else would carry it now.

Walking on, Pellam murmured, "Was that wise? Tipping our hand."

"It wasn't our hand. It was mercy with a price tag. Different animal entirely." She turned up toward the terraces where the wealthy lived behind their hedges of imported jasmine, where the next name on her list waited in a house with good locks and bad conscience. "Besides, the charm-seller isn't the quarry anymore. He's a thread. Pull it gently and it leads somewhere."

"To where, madam?"

To the network, she did not say. To the Hollow Choir, or whatever they were calling themselves this season — the hidden congregations of the gifted who believed hiding was living. She had broken three such cells in the northern provinces before her transfer, and each one had taught her the same lesson: they gathered around music. Around song, specifically. Some old belief that voice was the safest vessel for power, that a sung blessing left no residue a scryer could find. Sentimental. Dangerous. And useful, because singers needed audiences, and audiences left footprints.

There was a name surfacing in the informants' reports, repeated with the hushed fondness people reserved for saints and arsonists. A young singer in the dyers' district. Small concerts in candlelit rooms. *Her voice does something to you,* one informant had written, and then, in a shakier hand, as though the pen had grown afraid mid-word: *I mean that literally.*

Vael wanted very much to hear it for herself.

* * *

Lady Corvane received her in the blue salon at the appointed hour, pale and composed and ruined underneath, the way all her clients were. On the desk between them lay the deed of cooperation, signed, and beside it a folded statement naming the man she had hired — Whitlock, Silas Whitlock, charm-wright, formerly of the Third Mercenary Company — and the alley where the inquisitors might find him.

"You understand," Lady Corvane said, "that I had no choice. My sons—"

"Had names on a list," Vael said gently. "I know. I wrote the list." She let the silence do its work. "You've saved them, my lady. Whatever comes, hold onto that. Guilt is heavy enough without adding doubt."

"He was kind to me," Corvane said suddenly, staring at her own hands. "Twenty years buying charms off that man and I never once saw his face do anything but kindness. He fixed my daughter's night-terrors for the price of a dinner. He wouldn't take more." Her voice cracked along some old fault line. "What are we, Inquisitor, that we sell such men?"

Vael rose, collected the statement, and answered honestly, because honesty was the one luxury her position allowed her and she spent it lavishly, like perfume.

"We are the people who learned what the world actually costs," she said. "And he is a man spending counterfeit coin who never knew it was counterfeit. I am sorry for him, truly. But the bill has come due, Lady Corvane, and it is being presented to all of us, and the only question left is who pays quietly and who pays screaming." At the door she paused. "Your sons will be exempted. Full assessment waivers. See that they're grateful, and see that they're quiet."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The light coming down through the breaking clouds was the color of pewter, and somewhere below, in the wet labyrinth of the lower city, a wounded man was climbing a stair toward a candlelit room where a young singer waited — a singer whose name sat in Vael's satchel between two writs, spelled three different ways by three different informants, and underlined twice.

Wren.

Vael stood a moment on the terrace steps and breathed the washed air. Somewhere a bird was trying out the first phrase of spring. Everything, she thought, everything sings before it's gathered. Even the thrushes sing loudest just before the nets go out.

She adjusted her gloves and went down to order the carriage.

They would take the concert on Friday night. Quietly, courteously, with tea afterward for anyone who cooperated. And if the charm-seller Whitlock was there — and threads, pulled gently, always led somewhere — then she would assess him personally, kindly, thoroughly, and enter him in the ledger in her own fair hand:

*Assessed willingly. Expressed gratitude.*

Whether it was true or not, it would be written decently. And decency, these days, was the last currency the realm had left to spend.
