## 6. The Hollow Choir

The rain found him before the Choir did.

Silas had walked out of Veyra City through the corpse-gates at dawn, when the watch changed and even the most diligent sergeant preferred his breakfast to his duty. He had walked with a knife-wound in his side that he had bound with a strip of shirt and a charm so old its stitching had gone grey — a mending-knot his god had once blessed personally, back when blessings were things that happened to men like weather. The knot held the wound closed the way an argument holds a door: not well, and only while everyone kept pushing.

By dusk he was three miles into the Glass Steppe, and the rain had come down in earnest, the cold autumn kind that did not fall so much as arrive, everywhere at once, patient as a debt collector. It soaked through his coat, through the bandage, through whatever thin warmth he had managed to hoard since the sewers. His side throbbed in time with his heartbeat, and his heartbeat was unsteady, a drummer who had begun to doubt the song.

He knew he should keep moving. Inquisitors did not give up because their quarry crossed a boundary stone. But his legs had opinions of their own, and they had voted for the leaning trunk of an old rowan at the edge of a fallow field, and so he sat, and the rain came down, and he thought — with the strange clarity that blood loss lends a man — *this is how it ends, then. Not on a battlefield. Not with a name on anyone's lips. Under a tree, in the rain, holding nothing.*

He must have closed his eyes.

When he opened them, there was a lantern hanging from the rowan's lowest branch.

It had not been there before. He was nearly certain it had not been there before, and certainty about such things was the one luxury his profession still permitted him. The lantern burned without flickering in the rain, its light the color of hearth-embers, and beneath it, close enough that he should have heard her approach, stood a small woman wrapped in a shawl the grey of woodsmoke.

"You're bleeding on sacred ground," she said. Her voice was dry and unhurried, like paper turning in an old book. "Though I suppose all ground is sacred now, since the ones who made it holy stopped answering."

Silas's hand went to his knife. It got halfway there and stopped, mostly because his arm disagreed with the plan.

"If you're going to rob me," he said, "you've chosen a poor night for it. I have four coppers and a luck-knot that doesn't work."

"Everything works," the woman said. "Some things are just tired." She crouched, unhurried, and looked at his side with the frank appraisal of a woman who had dressed a hundred wounds and buried more than one man who'd refused her help. "That's a mending-knot. Old work. Whose blessing?"

"Gone," Silas said.

"Mm." She touched the knot with two fingers, and warmth spread through the cloth like wine through water — not healing, exactly, but encouragement, the wound remembering what it was supposed to be doing. "I'm called Mother Ash. You're coming with me, mercenary, because you'll die here otherwise, and dead men make terrible company. Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Liar," she said pleasantly, and whistled twice, low, a sound like wind through a keyhole.

* * *

They came out of the dark the way moths come out of attics: three figures in oilskin cloaks, one of them leading a mule whose saddlebags smelled of tallow and dried fish. They put Silas on the mule without asking his permission, which he appreciated, because if they had asked he would have had to say no, and then he would have fallen down, and everyone would have been embarrassed.

They traveled through the night by roads that did not appear on any map Silas had ever bought or stolen. Once, crossing a stream, the man in front paused and pressed his palm flat against the water, murmuring something too quiet to catch, and the current slowed around their ankles like a dog being asked to wait. Another time, the youngest of them — a boy of perhaps sixteen with ink-stained fingers — glanced at the moon and the clouds rearranged themselves overhead, politely, the way servants step aside in a corridor.

Small magics. Cheap magics. The kind Silas had sold in dockside alleys for coppers, the kind that would get a person dragged to the assessment houses now, and performed openly, casually, among strangers, as though the Edict were a rumor from another country.

"You're staring," Mother Ash said, walking beside the mule.

"Careless," Silas corrected. "You're careless. That boy bent the weather where anyone could see."

"The sky is very large, mercenary, and the Crown's eyes are very few. Fear makes people careful, and carelessness looks like fear, and fear is what they're farming." She adjusted her shawl against the drizzle. "We stopped hiding our gifts from each other a long time ago. Hiding is how they win — not with chains, but with silence. A magician who swallows her own gift chokes on it eventually. Better to choke together."

"You talk like a woman who's never been in an assessment house."

"I've been in two." She said it the way she might say *I've been to the coast*. "I walked out of both. Not everyone does. That's why we dig."

*Dig* turned out to be nearly literal. An hour before dawn, they stopped at the mouth of a flooded quarry, its sides terraced with dead bracken, its bottom a black mirror of rainwater. Mother Ash led them along a ledge behind a curtain of ivy to a seam in the rock — a crack barely wide enough for the mule, which, impossibly, the mule accepted. And then down, and down, a stair cut by no mason Silas recognized, the walls sweating mineral light, faint veins of green and gold glowing in the stone like the memory of sunlight stored against a long winter.

The passage opened into a cavern, and the cavern opened into a village.

That was the only word for it. Lamplight strung between stalactites. Houses built of quarried stone and salvaged ship-timber, stacked in tiers along the cavern wall like swallows' nests. Gardens under glass — actual glass, panes of it salvaged and fitted over beds of greens and pale mushrooms, tended by a woman whose hands glowed faintly green as she worked the soil. Children — actual children — chased one another between the doorways, laughing, and one of them stopped mid-game to stare at Silas with the open, unafraid rudeness of the young.

A bell somewhere rang a note that was less a sound than a feeling: welcome, welcome, welcome.

"What is this place?" Silas asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"The Hollow Choir," Mother Ash said. "Though we were calling ourselves something duller before the Edict. Now the name fits whether we like it or not." She gestured, and the tiered houses, the lamplight, the gardens under glass. "Two hundred souls, give or take. Weavers who sing their thread true. Smiths who coax iron. A midwife who can hold pain like a cup of water. Every one of them a criminal under the new law. Every one of them alive, fed, and — for tonight — free."

She looked up at him on the mule, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of banked coals.

"Welcome to the underground, mercenary. Mind the third stair. It bites."

* * *

They gave him a room that had been a storage niche in some earlier century: a cot, a blanket that smelled of lavender and stone, a candle stub. A healer named Ferren — broad, quiet, with hands that hummed faintly when they worked — cleaned the sewers out of his wound and scolded him for the state of the mending-knot.

"This was fine work once," Ferren said, turning the grey-stitched charm over. "Whoever blessed it poured real power in. You don't find knots like this anymore."

"No," Silas said. "You don't."

Ferren looked at him with the particular patience of healers, who spend their lives learning what people do not say. "Rest," was all he answered. "The Mother will want words when you can stand."

Silas slept ten hours without dreaming, which frightened him more than any nightmare would have. For twenty years his sleep had been a grey antechamber — the place where his god's silence lived, the hollowness he carried the way other men carried scars. To sleep like a man with nothing missing felt like theft.

When he woke, someone had left bread, hard cheese, and a clay cup of tea that tasted of pine and honey. He ate all of it, slowly, listening to the sounds of the Hollow through the stone: footsteps, laughter, the ring of a hammer, someone singing — actually singing, a working-song, the melody bending oddly in the middle the way melodies did when magic rode them, and no one stopping her, no doors slamming, no boots in the corridor.

He sat with the empty cup in his hands for a long time.

*This is what it was supposed to be,* he thought. *This is what the charms were always pointing at.* Luck-knots and road-blessings and fever-songs — little payments on a great debt, fragments of a covenant his god had made with the world before Silas was born. Serve the small needs. Keep the hearth-lights lit. He had thought, all these years, that he was the last clerk of a bankrupt house, stamping worthless paper. But the paper wasn't worthless. The house hadn't gone bankrupt.

The house had been robbed.

He didn't know yet that he knew that. It arrived later, the way all the important truths arrived — sideways, wearing ordinary clothes.

* * *

Mother Ash received him in a chamber at the cavern's heart, a round room walled with shelves of books, jars, rolled maps, and candles burned down to pools. She sat behind a table scarred by decades of use, and she did not look up from the letter she was reading until Silas had crossed the whole room, which he understood to be a test, and passed, which he understood to mean nothing yet.

"Sit," she said. "Tell me your name."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. Names are the first thing the Crown takes. Assessment, they call it — polite word. They write your name in a ledger, and after that you aren't a person who has a gift, you're an asset that has a use. So." She folded her hands. "Your name."

"Silas Whitlock."

"And what were you, before you became a fugitive?"

"A seller of small charms. Mercenary work, when the winters were lean. Warding, finding, mending. Nothing grand." He hesitated. "I had a patron. Lady Corvane. She hired me for a warding last month. She sold me to the inquisitors the same night."

"To buy her family's exemption," Mother Ash said. Not a question.

"You know her?"

"I know the pattern. The Edict runs on that pattern. They don't need to hunt us all — they only need to make betrayal cheaper than loyalty, and wait." Her voice stayed level, but something moved behind it, deep water over deep stone. "How many names did you sell, before she sold yours?"

"None."

"Many people say none."

"I kept ledgers," Silas said. "Every client, every charm, every payment. I burned them the morning the heralds read the Edict. There were two hundred and eleven names in them. Farmers, dockworkers, a ferryman, three prostitutes, a tax clerk. People who couldn't afford to be found." He met her coal-colored eyes. "I burned them before I ran. If you want proof I'm worth the bread, that's the only proof I have."

Mother Ash studied him for a long moment. Then she took a key from around her neck, opened a drawer, and set a folded paper on the table between them.

"Then here is what your bread is buying," she said. "We pulled this from a courier two weeks ago. He was carrying it to a magistrate in Veyra City, and he was carrying it inside his own skin, which tells you how much it mattered to somebody."

Silas unfolded it. The handwriting was precise, bureaucratic, beautiful in the way that instruments of cruelty are often beautiful. He read it once quickly, then again slowly, and the second reading was worse.

*Requisition, Quarter's End. Souls assessed: 340. Souls conveyed: 312. Conveyance route: the Underway, via the Ferryman's Stair. Destination per standing covenant: the Court Beneath. Payment rendered to the Crown of Veyra: continuation of the seasonal compact, per the Founders' Accord.*

"Court Beneath," he said.

"There is a court under the mountain," Mother Ash said. "Under the Glass Steppe itself, if the oldest songs are true. The songs say it keeps the seasons turning. Winter follows summer because something down there makes the wheel go round. And the wheel has never turned for free." She tapped the requisition. "Three hundred and twelve souls this quarter alone. Assessed, conveyed, delivered. The Crown isn't imprisoning us, mercenary. It's harvesting us. Has been for longer than the Edict — the Edict is just the harvest going industrial."

Silas looked at the numbers and felt the floor of the world tilt gently, the way it tilts when you learn a building you trusted has no foundations.

"Souls," he said. "Not lives. Souls. What does a court under a mountain want with—"

"Fuel," Mother Ash said simply. "Or singers. Or both. The songs disagree. The songs also say the old gods powered the wheel once, freely, gladly, before—" She stopped. Her gaze went, for half a heartbeat, somewhere far away and very cold. "Before something happened to them. Before they went silent. You'd know something about that, I think. Your knot was blessed by one of them, and you carry the silence like a man carries a dead wife's coat. Still wearing it. Still warm-shaped."

Silas's hands had gone still on the table. "My god went silent twenty years ago. All of them did. Every priest, every oracle, every charm-worker felt it happen on the same night. The Church calls it the Withdrawal. The mystery of faith."

"The mystery of bookkeeping," Mother Ash said. "Gods don't withdraw, mercenary. Gods get spent. Ask yourself what the realm's seasons cost, and who signed for the purchase, and why the payments have gotten larger every year since the silence fell." She leaned forward. "Why do you think the assessments started small? A soul here, a soul there, smuggled down the mountain stair by priests who told themselves it was pilgrimage? And now the Crown needs hundreds a quarter, because the wheel is slowing, because the fuel is running thin, because everything that burns eventually burns out."

The lamp flames in the chamber guttered, all together, though there was no draft. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, thunder rolled across the Steppe — or something that the people up there would call thunder.

Silas thought of the luck-knot he had sold the dockworker, ten days and one lifetime ago. The man had gripped it like a rope over a drop, and Silas had taken his coppers and lied with his eyes and said *this will hold*, and it had held, barely, the way everything held now — badly, briefly, on borrowed strength. He had thought he was selling scraps from an empty pantry.

He had been selling scraps from a larder someone else had emptied.

"My god," he said, and his voice sounded like someone else's, "was devoured."

"I don't know," Mother Ash said, and the gentleness in it was worse than certainty. "But I know the wheel eats. And I know your god is silent, and the mountain is not." She rose, came around the table, and stood looking down at him — a small grey woman with coal-fire eyes, offering him the worst gift anyone had given him in twenty years, which was the truth. "You're a finder, by trade. So find. There's a man in the east galleries who came down the Ferryman's Stair alive, which no one is supposed to do. He's been mad since, but madmen remember what the sane agree to forget. Talk to him. And then decide what kind of fugitive you intend to be — the kind who hides, or the kind who digs."

She gathered her letters. At the doorway she paused, without turning.

"One more thing, mercenary. The courier's packet had a second page. A list of names — the next quarter's requisition, already drafted. Gathered from informants, patron betrayals, parish registers." She held it out, and did not let go when he took it, so that they held it together for a moment, like dancers. "You'll want to sit down before you read the fourth name."

She left him alone with the lamp and the paper.

Silas sat down. He unfolded the list. He read the names the way a man reads a diagnosis, top to bottom, hoping to be wrong.

The fourth name was *Wren*.

And beside it, in the same beautiful, bureaucratic hand, a note in the margin: *the singer. Priority conveyance. The Court has requested her voice specifically.*
