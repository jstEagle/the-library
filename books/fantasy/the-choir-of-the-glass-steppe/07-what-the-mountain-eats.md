## 7. What the Mountain Eats

The Hollow Choir kept its sanctuary beneath a tannery, which was either the cleverest hiding place in Veyra or the cruelest joke ever played on the sense of smell. Silas decided it might be both as he followed Mother Ash down a stair that stank of lime and old hides, past vats gone black with decades of use, into a cellar where the air changed all at once — cooler, sweeter, carrying something like rain.

Someone had hung charms along the ceiling. Dozens of them, maybe hundreds: knotted cords, bird skulls wrapped in copper wire, strips of cloth embroidered with prayers in languages Silas half-remembered from his god's better days. They turned slowly in a draft he couldn't feel, and where they turned, the air shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone — except this was winter, and the shimmer was the work of frightened people pooling what little power they had left to keep one room unfindable.

"You'll want to sit," Mother Ash said.

"I've been sitting for three days." He lowered himself onto a bench anyway, because his leg still wept through its bandage from the sewers, and because there was a quality to her voice he'd learned to recognize in twenty years of selling small mercies — the tone of someone about to say a thing that could not be unsaid.

She was older than she looked or younger; her face refused to commit. Grey hair cropped close as a soldier's, hands stained the brown of walnut ink, eyes the pale yellow of candle flame seen through horn. She moved through the cellar lighting lamps with a taper, unhurried, and only when the last wick caught did she speak again.

"Vael took eleven souls this week. Eleven. And the week before, nine. She's not hoarding them, Whitlock. She's *delivering*."

"Delivering where?"

Mother Ash sat across from him, folding her stained hands like a woman settling in for a long confession.

"Where do you think the seasons come from?"

He almost laughed. "From the turning of the world. From—"

"From whom?" She let the question hang. "You're a charm-man. You know better than most that nothing works without a worker behind it. Luck doesn't pool in knots by accident. Rain doesn't fall on schedule out of politeness. So who turns the year, Silas? Who makes the frost come when the crown needs the rivers frozen for timber-hauling, and thaw when the granaries need opening? Who decides that spring arrives?"

Silas opened his mouth. Closed it. Somewhere above them, the tannery's day-shift began, boots thudding across floorboards, and the sound seemed very far away, as if the cellar were already halfway to somewhere else.

"The gods," he said at last. "It used to be the gods."

"Yes." Her voice went soft, and somehow that softness was worse than shouting. "Used to be."

* * *

He had been six years silent, his god. Twenty years, if he counted honestly — and he did count, every night, the way a man counts coins he no longer has. Twenty years since the last time the warmth had moved through him like a tide, since a blessing had answered back instead of dying in his hands. Twenty years of squeezing drops from a well that should have been an ocean, telling himself the silence was punishment, or distance, or the natural fading of an old faith in a new age.

He told Mother Ash none of this. She read it anyway, the way she seemed to read everything, in the way his hands curled around each other on the bench.

"There was a war," she said. "Not the kind they sing about. No armies, no banners. A quiet war, fought in contracts and signatures, four hundred years ago, when the first kings of Veyra looked at their gods and saw not majesty but *expense*. Gods are expensive, Whitlock. They demand worship, sacrifice, humility. They answer prayers you'd rather they didn't. And worst of all—" her mouth twisted, "—they belong to everyone. A king cannot own a god."

"And so?"

"So the founders made a bargain with something older than gods. Something that lived under the Steppe before there was a Steppe, before there was grass, when all this was glass cooling out of fire. The mountain wasn't always a mountain. It was a wound. And in that wound, something sang."

The lamps guttered, though there was no draft. Silas found he had stopped breathing and made himself start again.

"The founders fed it," Mother Ash said. "They fed it their gods. All of them, every name and every face, dragged down beneath the stone and devoured — and the devouring bought the realm a miracle. A court beneath the mountain, staffed by singers, that would turn the seasons forever. Perfect harvests. Timely snows. No famine that wasn't chosen, no flood that wasn't scheduled. The crown called it the Accord of the Turning Year, and they wrote it down in a book with seven locks, and they have paid the installments ever since."

"Installments," Silas repeated. The word tasted like sewer water.

"Souls." She said it plainly, without flinching, and he understood that she had said it plainly many times before, to many frightened people, and that plainness was the only mercy left in it. "The Court eats song, and song lives in souls. Every gifted person in Veyra is a note in a score the crown has been copying out for four centuries. The Purge isn't persecution, Silas. It's *harvest*."

* * *

He thought of the dockworker he'd sold the luck-knot to, ten days and a lifetime ago. Big man, cracked knuckles, a daughter with a crooked smile. He'd haggled Silas down to three coppers and walked away clutching the knot like it was made of morning.

He thought of the neighbor in his lodging house, old Marta with the herb-sense, dragged down her own stairs by men in grey while her cat screamed from the windowsill.

He thought of Wren, fifteen years ago, singing light back into his body with a voice too big for her small chest, and how he had sworn — clumsily, bleeding, unworthy — that nothing would ever take that voice from the world.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake as though they belonged to a stranger, and then he stopped pretending they didn't.

"My god," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "When it went silent. Twenty years ago."

Mother Ash's candle-flame eyes held steady on him. She knew. He could see that she knew, and she let him arrive at it himself, because some doors a man must open with his own hands.

"The harvests haven't always been equal," she said quietly. "Some years the Court takes more. Some years it reaches higher. Twenty years ago there was a season the old records call the Quiet Tithe — a single payment, unusually large, taken all at once. The chronicles don't say whose souls. But after that year, every working in the northern provinces grew thin. Charms failed. Blessings came back unanswered. Faithful men woke up hollow and were told they'd imagined the warmth."

The cellar tilted. Or he did. He gripped the edge of the bench and the wood bit his palms and the pain was good, was real, was the only thing keeping him moored.

"It wasn't silence," he whispered. "All this time I thought I'd been abandoned. I prayed into a dead well and hated myself for doubting, and the whole time—" His breath hitched, broke. "The whole time my god was screaming, and I couldn't hear it over the sound of my own shame."

"It was stolen," Mother Ash said. "You weren't forsaken. You were *robbed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that robbery can be answered."

* * *

Afterward — he didn't know how long after; the lamps had burned lower and his tea had gone cold untouched — she showed him the rest.

A map, drawn in walnut ink on tanned hide, pinned to the far wall behind a hanging curtain of prayer-cloths. The Glass Steppe spread across it in careful strokes, and beneath the mountain, rendered in lines so fine they seemed to tremble, was the shape of the thing below: galleries, stairways, a cathedral vast enough that the cartographer had written *scale uncertain* in a cramped hand beside it.

"They call it the Glass Court," Mother Ash said. "Because the singers there become glass, in time. Frozen mid-note. Beautiful, they say. The ferryman's tales describe a cathedral where every pane is a person, and the whole structure sings the year into turning — winter's slow movement, spring's arrival, the long argument between summer and autumn. Four hundred years of perfect weather, purchased one voice at a time."

"How many?"

"No one knows. Hundreds. Thousands. The Court doesn't kill them — killing would end the song. It keeps them. Forever." She touched the map, just below the mountain's roots, where the ink was smudged as though many fingers had traced the same hopeless line. "And now the queen wants more. Vael's assessments aren't executions, whatever the criers say. The gifted aren't being destroyed. They're being *filed*."

Silas stood. His leg protested; he ignored it. He stood in front of the map with his ruined charms clicking against his chest — the luck-knots and fever-wards and traveler's-blessings he'd carried for two decades, each one a little deader than the last, each one a prayer that had been falling all this time not into silence but into a throat.

"Wren," he said.

"What of her?"

"She sings." His voice cracked on the word. "Ash, she *sings* like nothing I've heard before or since. If the Court eats song — if it's starving, if the queen is scraping the barrel for voices — then a girl like Wren isn't one soul among thousands. She's a feast. She's the vintage they've been waiting four centuries to pour."

Mother Ash was quiet for a long moment. In the lamplight her face finally committed to an age, and it was ancient, and it was grieving.

"Then you understand what I'm about to ask," she said. "And what I'm about to tell you, which is worse. There is a way down. A stair beneath the ossuary at Grayfall Ford, guarded by a ferryman who takes payment in things you cannot spare. The Choir has sent three people down that stair in the last ten years." She held up three fingers, then folded them down one by one. "None came back."

"And yet you're telling me where it is."

"I'm telling you because you'd find it anyway. Men with your look in their eyes always do." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to the register healers use for setting bones. "So hear me, Silas Whitlock. The Court will offer you anything. That's how it feeds. It shows you your heart's desire wearing a crown, and while you reach for it, it fits the collar. Whatever it promises you — your god, your power, your girl's safety — remember that everything under that mountain is glass, and glass reflects, and reflection is not the same as return."

He nodded. It was a lie of a nod, and they both knew it, and she let him go anyway, because she had buried enough hope to know it grows best when planted against advice.

At the foot of the stair, pausing with his hand on the rail, he asked the question he'd been carrying since the map.

"The god I served. If it's down there — if some piece of it still exists inside that place—"

"Then it remembers you," Mother Ash said. "That's the cruelty of it. Souls in the Court don't sleep. They sing. And part of what they sing, forever, is everything they lost."

He climbed into the tannery reek and the grey afternoon, and above him the sky was doing what the sky had done every day of his life — arriving on schedule, obedient, punctual as a debt collector.

For the first time, Silas Whitlock looked at a cloudless winter morning and felt not gratitude but fury.

Three days later, word reached the tannery that the Inquisitor's riders had been seen on the road to Grayfall Ford — and that among the names on her assessment list, written in the crown's own hand, was a singer called Wren.
