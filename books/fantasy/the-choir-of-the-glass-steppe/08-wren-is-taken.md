## 8. Wren Is Taken

The concert was Mother Ash's idea, and Silas had hated it from the first moment she said the word *audience*.

"Music is a door," she told him, in the candle-smoked cellar beneath the tannery where the Hollow Choir kept its true name. "They come for a song. They leave having seen that a singer is only a woman, a boy, an old man with cracked hands. Fear dies in rooms like that."

"Fear doesn't die," Silas said. "It changes clothes and comes back with soldiers."

But he had been outvoted, and worse — he had been *needed*. The Choir's couriers were stretched thin since the Edict; someone had to walk the route from the tannery to the granary lofts and back, checking the bolt-holes, counting heads, carrying the little tin lamp that meant *all clear* in a language of shutters and candles. So on the night of the concert, Silas Whitlock walked the smuggler's quarter with his collar up and his charms gone quiet as river stones, and he did not know — could not have known, though he would spend the rest of his life not knowing — that the Crown Inquisitor had bought the tannery foreman's silence for less than the price of one good horse.

The quarter was beautiful in the way of things about to end. Frost furred the edges of the drain-grates. Somewhere a fishwife argued cheerfully with her son about eels. Lamplight came through shutter-slats in long gold blades across the cobbles, and Silas stepped over each blade like a man crossing a stream on stones, because twenty years of habit does not care how weak your magic has grown. You still count exits. You still read shadows. You still feel, in the hollow place where your god used to speak, the particular pressure of being watched.

He felt it outside the chandler's shop, and dismissed it. He felt it again by the cooper's yard, and told himself it was only the cold.

The concert hall was the old salt warehouse on Tanner's Reach, its floor swept, its crates pushed into tiers, forty candles doing the work of a chandelier. Silas slipped in through the loading door at the back just as the room went quiet, and there she was.

Wren stood at the front with her hands folded, sixteen years old and utterly unafraid, wearing the blue dress Mother Ash had bribed off a pawnbroker and the ribbon — green, frayed at one end, the ribbon she had worn since she was nine and had once used to tie a splint on Silas's forearm when he broke it falling from a loft. Her hair was pinned up badly. She always pinned it up badly. She said pins were a conspiracy against singers.

She looked out at the crowd — dockworkers, a midwife, two deserters from the garrison, an old couple who ran the pie stall and had no gift at all except kindness — and she smiled the smile she saved for rooms full of frightened people.

"This one's old," she said. "Older than me. Older than most of you. My grandmother sang it, and hers before her, back when nobody had to be afraid of a song." A pause, and then, lighter: "So if you cry, blame my grandmother."

And she began.

Silas had heard her sing a hundred times. He had heard her sing him back from the edge of death when she was a child with borrowed magic and more courage than the gods she'd never met. But hearing Wren sing in a cellar for eleven people was one thing. Hearing her here, in this swept warehouse, with the frost pressing at the windows and the whole realm hunting people exactly like her — that was different. Her voice filled the rafters the way water fills a hull: quietly, inevitably, from below. The song was a threshing song, a working song, something women had sung over grain a thousand years ago, and she made it mean everything — the harvest, the hiding, the stubbornness of seed. The midwife wept openly. One of the deserters put his face in his hands.

And Silas stood by the loading door with his hand on the bar, doing the thing he had done for fifteen years, which was love her without ever finding a word for it that wasn't *duty*, and watch the door.

That was why he saw them first.

Not soldiers — Vael's people didn't wear soldier's red. They wore grey, unmarked, moving up both sides of Tanner's Reach with the unhurried coordination of men who had done this many times and been paid well for it. Six at the front. More behind. And between two buildings opposite the warehouse, a carriage with no crest, its lamps hooded, waiting like a spider at the mouth of a web.

Silas's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with winter.

* * *

Wren felt the song change before she understood why.

It was a thing that happened sometimes — the music would turn in her chest like a fish sensing a net, and she had learned, in fifteen years of learning to survive her own gift, to trust it. The threshing song wanted to go somewhere else now. It wanted to go low, and fast, and sharp.

She looked past the candlelight, past the wet eyes of the pie-stall couple, to the loading door at the back of the warehouse, where Silas stood rigid as a fencepost, and she read him the way she'd learned to read him: the set of his shoulders, the angle of his chin, the hand leaving the door-bar and coming up in a small, flat gesture she had seen exactly twice before, both times followed by running.

*Back door,* the gesture said. *Now.*

Her voice did not falter. That was the discipline Mother Ash had drilled into her until it lived in her bones: *the song is the shield, girl. As long as you are singing, you are a concert, not a crime.* So Wren kept singing while her mind raced ahead of her voice, cataloguing — the tiered crates (too slow to climb), the coal chute (too narrow for the fat midwife), the crowd itself, forty bodies between her and whatever was coming, and every one of them a person the Crown would call an accomplice if they were caught shielding her.

No. Not like that. Never like that.

She changed the song.

It was a small treason, musically speaking — she dropped the threshing song into a round, split the melody so half the room carried one line and half another, and wove underneath it, plain as plain could be, the old traveling verse: *when the road splits, take the left fork.* Mother Ash caught it instantly; Wren saw the old woman's chin dip a fraction of an inch. The deserters caught it too, or their feet did, shifting toward the left-hand wall where a service door stood behind a hanging sailcloth.

And that was when the front doors came open, and the grey men came in, and Inquisitor Vael walked between them into the candlelight like a woman arriving slightly early for a lecture.

Wren had heard the name whispered in cellars for months. *Vael takes them polite. That's the worst of it. Vael thanks you.*

The Inquisitor was smaller than Wren had imagined. Neat. Grey-eyed, grey-gloved, with a scholar's stoop and a voice pitched perfectly to carry without shouting.

"Good evening," Vael said, into the dying echo of the round. "Please don't stop on my account. I do enjoy music." She let her gaze travel the room the way an assessor travels a house. "I regret the interruption. I truly do. What follows will be easier for everyone if it remains what it appears to be — an assessment, conducted with courtesy, of persons the Crown has reason to believe are gifted." Her eyes found Wren and stayed there, gentle and terrible. "Beginning, I think, with our soloist."

The room did not move. Forty people breathing at once, all of them thinking the same coward's arithmetic: *not me, please, not me, I have children, I am old, I am careful, I have been careful.*

Wren understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the arithmetic was going to win. That these good, frightened people were going to let her be taken, and that she could not even hate them for it, because she had spent her whole life being careful in exactly the same way.

Her eyes went to the loading door.

Silas was gone.

* * *

He had not abandoned her. He needed three things and he needed them in ninety seconds: the back alley cleared, the garrison patrol on Cooper Street moved, and a distraction large enough to pull six grey men toward one side of a building with two.

He got none of them.

The alley behind the warehouse was already held — two of Vael's greys, patient as furniture. Cooper Street's patrol had been pulled, which told Silas everything: this raid had been arranged with the garrison itself, paid for, scheduled. The tannery foreman. It had to be. Silas filed the name away in the place where he kept debts, and went through the cooper's yard instead, over a fence slick with frost, tearing his palm open on a nail-head and not feeling it, because the hollow place where his god had lived was screaming — not words, it hadn't spoken words in twenty years, but pressure, a white silent *now, now, now* like a fist against the inside of his ribs.

He came around the salt warehouse's seaward corner in time to hear, through the wall, Vael's courteous voice saying *our soloist*, and every plan he had built in the last thirty seconds turned to ash.

There was no clever version of this. There was no charm left strong enough, no trick, no route. There was a door, and a girl behind it, and men between.

Silas Whitlock put his shoulder to the loading door and went in.

* * *

Later — in the cells, in the years after, in every night for the rest of her life — Wren would try to order those minutes and fail. Memory kept them all happening at once.

The loading door bursting inward off one hinge. Silas coming through the candle-smoke with blood on one hand and his coat torn at the shoulder, looking older than she had ever seen him and younger too, the way he'd looked in the story Mother Ash told new recruits, the mercenary who'd once killed four slavers to get a stolen child back. The greys turning. Vael turning, unhurried, interested, like a woman watching weather arrive on schedule.

"*Run*," Silas said, and it wasn't a shout. It was worse than a shout. It was quiet and cracked and aimed at her alone, and it was the first time in fifteen years Wren had ever heard fear in his voice.

She ran. That was the part that shamed her afterward, though Mother Ash would later tell her, fiercely, that it was the part that proved she'd listened — the left fork, the service door, the sailcloth. She ran, and the crowd broke around her like water around a stone, and somewhere behind her a grey man said "Inquisitor?" in a question, and Vael answered:

"No. Let him. Watch the girl."

Let him. *Let* him. As if Silas were a current in a river whose course she had already charted.

Wren hit the service door with her shoulder. It stuck — swollen with damp, painted shut sometime in the last century — and she heard, behind her, the particular sound of a body hitting crates, and Silas's voice raised now, actually raised, saying words she couldn't make out, and the greys moving, and the candles guttering in the draft of all that motion.

She got the door open two inches. Cold air came through like mercy.

A hand closed on her hair.

Not rough. That was the horror of it, the detail she would carry longest: the grip was *careful*, gloved fingers gathering her pinned-up hair the way a librarian handles a rare book, and Vael's voice, close to her ear, almost kind:

"There you are. Don't struggle, please. You'll only hurt yourself, and I should hate for your voice to be damaged. It's a remarkable voice. We've been listening to reports of it for some time."

"They'll come for her," Silas was shouting, from somewhere under a pile of grey coats, "Vael! You hear me? Whatever hole you put her in, I will—"

"Yes," Vael said, conversationally, steering Wren backward through the chaos with that gentle terrible grip. "I imagine you will. That's rather the point of you, isn't it, Master Whitlock? Do give my regards to Mother Ash. Tell her the foreman sends his apologies — he has a family."

The door. The cold. The hooded carriage, its door already open, its interior dark as a well.

Wren did the last thing she could think to do. As they pushed her toward it, she tore the ribbon from her hair — her grandmother's ribbon, green, frayed, the one she'd tied on a bleeding mercenary's arm when she was nine years old — and dropped it on the frosted cobbles, deliberately, obviously, where anyone following would have to see it.

*Here,* the ribbon said. *I was here. Come find me.*

Then the carriage swallowed her, and the door closed, and the world went dark and smelled of iron and old wool, and through the wall of the carriage she heard the last sounds of the salt warehouse: a woman's voice thanking everyone for their cooperation, and a man's voice, raw and ruined, calling her name — not *Wren*, even, but the old child-name, the secret one, the one only two living people knew —

— and then wheels, and frost, and the city sliding away beneath her like water under ice.

* * *

Silas got free of the greys in eleven seconds, which would have been a record if anyone had been counting, and reached the cobbles outside in fourteen, and by then the carriage was a sound, and then a shape, and then nothing.

He stood in the middle of Tanner's Reach with the frost smoking off the stones and his torn hand dripping, and the empty street looked back at him with all the indifference of a world that had taken exactly what it came for.

On the cobbles, catching the lamplight, lay a scrap of green.

He knelt. His knees hit the frost and he didn't feel that either. He picked the ribbon up with both hands, the way you pick up something that might still be alive, and it was warm — it couldn't have been warm, it was impossible, it was frost and wool and wishful thinking — and he closed his fist around it and stayed there, kneeling in the empty street, while behind him the Hollow Choir's survivors drifted out of the broken warehouse one by one and did not approach him, because there are griefs that are walls and griefs that are fires, and everyone could see which kind this was.

Mother Ash came last. She stood over him a long moment, this old woman who had buried three generations of hunted children, and when she finally spoke her voice was stripped down to wire.

"The mountain," she said. "They're taking her under the mountain, Silas. Same as the others."

Silas opened his hand. The ribbon lay across his palm, frayed end pointing south, toward the Glass Steppe, toward the black mass of the mountain that made the seasons turn.

"I know where they sell souls," he said. His voice came out level, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "Now I need to know how to buy one back."

Mother Ash looked at him for a long time — at the hollowed-out mercenary, the godless man, the oath-keeper with nothing left to keep an oath with — and something moved behind her eyes that was pity trying very hard not to become despair.

"Then come inside," she said. "Because the price will kill you. And I need you to hear that from someone who loves you, before the ferryman tells you himself."

Far to the south, beneath the frozen Steppe, a glass pane hummed in the dark, and somewhere in the eternal choir a new voice was being fitted to the song — and that voice was singing, very softly, variations.
