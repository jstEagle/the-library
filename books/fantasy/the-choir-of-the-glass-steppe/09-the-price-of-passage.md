## 9. The Price of Passage

The river under the Steppe had no name, because names were the first thing it took.

Silas found it by following the cold. Not the ordinary cold of late autumn, which clung to the skin and could be argued with — but a colder cold, a cold that came up through the soles of his boots like a question being asked of him. He had walked three days since leaving the Choir's last safehouse, three days with Mother Ash's directions folded against his heart and Wren's ribbon tied around his wrist, and on the third evening the tunnel he'd been following opened without warning into a cavern where the air itself seemed to have been wrung out, every drop of warmth pressed from it long ago.

The river ran black and slow at the bottom of a shelf of wet stone. It did not reflect the faint phosphorescence that clung to the cavern's ceiling in patches, like lichen made of drowned stars. It did not reflect anything at all. Water that gave nothing back.

And moored at the edge of it, rocking gently though there was no wind, was a boat.

It was a poor-looking thing — a flat-bottomed skiff, its planks gray as old teeth, patched in places with metal that might have been coffin-lid. A single lantern hung from a crooked post at the stern, and inside the lantern burned no flame Silas could name: a soft, sourceless glow the color of a candle seen through milk.

The ferryman sat waiting in the stern, hands folded on the tiller, as if he had been sitting there for a very long time and would go on sitting there for a very long time more, and neither fact troubled him.

He was not tall. He was not short. He wore a coat of river-gray wool buttoned to the throat, and a hat with a wide brim that threw his face into shadow — except that when Silas came closer, walking down the slick stone shelf with the care of a man who had learned not to trust his own knees, he saw that the shadow was not cast *by* the brim. The shadow simply lived there, settled over the ferryman's features like a bird that had found a comfortable perch. He could see the suggestion of a mouth, the suggestion of eyes, and nothing else would resolve.

"Passage," Silas said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. Three days of silence will do that; three days of talking only to yourself, and only in whispers, because the tunnels above carried sound the way a snitch carries gossip.

The ferryman said nothing. The lantern swayed once, gently, and was still.

"I need to go down." Silas crouched at the water's edge, keeping his weight off his bad leg — the one the inquisitor's hound had torn open back in Veyra's sewers, the one that had healed crooked even with Mother Ash's salves. "Under the mountain. All the way."

Still nothing. But Silas had bargained before — with smugglers, with warlords, once with a plague-spirit in the marshes south of Corvane who had wanted a year of his laughter — and he knew the shape of a listening silence. The ferryman was listening the way a ledger listens.

So Silas laid out what he had.

He untied the pouch at his belt first, the one that had grown lighter every year for twenty years, and turned it out onto the flat stone between them. The charms tumbled out in a small, sad heap: a luck-knot braided from three colors of thread, still warm; a warding coin stamped with a god's face worn nearly smooth; a splinter of rowan wood that wept sap when danger was near; a knucklebone that always pointed home; two others whose purposes he'd half forgotten, bought or bartered or earned in years when his god still spoke and the magic had come through him like light through clean glass.

They looked very small on the stone. They looked like the contents of a child's pocket.

"That's everything," Silas said. "Twenty years of work. Every charm I've got left."

The shadow on the ferryman's face shifted — the suggestion of a mouth, perhaps, curving. Or perhaps not.

"No," Silas said, understanding arriving in his gut like a swallowed stone. "Not enough. I know. That's the opening offer."

He sat down on the wet stone, because his leg was shaking and there was no dignity left worth protecting, and he thought about what a man actually has to sell.

* * *

The trouble with a silent god, Silas had learned, is that you keep praying anyway. Twenty years of habit does not break just because heaven stopped answering. He still woke some mornings with the words half-formed behind his teeth — the old dawn-blessing, the road-prayer, the small thanks for small mercies — and each morning the words fell into the same silence a stone falls into a well, and each morning he got up and worked his fading charms and told himself the silence didn't mean anything, couldn't mean anything, because the alternative was unthinkable.

Now he crouched at the edge of a river that ate names, bargaining with its keeper, and understood that he had been selling pieces of himself for years already. Every charm he'd worked since the silence had cost him something he hadn't known how to count. He'd thought it was stamina. He'd thought it was years off his life. Now, looking at the ferryman's patient shadowed face, he suspected it had been something else entirely — that he'd been spending himself in installments all along, and this was simply the first honest transaction.

"My name," Silas said.

The lantern brightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Silas Whitlock," he said aloud, and heard the words come out of him and felt them leave — actually felt it, a sensation like a tooth coming free, like a splinter drawn from a wound too deep to have noticed until it moved. "That's my price. My name goes in the boat instead of me. You carry an unnamed man down the mountain, and when you bring him back up — if you bring him back up — you don't give it back unless he can prove it's his."

The shadow considered. The river made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than any sound.

"And," Silas went on, because the ledger was open now and he might as well show the whole account, "a memory. One memory. Of my god's face."

That landed differently. Even the phosphorescent patches on the ceiling seemed to dim, as if the cavern itself were leaning in.

He had never spoken of it directly, not even to Mother Ash, not even in the confessional dark of the Choir's safehouses. But he remembered the face. Of course he remembered the face. You do not serve a god twenty years and forget the face you saw the one time your god showed itself — the night he lay dying in a burned village fifteen years ago, gut-open and going cold, and something vast and grieving had bent over him out of the smoke, and a child's song had threaded through it like gold wire through cloth, and he had lived.

He remembered the face. He had built his whole stubborn life on remembering the face.

"You'll take that?" His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated it. "Out of everything. You'll take the one thing that—"

The ferryman raised one hand. The gesture was almost gentle. Almost apologetic. And in the gesture Silas read the whole terrible grammar of the place: the river did not want his grief. The river took payment, not pain. But the memory of a god's face — that was currency here, under the mountain, where gods had been spent like coin for centuries. Where his god, perhaps, had been spent.

Perhaps that was why the memory was worth so much. Perhaps the Court below collected such things. Perhaps somewhere beneath the Glass Steppe, in whatever vault held the devoured, there was a shelf, and on the shelf a jar, and in the jar—

No. He shut that door. He had learned, in three days of walking, to shut doors quickly or go mad.

"All right," Silas said. "All three. Charms, name, memory. Passage down."

The ferryman extended one hand, palm up. The sleeve fell back from a wrist that was either very pale or not flesh at all, and Silas found he could not look at it directly — not because it was horrible, but because his eyes kept sliding off it the way water slides off oiled leather.

He gathered up the charms first, one by one, and pressed them into that palm. Each one left him with a small, distinct loss. The luck-knot went with a pang — he'd braided it himself, in the old days, when his hands had been steady and his god's favor had run through the threads like current through wire. The warding coin went with relief, almost; it had never worked right since the silence, and carrying it had felt like carrying a portrait of someone dead. The knucklebone that pointed home went last, and hardest, because home had been an idea he'd held onto through everything, and now he was giving away the compass to it.

When the last charm crossed, the ferryman closed his fingers, and the charms were gone. Not hidden. Gone, the way a word is gone once it's spoken into deep water.

Then the hand extended again, palm up, waiting.

"The name first," Silas said. "Let me keep the memory till the far shore. If I'm going to forget my god's face, I want to do it on the water, where I can't run from it."

A pause. Then the shadowed head inclined — a fraction, a concession, a merchant agreeing to terms. The ferryman's other hand came up and made a small beckoning motion, and Silas felt it happen: the name lifting out of him.

There was no pain. That was the worst part. There should have been pain. Instead there was a quieting, a smooth subtraction, as if a room he'd lived in all his life had lost one piece of furniture and he couldn't say which. He opened his mouth to protest — to say *wait, I've changed my mind* — and discovered he didn't know what to protest about. Something had been taken. Something important. He could feel the absence the way you feel a missing stair in the dark, the foot finding nothing where the world promised ground.

"What—" he started, and stopped, because he couldn't remember what the sentence was for.

The ferryman gestured to the boat.

* * *

The crossing was longer than the river was wide.

Silas sat in the bow with his arms around his knees, watching the sourceless lantern glow at the stern and trying to take inventory of himself, the way you'd inventory a house after a burglary. He had a body. That much was certain: the bad leg ached, the scar across his ribs pulled when he breathed deep, his hands were his hands, scarred and ink-stained and steady enough. He had a purpose. That survived the crossing intact — Wren, taken; Wren, below; Wren, to be found. Purpose, it turned out, lived deeper than names.

But when he reached for the ordinary furniture of himself — the mother who'd died when he was nine, the master who'd taught him knot-work, the first job, the first kill, the first kindness — he found the shapes of the things without their handles. He knew a woman had loved him once, in Corvane, and could not recall her face, only that she'd laughed at his jokes and left him for a safer man, and that both facts had hurt in ways he'd needed years to file down. He knew he'd been born somewhere green. Somewhere green. The word *green* sat in his mind like a coin from a country he'd never visited.

And his own name — he could feel the hole where it had been. When he tried to think *I am*, the sentence simply stopped, politely, like a servant waiting for instructions that never came.

Halfway across, the ferryman spoke. First words since the shore, and the voice was exactly what Silas had expected and dreaded: mild, unhurried, the voice of someone reading terms aloud.

"The memory," it said. "Now, if you please."

Silas's hands tightened on his knees. "On the far shore. We agreed."

"We agreed the far shore." The lantern swayed. "This is the middle. In the middle, everything is negotiable. That is what the middle is *for*."

The river around them had gone very still. No current, no ripple, no reflection — and now Silas realized the stillness had a texture, an attention, like the held breath of something enormous listening to whether the bargain would hold.

"You'll get it at the shore," Silas said. His voice sounded strange to him — not because the name was gone, but because without the name the voice had nothing to anchor its pitch, and it drifted, older and younger than he was. "I keep my bargains. Ask anyone I've ever—"

He stopped. Because the proof he'd been reaching for was reputation, and reputation was woven out of a name, and the name was riding in the bottom of this boat in a pouch that didn't exist, and there was no one left to ask.

The ferryman waited. The shadow on its face did not move.

"Here," Silas said finally. "Fine. Here. Middle of the river. Take it."

He closed his eyes and went to the place where the memory lived — the burned village, the smoke, the vast grieving presence bending over him, the face. He had guarded that face for fifteen years. He had taken it out on bad nights and looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he's saving. And now he lifted it, whole, and held it out, and felt it leave him with a gentleness that was obscene — no tearing, no wound, just a warm subtraction, a lamp carried out of a room, and then the room was dark and he could not remember what the lamp had shown him.

He knew he had served a god. He knew the god had gone silent. He knew there had been a face, once, that he had loved.

He could not have described it to save his life. He could not have sworn, under torture, whether it had been kind or terrible, young or ancient, whether it had eyes at all. It was as if someone had taken a portrait from a wall and left the paler rectangle of wallpaper behind, and told him a picture had hung there, and he believed them, and that was all he had. Belief. Secondhand. A rumor of his own life.

"There," the ferryman said softly. "Was that so hard?"

Silas didn't answer. He was looking at his own hands, turning them over, checking — the way you check your pockets after a cutpurse works a crowd — for whatever else might be missing. Hands. Scars. The ribbon at his wrist, red silk gone gray with tunnel dust. Wren. The name *Wren* was still there, thank every god there wasn't. Wren was still there, bright and load-bearing, the one beam left holding up the roof.

"Hold on to that one," the ferryman said, watching him with its suggestion of eyes. "The girl. The river can't take what's freely given, and you gave her your protection long ago. Debts of that kind are iron. Even I don't touch them." A pause, and the mouth-shape might have curved. "Which is fortunate for you. Iron debts are the only things that float on this water besides my boat."

The far shore came up slowly, a shelf of black stone rising out of nothing, and set into it — carved deep and worn shallow by uncountable feet — the beginning of a stair. The stair went down. It went down farther than the lantern light could argue with, and along its walls, even from this distance, Silas could see the faint gleam of pictures. Murals. A history in painted bone and glass.

He stood, and his leg screamed, and he stood anyway.

"One more thing," the ferryman said as Silas stepped onto the stone. "A courtesy, free of charge, because you paid honest and haggled little. Below, they will ask who you are. Everyone asks. It's the first question the mountain asks, and it never stops asking." The shadow tilted. "You won't be able to answer. So when it asks — and it will ask in your own voice, remember that, it will ask *in your own voice* — tell it what you told me. Not your name. Your errand."

Silas turned. "What did I tell you?"

The ferryman considered him for a long moment, and something in the shadowed face — pity, perhaps, or its distant cousin — almost surfaced.

"You'll remember," it said. "Errands survive. Names don't. It's why I prefer them, professionally speaking." The ferryman drew back from the shore, and the boat slid away from the stone without oar or sail, back toward the middle, back toward the negotiable dark. "Go down. Find your singer. And when you find her — when you stand in front of whatever's left of the divine — try to remember which of you is the ghost."

The lantern shrank. The black water gave back nothing, as it had given back nothing all along, and soon there was nothing to give back.

Silas stood alone at the top of the ossuary stair, an unnamed man with a red ribbon on his wrist and a hole in his head shaped like a god. Below him the murals gleamed, and the cold rose to meet him like a tide, and somewhere far beneath — deeper than the stair, deeper than the dark — he could hear it.

Singing.

Hundreds of voices, blended perfect and bloodless, pouring upward through the bones of the mountain. And threaded through the harmony, thin and clear and unmistakable even at this distance, one voice that cracked deliberately, beautifully, off the note —

—and Silas was moving before he knew he'd decided, down into the dark, toward the sound.
