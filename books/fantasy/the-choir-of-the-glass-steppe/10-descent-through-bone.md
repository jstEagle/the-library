## 10. Descent Through Bone

The stair began where the river ended.

That was how the ferryman had put it, poling his black boat through water that did not reflect the torchlight — *the river ends, and the stair begins, and between them there is nothing you can carry.* Silas had paid. He had paid everything the creature asked: his charms, one by one, laid out on the gunwale like a merchant's wares; his name, spoken aloud and swallowed; and last of all the memory of his god's face, which he had guarded for twenty years the way a starving man guards the last heel of bread.

He could not remember it now. That was the strange arithmetic of the bargain — he knew *that* he remembered, knew the shape of the absence the way you know a missing tooth, but the face itself was gone, scooped out and handed over to a thing with eyes like drowned lanterns. The ferryman had smiled when he took it. Smiled, and said, *You'll want it back someday. They always do.*

Then the boat touched stone, and the ferryman was gone, and Silas Whitlock — or whatever remained of him, nameless now, charmless, carrying only a knife and a ribbon faded to the color of old blood — stood at the top of a stair that went down into the dark like a throat.

He counted the first hundred steps because counting was something. Because a man with no name needed some other way to hold himself together, and numbers were honest. One hundred steps, and the last grey light of the upper world died behind him. Two hundred, and the air changed, taking on a mineral coldness that tasted of iron and old prayers. Three hundred, and he understood that the dark down here was not an absence of light but a presence, something thick and patient that pressed against his eyes like wool.

He lit no torch. He had no magic left to light one with, and ordinary fire would have shown him too much. Instead he walked with one hand on the wall, and the wall was warm.

That was the first wrongness. Stone this deep should have been cold as a grave. But under his palm the rock held a faint, living heat, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and it pulsed — slowly, so slowly he might have imagined it — in a rhythm he almost recognized. A heartbeat. Or the ghost of one. Or the memory of ten thousand heartbeats absorbed into the mountain's hunger until the mountain itself had learned to beat.

*Keep walking,* he told himself. *Counting is something. Walking is more.*

***

The murals began at five hundred steps.

He felt them before he saw them — a change in the texture under his fingers, stone giving way to something smoother, glazed, faintly glassy. When his eyes finally adjusted enough to make out shapes, he wished they hadn't. The stair widened here into a gallery, and the walls on either side rose up in panels of fused bone and colored mineral, and every panel told the same story from a different angle.

The devouring.

He had heard the story as a boy, in fragments, the way children hear forbidden things — the old gods, the First Bargain, the price of the turning seasons. Mother Ash had told him truer versions in the Choir's cellars, her voice dropping low whenever footsteps passed overhead. But hearing a thing and seeing it carved by hands that had *been there* were different sacraments entirely.

The first panel showed the sky full of gods. He could not count them; they were rendered as vast interlocking figures of gold and white, faces radiant, arms spread over a green country. Rivers ran silver at their feet. The seasons turned around them like dancers. And below, small as ants, the people of the Steppe looked up with painted joy.

The second panel showed the founding of the crown. Kings kneeling. A bargain struck in a circle of standing stones. And rising from beneath the earth — rendered in blacks and deep bruise-purples, so dark the glaze seemed to swallow the torchlight he wasn't carrying — the first suggestion of the Court Below. A mouth. Only a mouth, opening in the mountain's root.

The third panel showed the gods falling.

They fell the way wheat falls before a scythe. The artist had given each god a distinct death: one torn apart by hands of glass, one folded down and down until it fit inside a box no bigger than a heart, one simply *poured out*, its golden body draining away in long ribbons that flowed toward the mountain's mouth. And Silas stopped before that panel for a long time, his hand flat against the warm stone, because the ribbons of poured-out gold were familiar.

He had spent twenty years feeling that pouring. He had called it silence. He had called it abandonment. He had prayed into a void and hated the void for not answering, and all the while the answer had been here — drunk, swallowed, burned as fuel to keep winter coming on schedule and spring arriving punctually, so that the realm above could live its comfortable, lawful, soul-eating life.

*You weren't silent,* he said to the mural, to the god in the mural, to whoever was listening. His voice came out cracked and small in the gallery's dark. *You were eaten. And I blamed you.*

The stone pulsed under his hand. Once. Twice. Like something acknowledging him. Like something *amused*.

He took his hand away and kept walking.

***

By eight hundred steps he had lost track of what he was.

This was the thing no one warned you about, selling your name. It wasn't just that other people couldn't say it. It was that *he* couldn't. He reached for *Silas Whitlock* and found a smooth blank place, a socket with nothing in it. He reached for *mercenary* and found a job description with no man attached. He reached for the oath — the old oath, the one he'd sworn over Wren's small sleeping body fifteen years ago, *while I draw breath, no harm comes to you* — and that, thank every starving star, that still held. The oath was the last hook holding his selfhood to the world, and he clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a spar.

*The oath remembers me even if I don't,* he thought, and found the thought both comforting and terrible.

He spoke aloud sometimes, just to hear a voice, just to prove there was someone inside it. He told the dark about the dockworker who'd bought the luck-knot. He told the dark about burning his ledgers, page by page, watching twenty years of quiet charity go up in smoke. He told the dark about the ribbon in his pocket, and the girl who'd worn it, and the song she'd sung over his dying body when she was seven years old and should have been afraid.

The dark didn't answer. But sometimes, between one step and the next, he thought he heard singing — far below, thin as wire, many voices braided into one. The choir. Her somewhere in it. He walked faster when he heard it, and hated himself for the times he couldn't hear it, and counted steps against the panic.

Nine hundred. A thousand. The murals continued, and he made himself look at every one, because forgetting was the mountain's whole method and he refused to cooperate. The later panels showed the harvests. Generations of souls marching down stairs exactly like this one, rendered in procession after procession, their painted faces calm, their painted hands empty. Here and there the artist had broken pattern — a single figure looking back, a single figure resisting, a single figure with raised fists — and every one of those rebels was depicted mid-dissolution, coming apart into motes of light that drifted obediently down toward the mouth.

*So that's my portrait already painted,* Silas thought. *Five hundred years early and better dressed.*

He laughed at that. It came out wrong — too loud, too jagged, bouncing off the glassy walls and returning to him sounding like several people laughing, none of them quite him. He stopped laughing.

***

The wraiths came at eleven hundred steps.

He heard them first: a dry whispering, like wind through reeds, except there was no wind down here and no reeds, only bone and stone and the mountain's slow pulse. Then the temperature dropped — truly dropped, for the first time since the stair began, cold biting through his coat — and he understood that the warmth in the walls was not comfort. It was appetite. The mountain warmed itself on what it caught, and these things were what it caught.

They came out of side galleries he'd taken for solid rock. They were shaped like people who had been wrung out — long, attenuated, translucent, faces stretched into expressions that might once have been screaming and had long ago worn smooth into something worse: *hunger without hope of satisfaction.* Soul-hungry. Strays, maybe. Souls that had slipped loose from the harvest and never made it down, or never made it *up*, lingering in the dark and feeding on whatever warmth passed by.

And he was the warmest thing this stair had seen in centuries.

There were six of them. Maybe eight. In the dark they moved like smoke deciding to be men, and they had no weapons because they didn't need any — they came at him with open, empty hands, and he knew without knowing how he knew that to be touched by those hands was to be wrung out too, to become one more attenuated whisper haunting a stairway forever.

The old Silas would have had answers. A warding sigil. A charm of severance. Twenty years ago, with his god loud in his blood, he could have burned six wraiths with a word and a gesture and barely broken stride.

The new Silas had a knife, a ribbon, and an oath.

So he did what the desperate do. He fought like a man, not a mage.

The first one lunged and he dropped under its reaching hands — hands passed through the space where his head had been and the cold of them kissed his scalp like frostbite — and he drove the knife up into what served it for a chest. The blade met no resistance and all the resistance in the world at once, sliding through the thing's body while the body screamed in a voice like tearing linen. It recoiled, unraveling slightly at the edges, and Silas learned two things in the same heartbeat: steel hurt them, and hurt was not enough.

The second and third came together, flanking. He backed against the mural wall — the warm wall, the hungry wall, a bad place to put your spine but the only one with no enemies behind it — and fought them off in short, ugly bursts. Knife hand. Elbow. Boot. He was forty-eight years old and tired and half-starved and he had not slept since the ferry, and every exchange cost him breath he could not easily buy back. A wraith's fingers brushed his forearm and the flesh there went numb, deeply, wrongly numb, and he felt something *pull* at him — not his blood, not his strength, but something finer. Something that felt horrifyingly like the edges of himself. His memories of rain. His knowledge of knots. Small things, unimportant things, sipped away like wine from a cup.

*It's eating my namelessness,* he realized, giddy with horror. *I sold my name and now these things are drinking the rest.*

And in that moment of horror, something answered.

Not his god. Not power. Nothing so grand. Just — the oath. Rising in him like a coal blown bright. *While I draw breath, no harm comes to you.* An oath freely given, sworn over a child, kept for fifteen years without reward. He had learned tonight, from murals older than kingdoms, that the Court Below ran on taken things — taken souls, taken gods, taken songs. Everything down here was stolen and therefore everything down here was brittle.

But an oath freely given was the one currency the dark could not digest.

He didn't decide to speak. The words came up through him like water finding a crack:

"You can't have me. I'm *spent*. Every coin of me is promised."

The nearest wraith froze — actually froze, hung in the air like smoke meeting glass. And Silas, understanding nothing and believing completely, pushed off the wall and *spoke the oath aloud*, all of it, the old formal words he'd sworn that night over Wren's bed, in the cadence of binding-magic though he had no magic left to bind with. His voice cracked on the third line. He was crying and didn't remember starting. The words rang down the stairwell and came back multiplied, and the wraiths shrank from the sound as if it burned, folding inward, unraveling, dissolving into threads of grey light that drifted — upward. All of them. Upward, toward the distant, impossible sky.

Six wraiths, freed by a sentence. He stood in the sudden stillness shaking, knife still raised, and laughed again — quieter this time, and real.

"Almost nothing," he said to the dark. "You told me I'd fight with almost nothing." He couldn't remember who had told him. Someone had. "Almost nothing turns out to be enough, if it's freely given."

His forearm stayed numb where they'd touched him. He flexed the fingers and they answered, mostly. He chose not to inventory what else was missing. There would be time for grief at the bottom, if there was a bottom, if there was a him left to grieve.

***

Twelve hundred steps. Thirteen hundred. The singing grew louder.

No longer thin as wire now — it had body, weight, harmony upon harmony, hundreds of voices woven into a fabric of sound that filled the stairwell the way water fills a hull. And threaded through it, unmistakable, impossible, *hers* — a voice he'd know in any crowd, in any century, in any state of living or dead. Not the child's voice from fifteen years ago. A woman's voice now, matured and magnificent, and yet the same voice, the same stubborn grain of it, the same slight bend she always put on the high notes as if refusing to let any note be exactly what it was told to be.

Wren. Alive. Singing. Rebellious even in captivity, bending every note.

Silas stood on the thirteenth-hundredth step with his hand pressed to his own sternum, feeling his heart slam, and wept without shame, because the oath was intact and the debt was alive and somewhere below him, past the last of the murals, past the final painted procession of the taken, the girl who had sung him back from death was singing still.

The stair ended.

Before him, where the tunnel should have continued into blind rock, the passage opened onto light — not firelight, not daylight, but a radiance with no source, white-gold and total, pouring up from a vast space below. Warm air rose against his face, smelling of ozone and lilies and something older underneath, something like the inside of a bell. The singing rolled up out of that light in waves he could feel in his teeth.

And silhouetted against the glow, descending toward him along a causeway of clear glass, came figures. Many figures. Gliding, graceful, robed in colors that shifted like oil on water, and at their head moved one shape taller than the rest, crowned in what looked like frozen lightning, whose face — as it drew near, resolving out of the blaze — was beautiful, and ancient, and made entirely of paned glass behind which lights moved like trapped weather.

The Regent of Glass had come up to meet him personally.

"Well," said the Regent, and its voice was a chord, many voices speaking as one, and one of those voices — faint, buried, half-erased — was one Silas had spent twenty years praying to and could no longer remember. "You're late. We've been keeping your seat warm."

Behind the Regent, far below in the blazing cathedral of the Court, the choir swelled — and within it, bent high notes and all, Wren's voice rose alone for one defiant measure before the harmony swallowed it again.

Silas gripped the ribbon in his pocket until the fabric bit his palm, and started down.
