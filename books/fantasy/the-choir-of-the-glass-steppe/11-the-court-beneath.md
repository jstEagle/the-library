## 11. The Court Beneath

The stair gave way to a gallery, and the gallery gave way to glass.

Silas had no other word for it. He had walked through cathedrals in Veyra City whose builders had spent lifetimes trying to make stone reach toward heaven, and every one of them would have knelt — if stone could kneel — before what opened beneath the Steppe like the inside of a held breath.

The cavern was vast beyond measuring. His eyes tried to fix its edges and failed; the walls curved away into a haze of light that had no source he could name, though everything glowed. Panes of glass — some tall as watchtowers, some small as coffin lids — rose from the floor in ranks and clusters and solitary splendor, thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one lit from within by a slow pulse of color that swelled and faded with the rhythm of a heartbeat too large for any single chest.

And each pane held a singer.

They stood frozen mid-gesture, mouths open on notes they had been singing when the glass took them. Some had their arms raised. Some had their hands pressed flat against the inner surface of their prisons, as if even now, after however many centuries, they were still trying to push through. Their faces were not contorted. That was the worst of it, Silas thought. They looked *attentive*. They looked like people listening to something beautiful just past the edge of hearing, leaning toward it, giving themselves to it entirely —

— and the sound. The sound came from everywhere and nowhere, a harmony so vast and so seamless that at first he mistook it for silence, the way a man born in a loud room mistakes the roar for stillness. It was only when he stopped walking, stopped breathing hard from the descent, that he could pick the voices out of it: a bass line deep enough to feel in his back teeth, a shimmer of sopranos like light off ice, and threading through all of it, patient as erosion, the melody.

He knew that melody. Every soul in Veyra knew it without knowing it. It was the reason spring followed winter. It was the reason the rains came in their month and the harvest ripened in its season. Farmers sang snatches of it to their crops without knowing why. Mothers hummed it over cradles. It was the oldest song in the world, and it was being sung down here, in the dark, by prisoners.

*The seasons are not weather,* Mother Ash had said. *They are a performance. And the performers have never once been asked if they wanted the part.*

Silas stood at the mouth of the gallery with his hand on the cold wall and let the awe move through him, because there was no stopping it. Whatever this place had done — whatever it was doing to Wren at this very moment — it was beautiful first, and he hated himself for how much of his fear it burned away, just for a moment, just long enough for him to take three steps forward before the horror caught up.

Because now he could see the panes more clearly, and clarity was a knife.

The nearest one held an old man, gray-bearded, dressed in the style of two hundred years gone, his mouth open around a note that had sustained the autumn of some forgotten age. The frost on the inner surface of his glass had grown in fern-patterns across his cheeks, and where it touched him, his skin had gone translucent, like wax held too near a flame. He was not decaying. He was *thinning*. Being sung out of himself, one note at a time, and poured into the world above as mild October air.

Beyond him, a young woman in a scholar's robes. Beyond her, a child — a child no older than eight, hands folded, eyes closed, singing a summer into being that she would never see.

Silas's legs carried him forward without his permission. He moved along the ranks of them, reading faces the way he'd once read ledgers, looking for one face among the thousands, and the choir's harmony poured over him and through him and he felt it working at him gently, the way water works at a stone, smoothing his terror, rounding his grief, offering to fold him into the music —

He bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted blood, and the pain cut a narrow channel through the song, and in that channel he heard it again.

Wren.

Not the swallowed measure from before. A thread of her, high and bent and deliberately, stubbornly *off* — a quarter-tone sharp where the harmony wanted true, a grace note falling where none belonged. Small rebellions, stitched into the fabric of the great song like flaws in a tapestry that only a weaver would notice. She was still in there. She was still *herself*, in whatever way a person could be themselves inside a cathedral of glass, and the relief hit him so hard he had to put his shoulder against a pane to stay upright.

The pane was warm. It pulsed under his palm like the flank of a sleeping animal.

"Don't touch the singers."

The voice came from behind and below — dry, unhurried, human in a way nothing else down here had been. Silas turned.

A figure stood on the gallery steps: a stooped old man in livery so faded its colors had surrendered to gray, holding a lantern that gave off no light Silas could see but somehow illuminated the space around it anyway. His face was deeply lined and utterly calm, the calm of a man who has done a terrible job for so long that the terribleness has worn smooth.

"I said," the old man repeated, "don't touch the singers. The glass remembers warmth. It gets ideas."

"Who are you?"

"Understeward." He said it the way another man might say *gravedigger* or *executioner* — a trade, plain and shameful and necessary. "I keep the ranks orderly. I fit the new ones." His eyes went to Silas's pocket, to the shape of the ribbon beneath the cloth, and something flickered there — not pity, exactly. Recognition. "You're the one the Regent came up to meet. That hasn't happened in four hundred years. I'd have dressed better if I'd known."

"Where is she?" Silas asked. "The girl taken last night. Dark hair. She sings."

"They all sing," the Understeward said. "That's rather the point." But he turned and began to walk, and after a moment Silas understood it was an invitation, and followed.

* * *

They descended through the ranks of the frozen, and Silas made himself look at them, made himself count them the way he'd once counted coin, because counting was a kind of witness and witness was all he had left to give. Ten thousand. More. Generations of the gifted, harvested across centuries — not just since the Purge Edict, not just since Vael. Since the beginning. Since the gods fell silent.

Since the gods were *eaten*.

He passed a pane that stopped him cold. The singer inside wore armor — old armor, ceremonial, chased with sigils Silas recognized from the ruined shrines of the hill country, from the faith he had served for twenty years and mourned for twenty more. The man's face was serene. Around his throat, half-faded, lay the mark of the Quiet God — *his* god, the god whose name he had sold to a ferryman and could no longer recall, the god whose absence had hollowed him out like rot in a beam.

A priest. Down here. Frozen mid-hymn, feeding his divinity into the seasons one note at a time, while above ground his last surviving servant prayed into a silence and called it mystery.

Silas pressed his forehead to the glass. It was warm. Somewhere inside it, faintly, the priest's bass note thrummed against his skin, and for one vertiginous moment Silas felt the note *recognize* him — felt something in the harmony lean toward him the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep — and then the great song closed over it again, smooth as water over a dropped stone.

"You feel it," the Understeward said quietly. He had stopped a few paces on, not looking back. "They always do, the ones who come down carrying a piece of the old blood. The Court knows its own."

"What happened to him?" Silas's voice came out scraped raw. "The priest. What happened to all of them?"

"The same thing that happens to everyone the Court takes." The old man resumed walking. "They get used."

* * *

The ranks thinned as they went deeper, and the panes grew newer. Silas noticed it without wanting to: the older glass was clouded, ancient, its singers worn nearly to silhouettes — but here the panes were clear as morning ice, and the people inside them wore clothes he recognized. A fisherwoman from the coast towns, taken maybe ten years ago. A hedge-witch whose charms Silas had once bought at a border market. And then, with a lurch, panes so new the glass still bore the marks of the making — weeks old, days old. Faces he didn't know. Faces he did.

Two of Mother Ash's people. The twins who played the reed-pipes at the concert. Taken in the raid, fitted in already, their mouths open on the same note, their eyes wide and wet and *moving* — tracking him as he passed, the newest ones still able to move their eyes, still able to see, and Silas understood with a horror that arrived whole, like a blow, that this was what fitting meant. First your eyes. Then your fingers. Then your voice. Then you.

"Hurry," the Understeward said, without urgency. "The fitting's nearly done for the last batch. You'll want to see it before it finishes. Everyone wants to see it. Nobody knows why."

Silas wanted to kill him. He catalogued the desire, set it aside like a weapon he couldn't yet afford to draw, and hurried.

* * *

The fitting happened in a bowl-shaped hollow at the heart of the Court, where the floor dipped and the great harmony gathered like water gathering in a well. Scaffolds of pale wood — wood, down here, impossibly, brought from the surface by hands like the Understeward's — rose around a dozen empty panes standing upright in iron frames, each one open along a seam at the back, waiting.

And beside the third scaffold, held upright by two figures in gray robes whose faces were smooth as eggshells, stood Wren.

She was alive. That was the first thing, and it hit him harder than anything the descent had thrown at him: she was alive, and awake, and her eyes were furious. They had dressed her in white — choir-white, burial-white — and her dark hair was loose around her shoulders because someone had removed the ribbon, and seeing her hair unbound did something to Silas's chest that he would remember for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

She wasn't struggling. That frightened him worse than screaming would have. She stood very still, chin up, watching the gray-robes work with the expression of a girl memorizing faces for later. And she was humming. Barely audible under the great song, pitched to reach no one but herself: a little rising phrase, bent and crooked, hers. Keeping time with her own rebellion.

The seam of the pane began to close.

It closed the way ice forms on a pond — silently, smoothly, from the edges inward — and Wren's eyes went wide, and for the first time she fought, twisting against the gray-robes' grip, and her voice broke loose from the hum into words, actual words, and the great harmony stuttered around her like a candle in wind:

"*Somebody will come.* Somebody always — "

The seam sealed. The light inside the pane bloomed soft and gold. Her mouth kept moving for three seconds — four — shaping words the glass would not release — and then the stillness took her from the jaw down, and her eyes alone remained, bright and terrified and *looking directly at him*, because Silas had stepped out of the shadow of the gallery with no memory of deciding to, and she had seen him.

Her eyes widened. Her frozen lips, behind the glass, might almost have shaped his name.

"Wren," Silas said. It came out as less than a whisper. He crossed the distance between them without feeling his legs do it, and put both hands flat against the warm glass, and her eyes held his, and inside the pane her voice — her real voice, the one that had sung a dying mercenary back from the dark fifteen years ago, the one he owed his every breath since — rose and joined the eternal song, and the note she was given was beautiful, and it was a cage, and she sang it anyway because she had no choice, and her eyes never left his face.

The gray-robes turned their smooth faces toward him. The Understeward appeared at his elbow, lantern glowing its lightless light.

"She'll settle," the old man said gently. "They all settle. In a year she won't remember there was ever anything to fight."

Silas did not take his hands off the glass. Behind his ribs, in the hollow where his god used to live, something that was not quite a prayer and not quite a scream began to build, and far above — or perhaps all around, or perhaps within the harmony itself — the Regent of Glass began, chord by chord, to laugh.

"Well," it said, in twenty voices, in one voice, in a voice Silas almost remembered. "Now we can talk terms."
