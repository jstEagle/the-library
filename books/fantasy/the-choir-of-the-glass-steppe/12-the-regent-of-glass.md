## 12. The Regent of Glass

The cathedral did not want him to leave.

Silas understood that the way a drowning man understands water — not as thought but as pressure, as the slow patient insistence of something vast and older than his grief. The moment he stepped off the last stair of bone and onto the floor of the Glass Court, the doors behind him sealed like a wound closing over a splinter. No sound. No grinding of stone. The ossuary passage simply ceased to be there, its murals of devouring gods folded away into smooth white wall, and Silas felt the loss of it in his chest like a held breath he could no longer release.

*No way back but through,* Mother Ash had said, pressing her cracked amber charm into his palm at the mouth of the stair. *And no way through but what you're willing to become.*

He had sold too much already to be afraid in any ordinary way. His name was gone — he'd felt it peel off him on the ferry across the black water, the ferryman's long fingers plucking it from him like a splinter, leaving a smooth blank place where *Silas Whitlock* used to sit. His charms were gone, all eleven of them, each one a small death. And the memory — gods, the memory. He had given up his god's face, and now when he reached for the twenty years of silence that had defined him, he found only a shapeless absence, a hole in the world shaped like devotion.

So he walked into the Court of Glass with almost nothing, which meant, he supposed, that he walked in honest.

The light came first. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale gold radiance without source, without shadow, the light of a day that had been frozen at mid-morning for longer than kingdoms had existed. It fell through walls that were not walls but panes — enormous vertical sheets of glass rising from the floor like the petals of some impossible flower, hundreds of them, thousands, curving inward toward a vaulted ceiling so far above that the farthest panes were only glimmers, suggestions, stars caught in amber.

And in every pane, a singer.

They stood suspended in the glass itself, half-submerged as though the panes were deep water, their bodies sheathed to the waist in translucent crystal. Their mouths were open. Their eyes were closed. From every throat came sound — not one song but ten thousand, layered and interwoven into a single sustained chord that Silas felt in his teeth, in the marrow of his shins, in the scar tissue over his heart. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard, and every note of it was stolen.

He recognized the work even if he didn't recognize the workers. This was the chord that turned the seasons. He could hear winter in the low voices, hear them holding the cold like a long bowed note; hear spring trembling in the upper registers, fragile as new leaves; hear summer blazing somewhere in the middle harmonies, and autumn falling through it all like slow gold rain. The realm above lived inside this music. Every harvest, every thaw, every snowfall on the Steppe was these throats, these lungs, these souls singing forever because they had no other choice.

Some of the singers had been here so long that the glass had crept past their waists, past their shoulders, up their necks. One woman near the aisle was sheathed to her chin, her lips still moving, her eyes still shut, frost flowering delicately along the pane's edge where her breath struck it from inside.

Silas made himself look at her. Made himself count. Made himself do the arithmetic that Mother Ash had refused to finish for him: *the old gods were consumed to start this choir, and every soul since has kept it fed.* Twenty years ago his god had gone silent. Twenty years ago, somewhere in this frozen gold light, a throat had opened and swallowed the divine.

His hands were shaking. He let them.

"I know you."

The voice came from everywhere, the way the light did. It was neither male nor female, or perhaps it was both, or perhaps it was simply very old and had outlived the usefulness of such distinctions. It resonated in the glass, and the glass resonated back, and for a moment the whole cathedral shimmered like the surface of a struck bell.

"You are the hollow one," the voice said, gentle as snowfall. "The godless mercenary. The man who sells small luck to dockworkers because he cannot bear to stop serving." A pause, and then, softer, almost tender: "I have watched you come down the long stair. I have watched you since the ferryman took your name. Do you know how rare it is, little remnant? Souls arrive here dragged, weeping, bargaining, broken. You arrived walking. You arrived *choosing*. There is a quality in a chosen soul that we have not tasted in three hundred years."

Ahead of him, where the curved panes converged like the ribs of a great beast, the floor rose in a shallow dais of clear crystal. And upon the dais stood the Regent.

It looked, at first, like a figure carved from a single flawless sheet of glass — tall, robed, crowned with a circlet of frozen light. But as Silas drew closer he saw that the Regent was not *in* the glass. The Regent *was* the glass: a standing pane given motion, given hunger, given patience beyond the reach of anything born. Light moved inside it the way weather moves inside a mountain — slowly, massively, with the weight of centuries. Where a face should have been there was a smooth reflective surface, and in that surface Silas saw himself reflected, small and ragged and bleeding from a dozen wounds the descent had given him.

"Sit," said the Regent, and a bench of glass rose from the floor beside the dais, warm where it should have been cold.

Silas did not sit. "I've come for someone."

"Yes," the Regent said. "You have. You always were going to. That is what makes you valuable." It drifted closer, and the chord of ten thousand singers bent subtly around its movement, harmonies leaning toward it like grass toward wind. "But before we speak of her — before we speak of bargains and prices and all the small arithmetic of souls — I want to show you something. Will you permit me? It costs you nothing. It is only a gift, and gifts, unlike contracts, bind no one."

Everything in Silas that had survived twenty years of mercenary work — the part that read contracts twice, that counted exits, that trusted generosity exactly as far as the blade hidden inside it — screamed at him to refuse.

He was so tired. He had crossed black water and lost his name and climbed down through the bones of the world on legs that barely answered him, and the chord was beautiful, and the bench was warm, and the Regent's voice sounded, more than anything else in the world, like rest.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

The Court reached into him.

There was no pain. That was the first obscenity of it. He braced for pain and instead felt fingers — not fingers, *attention*, vast and delicate — turning gently through his memories the way a jeweler turns a stone to find its facets. And then the cathedral around him changed.

The gold light deepened to the green-gold of high summer. The panes were gone. He stood on a hillside above Veyra City, on a morning twenty years and more gone, and the air smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke and river water, and his heart — his mortal, mercenary, unremarkable heart — stopped, because he knew this hillside. He had nearly died on it.

He turned, and there he was: young Silas Whitlock, twenty-six years old, face-down in the trampled grass with an arrow through his chest and his blood soaking into the earth in a dark spreading bloom. Around him the wreckage of a caravan raid — dead horses, dead men, the small smoking ruin of a life he'd been living on contract. And kneeling beside his dying body, a girl of perhaps nine years old, mud-splattered, her brown hair escaping its braid, her small hands pressed flat against his wound.

Wren. Before he knew her name. Before the debt. Before the oath.

She was singing.

Silas remembered this — of course he remembered this, he had carried it through two decades like a coal carried in cupped hands — but memory was a sketch, and this was the thing itself. Her voice rose thin and wavering over the battlefield stink, a child's song with no words he knew, and light moved under her skin like dawn moving under ice. She was borrowing power she didn't have, pulling it from somewhere, from anywhere, spending herself to stitch a stranger's chest closed, and her nose was bleeding, and she did not stop.

"She gave you her first true gift," the Regent murmured, and its voice now came from beside him, though he could not see it; the vision would not spare it room. "A child's whole unspent self, poured into a dying stranger. Do you understand what that made you, little remnant? Not merely a man whose life was saved. A man *woven*. Her song has been in your blood for fifteen years. It is why your fading charms still worked at all, long after they should have crumbled. It is why you could not stop serving people — her kindness is knotted into you, and you have been mistaking it for virtue ever since."

"It *is* virtue," Silas said, and his voice came out rough and real in the summer air.

"Is it?" The hillside rippled. The dying scene dissolved like sugar in rain, and re-formed —

— into light.

He stood somewhere that was not anywhere: a place of white-gold radiance without floor or sky, and he was *tall*, and he was *bright*, and he looked down at hands that burned softly at the edges like paper held near a flame without burning. Power moved through him the way blood moves through a body, effortless, total, ancient. He could feel the seasons. Not hear them — *feel* them, all four at once, wheeling around him like horses around a maypole, and with a thought he could slow the autumn rains, could hold the spring buds an extra week, could lay a mercy of early snow on a drought-struck field.

He was a god. Small, yes — a hedge-god, a hearth-god, the quiet patron of luck-knots and safe roads and hard winters survived — but a god, whole and awake and *present*, and the silence that had hollowed him for twenty years was filled to its edges with his own voice.

"This is what you were," the Regent said, and now he could see it, standing in the radiance beside him, its glass body drinking the light. "This is what was taken. We did not take it — understand that. Your crown's founders began the feeding, and the hungry years consumed the small gods first, and yours went down screaming into the first choir like all the others. But its substance did not vanish, Silas Whitlock. Substance never vanishes. It is *here*." The Regent raised one transparent hand, and within its palm, deep in the glass like a fly in amber, something small and bright flickered — a spark, a seed, a coal. "Every scrap of your divinity, preserved. Waiting. We are not wasteful. We are a court, not a grave."

Silas stared at the spark, and the terrible thing — the thing he would be ashamed of for the rest of whatever life he had left — was that his first feeling was not suspicion.

It was homesickness.

Twenty years of reaching for a god who did not answer. Twenty years of rationing charms like a miser, of feeling the divine dwindle in him grain by grain, of lying to clients and lying to himself, of waking at night with his hand pressed flat against his own chest as if he could find the hole and plug it. And here it was, the answer, held out in a palm of glass: *you were not abandoned. You were robbed. And the robber is offering to give it back.*

"What do you want," he said quietly, "in return."

"Ah." The Regent's reflective face tilted, and in it Silas saw his god-self reflected, radiant, and behind the god-self, fainter, the ragged mortal man, and the Regent had arranged the reflection so that the god stood in front, hiding the man. It had done that deliberately. He was almost sure it had done that deliberately. "Such a mercenary question. Very well. We are honest here; honesty is our oldest law, older than the crowns that feed us. The choir is failing."

The radiance flickered. For just a moment Silas saw the truth beneath the vision — the panes again, the frozen singers, and among them cracks. Hairline fractures running up through the glass like veins of darkness. Somewhere in the vast chord a voice had gone slightly, terribly flat.

"The old stock thins," the Regent continued, untroubled, as though reporting a poor harvest. "Souls wear out, even eternal ones. Especially eternal ones. The seasons above grow erratic — you have noticed, surely, the late springs, the hungry winters, the storms that come out of season. The realm blames its kings. The kings blame witches. And meanwhile the machinery of heaven runs down for lack of voices." Its glass hand extended, and upon it, the spark of Silas's divinity burned a little brighter, a little warmer, unbearably inviting. "We require new singers. Strong ones. Gifted ones. The crown's inquisitor gathers them for us — she believes she serves order, and so she does, in her way. But gathered souls resist. Dragged souls sing badly. A soul that comes *willingly*, that brings others willingly — ah. Such a voice could anchor the entire lower register for a century."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you may take the stairs back up, if you can find them, and spend your remaining years as you have spent the last twenty: hollow, rationing, listening to a silence that was never silence but theft." The Regent's voice did not harden; that was the worst of it. It remained gentle, reasonable, the voice of a steward discussing inventory. "Or you may stay. Sing. Be restored. And go up into the world wearing your godhood again, gathering the gifted gently, kindly, telling them — truthfully! — that below the mountain is a place where their gifts will never again be hunted or outlawed. A sanctuary. Which is even true. Everything I will tell you is true, little remnant. That is my cruelty, if you wish to call it that. I have no need of lies. The truth is seductive enough."

The vision held him in the white-gold light a moment longer. Let him feel the power one more breath, one more heartbeat — the seasons wheeling at his nod, the silence in his chest filled, the twenty-year ache ended. It let him feel what it would be like to walk into Wren's cell — no. Into wherever they were keeping her. To walk in *whole*, with power enough to break any lock, to carry her out over his shoulder like he had promised her father he would, like he had promised *her*, that night on the dock when she was sixteen and crying and he had said *nothing gets you while I'm breathing.*

That promise had cost nothing to make and everything to keep. The Regent was offering him a version where keeping it was easy.

"That's the trap," Silas said. His voice shook. "Isn't it. The easy version."

"It is not a trap," the Regent said. "It is an offer. Traps close. Offers wait." The radiance dimmed by degrees, reluctantly, like a lamp being shuttered, and the cathedral returned around them — the panes, the singers, the pale frozen gold. The spark of divinity still burned in the Regent's palm. "Take what time you need. Walk among the choir. Look at the faces. Consider what a hundred years of restored divinity purchases against what it costs. I am patient, Silas Whitlock. Patience is the one luxury eternity affords."

* * *

He walked.

He did not know why. Perhaps because refusing outright would end the conversation, and the conversation was the only door left open. Perhaps because some part of him wanted to see the faces — wanted to make himself look, the way he had made himself look at the woman sheathed to her chin, because looking was a kind of payment and he owed these people everything.

The panes rose around him like a forest of ice. Up close, he could see details the awe of arrival had blurred: the different clothes the singers wore, preserved in the glass — a fisherman's oilskins, a scholar's robe, a midwife's apron stiff with old stains, a child's coat with brass buttons. Generations of the gifted, harvested across three centuries of quiet arrangement between crown and court. Their mouths moved. Their songs poured out of them and into the architecture of the world, and none of them could stop.

He passed a pane and stopped so suddenly that his bad knee buckled.

The man inside was old, white-bearded, dressed in the vestments of a rural parish — the vestments of the Quiet God, the hearth-luck faith, the faith of luck-knots and safe roads. Silas knew those vestments. He had buried his mentor in them. And the old man's face, serene behind the glass, eyes closed, singing his single sustained note into the machinery of the seasons—

Silas knew that face.

Not from life. From the hole in his memory, the shapeless absence where his god's face had been, the void he had sold to the ferryman. He couldn't *remember* the face. But his body remembered it. His knees remembered it. Grief rose in him with no object and no name, twenty years of orphaned devotion finding at last its missing portrait, and he pressed his forehead against the cold glass and shook.

*Your god is here,* whispered a voice that might have been the Regent and might have been the choir and might have been his own heart. *Singing. Fed into the machine. And you could join him — or you could restore him. Did we say? The spark in our keeping is his remnant, and remnants can be regathered. Bring us voices, little remnant, and we will not merely make you a god. We will make you* him. *Whole. Continuous. As if the twenty silent years had never happened.*

"As if Wren's song had never saved me," Silas said against the glass. "As if none of it happened."

"As if the wound had never been," the Regent agreed, tender as a lie.

And that — that was the crack in the offer, the flaw in the flawless glass. Because the wound *had* been. The arrow had gone through him and a child had bled from the nose to pull it back out, and the debt and the oath and the fifteen gray years of service were not damage laid over his life. They *were* his life. Erase the wound and you erased the stitching. Erase the silence and you erased everything the silence had taught him — how to serve without reward, how to love without power, how to be, in the end, only a man, which was the one thing he had never once regretted being.

Somewhere above, in the world of weather and hunger and hunted magicians, a girl was being fitted into one of these panes. He could feel it — or he told himself he could, which amounted to the same thing. The thought of her voice flattened into a single eternal note, her variations silenced, her defiance frozen at her throat, made the Regent's whole golden offer turn to ash and ash and ash.

"No," Silas said.

He said it quietly. The choir sang on around him, ten thousand stolen voices, and his one free voice was nothing against them, a pebble dropped in a bell-tower. But the Regent heard. The light in the cathedral seemed, very slightly, to cool.

"No?" The glass figure drifted nearer, and for the first time Silas saw movement in its depths that was not weather — a kind of attention, a kind of appetite, focusing on him like frost forming on a window. "You would refuse restoration? Refuse your own divinity, your own *face*, held here in our keeping for twenty years like a coat at a door?" A pause. The reflective surface of its head turned, and in it Silas saw not himself now but the rows of panes stretching away into the gold distance, and among them, far off, one pane that gleamed differently — newer, brighter, its occupant small and slight and brown-haired. "Then perhaps we misjudged what you value. Shall we discuss the girl instead? She has been with us only six days. The glass rises slowly at first. There is time. There is always time, here. Time is the one thing we have in abundance, and the one thing you do not."

Silas followed the Regent's gaze down the endless aisle, and his heart stopped, and started, and stopped again.

Far away, half-lost in the golden haze, a new pane stood apart from the others. And even at this distance — even across a hundred yards of frozen light — he heard it.

A voice, threading through the great chord, refusing it. Bending the notes. Singing *around* the melody instead of with it, sly and stubborn and alive, a variation the Court could not quite digest, and Silas knew that voice the way he knew his own heartbeat, and it was smiling.

It was smiling, and it was not alone in its rebellion.

The Regent's glass hand closed slowly over the spark of Silas's divinity, extinguishing it like a snuffed candle.

"Come," it said. "Let me show you what your refusal has purchased."
