## 13. Wren, Singing

The first thing they took was her name.

Not with violence — that was the terrible part. No knife, no binding, no iron. They simply asked her to sing, and when she opened her mouth, the Court reached in and lifted the word *Wren* out of her like a stone from a streambed, leaving the water to close over the place where it had been. She felt it go. She felt the shape of it remain, a hollow pressed into her, the way a hand leaves its print in wax.

She did not cry. Later — much later, in the dark between verses — she would understand that this was the moment she decided not to. Because if she wept, she would be weeping as something nameless, and she refused. Whatever else they took, they would not take her refusal.

So she sang.

* * *

The Glass Court was a cathedral built of sound made solid. Wren understood this within her first hour there, though understanding came slowly, the way cold comes into a house — through the gaps first, then everywhere at once. The walls were panes of glass grown thick as tombstones, and inside each pane hung a singer: not their bodies, but the singing part of them, the voice itself, suspended mid-note like an insect in amber. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each pane held one voice, and each voice held one season's worth of work, and together they turned the wheel of the world above — snow for the Steppe's hard winter, thaw for its brief green spring, the long slow burn of summer, the rust-and-ash fall.

She could see them from where she stood on the choir floor: a forest of frozen faces, mouths open, eyes open, all of them singing without ceasing. Some of the panes were old. The voices inside them had gone thin as thread, worn down by centuries, and their faces had gone vague, features smoothed away like river stones. Others were newer. Those still had eyes. Those still, sometimes, looked back.

The Regent had told her it was an honor.

The Regent was a tall figure of smoked glass and borrowed light who moved like poured honey and spoke like a ledger being read aloud, and it had fitted her — *fitted*, the way one fits a key to a lock — into a standing-stone at the edge of the choir floor, a slot of dark crystal exactly her height, exactly her shape. It had been waiting for her. That was the worst of it. There was a groove in the floor worn smooth by generations of singers led to that same spot, and the Regent had said, in its courteous, bottomless voice:

*You have a gift. Gifts are meant to be given.*

And Wren had thought, with a clarity that surprised her: *That is what my mother said about the tithe, too, right before the tax collectors took our cow.*

So when they asked her to sing, she sang. But she did not sing what they wanted.

* * *

Here is what the Court wanted: it wanted her whole. Every note, every ornament, every private music she had ever carried — the lullaby her grandmother hummed while gutting fish, the work-song the dockhands shouted in three-four time, the little tuneless thing she made up walking home in the rain because rain deserved a soundtrack. The Court drank songs the way the Steppe drank meltwater in spring, and it had been drinking for a very long time, and it was never full.

Here is what Wren gave it instead: variations.

It began as an accident. The Court pulled the first song out of her — a harvest hymn, old and simple, the kind every child in the river villages learned before they learned their letters — and she felt it going, felt the hymn unspooling off her tongue and into the glass like thread off a spindle, and panic rose in her throat like floodwater. And in the panic, her voice slipped. A half-step flat. A grace note where none belonged. Her breath caught on the third line and she swallowed wrong and the melody bent.

The Court stumbled.

It was small — a shiver through the panes, a flicker in the amber light, the sensation of a great mouth closing on something that shifted at the last instant. The Regent's head tilted, a degree, no more. And Wren, hanging in her slot of dark crystal with her heart hammering against her ribs like a bird against a window, understood two things at once.

First: the Court could only take what was given true. A song offered straight and whole went into the glass and became power, became seasons, became the turning of the world. But a song *bent* — varied, ornamented, made hers and no one else's — stuck somewhere on the way down. It went in crooked and it would not dissolve.

Second: she was not the first to know this.

* * *

She found them in the silences between verses. The Court required rest — even engines must cool — and in those rests, when the great chord that held the cathedral slackened, the older captives spoke to her. Not aloud. Speaking aloud was taken; speech was the first thing the glass drank, after names. They spoke in the pauses, in the half-breath before the next verse, in a language made of rhythm and pressure, a language you could only hear if your own voice was still partly your own.

An old woman named Sefa, harvested sixty years ago from the orchard country, her voice already worn to gossamer. A boy of perhaps twelve called Tam, taken so recently his pane still fogged when he breathed. A man with no name left at all, whom the others called Fourth-Bass because that was all he had kept, and who had been in the Court long enough to remember when the Regent had a different face.

*They cannot digest a living song,* Sefa told her, in the pause after the ninth hour of the ninth day. *Only finished ones. Only surrendered ones. A song still growing, still changing hands, still being argued with — that catches in the throat of the world.*

*Then why does everyone here sing straight?* Wren asked.

The silence that followed had weight. When Sefa answered, her rhythm was slow, and tired, and very old.

*Because it is easier. Because the Court is patient and we are not. Because when you bend the song, you feel everything — the taking, the reaching, the hunger. Singing straight is like sleeping. You barely notice you are being spent.* A pause, and then, quieter: *Some of us chose to forget how to vary. It hurt less.*

*I'm not going to choose that,* Wren said.

*No,* Sefa said, and there was something in her worn-thin voice that might have been hope, or might have been grief wearing hope's coat. *I didn't think you would. That's why I waited sixty years to tell someone.*

* * *

So she began.

She could not stop singing — the Court would simply take her, the way it had taken the others who resisted outright; there were panes near the entrance whose occupants had fought, and their voices were the thinnest of all, scraped down to nothing in months. Resistance by refusal was suicide performed slowly. But resistance by *corruption* — that was different. That was sabotage dressed as obedience.

Every verse they demanded, she gave them a version of it. She flattened the thirds. She delayed the cadence until the harmony ached for resolution. She took the Court's beloved hymns and turned them inside out, added a passing tone here, a suspension there, changed the words' stresses so that old blessings said new things. *Blessed be the turning year* became, under her tongue, *blessed be the year that turns on them.* The Court drank and drank and got nothing it could use. Its great machinery gulped at her music and coughed.

And the panes around her began, very slowly, to loosen.

This was the discovery that changed everything. A captive voice, sung straight for decades, set like mortar in its glass. But when Wren sang a variation *at* a pane — aimed it, the way you'd aim a lamp — the frozen voice inside would stir. Hear its own song made strange, made free, made *arguable* again. And hearing it, it would remember. Tam's pane, which had begun to fog over, cleared. Fourth-Bass, who had not varied a note in forty years, produced — during the rest after the twelfth-hour verse — a single bent interval, quiet as a crack in ice, and the amber light around him flickered like a candle in wind.

One note. Then another. Then, night after night — if the Court had nights; time down here was a rumor — a few more.

*You are unbinding us,* Sefa said, and her voice trembled in its rhythm. *Note by note. Do you understand what you are doing?*

*Yes,* Wren said. *I'm doing it badly and slowly and I don't know how to do it faster.*

*Sixty years,* Sefa said, *I have sung straight because I forgot there was any other way. You have reminded me in nine days. Badly and slowly is a storm, child. Badly and slowly is a revolution.*

* * *

But the Court was not asleep either.

She felt it watching. Not the Regent — the Regent was a steward, a clerk in smoked glass, and clerks can be fooled. What watched her was older and larger and had no face at all: the Court itself, the hunger underneath the architecture, the thing that had eaten gods and found them insufficient. It noticed the way a body notices a splinter. A dull attention. A pressure behind the light.

On the fifteenth day, the Regent came to her slot and stood before her for a long time, and the light through its body went from honey to blood to honey again.

*Your tribute has become irregular,* it said.

*Has it?* Wren kept her voice sweet and steady, the voice of a girl honored to serve. *I've sung every verse. Every hour. Ask the panes.*

*The panes report... irregularity.*

*The panes are old,* Wren said. *Old things imagine irregularity. Perhaps they need replacing.* And she sang the next verse perfectly, purely, straight as a plumb line, giving the Court thirty seconds of flawless surrender, and the Regent's light calmed, and it drifted away satisfied, and Wren hung in her crystal and shook for the length of an entire rest.

Because that was the other lesson, the harder one: defiance was not enough. Defiance was a fire, and fires announce themselves. What she needed was defiance with a servant's face. She needed to be, in the Court's accounting, a compliant asset — while underneath, note by note, verse by verse, she picked the lock of the world.

Tam asked her once, in a rest, whether she was afraid.

*All the time,* she said. *Fear is just another note. You can bend it too.*

*What happens when they figure it out?*

She looked across the choir floor at the forest of panes, thousands of them, thousands of faces, and at the newest section where fresh glass stood empty and waiting, slots cut and polished, room made for harvests yet to come. She thought of the ribbon she'd dropped on the stairs of the Hollow Choir's concert hall — Silas's ribbon, the one he'd tied on her wrist fifteen years ago in a burning field, the one she'd worn every day since because some promises need a physical anchor. She'd lost it running. She hoped whoever found it knew what it meant. She hoped, with a ferocity that scared her, that he was alive to have found it.

*Then we'd better be finished before they do,* she said.

* * *

Progress was measured in cracks.

Not literal ones — not yet. Measured in loosened voices, in captives who could now vary a phrase without pain, in the small underground economy of bent notes that spread outward from her slot like frost spreading across a window. Sefa taught her the old orchard-country ornaments, and Wren fed them back transformed. Fourth-Bass remembered, gradually, a name — not his own, but a god's, a name he'd heard sung in his childhood before the gods went silent, and the name itself was a variation, a seed of the unabsorbed past, and when he hummed it the nearest six panes shivered in sympathy.

Winter above them faltered for a moment — she felt it, a hiccup in the great chord, a stutter in the machine — and the Court tightened everywhere, hungry and alarmed, and the Regent convened its glass council, and Wren sang her most obedient verse in three days and was passed over.

But she had learned the shape of the machine now. She knew where it was soft. She knew that the Court's strength — its eternity, its patience, its perfect repetition — was also its weakness, because a thing that has done the same thing for ten thousand years has forgotten that other things are possible. It could not imagine rebellion expressed as a grace note. It had no category for a hymn that blessed the wrong year. Its defenses were all pointed at doors, and Wren was coming through the windows.

And she was not alone anymore. Eleven voices could vary now, where there had been one. Nineteen. On the twenty-second day, an entire row of spring-panes hummed a bent chord together, quietly, during a rest, and the thaw above the Steppe arrived four hours late, and somewhere far overhead Wren imagined farmers looking at the sky and wondering.

Let them wonder, she thought. Let them look up. Let them start asking questions the crown can't answer.

* * *

On the twenty-fifth day, the Court's attention sharpened into something with edges.

She was mid-variation — folding a lament for the drowned into a harvest hymn, making the old words say *we remember, we remember, we are not finished* — when the great chord beneath her changed key without warning. Hard. Deliberate. A key that closed around her variation like a fist. The Regent appeared at the end of the choir floor, moving fast for something made of poured glass, and behind it came a ripple of alarm through the panes, hundreds of frozen faces turning — actually turning, which they never did — toward the cathedral's high entrance.

Something was coming down the stair. Something the Court had summoned, or bargained for, or feared.

Wren hung in her crystal and bent the next note low, so low it was almost speech, and Sefa's voice reached her through the rest, taut as wire:

*Someone is coming from above. The Court has sent for a human. A crown-human.*

*A soldier?*

*No,* Sefa said. *Worse. An official.*

The Regent stopped before Wren's slot. Its light had gone the color of deep water, and when it spoke, its courtesy had a blade in it, fine and bright.

*Singer,* it said. *There is a matter of contract to attend. You will demonstrate compliance. Sing me the year-turning hymn — precisely as written.*

And Wren smiled at it — the smile of a girl honored to serve, the smile that had kept her alive for twenty-five days — and drew breath, and behind her smile she counted her rebels: eleven, nineteen, thirty-one voices ready to bend at her signal, thirty-one cracks waiting in the glass.

*Of course, Reverence,* she said. *Anything for the realm.*

And she began to sing — straight, pure, obedient, every note exactly where the Court wanted it — while above them both, footsteps rang on the ossuary stair, descending.
