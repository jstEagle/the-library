## 14. Vael Descends

The mountain had a throat, and Inquisitor Vael descended it the way she did everything: precisely, without waste, and with her hands folded as though the world were a document awaiting her signature.

The ferryman's barge carried her down the last stretch of the underground river, past walls of stone that wept mineral light in slow green tears. She stood at the prow because sitting felt like surrender, and because the cold coming off the water was honest in a way the court above had never been. It did not flatter. It simply took your warmth and gave you nothing back, and Vael had always respected an honest transaction.

Her escort of six crown soldiers huddled in the stern, wrapped in oilcloth, their breath steaming. They had volunteered — or been volunteered; the distinction had blurred years ago — and now they stared at the dark with the particular stillness of men who had begun to suspect that the edicts they enforced were written by someone else entirely.

Vael did not share their unease. Or rather, she had decided not to, which amounted to the same thing in practice.

She carried with her a ledger bound in white calfskin, its pages listing two hundred and eleven names. Each name represented a soul assessed, catalogued, and delivered. Two hundred and eleven contributions to the realm's continuance. She knew some officials above would have preferred the word *harvest*, and she permitted them their coarseness in private, but in her own mind there was only ever the word *payment*. A kingdom does not run on prayers. It runs on accounts kept current.

And the account, she had learned three weeks ago from a courier who arrived grey-faced and trembling, was falling into arrears.

* * *

The Glass Court received her the way it received everything: silently, magnificently, and without any indication of whether it noticed her at all.

She had been down twice before — once as a junior assessor, twenty years ago, when the descent had been described to her as a sacred trust, and once last spring, when the Regent had first mentioned, in its voice like wind through a wineglass, that the choir was thinning.

Thinning. As if souls were thread.

She walked the long gallery now, her boots ringing on glass flagstones that showed her reflection from below as well as above, so that she seemed to walk upon herself. The frozen singers stood in their ranks on either side, hundreds of them, encased in panes of translucent crystal from throat to knee, their mouths open mid-note. Their eyes were open too. That was the detail the court above never put in its histories. The eyes stayed open.

Vael had made herself look, that first time, at every face. She had told herself it was duty — an assessor must know what assessment means — and there was truth in that. But there was also penance, though she would have cut out her tongue before naming it so. She looked at them so that she could not pretend she did not know. It was the only honesty available to her, and she rationed it carefully, the way a starving woman rations bread.

A young woman near the gallery's end was new. Vael recognized the face from her own ledgers — Wren, no surname given, assessed under the singer's exemption clause, which Vael herself had drafted to spare the gifted a summary beheading and had since come to understand was merely a slower knife. The girl's lips were parted around a note Vael could almost hear, a note that seemed to bend toward something the other singers' notes did not. Defiance, perhaps. Or variation. The Court absorbed conformity easily. Variation it had to chew.

Vael paused before the girl for exactly four seconds — she counted, because counting was how she kept the horror at arm's length — and then walked on.

The Regent awaited her in the Antechamber of Accounts, a dome of black glass where the ceiling displayed the seasons as they truly were beneath the Steppe: not weather, but machinery. Rivers of pale fire turned a wheel of frost. A sun the size of a coin burned in a socket of bone. Vael had once believed this a holy sight. Now she saw it for what the founders' documents said it was: a furnace, and a feed-line, and a debt older than the crown itself.

"You have brought the contract," the Regent said. It did not turn. Its body was a robe of hanging glass shards over a suggestion of a figure, and its face — when it chose to show one — was a smooth pane that reflected whoever looked at it. Vael had spent years avoiding her own face in it. Tonight she made herself meet it. Her reflection looked tired. Her reflection looked like a woman who had stopped sleeping sometime around the third hundred name.

"I have brought terms," Vael said. "The crown requires clarification before renewal."

"The crown," said the Regent, "has required nothing for nine hundred years. The crown has only ever *paid*."

"Then let us discuss the payment."

* * *

It was the archivist's key that had started all this, though Vael had not known it at the time.

Six months ago, cataloguing the effects of a dead lord chamberlain, she had found a sealed folio in his strongroom: *The Foundational Accord*, written in the hand of Aurelian the First, first king of Veyra, in ink that still smelled faintly of iron. She had read it alone, by candle, over the course of one night that had lasted, in her memory, approximately forty years.

She had expected heresy. What she found was bookkeeping.

The Accord was simple. In the realm's founding age, the seasons had not turned on their own. They had been *sung* — sung by the old gods, whose names the histories recorded as vanished, ascended, or transcendent. None of these were true. They had been consumed. All of them. Aurelian's armies had not conquered the land; they had conquered the *singers*, and the gods who sang through them, and had fed the whole chorus into the furnace beneath the Steppe to power a machine that would make the seasons obedient — regular, taxable, predictable. Harvest always after sowing. Winter always brief enough to survive. A realm where the crown could promise tomorrow.

And when the gods ran out — and they were running out even then, the Accord admitted, in a marginal note in a different, later hand — the founders had substituted. Souls. Gifted souls, whose magic was, the Accord explained with terrible blandness, merely the residue of divinity anyway. The Purge Edict was not persecution. It was procurement. Every herald's proclamation, every soul assessment, every cage on every cart — all of it was the crown honoring a nine-hundred-year-old invoice.

Vael had sat until dawn with the folio in her lap, and she had done the thing she was best at: she had read it again, looking for the flaw, the forgery, the escape clause. There wasn't one. There was only a line near the end, in Aurelian's own script, that she had copied out and carried against her heart ever since:

*Let it be recorded that we did not build a kingdom. We bought one, and the price is perpetual, and we have arranged that our descendants shall pay it without knowing they pay.*

*We have arranged,* he wrote, *that they shall call it justice.*

She had called it justice for nineteen years.

Now she descended with the Accord's copy in her case and a renewal contract in her hand, and she understood, finally, what she actually was. Not the realm's shield. Not the gifted's merciful alternative to the pyre. She was a collections agent for a debt contracted by dead men, and every soul she had ever taken had gone to service interest on a loan she had never signed.

She was, she thought as she crossed the Antechamber, trapped in the sins of the founders just as thoroughly as the singers in their panes were trapped in hers.

The difference — and she held to this difference the way a drowning man holds to a plank — was that she had chosen to learn the truth. That had to count. Somewhere in the arithmetic of whatever judged such things, it had to count.

* * *

He was already there.

She saw him the moment she entered the negotiating vault — a side chamber off the Antechamber where the Court conducted its transactions, all mirrors and low amber light. Silas Whitlock stood at the far end, and the sight of him went through her like a splinter finding a vein.

She had hunted him for two months. His file was the thickest in her cabinet: mercenary charm-smith, godless, dangerous in the specific way of men who had nothing left to lose except other people. He had escaped her cordon in the smuggler's quarter with a wound she had personally ordered dressed rather than left to fester — mercy, she had told herself, though even then the word had tasted like ash. And now here he stood in the heart of the machine she served, alive, unbound, and *negotiating* — the Regent's mirror-face angled toward him with what she could only describe as appetite.

"Inquisitor." He did not bow. His voice was roughened, and there was a hollowed quality about him, as if parts of him had been sold off in installments. She knew the ferryman's tolls when she saw them. She wondered what he had paid, and what remained.

"Whitlock." She set her case on the obsidian table between them and did not sit. "You're a long way from your cell."

"Your cells leak." A ghost of a smile. "So do your convictions, I'm told. Though I notice you kept mine on file. Dressed my wounds. Filed my charms as evidence instead of destroying them." He tilted his head. "Sentimental, Inquisitor?"

"Thorough," she said. "Evidence is evidence."

"And yet you're the only inquisitor in the history of the purge who keeps a separate ledger of the ones she spares." He stepped closer, and up close she could see the cost of his descent written on him — the fine tremor in his hands, the way his eyes moved to the mirrors and flinched. "Two hundred and eleven names in the white book. My people count them too, you know. The Choir keeps its own records. Mother Ash has yours memorized. Do you want to know what they call you?"

"No."

"'The Courteous Knife.'" He said it without venom, which was worse. "Because you apologize while you cut. Because you offer the assessment gently. Because you genuinely believe—" he gestured at the vault, the mirrors, the faint eternal hum of the choir beyond the walls, "—that this is rescue."

"It is rescue." The words came out level, practiced, and for the first time in nineteen years they came out *hollow*, and she heard the hollowness herself, and hated him for hearing it too. "The alternative is the pyre. I have watched the pyres. I drafted the exemptions that emptied half of them."

"Yes. You saved them for this." He touched the table, and his reflection rippled. "I've seen the choir, Inquisitor. Have you? Truly seen it? Not the gallery tour. The ranks. The open eyes."

"I have seen it." Four seconds per face. Hundreds of faces. "Every time."

"Then how do you stand here and call it rescue?"

And there it was — the question she had been circling for six months, asked plainly by a branded heretic in a room built for selling souls, and she found that she could not deploy a single one of her answers. They were all still in her mouth, fully formed, nineteen years polished. *Order. Mercy. Continuance.* But the Accord lay in her case like a corpse, and every answer had its throat cut.

Instead she heard herself say: "How do you stand anywhere, Whitlock, knowing your god was eaten to build this? Knowing your silence wasn't abandonment — it was theft? You've spent twenty years praying into a grave."

She had meant it as a blade. She watched it land as something else — watched him go still, watched something move behind his face that was not pain exactly, or not only pain. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own secret said aloud by a stranger and discovering it weighed less in the open air.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's exactly how I stand."

* * *

They negotiated afterward, because that was why they had both come, and the negotiation proceeded with the strange civility of two duelists who have realized, mid-circle, that they hold identical swords.

The Regent wanted renewal: two hundred souls a year, rising to three hundred as the choir thinned. Vael countered with the crown's position — reduced quotas, the release of singers under fourteen, written guarantees. The Regent reflected her own face back at her, amused, and quoted clauses from the Foundational Accord that Vael had not yet shown anyone, and Vael understood that the Court had known about her discovery for some time, and had let her descend anyway, and she filed that away with cold precision because it meant the Regent considered her either harmless or useful, and both possibilities were instructive.

Whitlock watched all of it from the edge of the light, saying little, and she caught him watching *her* more than the Regent. Studying her the way she studied documents. Looking, she supposed, for the flaw.

At length the Regent withdrew to consider the counter-terms, leaving them alone in the amber vault with their reflections, and the silence between them changed texture. It stopped being the silence of enemies and became the silence of two people in the same locked room, each holding half a key.

"The founders," Vael said, because someone had to say it first, and she found she would rather it be her than let him think her ignorant. "You know."

"I know the shape of it. Ash gave me the outline. You have the document."

"Aurelian's own hand." She opened her case, hesitated — nineteen years of instinct screaming that knowledge was leverage and leverage was life — and then laid the copied folio on the obsidian between them. "Read it. I have it memorized. It will do me more good in the world than in my keeping."

He looked at the folio, then at her, and his eyes narrowed slightly, the wariness of a man being handed a gift that might be a trap. "Why?"

"Because I am tired, Whitlock." She said it flatly, without performance, and the flatness was the most honest thing she had offered anyone in two decades. "Not weary — I know weariness, it's manageable. Tired. Tired the way a structure is tired, when the load has shifted and the walls are carrying weight they were never built for. I have spent nineteen years believing I was the mercy between the gifted and the flame. I have learned this year that I am the flame's delivery system. And I find that I cannot unknow it, and I cannot unsee their faces, and I have run out of ways to arrange the arithmetic so that it balances."

"And so?"

"And so I am going to negotiate the smallest possible renewal, deliver it, ascend, and then decide what kind of woman I intend to be for the remainder of my life." She met his eyes. "Which is a sentence I never expected to say inside this mountain."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside the vault, faintly, the choir sang on — and beneath the great conforming chord, so faint she might have imagined it, one voice bent a note the wrong way. Bent it toward freedom. Vael found her gaze drifting toward the sound before she could stop it.

"That girl," Whitlock said softly, following her attention. "Wren. She's in there."

"I know. I assessed her myself."

"Then you wrote her name in the white book."

"I write every name in the white book." Her voice did not waver, and she despised how much effort that took. "That is the entire horror of me, Whitlock. I do not delegate my cruelty. I perform it personally, courteously, and in triplicate."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through him and died. "God help me," he murmured, "I believe you're telling the truth."

"Your god is dead," Vael said. "Eaten. Like mine — the god I was raised on was Duty, and it turns out Duty was a fiction written by slaveholders. We are both orphans of other people's crimes, you and I. The only question is what we do with the inheritance."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "You could refuse. Sign nothing. Walk out and burn the Accord in the square."

"And winter fails within the month. Crops fail. The rivers freeze to their beds. Thousands die above ground screaming names I put in ledgers." She shook her head slowly. "You think I haven't run that sum? I run it hourly. There is no clean exit, Whitlock. That is what the founders built — not a machine, a *trap*, with themselves outside it and everyone else inside. Even rebellion feeds it. Even my guilt feeds it — the Regent finds my hesitation charming. I saw it in the mirror-face. My conscience is, to this thing, a flavor."

"Then what?"

"Then I buy time," she said, "and I look for the flaw. There is always a flaw. Aurelian was thorough but he was a king, and kings always believe their ink is stronger than the world." She gathered her case, and paused, and for a moment the courteous mask slipped entirely, and what looked out from behind it was exhausted and middle-aged and afraid. "Whitlock. If you find the flaw before I do — use it. Don't tell me. Don't trust me. I wouldn't trust me. But use it."

"And if I need help getting out of this mountain?"

"Then you'll have to decide I'm worth the risk." She allowed herself the thinnest ghost of a smile. "Consider it repayment for the wound dressing."

* * *

The Regent returned, and the terms were struck: one hundred souls for the coming year, the crown's seal affixed in wax that gleamed like frozen blood, Vael's signature steady as a plumb line. She performed it flawlessly. She had always performed flawlessly. It was the one talent the crown had ever actually paid her for.

As the guards escorted her back toward the river, she passed the gallery of frozen singers one final time. The girl — Wren — was singing still, and this time Vael did not count four seconds. She stopped, and she listened, and she heard what the girl was doing: threading dissonance through the great chord, small and patient as a pickpocket, working at something. Working at *loosening*.

Vael looked at the open eyes, and the open eyes regarded her, and for the first time in nineteen years she did not look away first.

"Hold on," she said quietly, in a voice no guard could hear, and did not permit herself to wonder whether she meant it as comfort or instruction.

Then she walked on into the dark, carrying her signed contract and her borrowed conscience, while somewhere far above, over the frozen Steppe, the first snow of the season began to fall — and fell, she could not help noticing, a half-beat out of rhythm.
