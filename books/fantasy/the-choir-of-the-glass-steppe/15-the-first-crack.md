## 15. The First Crack

The Court taught its singers to be perfect. That was the first thing Wren understood about it, in the long amber hours before her fitting was complete: perfection was not a virtue here. It was a function. The glass panes that rose in their thousand ranks through the cathedral dark were tuned instruments, and the choir that stood before them — the frozen ones, the patient ones, the ones whose eyes still moved when they thought no one watched — existed to sound them exactly, endlessly, without deviation.

A pane struck true held the season. A pane struck false did nothing at all.

Or so the Regent's attendants said, in voices like poured honey over broken stone.

Wren had been three days in the fitting hall, or what she thought of as days, though beneath the mountain there was no difference between them except the changing of the lights along the vaulted ceiling, a slow aurora of green and violet that cycled whether anyone sang or not. They had dressed her in white. They had braided silver thread into her hair. They had set her on a low dais of frosted glass with eleven other new arrivals and begun, with terrible gentleness, to teach her the Winter Canticle.

She learned it. Of course she learned it. She had been singing since before she could talk; her mother used to say Wren had come out of the womb already harmonizing with the midwife's humming. The Canticle was beautiful in the way that traps are beautiful — all its intervals resolved, all its phrases returning home, nothing left unresolved anywhere in it. It was a song with no questions in it. A song that had forgotten how to want anything.

That was how it worked, she realized, somewhere around the fortieth repetition. The song didn't hold winter in place by force. It held winter in place by *agreement* — by making every soul in the choir consent, note after note after note, to the way things were. The frozen singers weren't prisoners the way chains make prisoners. They were prisoners the way a lullaby makes prisoners. Somewhere deep under the discipline and the beauty, each of them was still saying yes.

And yes, said often enough, became the only word you remembered how to say.

Wren looked down the rank of singers beside her. There was a ferryman's widow two places to her left who had stopped blinking. There was a boy of maybe twelve whose lips moved constantly now, even between verses, practicing, always practicing, his eyes gone soft and far. Three days. It had taken three days.

Her own voice felt strange in her throat, like a borrowed coat. When she sang the Canticle she could feel it doing something to her — smoothing her edges, filing down the little irregularities that made her *her*. The place inside her where her anger lived was getting quieter. The place where her mother's kitchen smelled of cinnamon was getting quieter. She held onto those rooms in her mind the way you hold a door shut against wind, and every verse tried the latch.

So she began, very quietly, to sing wrong.

Not wrong enough to be noticed. Not yet. A grace note where none belonged. A suspension held one breath too long, resolving late, lazily, like someone reluctant to come home. Small disobediences, smuggled into the spaces between the notes, where the Court's attention was thinnest because nothing there required governing.

The pane nearest her dais — a tall sheet of greenish glass veined with frost-ferns — flickered once, like a candle in a draft.

None of the attendants saw. But the widow two places down blinked, slow as a waking cat, and looked at Wren, and something moved behind her eyes that had not moved in days.

*Oh,* Wren thought, and her heart went loud in her chest. *It works both ways.*

If perfect agreement kept the souls asleep, then disagreement might wake them. And if one soul woke —

She thought of Silas. She didn't know where he was, whether he was alive, whether he had even tried to come. But she knew him the way you know a song you learned as a child: stubborn, scarred, constitutionally incapable of leaving a debt unpaid. If there was any chance at all that he was coming down the long dark toward her, then her job was not to survive the choir.

Her job was to break it.

She waited until the great cycle turned — the Regent's attendants called it the Turning, the moment when all thousand panes were sounded together and the season above was renewed. The whole cathedral drew breath. The frozen singers straightened. The aurora overhead dimmed to a single waiting green.

And into the vast consonance of the Winter Canticle, Wren sang one note that did not belong.

She chose it carefully — a minor second against the dominant tone, close enough to sound almost intentional, ugly the way a wound is ugly. She put her whole self into it: the ribbon Silas had given her, lost now somewhere in Vael's raid, but remembered; the smell of cinnamon; the dockside taverns where she'd sung for copper; the specific, unrepeatable fact of being Wren, daughter of nobody, savior of a dying mercenary, afraid and furious and absolutely unwilling to say yes anymore.

The note went out across the cathedral like a crack going through ice.

The great green pane beside her dais shivered. Frost-ferns bloomed across it in a rush, wild, unpatterned — and then, with a sound like a bell dying, a fracture ran from its base to its crown.

The Canticle faltered. For half a heartbeat, a thousand frozen singers wavered off-key, and in that wavering Wren heard them — *all* of them — gasp, inwardly, like drowning people breaking surface.

Then the pane burst.

Not shattered — *opened*, the way a window opens, the two halves swinging outward on hinges of light, and through the opening came a rushing cold clean wind that smelled of snow and pine and distance, and in the wind, rising, a shape of pale fire uncoiled upward — a soul, whole and shrieking with joy, tearing free of the choir and streaming up through the vaulted dark toward some distant shaft of air, toward the surface, toward the sky.

The last thing Wren heard of it was laughter.

Then every alarm the Court possessed began to ring, and the honey-voiced attendants turned toward her dais with faces no longer gentle at all.

* * *

Two hundred miles north, winter broke.

Silas felt it before he understood it. He was three-quarters down the ossuary stair, in a gallery of murals so old the pigment had gone the color of dried blood, when the air changed. The stair had been cold the whole descent — grave-cold, the cold of stone that has never known sun. Now it grew *warmer*, abruptly, by a degree, then another, and a draft came up from below carrying a smell that did not belong in the under-mountain: wet earth. Thawing earth.

He stopped, hand on the wall, ribs aching where the wraith in the last gallery had raked him. His remaining charms hung at his belt like dead birds — he had sold most of himself to the ferryman, name and memory and nearly everything else, and what was left barely glimmered. He was, he reflected, mostly an ordinary man with a sword now, descending into a cathedral of gods' corpses. The arithmetic was bad. It had been bad since the dockside. He had stopped doing it.

The draft warmed again. Above him — far above, through a mile of bone and rock — he heard a sound he had no right to hear this deep: a groan of shifting ice. The Steppe's great frozen marshes, the ones that hadn't cracked open in living memory, groaning in their sleep.

Something below had loosened winter's grip.

He should have been afraid. Panic above meant panic below; a court that kept the seasons would not take kindly to interference. Instead he found himself grinning like an idiot in the dark, blood on his teeth, because there was only one kind of interference he could imagine, only one person reckless enough and gifted enough and *stubborn* enough to make winter itself flinch.

"Wren," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, and he took the next flight of stairs at a run.

The murals hurried past him. He had studied them on the way down, cataloguing the story they told in their faded processions: gods walking the Steppe in glory, gods bound at a table of glass, gods devoured — rendered as rivers of gold light pouring from open mouths into the mountain's roots. He had found, in the third gallery, a figure he had stared at until his eyes burned: a tall god with a lantern, faceless with age, standing apart from the others, looking back over its shoulder as if it had heard something calling from above.

He had felt, staring at that faceless god, a grief so specific it frightened him. Like missing a man whose name you'd sold.

Now he ran past all of it, past the devouring, past the binding, down toward the sound of ringing bells and rising wind, and the warmth followed him down like a companion.

* * *

Inquisitor Vael felt the winter break from the high road above the Steppe, and it undid twenty years of certainty in the space of a single hour.

She had been riding to the mountain with a fresh contract — forty souls assessed and approved, sealed in lead and wax, the crown's seal and hers side by side. Routine work. Necessary work. She had done the arithmetic of it a thousand times and it had always balanced: a few hundred gifted lives, gently taken, weighed against a realm that lived. The founders had made the bargain; she merely kept the books. Someone had to keep the books. The alternative was chaos, famine, the long death of everything green.

Then the temperature rose nine degrees in an afternoon, in midwinter, and the marshes began to weep, and her horse refused to go on, and the peasants in the villages she passed through came out of their houses pointing at the sky, where clouds were forming that had no business existing — soft, confused, undecided clouds, clouds that seemed to be *inventing themselves*.

Vael sat her horse on the ridge above the Steppe and watched snowmelt run silver down a furrow that had been solid ice that morning, and felt the ledger in her mind refuse, for the first time, to balance.

Because here was the thing she had never let herself write down: the seasons had never been *natural*. She had known that since her initiation into the Inquisition's inner records. The court below did not govern winter the way a king governs a kingdom. It *was* winter, sustained by stolen agreement, by a thousand souls singing yes forever. And if a single voice could crack a pane —

Then the whole edifice was not order. It was a dam. And dams did not leak gracefully.

"Ride on," she told her escort, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "We're expected."

But her hand shook on the reins, and she did not look back at the melting Steppe, because she was afraid of what her face was doing.

* * *

They took Wren off the dais quickly, but not roughly — roughness was wasted on the condemned, and besides, the Regent wanted her intact. They marched her through corridors of singing glass to a chamber where the walls were one continuous pane, and behind the pane, dim and enormous, something turned over in its sleep.

The Regent did not appear as a body. It appeared as a pressure, a presence like the moment before lightning, and its voice arrived from everywhere, courteous and vast.

"You are either very brave," it said, "or very badly advised."

"I get that a lot," Wren said. Her knees were shaking. She made them stop.

"You cost me a soul. Do you understand what that means? That soul was keeping frost off the northern valleys. Children will be born this spring in villages that have never seen a thaw flood. Rivers will move. Roads will drown." The pressure tightened, gentle as a thumb on a moth's back. "You call it freedom. From down here, it looks like damage."

"From down here," Wren said, "everything looks like damage. That's what happens when you build your house out of people."

A silence, long and considering. Then, almost softly:

"The mercenary is coming for you. Did you know? He sold pieces of himself on the way down — his name, his memories, his god's face. By the time he arrives he will barely remember why. You could spare him that. Sing the Canticle truly, and I will send him up alive with no memory of this place, whole, mortal, free. Refuse, and I will fit him into the choir beside you, and you can watch him forget you note by note."

Wren's heart went cold and small. She thought of Silas selling his name to a ferryman, selling his god's face, climbing down through galleries of murdered gods with less and less of himself at every step — coming for *her*, always for her, the oath-bound fool.

And she thought about what she had learned in three days of quiet sabotage: that the Court could not digest a freely given thing. That its whole architecture was built on consent, harvested and hollowed. That every crack began with someone saying no.

"He gave me a choice once," she said. "When I was small and scared and my song was bigger than I was. Everyone else wanted to use it or cage it. He just asked me what I wanted to do with it." She lifted her chin. "So I'm giving him the same courtesy. He gets to choose. And whatever he chooses — I'll be singing when he gets here."

The pressure withdrew, unhurried, like a tide deciding.

"Then we will see," the Regent said, "whose song survives the winter."

They led her back to the dais. The broken pane had been replaced overnight — a fresh sheet, flawless, blank as an unwritten page. Wren looked at it for a long moment.

Then she opened her mouth, and began — quietly, patiently, in the spaces between the notes where no one was listening — to teach the widow two places down a brand-new harmony.

* * *

Silas reached the doors of the Glass Court as the first tremor ran through the mountain.

He had no name left to speak — the ferryman had taken it, and when he tried to think of himself, he got a shape without a word, a man-shaped hole wearing his scars. He had almost no charms. He had one memory he couldn't account for: a lantern, held by someone tall, in a golden room. He had a sword, a broken rib, and a certainty that had survived every sale: *someone down here is mine to protect.*

The great doors stood open. Of course they did. Things that believed themselves eternal never bothered locking.

Inside, the cathedral blazed with alarm-light, a thousand panes ringing discordantly, and above the noise — thin, defiant, unmistakable, threading through the chaos like a needle through cloth — a voice he would have known at the bottom of the sea was teaching a room full of the dead how to sing off-key.

The hole shaped like a man started, against all arithmetic, to run.
