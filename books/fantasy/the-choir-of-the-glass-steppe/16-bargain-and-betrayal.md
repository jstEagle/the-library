## 16. Bargain and Betrayal

The Regent of Glass did not breathe, but it waited as though breathing were a courtesy it had once practiced and could resume at will.

Silas knelt on the frozen floor of the cathedral, where the panes beneath his knees showed him reflections he did not want: himself, gaunt and gray, his name sold away somewhere on the ossuary stair; and behind him, the choir — hundreds of singers encased in glass from throat to ankle, mouths open on notes that never ended, eyes fixed on distances no living person chose.

*You have considered my offer,* the Regent said. Its voice came from everywhere, from the walls themselves, from the ice in his own blood. *You have walked among them. You have seen what they are.*

He had. He had spent three days in the Court's hospitality — fed, warmed, flattered — while it paraded its captives past him like wares at market. A farmer whose hands still moved in the shape of sowing songs. A midwife who had sung fevers out of children for forty years. A boy no older than twelve whose gift was nothing more than making bread rise true, and who wept glass tears that froze before they fell.

*I have seen slaves,* Silas said.

*I have seen salvation.* The Regent's tone did not change. It never changed. That was the horror of it — a thing so old it had worn its own voice smooth. *The seasons turn because they sing. The Steppe lives because they do not stop. Would you trade ten thousand harvests for one child's freedom? You already know what the world above becomes without us. You saw the winter falter when one pane cracked.*

One pane. One soul escaped upward, and all Veyra had shuddered. Frost-kill in the vineyards. Rivers locking early. Panic in the markets, panic in the crown, panic that had sent Inquisitor Vael down the mountain with fresh contracts clutched to her breast like scripture.

Silas bowed his head. It was not entirely an act. Despair is honest work; it asks only that you show up.

*I'll bring them,* he said. *Souls. From above. I know where they hide — the Hollow Choir, Mother Ash, all of them. I can name every safehouse between here and the coast.*

The lie went through him like a swallowed coal. He thought of Ash's crooked house with its kettle always on, of the deaf fiddler who kept watch on the roof, of Wren's ribbon folded against his heart where a memory of his god's face used to be. He offered all of it up for slaughter, and the words came out steady, and he hated how steady.

*And your price?*

"Wren." Her name in this place felt like striking a match underwater. "The singer. Cut her free. Give her back her breath, her name, her road home. Then I'm yours."

*One soul,* the Regent said, *for many.*

"One soul for all the rest you'd ever eat."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against him like deep water. Somewhere in the cathedral a pane hummed, a single held note, and Silas let himself look — just once, just briefly — toward the place where Wren hung.

She was third from the eastern wall, suspended in her column of glass, and she was singing. They were all singing; that was the engine of the place. But Wren sang the way water finds cracks. She bent each note a hair off true, slid a quarter-tone sideways, dropped a syllable where the Court expected it held — small rebellions, invisible to anything that listened for obedience rather than music. He had watched her for three days and understood: she was not merely surviving in there. She was working. Loosening stitches. And she had not looked at him once, not since he'd arrived, and he knew why. She was protecting him by pretending he meant nothing. She was very good at it. She had been good at pretending since she was seven years old and sang a dying mercenary back into his body with a song she'd stolen off her grandmother's knee.

*It shall be done,* the Regent said.

And Silas Whitlock, who had no name anymore and almost no magic and nothing left to sell but the truth, felt hope move through him like blood returning to a numb limb — painful, warm, unbearable.

That was when the doors opened, and Inquisitor Vael came down the aisle like winter itself arriving on schedule.

* * *

She walked alone. Two glass sentinels flanked the doors but did not follow her; whatever contract she carried, it bought her passage unescorted even here. Her boots made no sound on the frozen floor. Her gray coat was dusted with mountain snow that did not melt.

"Regent," she said, and inclined her head with the precise courtesy of a woman greeting an equal she despised. "I've brought the amended schedules. The crown requires—"

*The crown requires nothing,* the Regent said. *The crown's founders required. You require. Speak plainly, inquisitor. We are among honest creatures now.*

Vael's gaze found Silas then. He watched her take him in — kneeling, penitent, positioned exactly where a man would kneel if he had just sold everything he loved. He watched her decide what she was seeing.

"You," she said. "Whitlock."

"That's not his name any longer," the Regent observed mildly. "He sold it on the stair. He is currently unnamed, which makes him remarkably easy to bind."

"I see." Vael came closer, stopping an arm's length away, close enough that he could smell the ink and iron of her, the cold paper smell of contracts. "And what has the unnamed man been offering?"

"The Hollow Choir," Silas said. His voice came out flat and dead, which was the performance, which was also simply true. "Safehouses. Names. All of it. In exchange for one singer."

"Ah." Vael turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. She studied him the way she had studied him across a burning safehouse in the smugglers' quarter, the way she had studied everyone she ever intended to own: patiently, thoroughly, looking for the seam. "Three days ago you fought through my wraiths on the ossuary stair with nothing but a broken charm and your bare hands. You bled on every step. Men don't bleed like that for a transaction."

"Men bleed like that when they're tired."

"Yes," she agreed softly. "They do. But they don't *kneel* like that."

The pane beneath Silas's knee gave back his reflection, and in it he saw what she saw: a man kneeling too straight. Grief slumps. Surrender goes loose. Only performance holds its spine.

*Inquisitor,* the Regent said, and there was something new in the smooth voice now — something that might, in a younger creature, have been impatience. *Your contract awaits amendment. Attend to it.*

"My contract," Vael said, "requires me to certify the souls I deliver. Freely given, honestly bargained, without coercion or deceit. Those are the founding terms. The founders were very particular." She drew a slim black ledger from her coat. "Because the founders understood something about this place. It cannot digest a lie. It cannot hold what is freely given and falsely taken. Every binding in this cathedral rests on the honesty of the bargain — that is the whole architecture of it. That is why the choir sings forever and cannot simply walk away. They were taken *truly*."

The temperature of the room seemed to drop further, if such a thing were possible.

"So when an unnamed man offers to sell his comrades," Vael continued, opening the ledger, "I am required by my office to ask whether he means it. And I have read this man's file. I have read every scrap of paper he ever burned, because burning leaves ash, and ash can be read by someone patient enough." She looked down at him, and for one unguarded moment there was something in her face that was not triumph. It was almost grief. Almost respect. "You wrote your oath down when you were nineteen years old, Whitlock. *I will not sell what I have sworn to keep.* Your god witnessed it. Your god is in these walls. Did you think I wouldn't know?"

Silas rose. Slowly, because his knees had gone stiff with cold and fear, but he rose.

"It's a fair copy," he said. "Oaths wear out. Gods go silent. Ask yours."

Something flickered behind Vael's eyes — the same wound, he realized, the same hollow place, dressed in different clothes. She believed she was saving the realm. She had given her life to a bargain older than her grandmother, and she had never once asked who was eating.

"He means to betray you," Vael said to the Regent, and her voice was steady and clear and carried to every corner of the cathedral, and Silas understood that this, too, was a kind of testimony. "I certify the offer false. I invoke the founding clause. Let the bargain be weighed."

*Weighed,* the Regent repeated.

The floor opened.

Not violently — that would have been mercy. The glass beneath Silas simply became liquid, then became intention, and he sank to the waist before he could draw breath to curse. Threads of it wound up around him, patient as ivy, tasting him. He felt them reading: the sold name, the sold memory, the spent charms, the oath buried under all of it like a coin at the bottom of a well.

*There is little left to bind,* the Regent mused. *You have sold yourself nearly empty, little thief. What remains?*

"Nothing you want," Silas said through his teeth.

But the threads kept reading, and he felt the moment they found it — the thing he had forgotten he was carrying, folded so deep that even he had lost track of it. Not the memory of his god's face; that was sold, spent, gone with the ferryman. Something older and smaller. A promise made in a burning field fifteen years ago to a child with a borrowed song: *I will come back for you. Whatever it costs. I swear it on what's left of me.*

Freely given. Never taken back. Still true.

The threads recoiled from it as if scalded.

*What is this?* the Regent asked, and for the first time in what might have been centuries, the smooth voice roughened.

"A debt," Silas said, and began, despite everything, to laugh — a broken, freezing, furious sound. "You can't digest it. Your whole cathedral runs on honest bargains, and I made one you can't collect. Go on. Bind me anyway. See what it does to your architecture."

*Bind him beside the singer,* the Regent said coldly. *Let the debt be a weight upon him. Let him sing until the promise rots out of him. Everything rots here eventually. Everything stops singing true.*

The glass took him the rest of the way.

* * *

It closed over his legs, his chest, his arms, gentle as snowfall and final as a grave. It left his head free — they needed the mouth, the breath, the voice. It fitted itself to him with horrible intimacy, learning the shape of his ribs, and then it lifted him, and the cathedral arranged him through the air with the unhurried care of a librarian reshelving a book, and set him down in the column beside Wren.

Only then did she look at him.

Her eyes were the same. That was the cruelest thing. Fifteen years, a war, a purge, a cathedral of the damned, and her eyes were exactly the eyes that had looked down at a dying man in a burned field and decided, with a child's absolute authority, that he was not finished yet. There was frost in her lashes. Her lips were cracked and still moving, still singing, still bending every note a hair off true.

Her gaze flicked down — once — to his chest. To the place under the glass where a faded ribbon lay folded over his heart.

Then, deliberately, so slowly it could have been accident, she slid one finger against the inside of her prison. And tapped. Twice. Pause. Twice more.

An old signal. Smuggler's cant, dockside code, the knock dockworkers used to say *cargo's marked, wait for the second cart.*

Wait. Don't act. Help is coming.

Silas wanted to answer. Wanted to tell her there was no second cart, no help, no plan left — that he had lied to a god and been caught by an inquisitor and now hung in glass beside her with nothing in his hands but a promise the architecture couldn't swallow. He wanted to say he was sorry. He had carried that apology fifteen years, through every silent year of his god's absence, and it had grown so large he could barely breathe around it.

Instead he did the only thing left. He opened his mouth, and he joined the choir.

His voice came out ragged, mortal, untrained — a soldier's voice, a drunk's voice, nothing like the pure instruments around him. The Court took it and tried to fit it into the great turning harmony, the engine-song that spun the seasons, and for a moment it worked, and he felt himself beginning to smooth out, to simplify, to become a note instead of a man.

Then Wren shifted her song. Barely. A half-step, a held breath, a crack in the right place — and suddenly his ragged voice wasn't noise to be corrected. It was a harmony line. It had a part. She wove him in the way she had woven him back into his body in a burned field, taking everything broken about him and making it load-bearing.

And far below the note, underneath the melody, where the Court's listening could not quite reach, she began to sing words.

Old words. Borrowed words. The song she had sung over a dying man fifteen years ago, the one her grandmother taught her, the one that belonged to no god and no court and no crown — the one that was given, freely, and could therefore never be owned.

Silas sang with her.

Across the cathedral, Inquisitor Vael stood alone among the glass columns with her ledger open and her certification complete, and she did not leave. She told herself it was oversight. Due diligence. She watched the two of them harmonize in their chains, and she felt, in the hollow place where her own god used to live, the first faint vibration of a crack.

High above, through the mountain's roots, a pane in the eastern wall began — very quietly, note by note, stitch by loosened stitch — to sing off-key.

And the winter over the Glass Steppe stuttered, like a heart missing a beat.
