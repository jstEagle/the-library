## 17. Duet in Chains

The chains did not feel like iron. They felt like winter — a cold that began in the marrow and worked outward, threading through the bones as though the bones had always been hollow and had only now been filled. Silas hung beside Wren in the choir's great frame, his wrists bound above him by filaments of glass so fine they sang when he breathed.

Around them stretched the Court: ten thousand panes, each one a captive soul frozen mid-note, and each pane lit from within by a light the color of the season it carried. Spring panes glowed green-gold near the eastern wall. Summer burned amber at the crown of the vault. Autumn smoldered rust-red along the southern arc, and winter — winter was everywhere, winter was the floor he could not see beneath his feet, winter was the silence between notes.

And through all of it ran the song.

It was not music, not really. It was maintenance. It was the sound of a machine made of throats, grinding out the turning of the year note by note, hour by hour, century by century. The captives did not sing it; the song sang *them*, pulling their voices out like thread from a spindle, and what remained after the pulling was less than memory.

Silas had been bound for — he did not know how long. Time moved strangely here, measured in refrains rather than hours. Long enough that his arms had stopped screaming and settled into a dull, patient ache. Long enough that he had begun, horribly, to feel the song's pull on his own throat, a gentle insistent tugging, like a tide testing a mooring rope.

*Sing,* the Court whispered. *You are here to sing.*

Beside him, Wren's head hung forward, her dark hair curtaining her face, her wrists bound in the same singing glass. She had not spoken since they'd chained her back into the frame after Vael's betrayal. He had called her name until his voice cracked. She had given no sign she heard.

But her fingers were moving.

Small movements. Almost nothing. The index finger of her right hand, tapping against her palm — once, twice, then a pause, then twice more. A rhythm he knew. Every smuggler in the quarter knew it. *All quiet. All quiet. Watch and wait.*

She was still in there. Whatever the Court had swallowed of her voice, it had not swallowed *her*.

Silas closed his eyes and let himself hang, and thought about promises.

His oath was an old thing, older than his silence, older than his scars. He had sworn it kneeling in the mud outside a burning village with his god's last audible word still ringing in his ears — a word like a bell struck underwater. *Keep them.* Two syllables. He had built twenty years on those two syllables, and the god who'd spoken them had been dead the whole time, devoured down here in the dark, rendered into season and glass, and the oath had outlived its maker the way a river outlives its spring.

Oaths outlive gods. That was the thing the Regent had never understood. That was the thing the whole hungry cathedral had never understood.

Because the Court ate souls. It ate names, memories, faces, futures. It had eaten his god down to the marrow and worn the marrow as a crown. But there was one substance in all the world it could not digest, and Silas had figured it out hanging in chains with nothing left to lose, and the irony of it nearly made him laugh: the Court could not digest a freely given promise.

Everything here was taken. Every soul in every pane had been bought or stolen or traded, harvested by Vael's courteous men, ferried down by the ferryman's black boat, fitted into the frame like panes into a window. Nothing in this place had ever been *given*. The Regent's whole architecture was built on theft, and theft has a flaw running through it the way ice has a flaw, and you only have to find the flaw and speak to it.

A promise, freely given, cannot be stolen. It can only be kept or broken. And Silas Whitlock — charm-seller, mercenary, heretic, man with no god and no name and almost no charms left — had come down into the dark specifically, stupidly, stubbornly to keep one.

He opened his eyes. "Wren," he said, and his voice came out ragged but his own. "Little wren. Look at me."

Her fingers stilled. Then, slowly, her head came up.

Her eyes were wrong — filmed over with a faint pearly light, the Court already wearing her like a glove — but behind the film something looked out at him, and recognized him, and grieved.

"There you are," he said softly. "There you are."

* * *

Wren heard his voice the way you hear a voice through deep water — muffled, warped, arriving late, arriving anyway.

She had been inside the song for what felt like years. That was the trick of the Court, the cruelty under its beauty: it dilated time inside its singers until a night felt like a decade and a decade felt like a single held breath. She had forgotten the taste of bread. She had forgotten her mother's kitchen, the smell of the river in summer, the ribbon she'd dropped in the concert hall — no, not forgotten, exactly. Those things were still in her, but they were far away, pressed flat like flowers in a book someone else owned.

The song pulled. It pulled constantly, patiently, without malice, the way a millwheel turns. *Give us the high part. Give us the long part. Give us the part of you that remembers spring.* And she gave, because refusing hurt worse than giving, because the glass bit deeper when she fought it, because somewhere in the first hour — the first true hour, before time went strange — she had learned the terrible arithmetic of this place: every note she withheld was taken from someone weaker.

So she gave. But she gave *crooked*.

That was her rebellion, small and stubborn and hers: she sang everything a half-tone off. Not enough to break the harmony — the Court corrected around her, the way water corrects around a stone — but enough that nothing she gave fit perfectly. Enough that the machine had to work a little harder for every measure she fed it. Enough that she stayed, in some tiny essential way, *herself*. A wheel that does not quite mesh grinds. She meant to grind.

They had tried to sand the crookedness out of her. The Regent itself had come, all glass and courtesy, and turned her face toward the vault and shown her the summer panes and said, *See how beautiful. See how necessary. You could be necessary forever.* And for one awful moment she had wanted it — wanted to be necessary, wanted the ache to stop, wanted to dissolve into something vast and purposeful instead of remaining small and hurting and alone.

Then she had remembered a man bleeding out in a ditch fifteen years ago, and how she had knelt in the mud — a child, nine years old, knowing nothing of magic except that her grandmother said songs could carry things if you loved the thing enough — and how she had sung his breath back into him, note by note, borrowing the melody from a lullaby and filling it with everything she had.

She hadn't given that song because she was necessary. She'd given it because she loved him. There was no machine in the world that could want that from you, and no machine that could make it.

Now his voice came through the water of the song, ragged and mortal and calling her name, and the flowers pressed in the book stirred, and Wren lifted her head.

"There you are," he said.

She couldn't answer. Her throat belonged to the song; when she tried to shape words, the melody poured out instead, three rising notes, the Court's own phrase, *yield, yield, yield*. But her fingers tapped against her palm: *all quiet, all quiet, watch and wait.*

And then Silas Whitlock, hanging in his chains with his silver-shot hair falling out of its tie and his wrists bleeding thin lines of red down the glass, began to sing.

* * *

He had no right to be any good at it. His voice was a dockworker's voice, a bar-fight voice, a voice that had shouted orders across storm-water and sung exactly one kind of song for twenty years: the low wordless working hum of a man keeping his own courage company. The Court's sopranos could have shredded him. The Regent's harmonies could have folded him into themselves like a drop of wine into the sea.

But he wasn't singing to the Court.

He was singing to her.

He started low, below the song's register, in the basement of his range where the Court's machinery didn't bother to listen — a drone, a foundation, the sound a keel makes against deep water. And over that drone, rough and slow, he laid the words of his oath. Not the formal words. The mud words. The two syllables a dying god had spent its last breath on, which Silas had carried ever since like a coal in his chest:

*Keep them. Keep them. However long. However far. Keep them.*

The song faltered.

Not much. A stutter, a skipped beat, the way a loom skips when one thread goes suddenly true. Ten thousand panes flickered. Somewhere in the vault a summer pane guttered like a lamp in wind, and the amber light steadied, and guttered again.

The Court noticed. Of course it noticed. The whole cathedral shivered, and the whisper that lived in the walls — the voice that was not the Regent and not the singers but the hunger underneath both — turned its attention on Silas like a lens focusing sunlight.

*What is this?* the whisper asked, and its curiosity was worse than its anger. *This is not in the score.*

"No," Silas agreed, and kept singing. "It isn't."

*You are bound. You are ours. Your voice is ours.*

"You bought my body," he said. "Vael sold it to you, and the ferryman carried it down, and you've chained it up nice as you please. But an oath isn't in the body." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down, and gave it to the drone. "An oath is a debt I chose. You can't spend what a man chose. You can only watch him pay it."

Wren's fingers had gone still against her palm. Through the pearl-film of her eyes, something was waking — he could see it happening, see the far-away pressed-flat things coming closer, the lullaby, the ditch, the mud, the child kneeling with her whole heart in her throat.

"Come on, little wren," he murmured between phrases, barely singing it, barely saying it. "You taught me this. Fifteen years ago. You sang me back from the dark with borrowed music and I have owed you ever since. So here's the tune. Take it. Make it yours. Make it *crooked*."

* * *

Crooked.

The word reached her and unlocked something.

Because that was the secret she had guarded through all the long dilated years of the song — that perfection was the Court's food and imperfection was its poison. The Court wanted clean tones, seamless harmony, souls sanded smooth as window glass. Everything it could digest was smooth. And she had spent herself half-tone by half-tone staying rough, staying grainy, staying *Wren*, and the Court had ground against her grain this whole time and told itself the friction was nothing, was tolerable, was a flaw in one pane among ten thousand.

But it wasn't one pane. It had never been just her.

She let the film fall away from her voice — not her eyes, she needed the film there still, needed to see — and found the drone Silas was laying down, that low keel-sound, and set her voice on top of it.

She took his oath and harmonized it. Third above, then sixth, then a suspended fourth that ached and refused to resolve, because resolution was smoothness and she was done being smooth. She wove the lullaby through it — *her* lullaby, the one she'd borrowed at nine years old to drag a dying man back into the world — and where the Court's machinery reached for her notes she bent them away, sly as a fish, generous as rain.

And the strangest thing happened, the thing she would remember for the rest of her life however long or short it proved: the song *made room for them*.

Not surrendered. Not yet. But the great grinding millwheel of the Court's music encountered, for the first time in its long hungry existence, a passage it could not digest — a duet woven from a promise freely given and a gift freely returned — and it could not absorb it, could not flatten it, could not file it into the score. It had to flow around them. And flowing around them cost it. Every measure, it cost it.

The panes nearest them began to change.

It started as a trembling. A spring pane on the eastern wall — some farmer's grandmother, some soul that had been green-gold and serene for two hundred years — trembled, and its glow stuttered, and inside the glass something *turned over*, the way a sleeper turns over, the way someone waking decides whether to open their eyes.

Then another pane. Then a cluster of them, autumn-rust along the south arc, flickering in a rhythm that was almost — almost — a tap against a palm. *All quiet. All quiet. Watch and wait.*

"They can hear us," Wren breathed, and her voice was her own again, cracked and young and furious with hope. "Silas — they can *hear*—"

"Then let's give them something worth hearing."

* * *

Above them, the vault darkened.

The Regent had been absent since the binding — withdrawn into whatever inner chamber a thing like that retreats to, digesting its new acquisitions, adjusting its ledgers. Now it came back all at once, and the whole cathedral knew it, the way a forest knows a storm before the first branch moves. The light in the panes leaned eastward. The whisper in the walls rose to a frequency that set Silas's teeth aching.

It did not appear as a figure. It appeared as *attention* — vast, cold, precise, settling over the two chained singers the way a jeweler's loupe settles over a flawed stone.

*You are damaging the seasons,* it said. Its voice was reasonable. That was the horror of it; it was always reasonable. *Winter falters when a pane fails. Crops die. Children starve. You have seen the Steppe in a hard year. Do you propose to make every year a hard year?*

"We propose to make every year an *honest* year," Wren said.

*Honesty is a luxury of the unburdened,* the Regent said. *We carry the burden. We have always carried it. Someone must turn the wheel.*

"Someone must," Silas agreed, still singing, because stopping felt like dying and maybe it was. "But it doesn't have to be *them*. And it doesn't have to be stolen. You know that, don't you? Down in whatever passes for your heart. You know there was another way, once, before your founders got lazy and greedy and decided people were fuel." He lifted his chin, and the glass filament at his wrist drew blood, and he sang through it. "My god is in your walls. My god is in your *floor*. And the oath it gave me is stronger than anything you built, because you built yours on theft and mine was *given*."

The attention contracted. Tightened. For the first time, something other than reason moved in it.

*Stop,* said the Regent.

"No," said Wren, and hit the suspended fourth again, higher, brighter, deliberately unresolved.

*STOP.*

"No," said Silas, and dropped into the deepest note he had, the keel-note, the keep-them-note, and Wren rose over him like a bird over water, and together they threw the duet out across the cathedral —

— and the panes answered.

Not all of them. Not cleanly. But dozens, then scores, then hundreds, souls turning in their glass like sleepers hearing their names called from a great distance, flickering off-beat, off-key, *crooked*, each one a small rebellion, each one a little friction, and the flawless machine of the seasons began, for the first time in living memory, to grind.

A crack spidered across a summer pane high in the vault. Then another, in the autumn arc. A winter pane near the floor split with a sound like a lake breaking up in thaw, and a thread of light escaped upward — actual light, moving, free — and vanished toward the distant ceiling and whatever lay beyond it.

Far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of mountain, the Steppe felt it. Silas could not have said how he knew, but he knew: somewhere up there, snow was falling wrong. Somewhere up there, a hearth-fire guttered blue. Somewhere up there, people were stepping out of their doors and looking at the sky and feeling, without words, that the world had slipped a gear.

*You will kill the realm,* the Regent said, and now there was fear in it, real fear, and fear made it ugly. *I will unmake you both. I will grind you into the score note by note—*

"Then you'd better start," Wren said, "because we're not done singing."

And that was when Silas felt it — deep beneath his sternum, deeper than the chains, deeper than the ache, a stirring in the place where his god's silence had lived for twenty years. Something turning over. Something waking. A memory pressing upward through the rubble of everything he'd sold to the ferryman, everything he'd thought was gone forever.

The ferryman had taken his name, his charms, and a memory of his god's face. But debts freely given came back, somehow. Promises echoed. And as the duet swelled and the panes blazed and cracked and blazed again, Silas felt the missing memory rising in him like a diver rising toward light — rising with a face attached, a face he suddenly, terribly, needed to see.

"Wren," he said. "Don't stop. Whatever happens — don't stop."

"Why?" she asked. "What happens?"

He stared up into the blazing, cracking vault, at the light escaping upward through the widening seams, and felt his own buried divinity climb toward him through the dark.

"I think," he said, "I'm about to remember whose voice I've been singing with all along."
