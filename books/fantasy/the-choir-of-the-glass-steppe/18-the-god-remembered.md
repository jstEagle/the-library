## 18. The God Remembered

The chains sang when they tightened.

Not a pleasant song — nothing in this place was pleasant — but a song nonetheless, a thin metallic keening that ran through Silas's wrists and up into the bones of his arms and settled somewhere behind his sternum like a fishhook of cold iron. The choir's binding was not rope or chain as the world above understood them. It was harmony itself turned to restraint: each captive soul locked into its place in the great frozen score, unable to move a note left or right without dragging every other soul with it. He had been fitted beside Wren the way one fits a broken tile into a mosaic — pressed in, grouted, made permanent.

He could feel her. That was the one mercy the Regent had not thought to strip away, or perhaps had never thought mattered. Wren sat one pane away from him, her voice already woven into the endless winter-maintaining drone, and through the glass he could see the small stubborn movements she still made — the way she bent a phrase half a tone flat before letting the Court take it, the way her fingers twitched against her own chains in patterns that were not random, that were *counting*, that were *waiting*. She had not surrendered. Even here, even swallowed, she was singing *around* the Court rather than *for* it.

And now, bound into the choir beside her, Silas could hear what the Court heard.

That was the trap inside the mercy. To sing in the Glass Court was to be heard by everything, and everything here listened back. The cathedral of panes rose around him like a forest of frozen waterfalls, each one holding a soul suspended mid-note, and the whole vast architecture hummed with the accumulated weight of centuries — every harvest Vael's predecessors had delivered, every bargain struck in smoke-filled rooms above, every god that had ever gone silent over the Steppe. The Court did not merely keep the seasons. It *was* the ledger of everything it had eaten.

Silas opened his mouth because the chains required it, and gave the Court his voice.

It took only his voice. It tried for more.

* * *

The song they forced on him was old. Older than the Edict, older than the crown, older than the ferryman's price he had paid at the river of bone. He recognized its shape within three bars — a slow, patient melody built to hold warmth in the low places of the world, to keep sap moving under bark, to keep the ground from forgetting it was ever spring. It was a keeping-song. A tending-song. The kind of thing you would write if your whole purpose was to hold a realm together against the dark.

He knew it because he had sung it before.

Not here. Not in this life. But somewhere, in some part of himself so deep that twenty years of silence had buried it under ash and mercenary pragmatism and the small daily business of staying alive — somewhere he knew this melody the way a man knows his own heartbeat. His mouth moved through the notes and his body remembered them ahead of his mind, leaning into phrases before they arrived, and the horror of that recognition was so complete that for a moment the chains felt like the least of his bindings.

*You know this,* said something in the song. Not the Regent. Not any voice he could point to. It was more like the melody itself speaking, the way a river speaks in its current. *You have always known this.*

*I don't,* Silas answered, inside the place where his thoughts lived, which was apparently no longer private either. *I'm a sellsword. I sell luck-knots to dockworkers. I burn ledgers. I don't know songs like this.*

*You burned your ledgers,* the song observed, gently, terribly, *because you could not bear to read them.*

The pane beside him flickered.

He saw it happen — saw the frost on its surface thin in a single long streak, like breath clearing a window, and in that cleared space there was light, and in the light there was an image, and the image was of a hillside under a sky the color of new copper. He knew that hillside. He had dreamed it for twenty years without knowing he was dreaming it: the long grass bending in wind that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet, the standing stone at the crest worn smooth by hands, the little shrine where travelers left bread and buttons and broken things they hoped could be mended.

His god's hillside. The shrine where he had first knelt, a boy of nine, and been given the charm that would define his life — the small warm knot of power that had let him mend other people's broken things for thirty years after.

The pane cleared further. And in the shrine, in the memory, someone stood.

* * *

He had sold the memory of his god's face to the ferryman. That had been the worst of the prices, worse than the charms, worse than his name. A name could be rebuilt out of habit; people would still call him Silas, and eventually the sound would stick again. But a face — the face of the thing you had loved and served and mourned — once given away, it left a hole shaped exactly like devotion, and nothing else fit in it. For two decades he had walked around with that absence in him, telling himself the silence was abandonment, that his god had died or fled or never existed at all, that faith was just the first scam a young mercenary falls for.

Now the Court was giving it back.

Not kindly. Nothing here was kind. The Court gave it back the way a debt collector returns a seized possession — laid out on the table, itemized, with interest. Every note he sang pried another layer of frost off the pane beside him, and every layer showed him more, and the more he saw the less he could look away.

The figure in the shrine wore a traveler's coat, patched at both elbows. Its hair was the brown of river-wet oak. It had a scar through one eyebrow — a real scar, the kind earned falling out of some tree in childhood, not the decorative kind gods were supposed to have. It was tall the way he was tall. It stood with its weight on one hip the way he stood when he was waiting for a client who would probably cheat him. And when it turned toward the boy kneeling at the shrine — toward the small figure in the memory who was himself, forty years younger, nine years old and desperate and full of a faith he hadn't yet learned to be ashamed of —

— it had his face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance, not the vague kinship of a patron deity and devotee. It was *his* face, older by perhaps no years at all, unlined where his had been weathered, but his: the same crooked nose broken in the same bar fight, the same deep-set gray eyes, the same mouth that smiled a half-second after the eyes did, as though humor had to travel a longer road in him than in most men.

Silas stopped singing.

The chains wrenched the note out of him anyway — the Court did not permit silence, silence was a crack, cracks were the enemy — but for one full measure his will and the choir's pull fought to a standstill, and in that stolen moment he stared at the pane and the pane stared back, and the face in the shrine looked at him with his own eyes and *wept.*

*Do you understand now,* said the song, and it was not the song anymore, it was not the Court, it was something older speaking through the melody like a hand speaking through a glove, *why your god went silent?*

"No," Silas said aloud, and his voice cracked the note he was supposed to be holding, and somewhere far above them a season shuddered. "No. I understand nothing. I understand that I've been robbed for twenty years and everyone keeps telling me it was a gift."

*You were not robbed,* the voice said. *You were spent.*

The frost peeled back all at then, the whole pane going clear as lake ice, and the memory flooded him — not an image now but the thing itself, the actual past, pouring in like water into a dry well, and it hurt the way rehydration hurts, every cracked place in him splitting wider to drink.

* * *

There had been a bargain. Of course there had been a bargain; there was always a bargain; the Steppe ran on bargains the way mills ran on water. In the years before Silas's birth, the founders of the crown — the same founders whose signatures Vael had traced in the archives below, the same ink, the same seal — had come to the gods of the Steppe with a proposal dressed as a request. The seasons were failing. The old wild order of the world, ungoverned, was killing crops and drowning valleys and freezing flocks in the fields. The crown offered order. The crown offered permanence. All it required was that the gods lend their strength to the great engine beneath the mountain — and, when lending proved insufficient, give more. And more. Until giving was all they were.

Most of the gods had refused. Most of the gods had been devoured anyway, their names scraped from stones, their shrines plowed under, their faithful scattered — because a god outside the bargain was a god who might one day break it.

But one god had said yes.

One god had looked at the starving valleys and the frozen harbors and the children dying gray-lipped in the long winters, and had done the arithmetic that merciful things always do badly, and had concluded that one god was an acceptable price for a livable world. He had descended beneath the mountain willingly. He had poured himself into the engine. And because a god cannot pour himself out entirely while any piece of him remains free above — because divinity is indivisible, a flame cannot be half-carried — he had left a fragment of himself walking the surface, wearing a mortal life, tending small broken things with small broken charms, so that the world above would not entirely lose the shape of his care.

A tithe of himself. A hand's-breadth of the flame, kept burning in a lantern, in case the world ever needed lighting again.

The fragment had a name. The fragment had grown up on a hillside with a shrine, had taken work as a soldier because soldiers were always near broken things, had married badly and buried a wife and drifted into mercenary charm-work because it let him spend his days doing the only thing that had ever felt true: mending. The fragment had wondered, on bad nights, why his god never answered. Had prayed into the silence until the silence became his native country. Had assumed, with the bitter humility of the abandoned, that he had simply never been worth answering.

The fragment was Silas Whitlock.

* * *

He came apart for a while. There is no clean way to say it. The knowledge went through him not like a revelation but like a collapse — a mine shaft giving way, level after level falling into the dark — and the choir held him together the way scaffolding holds a ruined wall, mechanically, without tenderness. His hands shook against the chains. His voice, when the Court dragged the next note out of him, came out ragged, and the raggedness rippled through the panes around him like wind through wheat, and for a moment — one dangerous, luminous moment — the entire cathedral faltered.

Wren felt it. Through the glass between them he saw her head turn, saw her eyes find his, saw her read in his face something enormous and terrible, and saw her do the thing she had been doing since they bound her in: she bent her next phrase to meet his broken one. She caught his failing note and carried it, harmonized with his ruin instead of correcting it, and for four bars the two of them sang something the Court had no name for — a duet of damage, grief and defiance braided into a single line — and the pane between them thinned to almost nothing.

Then the Regent's will closed over them like a hand over a candle, and the score resumed, and the moment passed. But it had happened. He had felt it happen. And Wren had seen his face when it happened, and whatever she had seen there, she had not flinched.

*You see,* the voice in the song said, quieter now, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. *This is why we kept you. This is why the ferryman's price was permitted. A god who forgets himself cannot be missed. We let you walk the surface for forty years believing yourself abandoned, because a man who believes himself abandoned asks no questions about where his strength goes. Every charm you sold, every knot of luck, every mended bone — you thought you were spending your last reserves. You were spending us. You were the drip-feed. You were how we tasted the world we had given up.*

"Then take it back," Silas snarled into the humming dark. "Take it all back. Open the lantern. I'll come home."

*No,* said the voice, and now there was something in it he had never heard from a god or a court or a man: fear. *Not yet. Not like this. If you return to us now, willingly, you complete the circuit. The engine seals. The bargain becomes eternal. Everything you are pours in and the lid closes and the seasons run forever on the strength of a god who chose it — and no one, not Wren, not the Choir, not the wraiths in the stair, not the queen herself, will ever be able to argue with a choice freely made.*

"That's what the Regent offered me."

*The Regent offered you a cage and called it a throne. I am offering you the truth, which is crueler: you are the key, Silas Whitlock. You have always been the key. Your godhood was never lost. It was buried — here, in this glass, in this song — and the burial was the only act of rebellion our nature allowed us. We hid ourselves from ourselves. We put the flame in a lantern and sent the lantern walking, and prayed the lantern would stay lost.*

"And now?"

*Now the lantern has found its way home,* the voice said, *carrying a girl's ribbon and a ferryman's debt and an oath sworn over a child's borrowed song. Now everything depends on what the lantern does next.*

* * *

The chains shifted. The Regent, sensing — as these things always sense — that its raw material was undergoing unscheduled transformation, began tightening the score around him, folding him deeper into the winter-keeping melody, wrapping the old keeping-song over his shoulders like a shroud. Sing, it instructed, without words, with pressure alone. Sing and be filed. Sing and be useful. Sing and be home.

And Silas Whitlock — mercenary, heretic, orphaned devotee, fragment, key — hung in the great humming dark between Wren's defiance and his god's buried flame, and did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the descent, harder than the ferryman's price, harder than twenty years of praying into silence.

He waited. He listened. He let the Court think it was winning.

Because he finally understood what the voice had been trying to tell him, underneath the terror and the itemized grief: the divinity in him was not a possession to be restored. It was a choice waiting to be made. The Regent wanted him to make it *into* the engine — willingly, sealing the circuit forever. But a flame in a lantern can be carried anywhere. It can be carried *out.*

All he needed was Wren's voice, and a crack wide enough to carry a song.

He turned his head, slowly, against the pull of the score, and found her eyes through the thinning glass. Her lips moved — not singing, not yet, saving it — shaping words too quiet for the Court to parse, meant for him alone:

*On my count.*

Far above them, through a thousand feet of bone and stone and frozen song, the first pane in the cathedral's western wall gave a sound like a held breath about to break.
