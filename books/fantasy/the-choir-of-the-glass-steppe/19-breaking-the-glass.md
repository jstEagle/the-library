## 19. Breaking the Glass

*Silas*

The crack in the western wall sang first.

It was not a sound the ear caught so much as a sound the bones did — a high, thin keening that ran through the cathedral floor and up through Silas's chained ankles, through his knees, his spine, settling behind his teeth like a splinter of winter. The Regent of Glass turned its many-faceted head toward the sound, and for one heartbeat — one true, unmeasured heartbeat, the first he had felt since descending — the great score that bound them all faltered.

On my count.

Wren's eyes held his through the thinning pane between their stalls. Around her throat the collar of frozen light pulsed with the Court's rhythm, but her lips had already begun to move again, shaping the first syllable of something old. He knew it. Of course he knew it. It was the song she had sung over his dying body fifteen years ago in a ditch outside Marrowfen, the borrowed melody of a child who should not have known how to give her breath away.

She was singing it back to him now. Not borrowed this time. Given.

Silas closed his eyes and went looking for what remained of himself.

He found it where he had always been told there was nothing: beneath the hollow oath, beneath the twenty years of silence, beneath the sold name and the spent charms and the memory he had traded to a ferryman with a face like river mud. There was a door down there. There had always been a door. The Court had buried it under its own foundations and called the silence theft, and it had been — but a buried thing is not a dead thing, and a god's remnant does not stop dreaming just because no one remembers to pray.

The dream looked up at him.

It had his face. It had always had his face — the face he could not recall selling, the face the ferryman had taken and the Court had kept, folded into the glass like a pressed flower. It was younger than him and older than him, worn smooth by centuries of being sung at, and when their eyes met across the distance that was no distance at all, it did not speak. Gods past speaking do not need to. It simply opened, the way a hand opens, the way a throat opens for a note it has been holding too long.

*Take it,* said the silence that was not silence. *Or break it. Either way, I am done being furniture.*

"Silas." Wren's voice, aloud now, threading through the choir's mandated harmony like a blade through rope. "Now."

* * *

*Wren*

She had learned the Court's grammar from the inside, which was the only way anyone ever learned anything truly.

For what might have been days or years — time moved strangely inside the score — she had sung her assigned line perfectly and then bent it, a quarter-tone here, a breath stolen there, small mutinies disguised as imperfection. The Court absorbed everything offered to it; that was its nature and its hunger. But it could not absorb what was freely given, because giving was the one language it had never learned to read. Every note she surrendered willingly slipped through its fingers like water through a statue's hands.

And every note she gave away, she gave to someone else.

The woman two stalls down — Maren, a hedge-witch from the salt marshes, captured with her herbs still in her apron pockets — had hummed her own name back to herself three nights ago and wept without stopping for an hour. The old man beside her, whose name none of them knew because he had forgotten it himself, had remembered it that morning: Aldous. Just Aldous. He had said it over and over like a man tasting bread after famine. Little by little, note by note, Wren had been unbinding them, passing her stolen-and-returned selfhood down the rows of frozen singers like a loaf broken at table.

But unbinding was not freeing. They were still chained to the score. Their names came back to them only so they could feel the weight of the cage more precisely.

That was why she needed Silas. That was why she had waited, saving the one song she had never given to anyone, hoarding it in the marrow of her like a coal banked against winter.

The song from the ditch. His death-song. Hers now, grown up with her, changed by everything she had suffered and everyone she had saved — and underneath it, woven through it so finely the Court had never once detected it, ran the words of a mercenary's oath, spoken over a dying child's head in another lifetime:

*While I draw breath, you are not alone.*

She had thought, all these weeks, that she was protecting him with it. Standing in the choir now, watching his eyes open — watching them fill with light that did not come from the Court, light that came from somewhere deeper than the Court had ever dug — she understood that the oath had never been hers to keep safe.

It had been waiting for him to come and take it home.

"Now," she said, and gave him everything.

* * *

*Vael*

Inquisitor Seraphine Vael stood at the edge of the Glass Court with her ledger open and her pen dry, and for the first time in her career, she could not make the numbers agree.

She had descended expecting a countinghouse. That was what the archives promised: the Founders' Concordance, sealed in the third reign, binding the crown to provide "sustenance in souls, assessed and willing or made willing" to the court beneath the mountain, in exchange for ordered seasons, predictable harvests, and a realm that need never know fear of an ungoverned sky. She had believed — she had built her whole life on the belief — that the Purge was mercy. That harvesting the gifted before they could be burned by frightened mobs was salvation. That the assessment chambers were gentle. That the souls went somewhere, served something, were not wasted.

She had told herself the word *willing* meant what it said.

Now she watched a mercenary heretic stand chained in the choir with light pouring out of his chest like dawn through a cracked shutter, and she watched the Regent of Glass recoil from him — recoil, the Regent, the eternal engine, the thing her ancestors had fed for nine hundred years — and she understood, with the terrible clarity that comes too late to be useful and exactly soon enough to matter, that nothing in the Concordance had ever been willing. Not the souls. Not the seasons. Perhaps not even the Court itself, which had eaten the gods to survive and had been eating ever since, gorged and starving, a mouth that had forgotten it was once a face.

Her pen was dry because her hand had stopped moving.

"Inquisitor." The Regent's voice filled the cathedral, resonant and cracking at once, a bell with a flaw running through it. "The contract stands. Deliver the choir above. Deliver the singers, the hidden ones, the mother of ash and her nest of thieves. Do this and your family's names are struck from the rolls forever. Your sister's children will never be assessed."

Her sister's children. Vael had climbed thirty years on that lever. She had sold Silas Whitlock's client with it, and the client had sold Whitlock with it, and the whole rotten architecture of the crown rested on levers just like it, each one pulled by someone certain they were the last honest hand.

Above her, the western wall split with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"No," Vael said.

She was surprised by her own voice. So, evidently, was the Regent, whose facets flared white.

"I find," she said, closing the ledger with a snap that echoed, "that the contract is void. Article One. The parties must enter freely." She drew her sword — not the ceremonial blade, the real one, the plain steel she kept for work — and drove it point-first into the frozen floor, where it stood humming. "I am withdrawing the crown's signature. Retroactively. As best I can."

"You are one woman," the Regent said.

"Yes," said Vael. "Finally, something true in this entire mountain."

* * *

*Silas*

The god's remnant poured into him like a river remembering its bed.

It did not feel the way the old charms had felt — those little sips of warmth, luck-knots and fever-binds, candles burning down to nothing. This was the well itself. This was twenty years of drought ending in a single hour, and it would have drowned him if Wren's song had not been there to give it shape: her voice rising through the choir's mandated harmony, bending it, breaking it, wrapping the flood of him in a channel cut by love and grief and fifteen years of patient, stubborn care.

He opened his eyes, and the cathedral saw itself in them.

Every pane. Every frozen singer. The Court was glass, and he was — he understood now, with the god's memory restored whole — he had always been its opposite number. The Court consumed; the god had given. The Court froze; the god had turned. When the founders had dragged the gods beneath the mountain and devoured them to fuel their bargain, his own divinity had not died. It had been buried deepest of all, folded into a mortal man as a seed is folded into winter soil, because even the devouring Court could not stomach destroying something whose whole nature was to be spent.

A god of small things. A god of luck-knots and dockworkers' cold hands and fevered children pulled back from the brink. The Court had never understood what it was holding, because the Court only valued what was kept.

Silas had spent his whole life learning what it meant to give.

"Wren," he said, and his voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, mortal and divine in the same breath. "Together."

"Together," she said, and hit the note.

The oath went into the song — *while I draw breath, you are not alone* — and the song went into the glass, and the glass, which had spent nine hundred years digesting souls that were taken, encountered for the first time in its existence a promise that was freely given, and it could not hold it. Could not absorb it. Could not do anything but shatter.

The western wall went first, exploding outward in a bloom of glittering ruin, and through the breach rushed something the Court had never contained: air. Real air, cold and mineral and alive, smelling of stone water and distant thaw. Then the southern wall, then the vaulting overhead, pane after pane failing in cascading chords, each one releasing its captive — Maren the hedge-witch crying out her own name, old Aldous laughing, dozens, hundreds, the whole stolen congregation of the Steppe's gifted waking mid-note into freedom.

The Regent screamed. It was not an angry sound. It was the sound of a machine discovering it had been a person all along, and grieving.

Silas stood in the center of the collapsing cathedral, holding Wren's hand, and made his choice the way he had made every choice that mattered: quickly, completely, without looking back.

"Not restore," he said to the god within him and the god within the glass and the god within himself, all three the same. "*Spend.* All of it. Everyone goes."

The divinity rose in him like sunrise, and he broke it.

There was no other word for what he did. He took the whole of what he was — the seed, the remnant, the buried face, the twenty silent years and the twenty loud ones before them — and he shattered it as deliberately as the Court had shattered, and he gave it away. To the freed souls flooding upward through the breach, a spark apiece, enough to carry them home. To the wraiths in the ossuary stair above, who had once been singers themselves, enough to remember resting. To the land itself, seeping down through a thousand feet of bone, enough to teach the seasons to turn on their own.

And to the Regent — to the mouth that had forgotten its face — he gave the largest portion, the god's own oldest grief and oldest mercy, and watched the great faceted body soften, and crack, and come apart not in violence but in release, like ice going out of a river in spring, like a held breath finally let go.

The last thing the Court showed him, in its final panes, was his god's face smiling with his own mouth.

Then it was only glass on the floor, and ordinary light coming through, and Wren's arms around him as his knees gave out.

* * *

*Wren*

He weighed almost nothing.

That was her first thought, absurdly, kneeling in the ruins with his head in her lap — that the man who had carried her out of a ditch when she was six, who had carried an oath for fifteen years, who had just carried an entire cathedral's worth of souls on his back, weighed less than the ribbon she had dropped in Mother Ash's concert hall. Whatever he had spent, he had spent all of it. His skin was cool. His breathing was slow but present, stubborn as the rest of him, and when she pushed the hair back from his forehead his eyes fluttered, unfocused, and found her anyway.

"Told you," he whispered. "Not alone."

"You absolute fool," she said, and her voice cracked clean in half, and she sang to him instead — not magic, there was hardly any magic left in the world at this moment, the air still ringing with everything he had emptied out of himself — just a song, a plain human song, the kind mothers sing and lovers steal. Around them the freed souls were picking their way through the glittering wreckage toward the breach, toward the long climb, some helping each other, some weeping, Maren with her arm around old Aldous, everyone alive, everyone *theirs*, no one owned.

The mountain groaned around them. Without the Court, without the engine and the score and the nine-hundred-year bargain, the deep places were settling like a house at nightfall. Somewhere far above, snow was falling onto the Steppe in drifts that no longer obeyed anyone.

"We have to move him," said a voice she did not expect, and looked up.

The inquisitor stood at the edge of the rubble, sword still planted in the floor, ledger abandoned, her immaculate coat gray with dust. Her face was the face of a woman who had just burned her own life's work and was surprised to find herself still standing in the ashes.

"The stair is unstable," Vael said. "The ossuary galleries will start coming down within the hour. I know the load-bearing routes — I mapped them for the assessments." Something moved through her expression, bitter and rueful at once. "Nine hundred years of my predecessors' paperwork. Let it be good for one thing."

"Why?" Wren asked. She shifted Silas's weight, wary. "Why help us?"

Vael looked at the ruined Court, at the freed souls, at the place where the Regent had been, and her jaw tightened.

"Because I read the contract," she said, "all the way to the end. And because he—" she nodded at Silas, unconscious in a singer's arms amid the wreckage of a god— "just paid a debt the crown owed for nine centuries. Someone should hold the door."

* * *

*Silas*

He surfaced in and out of the dark, and each time he surfaced, there was more climbing.

He felt himself carried — Wren's shoulder under his arm, her strength astonishing, her voice in his ear keeping time like a drummer on a forced march. He felt cold stone steps, endless, spiraling up through walls lined with bones that were no longer hungry. He felt the wraiths pass them in the galleries, drifting close, and felt them *bow* — a rustling, reverent motion, the dead acknowledging the man who had unmade their prison — and heard Wren's breath catch and steady.

And once, near the top, when his legs would no longer answer and Wren and the inquisitor hauled him between them through a narrowing chimney of rock, he felt the mountain shudder around them and heard Vael shout, and looked back down the stair they had climbed.

The passages below were folding shut, the mountain closing its throat, sealing its dead away at last in peace. The gap between the falling rock and the ledge where they stood was ten feet wide and shrinking, and beyond it, limping and stumbling, came the last stragglers of the freed — a boy with salt-white scars on his wrists, an old woman carrying a child not her own, a dozen more, too slow, too far.

Vael let go of Silas.

She wedged herself into the closing gap, braced her back against one wall and her boots against the other, and became, with the plain stubbornness of her whole rigid life, a door.

"GO!" she roared at the stragglers, dust sheeting off the stone around her, cracks spidering through the rock above her head. "ALL OF YOU — THROUGH — *MOVE!*"

They moved. Through her arms' reach, one by one, the last of the stolen souls of Veyra passed out of the mountain's mouth while the inquisitor who had hunted them held the earth itself open with her body, and Silas, half-conscious, mortally tired, free, watched her do it and understood that redemption was not a feeling.

It was a weight, held as long as it needed holding.

The last soul through was the scarred boy, and behind him the rock came down like thunder, and the dust rose white as first snow, and far above — faint, impossibly, filtering down through a thousand feet of shifting stone — came a sound none of them had heard in the mountain before.

Birdsong. Thaw-water dripping. The ordinary, ungoverned music of a world beginning to wake up.

Wren squeezed his hand. "Almost there," she said.

Above them, daylight waited at the top of the stair — and with it, a realm that no longer had any master, and a reckoning Vael would have to face, and a Steppe where the snow was already, quietly, starting to melt.
