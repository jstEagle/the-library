## 20. Seasons Unbound

The light that met them at the mountain's mouth was wrong, and Inquisitor Vael knew it at once, the way a woman who has spent her life reading ledgers knows when a column will not sum.

It was spring — or it had been spring when they went down. Now the air above the Steppe carried winter and summer in the same breath: frost silvering the grass on one side of the trail while the other steamed green and warm as a kitchen garden. Clouds moved without pattern. A gust came down off the peaks smelling of cut hay, and before it finished passing, a second wind arrived behind it, cold enough to crack the puddles at their feet. The sky itself seemed undecided, bruise-purple in the east, pale gold in the west, neither willing to yield.

The freed souls stood blinking at it, hundreds of them, gaunt and glass-pale from years beneath the stone. Some wept. Some laughed. One old man fell to his knees in the slush and began pulling off his boots, determined, it seemed, to feel the ground with his bare feet no matter what temperature it chose to be.

Vael stood apart from all of them, as she had stood apart for twenty years of service, and watched the seasons fail.

*Fail.* No. That was the word the crown would use. The word her training had given her. But standing here with the wind changing its mind every thirty heartbeats, she found she could not make herself believe it. The seasons were not failing. They were *waking*. They moved like animals now, like weather rather than law — sudden, local, alive. A rain shower crossed the valley in a visible gray curtain, trailing rainbows through its own spray, and where it passed the frost melted and the grass shivered upright.

Ungoverned. That was the truth of it. And Vael had spent her whole career teaching the realm that ungoverned meant deadly.

She looked down at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady. But inside her chest, something sat very quietly, like a bird that had stopped singing because the cage door was open and it could not yet believe the open air.

Whitlock was somewhere behind her among the survivors. She had not spoken to him since the Court collapsed. She did not know what she would say. There was a debt between them now that had no ledger, no contract, no terms — and Vael had built her entire self out of contracts and terms, and so the debt simply sat there, unpayable, enormous, humming like a struck bell that would not fade.

"Inquisitor."

Captain Dree of her own escort — her own soldiers, men and women she had drilled and promoted and paid from the crown's purse — stood at her shoulder. His face was carefully blank, which was how soldiers' faces went when they had been given an order they hated.

"Captain."

"The dispatches came up the pass an hour ago. While you were below." He held out a sealed folio, wax stamped with the triple crown. "From Lord Chancellor Merric. For your eyes only."

Vael took it. Her gloves were torn; one fingertip showed through the leather, still gray with the dust of shattered glass. She broke the seal and read.

The words were brief. They were always brief, with Merric. The crown thanked her for her service. The crown noted that circumstances had changed. The crown instructed Inquisitor Vael to seal the mountain's exits, secure all surviving witnesses, and await a delegation empowered to negotiate a *renewal* of the seasonal compact upon terms favorable to the realm.

*Renewal.* As if the Court were a lease that had lapsed. As if there were anyone left down there to sign it. As if the souls they'd fed into the dark for three hundred years were a line item that could simply be renegotiated at better rates.

And *secure all surviving witnesses.*

She looked up. The freed souls were wandering the slope in bewildered clusters, touching the grass, the stones, each other's faces, as if confirming the world still admitted them. A young woman with a singer's throat — Wren, the one Whitlock had come for — was helping an ancient man stand. Somewhere in the crowd Silas Whitlock's gray head was bent over a child, checking her hands for frostbite with the unhurried competence of a man who had done small kindnesses his whole life.

Witnesses. Three hundred souls who had been stolen by the crown's bargain and had just watched the crown's inquisitor walk out of the pit beside them.

"They want me to seal the mountain," Vael said. Not a question. Dree had read the orders too; she saw it in his jaw.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And hold these people for the delegation."

"Yes, ma'am." He paused. His hand rested on his sword belt, not on the hilt. "Ma'am — I was at Harrow Ford. When you ordered us to stand down from the assessment wagons. You remember?"

"I remember."

"You told us the crown's law existed to protect the realm's people, and when the two came apart, we were to choose the people." He said it flatly, like a soldier reciting drill. "You made us swear it. All forty of us. On our oaths."

Vael closed the folio. The paper felt suddenly very light, and very heavy, both at once.

"I know what I told you, Captain."

"Yes, ma'am." He still hadn't touched the hilt. "So I'm asking you, plain, before any of my people have to decide anything. What are your orders? The ones from the crown—" he nodded at the folio, "—or the ones from Harrow Ford?"

The wind shifted again. Warmth rolled across the slope, and the snowmelt ran bright and quick down the trail, carrying petals — actual petals, from some orchard miles away that should not be blooming for another month — past all their boots toward the valley below.

Vael thought about the Court. About the frozen singers, mouths open on notes they had held for centuries. About the Regent's voice, smooth as poured glass: *the realm must have its seasons, Inquisitor. Someone must pay. Would you rather it were chosen poorly, or well?* She had believed it. Gods help her, she had believed it with her whole careful heart — that she was the dam holding back a flood, that every soul she assessed and shipped below was a stone in that dam, and that the weeping of the families was the price of ten million lives kept warm.

And then she had seen what the payment bought. Not warmth. Ornament. A court of glass singing to itself in the dark, gorgeous and useless, while the gods it had eaten rotted in its walls.

"We're going down to the city," Vael said.

Dree blinked. "Ma'am?"

"All of us. The witnesses, my escort, everyone who can walk. We're going to Veyra City, and I am going to stand in the Grand Square, and I am going to tell the realm exactly what its crown has been buying for three hundred years, and with whose coin." She folded the folio once, twice, precisely, the way she folded everything. "Then they can arrest whoever they like."

* * *

They came down off the mountain over four days, and the world came apart around them gently, the way ice leaves a pond.

Without the Court to hold the calendar, the seasons had gone feral — but feral like foxes, not wolves. Spring chased itself up and down the valleys. A hard frost struck the caravan one night and was gone by noon, leaving every blade of grass wearing tiny crowns of ice that flashed and died in the sun. Farmers stood in their fields staring at wheat that had ripened overnight and then, confused, unripened again by evening. In the town of Lowmark, the caravan passed a wedding held outdoors in January sunlight while the groom's breath fogged like a forge.

People were afraid. Vael saw it everywhere — in the way villagers clustered at their doors, in the way they looked at the sky as if it had become a stranger. Twenty years ago she would have known exactly what to do with that fear. She would have channeled it, aimed it, issued assessments. Fear was the crown's best harvest, and she had been its best reaper.

Now she walked at the head of a column of the formerly stolen, and the fear followed them like a dog, and she let it follow, because it was honest fear, and honesty was the only currency she had left.

The freed souls talked as they walked. That was the thing she had not anticipated — how much they talked. Three hundred people who had spent years as instruments, as fuel, as *song*, and now could not stop saying their own names aloud. Wren — the singer, the girl Whitlock had sold his godhood for — had appointed herself their cantor without asking anyone. She taught them rounds to sing on the march, simple things, name-songs, each verse built from someone's true name so that no one could be lost in the crowd. By the third day, four hundred voices were doing it badly and joyfully, and the sound rolled ahead of them down the valleys like weather of its own.

Vael rode at the rear and listened, and did not sing, and noticed that Whitlock had taken to riding near her without ever quite riding beside her. An old mercenary's trick, she supposed. Flanking. Guarding. Or perhaps simply refusing to let her be alone with whatever was happening inside her.

On the fifth day he finally spoke.

"You could run," he said. His voice was gravel and patience. "Dree would let you get to the treeline before anyone decided to care. Half those soldiers owe you their consciences. The other half owe you their careers. Either way, you'd make the coast by month's end."

"And do what, Master Whitlock? Retire?" She kept her eyes on the road. "I have one skill. It is knowing what powerful people are willing to feed to other powerful people. It seems wasteful to die without spending it."

"That's not why."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He waited. He was good at waiting; she'd seen it in the Court, the way he'd stood in front of the Regent with nothing left but a promise and refused to hurry.

"When I was nineteen," Vael said, "I assessed a woman in the fishing quarter. Gifted — a tide-caller, minor talent, the kind we used to license and leave alone before the Edict. She had three children. She asked me who would feed them." The road unspooled ahead of them, brown and green and impossibly both. "I told her the crown provides for the families of the surrendered. Which was true. The crown provided. Four marks a month and a letter of condolence written by a clerk who never met her." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the way it went when the alternative was not being able to speak at all. "I put her on the wagon. I filed the paperwork. I slept well. I had trained myself to sleep well. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"You're telling me running would be easier than the square," Whitlock said.

"I'm telling you I have been comfortable for twenty years, and comfort was the crime. Everything else was just its method." She turned and looked at him directly for the first time since the mountain. His face was seamed and tired and entirely without judgment, which was somehow worse than judgment. "You gave away a god for one girl. I don't have a god to give. All I have is the square."

* * *

Veyra City knew they were coming before they reached the gates.

Word had run ahead of them the way word does — distorted, multiplied, dressed in rumor's finery. The mountain had fallen. The seasons had broken loose. The Inquisitor had descended into hell and come back leading an army of the dead. By the time the caravan crested the last hill, the Grand Square was full, and the walls were lined, and the bells were ringing in no particular order, which had never happened in the city's history, because the bells had always been rung by schedule, and schedules, like seasons, had recently lost their keeper.

Lord Chancellor Merric had sent soldiers too. Vael counted them from the saddle: two hundred crown regulars drawn up before the palace steps, pikes leveled not at the crowd but at her column. And behind them, on the steps themselves, Merric in his chancellor's grays, flanked by the Privy Council, wearing the expression of a man performing calm for an audience of thousands.

Dree drew up beside her. "Ma'am. They'll take you the moment you dismount."

"I'm counting on it." She swung down. Her boots hit the cobbles of the Grand Square, and the sound went through the crowd like a dropped glass goes through a silent room.

She walked forward. Alone. She had considered bringing the witnesses, letting three hundred living proofs testify with their bodies, but this part — this first part — was hers alone. She had signed the assessments. She would sign the confession.

Merric's voice carried across the square, polished by decades of council chambers. "Inquisitor Vael. The crown welcomes its servant home. You are instructed to surrender your prisoners for processing and present yourself for debriefing—"

"My lords." She did not raise her voice. She had never needed to; she had learned long ago that quiet, correctly placed, carries further than shouting. She stopped ten paces from the steps, in the open ground between the pikes and the palace, and she turned slowly, so that she faced not Merrick but the square — the fishermen and the millers and the mothers, the ordinary mass of Veyra that the crown had spent three hundred years feeding to the dark one discreet wagonload at a time. "My name is Ilsette Vael. For six years I was Crown Inquisitor. Before me there were five others. Before them, others still, back to the founding. And I am here to tell you what we did."

The square went so quiet she could hear the fountain.

"There is a court beneath the Glass Steppe," she said. "It keeps the seasons. Or it kept them — until four days ago, when it was destroyed. It kept them by singing, and it sang with stolen voices. Every gifted soul the crown's edicts ever gathered — every assessment, every surrender, every family told their mother or son had gone to a sanctuary in the south — went into that mountain. Into that song. Your neighbors. Your children. Mine." Her hands stayed folded. Her voice stayed level. Only her eyes moved, from face to face, gathering them the way she had once gathered souls, except that this gathering was for the opposite purpose, and she knew the difference mattered even if it could not undo anything. "I believed it was necessary. I was told it was necessary, and I chose to believe, because believing was comfortable, and I want you to understand that comfort was the whole of my sin. The crown's founders made the bargain. The crown's chancellors renewed it. And the crown's inquisitors — I — made sure the wagons were never empty."

She drew the confession from her coat. She had written it over three nights on the road, in a fair hand, complete, with names and dates and the locations of every record cache she knew. She had addressed copies to the guildhalls, the temples, and the coastal powers. Those were already sent. This copy she held up, so the crowd could see it, so Merric could see it.

"This is the whole of it. Every record I possess. I give it freely to the realm."

"Inquisitor." Merric's voice had lost its polish. "You are under arrest for treason against the crown—"

"Yes," Vael said. "Among other things."

And then Dree did the thing she had not ordered and would remember for the rest of her life. He stepped forward, drew his sword, and drove it point-down into the cobbles between Vael and the palace steps — a soldier's gesture older than the crown, the old mark of a guard refusing an unlawful order. Behind him, one by one, with the slow inevitability of a season turning, forty soldiers of the Crown Inquisition laid their pikes in the snow-mud of the Grand Square.

Not all of them. She counted. Thirty-one of forty. Nine stood fast for the crown, faces miserable, and were disarmed gently by their former comrades, and Vael noted them with a strange tenderness — because they were right too, in their way. Oaths were oaths. The realm would need people who kept faith even when faith was misplaced. Imperfect justice, she thought. Justice outside the law, assembled from deserters and confessions and a crowd's stunned silence. It would not be clean. Nothing worth having down here was clean.

The crown guards advanced to take her. She went willingly, hands out, wrists bare. As they bound her, she looked past them at the crowd — at a fisherwoman holding a child who was too young to understand and covering the child's eyes anyway, as if to say *not this, you won't see this, not anymore* — and at the edge of the square, where a gray-haired mercenary stood with a young singer's hand on his arm, both of them watching, and the singer was crying, and the mercenary was not, and neither of them looked away.

The last thing Vael saw, as they turned her toward the cells beneath the same palace she had served, was the sky over Veyra City — half winter, half spring, the clouds running like spilled ink, the light coming down gold and cold and utterly unowned.

It was going to be the hardest year the realm had ever lived through. Crops would fail and then inexplicably flourish. Old calendars would be useless. People would curse the sky and pray to it and, eventually — she believed this, she had to believe it — learn to dress for weather instead of decree.

They took her down the steps. At the bottom, in the shadow, the arresting officer hesitated. "Inquisitor. Ma'am. Is it true? What you said?"

"Every word," Vael said. "That's what makes it treason."

The cell door closed on her, and above it, through the high barred window, a shaft of impossible April light fell across the floor of her cell and lay there, warm, moving slowly as the sun wandered off its ancient track — and Vael sat down on the cot, folded her hands, and began, methodically, to compose the testimony she would give at trial, because the realm was going to need every record, every name, every truth, and she had promised the square she would spend everything she had.

Outside, far to the north, on the open Steppe, the first thaw of the new world was beginning — and a choir was gathering that had no master, no calendar, and no permission to ask.
