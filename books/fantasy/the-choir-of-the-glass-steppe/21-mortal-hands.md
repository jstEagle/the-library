## 21. Mortal Hands

The infirmary had been a granary once, back when the Hollow Choir's refuge above the Steppe was a working farmstead. Now the grain bins held blankets instead of barley, and the long stone troughs where wheat had been winnowed were lined with straw and pallets, and the people in them shivered even under three quilts apiece.

Silas carried another armload of firewood through the door and paused, as he did every time now, to feel the absence of what he used to do next.

There had been a charm for this. A small one — a hearth-blessing his god had taught him in the first years, when the god still spoke. You laid your palm against the cold iron of a stove and whispered the old word, *vess*, which meant both *warmth* and *welcome* depending on how you bent it, and the iron would drink the flame's heat deep into itself and give it back slow and even all night. He had done it a thousand times in a thousand hired rooms. It was the cheapest magic he'd ever sold, the charm he gave away free to widows and night-watchmen who couldn't pay.

He set the wood down by the stove, knelt, and built the fire with his hands. Kindling first, then split logs, then the match struck on the box with a smell of sulfur that made one of the children near the window flinch. He fed the flames patiently, the ordinary way, the way any farmhand or fishwife would have done it, and when the stove began to tick and breathe he sat back on his heels and let the warmth find him.

It took an hour to do what *vess* had done in a breath.

He found, examining himself the way you'd probe a bruise, that he didn't mind. That was the strange thing. Twenty years he had grieved his silence like a man grieving a severed limb, phantom-aching for a hand that wasn't there. But this was different. This was not a limb missing. This was a limb he had cut off himself, willingly, at the altar of the Glass Court, and the wound was clean, and it was *his*.

His hands were just hands now. Scarred knuckles, a burn scar across the left palm from a job in Corvess when he was twenty-three, dirt in the creases no matter how he scrubbed. Mortal hands. They could carry wood. They could hold a bowl of broth steady while a shaking girl drank from it. They could not make warmth out of a word.

They could sit beside someone until warmth came some other way.

* * *

The freed came up from the mountain in ones and twos and then in dozens, blinking in daylight they hadn't seen in months or years, some of them since before the Purge Edict. The Court had kept them singing so long that most of them had forgotten their own names; Wren spent her days going pallet to pallet, singing each captive's name back to them like returning something borrowed, watching recognition break over their faces like ice going out of a river.

Silas handled the rest. The eating, mostly. The sleeping. The weeping, when it came, which was often and without warning and always at strange hours — a woman named Odile who had been in the choir eleven years would stop mid-sentence and stare at her own hands and begin to shake, and Silas learned not to ask what she saw in them but simply to sit down across from her and wait, and eventually she would look up and see him there and remember where she was, and the shaking would slow.

"You were in the choir," she said to him once, hoarsely. "I saw you. Near the east panes. You were singing."

"For about an hour," Silas said. "Then I broke the place."

Odile laughed — a cracked, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter — and then she cried, and he sat with her through that too, holding a cup of water she never drank, because that was the whole of what he had to offer anyone anymore: presence. Attendance. The stubborn refusal to be elsewhere.

Mother Ash found him at dusk, hauling the day's wash-water, and watched him work with an expression he couldn't read.

"You know," she said, "there are those among us saying you should be leading. What you did down there—"

"What I did down there," Silas said, setting the yoke down and flexing his aching shoulders, "was die. I'm told I got better. Mostly." He nodded toward the scar tissue that wrapped his ribs where the divinity had torn loose coming out of him. "But whatever I was, I spent it. There's nothing left to lead with. Ask me to haul water and I'm your man."

"That's exactly why they want you," Mother Ash said quietly. "You gave everything away. People trust a man like that more than they trust a miracle."

"Then people are fools."

"They usually are," she agreed, and left him to his water.

* * *

Wren found him after midnight, as she often did, sitting on the granary steps with a blanket around his shoulders, watching the frost form on the fence rails. The seasons were drifting now — everyone said so. Spring had come late and ragged, summer threatening to arrive all at once in a great ungoverned rush, and the farmers at the edge of the Steppe muttered about their planting and cast anxious eyes at skies that answered to no one. But tonight was cold and clear, and the stars were hard and bright, and Silas thought: *no one is keeping this sky turning. It keeps itself.*

"You're doing it again," Wren said, sitting down beside him. She pulled the blanket half off his shoulders and half onto hers, the way she'd done since she was nine years old and he'd carried her out of a burning waystation. "Sitting out here counting things you can't do anymore."

"I was doing no such thing."

"You were. You get a particular line between your eyebrows." She reached up and pressed it flat with one finger, an impertinence she'd been committing since childhood and showed no sign of abandoning. "Listen. Odile sang today. Actually sang — a harvest round, the one about the threshing floor. Her voice cracked in the middle and she kept going anyway. That's because you sat with her for six days."

"That's because she's strong."

"It's because you're *there*," Wren said. "Every hour. Every pallet. You think the charms were the work? Silas. The charms were never the work. I sang you back from the dead when I was seven years old and I couldn't have done a single spell. I just refused to let you go. That's all magic ever was underneath — attention. Caring where it hurts. Everything else was plumbing."

Silas looked at her. In the starlight her face was young and old at once, marked by the Court the way a riverbed is marked by floodwater, and there was a thread of glass-fine scarring at her throat where the choir's binding had gripped her, and she was alive, and she was here, and twenty years of silence and two decades of grief and one act of final sacrifice had bought exactly this: a girl on a step, stealing his blanket, telling him he'd been wrong about what mattered.

"The Regent offered me my godhood back," he said. He'd never told her that plainly. "Everything I lost. All of it, if I'd brought them souls."

"I know. Vael told us. During the trial." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You chose a girl with a ribbon over being a god. Best bargain anyone ever struck under that mountain."

"I don't feel peaceful about it," he admitted. "Some days I reach for it. The old word. *Vess.* And there's nothing there, and it's like falling."

"Then fall," Wren said simply. "I'll be at the bottom. I'm always at the bottom. It's practically my defining feature."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time since the mountain — and the sound went up into the unowned sky and nobody governed it, and it came back down again just the same.

* * *

In the morning there was bread to bake and bandages to boil and a boy of perhaps twelve, newly surfaced, who screamed himself hoarse at the sight of enclosed doors and could only be coaxed inside by leaving every shutter open, and Silas coaxed him, and sat with him, and taught him to whittle, because busy mortal hands leave less room for terror than idle gifted ones.

And in the afternoon a rider came up the farm road, mud to the stirrups, wearing no colors Silas recognized, and asked at the gate for "the man who broke the Glass Court," and when Silas presented himself the rider handed over a sealed letter bearing a wax stamp Silas knew too well — the crown of Veyra — and said only:

"You're summoned. The Queen's soldiers are marching on the Steppe within the fortnight. And there are those at court," the rider added, glancing back the way he'd come, "who say the seasons are broken, and know whose fault it is, and are looking for someone to hang for it."

Silas broke the seal with his thumb — his plain, mortal, unmagical thumb — and read, and his face must have changed, because Wren appeared at his elbow before he'd finished the second line.

"What is it?"

He turned the page toward her. At the bottom, beneath the crown's formal summons, someone had written a single line in a small, precise, familiar hand — the hand of a woman who had once held the exit open rather than seal it:

*They mean to make you the scapegoat. Come armed with truth, not steel. — V.*

Wren read it twice. Then she looked up at him, and there was frost and fire in her expression together, and she said, "Then we'd better start teaching them to listen. Get the choir ready."
