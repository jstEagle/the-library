## 22. The Choir of the Open Air

The thaw came late that year, and it came all at once.

Silas felt it before he saw it — a loosening in the air above the Steppe, the way a fist unclenches in sleep. For three days the wind had carried the smell of wet stone and green things deciding to be green again, and on the fourth morning he woke in the loft above Mother Ash's rebuilt safehouse to birdsong that had no magic in it at all. Just birds. Just morning. He lay still for a long time and listened, because there had been years — twenty of them, then one more beneath the mountain — when he would have given anything to hear something sing without being asked.

He was fifty-one now, or near enough. His hands had gone knuckled and brown like an old dockworker's, which was fitting, since much of his work these days was hauling: timber for the new roofs, sacks of grain for the freed who could not yet feed themselves, children who had forgotten how to walk under open sky and needed carrying until they remembered. His charms were gone. All of them. The luck-knots, the warding-threads, the little warmth-that-clings he used to sell for two coppers in the smuggler's quarter — spent, or shattered, or given away in a single white instant under the Glass Court when he had broken his own divinity like bread and fed it to every soul in that frozen cathedral.

Some mornings he missed it the way you miss a limb. Some mornings he reached for the old reflexes — the flicker of charm-light at his fingertips when the dark got close — and found only skin, only callus, only a man. And some mornings, like this one, he woke with his heart going easy and slow and thought: *this is what it cost, and this is what it bought,* and both halves of the sentence were true, and neither one canceled the other out.

Downstairs, the house was already awake. It was always awake now. They had come back to Veyra's capital in ones and twos through the winter — the freed, the frightened, the ones who had hidden so long they'd forgotten their own names and had to be reminded by people who loved them. Mother Ash ran the place like a harbor in a storm, which was what she had always done; she had simply stopped pretending it was something smaller. Her hair had gone fully white since the collapse, and she wore it loose now, which Silas suspected was her version of a proclamation.

"You're up," she said, not looking up from the kettle. "Good. Wren wants everyone on the Steppe by midmorning."

"Everyone?"

"Everyone." She poured him tea without asking, the way she'd been doing since he'd come back half-dead and wholly mortal. "She says the ground's ready. She says—" and here Mother Ash paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been amusement or might have been awe, "—she says the Steppe itself is ready. That it's been waiting to be sung to properly."

* * *

The Glass Steppe in early thaw was a country made of light.

The frost that had locked the grasslands since autumn was letting go in long silver seams, and where it released, the old glass showed through — the vast sheets of fused sand that gave the region its name, buried for centuries under soil and superstition, now emerging into daylight like the bones of something enormous surfacing after a long dive. When the sun hit them they threw color everywhere: green-gold, river-blue, the pale violet of dusk even at noon. Children of the freed walked out onto the panes and looked down at their own reflections floating ten feet beneath their feet, and screamed with delight, and were not told to hush.

There had been a time — most of Silas's life — when standing openly on the Steppe as a marked man would have meant the pyre. There had been a worse time, only months ago, when standing here at all meant the mountain. Now the inquisitors' black wagons sat abandoned in a ditch outside the city, rusting into the landscape, and nobody had bothered to burn them because burning things had briefly lost all fashion.

Vael's trial had ended in February. Silas had not attended. He'd heard the accounts secondhand: how the Crown Inquisitor had stood before her own soldiers and read the original bargain aloud — the founders' contract, the souls owed, the seasons purchased in blood and voice — in a clear unhurried voice, declining every opportunity to soften it. How she had named each soul she'd delivered, all four hundred and eleven, from memory, because of course she had kept records; she had believed in her work. How the soldiers who arrested her had wept while doing it, and how she had thanked them for their courtesy. She was in the deep cells now, the ones without windows, and Silas did not know if that was justice or merely another kind of choir, and he suspected the answer would take a generation to arrive. But she had held the exit open. Whatever else was true of Inquisitor Vael, when the Glass Court came down around them and every instinct of her office screamed to seal the door and preserve the realm's order, she had planted herself in the collapsing passage and held it open with her own two hands until the last freed soul passed through. You could spend a lifetime arguing about a person like that. Silas chose, most days, simply to remember her hands.

He thought about her sometimes on the walk out to the panes. Mostly, though, he thought about nothing at all, which was new, and took getting used to, and turned out to be its own small luxury.

* * *

They gathered where the first great pane met the grass — perhaps three hundred of them, maybe more, come out from the city and the hidden farmsteads and the far villages where gifted folk had lived bent double under the Edict for a decade. Silas stood at the edge of the gathering, as he always did, holding a thermos of tea he didn't drink and a scarf someone had knitted him that he didn't need but wore anyway. A woman with a scarred throat stood beside him, one of the ones who had been longest in the choir; she had not spoken above a whisper since her rescue, but today she had come, and today she stood in full sun, and that was already more song than the crown had permitted her in six years.

Wren stepped up onto the glass.

She was twenty now, though she carried it strangely — older in the eyes, younger in the spine, as if the mountain had taken some years and returned others. Her hair was short these days; she'd cut it after the descent, saying she wanted nothing left that anyone could grab. She wore plain wool and no ribbon, having retired the old one to a box under her bed where Silas pretended not to know it lived. When she turned to face the crowd, the light off the pane came up through her and lit her from below, so that for a moment she seemed to stand inside her own halo.

She didn't announce anything. She never did. She just lifted her chin, breathed the way singers breathe — from the floor of herself — and began.

It was not a grand piece. That was the first thing Silas noticed, and the thing that undid him. It was a working song, a threshing-floor tune, the kind farmhands had once sung over the harvest before singing over anything had become a crime. Simple melody, plain words about wheat and weather and coming home. But she sang it *openly* — no muffling cloth, no cellar acoustics, no borrowed cover of a concert or a funeral — and she sang it *with power*, and the power went down into the glass like water into cracked earth.

The pane lit.

Not the thin reflected shimmer of sunlight — a true light, rising from within, spreading outward from under Wren's feet in slow rings. And where it spread, the frost let go. Not melting, exactly. *Releasing.* Ice that had gripped the Steppe since the Court fell — ice that had clung on out of habit, out of grief, out of the simple momentum of a thousand years of enforced winter — heard the old threshing song and remembered that it, too, was allowed to change. It slid free in whole translucent sheets and caught the sun and burned there like scattered jewels, and beneath it the first green of the year stood up blinking.

Then the others joined her.

That was the part Silas had not been warned for. He had expected a performance; instead it was a contagion. A tenor from the Hollow Choir picked up the harmony line, then the scarred-throated woman beside Silas opened her mouth and — haltingly, in a rasp that broke and healed and broke again — joined on the third. Then a child. Then a dozen children. Then Mother Ash, whose voice was smoke and gravel and utterly certain. Three hundred voices, none of them trained to blend, all of them singing *together on purpose*, and the magic that rose off the gathering wasn't the polished, dangerous, devourable thing the Court had farmed — it was rough, and human, and various, and it could not be swallowed because it did not want to be anything other than exactly what it was.

The light rolled across the Steppe like a tide going out in reverse. Miles away, Silas knew, farmers would look up from their fields and see the horizon glowing and feel — he hoped — not fear. Or fear, yes, but fear with a door in it. Fear you could walk through.

And the seasons — the seasons *drifted*. That was the price, and everyone knew it now, and most days Silas thought it was the best bargain the realm had ever struck. No court beneath the mountain turning winter like a crank. No calendar guaranteed by stolen throats. Spring would come when spring came, early or late, generous or stingy, alive and ungoverned, and the people of Veyra would have to meet it the way people meet weather: with attention, with humility, with granaries filled against the lean years and songs sung for luck rather than for law. Some nights the frost came back out of season and took a field or an orchard, and the villages mourned, and then — this was the miracle, the quiet daily miracle — they planted again, together, without checking whether the planting was licensed.

Wren's voice climbed. The crowd's rose with it. On the far edge of the pane the last great sheet of winter ice groaned, tilted, and let go all at once, and the sound it made was like a bell the size of a cathedral finally being allowed to finish ringing.

* * *

Afterward, the gathering dissolved into the ordinary business of freedom: shared bread, arguments about seed, a fiddler tuning up with the shameless confidence of a man who has decided no one will ever arrest him for it again. Silas drifted along the edge of it, doing what he did now, which was nothing that required magic and everything that required hands. He righted a tipped cart. He found a lost mitten and its equally lost owner. He sat with an old ferryman's widow — the ferryman himself had dissolved with the Court, paid out at last into whatever waits past waiting — and let her talk about her husband until the talking became enough.

He was aware, distantly, that people knew who he was. Word traveled; it always had. The man who broke the Court. The mercenary who spent his god. He saw it in the way strangers glanced at him and then, catching his expression, glanced away with something like tact. He did not correct them and did not confirm anything. The truth was stranger than the stories anyway: that his god had never been silent, only stolen; that the face he'd sold to the ferryman of the dead had been his own; that divinity, it turned out, was not a throne but a seed, and seeds are meant to be planted, and planting means the seed stops being a seed. He had been somebody's god once, or the beginning of one. Now he was a man with strong hands and a good coat and tea going cold in a thermos, and when he took inventory — and he took inventory often, the way a man checks a wound to make sure it's healing — he found no regret large enough to name.

"Wren's looking for you," said Mother Ash, materializing at his elbow with the timing she probably called coincidence and Silas privately suspected was a minor charm she'd never surrendered.

"She knows where I am."

"She knows where you *were*. You've moved twice since dawn. You do that." Ash studied him with her old flat appraising stare, softened now at the edges the way everything was softened, a little. "You know she's going to ask you to sing with them next time. Properly. In the circle, not the edge."

"I don't sing."

"You hum. I've heard you. Terribly. Which is why it'll be good for you." She patted his arm with a hand like a bundle of kindling and left him to his tea.

He stayed on the pane a while after the crowd thinned. The light underfoot was fading back to ordinary reflection, but the glass remembered — he was sure of it now. Everything here remembered. Every soul that had ever been pulled screaming down the ossuary stair, every god devoured to keep the winters punctual, every note Wren had bent out of shape in the dark to unbind her fellow captives one at a time — it was all in the glass somewhere, the way heat stays in stone. Not haunting. Just *kept*. The Steppe had been a prison and a furnace and a larder for a thousand years, and now, slowly, pane by pane, song by song, it was becoming an archive of the opposite: proof that voices taken by force can be returned by choice.

His own god's remnant had gone up with the rest. He had felt it leave him — or return to him, or simply end; the theology was beyond him and he had stopped needing it to resolve. What he remembered was the moment of shattering: the whole buried cathedral of glass failing at once, four hundred souls rising through the breaking dark like sparks up a chimney, and at the center of it all a presence he had mourned for twenty years unfolding its wings one final time — his own face, his own eyes, looking at him with what he could only call gratitude — and then choosing, as he chose, to spend itself rather than reign. Two sacrifices, one act. After that: silence, but a different silence. The silence of a room after the guests have gone home happy.

Footsteps on the glass, light and familiar.

Wren sat down beside him without ceremony, the way she had since she was seven years old and had dragged a dying mercenary back from the brink with a song she had no business knowing. For a while neither of them spoke. Below their feet, faint as breath, their reflections floated side by side in the blue-green depth of the pane.

"You cried," she said at last. "During the third verse. Don't argue. Ash saw you."

"Ash sees everything. It's unsettling."

"Mm." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder — a debt repaid in the smallest coin, endlessly renewable. "Next month. Full moon. We're singing the whole Steppe clear, corner to corner. Everyone. Even you."

"I told you. I don't—"

"You'll hum," she said, in the tone of a woman who had once sung a grown man back into his body and had not yet encountered a problem that tone couldn't solve. "Terribly. It'll be perfect."

Far out across the panes, the sun was going down in a spill of copper and rose, and the glass took the light and held it and gave it back doubled, and somewhere behind them the fiddler started up a reel, and the children screamed with delight at nothing, at everything, at being alive and unfrozen and unowned. Silas Whitlock — unnamed in no ledger now, unimportant in every history, content beyond the reach of any charm he had ever sold — sat on the warm glass with the person he had sworn to protect and had, at the last and greatest cost, actually protected, and watched the first true evening of the first true spring settle over the Steppe like a blessing no one had to die for.

Under his feet, very softly, the glass began — he would have sworn it, though no one ever asked him to — to hum.
