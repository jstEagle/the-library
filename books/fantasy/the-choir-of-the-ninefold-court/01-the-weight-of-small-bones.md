## 1. The Weight of Small Bones

The dead man's name was Aldous Merrin, and he would not stop complaining about the roof.

Sable Galloway heard him the moment the knife touched the bone—a sparrow's femur, pale and clean, no bigger than a thumbnail. The voice came up through the steel and into his knuckles like cold water through a well rope: *the tiles were loose on the north slope, I told her, I told her a dozen times, and now she'll let it rot because she never listened when I was—*

He tuned it out the way a ferryman tunes out the river. Not ignoring it. Respecting it. You did not argue with water, and you did not argue with the dead; you simply kept your hands steady and your ears polite.

The workshop above the tannery was small enough that Sable could touch both walls if he stretched, and he liked it that way. Small rooms held sound close. One window, north-facing, so the light stayed gray and even all day and his eyes never had to adjust. A pegboard of knives sorted by fineness, from the heavy splitting blade down to the needle-graver he used for work finer than prayer. A dish of bones in their bath of lye-water, soaking soft. And in the corner, wrapped in oilcloth where no visitor would ever think to look, the ledgers of the Vellum Guild—because Sable Galloway was two things, and only one of them could be seen.

The widow Merrin sat across the workbench with her hands folded like a woman at graveside, which in a sense she was. She was perhaps fifty, round-faced, with the particular redness around the eyes that meant she had cried herself empty days ago and was now running on something harder than grief. She watched his knife move over the little bone and said nothing, because he had asked her for silence—not rudely, but the way a physician asks a patient to hold still.

*—and the gutter, the gutter on the kitchen side, she'll never clear it herself, her brother won't come, he never came when I was alive either, why would he—*

"Your husband," Sable said quietly, without looking up, "wants you to know about the roof."

The widow's breath caught. It always caught. He had learned to give people a moment for it, the way you gave bread time to rise.

"The north slope," she whispered. "He was always after me about the north slope."

"He says the tiles are loose. He says—" Sable tilted his head, listening with an attentiveness he had practiced until it looked like nothing at all, just a craftsman concentrating. *Tell her about the gutter too, tell her, she'll forget, she forgets everything I say.* "And the gutter on the kitchen side. He says your brother should be the one to clear it."

"My brother." Her mouth twisted. "Aldous never trusted Tam."

*I didn't trust him,* the dead man agreed, aggrieved, from inside the sparrow's leg. *I don't trust him dead, either. Tell her to count the silver before he leaves the house.*

"I wouldn't put much weight on anything else he says," Sable offered, and the widow laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one, the first he'd heard from her. The dead man made a sound of wounded dignity that Sable chose not to translate.

This was the trade, then. This was what he did, three mornings a week, in a room that smelled faintly of tallow and lime: he carved mourning charms, and the dead spoke through them, and he passed their last nagging loves along to the living like a courier who never got to rest. A charm of remembrance, the guild called it in its price lists. In the lanes they called it a whisper-bone, and paid in copper, and told no one they'd bought one, because telling anyone was how you ended up on a list.

There were lists now. That was new. That was the weather coming.

But that morning, six days before the criers, the weather had not yet arrived, and Sable worked with the window open to the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and pretended the world was still the size of this room.

The sparrow's femur took shape under his graver. He cut the old signs into it—the knot that meant *remembered*, the open line that meant *still speaking*, the small closed circle at the end that meant *at peace*, though he left that one unclosed for now, because Aldous Merrin was very far from peace. Around the middle he carved the widow's initial and, beneath it, hidden in the grain where only another carver would ever find it, his own mark: a single vertical stroke crossed by three horizontal ones, small as a splinter. His father's mark. The only inheritance worth the name.

"You can talk to him," the widow said suddenly. "Can't you. Properly. Not just—reading the carving."

Sable's knife did not pause. He had rehearsed this answer the way other men rehearsed alibis, and he delivered it with the same flat warmth. "The bone holds what he gave it. Grief leaves an impression, mistress, the way a boot leaves a print in wax. I read the print. That's all."

"That's not what my mother said your kind could do."

"Your mother," Sable said, "was generous."

She studied him for a long moment. He kept his face in the place he kept it during inspections: pleasant, slightly tired, entirely unremarkable. A thin man of thirty-odd years with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone accustomed to being overlooked. He had spent years building that face the way he built charms—one careful cut at a time—and he resented, mildly and perpetually, how well it worked.

"She said there was a carver on Tanner's Row," the widow said, "who could hear them plain as parish bells. But that he'd deny it to your face, because he's careful."

*Aldous,* Sable thought wearily, *your wife is sharper than your roof.*

"Mistress," he said, "if I could hear the dead, do you imagine I'd be living above a tannery?"

That landed as he intended it to—as a joke with a sad bottom to it—and she smiled despite herself, and let it go, and he felt the familiar small shame of a lie well-told. It never stopped feeling like that. Ten years of hiding the gift and the shame had not dulled; it had simply gotten better at keeping quiet, the way he had.

*Ask her if she's eating,* Aldous said suddenly, and his voice had changed—less peevish, more careful, the way men got when they were embarrassed by tenderness. *She goes thin when she's alone. Ask her.*

Sable set down the graver. He blew bone dust from the half-finished charm and held it up to the gray light, turning it, buying himself the pause.

"He wants to know if you're eating," he said.

The widow's composure went all at once, like ice off a branch in spring. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "That stubborn old—" Her voice broke. "Yes. Yes, I'm eating. Toast. Mostly toast."

*Toast,* Aldous repeated, disgusted. *Tell her eggs. Tell her Mrs. Callow next door keeps hens and owes me for the fence.*

"He says Mrs. Callow keeps hens," Sable relayed, "and owes him for a fence."

The widow laughed and sobbed in the same breath, and Sable looked back down at his work and let her have the moment privately, because that was part of the craft too. You did not watch people grieve directly. You gave them the courtesy of a lowered gaze, the way you gave the dead the courtesy of patience. He finished the closing circle on the charm—*at peace*, or near enough—and bound it with a thread of red wool, and warmed it briefly in his palm, and felt the voice inside settle from a complaint into a hum. Not gone. Just quieter. Held.

"There," he said, and slid it across the bench. "Wear it against the skin. When you want to feel him near, hold it and speak his name. He'll hear you. Whether I hear him afterward is—" he shrugged, delicately, "—a matter of the bone."

It was, in fact, a matter of Sable. The charm would carry Aldous's voice for years, fading slowly, and any carver with the gift could listen in. Sable had no intention of listening in. He had a drawer full of finished charms whose residents he checked on maybe once a season, out of duty, the way a man visits graves. The dead deserved their rest even if they complained about wanting it.

The widow paid him eleven coppers, counted twice, and then a twelfth she pushed across separately. "For the eggs," she said, daring him with her chin to refuse it.

He took it. Refusing offended people worse than taking did. "Mrs. Callow's fence," he said gravely, and she laughed again, wetter but easier, and let herself out down the stairs with the little bone fisted in her hand like a talisman, which is exactly what it was.

*Thank you,* Aldous said, from the drawer where Sable had already filed the day's record. The dead rarely thanked him. It sat strangely in Sable's chest, warm and uncomfortable, like a coal swallowed whole. *She'll be all right?*

"She'll be all right," Sable murmured to the empty room, locking the drawer. "They usually are. It's the ones who aren't that worry me."

* * *

The Vellum Guild occupied a narrow building on Quill Street that was wider inside than outside, which was the least impossible thing about it. From the street it looked like a stationer's shop that had given up on ambition: dusty panes, a hand-painted sign of a goose feather, a door that stuck in damp weather. Inside, past the public counter where clerks brokered contracts for the merely strange, a stair went down farther than the building had depth, and the air changed, and the trades began.

Sable had worked the lower floors for nine years. He kept the ledgers—actual vellum, actual ink, entries in his own cramped hand—and the guild trusted him with them precisely because he seemed to have no gift at all. A bone-carver with a deft hand and no ear, that was Sable Galloway. Useful, discreet, unremarkable. The perfect man to write down what the gifted traded without ever understanding it.

He understood every word of it. That was the joke he kept to himself, alone, forever.

That afternoon the ledger wanted three entries.

The first was simple enough: Master Ellery-Voss had sold a bottle of laughter to a merchant from the coast towns. The laughter had been harvested—from whom, the entry did not say, and Sable did not ask, because asking questions was Odile's business and Odile asked enough for everyone. He recorded the volume (one dram), the vintage (*a child's, unsoured*), and the price, and noted in the margin that the seal was intact and the buyer had wept upon receiving it, which was common, and none of his affair.

The second was less simple. A door to nowhere. It stood in the vault's third gallery, a plain farmhouse door in its frame, freestanding, that opened onto a room of gray mist that went on farther than any room should. People used it to lose things they couldn't bear to keep. The guild charged storage rates. Sable wrote the transfer of custody—some minor noble paying to lose a portrait of a father he hated—and tried not to think about what the mist did with everything it was given, or whether somewhere in that endless gray there was a room stacked floor to ceiling with portraits and letters and wedding rings, all of it waiting, all of it remembered by nothing.

The third entry stopped his pen.

"A saint's knucklebone," said the clerk who brought it down, a nervous young man called Fetter with ink permanently on his lip. "Authenticated. Third phalanx, right index. Saint Vessamine, the Listener. It's to go in the deep vault, and it's to go in under your hand only, Master Galloway. Madam's orders."

"Why my hand?"

Fetter blinked. "Because you don't ask questions, master. Madam says it makes you honest."

*Odile,* Sable thought, *you are a liar and I love you, and one day those two facts are going to grind each other to dust.*

He took the reliquary box up in both hands. It was heavier than it should have been, the way certain holy things were, and through the cedar and the lead lining he felt—faintly, distantly, the way you hear a bell from three streets over—a voice. Not speaking. Just present. A single note held so long it had become a kind of silence.

Saint Vessamine, the Listener. The one saint, if the stories held any truth, who had heard the dead the way Sable heard them—plainly, constantly, without relief. They said she had gone deaf at the end of her life and died smiling.

Sable carried her knucklebone down to the deep vault, logged it, shelved it, and stood a moment in the dark among the guild's impossible inventory, listening to that distant held note, feeling an emotion he refused to name because naming it would make it true.

It felt like kinship. It felt like a warning.

* * *

Odile Vess found him at dusk, as she always did, materializing in the doorway of the ledger room with a lamp she didn't need—she knew these stairs blind—and a bottle she did. She was sixty and built like a folded letter, all sharp creases, with white hair cropped short and eyes the color of tarnished silver. Fifty years in the guild. Founder's own apprentice, the story went. Keeper of more secrets than the vault had shelves.

"You gave the Merrin woman her charm this morning," she said, setting the bottle on his desk without asking. Two cups appeared from her sleeves, because Odile produced things from her sleeves the way other women produced opinions.

"I did."

"And told her you couldn't hear him."

"I told her what keeps us both safe."

Odile poured. The wine was cheap and dark and she drank it like it owed her money. "Mm." She looked at him over the rim of her cup with that gaze that made junior clerks confess to crimes they hadn't committed. "You know what I admire about you, Sable?"

"My discretion."

"Your cowardice," she said fondly. "It's the same thing, done well." She set down the cup. "You hear a dead man complain about gutters, and you pass it along like a good little courier, and you never once ask the question."

"What question?"

"The question you've been not-asking for nine years." She leaned forward. "Why does it work, Sable? What is it in the bone that remembers? Where do they go when the charm finally crumbles?" Her voice dropped, and the lamplight made her look, for once, her age. "You sit at the center of the largest market in impossible things on this coast, and you ask fewer questions than my laundress. Either you're a fool—and we both know you're not—or you're afraid of the answers."

Sable turned his cup slowly on the desk. Outside, the city was settling into evening; he could hear the ordinary sounds of it through the wall, cart wheels, a dog, someone's supper argument, the vast indifferent noise of the living.

"If I start asking," he said finally, "I won't stop. And a man who asks questions like that stops being invisible. And invisible is the only way I stay alive."

"Invisible." Odile snorted. "Boy, I've watched you spend ten years making yourself smaller. You're not invisible. You're *unnoticed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that unnoticed people get noticed eventually, usually by the wrong person, usually at the worst time." She drained her cup and stood, gathering the bottle, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "The world's changing. Can you feel it? Even the dead are quieter lately. Like birds before a storm."

"They're always quiet around you. You terrify them."

For a moment she almost smiled. Then it faded, and she looked at him with an expression he'd never seen her wear before—something old and tired and grimly affectionate, a woman looking at a younger version of a mistake she'd already made.

"When I tell you to burn something," Odile Vess said, "burn it. No questions. Promise me that much, at least."

"It depends what it is."

"No," she said. "It doesn't. That's rather the point."

She left him with the lamp and the ledger and the taste of cheap wine, and Sable sat a long while in the gray room above the tannery, listening to the ordinary evening, and did not sleep well.

In the deep vault below the guild, in a cedar box lined with lead, a saint's knucklebone held its single long note. And three galleries over, in a crate that had not been opened in nine years—in a crate Sable himself had packed, sealed, and shelved, and had not touched since, and dreamed about more often than he admitted—something older than saints waited with nine voices, and began, very softly, to sing.
