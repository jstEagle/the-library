## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The Vellum Guild opened its doors at the seventh bell, and by half past, someone had already tried to sell us a storm.

Sable Galloway watched from behind the counting desk as the man set a stoppered glass jar on the marble and stepped back with the particular pride of a merchant who knows his goods are beyond price. The jar was ordinary—green glass, chipped at the shoulder, cork sealed with black wax—but what moved inside it was not. It shifted like smoke that had opinions, pressing against the glass in slow coils, and when the morning light from the high windows caught it, Sable could see the shape of rain in it, the grey suggestion of a sky about to break.

"Harvested it off the Saltreach moors," the man said. "Storm that's been circling the same valley for eleven years. Never lands. Never breaks. Just *waits*." He tapped the glass, and the smoke inside flinched away from his finger like a scolded animal. "Caught what was left when it finally got tired."

"And what does it do?" asked Odile Vess, from her chair by the hearth.

She never rose for clients anymore. Her knees had gone the way of her patience, which was to say: entirely. She sat wrapped in a shawl the color of old wine, hands folded over a cane of blackthorn, and she looked at sellers the way a magistrate looks at liars—with interest, but no illusions.

"It waits," the man said again, as if this were a virtue.

"Everything waits," Odile said. "That's hardly a selling point. What does it do when it stops waiting?"

The man hesitated. Sable saw the hesitation for what it was—the small honest pause of a man who has never actually opened the jar—and he bent his head over the ledger and began to write.

*One (1) bottled storm, provenance Saltreach moors, seller claims eleven-year accumulation. Seller uncertain of contents' disposition.*

That was the trick of the ledger, the thing Odile had taught him in his first year, when his handwriting still shook: write down what is claimed, mark what is unproven, and never—*never*—let your own wonder bleed into the ink. The Vellum Guild did not deal in truth. It dealt in *transactions*, and a transaction only needed two parties willing to agree on a price.

"The Guild offers forty crowns," Sable said, without looking up.

The man made a wounded noise. "Forty! I've been offered sixty in Lowmarket—"

"Then sell it in Lowmarket." Sable turned the page. "They'll also sell you a bridge, afterward, and you'll need somewhere to keep both."

There was a silence, and then the man laughed—a short, defeated bark—and said, "Forty-five."

"Forty-two, and we don't ask where you found it."

Odile's eyes flicked toward him at that. He felt the look land between his shoulder blades like a coin dropped down his collar. But the man was already nodding, already spitting into his palm and holding it out, and there were oaths to administer and wax to melt and a line of other sellers forming behind him out in the fog-wet street, so Sable let the look go unexamined, the way he let most things go unexamined. It was easier that way. It had always been easier that way.

By the ninth bell they had bought the storm, a length of rope that untied itself when its owner lied (seller: a hangman, retiring), and a small tin whistle that played whatever song the listener most missed. Sable recorded each one in the great ledger in his careful, narrow hand, and each entry went into the vault below with a tag and a shelf and a place in the vast, patient catalog of things the world had produced that it had no business producing.

This was the work. This was the comfort of it. The world outside the Guild's green door was a city of ten thousand secrets, but inside these walls everything had a number, a shelf, a price. Sable had spent six years learning that a life could be built out of categories. You put each dangerous thing in its box, you labeled the box, and you did not open it after dark.

He was very good at not opening things after dark.

* * *

The saint's knucklebone came in just before noon, carried by a nun who was either genuinely devout or genuinely the best liar Sable had ever seen, and he honestly could not decide which, which was itself a kind of professional admiration.

It was a small thing, yellowed with age, no bigger than a thumbnail. She laid it on a square of white linen on the counter as though laying down a sleeping infant, and folded her hands, and waited.

"Saint Elowen," she said. "Third knuckle of the right index finger. The finger she used to point the way out of the burning hospital." The nun's voice was steady, rehearsed, the cadence of a story told so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. "It has healed four fevers, two cases of the red pox, and one crisis of faith. I have letters."

"We don't require letters," Sable said, and reached for it.

And stopped.

His hand hung in the air above the bone, fingers curled, and for a moment—one long, cold moment—he stood on the edge of something he could not name. Because here was the trouble with his particular affliction, the gift he had spent his whole adult life folding up small enough to hide: relics spoke to him. Not all of them, and not loudly, but bone especially—bone remembered. A saint's knucklebone, steeped in centuries of prayer and desperation, would be *loud*. It would have a voice like a crowded room. And if he touched it here, in the open, with the nun watching and two porters stacking crates by the door, his face might do something his face should not do.

So he performed the ritual every carver learned and no carver admitted to: he picked it up with a lens-cloth, held it to the light, weighed it in his palm through the fabric, and set it back down.

"Genuine," he said. "Older than the church claims, if anything. The Guild offers three hundred crowns."

The nun blinked. "Three hundred? The diocese offered—"

"The diocese wants to bury it under an altar where no one can check whether it works." He was already writing. "We want to sell it to someone who'll swear by it. Different markets. Three hundred, plus five percent if it heals a fifth fever within the year."

"That's—" The nun pressed her lips together, fighting a smile and losing. "That's very modern of you."

"We're a modern institution," Sable said gravely. "We have a filing system."

She signed. She took her coins. She left, and the bell above the door sang its little brass note, and Sable sat looking at the knucklebone on its linen square and felt the silence around it the way you feel a missing tooth.

Because it hadn't spoken. Of course it hadn't—he'd never truly touched it. But even through the cloth, even at a hand's distance, there should have been *something*. A hum. A warmth. Bone that had worked miracles for four hundred years should have radiated presence like a stove radiates heat, and instead it lay there on the linen as quiet as a pebble, as quiet as—

As quiet as the thing in the vault. The thing he'd carved seven years ago and shelved and told himself, every day since, was just another commission.

"Sable."

Odile's voice, from the hearth. Not loud. She never needed to be loud; the room arranged itself around her voice the way iron filings arrange around a magnet.

"Bring the tea, would you. And bring yourself. We're going to have a conversation."

* * *

Her office was really just the good corner of the back room: her chair, the fire, a table scarred by forty years of rings from forty years of cups, and the window that looked out onto the alley where the porters smoked. Sable poured the tea—black leaf, bitter, the way she liked it and pretended not to—and sat across from her and waited, because waiting was the first lesson she'd ever taught him. *Let them spend their words before you spend yours.*

Odile blew on her tea. She was seventy-one years old and had run the Vellum Guild for thirty of those years, and she had a face like a map of somewhere unpleasant: all ridges and sudden drops, weathered past sympathy. People underestimated her because she was small and old and drank her tea with her pinky crooked like a duchess. Those people generally ended up poorer.

"You handled the nun well," she said.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet, it wasn't entirely a compliment." She sipped. Set the cup down. Turned it, precisely, so the handle faced the window. "You didn't touch it."

"I used the cloth. Standard practice for—"

"For relics, yes. Standard practice for relics." Her eyes came up, grey and sharp as awls. "You've used the cloth on every relic that's come through that counter for six years, Sable. Every single one. Do you know what I call that? I call that a man who's afraid of the merchandise."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, Pip—who was technically employed as a runner and practically employed as a nuisance—was chasing a cat across the rafters, judging by the thumps and the swearing.

"I'm careful," Sable said.

"You're *invisible*." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "There's a difference, and the difference matters now more than it used to. Careful men ask questions, Sable. Careful men want to know what's in the jar before they price it. You—" she waved her cup at him, at all of him, "—you price things without ever wanting to know them. It's made you the best ledger-keeper I've ever had, and it's going to get you killed."

"That seems dramatic."

"Everything dramatic seems dramatic right up until it happens." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in the deep lines of her face. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I've known the answer for years, and I'm asking anyway, because I want to know what you'll say. Are you listening?"

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "Yes."

"What did you carve, seven years ago, for the client who paid in old coin?"

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when the swell comes under the keel. He kept his face still. Six years of practice at keeping his face still.

"A reliquary," he said. "Commissioned anonymously. Paid in pre-Union coin, which I noted in the ledger, and which you told me at the time was none of my business."

"Mm. And what was in it?"

"I don't know. The client supplied the contents. I carved the case. That's the trade."

"That's the trade," Odile agreed softly. "That's exactly the trade. Carve the case, take the coin, ask nothing." She sat back. "You were twenty-three, and hungry, and I let you do it because everyone in this building lets everyone else do it—that's how the Guild survives, we are a cathedral of *minding our own business*—but I have watched you for six years, boy, and I am telling you now, at the end of a very long career, that minding your own business is a skill, and like all skills it can be overused."

"Odile—"

"There's a storm coming." She said it flatly, looking into her tea. "Not the one in the jar. A real one. I've had letters from three cities this month. In Callowmere they're burning bones in the squares—not relics, *bones*, grave-goods, mourning charms, anything that whispers. In Harrowgate the Gift-Hunt posted its first lists. Names, Sable. Printed names, nailed to church doors." She looked up. "The crown has decided that people like you are a disease, and diseases get quarantined, and when quarantine fails they get burned out."

The word landed in Sable's chest like a swallowed stone. *People like you.* She had never said it so plainly. In six years she had circled it, hinted at it, built a hundred small shelters around it without once naming it—and now she had said it over tea, in daylight, as though it were simply an entry in the ledger.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, automatically, and hated himself for it.

"No," Odile said. "You don't. That's rather my point." She rose, slowly, using the table, using the years, and crossed to the window. Below, in the alley, the porters had stopped smoking and were reading something one of them had brought—a broadsheet, cheap ink, the kind sold by criers for a copper. One of them read aloud and the others went quiet in a way Sable could feel through the floor.

"When the questions start being asked," Odile said, without turning around, "you will wish you had practiced asking them. Because the men coming will ask *plenty*, and they will not accept 'that's the trade' for an answer, and the only people who survive interrogations are the ones who learned, early and often, to look directly at the thing they were afraid of." She turned. "Look at the thing, Sable. Whatever it is. Whatever you shelved and tagged and told yourself was finished. Look at it before someone makes you."

The bell above the front door rang. Then rang again—fast, repeated, the way it rang when someone came through it running.

Pip's voice, breathless, cracking on the last word: "Mistress Vess! Mistress Vess, there's criers on Coronet Square, there's a *proclamation*, they're nailing it to the fountain and there's soldiers, real ones, with the white sashes—"

Odile closed her eyes for one heartbeat. When she opened them, all the age had burned off her face and what remained was the woman who had run the Vellum Guild for thirty years, and who had, Sable suddenly understood, been waiting for this day the way the storm in the jar had waited for the moors.

"Well," she said. "Fetch the ledgers, boy. All of them. And then fetch me the key to the deep vault."

Sable stood. His legs felt borrowed. "Which ledger?" he asked. "What do you want me to record?"

Odile was already moving toward the stairs, cane striking the boards like a gavel, and what she said over her shoulder was not an answer but an instruction, and it was the first time in six years she had ever given him one that frightened him:

"Nothing. From this hour, we record nothing. Today, Sable, the Guild starts remembering how to forget."
