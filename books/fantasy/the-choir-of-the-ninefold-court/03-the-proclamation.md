## 3. The Proclamation

The morning the criers came, Sable Galloway was carving a saint's finger and thinking about breakfast.

It was a habit of his to think about breakfast while working—something small and domestic to anchor him against the voices that drifted up through bone like smoke through a floorboard. The saint's knucklebone had arrived at his workbench three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note in Odile's precise hand: *For cataloguing. Do not listen longer than necessary.*

*Do not listen longer than necessary.* As if listening were a tap he could turn off. As if the dead were polite enough to wait for an invitation.

The saint in question had been dead four hundred years and was, by Sable's estimation, insufferable about it. He didn't speak so much as declaim—a voice like a sermon delivered from a great height, all vowels and certainty.

*—and I told them, I told the poor wretches plainly, that suffering ennobles, that their hunger was a ladder—*

"You starved them," Sable murmured, turning the bone under his knife. "You built a cathedral on grain you'd hoarded."

*The soul is fed by other bread,* the saint said serenely.

Sable's knife paused. He had learned long ago not to argue with the dead; they never conceded a point, and they never left. Instead he did what he always did: he listened politely, the way one listens to a drunk uncle at a wedding, and he carved, and he waited for the voice to thin into silence as the charm took shape around it.

This one would be a binding-charm, a quieting piece. The Guild sold such things to monasteries—to houses that wanted their relics to *stop talking*, which struck Sable as a confession of sorts. Every order that bought a silencing charm had, somewhere in its crypt, a saint saying things the living preferred not to hear.

He was smoothing the last facet when the bells began.

Not the hour-bells. Not the alarm-bells from the harbor, which rang in short sharp bursts for fires and floods. These were the proclamation-bells—the deep bronze throats of the Citadel quarter, rung only twice in Sable's lifetime: once for the old king's death, once for the armistice that ended the Border War.

They rang now in a slow, deliberate rhythm, and beneath them, faint but spreading like ink through water, came the criers' voices—from the Citadel steps, down the Processional, out along the Ringway, multiplying street by street.

Sable set down his knife. His hands, he noticed distantly, had gone very still.

He crossed to the window of his workroom above the guildhall. Below, Copperlane was doing what Copperlane always did on market mornings: shouting prices, hauling carts, cursing mules. But heads were turning toward the sound. A knot of people gathered at the corner where the crier's road met Tanner's Walk, and Sable saw the man himself—liveried in white and ash-grey, the Inquisition's colors, flanked by two soldiers—and saw the crowd's particular quality of stillness, the way a flock of birds goes silent before a hawk crosses the sun.

The crier unrolled a scroll. Even from three floors up, Sable caught the trained carry of his voice, pitched to reach every ear in the square without seeming to shout.

*"By decree of Lord-Protector Cassian Vael, in the name of the Crown and the preservation of the realm—"*

Sable's mouth went dry.

*"—let it be known throughout the city and its liberties that the practice of necromantic arts, the traffic with the departed, the hearing of the dead by any means natural or crafted, is hereby declared an abomination before law and faith alike."*

A woman near the fountain dropped her basket. Sable heard the crack of eggs even from above.

*"All persons possessing such gifts, or harboring those who do, shall present themselves at the House of Ash within nine days to be registered, examined, and marked."*

A pause. The crier wet his lips. Sable watched him do it, watched the small human hesitation, and understood that the next words were the ones the soldiers were there for.

*"Those who fail to register shall be accounted enemies of the realm. They shall be tried by fire."*

Tried by fire. The phrase had a history. It came from the old war, from the suppression, from the pyres that older folk still wouldn't name aloud—they said *the Cleansing Years* instead, the way you'd say *the fever* instead of naming the sickness. Sable had grown up on stories of it the way children grow up on stories of wolves: frightening, distant, finished.

The crier rolled his scroll. The soldiers turned. And the crowd of Copperlane did something Sable had never seen a crowd do: it dispersed without speaking. No outrage, no gossip, no clustering to trade theories. People simply folded back into their errands like water closing over a stone, each face carefully arranged into the expression of someone who had heard nothing worth remembering.

That was the worst part, Sable thought. Not the words. The silence after them. Fear arriving like weather—not suddenly, but as a change in pressure every animal feels in its teeth.

Behind him, in the workroom, the saint's knucklebone had gone quiet too. Even the dead, it seemed, were listening.

* * *

He made it down the stairs on legs that felt borrowed.

The Vellum Guild's main hall was full at this hour—clerks at their desks, appraisers bent over lots, the morning trade of impossible things flowing through ledgers like water through channels. Bottled laughter from the Laughing Duke's court, three vials, provenance disputed. A door that opened onto nowhere, currently leased to a merchant who used it to store things he didn't want found. A reliquary of minor saints, whispering among themselves in Latin no one alive could parse.

All of it stopped.

Sable stood in the doorway and watched the news move through the hall the way frost moves across a window—not arriving all at once but claiming the room pane by pane. A clerk set down her pen mid-stroke and did not pick it up. An appraiser closed his loupe case with exaggerated care, as if the small click were the only thing holding the world together. Two porters by the loading door looked at each other and then, very deliberately, away.

Nobody said the word *pyre*. Nobody had to. It hung in the air of the hall like smoke.

Odile Vess stood at the head of the room, behind her high desk, and she was the only person moving. She had heard, obviously—she heard everything in this building before anyone else, a fact Sable had attributed for years to shrewdness and was only now, with a cold sliding feeling, beginning to reexamine. She was already pulling ledgers from the shelf behind her: the red ones, the deep-red ones with the plain spines that held the Guild's true business, the trades that could not survive daylight.

"Master Quill," she said, and her voice was level, almost gentle, which frightened Sable more than shouting would have. "Close the shutters on the north wall. We'll work by lamp today."

"Madam Vess," the old clerk began, "the morning lots—"

"Will keep." She set the first ledger on her desk. "All of it will keep. What won't keep is paper."

They burned papers for the rest of the morning.

Sable had imagined, in the vague way one imagines disasters, that the burning of evidence would feel dramatic—flames leaping, pages whirling. It felt instead like laundry. Odile worked methodically through the red ledgers, tearing out pages with a bookbinder's precision, feeding them to the great hearth in the records room in batches small enough not to flare. Sable carried stacks. Pip, the guild runner, thirteen years old and all elbows, ferried bundles from the vault with his usual scowl replaced by something tighter, younger.

"What's in these?" Pip asked, nodding at a bundle Sable carried.

"Names," Sable said.

"Of who?"

"People who bought things they shouldn't have. People who sold them." He hesitated. "People like me."

Pip looked at him then—really looked, the way street kids look, taking inventory—and Sable realized what he'd done. He had said *people like me* to a child whose survival depended on knowing exactly whom to trust, and he had just made himself a liability. Or worse: a mark. Informants were paid. Informants were always paid.

But Pip only shifted the bundle under his arm and said, "Better burn fast, then," and trotted off toward the hearth, and Sable felt something in his chest loosen and ache at the same time.

By noon the records room smelled of ink and ash, and the hearth held the names of forty years of quiet commerce. Odile stood before it with her sleeves rolled, watching the fire with an expression Sable couldn't read—until he realized there was nothing to read. She had arranged her face into blankness the way the crowd below had arranged theirs, and the effort of it showed only in her hands, which gripped the poker hard enough to blanch the knuckles.

"They'll come anyway," Sable said quietly. "Burning paper doesn't unmake what we've traded."

"No." Odile didn't turn. "Paper burns clean. People leave residue." She finally faced him, and her eyes were the flat grey of a sea before weather. "Listen to me, Sable. Nine days. In nine days the House of Ash opens its rolls, and everyone who walks through that door gets examined, and everyone who gets examined gets marked, and everyone who gets marked—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. "The Guild has survived plagues, sieges, three changes of crown. We survive by being boring. By being furniture. From this moment, we are furniture."

"And the ones who can't be furniture?"

The question came out smaller than he intended. Odile looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired and, he thought with a start, afraid.

"The ones who can't be furniture," she said, "had better learn."

* * *

He went back to his workroom because there was nowhere else to go, and because his hands needed work the way other men's hands needed drink.

The saint's knucklebone sat where he'd left it, half-carved, its silencing charm incomplete. When he picked it up, the voice came flooding back mid-sermon, as though the dead man had been holding forth to an empty room and considered the interruption rude.

*—and on the third day I raised my staff and the waters parted, though the historians will tell you it was the drought—*

"Be quiet," Sable said.

The saint, for the first time in four hundred years, obeyed.

Sable stared at the bone in his palm. Around him, the city sounds rose through the window—the ordinary noise of Copperlane, carts and gulls and a fishwife arguing—but underneath it all he could feel the new silence spreading, block by block, house by house. All over the city, he thought, there were people like him. Bone-singers and death-whisperers, hedge-witches who talked to grandmothers, mediums reading last letters into cold hands. All of them had heard the criers. All of them were sitting very still right now, in attics and cellars and back rooms, listening to their own hearts and calculating nine days.

And all of them, he realized with a chill that started in his spine and spread outward, were wondering the same thing he was: *who else knows? Who has seen me? Whose debt is worth my name?*

Nine days. The House of Ash. Register, examine, mark—or burn.

He thought of Odile's face in the firelight. *We are furniture.* He thought of Pip's quick inventory glance. He thought of the red ledgers curling in the hearth, names becoming smoke, and how smoke doesn't stay where you put it.

On the workbench, the saint's knucklebone lay silent as a stone. Sable picked up his knife, set it down again, and pressed his palms flat against the wood, grounding himself in the grain, in the smell of shavings, in the small solid world of craft.

Below, in the hall, someone was lighting lamps against the shuttered afternoon. He could hear Odile giving orders in a voice like level water, and Pip's footsteps on the stairs, quick and light, carrying nothing.

Nine days, he thought. And somewhere in the vault below his feet, in the dark among the Guild's impossible inventory, something he had carved years ago was waiting—patient as bone—for him to remember it existed.

He didn't know yet why the thought came to him just then. He only knew that it arrived the way fear had arrived that morning: like weather, like pressure in the teeth, like the first far-off bell of something that had already begun.
