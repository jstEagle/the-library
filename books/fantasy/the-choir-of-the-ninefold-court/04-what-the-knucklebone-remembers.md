## 4. What the Knucklebone Remembers

The vault beneath the Vellum Guild had a smell Sable trusted more than any person: cold stone, tallow smoke, and the faint sweet rot of things that had once been loved. He came down before dawn on inventory days, when the city above was still gray and undecided, because the vault deserved his best hours. It held everything the guild had ever traded in that could not be priced honestly—impossible things, Odile called them, though she said it the way other people said *secondhand*.

He lit the three lamps on their iron hooks, checked the seals on the door behind him out of habit rather than fear, and unrolled the ledger across the counting table. The pages were vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, and his own handwriting marched down them in columns: item, provenance, condition, voice.

That last column was his alone. No one else at the guild knew what he wrote there. *Voice* meant what the object remembered, if it remembered anything. Most entries read *none* or *faint* or, in one memorable case, *complains about its previous owner's table manners*. A saint's knucklebone in the third cabinet hummed with something like weather. A bottled laugh from the Laughing War whispered itself over and over, delighted even now, even sealed in glass.

Sable worked methodically, shelf by shelf, touching each piece bare-handed. That was the rule he'd made for himself years ago and never broken: skin to bone, skin to wood, skin to whatever the thing was made of. Gloves muffled the voices, and half-heard voices were worse than none—they were like names you almost recalled, itching all day.

The first hour was quiet work. A mirror that showed rooms instead of faces (voice: *bored*). A key that fit no lock but opened a drawer in a house in Vellamarch where someone had hidden letters they were ashamed of (voice: *sorrowful, repetitive*). A child's wooden horse worn smooth by hands long dead (voice: *a girl laughing, a name like Nella or Nolla, gone before the second syllable*). Sable recorded each one in his small precise script and set it back exactly where it had lived, because the vault tolerated disorder the way a sleeping dog tolerated being moved—badly, and with consequences.

It was when he reached the far wall—the old wall, the deep shelves where items went when they stopped trading and started merely existing—that he found the box.

He knew it before he touched it. That was the strange part, the part he would turn over in his mind for days afterward. He was six shelves away, writing an entry about a funeral mask, when a sound reached him that had no business reaching him at all. Not through his ears. Through his hands. Through the bones of his wrists, up the long bones of his forearms, a vibration like a bell struck somewhere below hearing.

His pen stopped mid-stroke.

The box sat on the lowest shelf of the deep wall, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, tied with cord that had been knotted in the guild's own style—a carver's knot, triple-locked, the kind used only for pieces too dangerous to trust to simple wrapping. The dust on it lay thick and undisturbed. Years thick. And yet the sound coming off it was not faint at all. It was patient. There was a difference, Sable thought, between a voice grown quiet and a voice waiting, and this was the second kind.

He should have logged it and moved on. That was procedure. Anything on the deep wall that wasn't scheduled for appraisal stayed on the deep wall; Odile had been explicit about it a hundred times, in her way that was less an order than a raised eyebrow that never quite came down. *Some things keep better unexamined, Sable. Curiosity is a trade good like any other, and we are not rich enough to spend it.*

But the sound was in his wrists now, and his feet were already moving.

He lifted the box down with both hands. It was heavier than its size suggested—not unnaturally so, just the honest weight of dense old wood—and the oilcloth crackled as he turned it, reading the tag. The guild's tags were supposed to be standardized: item number, date of intake, appraiser's mark. This one had been scraped. Deliberately, with a knife, until the wood was pale and blank, except for two words cut fresh into the corner in a hand he recognized because it was his own:

*Do not listen.*

He had written that. He could not remember writing it. But no one else alive carved letters with that particular hooked descender—he'd mocked himself for it often enough—and the knife-work was his too, the shallow confident strokes of a man who'd done ten thousand of them. Some night, some version of himself he had no memory of being, had stood in this vault and gouged those words into the tag with enough force to split the wood grain.

*Do not listen.*

Sable Galloway had spent eleven years learning exactly how to listen without appearing to—to the dead man grumbling inside a widow's mourning charm, to the gossip of grave-goods, to the small sad choruses of ordinary objects that had outlived their owners. He had built his whole careful invisible life on listening politely and telling no one. Every instinct he owned said obey the tag. Put the box back. Dust your hands. Log it as *unassessed, per prior instruction*, and let the past keep its reasons.

Instead he heard himself say, quietly, to the empty vault: "One touch. Just to log the voice."

The lie was so smooth he almost believed it.

* * *

He unwrapped the oilcloth the way he did everything—slowly, precisely, keeping the folds intact so it could be rewrapped identically. Inside was a reliquary casket of blackthorn wood, and inside that, packed in wool gone gray with time, lay a knucklebone the size of a goose egg.

He knew it instantly. Not the object—objects passed through the guild in their hundreds—but the carving. His own. Seven years ago, maybe eight, back when he was young enough to take commissions from the trade floor without asking who wanted them or why. A knucklebone of some great beast, polished to cream, and carved across its surface with a pattern he remembered being proud of: nine interlocking rings, each ring threaded through the next, so that no ring could be lifted free without breaking all the others. He'd spent three weeks on it. He'd been paid too little and hadn't cared, because the work had felt less like carving than like remembering—as if the pattern had already existed inside the bone and he was only clearing away what hid it.

He had never heard a voice from it. That was why it had been shelved, presumably. A silent relic was dead stock. Someone had bought it, or tried to, and—

No. The intake records would tell him. He reached for the ledger, and his sleeve brushed the bone.

The world rang.

Not one voice. Nine.

They came up through his palm like bells struck in a cathedral he was standing inside of, layered and interlocked, each one distinct and none of them separate, harmonics braided through harmonies until he couldn't find the bottom note. They were not human voices, or not only. They were the voices of people who had been human so long ago and had become so much more than human that humanity was, to them, a childhood language—retained, but spoken now only for his benefit.

And they spoke in the plural. That was the detail that dropped through him like cold water. Every ghost he had ever heard spoke as *I*. These said *we*, and the *we* had weight, had territory, had the resonance of a hall built for thousands.

*Carver.* The word arrived whole, without passing through sound. *Little maker of small deaths. You kept us well.*

"I didn't—" His voice cracked absurdly in the lamplight. He was standing in a cellar holding a bone, talking to it, and every trained instinct screamed at him to put it down, and his hands would not open. "I don't know what you are."

*You know.* Amusement, then, rolling through the harmony like a swell through a fleet at sea. *You have always known. Why do you think your hands remembered our pattern? Why do you think you wrote upon the tag what you wrote? You listened once before, carver, long ago, and what you heard frightened you into forgetting. We have waited for the forgetting to wear thin.*

The lamps guttered. Or seemed to. Sable couldn't be sure anymore where the vault ended and wherever he was now began, because the stone around him had acquired depth—shadows falling farther than the walls allowed, air tasting of incense and cold metal and something older, something like the inside of a mountain. He saw, for half a heartbeat, a floor of white stone stretching to distances no cellar contained, and pillars carved with the same ninefold rings, and thrones—

Nine thrones. Occupied. Shapes seated in them, vast and crowned, faces he could not fix his eyes on the way he could not look directly at the sun, and every one of them turned toward him with the attention of a court receiving an ambassador it had been expecting for a very long time.

He wrenched his hand back.

The vision snapped shut like a book. The vault reassembled itself around him—three lamps, counting table, ledger with its ink still wet. His heart was going hard enough that he could hear it, which was its own small miracle, because for a moment there he had heard nothing else at all, and silence of that quality terrified him more than the voices had.

The knucklebone lay in the open casket, cream-pale, innocent, its nine rings catching the lamplight. Waiting.

"Ninefold," he said aloud, testing the word against everything he'd ever read. The war histories in the guild library were censored to lace—everyone knew the crown had burned the archives after the Suppression—but everyone also knew the shape of what had been removed. The Nine Kings. The Choir of Nine. The Court that had ruled the eastern half of the world before the war that burned it, whose names were illegal to carve, whose likenesses had been chiseled off monuments with such thoroughness that modern scholars argued about whether they'd ever existed at all. Children's rhymes still carried the count of them, scrubbed of meaning: *nine bells, nine bells, ring us down, ring us down.*

He had carved their sigil at nineteen years old because a trader paid him four silver marks and told him it was a decorative motif from old Vellamarch tilework.

And they had been here ever since. In the vault. Under his feet. Under Odile's shop. While he'd balanced ledgers and charmed widows and kept his head down and his gift secret, he had been sleeping above the most dangerous thing the guild had ever stored, and the guild had stored it *because of him*. Because he'd made it. Because whoever commissioned it had known—had known what Sable was before Sable had admitted it to himself—that a relic carved by a listener would hold more than echoes.

*We are not angry,* the voices murmured, softer now, coaxing, and he realized with horror that they had felt him flinch and adjusted themselves accordingly, the way a skilled negotiator lowers his voice. *We have never been angry with makers. You gave us shape when the world took ours away. Ask us anything, carver. We remember everything. We remember the true history of the war they burned the books to hide. We remember where every gifted soul in this city hides tonight—we can hear their hearts from here, the ones who think themselves so cleverly concealed. Would you like to know who they are? Would you like to know how safe they truly are?*

"No." The word came out flat and fast.

A pause. Then, gently, terribly: *You thought of Pip before you thought of yourself.*

Sable went cold to the marrow. Pip—the guild runner, sixteen years old, all elbows and stolen names, who slept in the loft and ate like a siege army and had never once asked why Sable sometimes went pale holding a delivery. Pip, whose heartbeat the thing in the casket could apparently hear from a cellar.

"Stay out of my head."

*It is not your head we occupy, carver. It is the world. That is rather the point of us.*

He closed the casket. His hands were shaking badly enough that the triple-knot took him three tries, and he wrapped the oilcloth over it and set it back on the low shelf with the tag facing outward—*do not listen*—and stepped back, and the bell-sound faded to a hum, and the hum faded to nothing, or to below nothing, to patience.

Then he did the only sensible thing left. He picked up his ledger, and in the voice column, under a new entry line, he wrote: *nine.*

He looked at the single word for a long time. It looked insufficient. It looked like writing *ocean* on the surface of the sea.

Underneath it, in smaller letters, he added: *they know my name. They know Pip's heart. I carved this. God forgive me—I carved this.*

* * *

Odile found him there at midmorning, which she never did, because Odile did not come down to the vault—it was understood between them that the vault was his country and she its absent queen. She came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and she was carrying two cups of chicory coffee, which she also never did, and Sable understood before she said a word that she had felt it too. Whatever had rung through the stone of this building, whatever had stirred in the deep shelves—she had felt it upstairs, through floors and ledgers and forty years of practiced indifference.

She looked at the low shelf. At the rewrapped box. At his face.

"You listened," she said. Not a question.

"It listened first."

Odile set both cups down on the counting table without drinking, which from Odile was equivalent to a scream. She was sixty and built like a ship's figurehead, with silver hair cropped short and hands that had appraised more impossible things than anyone living, and in eleven years Sable had seen her unsettled exactly twice. This was the third.

"What did you hear?" she asked. "How many?"

The question landed strangely. *How many,* as if she'd known to ask in numbers.

"Nine," Sable said.

Odile Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them again, all the commerce had gone out of her face, and what remained was something older and harder and afraid.

"The founder's journal is in the third drawer of my desk," she said. "The real one, not the one the tax assessors see. Read it tonight. Then come to me, and we will decide—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Sable. What did they say to you?"

He hesitated. Told the truth, because lying to Odile had never once worked: "They offered to tell me where every gifted soul in the city is hiding."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the morning sounds of the guild waking—Pip's boots on the stairs, the kettle, someone laughing. Ordinary life, going on unaware, one thin floor above a buried throne room.

Odile followed his eyes upward, toward the sound of Pip's boots, and Sable watched her arrive at the same thought he had been drowning in since dawn: *the Court knows. The Court has always known. And now the Court knows that Sable knows.*

"We may already be too late," Odile said.
