## 5. Odile's Bargain

The hammer came down on the guild's front door at the hour when honest people were still asleep and dishonest ones were pretending to be.

Sable was already awake. He had been awake since the knucklebone, since the nine voices had poured through him like cold water through a cracked jug, and he sat now in the counting room with a lamp turned low and the relic's box open before him on the ledger desk, not touching it, not quite able to stop looking at it. The bone was small—a knuckle of something old, yellowed to the color of candle tallow, carved over with his own marks from six years ago. His own knife-work. He remembered the job: a private commission, no name given, payment left in a dead-drop by the fountain in Chandler's Square. He remembered thinking it was strange that the client wanted *no* charm at all, only the carving, only the vessel, empty.

It had not been empty. It had never been empty. He simply hadn't been listening closely enough—or the thing inside hadn't been speaking loudly enough—and there was a difference between those two explanations that made his hands sweat.

From within the box, faint as a bell rung under a blanket, came the singing.

Not words. Not yet. A chord, rather—nine tones braided into one, some high and silver, some low enough that he felt them in his back teeth. They rose when he leaned close and settled when he pulled away, patient as tide. *We are here,* the chord said, without saying anything at all. *We have always been here. You made us a door.*

*I made you a box,* Sable thought back, and flinched at how childish it sounded, how much like a boy arguing with the dark.

The hammering stopped. Voices murmured beyond the door—Odile's voice, dry as old vellum, and beneath it the wheedling tone of a city watchman who wanted to be somewhere warmer. Sable closed the box, slid it into the drawer beneath the false bottom where the guild kept its most impossible inventory, and got the ledgers open just as the counting room door swung wide.

Odile Vess filled a doorway the way a hawk fills a sky—not large, but absolute. She was seventy if she was a day, thin as a lath, her white hair pinned up with two lacquered sticks that Sable had privately suspected for years were sharpened. Her eyes went to the ledgers, to the lamp, to Sable's face, and whatever she saw there made her mouth flatten.

"Watchman Tull," she said over her shoulder, "is concerned about our late hours."

"We keep accounts," Sable said. "Accounts don't keep themselves."

"Tell him that." She shut the door, shot the bolt, and stood with her hand on it a moment longer than she needed to, listening to the watchman's boots fade down Candlemaker's Row. Then she crossed the room, took the lamp, and raised it so its light fell full across Sable's face, the way an appraiser turns a piece to catch the flaws.

"You've heard it," she said.

Not a question. Sable's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going.

"Heard what," he said. "I've been doing the quarterly tallies. The bottled laughter is down two units, someone's been drinking the stock—"

"Sable." Just his name, in the voice she used on clients who tried to renegotiate terms after signing. She set the lamp down between them, and the little flame stood straight up in the still air like a soldier at attention. "Six years ago I sent you to carve a commission. No client name. Payment by dead-drop. You asked me one question—you remember?"

He did. He'd asked why the client didn't want a listening charm, if they were paying for bone-work at all. And Odile had told him: *Some things need a vessel, not a voice.* He'd thought it was guild cant. He'd written it off the way he wrote off half of what Odile said, filed under *things mentors say to sound wise.*

"You knew," he said. The singing in the drawer seemed louder, or perhaps that was only his blood. "You knew what it was. What was *in* it."

"I knew what it might be." She lowered herself into the chair across from him, unhurried, folding her hands on the desk the way she did when the guild's fortunes were being weighed. "I knew what it claimed to be, which is a different animal entirely. Sit down, Sable. This is a long story and we may not have the night to tell it."

He sat. His knees felt borrowed.

"The founder of this guild," Odile said, "was a woman named Marenca Vell. Before your time. Before mine, even—the guild was forty years old when I signed my first contract. She built the Vellum Guild out of nothing but nerve and a warehouse on the river, and she had one rule above all others, carved into the lintel of the old office before it burned: *We trade in impossible things, but we do not make them.*" Odile's thumb moved along the edge of the desk, tracing a groove worn by decades of other thumbs. "Do you know why a woman who sold doors to nowhere and saints' fingerbones would forbid the making of impossibilities?"

"Because making is riskier than trading?"

"Because she'd seen what happens when someone makes one." Odile's eyes were very steady. "Marenca Vell was gifted. Not like you—she never heard the dead. But she could find things. Lost things, hidden things, things that did not want to be found. And late in her life, something in this city began calling to her. Calling and calling. Something buried under the war-vaults below the Old Quarter, wrapped in lead and oath and silence, and it wanted *out*, and it had learned that the surest way out of a grave is to be carried."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft. Sable told himself it was the heat of his own face leaning close.

"She found it," he said. "She dug it up."

"She was *brought* to it. That's the part nobody understands about gifts, boy—they're not yours. You think your hearing is a tool you hold. It holds *you*. Marenca spent three years resisting that call, and then one winter she stopped coming to the guild, and when we finally broke into her house we found her dead at her own table, starved to death with food in the pantry, and on the table before her—" Odile paused, and for the first time in all the years Sable had known her, her hands were not perfectly still. "On the table was a piece of carved bone. Yellowed. Old. Covered in knife-marks that no one in the guild could read."

The drawer might as well have been shouting. Sable could feel the chord through the wood, through the floor, up the legs of his chair into his spine. Nine notes. Nine sovereigns. The Choir of the Ninefold Court, singing in a box under his hands.

"And you put it in the vault," he said hoarsely.

"I put it in the vault. And I hired a bone-carver with a careful reputation and a talent for keeping his head down, and I gave him a commission from a client who did not exist, and I asked him to carve a vessel around it—because the binding on the thing was failing, Sable. Marenca's death had been the last knot in it, and knots tied in dying flesh rot fast. Fresh carving, fresh intent, a craftsman's attention—that would hold it another lifetime. Maybe two." She looked at him without apology and without mercy. "I did not tell you because telling you would have put the voices in your ear. And here you sit, six years later, having heard them anyway, because you were always going to. Because that is what gifts do."

Sable stood up. He didn't decide to; his body simply decided for him, and suddenly he was on his feet with the drawer open and the box in his hands, and Odile had gone very quiet, watching him the way one watches a man standing too near a cliff edge.

"It sings," he said. His voice came out wrong, roughened. "It's been singing all night. Not words—yet—but it knows me, Odile. It knows my hands. It says I made it a door, and it's right, I did, I carved every line of it, and when I touched it yesterday it—" He stopped. He could not say *it poured itself into me* without admitting how much of it had stayed.

"Put it down."

"They said they were nine." The words spilled now, dam broken. "Nine voices, layered like bells. Sovereigns. The Ninefold Court, Odile—the ones from the histories, the ones the war was about. They've been in my vault. Under my desk. For six years while I sharpened pencils over their heads."

"*Put it down.*"

And there was something in her voice—not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, old and scarred over—that reached him where commands hadn't. He set the box on the desk. His fingers did not want to let go of it. He noted that, clinically, the way he noted everything, and the noting frightened him more than the wanting did.

* * *

They sat for a long while without speaking. Somewhere outside, the city was waking—the first cart wheels on the cobbles, a milk-bell, the cough of a chimney learning to draw. Ordinary sounds. Sable clung to them like a drowning man to driftwood.

"Marenca died hiding it," he said at last. "You said the founder died hiding the same relic. So it's been passed hand to hand like a debt nobody can pay."

"Three keepers before me. Two before Marenca, though she never learned their names—only that the thing surfaces every few generations, always carried, always by someone who hears more than they should." Odile drew one of the lacquered sticks from her hair and turned it over; the end, as Sable had always suspected, was honed to a needle's point. "Every keeper reaches the same crossroads. The thing offers. That's how it works—it doesn't force, it *invites*. And the invitation is tailored, Sable. Made to measure. It shows each keeper exactly the door they most want to walk through."

"It offered me knowledge." His mouth was dry. "Last night. It said—if I opened it properly—it could tell me where every gifted soul in this city is hiding. Every one. Before the Inquisition finds them." He heard how it sounded even as he said it, how reasonable, how *good*. "It dressed itself up as rescue."

"Of course it did. It's had centuries of practice." Odile set the hair-stick down on the desk between them, a small black exclamation point. "Here is what I know of the Ninefold Court, from records Marenca left and records older than her. Nine rulers, once, of the lands that are now nine duchies and a burned waste. They were not conquerors from abroad—they grew from here, from us, root and branch. And the histories the crown teaches say the old kingdoms rose against them in righteous rebellion. But Marenca's papers say something else. They say the Court held dominion for four hundred years, and that the rebellion burned half the world, and that when the smoke cleared, the nine did not die. They could not die. So they were *bound*—each sovereign pressed down into bone, and the bones joined into one, and the whole buried under oaths and lead where no gift could reach them."

"Until a gift reached them anyway."

"Gifts always reach. That is their nature and their curse." She pushed back her chair and stood, moving to the window, where grey light was beginning to thin the dark over the rooftops. "For six hundred years the Court has sung to whoever could hear it. Carriers, keepers, fools. And every song is different, because every listener is different. To a soldier it sings of order. To a scholar, of lost libraries. To a grieving mother—" her voice caught, barely, and smoothed itself over, "—it sings of the child come home. It has never once, in six hundred years, told anyone the truth on first meeting. Truth is the last coin it spends, and it spends it only when lies have bought all they can."

"So we destroy it." Sable said it quickly, before the singing could rise again, before he could hear the counter-argument forming in his own chest. "That's why you're telling me this. That's the bargain. Burn it, grind it, drop it in the river."

"Bone doesn't burn clean and rivers give things back." Odile turned from the window. "It must be unmade. Unworked. Every line you carved must be taken out, stroke by stroke, by the hand that cut it—which is why I am telling *you* and not Brannoc or Corvin or any of the others. The binding answers to its maker. Your knife opened the door, Sable. Only your knife can close it."

He looked at the box. Small. Light enough to hold in one palm. Six hundred years of nine imprisoned kings, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

"When?" he asked.

"Now. Tonight. Before the Inquisition finishes its list and comes knocking, before Corvin gets curious, before—" She stopped herself. Sable watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and understood that the unfinished part was *before you listen long enough to want it.*

But he had already listened. That was the poison in it. He had lain awake half the night with the chord running through him like a second pulse, and in the small hours, drifting, he had caught himself *answering*—not in words, not deliberately, but with some deep part of him leaning toward the sound the way a cold man leans toward a fire. And the fire had noticed. The fire had warmed.

"Odile." He made himself look at her. "Before I take the knife to it. I have to ask. What if they're telling the truth? What if the crown's histories are the lies? What if the Court was bound unjustly, and every year we keep them buried we're—"

"We're keeping tyrants in a box," Odile said flatly. "Whatever they were, whatever the war was, they ruled for four hundred years and the world they ruled is ash and legend. Whatever they are *now*, they have spent six centuries doing one thing: getting out. A prisoner's innocence does not make his escape safe, Sable. Especially not this prisoner."

"But you don't *know*."

"No," she said quietly. "I don't know. None of us knows. That is the whole horror of it. We are asked to either free or destroy something we do not understand, based on testimony we cannot trust—from it, or from the crown that buried it, or from a dead woman's papers. There is no clean choice. There is only the choice that leaves the fewest graves." She picked up the hair-stick and slid it back into her white hair, and when her hands came down they were steady again, and her voice with them. "Unmake it. Tonight, after the shop closes. I will stand with you while you work. And when it's done, we burn the shavings, salt the ash, and you and I will drink the good brandy that I have been saving for a celebration I hoped never to need."

It should have been simple. It was simple. Take out the stitches, close the wound, go on.

So why, Sable wondered, staring at the little box in the grey morning light, could he still hear it singing? Why did some traitor chamber of his heart keep turning the question over—*what if, what if, what if they meant it, what if the knowledge could save Pip and Brannoc and the bone-singers in the Warrens, what if refusing is just another kind of killing*—

"Say you'll do it," Odile said.

"I'll do it," Sable said. And it was even mostly true.

She nodded once, satisfied, and moved toward the door to open the shop, because the guild had clients and clients did not care about the end of the world. At the threshold she paused, her back to him.

"One more thing you should know about Marenca Vell," she said. "In her last months, she wrote that the Court had begun to speak to her plainly. No more songs. Plain speech. And do you know what she wrote they said to her, at the end, when she was starving at her own table with the food an arm's length away?"

Sable's throat wouldn't work. He shook his head.

"They said: *We have waited six hundred years. We can wait six hundred more. But you will open the door yourself, carrier, because a door must be opened from the side of the living.*" Odile's hand rested on the bolt. "She starved rather than open it. Remember that, tonight, when it starts talking to you. Remember what it cost her to refuse, and what it will cost you, and do it anyway."

The door opened and shut behind her. Daylight came in with the smell of the street—bread, horse, river mud. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary light.

Sable sat alone with the box, and the ledgers, and the lamp.

And the singing changed.

It was subtle—he almost missed it, the way you miss the moment a friend becomes a stranger. But the chord shifted, the nine tones rearranging themselves, and underneath the music, for the first time, came something that was not music at all. Words. Quiet ones. Patient ones. Spoken directly into the marrow of him, in a voice like his own voice heard from the inside:

*Good morning, carver. Shall we talk about your friends?*

Downstairs, the shop bell rang. Footsteps on the stairs—light, quick, familiar. Pip's voice, breathless, too loud for the hour:

"Sable! Sable, you've got to come see—there's men at the fountain square, gray cloaks, and they've got a *list*—"

The box sat on the desk between Sable's hands, warm as a living thing, and sang.
