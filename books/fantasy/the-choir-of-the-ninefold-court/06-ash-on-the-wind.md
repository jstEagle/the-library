## 6. Ash on the Wind

The river brought the smell of smoke before it brought the city.

Maren Vale stood at the rail of the ferry as Vespergate resolved out of the morning haze—first the bridge towers, then the tiled roofs climbing the hillside like scales on some sleeping beast, then the spire of the Chantry of Unanswered Prayer, which had been leaning for two hundred years and showed no sign of falling. Behind her, in the cabin, her two juniors were arguing about luggage. In front of her, on the water, a barge loaded with charcoal slid past, and its wake rocked the ferry, and she gripped the rail and thought: *this is where they burned them.*

Not here, exactly. Not this water. But the river remembered, the way rivers do—slow, patient, keeping everything—and somewhere upstream, years ago and in another lifetime, the pyres of the last suppression had sent their ash into this same current. She had read the reports. She had read all of them, twice, because reading was safer than remembering.

"You'll want your coat, Inquisitor," said the ferryman, not looking at her. "Vespergate wind comes off the water mean."

"Thank you." She did not put the coat on yet. The gray wool bore the sigil of the Office of Inquiry—a closed eye stitched in silver thread—and putting it on meant becoming it, and she wanted three more minutes of being only a tired woman on a boat.

Three minutes was what she allowed herself. Then she went below, took up her case, shrugged into the coat, and became the thing the city would need her to be.

* * *

The Office kept rooms above the customs house, four floors of ledgers and dust and one window that looked out on nothing but the neighboring wall. Maren unpacked with the economy of someone who had done it eleven times in six years: robes hung, boots polished, the small iron box placed in the bottom drawer beneath a false lining of prayer books. She did not open the box. She never opened the box. It was enough to know it was there, the way it was enough to know the sea was full of drowned things.

Corvin Thane arrived an hour later, carrying her files as though they were made of glass and he resented their fragility. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular hunger of those who rise by handing others down.

"The local registry is useless," he announced, setting the files on her desk. "Half the parishes haven't submitted rolls since the old King's reign. The guild quarter is worse—they keep their own records, and the Vellum Guild—" his mouth curled around the name— "keeps records of things that shouldn't exist."

"That's why we're here."

"There's talk already. Market talk. A bone-carver on Chandler's Row who sells charms that *work*. A woman in Salt Lane who can find lost children by holding their shoes. And—" he consulted a folded paper with the reverence of a man handling scripture— "a bone-singer. An actual bone-singer, still practicing. Name of Hesper Lune. Sings over the dead so they don't come back restless. Half the dockside uses her. The other half pays her."

Maren laid out her pens. Black for record, red for judgment, and the third pen, plain horn, that she used for nothing and carried everywhere. "A bone-singer," she said. "That's a gentle trade, usually. Grief-work."

"Grief-work is still traffic with the dead. The Proclamation doesn't distinguish."

"No," Maren said quietly. "It doesn't." She squared the files. "We'll see her tomorrow. Tonight I want the parish boundaries mapped and a list of every registered death in the dockside for the past year. If she sings for the dead, the dead will have left a trail of grateful families."

Corvin wrote it down, though she could see he thought the mapping was beneath him. Let him think it. The map mattered. Everything, always, came down to knowing where the walls were—who lived inside them, who hid behind them, and how thin the plaster had grown.

That night she slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of her mother humming, which was not.

* * *

Hesper Lune lived above a rope-maker's store at the crooked end of Salt Lane, in rooms that smelled of beeswax and river damp and, underneath it, faintly and unmistakably, of old bones. Maren climbed the stairs alone. She had told Corvin to wait at the corner; interrogations went better without an audience hungry for confessions.

The woman who opened the door was sixty-odd, broad as a doorframe herself, with hands like knotted rope and eyes the color of tea left standing. She looked at the closed-eye sigil for a long moment. Then she stepped aside.

"I wondered when you'd come," Hesper said. "Sit. There's tea, if you drink it. Most of you don't, when you're working."

"I'll drink it." Maren sat in the offered chair—a good chair, worn smooth by decades of grieving visitors—and set her case beside her feet, unopened. "You know why I'm here."

"Proclamation went up on the Chantry doors nine days ago. Register or burn." Hesper poured tea from a brown pot, unhurried. "I can read, Inquisitor. Surprising, I know."

"It isn't." Maren accepted the cup. "How long have you sung?"

"Forty-one years. Learned it from my mother, who learned it from hers. We're not what you think we are." She settled across the table, and her gaze was steady and unafraid, and Maren found that steadiness more unsettling than fear would have been. "We don't raise anything. We don't call anything. A body goes in the ground still humming with what it was—the way a bell keeps ringing after you've stopped striking it. All I do is sing the last of the sound out of it. Gently. So it rests instead of rattling."

"And if you didn't?"

"Then sometimes the dead stay restless. Not demons. Not monsters. Just—" Hesper searched for the word, found it, and gave it plainly— "unfinished. Like a letter with no signature. They knock about inside the people who loved them. Grief gets teeth. You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't."

Maren had seen it. She had seen a widow in Corvane who hadn't slept in a year because her dead husband's voice wouldn't stop reciting the shopping list in her skull. She had seen a boy in Marrow Ford who heard his drowned sister laughing in the well and had tried, at nine years old, to join her. The work she did was supposed to stop such things. She held onto that. Some mornings it held.

"The law doesn't weigh the gentleness of the gift," she said. "Only its existence."

"I know what the law weighs." Hesper drank her tea. "So. Are you here to arrest me?"

Maren set down her cup. This was the part of the work that required precision, the way surgery required it—not the questions, but the pauses between them. "I'm here to register you. That's what the Proclamation offers. Registration, oversight, a mark in the rolls. You'd report quarterly. You'd be restricted to registered deaths—no singing over the unclaimed, the criminal, the foreign. And you'd be subject to inspection."

"Inspection." Hesper smiled without warmth. "Meaning you watch me work."

"Meaning I confirm you're doing what you say you do."

"And if I refuse?"

Maren met her eyes. "Then I take you to the Office, and the process stops being mine."

The room was quiet. Down in the lane, a cart rattled past, and the rope-maker's wheel creaked its slow circle, and somewhere a child was counting stones against a wall—one, two, three, four—in a voice that snagged something under Maren's ribs and pulled.

"I'll register," Hesper said at last. "Because forty-one years of work shouldn't end on a pyre, and because if I run, they'll pull apart everyone I've ever sung for looking for me." She leaned forward. "But hear me, Inquisitor, because I'll only say it once. Whatever brought you to this city—it isn't us. We're the small fish. The charms and the grief-songs and the women finding children by their shoes. Something bigger washed up here recently. Something old. My dead have been talking about it."

Maren kept her face still, though the words landed like cold water. "Your dead talk to you after you've sung them to rest?"

"Not to me." Hesper's voice dropped. "Around me. When I work, I stand in the shallows of a very deep place, Inquisitor, and lately the deep place has been... stirring. The dead aren't chattering the way they used to. They're listening. All of them, all at once, facing the same direction. Like sheep before a storm." She sat back. "Two nights ago I sang a drowned pilot to rest, and halfway through the song, he stopped answering me. Not gone. *Attentive.* Turned toward something under the city, the way a congregation turns toward the altar. I finished the rite with my skin crawling, and I've been carving salt-marks on my doorsills ever since."

"What direction?" Maren asked, and hated how quickly the question came. "Under the city—which quarter?"

"Why? Writing it in the black book or the red one?"

"Hesper."

"Guildside." The old woman's eyes were flat and certain. "Under the guild quarter, where the Vellum Guild sits on its vaults like a hen that's been sitting too long on eggs nobody remembers laying." She refilled Maren's cup unasked. "There was a carver there once, years back. Young man, quiet, carved my mother's mourning piece. Finest work I ever saw. He listened to the bone while he cut it—I watched him do it, tilted his head like a man hearing music through a wall. Never said a word about it, and I never asked, because that's the courtesy we owe each other in this trade." She sipped. "Sable Galloway. Keeps their ledgers now, I hear. If your deep place has a door, Inquisitor, I'd wager my mother's ring it opens somewhere near him."

Maren wrote the name in the black book. Her hand was steady. Inside, something that had been quiet for years shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns.

She finished the tea. She thanked Hesper Lune for her cooperation, and meant it, which was the worst part. At the door, the old woman caught her sleeve—not gripping, just touching, the way you'd touch something you weren't sure was solid.

"You didn't flinch," Hesper said, studying her. "When I talked about the dead listening. Most of your kind flinch."

"Most of your kind assume we're made of stone."

"Mm. And what are you made of, Inquisitor Vale?"

Maren looked at her for a moment longer than the question deserved. "Duty," she said, and went down the stairs into the gray afternoon.

* * *

Corvin was waiting at the corner with news spread across his face like butter. "Commander's dispatch came while you were out. He wants results by month's end—registrations, seizures, names. He's sending twelve more inquisitors next week. Vespergate is to be a model for the whole province." He fell into step beside her. "And there's word from the guild quarter. A trader named Corvus—no relation—says the Vellum Guild burned a cartload of papers two nights ago. Ledgers, contracts. Odile Vess herself fed the fire."

"Odile Vess." Maren turned the name over. She knew it from the provincial files: guildmistress, thirty years in post, a woman whose guild traded in bottled laughter and doors to nowhere and other commodities the law hadn't invented words for yet. "Burning her own records. Either she's destroying evidence, or she's protecting people."

"Same thing, from where the Commander stands."

"From where the Commander stands, yes." Maren walked on. The evening was coming down over Vespergate, lamplighters moving along the streets with their long poles, and the smell of bread and coal smoke and the river, and beneath it all—if she let herself be honest, and honesty was the one luxury she permitted herself only in the privacy of her own skull—the smell of ash on the wind, though no fire was burning anywhere she could see.

At the customs house she paused on the steps and looked back at the city: the roofs going gold and shadow, the leaning spire, the far dark line of the guild quarter hunched along the hill like something waiting.

*Sable Galloway.*

She would start there. Names first, then faces, then the slow closing of distance that was her craft, honed over six years and eleven cities. She told herself it was method. She told herself the quickening in her chest was only the hunt, the old clean pleasure of the trail warming under her feet.

She almost believed it.

Above the rooftops, thin and far away, a bell began to ring—though she knew, checking the light, that the Chantry bells did not ring at dusk. It rang nine times, and stopped, and the silence afterward felt less like quiet than like a held breath.

Maren Vale stood on the steps until the cold came through her coat, and then she went inside to write her first report, and did not mention the bell at all.
