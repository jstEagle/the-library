## 7. The Boy Who Sold His Name

The name Pip had been wearing for three years belonged to a dead boy.

Not dead in the way that mattered to bone-carvers and Inquisitors—no grave, no ghost, no grieving mother burning tallow at a wayside shrine. Dead the way street things died: quietly, unrecorded, folded into the city's endless arithmetic of loss. The real Pip had been a runner for the Vellum Guild before him, a quick-footed kid with a chipped tooth and a laugh like a dropped bell. Then the fever winter came, and the laugh stopped, and the guild needed someone small enough to slip through alleys with packages no one asked about.

So when the man from the counting house looked down at the skinny, soot-cheeked orphan shivering on his step and said, "You'll do. What's your name?"—the boy had said *Pip* the way you'd pick up a coat left out in the rain. It fit well enough. It was warm where it counted. And nobody, in three years, had ever asked him to take it off.

He thought about that sometimes, usually at night, usually when he couldn't sleep for the sound of his own stomach. Names were funny things. You could steal one easier than bread, and nobody chased you for it. But it sat different in you after a while. Heavier. Like it grew roots.

Today, though, Pip wasn't thinking about names. Today he was thinking about the two men drinking tea across the lane from Brightwater's workshop, who hadn't ordered anything but had been sitting there since the lamplighter made his rounds, and who kept looking at the door the way cats look at mouse-holes.

---

Pip ran messages for half the low quarter of Vellamarch, which meant he knew every face that belonged on its streets and, more importantly, every face that didn't. The city had a rhythm, and he'd learned to hear it the way Sable Galloway supposedly heard the dead—except Pip's ghosts were living ones: the fishwife who cursed at gulls every morning at the same hour; the lamplighter who whistled off-key; the pair of dockhands who argued about dice outside the Salt Cellar every evening whether or not they'd actually gambled that day.

These two men broke the rhythm.

They sat at the corner table of the Drowned Kettle, a tea-house so cheap the tea was mostly hot water with ambitions. One was big, with a jaw like a shovel and hands too clean for a laborer. The other was thin as a whipstock, with a clerk's ink stains and eyes that never settled anywhere longer than a breath. Neither drank much. Neither talked. And both of them, every time the door of the bone-carver's shop opened across the cobbles, went still as herons.

Pip clocked them from the top of a delivery cart stacked with herring barrels, chewing the heel of a loaf he'd charmed off the baker's boy, and he did the math without even meaning to. The Gift-Hunt proclamation had gone up four days ago. Criers on every corner, the crown's seal dripping red wax off every notice board: *all who traffic with the dead must present themselves for registration, upon pain of—* well. Upon pain of fire, basically. The whole quarter had gone quiet in that particular way quiet gets when everyone's pretending nothing's wrong. Odile Vess had burned papers in the guild's back yard until the smoke made the neighbors complain. Master Ferren had stopped laughing at jokes. And Sable—

Sable had just kept carving.

That was the thing about the bone-carver that Pip couldn't figure out. Everyone else in the guild had gotten twitchy, watchful, mean around the edges. But Sable came in every morning with sawdust on his sleeves and that careful, closed-off look he always wore, and he sat at his bench and he worked, like the world outside the window was weather happening to somebody else. Pip had decided, somewhere in the last few days, that this was either very brave or very stupid, and he'd been leaning stupid.

But now there were heron-men watching the shop, and Pip's opinion was starting to shift toward brave.

He slid off the cart, dusted herring scales off his knees, and did what any sensible street kid would do with information worth money: he tried to sell it.

* * *

The problem was who to sell it to.

Corvin would pay, but Corvin paid in that smiley way of his that made Pip feel like he'd swallowed something slimy, and lately Corvin had been asking questions Pip didn't like. Questions about the vault. About what Sable worked on late. About whether Odile kept ledgers at home. Pip wasn't smart the way ledger-keepers were smart, but he was smart the way gutters were smart, and gutters knew when someone was measuring a house for its coffin.

Master Ferren was drunk by noon most days now. The other runners were younger and dumber. Which left, really, only one person, and Pip stood across the lane from the Drowned Kettle for a long moment, working up to it, because going to Sable Galloway felt like admitting something he didn't want to admit.

It felt like saying: *you matter to me, and I don't have anyone else.*

Pip had sold his name and kept everything else close. Feelings were the first thing the street took and the last thing it gave back.

But the heron-men were still watching, and the light was dying, and Sable was inside alone with a door that didn't lock properly because Pip himself had broken the bolt two winters ago sliding through it with stolen chestnuts, and Pip was nobody's fool.

He crossed the lane.

* * *

The bell over the shop door announced him the way it always did—a sad little clank, half-swallowed by dust—and the smell hit him the way it always did: bone dust and linseed oil and beeswax, with something underneath like cold stone. Carvings lined the walls on little wooden pegs. Mourning charms, memory beads, a whole rack of tiny carved animals that Pip privately thought were the best things in the room and had told absolutely no one.

Sable sat at his bench near the back window, a half-finished sparrow under his knife, and he didn't look up. "Shop's closing."

"It's not even dark yet."

"Closing early." The knife moved. Shavings curled away from the sparrow's wing like pale petals. "Tell Odile I'll have her order done Thursday."

"Sable." Pip hopped up onto the stool by the door—the one customers weren't allowed to touch, which is why he touched it constantly—and swung his feet. "There's two men in the Kettle over the way been staring at this door since sundown. Big one, thin one. Clean hands. Haven't bought nothing but the one pot of tea in three hours, and they split it."

The knife stopped.

For a moment the shop was so quiet Pip could hear the settling of the old building, the tick of cooling wax, his own heartbeat doing something embarrassing in his ears. Then Sable set the knife down—carefully, the way he did everything, like the world was made of eggshell—and turned on his stool. His face didn't change much. That was the thing people got wrong about Sable Galloway. They thought he was cold. Pip had watched him long enough to know it wasn't coldness. It was cost. Every expression cost him something, and he'd decided a long time ago he couldn't afford many.

"Describe them," Sable said.

"Big one's built like a door. Shovel jaw. Hands soft as a lord's. Thin one's got ink fingers and eyes like he's counting everything, including how many times you blink." Pip shrugged. "They're not thieves. Thieves look bored. These two look *patient*. Patience costs money, and money means somebody's paying, and somebody paying means the Guildhall or the Inquisition, and I don't need to draw you the rest of the picture."

"No," Sable said quietly. "You don't."

"They came in an hour after you lit your lamp. Been there since." Pip hesitated, then made himself say the rest, because the rest was the part that mattered. "Sable, they're not watching the shop. Shops get robbed, shops get taxed. They're watching the *door*. They want to know who goes in and who comes out. That's not a robbery. That's a net."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes—something quick and gray, like a fish under ice. His hand drifted to the bench, to a small shape lying beside the sparrow: a knucklebone-sized carving wrapped in cloth, which Pip had seen him handle maybe six times in the last week, always carefully, always when he thought no one was looking. Pip had opinions about that. Pip kept them behind his teeth, where he kept most things.

"You should go home," Sable said. "Not here tonight. Not for a few days, maybe."

"I don't got a home. I got a loft above the cooper's that smells like vinegar and a mattress with more lice than stuffing." Pip grinned, because grinning was armor and he owned several sets. "And anyway, I'm not the one they want. I'm nobody. I run packages. Nobody burns nobodies."

"That's not—" Sable stopped. Started again, slower. "Pip. When the Inquisition takes people, they take everyone around them. Associates. Runners. Anyone who can be made useful or made an example. Being nobody doesn't protect you. It just means no one pays your ransom."

The words landed heavier than Sable probably meant them to. Pip looked down at his boots—too big, secondhand, stuffed with straw at the toes—and found he didn't have a grin for that.

"Yeah, well," he said. "Somebody ought to warn Odile anyway. And Ferren. And the others." He jerked his chin at the wrapped thing on the bench, casual as he could make it, which was not very. "You staying?"

"I have work."

"You always have work."

"Yes," Sable said. "I do."

* * *

Here is what Pip did instead of going home, which was not what anyone sensible would have done, but Pip had stopped being sensible around the same time he'd stopped being the original Pip:

He sat on the stool by the door and refused to leave.

Not dramatically. Nothing so useful as drama. He just produced, from inside his coat, a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper, and began drawing the sparrow on the bench, badly, tongue between his teeth, occasionally asking questions like "how many wing-feathers do sparrows actually have" and "do you think birds know they're small" and other questions designed purely to be annoying. Sable bore it with the weary patience of a man who has learned that some weathers cannot be argued with.

"You could stay in the back," Sable said eventually, not looking up from the sparrow. "There's a cot. It's not comfortable."

"It's a cot."

"It's a plank with a blanket."

"See, you're selling it too hard. Now I definitely have to try it."

Something happened to Sable's mouth then. It took Pip a full three seconds to recognize it, because he'd almost never seen one before: a smile. Small, crooked, gone again almost immediately, like a fish surfacing—but real. Pip felt it land somewhere in his chest, warm and inconvenient, and pretended he hadn't.

"You're a menace," Sable said.

"I'm a professional," Pip said. "There's a difference. Menaces do it for free."

He drew the sparrow's bad wing. Outside, the last light drained off the cobbles, and the lamp in the Drowned Kettle glowed yellow, and the two heron-men sat in it like stones in a stream, patient as patience itself. Pip thought about telling Sable the other thing—the thing he'd heard that morning, down by the fish market, from a girl who sold secrets cheaper than he did: that the Inquisition had posted men at the guildhouse too. That Odile Vess's name was on a list. That lists got shorter by fire.

He didn't say it. Not because it wasn't important, but because saying it would make it true in the room, and the room was warm, and the room smelled like beeswax, and for one more hour Pip wanted to sit in a place where somebody had smiled at him, even a small crooked fish of a smile.

Instead he said, "Teach me to carve sometime?"

Sable looked up. Really looked, for once—not through him, not past him, but *at* him, the way you look at a thing you're deciding whether to keep.

"Why?" Sable asked.

Pip shrugged, elaborate and careless. "Dunno. Seems useful. Seems like the kind of thing a person should know. What happens if you break both your hands, huh? Who carves the sparrows then? Society collapses, that's what. I'd be doing the city a favor."

The fish-smile surfaced again, stayed a little longer this time.

"All right," Sable said. "Thursday. After I finish Odile's order. Bring your own knife."

"I don't own a knife."

"Then bring yourself. We'll find you one."

* * *

Pip left well after dark, out the back way Sable showed him, through the yard where Odile's paper-burning had left a drift of ash against the wall like dirty snow. Before he went, he paused at the door, hand on the frame, and almost said the thing about the list. Almost said the thing about the guildhouse. Almost said *thank you*, which was the hardest word in his whole stolen vocabulary.

What he said was: "Don't stay too late. Herons hunt at dawn."

Sable nodded, already turning back to his bench, to his sparrow, to whatever gray thing lived under the ice of his face. "Go on, then. And Pip—"

Pip waited.

"Thank you," Sable said. "For the warning."

Pip grinned, tipped an imaginary hat, and dropped into the alley like the gutter-rat he officially was. But the warmth followed him all the way down Vine Street and past the cooper's and up the ladder to his vinegar-smelling loft, and it was still there when he lay on his lousy mattress listening to the city breathe.

Because here was the thing Pip understood, lying in the dark with his stolen name and his empty pockets: the noose was tightening. He'd known it since the criers, felt it in the way the quarter flinched, seen it plain as paint in the heron-men's patient faces. Tomorrow or the day after, the net would come down on the shop, on the guild, on all of them. There'd be running, probably. Losing things, certainly. Maybe worse.

And he also understood—clearly, simply, the way he understood which drains flooded in rain—that when it came, he wouldn't run alone. He'd run toward them. Toward the bone-carver with the expensive face and the wrapped secret on his bench. Toward the guild of impossible things and the people inside it who, without ever quite meaning to, had started becoming the closest thing to family a name-thief could claim.

He fell asleep planning escape routes he hoped he'd never use, and woke before dawn to shouting in the street below, and the smell of smoke that was not a chimney's smoke, and the certain knowledge, cold in his belly, that the dawn the herons had been waiting for had finally come.

By the time he reached the guildhouse, running flat-out through streets gone strange and silent, Inquisitor Maren Vale was already standing in its doorway.
