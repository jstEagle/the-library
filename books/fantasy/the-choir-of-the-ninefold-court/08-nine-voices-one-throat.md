## 8. Nine Voices, One Throat

The vault below the Vellum Guild did not have a lock. It had a mood.

Sable had learned this in his first year under Odile's tutelage, when he'd tried to pick the vault door with a bent nail and a great deal of confidence and found the mechanism rearranging itself faster than he could read it. Odile had watched him struggle for a quarter hour before saying, mildly, "It opens for patience, not cleverness. Most things do." She'd laid her palm flat against the iron and the door had sighed open like an old man settling into a chair.

Tonight it opened for him too, though slower than usual, as if the door itself were reluctant about what waited on the other side.

He told himself he was doing inventory. That was the lie he'd chosen, and like most good lies it was built on a frame of truth: the guild's ledgers did need reconciling, and the Gift-Hunt proclamation had made every entry in them a potential confession, and someone—Odile, most likely, in her quiet ferocious way—would need to know what could be burned and what had to be smuggled out through the Undercroft before the Inquisitors came knocking. It was responsible work. Necessary work. Work that happened to require him to stand within arm's reach of the relic he'd carved seven years ago and had not touched since the night he'd heard it sing.

The lamp guttered as he descended the last stair. The vault was a long room of shelved stone, cool as a cellar, organized by Odile's taxonomy of the impossible: volatile things near the door, docile things deep in, and along the far wall, behind a curtain of grey silk, the things nobody wanted to name aloud. Bottled laughter that had gone sour in its jar. A saint's knucklebone wrapped in three layers of wool because even secondhand holiness kept people awake at night. The door to nowhere, folded flat like a sheet of vellum, humming faintly when the moon was full.

And on a shelf of black basalt, in a box lined with ash-wood, the ninefold reliquary.

Sable set his lamp down at the far end of the room. He took out his ledger. He uncapped his ink. He began at the door and worked inward, cataloguing with the mechanical precision that had made him valuable to the guild long before anyone knew what else he was worth. *One jar, laughter, spoiled. One door, destination null. One knucklebone, provenance disputed.* His handwriting was beautiful. He took a small, ashamed comfort in that—the one part of himself he displayed openly, the only flourish he permitted.

It took him two hours to reach the grey curtain.

He stood before it longer than he stood over any shelf, ledger hanging forgotten from his hand. Through the silk he could see nothing, but he could feel it—that pressure behind the ears, like descending into deep water, like standing too close to a struck bell. Seven years ago he had been a journeyman carver with more talent than sense, and Odile had handed him a lump of material and said, *Carve what it asks to be.* He hadn't known then what the material was. He still didn't. Bone, certainly, but bone of what animal—or what age, or what manner of death—he had never determined. He only knew that his knives had moved as if guided, that the shape had emerged from the block already ancient, a reliquary no larger than a heart, faceted like a cut gem, nine faces to a point. And when he'd brushed the shavings away and held it up to the light, he had heard—

Not a voice. Voices. Nine of them, layered like bells rung in a tower, each one distinct and all of them speaking at once, and beneath the words something vast turning over in its sleep.

He had dropped it. Odile had picked it up without flinching—which should have told him something—and locked it away, and neither of them had spoken of it since. That was the guild's way. You recorded the impossible thing, you shelved the impossible thing, you went home and ate your supper and did not ask questions. Odile had once told him that curiosity was the only debt the guild never forgave.

But the proclamation had changed the arithmetic of everything. Three days now since the criers, and the city had gone quiet in the specific way of a held breath. And Sable had spent those three days doing sums he didn't want to do: if the Inquisitors came, if they dragged the vault, they would find the reliquary, and whatever the Court was—and he knew, now, from the way the word sat in his mouth, that it was a *Court*—it would not stay shelved forever. Better to understand the danger than to die of it ignorant.

That was the second lie. Or perhaps just the first one wearing better clothes.

He drew back the grey silk.

The reliquary sat in its ash-wood box, small and patient, catching the lamplight along its nine facets. Up close, in the quiet, it looked almost humble. A carving. His carving. He had signed nothing in those days—apprentices didn't sign—but he could find his own hand in it the way a mother finds her child in a crowd: the particular bevel of the facets, the way the point tapered to a edge fine enough to draw blood.

"Don't," he said aloud, to himself, to the room. His voice sounded thin in the stone silence.

He touched it anyway.

* * *

The bells began before his skin finished registering the cold of the bone.

They came up through his palm, through the marrow of his arm, into the architecture of his skull, and there they arranged themselves—not sound exactly, not anymore, but the memory of sound, the way a bell keeps ringing in the metal after the air goes still. Nine tones. He had spent seven years trying to forget their intervals and failed completely; they rose in him like water finding its old channels.

*Carver,* said the first voice, and it was a woman's voice, or had been once, worn smooth as river stone by centuries of use. *You came back.*

"I came to take inventory," Sable said. His own voice sounded absurdly small. He was aware of himself kneeling on the vault floor without any memory of kneeling, the reliquary cradled in both hands like something rescued from a fire. "I'm recording what's here. For the ledgers."

*Ledgers,* said a second voice, deeper, amused, a man who had clearly enjoyed being alive and resented stopping. *He keeps accounts. Oh, that's rich. We ruled kingdoms and our last servant is a bookkeeper.*

"We are not servants," said a third, cold and precise, the voice of someone accustomed to being obeyed mid-sentence. "We are the Ninefold Court, and this one is ours. He shaped our house. He has been ours since the first knife-stroke."

"I'm no one's." Sable's hands were steady, which surprised him. His craft lived in his hands and apparently his fear had not reached them yet. "I carved a box. I didn't build a throne room."

*No?* The smooth voice again, gentle now, the voice of a grandmother at a bedside. *Look, then. Look at what you built.*

The vault dissolved.

He was standing on a terrace of white stone above a city so beautiful it hurt—a city of spires and hanging gardens, canals bright as poured silver, bridges arched like drawn bows. Banners moved in a warm wind. The banners bore nine sigils, and he knew them the way he knew his own knuckles: the crowned stag, the sunken crown, the weeping star, the six others he could not have named a moment ago and now could not unknow. People filled the streets below, thousands upon thousands, and every face was turned upward toward the terrace, toward the nine thrones, toward—

Toward him. The vision put him where a sovereign would stand, and the adoration of a hundred thousand souls broke over him like surf, and it was the most terrible thing he had ever felt, because some starving cellar of his heart drank it in and asked for more.

"No." He wrenched himself free of it, gasping, the vault reassembling around him in cold stone and lamplight. His knees ached against the floor. The reliquary hummed in his grip, satisfied. "That's not real. That was a hundred years ago, if it was ever anything at all."

*Time is a door,* said the cold voice, *and doors open both ways. What we show you is true. What you choose to believe is your own affair—we learned long ago that belief cannot be commanded. Only invited.*

"Then hear my refusal."

*Hear our offer first.* The smooth voice again, and now the gentleness in it had edges, the way kindness does when it has been rehearsed. *You are afraid, little carver. We felt it come off you like heat. The criers in the square. The pyres being built in the yard of the Inquisition. Your gift is a death sentence now, and you have spent your whole life hiding it, and hiding is such wearying work.*

Sable said nothing. The silence admitted everything.

*We know names,* the voice continued, soft as snowfall. *Every gifted soul in this city, every one who hides, every one who pretends. The woman who reads fortunes in spilled tea and calls it intuition. The mason whose walls never crack because the stones love him. The child in the tanner's row who talks to rats, and the rats listen. We have listened to this city for a century through a hundred thousand bones buried in its foundations. Ask us, and we will give you every name. Every hiding place. Every soul the Inquisitors would burn.*

"Why," Sable said carefully, "would you give me that?"

*So you can save them.*

The words landed in the vault like coins dropped down a well. He heard them fall and fall and never touch bottom.

"You want me to gather them," he said slowly. "The gifted. Bring them together. To you."

*We want them safe,* said the smooth voice, wounded. *Is that so monstrous? We were sovereigns. Keeping people safe was our whole office, once. And you—you sit in your little shop carving charms for grieving widows, taking their coin, hearing their dead complain about the weather, and you tell yourself it is nothing, it is small, it is honest work. But you know, don't you, that you could be more. That the gift in you is the same gift that was in us. That a vessel exists to be filled.*

The vision came again, unbidden, and this time it was not the beautiful city. It was the war. Fire on the horizon in a ring, the sky the color of a wound, armies moving like dark water across burning fields—and through it all, threaded under the roar, the same nine voices singing, and the song was a command, and the fire obeyed it, and half the world went to ash in time to music.

Then the vision shifted, softened, rewove itself: the same burning fields going green again, the same armies laying down arms, the same nine voices singing healing instead of ruin. *Or this,* the voices murmured, overlapping now, harmonizing, gorgeous. *The same power, carver. It depends entirely on the throat it passes through. Ours burned the world because we were betrayed by it. Yours could mend it. All you must do is stop pretending to be small.*

Sable became aware that he was crying. Not dramatically—just tears sliding down his face in the cold, his body responding to something his mind was refusing. Because the terrible thing, the thing he would never say aloud to any living soul, was that the temptation worked. Not the power. He had watched power all his life from the margins of the guild, watched what it did to buyers and sellers of impossible things, and he had no illusions about his own immunity. No—the temptation was simpler and far worse.

*Every name. Every hiding place.*

Pip. Seventeen years old, all elbows and stolen swagger, who slept in the loft above the workshop and talked in his sleep to someone named Marrow who was either an imaginary friend or something considerably less imaginary. Pip, who had a knack Sable had noticed months ago and politely never mentioned: locks opened for Pip. Doors, windows, padlocks, the vault itself on one memorable occasion—all of them sighed open like the boy was someone's favorite nephew. Small magic. Harmless magic. The kind the proclamation called *traffic with unclean forces*, punishable by registration, branding, or fire, at the Inquisitor's discretion.

And Mrs. Halloway on Tallow Street, who left milk out for the ghost of her son and swore the level in the bottle went down. And the ferryman who never lost a passenger in forty years because the drowned told him where the snags were. And Odile—

Odile, who had picked up the singing reliquary bare-handed without flinching. Who had known, somehow, what Sable was before he'd ever spoken of it. Whose past the guild records described in a careful, deliberate blankness, forty years of it, like a page torn out.

*You see,* the voices murmured, reading him the way he read bone, *you see how many you love. And how easily they burn. Pyres are not choosy, carver. They take the guilty and the kind in the same flame. But with our knowledge—*

"With your knowledge I'd be leading them to you," Sable said. His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "A gathered choir. That's what you want. That's what you've always wanted. Not safety. Congregation."

The silence that followed had texture. He had the distinct sense of nine ancient intelligences recalibrating, the way a card sharp recuts the deck.

*You misjudge us,* said the cold one finally.

"I misjudge nothing. I keep ledgers." He got to his feet, legs unsteady, and set the reliquary back in its ash-wood box with exaggerated care, the way you handle a snake you cannot afford to startle. "I record what is given and what is taken. And here is what I've recorded tonight: nine sovereigns, bound a century ago by people who had good reason, offering a frightened man a list of victims dressed up as a list of the saved. That entry goes in the book."

*The book will burn,* said the deep voice, almost kindly. *Everything burns, little carver. It is the one law we never managed to repeal. The only question the fire asks is what you were holding when it came for you.*

Sable closed the box. Closed the lid. Drew the grey silk across the shelf, and the pressure behind his ears eased but did not vanish—it never fully vanished anymore, he realized; it had been building for three days, since the proclamation, since the criers, since the city began holding its breath. As if the reliquary could feel the weather changing too. As if it were waking.

He climbed the stairs on legs that felt borrowed. At the top, he paused with his hand on the iron door and looked back down into the dark, and heard—one last time, faint as a bell heard across a valley, or perhaps only imagined, which amounted to the same thing—

*When you are ready, carver. We have waited a century. We can wait out your fear.*

* * *

Upstairs, the workshop was dark except for the banked coals of the forge, and the loft ladder stood where it always stood, and from the loft came the small sounds of Pip sleeping—murmuring, restless, saying a name under his breath that Sable chose, as always, not to hear.

Sable sat at his bench and did not light a lamp. He took out the guild ledger, the true one, the one Odile would need if the worst came, and he wrote by coal-light, his beautiful handwriting steadier than his hands:

*Ninefold reliquary: active. Communicant. Offers intelligence regarding persons of interest to the Crown's Inquisition. Assessed intent: recruitment. Do not trade. Do not touch. Do not listen.*

He stared at the last line a long moment. Then, because he was honest in his ledgers if nowhere else, he added:

*I listened.*

He was blotting the page when he heard it—faint, from the direction of the street, carried on the cold night air: hoofbeats. Not one rider. Several, moving at a walk, unhurried, the pace of men who owned the night and knew it. They passed the front of the shop without stopping.

They stopped at the corner. And stayed there.

Sable blew out the coals' glow with a breath, sat motionless in the dark, and counted the seconds between his heartbeat and theirs, and understood that whatever he decided about the nine voices in the bone, the decision was about to be taken out of his hands—because the Inquisition had not come for the relic.

They had come for everyone in the building that kept it.
