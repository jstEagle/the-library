## 9. A Door Kicked In

The bells woke me.

Not the cathedral bells, which rang the hour with the dull obedience of hired men, but the other bells—the ones that had been murmuring at the edge of my hearing for days now, ever since I'd stood in the vault with the ninefold relic cupped in my palms like a bird with a broken neck. They had been singing since. Softly. Patiently. The way a spider hums to the thing caught in its web, if spiders had the courtesy to hum.

I sat at my workbench in the back room of the Vellum Guild, a mourning charm half-carved under my knife, and I did not answer them. That was the discipline of it. That was the whole of my life's discipline, distilled: *do not answer*.

The charm was for a fisherman's daughter whose father had drowned off the Weir Stones. She'd brought me his fingerbones in a twist of oilcloth, because that was what you did, in this city, when the Gift-Hunt hadn't yet reached your street. *Keep him close. Keep him quiet. Keep him from knocking on the inside of my skull at three in the morning.* I'd taken the commission because refusing would have raised questions, and questions were the one commodity the guild never traded in.

"He's going on about the nets," I murmured to the bones, because the dead man's voice had been leaking out of them all morning, aggrieved and damp. "The nets were left out. I know. I'll tell her."

The nets were *always* left out. The dead were a narrow people. Give them eternity and they'd spend it cataloguing their grievances.

Outside, Greyharbor Street went about its morning. Cart wheels. A fishwife arguing with nobody in particular. The particular silence of a city that had learned, in the space of a single proclamation, to lower its voice. Three weeks since the criers. Three weeks since the word *register* had entered the language like a splinter. Odile had burned papers for two days straight after that—the guild's true ledgers, the ones in my hand, the ones that named names—and the ash had come down on the rooftops like grey snow, and the fishwife had swept it into her gutter without comment, because everyone in Greyharbor understood ash now.

I was reaching for the burin to cut the charm's binding groove when the fishwife stopped arguing.

That was the first wrongness. Not a sound—a *cessation*. Her voice cut off mid-syllable, the way a candle cuts off when pinched, and the street held its breath, and I sat very still with a burin in my hand and listened to the absence.

Then the boots started. Many boots. In step.

I was moving before I'd finished deciding to move. That's what Odile had drilled into me in my first year, back when I was seventeen and careless and she'd caught me humming along to a dead man's voice in the front room with the shutters open. *Sable. When the knocking comes, you will not pack. You will not bargain. You will walk to the vault door as if you own the building, because hesitation is the only thing they can convict you of faster than the gift itself.* She'd made me rehearse it. I had thought, at seventeen, that she was being theatrical.

The front door of the guild came off its hinges with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

* * *

They came in fast and organized, which told me this wasn't a rumor-driven mob or a greedy neighbor's tip-off. This was the Inquisition proper, and they'd done their reading. Four through the front in grey-and-silver, two more already at the back—I heard their boots on the kitchen flags, the kitchen door that led to the alley, the door I'd have chosen. They'd closed the circle before I'd taken three steps.

I did what the dead do. I went still, and I listened.

"By authority of the Crown and the Office of Inquiry," a voice said from the front room, level and unhurried, a voice that had said these words enough times to wear them smooth, "this premises is seized for examination. Touch nothing. Speak nothing. The Gift-Hunt is not a discussion."

Maren Vale. I hadn't heard her before, but I knew it was her, the way you know a storm by the pressure in your teeth. The Inquisitor who'd come up the coast leaving a wake of quiet verdicts—no pyres in her column, the whispers said, just confessions, just people walking into the Inquisition house and not coming out the same. The whispers couldn't decide if that made her merciful or worse.

Odile's voice answered from the front room, dry as old vellum. "You're standing on a mosaic worth more than your commission, Inquisitor. Mind the third tile."

"Madam Vess."

"Odile. We've corresponded."

I pressed myself against the vault door in the back room, my heart going like a trapped bird, and the dead man's fingerbones chattered in their dish behind me, excited, wanting to know what was happening, wanting to be part of everything. I put my hand over them. *Quiet. Please. For once in your damp eternity, quiet.*

"We have a warrant," Vale said. "Signed this morning. It names this guild as a clearing-house for unregistered traffic with the dead."

"How flattering. And does your warrant name me?"

"It names you as custodian. It does not name you as gifted."

"Because I'm not."

"No," Vale said, and I heard something in her voice then, a hairline fracture under the smoothness, "I don't believe you are. Which is interesting, Madam Vess—Odile—because my information says you employ someone who is."

The silence that followed had weight. I could feel Odile assembling it, brick by brick, the way she assembled everything: the pause of a woman considering a bad offer at market.

"You have bad information," she said.

"I have a description. A carver. Young. Works the back room. Left-handed."

My right hand was pressed flat against the vault door. My left held the burin. I looked at it—at the tool, at the fine bone dust on my sleeve—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like the nine voices' cold lucidity, that I had been described. That somewhere in the machinery of the Inquisition there was a page with a sketch on it, and the sketch was me, and it had been there for days.

"Then your information is a week old," Odile said, "and the young man in question sold his tools and took ship for Corvessy. I have the receipt. I keep receipts for everything. It's a disease."

"Show me the back room."

"Show me the signature on the warrant first. I knew a forger in Corvessy who'd have charged you double for that hand."

A pause. Then boots—hers, alone, the others held back by some command I didn't catch—and the door to the back room opened, and Maren Vale walked in.

* * *

She was smaller than the whispers had made her. Grey-eyed, plain-dressed, with the kind of face that gets called forgettable by people who then find themselves unable to stop thinking about it. Her gaze went across the room the way a hand goes across a shelf—inventorying, weighing—and I watched it pass over the workbench, the dish of fingerbones, the half-carved charm.

It did not pass over me. There was nowhere for it to pass. I was standing in the middle of the back room like an idiot, because the vault door behind me required a key, and the key was around Odile's neck, and I had rehearsed everything except the part where I'd be caught with my hand on a locked door.

Vale looked at me for a long moment.

"You're not the carver," she said.

"No," I said, which was true, in the way that everything I was about to say would be technically true. "I'm the ledger-keeper."

"The ledger-keeper." Her eyes went to my left hand, to the burin still in it. "Your ledger-keeper carves?"

"Charms. Mourning-work. The guild sells what people will buy, Inquisitor, and grieving people will buy anything. It's not traffic with the dead. It's traffic with the bereaved. The dead contribute nothing."

Something moved behind her eyes—there and gone, a fish turning under ice. "That's a very practiced answer."

"I've given it before."

"To whom?"

"To the last Inquisitor who came through Greyharbor with a warrant. Inquisitor Hask. You'd have been a girl."

"I was never a girl," she said, absently, in the tone of a person correcting a small error in a large ledger, and then her gaze sharpened and came back to me like a hawk remembering its business. "Open the vault."

"I don't have the key."

"I know. Your mistress does." She raised her voice without turning her head. "Madam Vess. The vault, please."

Odile came through the doorway with the unhurried tread of a woman entering her own kitchen. She looked at Vale, then at me, and her face did nothing at all, which from Odile was a speech. *Steady. I have this. You have your part.*

"The vault contains guild inventory," she said. "Bottled laughter. A door to nowhere. A saint's knucklebone. None of it hears you, Inquisitor, and none of it will answer. But if the Crown wants to pay for the privilege of looking, the Crown may look."

"The Crown doesn't pay," Vale said. "The Crown confiscates." She stepped aside. "Open it."

Odile drew the key from her bodice, and as she did, her fingers brushed the chain, and I saw—only because I had spent eleven years learning to read her hands the way other men read faces—the smallest gesture. Two fingers, then three. A counting. A signal I'd never been taught but understood instantly, because Odile had spent eleven years teaching me without teaching me.

*Two, then three. The second shelf. The third door.*

The impossible door. The one in the vault's back wall that looked like shelving, the one that opened, when it chose to, onto places the guild's founder had never explained and Odile had never asked about. The one that only worked for people the guild trusted, which was, in eleven years, Odile and me.

She was going to open the vault. She was going to let them search it. And somewhere in that search, if the door was willing, I was going to be on the other side of it.

"Odile," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, and I hated myself for it.

"Hush, Sable. Adults are conducting an inventory." But her eyes, when they flicked to mine, were not dry at all. They were the eyes of a woman standing at the edge of a thing she had decided on, looking back once, briefly, the way you look back at a house you're leaving burning behind you on purpose.

She unlocked the vault.

* * *

What followed was the longest hour of my life, and I include the hour I spent holding a dead man's memories in my palms while nine sovereigns sang to me from a relic I had carved with my own ignorant hands.

Vale searched the way she spoke—thoroughly, unhurriedly, without malice and without mercy. Her soldiers brought lamps. She went shelf by shelf, item by item, lifting the bottled laughter and holding it to the light as if she could see the laughter inside, setting down the saint's knucklebone with a strange gentleness, standing for a long time in front of the door to nowhere, which looked, today, like an ordinary door painted on a wall.

"Does it open?" she asked.

"It hasn't in years," Odile said. "It opens when it likes. Guild founder's whimsy."

"And the founder?"

"Died. Eleven years ago. Ask your records."

"I have," Vale said. "The record says he died of a fever. The record also says this guild has three employees. I count two."

"The third is the boy who runs messages. Pip. He's out."

"Where?"

"Being a boy. Somewhere with better sense than this street."

I stood by the workbench and kept my hands busy and my face empty, and all the while the second shelf and the third door waited behind me like a held breath. The dead man's fingerbones had finally gone quiet, which was the only mercy of the morning. And under everything, so faint I could pretend I imagined it, the ninefold relic sang from its place on the second shelf, third door's shadow, wrapped in oilcloth where I'd hidden it three days ago in the one place I'd judged even Odile wouldn't look without reason.

I had hidden it from her. I understood that now, standing in the vault with the Inquisition between me and it. I had hidden it because the nine had asked me to, in the night, in the voice of a dead friend, in the voice of my mother, in voices I had loved, and I had told myself it was prudence and it was nothing of the kind. It was the first thread of the web, and I was already in it, and Odile knew—Odile had known when she looked at me and said *hush*—and she was going to pay for my thread.

"Here." Vale's hand stopped on the second shelf. Not the relic—she'd stopped a hand's width from it, at a jar of pickled something-or-other, guild inventory, worthless. "What's this?"

"Pickled figs," Odile said. "Guildmaster's weakness. Would you like the receipt?"

"I'd like you to stop offering me receipts."

"I'd like you to stop touching my figs."

And it was absurd, it was *absurd*, two women fencing over a jar while soldiers held lamps, and I let the absurdity carry me, because that was the last lesson Odile had left lying out for me: when the moment comes, be boring. Be furniture. Be the ledger-keeper with the burin.

The third door was behind me. I had eased back along the wall one shuffling step at a time, and Vale's people were watching Odile, because Odile was the performance, Odile had always been the performance, and I was the understudy nobody had listed in the program.

The door to nowhere, the soldiers said, was painted on. The third door was not painted on. The third door was a shelf unit, and when I put my palm flat against its middle panel and thought, as clearly as I had ever thought anything, *guild*, the panel went soft as skin under my hand.

Vale turned.

I will give her this: she was fast. Faster than the soldiers, faster than sense. She was already moving, hand going to the short baton at her belt, her mouth opening around my name—*the ledger-keeper, stop him, the ledger-keeper*—and Odile moved too.

I have replayed it a thousand times since. I will replay it a thousand more. Odile stepped into the aisle between Vale and me, into the space a small grey-eyed woman would need to cross to reach me, and she did not run, and she did not shout. She picked up the jar of pickled figs and threw it.

It caught Vale across the temple—not hard, not damaging, Odile's arm was seventy years old—but it was figs and brine and glass, and it was *undignified*, and it was the single most Odile thing I have ever witnessed, and it bought me two seconds. Two seconds is a long time when a door loves you.

I went through sideways, the way you go through a crowd, shoulder first, and the panel closed behind me like water closing over a stone, and the last thing I saw was Odile Vess standing in her vault with brine dripping off her chin, looking at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in eleven years.

It was not fear. It was not anger.

It was *go*, written in a woman's whole face, and then the door took it away from me.

* * *

The other side of the impossible door was the Undercroft's upper warren, the smugglers' crawl that smelled of tallow and rat and old rain, and I came through it at a stumble and caught myself against wet brick and was sick, because my body is an honest animal even when the rest of me is performing.

I lay against the wall in the dark and listened.

That's the curse of me, the whole of the curse: even fleeing for my life, I listened. The door behind me had gone silent—no voices, no boots, the guild sealed away as if it had never existed. The crawl stretched ahead into blackness. And beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the ninefold relic sang on the second shelf, third door, in a vault that now belonged to the Inquisition of the Crown of Vail.

*Come back for us,* the nine sang. Not in words. In bells. *You left us with her. Come back.*

"She's not *with* you," I said aloud to the dark, and my voice came out broken. "She's in a cell because of you. Because of me."

The bells did not answer, which was their way. The nine had never once answered a direct question. They only ever offered.

I made myself stand. I made myself walk. Pip's name was a stone in my throat the whole way down—Pip, who was supposed to be *out*, who was supposed to be somewhere with better sense, who slept three nights in four in the guild's attic because Odile pretended not to know and fed him anyway. If the soldiers had come at dawn, Pip would have been in the attic. Pip would have come down the attic ladder into four drawn blades, and Pip was fifteen and fast but not *that* fast.

I walked the crawl for an hour, maybe two, following the tallow-marks Odile had taught me in the first year—*always the tallow, Sable, the guild's founder was a smuggler before he was a saint*—and I came up at last through a coal chute into the grey afternoon behind the Rope-Walk, and I went to the places we'd agreed on, the three places, and I waited at each one past the time we'd agreed on, and no one came.

No Pip. No Brannoc—Brannoc who wasn't ours yet, who owed us nothing, who'd carried Odile's crates for coin and nothing more. No one. The family, if it had ever been a family, was scattered like ash on the wind Odile had burned the ledgers into, and I had put the wind to the paper myself.

I sat on the coal-black steps behind the Rope-Walk until the light went, and the dead of the city murmured their small grievances around me—the drowned man's nets, a grandmother's unswept hearth, a soldier's unpaid wage—and for the first time in my life I hated the sound of them. Not because they were cruel. Because they were *there*, and the living voices I wanted were not.

* * *

I found Pip at midnight, or Pip found me, which is the more accurate telling. I was buying a heel of bread from the all-night stall at Cooper's Stair with Odile's emergency coin, the coin sewn into my coat lining for a day I'd never let myself imagine, and a small shape detached itself from the shadow of the stair and said, in a voice roughened by trying not to shake:

"You're meant to be dead or gone."

"Pip—"

"I *watched*." He came into the lamplight, and his face was filthy and his eyes were too bright, and there was a bruise coming up along his jaw like a storm map. "I was on the roof across the street when they came. I watched them take her out in irons, Sable. I watched her *walk*. She told them her name like she was doing them a favor. And I lay flat on the slates with my heart going like a rabbit and I did *nothing*, because she'd told me—she'd left a note under my blanket, she must've known, she must've *known* days ago—the note said *if the grey men come, find Sable, tell him the door remembers him, tell him don't come back for me*."

The bread was in my hand and I couldn't feel it.

"She left a note," I said.

"Under my blanket. Days old. Days, Sable." His voice cracked clean through. "She knew. She *knew* and she sent you out the door and she walked into the wagon and she made it look like she was doing them a favor, and I lay on a roof and watched, and you were *inside*—" He stopped. He wiped his face with his sleeve, hard, once, the way boys wipe their faces when crying is a luxury with a price. "Where do we go?"

And there it was. The question, laid in my hands like the fingerbones in their dish. *Where do we go*—not *what do we do*, not *how do we get her back*, though both of those lived inside it. Pip was fifteen and had been sold by his own mother for a bottle and had learned before he could write that survival is a place before it is a plan.

"The Undercroft," I said. "Brannoc's warren. The crawl behind the Rope-Walk."

"He hates us."

"He tolerates us. It's a start."

"He'll want paying."

"Then we'll pay him." I looked down the dark length of Cooper's Stair, toward the river, toward the Inquisition house where Odile Vess was spending her first night in a cell in seventy years of never once having been caught. The bells sang faintly under the city, patient as tide. *Come back for us. Come back for us.* And beneath them, quieter, a voice I knew was not real and could not stop hearing: Odile's voice, dry as vellum. *Hush, Sable. Adults are conducting an inventory.*

"Pip," I said. "The relic. They have the relic."

He looked at me. He was fifteen and hungry and brave in the way that gets children killed, and he was the only person in the world I had left.

"Then we'd better get it back," he said, "before they work out how to listen."

We went down the stair together into the dark, and behind us the city bells rang the hour, and under the bells, under everything, the nine sang on.
