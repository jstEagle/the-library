## 10. Underneath the City

The stair went down farther than any stair in Ashfall had a right to. I counted forty steps before the light from the trapdoor above died to a gray coin, sixty before Pip's hand found my sleeve and held on, and at eighty we were walking through darkness so complete it felt like a substance, like wading through cold ink.

"Keep your left hand on the wall," Pip whispered. "Brannoc says the wall remembers the way even when you don't."

"Does Brannoc say much else?"

"He says shut up and keep your left hand on the wall."

The wall was slick with condensation and older than anything it touched—stone dressed by hands that had never heard of the Vellum Guild or the crown or the Gift-Hunt, stone that had probably been old when the Ninefold Court was young. My fingers read it as we descended: chisel marks, rope-burn grooves, a niche where someone had once set a candle for so many years that the wax had built up in layers like the rings of a tree. The dead had nothing to say down here. That was the first strange mercy of the Undercroft. Whatever ran beneath this city ran deeper than ghosts, and even the voices in my skull kept their distance from it—all except the nine, who did not keep their distance from anything.

*Deeper,* they murmured, layered like bells struck out of time with one another. *You are going deeper. Good. We were made deep.*

I did not answer them. I had learned, in the two days since the door came down, that answering was the first step of a long staircase, and I had already gone down enough stairs.

The dark ended the way tides end—not all at once but by degrees, black going to brown going to amber, until I could see Pip's face beside me, smudged and sharp-boned and watching everything, and beyond him a corridor of rough-hewn rock hung with lanterns of green glass. The light smelled of tallow and river water. Somewhere ahead, voices. Somewhere ahead, people who had also run out of city.

"Stay behind me," Pip said, which would have been funnier if his voice hadn't cracked on *behind*, if he hadn't squared his shoulders like a man twice his height doing it. "And let me talk first. Brannoc doesn't like strangers talking first."

"Why not?"

"Because then he knows what they want." Pip glanced back at me, and in the green light his eyes looked older than fifteen. "He can always tell what people want. He says it's the only gift he ever needed."

We came out of the corridor into a cavern that took my breath and held it.

It was not a room; it was a wound in the world that someone had decided to live in. The ceiling arched up into darkness hung with chains and pulleys and the hulls of small boats stored upside down like beetles. Terraces of shelving climbed the walls, stacked with crates, barrels, bolts of cloth, cages of dozing pigeons, jars whose contents I chose not to inventory. Walkways crossed the space on stilts over a channel of black water that moved without wind, slow and patient, carrying its own private night toward some river I couldn't see. Lanterns everywhere, green glass mostly, so that the whole warren glowed like the inside of a bottle. And people—dozens of them, more than dozens: a woman mending a net, three men arguing over a ledger at a table scarred by knives, children chasing each other along a walkway with the particular silence of children who have learned that noise costs something.

Every one of them, I realized, was listening past us. Every conversation had bent slightly toward the corridor mouth, the way grass bends toward a footstep.

A big shape detached itself from the shadows near the water channel and came toward us, and I understood why Pip had wanted to speak first.

Brannoc was not tall so much as *built*, a man assembled from things meant to bear weight—shoulders like a yoke, forearms like mooring posts—and he moved with the flat economy of someone who had once been paid to move efficiently and had never unlearned it. His left sleeve was pinned and folded at the wrist with a soldier's neatness, the empty fabric tucked through a belt loop so it wouldn't swing. His right hand, as he stopped an arm's length away, rested on nothing in particular, which somehow was worse than if it had rested on a knife.

"Pip," he said. His voice was gravel poured slowly.

"Brannoc." Pip jerked a thumb at me. "This is Sable Galloway. He's the one I told you about. Bone-carver. Guild ledger-keeper. He's—"

"I know what he is." Brannoc's eyes were pale gray, the color of weather coming, and they went over me the way a quartermaster goes over a shipment: weight, condition, defects. They lingered, just briefly, on my ears. On my hands. "Inquisitors burned the sign off your guildhouse yesterday morning. By noon they'd put a price on your name. By evening, every informant between here and the Tallow Gate knew your face better than their mothers'." He said it without heat, a man reading a supply list. "And you brought that face down my stair."

"There wasn't anywhere else," Pip said.

"There's always somewhere else. Usually worse." Brannoc scratched his jaw with the stump-end of his pinned sleeve, an oddly domestic gesture. "Odile Vess vouched for you once. Years ago. A debt she called in for a crate of saint's fingers that never existed. I don't forget debts." He turned, not waiting to see if we'd follow, and spoke over his shoulder. "You eat first. Then we talk. Nobody negotiates hungry—it makes them honest, and honesty is expensive."

* * *

The food was barley stew with river fish in it, served on tin plates at a long table under the largest of the green lanterns, and it was the best thing I had ever tasted, because hunger is the finest chef in the world. Pip ate like a small storm. I ate like a man remembering, bite by bite, that his body still belonged to him.

Around us the Undercroft went on with its evening. I watched it the way I watched everything now—with one eye on the living and one ear cocked for the dead, though here the dead were quiet, muffled, as if the deep stone drank their whispers. It should have been restful. Instead it left me alone with the nine, who filled the silence the way water fills a footprint.

*The carver is frightened,* said one voice, low and amused, a woman's voice worn smooth as river stone. *Frightened men make careful vessels.*

*Let him be frightened,* said another, deeper, patient as bedrock. *Fear is a door. We have always entered through fear.*

*I am not a vessel,* I said inside my skull, and immediately hated myself for answering.

Silence, then—a silken, satisfied silence, the silence of fishermen feeling a tug on the line.

"You're doing the face," Pip said quietly, around a mouthful of barley.

"What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with somebody who isn't there. You did it in the vault too, before—" He stopped himself. He was good at stopping himself; street kids learn early which sentences cost blood. He pushed a heel of bread across the table at me instead. "Eat. Argue after."

I ate. And I studied the Undercroft while I did, because studying things is the only prayer I know.

It was a city under the city, and like every city it had laws. I saw them operating in small ways: the way no one walked close to the water channel's edge without looking down first; the way the children, when a bell rang somewhere far above—a real bell, the cathedral's, thin as thread at this depth—all of them paused, counted, and resumed only when it finished. Counting bells. Knowing how many strokes meant safe and how many meant someone had come down the stair uninvited. These people had built an entire grammar out of a sound the rest of the city barely heard.

And threaded through it all, moving like a current, was Brannoc. He passed among the terraces with his plate untouched, saying a word here, taking a report there, settling a dispute between two dock-thieves with four sentences and a look. One-handed, and still every eye in the place checked itself against his. I had known soldiers in the guild's ledgers—mercenaries selling their swords as impossible goods, veterans trading medals for bread—and they all had a certain smell to them, gun-oil and old smoke. Brannoc had it worst of anyone. He smelled like a war that hadn't finished with him.

When the plates were cleared, he sat down across from us, and the table seemed to shrink.

"Now," he said. "Tell me what happened at the guild. All of it. Leave nothing out, because I will hear the parts you leave out anyway, from other mouths, and then I'll wonder why you bothered lying."

So I told him. The criers and the proclamation. The vault, the knucklebone, the nine voices. Odile burning papers by lamplight with her sleeves rolled up like a laundress. The door kicked in—the splintering of it still lived in my spine—and Odile turning to me with her chin up and her eyes furious and afraid, saying *the vault door, boy, the impossible door, go*, and me going, because she made it an order, because she knew I'd stay and die uselessly otherwise. The price on our names. The relic.

"The relic," Brannoc repeated, when I reached it. "The ninefold piece. You carved it?"

"Years ago. Before I knew what it was. It came through the guild as raw material—old bone, provenance unknown. I carved what was in it. That's how my gift works. I don't choose what the dead put in the bone. I just... make room."

"And now the Inquisition has it."

"That's what I believe." I turned the tin plate a quarter-turn under my fingers. "There's a chance Odile moved it before the raid. She was always moving things. But I can't know, and not knowing is—" I caught myself. "It's bad."

"It's worse than bad." Brannoc leaned forward, and the green lantern-light slid across his face and made the scars there stand out like channels on a map. "Do you understand what the Inquisition does with relics? They don't destroy them, whatever their proclamations say. Destruction is waste. They *use* them. A bone that holds nine sovereigns of the Old Court isn't evidence, carver. It's a weapon aimed at every gifted soul in this country. It's proof the hunt was justified. It's a reason to burn hotter." His gray eyes didn't blink. "And if any part of those nine can still speak—if they can whisper into the dreams of whoever guards them—then your Inquisitors are sleeping next to a lit fuse and calling it a trophy."

The nine stirred at that, and I felt them like pressure behind my eyes.

*He speaks of fuses,* said the smooth woman's voice. *We speak of crowns. Ask him which lasted longer.*

I pressed my thumbnail hard into the pad of my finger until the voice receded.

"There's something else," I said. "Something you should hear from me before you hear it from anyone else. The Court offered me things. Knowledge. The location of every hidden gifted person in Ashfall—including people in this room, if any are. I haven't told them yes. I won't tell them yes. But they don't stop offering, and I thought you deserved to know what's sitting at your table."

For a long moment Brannoc said nothing. Around us the Undercroft's life flowed on, oblivious, and I was aware of how loud my confession must have sounded against all that quiet industry.

Then he laughed. It was a short, harsh sound with no joy in it, a laugh like a door slamming.

"You think that frightens me?" He laid his right forearm on the table, and pulled back the cuff, and showed me the inside of his wrist: a brand, old and white, the mark of a deserter—or of something else. "Twenty-two years ago I marched with the Third Crown Regiment into the Veywater hills, and my orders concerned people exactly like you. People who heard things in bones and stones and rivers. I was twenty years old and I believed my orders were clean." His voice stayed level, which was somehow more terrible than if it had broken. "I lost the hand at Veywater. I lost the cause the same day, when I finally asked what we were burning and got an honest answer. So no, carver. Voices in your head don't frighten me. I've spent two decades listening to the ones in mine."

He pulled the cuff back down.

"You'll work," he said. "Both of you. Pip runs messages—you know the ways, you've earned that. The carver..." He considered me. "You'll carve. There's folk down here with dead they can't bury properly—no priests come below, no consecrated ground. Charms, mourning pieces. You'll take no payment but food and silence about what you hear. Can you carve without listening when it matters?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I can listen without telling. That's most of the craft anyway."

Something that was almost approval moved through his face and was gone.

"One rule above all others." He stood, and the lantern-light threw his shadow huge across the shelving behind him, one-armed and mountain-shaped. "No one goes up. Not for bread, not for news, not for love. The city above is a mouth right now, and it is closing. When it opens again, I'll tell you." He paused at the edge of the lantern-light. "And carver—if those nine start singing louder, if they start making you promises about doors and vessels and saving anyone—you come to me before you decide anything. Not after. I've seen what comes after."

He walked away into the green gloom, and the Undercroft closed around him like water around a dropped stone.

* * *

Pip found us a corner to sleep in—an alcove behind a rack of pigeon cages, warm with feathers and grain-smell, soft with old sailcloth. He arranged his territory with the quick territorial precision of a cat, claimed the inner wall, and was asleep almost before he finished lying down, the way the young sleep and the hunted sleep, suddenly and totally, as if sleep were a door he could slam.

I lay awake and listened to the Undercroft breathe.

Water moving in its channel. Pigeons shifting. Someone laughing softly two terraces up, someone else coughing, a baby crying and being hushed. Above it all, faint as memory, the cathedral bells rang some small hour, and the whole warren paused to count, and resumed.

And beneath it, patient as sediment, the nine.

*You have found a burrow,* said the deep one, almost gently. *Burrows are for things that intend to hide forever. Is that what you intend, carver? To grow old in the dark, carving little comforts for little griefs, while your mentor rots in an Inquisition cell?*

I kept my breathing even. Beside me Pip slept on.

*She ordered you to flee,* the smooth voice murmured. *Orders are what cowards give so they needn't watch the result. She is in chains tonight. She hummed a lullaby today, we are told, that made her interrogator go pale. Even chained, she works. And you—you count bells in a hole.*

"They have the relic," I whispered into the dark, so quietly it was hardly breath. "Whatever you are now, you're theirs. In a vault. In iron. Why sing to me at all?"

A pause. When the answer came, all nine voices spoke together for the first time, harmonized, and the harmony was beautiful, and that was the most frightening thing yet.

*Bones remember their carver. Iron cannot hold what was never bound in iron. And carver—we were not made to belong to anyone. Not to crowns. Not to inquisitors.* A ripple through the harmony, like a smile. *Not even to you. But you are the only living soul who ever listened to us kindly. Remember that, when the choosing comes.*

Then quiet. True quiet, for the first time in days, and I lay in it staring up at the dark underside of the pigeon racks, and I did not sleep.

Near dawn—I judged it by the change in the water's sound, the channel running fuller with the morning rains far above—footsteps stopped at the mouth of our alcove. Brannoc stood there with a lantern hooded to a slit of green.

"Message came down the pigeon line an hour ago," he said, low, so as not to wake Pip. "From a contact in the Inquisition's clerical pool. She sells paper-truths, cheap ones." He crouched, and the light found his face, and for the first time since I'd met him he looked uncertain, and I understood that what he was about to say was the reason he'd let us stay at all.

"The relic you're mourning," he said. "According to the requisition logs, it never arrived. No ninefold piece entered the Inquisition vaults after the raid. Either your Odile hid it well enough to fool her own captors—" his gray eyes held mine, steady and grim— "or someone else got to it first. And carver, there's a name on the guild's roster that's been asking questions about salvage rights. Corvin. The one who handled acquisitions."

I sat up in the dark, and behind my ribs the nine began, very softly, to laugh.
