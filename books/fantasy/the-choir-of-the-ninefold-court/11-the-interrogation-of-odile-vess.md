## 11. The Interrogation of Odile Vess

The cells beneath the Inquisition's chapterhouse had been built for men who screamed.

Maren Vale knew this because she had read the architect's original plans—part of her habit of knowing everything about the places where she worked, the way a surgeon knows the body before cutting into it. The walls were granite, three feet thick, dressed with iron studs to deaden sound. The doors had no handles on the inside. The lamps burned behind glass so that nothing flammable could be reached by desperate hands. Every detail spoke of a certain kind of prisoner: the kind who wept, raved, clawed at stone, begged for mothers or gods.

Odile Vess fit none of it.

She sat on the cell's narrow cot with her ankles crossed and her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for tea, and when Maren entered for the first time, the old woman looked up with an expression of mild, almost fond curiosity—the look of someone greeting a guest who had arrived late to dinner.

"You're taller than your reputation," Odile said.

Maren closed the door behind her and did not answer. She had learned long ago that the first minutes of an interrogation belonged to silence, and that whoever filled them first was usually whoever wanted something more. She crossed the cell, set her ledger on the small iron table bolted to the floor, and took her seat opposite the prisoner. The lamp between them hissed softly.

"Odile Vess," Maren said, opening the ledger. "Proprietor of the Vellum Guild, licensed under charter forty-one, trading in curiosities and antiquities. You've held that charter for thirty years."

"I have."

"Your guild is named in four separate complaints filed under the Gift-Hunt edict. Two informants place bone-charms of unregistered provenance in your inventory. A third swears he sold you a relic that *spoke* to him." Maren looked up. "You understand what registration would have required. You understand what concealment carries."

"Fire," Odile said pleasantly. "The criers were very clear. They always are. It saves money on executioners when the crowd does the work."

Something in the flatness of it made Maren's pen pause. She had interrogated bone-singers and grave-whisperers, hedge-witches and name-thieves, and they all shared one grammar: fear. Fear shaped their sentences, shortened their answers, made their eyes flick toward the door. Odile Vess spoke as though the edict were a change in the weather—as though fire were simply the season.

"You're not afraid," Maren said.

"Should I be?"

"It would be sensible."

"Sensible." Odile unfolded her hands and examined her knuckles, the joints swollen with age, one finger crooked from an old break badly set. "I was sensible for thirty years, Inquisitor. I paid my taxes. I kept my ledgers clean. I buried two husbands and a daughter and I did all of it sensibly, and then your criers came through the streets telling everyone like me that the sensible thing no longer counts for anything." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only precision. "So no. Fear seems a poor investment at my age. I'm told the returns are terrible."

Maren wrote: *Subject displays no affect consistent with threat exposure.* Then she stopped writing, because the sentence felt like a lie, and lying in her own ledger was a thing she had never done.

*Not yet,* said a voice in the back of her head that sounded uncomfortably like her mother.

"The relic," Maren said. "The speaking one. Where is it?"

"Where is what?"

"Don't play games with me, madam. You know precisely what I mean."

"Oh, I do." Odile leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight caught the deep lines around her eyes, making her look suddenly ancient—not frail, but old the way bedrock is old. "That's rather the point, isn't it? You want me to tell you where it is, and I'm going to disappoint you, and you're going to ask again in different words, and eventually you'll bring out whatever instruments the chapterhouse keeps for stubborn old women. And I will still disappoint you. Not because I'm brave." She tapped her temple. "Because I'm *old*, Inquisitor. There's very little left in here worth burning. But there are things in this city worth protecting, and some of them are younger than me and softer than me and they still think the world can be kind if they just keep their heads down. I won't trade those for my comfort. So ask your questions. I'll give you riddles. It's the only honest currency I have left."

For a moment the cell was silent except for the lamp.

Then Maren said, "Why tell me your strategy? It gains you nothing."

"Doesn't it?" Odile settled back against the wall. "It tells you exactly who you're dealing with. And you strike me as a woman who respects being told the truth, even when it costs her."

Maren wrote nothing for a long moment. Then she turned to a fresh page and began again, and the interrogation proper began—and it went, in the words she would later use in her report, *nowhere*.

* * *

Three hours in, Maren had eleven pages of transcript and not one usable fact.

It wasn't that Odile refused to speak. That would have been simple; refusal was a wall, and Maren had hammers for walls. Instead the old woman answered everything, cheerfully, at length, in language that circled meaning the way flies circle a lamp.

Asked where the relic was, she said: *"Where do you keep a thing that wants to be found? Carefully. Very carefully. Like a letter you regret sending."*

Asked who else in the guild was gifted, she said: *"A household is a choir, Inquisitor. Some sing loud, some hum. You'd be surprised how many people in this city are humming."*

Asked whether Sable Galloway—whose name appeared in the guild's employment rolls, whose carving work appeared in three separate evidence lockers—had the gift, Odile had laughed. Actually laughed, a dry rustling sound like paper turning.

"Galloway has the gift the way a river has water," she said. "You could dam him. You could divert him. You could poison him. But you should ask yourself, before you try, what the valley looks like downstream when the river decides it's had enough."

"And where is Galloway now?"

"Gone where rivers go when the weather turns."

Maren set down her pen. Her hand ached. Through the high slit of window near the ceiling, the light had gone from grey to gold to grey again, and she realized with a small shock how much of the day had poured away.

"You're enjoying this," she said.

"A little." Odile didn't deny it. "You mustn't take it personally. It's been a long time since anyone asked me real questions. My customers only ever ask about price."

"They're not real questions. They're procedure."

"Mm." Odile studied her across the lamp, and Maren had the uncomfortable sensation of being read—thoroughly, expertly, the way she herself read suspects. "And what happens after the procedure, Inquisitor? When you've written your pages and sealed your report and sent it up the chain to men whose boots never touch street mud? What happens to the humming people?"

"They burn," Maren said. "If they don't register."

"Yes. And do you believe they should?"

It was the first question anyone had asked *her* in this building in months. Maren heard herself give the trained answer—"The law exists to protect the many from powers they cannot see or contest"—and heard, at the same moment, how it sounded: rehearsed, hollow, a coin rubbed smooth by handling.

Odile heard it too. She nodded slowly, without triumph, which was somehow worse.

"That's a beautiful sentence," the old woman said. "Someone taught it to you. I wonder if they believed it."

* * *

Maren requested a second session because the first had produced nothing, and requesting sessions was what diligent inquisitors did. She told herself that. She was becoming, she noticed, very good at telling herself things.

The second session was worse.

Because somewhere past the second hour, having exhausted procedure, having tried the good-cop warmth and the cold-cop arithmetic and even the gambit of leaving the file open on the table where Odile could read the names of the already-burned—having tried everything—Maren made a mistake.

She got tired.

And tired, she let a silence stretch too long, and into that silence Odile Vess began to hum.

It was nothing, at first. A thread of sound, half under her breath, the way old women hum while shelling peas. Maren barely registered it. Then the melody turned, climbed two notes, fell back, and every hair on Maren's arms stood up at once.

She knew it.

Not knew it the way one knows a popular air, hummed in markets and taverns. Knew it the way one knows a scar. It was a lullaby in a mode the conservatories called *hollow-fifth*, old rural music, the kind the Church had spent two centuries suppressing because its intervals were said to carry grief too honestly. Her mother had sung it. Only her mother—Maren had searched for it once, years ago, in the archives, in songbooks, in the memories of other women from her mother's village, and found nothing. Not one other source. As if the song had lived in one throat in all the world, and that throat had gone into the ground when Maren was nine years old.

Her mother had died of lung-rot in a charity ward in Corveth, coughing the last verses into a cloth, holding Maren's hand with fingers gone bird-light. *Sing it back to me, little wren, so I know it won't die with me.* And Maren, fourteen and embarrassed and already training her face into stillness, had sung it back, once, badly, and then never again.

Now it floated out of a prisoner's mouth in a granite cell three hundred miles from her mother's village.

Maren did not move. She was aware of her own heartbeat, absurdly loud, and of the pen in her hand, and of the transcript page in front of her, blank except for the header.

Odile hummed the whole verse. Then she stopped, and looked up, and her eyes—mild, patient, terribly clear—found Maren's face and stayed there.

"My mother sang that," Maren said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride. "Where did you hear it?"

"Did she?" Odile said softly. "What a small world it is, after all."

"*Where did you hear it.*"

"Inquisitor." The old woman folded her hands again. "I'm eighty-one years old. I have traded in impossible things for longer than you've been alive. Do you truly imagine I heard it anywhere? Songs like that aren't heard. They're *carried*. Someone loved your mother enough to teach her that song, and someone loved her enough to learn it back, and somewhere in the long chain of loving women it walked from village to village until it reached a girl in a charity ward who thought she was the last one holding it." She tilted her head. "It wants to be sung, child. That's all a song ever wants. And things that want to be sung find their singers."

"You're saying you're gifted."

"I'm saying I'm old, and old women collect things. Songs. Secrets. The names of girls who grew up to burn other girls' mothers." Odile's voice never rose, never sharpened; it was the gentleness that made it land like a blade. "Tell me, Inquisitor Vale. When you verified the confession of the tanner's boy last month—the one whose testimony put the widow Hesk to the pyre—did you touch his skin? Did you feel the truth of him?"

Maren went cold from the scalp down.

No one knew. No one could know. She had falsified the report herself, alone, at her own desk, at midnight: *verification inconclusive, corroboration sufficient.* Because she had touched the boy's wrist and felt the truth in him like a struck bell—felt that he had lied, that the widow Hesk had never cursed his cattle, that he'd invented it all for the bounty the edict offered informants—and she had written *corroboration sufficient* anyway, because the pyre was scheduled, because questioning the verdict meant questioning the commander, because she was a tool and tools do not inspect the hand that holds them.

"How," she said, and her voice was not quite level anymore, "do you know that."

Odile smiled, and for the first time it reached her eyes, and what lived in her eyes was not malice but something older and sadder—an enormous, weary tenderness.

"I don't," she said. "But look at your hands, child. Look at where they've gone. Folded on the table like a good girl's, and shaking like a sinner's. Whatever it is you're carrying—you've been carrying it alone, haven't you? All this time. In a building full of brothers and sisters, carrying it alone." She shook her head slowly. "That's how they keep us, you know. Not the chains. The aloneness. A gift shared is a gift survived. A gift hidden is a gift that eats its keeper from the inside."

"This is manipulation," Maren said. "This is exactly the kind of whisper-work the edict was written to stop."

"Of course it is." Odile spread her hands, agreeable. "Everything true is manipulation if it threatens the powerful. Now." She leaned forward, and lowered her voice, and the lamplight made a small warm island of her face in the cold cell. "Here is my last riddle of the day, and you may write it in your ledger however you like. *The fire you're building is meant to burn a garden, but there are roots under the whole city, and fire does not check what it feeds on.* When the Undercroft goes up—when your commander sends his soldiers down into the dark places with torches—they will not find nine monsters, Inquisitor. They will find thousands of people who hum. People who mend bones with a touch. People whose bread rises better than it should. And the smoke will go up over this city for a week, and afterward everyone will say it was necessary, and your mother's lullaby will die with the last of the women who carried it, and no one will sing anything in this city again but orders."

She sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, boots crossed a floor.

"Ask your next question whenever you're ready," Odile said. "I have all the time in the world. It's the only inheritance I ever got."

* * *

Maren did not sleep that night.

She sat in her quarters in the chapterhouse with the transcript open on her desk and her pen uncapped beside it, and the page waited for the summary line the way a gallows waits. *Subject remains uncooperative. Recommends escalation.*

Recommends escalation. Three words that meant: *bring her to the questioner with the irons. Bring her to Brother Cassian, who is very thorough.* Three words that meant: *I am finished pretending this woman is a case instead of a person.*

She thought about writing it. She picked up the pen. She put it down.

Instead she found herself doing something she had not done in twenty-two years. She hummed.

Quietly. Badly. The hollow-fifth climbing and falling, the melody her mother had pressed into her like a flower into a book. It sounded thin in the bare room, and it cracked in the middle where her throat tightened, and she kept singing anyway, all the way to the end of the verse, because Odile Vess was right about one thing—it wanted to be sung, and she was tired of being the last throat in the world.

When she finished, the silence afterward felt different. Populated. As if somewhere in the walls, in the stones, in the old bones of the city, something had heard and approved.

She pulled the transcript toward her. Beneath the final exchange she wrote, in her precise clerk's hand:

*Subject's statements inconsistent with profile. Recommend continued personal interrogation. No escalation at this time. — M. Vale*

It was stalling. She knew it was stalling. She could buy days, perhaps a week, before the commander noticed that the Vess woman was producing nothing and reassigned her. A week to find out what the old woman actually knew. About the relic. About Galloway. About the roots under the city.

About why a song that existed in one throat in all the world had found its way into a prison cell to wait for her.

Maren capped the ink, snuffed the lamp, and lay down in the dark with her mother's melody turning over and over in her mind like a key looking for its lock.

In the morning, she would request the Vess woman's full history—every town, every associate, every year. And she would start with the guild's employment rolls, and the name of the carver who had signed the charms in three evidence lockers.

*Sable Galloway.*

Find the carver, she told herself, closing her eyes. Find the carver, and find out what the nine voices want.

She did not yet know where the number nine had come from. It had simply arrived in her head sometime during the third hour of the interrogation, unbidden, the way songs arrive—whispered up through the stones of the cell, perhaps, by something old that was tired of waiting.

And it was singing, faintly, in nine parts.
