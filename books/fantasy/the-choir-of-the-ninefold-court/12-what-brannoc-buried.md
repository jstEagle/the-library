## 12. What Brannoc Buried

Brannoc had learned, over thirty years of soldiering and ten more of hiding from what soldiering made him, that the worst nights were never the loud ones. Loud nights you could fight. It was the quiet ones—the ones where the tunnels hummed with nothing but water dripping off old stone and the small sounds of people trying to sleep—that let the past climb up out of its grave and sit down beside you like an old comrade who didn't know he was dead.

Tonight was one of those.

He sat at the long table in the Undercroft's common hall, a room that had been a cistern once, then a smugglers' counting-house, then whatever it was now—a refuge, a wound dressed loosely, a place where three strangers and half a dozen strays waited out a hunt that got closer every day. The lamp above him burned whale oil, cheap and smoky, and threw his shadow huge against the brick. He'd taken the seat facing the stairs. Old habit. You always took the seat facing the stairs.

The relic was upstairs. Wrapped in three layers of wool and one of lead-lined leather, locked in Brannoc's own strongbox because Sable had asked him to hold it and Sable had earned that much trust. But Brannoc could still hear it.

That was the thing he hadn't told them. That was the thing sitting beside him tonight, heavier than the missing hand.

He could hear it. Not the way Sable heard it—Sable heard voices, whole conversations, nine sovereigns holding court inside a lump of carved bone the size of a fist. Brannoc heard only the edges of it. A hum. A resonance, low and sweet, like a choir warming up behind a cathedral wall. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep and murmuring.

He'd heard it before. Once. Twenty-two years ago, on a hillside in the Vaelmark, standing in front of a village called Ashen Hollow, listening to the same song come up through the ground.

The lamp guttered. Brannoc watched the flame steady itself and thought: *You're going to have to tell them. Tonight, or not at all.*

Because tomorrow they meant to move. Sable had laid it out over supper with that careful, ledger-keeper's precision of his—the relic couldn't stay in the city, Vale's net was tightening, Corvin was still unaccounted for and Corvin's absence was louder than his presence ever been. They'd go downriver, or into the deep tunnels, somewhere the Inquisition's writ ran thin. And every plan they'd made rested on a lie of omission: that Brannoc was just a one-handed ex-soldier with a talent for logistics and a soft spot for street kids.

He wasn't. He was the thing they were running from. Not a gifted man—he'd never carried the gift, not a drop of it—but worse, in a way. He was the hand that had done the crown's burning, back when the burning was policy instead of panic.

Footsteps on the stairs. Small ones, quick ones. Pip, of course. The boy slept like a cat in a house full of dogs—one ear always open—and he came padding down into the lamplight wrapped in a blanket, hair sticking up, eyes too sharp for the hour.

"You're doing the brooding thing," Pip said. "It's very loud for someone being silent."

"Go back to bed."

"It's my bed you're keeping me from, isn't it." But he didn't go back up. He dropped onto the bench across the table, tucked his blanket around himself, and looked at Brannoc with that flat, unblinking stare he'd learned from years of watching marks on the street. "Sable can't sleep either. He says the relic's singing louder since we brought it down here. Deeper ground, he thinks. He says—" Pip hesitated, and for once the boy's tongue slowed. "He says it knows this part of the city. Like it's been here before."

Brannoc's stomach went cold and stayed there. *It has been here before.* The war-vaults under the old quarter, the binding chambers the histories pretended were grain stores. Of course the song was stronger here. They were sitting in the throat of the thing.

"Pip," Brannoc said. "Get Sable. And the girl—Wren, if she's awake. I need to say something, and I'm only going to be able to say it once."

* * *

They gathered the way frightened people gather—too close together, too quiet, the lamp turned up higher than it needed to be. Sable came down with his carving roll under his arm out of pure habit, as if bone knives would help with what was coming. Wren, the guild's lockwoman, arrived last and stood by the wall with her arms crossed, which was how she did everything. Two of the Undercroft strays hovered at the top of the stairs until Brannoc waved them off.

"I'm going to tell you why I know the tunnels," Brannoc began, "and it isn't because I used to run cargo."

Nobody spoke. Sable sat very still. Brannoc had noticed that about him—the man listened the way other people prayed, total and silent, like listening might cost him something he'd have to pay later. Maybe it did. Maybe that was the gift's price.

"I was Crown Fourth Regiment," Brannoc said. "Twelve years. I fought in the Vaelmark suppression when I was twenty-three. You'll have heard it called the Pacification, if you heard of it at all. Official histories say it was banditry. Rebel lords. That sort of lie." He turned his mug slowly on the table, watching the ring of damp it left. "It wasn't. It was the gifted. Whole communities of them, living out past the king's roads, farming, raising children, minding their own business. The crown decided that wouldn't do. Sent us to make it not-do."

"The last suppression," Sable said quietly. "Before the Gift-Hunt, there was—"

"There was always a Gift-Hunt," Brannoc said. "It just used to be seasonal. Like harvest." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I was good at my work. That's the first thing you should know about me. Not brave, not stupid—good. Promotion-track good. Sergeant by twenty-five, and the reason was written in villages that stopped existing."

Wren shifted against the wall. "Brannoc—"

"Let him finish," Sable said. His voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse.

"Ashen Hollow," Brannoc said. "Third year of the campaign. We'd been told the village there was a nest—gifted elders teaching children, relics buried under the meetinghouse, the whole catalogue of crimes. My captain wanted it burned clean. Standard practice: fire the buildings, salt the wells, count the survivors for the pyre wagons." His hands were steady. He'd trained them to be steady even now, even telling this. "But the night before, I went up to the tree line to scout it. And I heard singing."

He looked at Sable. "Not voices. Not words. Just—song. Coming up out of the ground, out of the roots, like the hill itself was humming. Low and layered. Nine parts, though I didn't know to count them then. I thought it was the villagers. Some rite. And I told myself it didn't matter what it was, because orders were orders, and I walked back down and I gave my captain a clean report and I lit the first torch myself."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far off in the tunnels, water dripped into water, patient as a clock.

"They didn't run," Brannoc said. "That's the part I need you all to carry, because I've carried it alone long enough and it's bent me crooked. They stood in the square and they sang while we came down the hill. Old woman in front, holding a carved bone up like a lantern. And the fire took the houses and the singing kept going, and it wasn't screaming, it was the same nine-part song, and it didn't stop until the meetinghouse roof fell in. And when it stopped—" His voice caught, and he let it, because hiding the catch was how he'd gotten here. "When it stopped, every man in my company heard the silence. All of us. Men who'd never felt anything stranger than a hangover. We heard it like a door closing."

Sable's hand had gone to his pocket, where he kept the smallest of his carving tools, the way another man might touch a saint's medal. "The relic," he said. "There was a relic in Ashen Hollow."

"There was a relic under the meetinghouse," Brannoc said. "We dug for it after. Captain wanted trophies—proof for the tribunal. We found a cache in the foundations. Charms, bones, things I didn't look at too hard because looking was the beginning of sympathy and sympathy was treason." He swallowed. "And one piece that was different. Darker bone. Older. Carved so fine it looked grown, not made. When I picked it up, the song started again—quiet, just for me, like it knew what I'd done and was introducing itself anyway."

"And you kept it," Wren said. Not accusing. Wondering.

"I was supposed to turn it in. I told my captain it was nothing, a funeral token, worthless. And I kept it." Brannoc met their eyes one at a time. "For eleven years I kept it, and I told myself I kept it as evidence, as a trophy, as any of the hundred lies a man tells about the worst thing he owns. Then one night I took it out and I held it and I said, out loud, like a madman: *What do you want from me?*"

"What did it say?" Pip whispered. He'd pulled the blanket up to his chin. Nobody told him to stop listening anymore; nobody had for a year.

"Nothing. That was the horror of it. Silence. Total silence, for the first time since Ashen Hollow. And I understood—I understood right down in my bones—that it hadn't been singing *to* me at all. It had been singing the way a bell rings when struck. I'd struck it. Me and my torch and my captain's orders. Everything it had ever said to anyone, we'd taught it to say." He set the mug down carefully, because his hands had finally decided to shake. "So I brought it to the guild. To Odile Vess. I'd heard she traded in impossible things and asked no questions, and I wanted it gone, and I wanted it gone somewhere it couldn't be found by men like the man I'd been."

Sable had gone very pale. "Odile gave it to the vault."

"She gave it to the vault, and she gave me a job sweeping floors, and neither of us ever spoke of it again for six years. Until you inventoried the deep shelves, carver, and came up the stairs white as tallow saying you'd heard nine voices in a knucklebone-sized piece of grave-goods." Brannoc spread his one hand flat on the table. "That's my confession. I burned a village of your people, Sable. Maybe of yours too, Wren—who knows what blood runs where, after that campaign scattered everyone. I lit the torch. And the thing you're all risking your necks to protect or destroy or decide upon? I'm the reason it sings the way it does. Every voice in it learned its grief from me."

The silence afterward was the bad kind, the kind with weight. Pip had stopped breathing. Wren stared at Brannoc with an expression he couldn't read and didn't dare try.

It was Sable who moved. He rose, crossed the room, and sat down on the bench beside Brannoc—not close, but on the same side of the table, which was its own kind of verdict.

"The dead talk," Sable said. "You know that's what I do. What I *did*." He smiled without humor. "Do you know what they almost never talk about? Their own deaths. They circle it. They talk about the wedding ring they were wearing, the argument from that morning, the smell of the bread. But the moment itself—they go quiet, or they lie, or they scream and won't stop. Grief is the one country none of them will map." He looked at Brannoc steadily. "You just mapped it. Out loud. To the people least safe to hear it. Do you understand what that costs a man like me, hearing that?"

"No," Brannoc said honestly.

"It means I believe you." Sable's voice was rough. "The dead lie to spare themselves. The living do it better. You just handed us the knife handle-first."

"Don't make me noble," Brannoc said. "I'm not confessing because I'm clean. I'm confessing because the song's getting louder and I know what that means. I've heard it get louder before." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, all of you. The night Ashen Hollow burned, the song didn't die with the singers. It *changed*. Before the fire it was—warm. Lullaby-warm. Afterward it had an edge, like a blade being sharpened somewhere underground. Grief did that. Or rage. I don't know the difference in something that old." He looked toward the ceiling, toward the strongbox two floors up. "It's changing again. Since we brought it down here, since the hunt started, since Odile was taken—it's sharpening. And there are more gifted souls in this city than the Inquisition dreams, hiding in cellars and attics, and every one of them is afraid tonight, and fear carries, and that thing upstairs drinks fear like wine."

"So what do we do?" Wren asked. Practical as ever. Bless her for it.

"We don't run yet," Brannoc said. "Running's what I'd have planned a week ago, and running is what the captain would have planned, and I am done making that man's plans. We move the relic deeper—not downriver, down *under*. There are ways below the old quarter that the regiment sealed after the war and never unsealed, because the crown was scared of what was down there and I know the seals because I helped build them." He heard himself say it and marveled at it: twenty-two years of carrying the knowledge like a coal in his chest, waiting for exactly this use. "I can get us in. Past my own locks. Into the vault where the Court was first bound, if half the stories are true."

"That's where it wants to go," Sable said softly. Everyone looked at him. He had his hand pressed flat against his own chest, over his heart, as if holding a door shut from inside. "It's been asking me. Every night since we came down here. It doesn't say *come*, it says *home*." He lifted his head, and his eyes were wet and terrified and more alive than Brannoc had seen them. "Brannoc—if you built those seals, tell me truly, and think hard before you answer, because I'll hear it in your bones if you don't. Is the way down still guarded? By soldiers?"

"No," Brannoc said. "By something older than soldiers. Why?"

Sable closed his eyes. And faintly—so faintly that Brannoc might have imagined it, except that Wren's face said she heard it too, and Pip's, and the strays frozen at the top of the stairs—through the stone, through the water, through twenty-two years of ash, the song rose up out of the deep places of the city.

Nine parts. And for the first time, Brannoc caught words in it.

They were his name.
