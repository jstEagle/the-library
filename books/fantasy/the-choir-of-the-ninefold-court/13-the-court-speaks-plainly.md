## 13. The Court Speaks Plainly

The relic sat in Brannoc's iron strongbox like a prisoner who had learned to enjoy the cell.

Sable had not meant to open the box. He told himself that afterward, and for a long time he almost believed it. He had gone down to the Undercroft's lowest room—the one Brannoc called the cold store, where salted meat hung from hooks and the walls wept mineral stains—to fetch oil for the lamps, and the box was simply there, and his hands were simply on it, and then the lid was simply up.

Nine voices rose out of the dark like heat off a summer road.

*Carver.* The word was not spoken so much as remembered at him. *We have waited.*

"You don't wait," Sable said aloud, because saying things aloud kept him anchored, kept him *Sable Galloway, bone-carver, keeper of ledgers, nobody* instead of whatever he became when he listened too long. "You scheme. There's a difference."

*Is there?* The voices layered over one another, nine tones braided into a single chord, and beneath the chord something older, patient as sediment. *You have heard our names from frightened men. You have heard our war from victors. Come and hear it from us. Then judge.*

He should have put the lid down. He knew that the way a man knows a stair is rotten—knowing it changes nothing about where his foot has already fallen.

"I'm listening," he said, and hated himself a little for how easily it came.

*Then see.*

---

The vision took him the way deep water takes a swimmer—not violently, but completely, until the surface was a rumor overhead and the light came from somewhere else entirely.

He stood on a plain of white grass under a sky the color of old pewter, and the grass was not grass but bone, miles of it, a sea of femurs and ribs grown smooth as wheat stalks, and the wind moving through it made a sound he recognized with a lurch: the sound of the relic when Odile first let him hold it, years ago, before either of them understood what slept inside.

A city stood at the edge of the bone-plain. Not a ruin—a living city, white-walled, its towers crowned with gardens, its gates open. He knew the architecture from the oldest ledgers in the guild vault, the ones written in ink mixed with ash: this was Vael Correnne, the Court's seat, the capital of the world as the world had been before the burning.

And marching toward it across the bone-grass, banners snapping, came an army wearing the sun-and-scepter of the crown that still ruled the eastern kingdoms.

*Look,* said the voices, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck. *Look at what they tell you came to punish us.*

Sable looked. The army was not a punitive force. It was not even primarily soldiers. Behind the ranked spears came wagons, and the wagons carried chains, and the chains were sized for children. Priests walked beside the wagons in grey vestments, carrying censers and long iron tongs, and their faces were the faces of men doing necessary work they expected to be thanked for.

"They came for people," Sable said. His voice sounded small on the plain. "Not for you. For the gifted."

*Nine hundred thousand souls in these lands could hear the dead, or speak to rivers, or mend flesh with a touch,* said a voice that identified itself, without pomposity, as Aurelian, First of the Nine. *The crown called it corruption. The priests called it plague. We called them our people. When the king demanded we surrender every gifted child in our territories for 'cleansing,' we refused. That refusal is what your histories call the beginning of the war.*

The vision shifted, the way dreams shift, seamless and unarguable. Now he stood inside the city walls while fire fell from the sky—not dragonfire, though dragons came later, but something worse: consecrated fire, white and silent, that burned nothing except what the priests deemed unclean. It slid through streets and left the stone untouched and took only people, whole families, leaving behind outlines of ash on doorsteps like the shadows at Hirashima—but no, he didn't know that word, that wasn't his memory, that was the vision borrowing shapes from somewhere deeper than him, and he shook himself and made himself watch.

A woman ran through the falling silence carrying a child whose gift was the mending of broken things. The fire took them both mid-stride. Where they fell, the white grass of the plain beyond the walls bloomed overnight.

*This is what defense looked like,* said the ninth voice, the deepest one, the one that had not yet given him a name. *You have been taught that we burned half the world out of hunger for thrones. Ask yourself: who wrote those histories? The same crown that armed the pyres.*

Sable wanted to argue. He reached for the counterarguments the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood—the sieges, the scorched provinces, the names of towns that no longer existed, all of it drilled into every schoolchild east of the mountains. *But the western marches. But the Salt War. But—*

*Yes,* the voices said, and there was something almost tender in the chorus now, the way a good liar makes tenderness the final coat of paint. *We did those things. In the eleventh year, after our cities were ash and our people were chains, we stopped defending and began avenging. We will not lie to you, carver. That is the difference between us and the crown. They call their massacres justice. We call ours grief.*

The vision turned again, and this time it showed him the end: the binding, the thing he had never once heard described from the inside. Nine sovereigns standing in a chamber of black stone while crown-priests sang the chaining hymns, and the sovereigns not fighting—not because they couldn't, but because around them, held hostage in cages of consecrated iron, were three hundred gifted children, and the priests' terms were simple: submit to the binding, or watch the children burn one by one while the army watched and ate a celebratory supper.

They submitted. All nine. And the histories, written afterward by the victors, recorded it as a great victory of righteousness over tyranny, and the children were cleansed anyway, quietly, in a valley whose name was struck from every map.

Sable came back to himself kneeling on the cold-store floor with tears freezing on his cheeks and Brannoc's spare lamp-oil spilled across his boots, and for a long moment he could not remember his own mother's face, only the faces the vision had shown him, hundreds of them, thousands, all of them burning or chained or both.

---

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He sat in the corner of the Undercroft room he shared with three crates of smuggled brandy and Pip's snoring, and he turned the vision over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

Because here was the thing Odile would have asked, if Odile were here—Odile, who was in an Inquisition cell because Sable had run instead of staying, and whose absence had a weight he carried in his chest like a swallowed stone. Here was the thing she would have said with her terrible gentle smile: *A story that flatters the listener is still a story, boy. The devil doesn't need you to sign anything. He just needs you to feel sorry for him.*

And the vision *had* flattered him. That was the part he kept circling. It had shown him exactly the history a man like him—guilty, grieving, hunted, hungry for a reason that his running had not been cowardice—would want to believe. *You didn't abandon Odile. You're gathering strength. You're on the right side of an old injustice.* Every temptation he'd ever met wore that same tailored coat.

But—and his hands wouldn't stop shaking, and it wasn't entirely fear—the details rang true. He had spent eleven years keeping the Vellum Guild's ledgers, and impossible things leave fingerprints in paper the way anything leaves fingerprints anywhere. He had catalogued crown requisitions from the war centuries: the tonnage of consecrated iron, the invoices for child-sized manacles billed to the Office of Pieties, the shipping manifests for censers by the thousand. He had read those entries a hundred times and thought, the way everyone thought, *war is ugly, someone had to do the ugly arithmetic.* He had never once added the columns and seen what they summed to.

Child-sized manacles. By the gross.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Pip's voice, thick with sleep, from the dark. The boy sat up on his pallet, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog, and squinted at Sable sitting rigid in the corner with the lamp unlit.

"Thinking," Sable said.

"You do it loud." Pip scratched his ribs. "Brannoc says you talk to the box. The iron one. I heard you through the wall last night, too. Talking to nobody."

"To somebody."

"Yeah, well." Pip lay back down, and then, because Pip was Pip and had never met a silence he trusted: "If the somebody tells you to open the guild vault or stab Brannoc or sell me to the crows, you'll say no, right? Only asking. For planning purposes."

Sable laughed despite everything, a short cracked sound. "For planning purposes. Yes. I'll say no."

"Good." A pause. "Because I'm worth more than the crows are paying anyway. I checked."

Pip's breathing slowed again, and Sable sat alone with the truth he hadn't spoken: that the Court had asked for nothing yet. That was the most frightening part. Nine ancient sovereigns, awake in a lump of carved bone, and so far all they had done was *show* him things, patiently, generously, like scholars correcting a student's misread text. Everything he knew about power said that patience was the most expensive thing power could buy, and nobody spent it without expecting returns.

---

* * *

In the morning he went looking for Brannoc and found the big man in the Undercroft's map room, which was a former wine cellar with a smuggler's chart of the city nailed to one wall, annotated in three generations of different handwriting and at least two languages that had stopped being spoken.

"The vision," Sable said, without preamble. "The one the Court showed me. The war. The chains, the priests, the children."

Brannoc set down the knife he'd been using to trim lamp wicks. He had a way of going very still when something mattered, a soldier's stillness, the whole body becoming a wall. "What about it?"

"It might be true."

"That's worse than a lie, then." Brannoc folded his arms—one arm and the scarred stump where the other ended. "A lie you can spit out. A true thing gets inside you and starts rearranging the furniture."

"The ledgers support it. Some of it. The requisitions—" Sable made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way he could carry it. "The crown bought child-sized restraints by the gross during the war years. Billed through the Office of Pieties. I've catalogued those pages, Brannoc. I just never read them."

Brannoc was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above them, water dripped through the old city's bones, patient as the Court itself.

"I fought in the last suppression," Brannoc said finally. "Twenty years ago. Little village in the hill country, forty-one souls, maybe six of them gifted. We were told the gifted were feeding the others to something. We were shown evidence. It was convincing." His jaw worked. "It was forged. I found out two years later, from a clerk who'd drunk himself honest. Forty-one people, Sable. I helped burn them on a lie, and the lie was written by men who'd had four hundred years of practice writing lies about the gifted, and I believed it because believing it was easier than asking questions in front of my officers."

"And now?"

"Now I'm down here with one hand and no country, keeping smugglers' books." He looked at Sable with eyes that had seen the inside of that particular furnace. "So here's what I know that your nine sovereigns didn't show you. Even if every word of their vision is gospel—even if the crown was the monster and the Court the martyr—it doesn't change the ending. Because I've read the older histories too, the ones the guild keeps locked, and the Court's *defense* killed more smallfolk than the crown's pyres did. When a giant defends itself, it doesn't get to choose what it falls on."

"So we destroy it."

"Do you want to destroy it?"

Sable opened his mouth. Closed it. The honest answer sat in him like a fishhook: *I don't know.* The nine had shown him a history that made his blood boil and his grief make sense, and some traitorous chamber of his heart had already begun drafting the argument for release—*they were wronged, they were bound unjustly, freeing them would be justice four hundred years overdue*—and he could not fully trust any judgment formed in that heat.

"I want to know the rest," he said. "There's a hole in their story. The eleventh year. They admitted the turn—from defense to vengeance—but they showed me nothing of it. Nine sovereigns, four hundred years of memory, and the middle of the war is a closed door."

Brannoc grunted. "When a witness volunteers everything except one hour of one night, in my experience, that hour is where the body's buried."

"Exactly." Sable flexed his hands, feeling the carver's calluses, feeling how badly they wanted to hold the relic again, and made himself say the next part aloud because saying it aloud was the only leash he had. "So tonight I go back. And I ask them to show me the eleventh year. And if they refuse—"

"Then you'll know."

"Then I'll know."

Brannoc picked his knife back up and returned to the wicks, and the conversation was over, because with Brannoc conversations ended like doors closing, cleanly and without echo. But as Sable turned to go, the ex-soldier spoke once more, quietly, to the wall rather than to him:

"When I burned that village, there was a woman who sang while the fire took her barn. Not screaming. Singing. A harvest song, the kind you'd sing over bread." He paused. "I've wondered for twenty years whether she was singing for herself or trying to teach the flames a better use. If your nine sovereigns start sounding like her—like grief that's decided grief is a weapon—you come find me before you decide anything. Promise me that much, carver."

"I promise."

---

* * *

He went down at dusk, when the Undercroft was loudest with supper noise and no one would mark his absence, and he unlocked the strongbox with hands that only shook a little.

The nine voices rose to meet him, warm as banked coals.

*Carver. You return.*

"I have questions."

*We hoped you would.* A ripple through the chorus, and he could not tell if it was pleasure or amusement or the tide of something vast adjusting its position. *Ask.*

"The eleventh year," Sable said. "The turn. You showed me the beginning and the end and the binding, but not the middle. Show me the middle. All of it. What you did, and why, and who paid for it."

Silence.

Not the ordinary silence of a room, but a *held* silence, nine breaths drawn simultaneously and kept, and in that held breath Sable heard—for the first time since the vault, since the very first moment his thumb had found the flaw in the old carving and the voices had poured through—heard the chord *strain*. Like a choir holding a note past endurance. Like nine things that had ruled the world being asked, politely, by a ledger-keeper with ink-stained fingers, to account for themselves.

*That year is grief,* said Aurelian at last, and the warmth in the voice had cooled several degrees. *Even we do not look at it often.*

"Grief or shame?" Sable asked. "I've kept books for liars and books for honest men, and I can tell you the difference. Honest men show you the page that costs them. Liars show you every page but that one."

Another silence, longer. And then, softly, in the voice of the ninth—the nameless one, the deepest, the one whose tone felt like the bottom of a well—

*Careful, little carver. You are close to the shape of the truth, and the truth has edges.*

"Show me," Sable said, "or I put the lid down and I don't open it again. I'll melt the box. I'll sink it in the sump. I've got friends downstairs who owe me favors and a city full of priests who'd pay for the privilege. You need me more than I need you—we both know it, and we both know I know it. So. The eleventh year. Or the dark."

The chord trembled. And then, with the reluctance of a debtor opening his ledger to the creditor he despises, the nine began to speak—and what they showed him first was not a battlefield, not a burning, but a single room in a single tower in Vael Correnne, where eight of the nine sovereigns stood around a table arguing in voices low enough that the walls themselves strained to listen, and the argument was about a weapon, and the weapon was not fire, and the ones they intended to use it on were not soldiers.

Above him, faint through the Undercroft's stone, a bell began to ring the evening hours—and Sable, deep in the vision, did not notice that the bell tolled thirteen times, or that Pip was standing at the top of the stairs with a lantern, watching the strongbox glow.
