## 14. Pip's Price

Pip had three names, and he was about to lose the only one that mattered.

The first name he'd been born with, in a rookery off Tanner's Row, to a mother who'd traded it away for a jug of gin before he could walk. The second was the one the streets gave him—Ratling, Sparrow, whatever came easiest to whoever was shouting. The third was *Pip*, and Odile Vess had given him that one herself, seven years ago, when she'd caught him lifting apples from the guild's kitchen and instead of calling the watch had said, *You've quick hands and quicker eyes. Want honest work?* It hadn't been honest work, not really. Nobody in the Vellum Guild did honest work. But it had been *his* work, his name, his place, and now the place was a hole under the city and the name was the only thing left in his pockets.

That was why he was doing it. That was what he told himself, anyway, as he sat on an overturned crate in the fish-smoke gloom of Saltmarket Row with his knees drawn up and his hood down just enough to look harmless. He wasn't betraying anyone. He was *feeding* them. There was a difference. Brannoc's stores were running thin—Pip had counted, because Pip always counted—and Sable hadn't eaten properly in days, too busy staring at that cursed glowing bone like it was going to sing him a lullaby. Somebody had to put food in the pot. And somebody had to find out how close the Inquisition was to kicking in their door.

Both of those somebodies were Pip. That was the arrangement he'd made with himself, sitting up at night while Brannoc snored like a landslide and Sable murmured in his sleep in nine voices that made Pip's skin crawl. He'd tell himself: *You're not selling them out. You're buying them time.* And mostly, mostly, he believed it.

The man across from him was called Ferro Gask, and he had a face like a dropped pie and the eyes of a bookkeeper counting someone else's funeral. He worked for the Inquisition—not as one of them, never as one of them, but as one of the little gears that turned around them. An informant of informants. A man who bought whispers wholesale and sold them retail.

"You're late," Gask said.

"Streets are crawling." Pip kept his voice bored. Bored was safe. Scared got you followed. "Half the watch on Saltmarket are new faces. Grey cloaks."

Gask's eyes sharpened. "Grey cloaks where?"

"That's worth more than bread money." Pip held out his hand, palm up, and hated himself for how steady it was. "Copper first. You know the rule."

Gask dropped two coins into his palm, then a third, watching Pip's face the whole time like a man testing a floorboard. "The grey cloaks, boy."

"They're asking after a bone-carver. Youngish. Dark hair. They've got a drawing." Pip turned the coins over, bit one, tucked all three away. "And they're paying street kids to look at doorways. Not watchmen. Kids. Because nobody watches a kid."

Something moved behind Gask's face—satisfaction, maybe, or appetite. "Anyone else? Anyone you know?"

Here it was. The edge of the thing. Pip felt it under his feet like the lip of a stair in the dark.

"There's a woman," he said slowly, "who comes out of the Undercroft stairs by the tannery every third night. Buys candles. Lots of candles. More than a person needs who isn't hiding something underground."

It was true. It was also a lie, because the woman was Brannoc's cousin twice removed and the candles were for them, and if Gask pulled that thread the whole warren would unravel. But it was *small*. A thread, not a rope. Something to keep Gask fed and looking sideways while the real business—the grey cloaks, the drawings, the kids at doorways—stayed buried in Pip's other pocket.

Gask leaned forward. "Where does she go after?"

"Nowhere. She goes home. I told you what I saw." Pip stood, brushing off his breeches, making himself small and done. "Same time next week. If your coin holds."

"Pip." Gask said his name softly, and something in the softness made the hairs on Pip's neck stand up. "You're a good ear. The best I've got. But good ears get eaten if they hear too much and say too little. You understand me?"

"I understand coin," Pip said, and walked away without hurrying, which took everything he had.

* * *

He should have gone straight back. That was the rule—sell, walk, lose your shadow, come home. But the baker on Cordwainer Lane threw out day-old loaves at dusk, and there were six mouths down in the Undercroft instead of four now that Vale's defection had brought... no. Wait. That hadn't happened yet. Pip sometimes got the order of things tangled; the last weeks had been one long falling staircase. What had happened was simpler and worse: Corvin was still among them, smiling his guild smile, and Sable was spending hours a day with the relic, and Brannoc had started drinking the medicinal brandy that was supposed to be for wounds, and Pip was thirteen years old and carrying all of it in a sack that used to hold apples.

So he took the bread. Three loaves, stuffed under his shirt, warm as living things against his ribs. And because he had the bread, he took the long way, and because he took the long way, he didn't see the second man until the second man stepped out of the alley and put a hand on his shoulder.

"Not so fast, sparrow."

Pip knew that voice too. Dree Hallow, a watchman's runner, a year older than Pip and twice as hungry, working the same streets from the other side. Dree smiled with all his teeth. Behind him stood a grey cloak—a real one, tall, faceless under its hood, and Pip's stomach dropped through the cobbles into the dark water below the city.

"Dree says you've been talking to Gask," the grey cloak said. Its voice was a woman's, flat and patient. "Gask sells to us. Which means you sell to us. Which means you know things." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Or we can assume you know nothing, and take you in anyway, and ask questions where questions are asked. Your choice."

"I'm nobody," Pip said. "I sell weather. I sell rumors. I don't know anything."

"The woman with the candles," the grey cloak said, and Pip's blood went to ice water. "Undercroft stairs. Tannery side. We already know. We want to know *where she goes*."

They already knew. The words rang in Pip's skull like a cracked bell. Gask had sold him out even as Pip was selling to him—or worse, Gask had never been the buyer at all, just the funnel, and Pip had been pouring himself down it for weeks. Every small truth he'd traded to buy safety had been a brick in a road leading straight to the tannery stairs.

"I don't follow her," Pip said. His voice came out younger than he wanted. "I swear it. I see her, I sell what I see, I go."

"Then this will be easy." The grey cloak produced a scrap of paper and a stub of charcoal and held them out. "Draw me the way in. The passages under the tannery. Where the stairs lead. Do that, and you walk away with silver instead of irons. Refuse—" the hood tilted, considering him, almost gently, "—and we burn the Undercroft district anyway, starting with everyone in it, and your name will be in our report as the child who helped us find it. Do you understand what happens to informants when the people they informed on come to harm?"

Pip understood. He understood it so completely that for one long moment he couldn't breathe. They would hang the word *informant* around his neck like a millstone, and Brannoc—who had burned a village once, who carried his guilt like a stone in his chest—would look at Pip and see exactly the kind of creature he'd spent his whole life despising. Sable would look at him and see the reason Odile was ash. The family would close like a fist.

And if he refused—if he drew nothing—they'd take him anyway, and search him, and find the bread, and find the coins with Gask's mint-marks, and it would end the same except slower and louder.

His hand came up. He watched it come up like it belonged to somebody else. It took the charcoal.

"Just the tannery stairs," he heard himself say. "That's all I know. I swear that's all I know."

He drew. His hand shook and the lines wobbled and he drew them anyway: the arch behind the candle-seller's shop, the descending stair, the first landing. His charcoal hovered over the place where the passage forked—one way to the smugglers' warren, one way to a dead-end cellar full of rainwater and rats—and Pip, thirteen years old, three names deep in his own lies, made the choice that would define him for the rest of his life or end it tonight.

He drew the dead end.

"There," he said, pushing the paper back. "That's everything. There's a flooded cellar and nothing else. If you kick that door in you'll find rats and my honesty."

The grey cloak studied the drawing for a long moment. Long enough for Pip to count his own heartbeats and run out of fingers. Then the hood nodded, once.

"Silver," the grey cloak said, and dropped three coins into his hand, heavy and cold. "Come to the chapter-house on Bell Street tomorrow. Ask for the grey desk. There's more work for ears like yours."

Pip walked away. He did not run. Running was a confession written on the body. He walked, one foot and then the other, past the baker's dark window, past the gutter where the day's filth ran seaward, around the corner, down the next street, and then he ran, he ran until his lungs tore, three loaves of bread thumping against his ribs like hearts, three pieces of silver biting into his palm, and behind him he could feel the drawing fluttering in the grey cloak's grip like a moth that had flown the wrong way.

He'd drawn the dead end. He'd saved them. He'd saved them and damned himself, because tomorrow they'd kick in a flooded cellar and find rats and no honesty at all, and then they'd come back for the boy who lied to the Inquisition, and there was nowhere in the world left to run except down.

* * *

Brannoc met him at the bottom of the stairs.

Pip had meant to slip in quiet, mean to hide the silver, mean to bury the whole night somewhere deep and never let it surface. But Brannoc was sitting at the foot of the steps in the lamplight with his one hand wrapped around a cup of brandy he wasn't drinking, and he looked up at Pip with those old soldier's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and Pip knew, in the marrow-deep way you know a storm is coming, that it was already over.

"You're late," Brannoc said.

"Bread run."

"You're limping."

"I'm not."

"You're holding your right hand closed like it owes you money." Brannoc set down the cup. It made a very small sound against the stone, and somehow that sound was worse than shouting. "Open it."

"Brannoc—"

"*Open it.*"

Pip opened it. Three silver coins lay in his palm, catching the lamplight, and above them Brannoc's face went through a slow terrible journey—from confusion to recognition to a kind of grief so vast and quiet that Pip would have preferred rage. Rage he knew how to survive. This was different. This was Brannoc looking at him the way you look at a wound you should have bandaged days ago.

"Where," Brannoc said, and his voice was gravel dragged over gravel, "did you get these."

"Ferro Gask. Off Saltmarket. I've been—I've been selling him things. Little things. Weather. Faces. Nothing that—"

"Nothing that matters." Brannoc rose. He was not a tall man anymore—war had taken height along with the hand—but he filled the passage the way a landslide fills a valley. "Say it plain, boy. Say exactly what you sold him."

"A woman buying candles by the tannery stairs." Pip's voice cracked down the middle. "I thought—I thought if I gave him something small, he'd stop digging. I was *protecting*—"

"You were feeding yourself." Brannoc said it without heat, which was the cruelest way to say it. "Feeding us, maybe, in your head. But a lie you tell to protect people stops being protection the moment someone else starts telling it for you. Did you think Gask was your friend? Did you think the Inquisition pays silver for *weather*?"

"They know about the stairs." The words tore out of him before he could stop them, and once they started, everything else came with them, six weeks of nights spilling onto the stone floor. "They know about the candles, they stopped me tonight, a grey cloak, they made me draw the way in, and I drew them the dead-end cellar, I lied, Brannoc, I lied to the Inquisition's face, and tomorrow when they break that door and find nothing they'll come back and they'll know I lied and they'll—"

He ran out of air. He stood there shaking, bread crumbs on his shirt, silver in his open hand, thirteen years old and finally, completely empty.

Behind Brannoc, at the mouth of the inner passage, Sable stood very still. How long he'd been there, Pip couldn't say. Long enough. Long enough to have heard all of it.

Sable came forward slowly. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked hollowed out—weeks of listening to nine dead sovereigns had worn grooves in his face—but his eyes, when they found Pip's, were not hard. That was the worst part. They weren't hard at all.

"You drew them a false map," Sable said quietly.

"To save us." Pip's chin came up, defiant and drowning at once. "I lied for you. All of you. I've been lying for weeks to keep food in this hole and nobody ever asked where it came from, nobody ever *wanted* to ask—"

"No," Sable said. "We didn't ask. That's ours to carry, not yours." He looked at Brannoc. "He lied to the Inquisition. For us. Think about what that costs him tomorrow."

"It costs him everything if they trace him here first." Brannoc's jaw worked. "One drawing, one description, one grey cloak remembering a limping boy with bread under his shirt, and they walk down those stairs while we sleep. I've seen what happens to havens that trust the wrong child. I *burned*—"

He stopped. The sentence died in his throat like a bird hitting glass, and Pip watched the old soldier wrestle his own ghost in front of both of them. When Brannoc spoke again, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, aimed as much at himself as at Pip.

"I burned a village full of people who trusted someone young and scared and hungry," he said. "Someone who told themselves the small betrayal was the price of the big survival. So hear me, boy, and hear me once. I am not angry because you lied. I'm angry because you lied *alone*. Family doesn't mean nobody sins. Family means nobody sins where the rest of us can't help carry it."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere far off in the dark, keeping the Undercroft's slow time.

"So what happens now?" Pip whispered. "Do I go?"

Brannoc looked at Sable. Sable looked at Pip for a long moment, and then he did something none of them expected: he held out his hand, palm up, the same gesture Pip had made to Gask an hour before.

"Give me the silver," Sable said. "All three coins. Tomorrow, Corvin takes them to Bell Street chapter-house and asks for the grey desk, wearing your description and your limp. Let the Inquisition chase a ghost that leads them wherever we want. And you—" his hand closed around the coins, and something flickered across his face, some faint echo of the nine voices, quickly mastered, "—you stay. You eat. And the next time the weight gets this heavy, you wake me up and put half of it in my arms. That's the price. That's all it costs. Agreed?"

Pip's eyes were burning. He scrubbed his sleeve across his face and stuck out his hand, and Sable shook it, solemn as a treaty.

"Agreed," Pip managed.

Brannoc grunted—an old soldier's blessing, worth more than any speech—and turned toward the storeroom. "There's stew left. Eat it before Sable gives it to the ghosts."

But as they moved toward the smell of onions and salvation, the lamp nearest the stairs guttered—though there was no draft—and every piece of bone in the vault room, hanging from its hooks and cords, began to chime. Softly. Sweetly. Like distant bells heard through water.

Sable went white. "They heard him," he breathed. "The Court heard him lie to save us. And they *liked* it."

From the vault room, clear as a struck bell, came a voice that was not Sable's—nine voices braided into one, warm as honey and old as graves:

*"Bring us the boy, little carver. Loyalty like that deserves a throne."*
