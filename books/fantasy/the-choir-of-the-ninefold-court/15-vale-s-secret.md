## 15. Vale's Secret

The cell smelled of tallow and old straw, and the man inside it had not stopped praying since dawn.

Maren Vale stood in the corridor outside with the case file under her arm and her gloves on, as she always wore them now, as she had worn them for eleven years. Lambskin, supple, unremarkable—the kind of gloves any Inquisitor might wear into a cell where a suspect had been known to bite. No one looked twice at them. That was the point of them. That had always been the point of them.

"Name," said Warden Cress, unlocking the door.

"You have his name."

"He hasn't said it himself. Not once. Just the prayers." Cress shrugged, a big shapeless man who had grown comfortable around other people's fear. "Begging's not talking, is it?"

Maren didn't answer. She stepped past him into the cell.

The man was younger than his file suggested—files always made people older, heavier, more finished than they were. He was maybe twenty-five, with ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles that no scrubbing would ever lift them, and he was kneeling on the stone with his hands folded so tightly that the blood had left his fingers. A scribe. The file said scribe. The file also said *suspected trafficker in the dead*, *informer reliable*, *witness places him at three night-markets*, and *recommended for registration or pyre, per the Proclamation*.

He looked up when she entered, and his prayers stumbled and died in his mouth.

"Inquisitor." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "Inquisitor, I'm not—I never—"

"Sit down properly," Maren said. "You'll want your hands free."

He stared at her. Then, slowly, like a man dismantling something he'd spent years building, he unfolded his hands and sat back against the wall.

Maren set the file down on the cell's single stool. She did not open it. She pulled off one glove.

This was the part no one knew. This was the thing she kept folded away beneath lambskin and procedure and the flat gray authority of her rank: that Maren Vale had been born with a gift, small and quiet and useless to anyone but herself—a touch for truth. Skin to skin, and she could feel whether a person's words were true. Not read minds. Not see visions. Just a warmth or a coldness, rising through her palm like water through cloth. True words were warm. Lies were cold as riverbed stones. It had seemed, when she was nine years old and holding her mother's hand, like the most natural thing in the world. Her mother had thought so too, right up until the first purges, right up until the night she'd pressed Maren's bare hand against her own cheek and whispered, *never let them know what you are*, and then gone out to buy bread and never come home.

Eleven years of gloves. Eleven years of asking questions she already knew the answers to, so that no one would wonder why an Inquisitor never touched anyone. Eleven years of feeling the truth of the world only through wool and leather, muffled, like hearing music through a wall.

She crouched in front of the scribe.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"T-Tomas Ellery." And warmth, faint and steady, through her bare fingers as she took his hand in both of hers—as if she were comforting him. As if this were mercy.

"Did you sell words to the dead? Charms, messages, anything carried between the living and those beyond?"

"No." Warmth again. Warmer.

"Were you at the night-market on the third of the month?"

"Yes." Warmth—but thinner, and Maren felt the shape beneath it, the way one feels the bone under flesh. He had been there. But the warmth told her the rest before he said it: he'd gone to buy medicine for his sister, from a woman who sold it cheap because she was gifted and couldn't sell it anywhere else. He hadn't known what she was until after. He'd been too frightened to say so.

"The informer who named you," Maren said. "Do you know him?"

"No." Warm. And then, because terror makes honest men stupid: "I think he owed me money. I lent him coin last spring and he never—he said if he ever got the chance he'd—" He stopped himself, eyes going wide, realizing what he'd just confessed to an Inquisitor of the crown.

Maren released his hand and stood.

The warmth stayed in her palm a moment longer, fading like bathwater cooling. Innocent. Entirely innocent, except for a debt and a kindness and being in the wrong market on the wrong night. The file recommended the pyre. The Proclamation did not distinguish between a bone-singer and a boy who'd bought feverfew from one.

"Inquisitor?" Tomas Ellery whispered. "Am I going to burn?"

*Yes,* said the file. *Yes,* said the Proclamation, signed in the King's name, countersigned by Commander Hesse, whose word was law in this city now. *Yes,* said the machine Maren had served for eleven years, the machine that had taken her mother and given her a rank and a pair of gloves and a purpose that fit her like armor fits a corpse.

"No," Maren heard herself say.

Cress, in the corridor, shifted his weight audibly.

"The informer's testimony is compromised," Maren said, loud enough for the warden to hear, in the voice she used for reports. "Personal grievance. Financial dispute. I'm marking the testimony unreliable and the suspect cleared of trafficking." She picked up the file. "He'll be released with a warning about unlicensed purchases. Get him some water; he's been praying all day and it's bad for the throat."

She walked out before her face could do anything her rank would regret.

* * *

The report took her twenty minutes to write and cost her something she could not name.

She sat in the Inquisition's scriptorium, a long cold room full of clerks scratching away at the machinery of the hunt, and she wrote: *Testimony of informant (name withheld per regulation) assessed and found motivated by personal grievance. Subject Tomas Ellery, scribe, questioned under standard procedure. No evidence of trafficking with the dead. Recommend release.* Every word of it true, which was its own kind of joke. She had learned long ago that the safest lies were built entirely out of true sentences arranged in merciful orders.

What she did not write: how she knew. What she had done with her bare hand. What she was.

A clerk came by and collected the page without looking at her. Somewhere across the room, another Inquisitor dictated a list of names for tomorrow's pyres, unhurried, the way a man reads a shopping list. Maren listened to the names and felt nothing, which frightened her more than feeling would have. She had been careful for so long to feel nothing that the nothing had started to feel permanent.

Except lately it wasn't nothing. Lately there was Odile Vess.

Three days now since the guild-mistress had been taken, and Maren had requested conduct of the interrogation personally—filed the request in triplicate, gave reasons of jurisdiction and continuity of evidence, all of it sound, all of it defensible. None of it true. The true reason was a lullaby.

She had gone into that cell expecting defiance or collapse, and instead the old woman had looked at her with eyes like a hawk considering weather, and when Maren had recited the charges, Odile Vess had begun to hum. Low, half under her breath, a tune with a skipping rhythm and a falling end—and Maren's whole body had gone cold, because she knew that tune. There was only one person alive who should have known that tune. Her mother had sung it to her over a cracked washbasin in a house that no longer existed, in a dialect of it that wasn't written anywhere, and her mother had been dead for eleven years, and this stranger in chains was humming it note-perfect.

Odile had watched her realize it. Had smiled, very slightly, and hummed the second verse.

Maren had ended the session early. She had filed no report on the lullaby. She had gone back to her quarters and sat in the dark with her gloves in her lap like shed skins, and she had not slept.

There were explanations. There were always explanations. Coincidences of folk music; shared regions of origin; a song that traveled. Maren Vale was a professional, and professionals did not build cases on lullabies, and they certainly did not build doubts on them.

But she had touched Odile Vess's wrist while binding the manacles. Bare finger to old skin, one careless second.

Cold. Cold as deep well water, colder than any lie she had ever felt.

Which meant the old woman believed whatever she was hiding. Believed it down to the marrow. People could lie about facts and feel warm about it if they'd convinced themselves; Maren had learned that early. But conviction that ran that cold meant the truth underneath was enormous, and terrible, and held without flinching.

An innocent woman would have felt warm saying *I don't know what you're talking about*. Odile Vess had felt like January.

So either the guild-mistress was the deepest liar Maren had ever touched—or she was telling the truth about something so large that innocence and guilt had stopped being useful words.

Maren dipped her pen and wrote the next line of the report on Tomas Ellery, and her hand was steady, and she hated it.

* * *

Commander Hesse found her at dusk on the pyre-yard wall.

He did that sometimes—appeared beside her without footsteps, a broad gray man with a voice like gravel poured slowly. He had been kind to her once, years ago, in the blunt way of men who promote people they can use. He had never asked why she wore gloves. She had always suspected he noticed everything and cared about nothing that didn't advance him.

"Ellery," he said. "Your release order. The scribe."

"He's clean. The informer had a grudge."

"Hm." Hesse looked out over the yard, where the pyre-wood was stacked in neat courses for the week's burnings, tarred and ready, orderly as cordwood. "We've had four informants this month retract or contradict themselves. The Guild's people are getting to them. Money, threats, who knows." He turned his head, and his eyes were pale and patient. "I'd hate to think our methods were slipping, Vale. You have the best clearance rate in the city. Best in the province. Whatever you're doing in those cells, keep doing it."

*Touching them,* Maren thought. *Taking off my glove and letting the truth run into my hand like blood into cloth.*

"I will," she said.

"And the Vess woman." He said the name lightly, but Maren had learned to hear the weight under Hesse's lightness; everything he said lightly mattered. "No progress?"

"She gives riddles. She's old and stubborn and she enjoys the attention."

"She's enjoying it less every day. The pyre's scheduled." He paused. "Unless you give me a reason to move it. Evidence. A confession. Something real, Vale—not riddles." He clapped her shoulder, gloved shoulder, and walked away along the wall, and Maren stood in the failing light and understood with perfect clarity that she had perhaps six days to decide what Odile Vess was worth.

Six days. Maybe fewer. Hesse did not bluff about schedules.

* * *

Her quarters were two rooms above the armory, spare as a cell by choice—she had refused the larger quarters that came with her rank, because large rooms echoed and echoes sounded like accusations. She lit one lamp. She took off her gloves, both of them, and laid them on the table, and looked at her hands.

They were ordinary hands. Ink-stained at the side of the right palm, a scar across one knuckle from a suspect's knife years ago. Hands that had verified the guilt of forty-one people and the innocence of—she made herself count—nine. Nine people she had quietly saved with falsified reports, one sentence of mercy at a time, over eleven years. Nine against forty-one. Nine small treasons buried in a career of obedience.

And every one of the forty-one had been guilty. That was the arithmetic she clung to on bad nights. She had never condemned an innocent. She had only ever failed to save them all.

But the hunt was changing. The Proclamation had changed it. Before, the Inquisition had hunted crimes—trafficking, coercion, the dead used against the living—and Maren could tell herself she hunted harm. Now the crime was simply existing. Being born with warmth in your palm. Buying feverfew from the wrong woman. The files came in thicker every week, and the informers multiplied like rats, and the pyre-yard woodpile grew, and Maren had begun to dream about it—not the burning, but the stacking. Neat courses of tarred wood, stretching past the walls of the yard, filling streets, and her walking the rows with her ledger, checking each stick for truth.

She put her gloves back on.

Then she took them off again, and went to the locked drawer of her desk, and took out the thing she had sworn she would never need: a single sheet of paper, bought years ago from a stationer who dealt in discreet things, blank except for a watermark visible only by lamplight. Paper for letters that could not be traced to the Inquisition. She had bought it the way one buys a rope to keep in a cellar—for emergencies one refuses to imagine.

She sat with the pen above it for a long time.

If she wrote to someone—if she warned someone—she was not an Inquisitor anymore. Everything ended: the rank, the access, the ability to stand in cells and take hands and pull nine innocents out of the fire one report at a time. Treason was not a thing you did in moderation. She knew the procedure for traitors better than anyone; she had written half of it herself.

But Odile Vess hummed a dead woman's lullaby and felt like winter when she lied, which meant she wasn't lying, which meant somewhere in this city was a truth so heavy that the Inquisition's whole machinery would break against it—and Maren Vale, best clearance rate in the province, keeper of the gloves, was the only person positioned to find out what it was. From the inside. For six more days.

She did not write the letter. She folded the blank paper away, and blew out the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the armory clock count the hours toward Odile Vess's pyre.

Sleep came late and thin, and the dream, when it came, was new. Not the woodpile this time. A bell—one bell, deep under the city, tolling once, and every dead soul she had ever touched with her bare hand turning their faces toward the sound. And beneath the bell, almost too low to hear, nine voices singing in harmony, and the harmony was beautiful, and that was the worst part. It was beautiful, and it was waking up.

She woke before dawn with her bare hand clenched around her gloves, and she dressed, and pinned on the gray cloak, and went down into the city that still smelled of night-market smoke—to the cells, to Odile Vess, to the lullaby and the cold and the six days.

On the way, she passed a notice-board where a new proclamation was being pasted up, and she stopped, because the seal on it was Hesse's own, and the heading was large enough to read from horseback:

*BY ORDER OF THE INQUISITION: ALL PERSONS SHELTERING THE GIFTED WITHIN THE UNDERCROFT WILL BE ACCOUNTED TRAITORS TOGETHER WITH THEM.*

The Undercroft. The smugglers' warren under the east quarter, where half the hidden city lived like roots under a floor. Hesse wasn't hunting individuals anymore. He was hunting the ground itself.

Maren stood in the gray light reading the notice twice, and her gloved hands hung at her sides like weapons she no longer knew how to hold, and somewhere beneath her feet—she would have sworn it, later, though she knew it was impossible—she felt a bell begin to toll.
