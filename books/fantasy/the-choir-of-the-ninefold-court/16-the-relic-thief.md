## 16. The Relic Thief

The relic had learned to hum.

Sable noticed it first on the third morning in the Undercroft, when the tunnels were quiet except for Brannoc's one-handed chopping of onions and Pip's snoring from the cot by the drainage grate. A sound beneath the silence, thin as wire drawn through wax. Not a voice yet—voices came later, layered and sovereign and terrible—but the promise of voices, the way a held breath is the promise of speech.

He had wrapped it in three layers of wool and sealed it in an iron ration box, and still it hummed.

"You could stop listening," Brannoc said without looking up from his cutting board. The man had a way of speaking that made every sentence sound like a report from a losing front. "That's what I'd do. I spent thirty years learning not to hear things."

"It's not the hearing that's the problem." Sable turned the iron box over in his hands, feeling the vibration through the metal like a wasp trapped under glass. "It's that it's getting louder. Whatever Odile did to bind it—the work she died protecting—it's loosening. Every day it's a little looser."

"Then we bury it deeper."

"Burial is how it got here in the first place." He set the box down on the scarred plank table between them, between the onions and the lamp. "Someone buried it once, Brannoc. Someone bound it and hid it and told no one, and now it's waking up in a smugglers' warren under a city full of Inquisitors, and the only people who know what it is are a deserter, a street thief, and me."

Brannoc's knife paused. His missing hand twitched at the wrist-stump the way it sometimes did, reaching for something that wasn't there anymore. "And Corvin," he said.

Sable looked up. "What about Corvin?"

"He's been asking questions." Brannoc resumed chopping, each stroke precise, deliberate, the violence of a man who had learned to make small motions do the work of large ones. "How long we're staying. Where the other entrances come up. Who else knows about the box." The knife stopped again. "I don't like it. I've fed men who asked those questions before, and I've dug graves for the ones who answered them."

Corvin had come to them nine days ago, gray-faced and shaking, claiming the Inquisition had raided his lodging house and burned his papers. He'd been a guild man for years—a surveyor of impossible things, the one who appraised doors and distances and the weight of a name—and Sable had known him since before any of this started, back when the worst secret in the Vellum Guild was how much Odile overcharged for bottled laughter. Corvin had wept when he heard Odile was taken. He'd wept again when they told him about the pyre yard, about the rescue that failed, about the way her last words had been instructions rather than goodbye.

Grief made him trustworthy, Sable had thought. Grief made him one of theirs.

But grief also made a man reckless, and recklessness made him cheap, and cheap men sold things.

"I'll talk to him," Sable said.

"Don't talk to him." Brannoc swept the onions into the pot with the flat of the blade. "Watch him. There's a difference. Talking tells you what a man wants you to know. Watching tells you what he can't help."

* * *

Sable watched.

He watched Corvin volunteer for the water runs, which took him up through the cistern galleries where the watch couldn't follow but neither could anyone see him. He watched Corvin count the lamps, count the cots, count the days aloud in a voice pitched just casual enough to seem like boredom. He watched Corvin stand too long outside the alcove where the iron box sat behind a loose stone, his shadow lying across the gap in the wall like a finger pointing.

On the sixth night of watching, Sable followed him.

The Undercroft at night was a country unto itself: old smugglers' galleries stitched through the bones of some older city, brick upon brick upon stone so ancient no one remembered laying it. Brannoc's people moved through it with lamps shuttered to slits, and the dark between the lights was thick enough to drink. Sable had lived above ground his whole life, among ledgers and lamplight and the polite murmuring of the dead, and even now, after weeks below, the dark pressed on him like a hand testing fruit at market.

Corvin's lamp moved ahead of him, shuttered low, bobbing along the Gallery of Nine Arches toward the cistern stairs. But instead of turning up toward the water, Corvin stopped at the third arch—the one that didn't lead anywhere, the one Brannoc called the Debt Arch because it had cost two smugglers their lives to learn that its far end was bricked shut—and knelt.

And pulled a brick loose.

Sable flattened himself against the cold of the archway, heart going hard, and watched Corvin reach into the cavity and draw out something long and flat wrapped in oilcloth. Even shuttered, the lamp caught the edge of it: paper. Folded paper. Many sheets.

A correspondence. Kept in a dead-end arch where no one would look, because no one had reason to.

Corvin read by the sliver of lamplight, lips moving, and Sable watched his face change—not guilt, not fear, but calculation, the particular tightening around the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Then Corvin folded the papers away, replaced the brick with a mason's practiced ease, and stood, and stood there a moment longer than a man with nothing to hide would stand, listening the way Brannoc listened, the way soldiers listen.

Sable drew back into the dark and did not breathe until Corvin's light had gone up the cistern stairs.

He should have woken Brannoc. He should have woken Pip, who slept like a cat with one ear open, who knew fifty ways into and out of the Undercroft and would have had opinions, loud ones, useful ones. Instead Sable went alone to the Debt Arch, worked the brick loose with his carving knife, and read by touch and memory what Corvin had hidden there.

The letters were in a neat commercial hand he recognized from a hundred guild manifests. They were addressed to *Undersecretary Halloway, Office of Sequestration*, and they were dated across the past nine days, and they described, with a surveyor's terrible precision, everything.

The layout of the Undercroft. The number of residents—eleven, then fourteen, then nineteen as the Gift-Hunt drove more of the guild's outcasts underground. The location of the iron box. And in the most recent letter, written in a hand gone hurried, the words that stopped Sable's breath:

*The relic is the ninefold binding. I have confirmed it. The bone-carver Galloway keeps it within reach at all hours. It can be taken. Name your price and your men and I will deliver both.*

Both. Both the relic and Sable himself.

He knelt there in the dark with the letters trembling in his hands and felt something he hadn't felt since the raid on the guild hall, since Odile's hands pushing him through the impossible door while boots came down the stair: the specific loneliness of being hunted by someone who had eaten at your table.

Because that was the thing about Corvin. Corvin had carved the frame for Sable's first masterwork. Corvin had taught him the guild cipher for pricing grief. When Sable's mother died, Corvin had been the one who sat with him through the wake and said nothing, which was the only right thing anyone said that week.

And underneath all of it, underneath the arithmetic and the oilcloth and the neat commercial hand, Sable could feel the shape of the fear that had made him do it. Corvin had no gift. That was the joke of it, the bitter guild joke—that a man could spend his life among the impossible and remain entirely possible himself. Corvin had watched the gifted burn and known that his only crime would be proximity, association, love. And so he had decided to purchase his innocence with the lives of the guilty, and had told himself, surely, every night, that it wasn't betrayal, it was survival, it was arithmetic, and arithmetic kept you alive.

Sable understood it completely. That was the worst part. He understood it the way you understand a blade coming at you: precisely, intimately, and much too late to matter.

He copied the letters—he had ledgers in his blood; copying was breathing to him—replaced them exactly as they lay, reset the brick, and went back through the dark to find Brannoc.

* * *

They argued in whispers over the banked stove while the rest of the warren slept.

"We take the letters," Brannoc said. "Tonight. Before his next delivery."

"Then he knows we know, and whoever he reports to sends someone else, someone we haven't counted." Sable spread the copies on the table, weighted at the corners with the salt dish and a spoon. "Look at the dates. Three days between letters. There's a pickup point—we just have to find it, and then we know who's receiving, and then—" He stopped, because the next part of the sentence was ugly. "And then we feed him."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Feed him lies, you mean."

"I mean we use him. Yes." Sable found he couldn't look at the word *both* anymore, so he turned the page face-down. "Odile used to say the guild ran on two currencies, coin and information, and only fools paid in coin."

"She also said trust was the only inventory that appreciated." Brannoc stared into the stove's red throat. "She'd have hated this."

"She'd have survived it. That was her whole religion." Sable rubbed his eyes. Behind his lids, uninvited, the relic hummed. He had left it in its box, but distance meant nothing to it anymore; it had gotten inside his hearing the way cold gets inside a coat. *We know,* the nine murmured, not in words, never quite in words, in the pressure of attention. *We know what he has done. We know what men are. Give us leave and we will show you his heart, and whether it is black or merely frightened.*

"No," Sable said aloud.

Brannoc glanced up. "No what?"

"Nothing. Them." He tapped his own temple. "They've been offering to help all evening. Apparently treachery offends them. Tyrants have such strong feelings about loyalty."

Something almost like a smile crossed Brannoc's ruined face and died there. "So what's the play?"

"The play is we let him steal it."

Pip's voice came out of the dark by the drainage grate, sleep-rough and furious. "You want to let him *what?*"

Sable hadn't heard her wake. Neither, apparently, had Brannoc, whose hand had gone to the knife on the table and relaxed by degrees. Pip sat up in her nest of blankets, hair like a crow's riot, thirteen years old and older than the city some mornings. "You want to hand the ninefold Court of dead kings to the Inquisition because—why? As a trick? Have you met tricks? Tricks go wrong. I've run tricks. I'm *good* at tricks and they go wrong."

"If we take the letters, Corvin vanishes into the wind and we spend the rest of our lives waiting for the knock on the wall." Sable kept his voice level, though his pulse wasn't. "If we let him take the relic—if we make sure he takes it, if we make it easy—then he leads us straight to his buyer. To the office. To the chain of command. And we swap the real relic for something else before it ever leaves his hands."

"Swap it with what?" Brannoc asked.

"With something that sings." Sable looked at the alcove, at the loose stone, at the iron box that had hummed against his palms for weeks. "I've been carving for fifteen years. I can carve a lie in bone that even the dead would believe. A charm with nine false voices in it—loud ones, hungry ones. Let the Inquisition open their prize and find something that screams and promises and delivers nothing. Let them waste their pyres on it. Meanwhile we hold the true binding, and we know exactly who wants it and how badly."

Pip was quiet for a moment, which from Pip was roughly equivalent to a minute of silence from anyone else. "And Corvin?" she said finally. "When it's done. When he's served his purpose and we don't need him walking our walls anymore. What happens to Corvin?"

Nobody answered. The stove ticked. Somewhere far up the galleries, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"That's what I thought," Pip said, and lay back down, and pulled the blanket over her head, and said, muffled, "I liked him. He taught me dice. This is why I don't like people," which everyone present understood to be the exact opposite of the truth, and no one said so.

* * *

The carving took two days.

Sable worked in the driest gallery, by a lamp Brannoc shielded with his body, shaping a knucklebone of ox into something that had never been an ox. He packed it with lies the way you pack a sausage: the hunger of nine sovereigns rendered in grain and whorl, ambition without a soul behind it, bells with no bell-tower. He sang to it as he worked—not the Court's song, but the counter-song, the one Odile had taught him in the vault a lifetime ago, the one that made a relic *insist*. By the second midnight it hummed convincingly. By dawn it hummed beautifully. If you didn't know—if you weren't the one who had emptied it—you would have sworn there were kings inside.

His hands shook when he finished. Not from the work. From the realization, arriving late and cold, that he had just done the thing the Court had offered to do for him: he had built a falsehood and given it a voice. The difference, he told himself, was consent. The difference was that the lie would be caught. The difference was Odile's voice in his memory saying *the guild trades in impossible things, Sable, not in untrue ones*, and the smaller, meaner voice underneath saying *she's dead, and you're improvising.*

"Will it fool Vale?" Brannoc asked quietly.

The question landed harder than intended. Maren Vale, who interrogated like a woman reading her own conscience; who had taken Odile and, by every report Pip's street network could gather, had not broken her; who had requested personal custody of the prisoner for reasons no one could explain and no one trusted. If Vale touched the false relic—if she had any gift for truth, as rumor sometimes whispered about the best Inquisitors—

"Vale isn't the buyer," Sable said. "Corvin's letters go to an undersecretary. Bureaucrats buy relics; Inquisitors burn them. We get it past Halloway's clerks and it's a problem for people who deserve problems."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong," Sable said, "and we lose everything, and at least we'll lose it knowing."

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. "That's not comfort, Galloway."

"It's not meant to be."

* * *

They made the theft easy, and ease was the cruelest thing they did to him.

On the appointed night, Sable drank with Corvin in the common gallery—cheap wine, real conversation, laughter that hurt on both sides, because Corvin was genuinely funny and genuinely afraid and genuinely, in whatever remained of his heart, fond of him. Sable let himself be fond back. It was not entirely performance, which was the trap he built for himself and stepped in willingly.

Then he pleaded exhaustion, went to his alcove in view of half the warren, and left the iron box—unlocked, for the first time ever—beside his cot while he "slept."

Inside the box, wrapped in the same three layers of wool, lay the false relic. The true binding had spent the afternoon in a hollowed-out leg of the stove, humming its patient sovereign hum, waiting.

Corvin came sometime past the deepest hour. Sable heard him—the careful tread, the held breath, the small metallic complaint of the unlocked hasp—and lay still with his heart hammering and his eyes closed and thought, absurdly, of the widow from another life, the one whose dead husband had complained through Sable's mourning charm about the quality of the shroud. Everyone wanted something carried for them. Everyone wanted their weight borne by other hands.

Corvin took the box. Corvin went, soft as smoke, up the cistern stairs toward the Debt Arch and whatever pickup waited beyond it.

Sable gave him a hundred count. Then he rose, woke Brannoc and Pip with a hand and not a word, and followed.

* * *

The trail led down, not up—which surprised them all. Out through the Debt Arch's neighbor, down a service stair slick with seep, into a stretch of tunnel so old the brick had gone soft as cheese. Corvin moved like a man following memorized directions, and at the end of the tunnel, where it opened into a flooded cistern chamber lit by a single hooded lantern, he stopped and raised his light twice, thrice, twice again.

A boat slid out of the dark across the black water.

There were four men in it, and none of them wore the gray of the Inquisition. They wore the undyed wool and plain steel of the Office of Sequestration's enforcement arm, and standing in the bow, holding the lantern high, was a soft-bodied clerk with ink-stained fingers whom Sable recognized from guild functions: Undersecretary Halloway himself, come down into the dark personally, which meant the relic mattered to someone above him, which meant the price of failure had just climbed past anything Corvin had imagined.

"Master Corvin," Halloway said, and his voice was pleasant the way ledgers are pleasant. "You have it?"

"I have it." Corvin lifted the iron box, and even across the water Sable saw his hands shaking. "And the bone-carver's location. And the others. All of it, as agreed. My payment—"

"Your payment." Halloway smiled. "Yes. Come aboard."

And Sable, pressed into the black shadow of the tunnel mouth with Brannoc's remaining hand gripping his shoulder like a shackle and Pip's breath quick and furious against his ear, watched Corvin step into the boat, watched the box pass from traitor's hands to clerk's hands, watched Halloway break the seal and peel back the wool with the reverence of a man unwrapping a saint—

—and heard, faint across the water, the false relic begin to sing.

Nine voices. Hungry, hollow, magnificent. Promising thrones and years and the undoing of death itself. Halloway's face went slack with wonder, then tight with greed, then—this was the part Sable would remember later, the part that woke him for nights afterward—settled into something calm and bottomless, the look of a man who has just realized the size of the weapon he is holding and likes it.

"Beautiful," Halloway whispered. "Oh, they'll want this. They'll want this very much." He tucked the box under his arm like a ledger and gestured, and the boat turned, and Corvin said something frightened about payment, and Halloway said, still pleasant, "You'll be paid in full, Master Corvin. Everything you were promised," in a tone that made Pip's fingernails dig into Sable's arm.

The lantern receded. The water swallowed the light. In the dark, the true relic hummed patiently in the stove-leg behind them, and Sable understood, with the clarity that comes only after the point where understanding helps, that they had traded a relic to the Inquisition and learned nothing they could use—no route, no safehouse, no weakness—only the sight of a soft clerk carrying nine dead kings up out of the dark toward the seat of power.

"Well," Pip said into the silence, in the smallest voice Sable had ever heard her use. "That went wrong."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of brick and stone, the city's bells began to ring—though it was nowhere near any hour, and no feast day, and no one, anywhere in the dark, had pulled a rope.
