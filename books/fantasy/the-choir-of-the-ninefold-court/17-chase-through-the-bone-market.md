## 17. Chase Through the Bone Market

The Bone Market woke at dusk the way a body wakes from fever—suddenly, and all at once.

By day it was a square of shuttered stalls behind the tanneries, unremarkable as any other corner of Vell's End. By night the shutters banged open, lanterns strung on wire between the eaves, and the traders came out with their trays of knucklebones and vertebrae, their jars of teeth sorted by animal and by something less easy to name. Sable had always loved it here, in the way you love a place that knows too much about you. Every stall was a confession. Every vendor had sold him something he'd rather not have touched.

Tonight he wasn't browsing. Tonight he was running.

"He went left," Pip gasped beside him, all elbows and breath, her stolen name rattling in her mouth like a coin in an empty cup. "Left past the tooth-wife's, toward the rendering sheds—Sable, *left*—"

"I heard you." He hadn't. His ears were full of bells.

That was the worst of it. The relic had been singing since Corvin lifted it off Brannoc's workbench three hours ago—a thin, sweet, nine-voiced thread of sound threading through the market noise, through the hawkers crying *ribs, ribs, fresh-scraped ribs*, through the clatter of dice and the low bargaining over femurs. The Court didn't sing for everyone. It sang for him, and it sang like a lure, and every step it pulled him deeper into the crowd was a step Corvin had already taken.

Brannoc was somewhere behind them with his one hand and his soldier's temper, having gone the other way around the square with Tess and two of the Undercroft runners. Sable had lost sight of them at the fishmonger's arch. Now it was just him and a fourteen-year-old pickpocket and nine dead kings pulling him forward like a hook through the sternum.

"There." Pip grabbed his sleeve. "Green coat. By the marrow-seller."

Corvin stood at the edge of the lantern light, and for one absurd moment he looked exactly as he always had—the guild's quiet man, the one who inked the shipping manifests, who'd taught Sable which ledger columns never to total aloud. Then he turned, and saw them, and the look on his face was not guilt. It was arithmetic. He was calculating what they were worth to him now, and finding the answer pleasing.

He ran.

* * *

The market swallowed him and spat them after.

Corvin moved well for a man who spent his life bent over paper—he shouldered through the crowd without apology, without slowing, knocking a tray of teeth from a vendor's hands so the whole aisle slipped and skidded on ivory. Sable went down hard on one knee, came up with someone else's blood on his palm where he'd caught himself on scattered molars, and kept moving because the bells in his skull would not let him do anything else.

*Faster,* said the second voice, the one that called itself Aurel, who had worn a crown of river-gold. *He is getting away with our heart.*

"Your heart is a lump of carved antler," Sable muttered, "and I'm going to feed it to a fire."

*Liar,* said the ninth voice, softly, the way it always spoke, the one that sounded almost kind. *You couldn't burn us if we begged.*

That was the trouble with the truth. It got everywhere.

Pip was faster than both of them. She took the alley behind the tooth-wife's stall—a gap Sable would have sworn was too narrow for a cat—and reappeared ahead of Corvin near the rendering sheds, where the great copper vats sat cooling under their canvas, the whole lane stinking of fat and lye. She cut across his path, arms spread wide, small and ferocious as a gull defending a fish head.

Corvin didn't even slow down. He hit her with his shoulder, sent her spinning into a stack of empty crates, and Sable felt something in his chest tear loose—not anger exactly, something older and colder, the thing Odile had once told him lived in every carver's hands whether they admitted it or not.

The relic was close. Close enough that the voices stopped being voices and became weather, pressure, a change in the air before lightning. The antler-and-wire cage Corvin carried under his green coat was waking up, and everything dead within fifty feet was turning toward it like flowers toward sun. Sable could feel them—the market's dead, the shed's dead, the nameless ones ground into glue and knife-handles generations ago—all of them leaning, listening, hungry for the sound of their old kings.

And then the Inquisition lanterns came up the far end of the lane.

Three of them, then five, white-lit and steady, and the crowd began to melt away with the practiced silence of people who had learned what those lights meant. Sable pressed himself into the shadow of a vat. Beside him, breathing hard, Pip did the same, a smear of blood at her hairline she hadn't mentioned and he hadn't asked about.

"That's Vale's detail," she whispered. "I know the tall one. He bought information off me twice before the Proclamation." She swallowed. "Sable. If they take the relic—"

"I know."

If the Inquisition held the Ninefold Court, they wouldn't destroy it. They'd study it. Weapon it. And the Court, which wanted nothing more than a strong arm and a willing throat, would give them whatever they asked, because nine thrones are patient, and patience is the oldest weapon there is.

Across the lane, Corvin had also seen the lanterns—and Sable watched him make his choice in real time. Watched him stop running *from* and start running *toward*. Watched him lift the bundle under his coat like a man carrying tribute.

"No," Sable breathed. "No, you don't—"

He broke cover.

* * *

Later, trying to remember it, Sable could only hold pieces. That was how running battles worked; the mind saved only what bled.

He remembered the marrow-seller's awning collapsing as he vaulted a cart, remembered the crowd parting around him with the superstitious dread people showed a man running *toward* Inquisitors instead of away. He remembered shouting Corvin's name and hearing it come out wrong, doubled, harmonized—the Court using his throat, spending his voice like coin—and the horror of that, the cold slide of it, nine sovereigns borrowing his mouth mid-word.

He remembered Corvin turning, at last, and the expression on his face when he realized Sable wasn't going to stop.

"You don't understand what they're offering!" Corvin shouted, backing toward the lanterns, holding the relic out like a shield. "They'll end the Hunt! All of it! Everyone registered, everyone burned—they'll stop it, they can stop anything—"

"With a war," Sable said. "You've heard them too. You've been listening."

Corvin's face did something complicated. "Everyone hears something," he said. "I hear numbers. I always have. Do you know what the guild pays a man who hears numbers? Nothing. Do you know what the Inquisition pays?" He laughed, high and cracked. "Everything. They pay *everything*, Sable."

"They're paying you with rope."

"They're paying me with *purpose*—"

The first crossbow bolt took the crate beside Corvin's head apart into splinters. Not aimed at him—at the space around him, a warning shot, and it told Sable everything: the Inquisition wanted the relic intact and its carrier alive, but they weren't particular about bystanders. The tall Inquisitor Pip knew was shouting orders. Lanterns swung. Somewhere behind Sable, Brannoc's roar went up from the far side of the square—"THERE! Left flank, girl, MOVE"—and the whole night tilted into chaos.

And in the middle of it, the relic began to sing out loud.

Not for Sable alone this time. For everyone. The sound came out of the antler cage like light through a cracked door, nine notes braided into one, and every person in the lane staggered. The vendors dropped their wares. The tall Inquisitor crossed himself with the wrong hand. A dog somewhere began to howl and could not stop. The dead of the Bone Market rose—not as ghosts, nothing so clean—but as attention, as weight, as every bone in every stall and vat and gutter suddenly remembering it had once belonged to somebody, and wanting, wanting, *wanting*.

Corvin screamed. It was not a scream of fear. It was a scream of a man hearing his price quoted back to him.

Sable ran through the falling dark and the rising song and put his shoulder into Corvin's chest, and they went down together among the spilled teeth, and the relic skittered free across the cobbles, singing, rolling, trailing nine voices like a comet's tail—

—and Pip caught it.

She shouldn't have been there. She'd been told, specifically, in words of one syllable, to stay by the vats. But she was Pip, and she was already kneeling in the open with the relic hugged to her chest, and she was grinning through the blood on her face like she'd just lifted a purse off a duke.

"Got it!" she crowed. "Sable, I got—"

Then everything happened very fast and very slowly at once.

Corvin came up off the cobbles with a knife in his fist—it must have been in his boot the whole time, a guild runner's blade, the kind they all carried, the kind Sable himself wore—and he didn't lunge for the relic. He lunged for Sable. Later Sable would understand why: the Court had told him. Through the singing, through the chaos, the ninth voice had leaned close to the traitor and said *the carver is the vessel, kill the vessel and the relic is only meat*, and Corvin, who heard numbers, had done the arithmetic.

Sable saw the blade coming and had time to think, absurdly, of Odile telling him once that a carver's hands were worth more than his eyes, and that he ought to insure them.

He didn't move fast enough. He was braced for it, actually braced, eyes open, watching the steel come—

—and Pip hit him sideways.

She'd thrown herself across the gap between them the way she threw herself at everything, without calculation, without the arithmetic Corvin loved, and the knife that had been meant for Sable's chest went into her instead, below the collarbone, angled down, and came out wet.

The world went very quiet. Even the nine voices stopped.

Then Pip made a small sound—not a scream, worse, a sound of surprise, of genuine hurt bewilderment, the sound of a child who has fallen off a roof—and folded, and Sable caught her, and the relic rolled free again across the stones, forgotten by everyone but the dead.

* * *

What happened next, Sable pieced together from Brannoc's account and Tess's and the tall Inquisitor's own report, later, when reports still existed.

Brannoc came through the smoke of a knocked-over brazier with a rendering-hook in his remaining hand and took Corvin across the jaw with it, opening his cheek to the bone. Corvin, bleeding and half-deafened by his own treachery's echo, scrambled for the relic, found it, found the Inquisitors' lanterns converging, and made the choice that defined him forever after: he ran *to* them. He fell at the tall Inquisitor's feet babbling, holding the ninefold relic above his head like a drowned man holding driftwood, saying *mine, I brought it, I delivered it, ask your commander what I'm owed*—and the Inquisition closed around him and carried him and it away into the white-lit dark.

And Sable knelt in the wreckage of the Bone Market with a dying child in his arms, and discovered what his hands were actually for.

They were shaking. He made them stop. He'd stitched wounds on smugglers in the Undercroft, but this was different, this was Pip, this was the boy-girl-child-nothing who had stolen a name and given it to him like a gift, and his hands shook until he looked at them and said, silently, *you belong to me, and tonight you will be worth what she is worth*, and they steadied.

The blade had gone in below the collarbone and down. Not the heart. Not the lung, he thought, not the lung, please. He packed it, bound it, tore the lining from his own coat because it was the cleanest cloth in reach. Brannoc arrived and became, instantly, the soldier again—clear-eyed, economical, terrible—lifting Pip one-handed while Sable kept pressure on the wound, and they moved through the abandoned market, through the stalls where bones lay scattered like the aftermath of a feast nobody survived, toward the Undercroft and Brannoc's friend the stitcher who asked no questions and charged in eggs.

Pip woke twice on the way. Both times she said the same thing.

"Did we get it?"

"No," Sable said, the first time.

"Yes," Brannoc said, the second time, lying, because Brannoc understood that some medicines aren't physical.

The second time, Pip smiled, and slept, and her blood soaked through Sable's coat lining into his shirt, warm as anything alive, and he thought: *this is the price. This has always been the price. Every family costs blood, and the ones worth having cost yours.*

* * *

They laid her on the stitcher's table in a room that smelled of yarrow and lamp-oil, and the stitcher—an old woman named Maren-of-the-Undercroft, no relation, the city apparently produced Marens the way it produced debts—worked in silence while Sable held Pip's hand and Brannoc stood against the wall looking like a man being flayed slowly from the inside.

"She jumped in front of a knife," Brannoc said finally. "For you."

"For the relic."

"For *you*." Brannoc pushed off the wall. "I've seen soldiers die for banners, for pay, for fear of the man behind them. She's none of those. She's fourteen and she took steel meant for you because—" He stopped. His hand opened and closed. "Because you're hers. That's all. That's the whole reason. You're hers and she's yours and that's what we built down here without anyone signing anything."

Sable looked down at Pip's small fingers wrapped around his. There was bone-dust under her nails. There always was; she was always handling his castoffs, his practice-carvings, wearing a little fox vertebra on a cord he'd made her months ago and never thought about again.

"The Court offered to heal her," he said quietly. "Last time. When they offered me the vessel-bargain. Heal the girl, they said. Free the mentor. Restore the hearing. All I have to do is open my throat and let them in."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

"And now she's bleeding on a table in a cellar."

"Now she's bleeding on a table in a cellar," Sable agreed, "because I said no. Because I chose." He heard his own voice go strange. "Do you understand what they've learned, Brannoc? They've learned my prices. Every time I refuse, they learn another way to ask."

The stitcher looked up, needle paused. Brannoc said nothing for a long moment.

"Then we stop letting you be the one they ask," he said at last. "Whatever comes next—whatever Corvin's handed them—we decide it together. All of us. Even the girl, especially the girl, when she's back on her feet. No more lone carvers carrying the whole choir." His jaw set. "I buried enough soldiers who died alone following orders. Never again. Not down here."

Above them, muffled by earth and stone, the city's bells began to ring.

It was late for bells. It was the wrong hour, the wrong pattern, and Sable felt the hair rise on his arms, because he could still feel, faintly, through floors and foundations and forty feet of packed dirt, the shape of the sound—and underneath the bronze, underneath the ringing, something else was answering. Something with nine voices, far away, in whatever vault the Inquisition had carried it to. Waking up. Being *handled*.

The bells rang on, and ordinary people across the city would stand in their doorways and wonder, and some few of them—the gifted, the half-gifted, the ones who'd hidden so long they'd half-forgotten—would feel the answer in their teeth.

"They're going to use it," Sable said. "Corvin gave it to them, and they're going to use it."

Brannoc was already reaching for his boots.
