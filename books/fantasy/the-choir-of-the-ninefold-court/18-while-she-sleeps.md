## 18. While She Sleeps

The knife had gone in under Pip's ribs, angled up and back, the way a butcher knows to cut. Sable had learned that much in the market's chaos—learned it with his hands slicked red and his ears full of screaming—and now he sat in a chair dragged to Pip's bedside and did not move from it.

Brannoc had done what soldiering he could: cleaned the wound, packed it with moss-balm and salt-thread, stitched her closed with gut-line boiled clean. "If fever doesn't take her by tomorrow's dusk," he'd said, not unkindly, "she'll keep." Then he'd gone to stand watch at the Undercroft door, because Brannoc processed fear the way other men processed grief—by standing between it and the people he loved, one-handed and immovable.

So Sable kept the vigil alone.

Pip slept in a borrowed bed too big for her, swallowed by blankets, looking younger than her fifteen years and older than most of the dead he'd ever spoken with. Her face was gray as tallow. Sweat darkened her hairline. Every breath came shallow and quick, like she was running somewhere even in sleep, and every few minutes her fingers twitched against the blanket—reaching for something, or letting go of it.

"You absolute idiot," Sable said softly. It was the third time he'd said it. He suspected he would say it many more times before dawn.

She'd taken the knife meant for him. There was no ambiguity in it, no accident of positioning. Corvin's hired blade had come out of the crowd at the Bone Market with the smooth economy of a professional, and Pip—who could have run, who should have run, whose whole life on the streets had been built on knowing when to run—had instead thrown herself sideways into the man's arm. She hadn't even been armed. She'd had a stolen apple in one hand and Sable's carving satchel strap in the other, and she'd held onto that strap as she went down, so that the relic wouldn't be lost in the scramble.

He'd carried both of them out—the girl bleeding through his shirt and the relic heavy against his spine, nine sovereigns singing their concern into his skull like a choir of concerned grandmothers. *The child is precious,* they'd murmured. *Preserve the child. All things may yet be mended.* And he'd wanted to scream at them, because nothing about that moment was mendable, and their comfort sounded exactly like a sales pitch.

Now the relic sat wrapped in oilcloth across the room, as far from the bed as he could put it without leaving. Even so, faint as smoke, he could feel it listening.

* * *

The dead of the Bone Market found him around midnight.

They always knew where he was; that was the curse of his craft. A bone-carver who listened left traces, the way a bell-ringer's fingers remembered rope. The dead gathered at the edges of rooms where he sat and waited politely, the way supplicants wait outside a magistrate's door, and usually he ignored them with the practiced ease of years. But tonight the Undercroft was quiet, and Pip's breathing was the only living sound, and silence invited company whether he wanted it or not.

They came first as a pressure behind his eyes. Then names.

*Marla,* said a voice like wet gravel. *I sold eels by the east arch. I remember you. You bought from me once, winter before last, and overpaid because you thought I looked cold.*

"I remember," Sable murmured. Marla had indeed looked cold. She'd been dead three weeks now, knifed over a stall dispute, and her ghost still wore the smell of brine.

*You carry the bells,* Marla said. Not a question. The dead talked to each other; he'd known this for decades, though he rarely thought about it. News traveled among them faster than among the living, unburdened by doors or distance. *The whole city's dead are talking about the bells. Nine of them. We hear them in our sleep, if we can call it sleep.*

"They're old kings," Sable said. "Older than the war."

*Older than the war.* Marla made a sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could curdle. *That's what they tell you, is it? That's what they sing?*

Sable went very still. Beside him, Pip breathed her quick shallow breaths, and the candle guttered, and he felt the temperature of the conversation change the way a sailor feels weather change.

"What do you mean?"

*We were there,* said another voice—older, thinner, a woman's. *Some of us. Not the ones they show you in the friezes, with swords and banners. The small ones. The ones who just died.* A pause, the way the living pause to choose words. *The war didn't start the way the stones say it started.*

"The stones say the Court defended the freeholds against the crown's purges," Sable said carefully. "That the crown feared them and struck first."

*And do you believe everything carved in marble?* Marla again, dry as salt. *You're a ledger-keeper, bone-man. You know what records are for. They're for whoever pays the carver.*

He opened his mouth to argue and found he couldn't—not cleanly, not with certainty. Because hadn't he already doubted? Hadn't the visions the Court showed him been too beautiful, too arranged, too much like a story told by someone who needed telling? Sovereigns defending their people, martyrs in crowns, a war of pure defense. It had moved him. It had also, he realized now, sitting in candlelight beside a bleeding child, never once mentioned what happened after. What conquest looked like from underneath.

*Ask them about Ashfall,* said the thin woman's voice.

"Ashfall?" He didn't know the name.

Silence. The particular silence of the dead deciding how much truth a living man can hold.

*Ask them,* the woman repeated, *what they burned at Ashfall, and why. Ask them who lit the first fire. If they answer you honestly, then maybe we're wrong, and you can sleep easy.* Another pause. *But they won't answer. That's how you'll know.*

*We don't want them back,* Marla added, and her voice had lost its wryness entirely. *Understand that, bone-man. Whatever they promise you. The dead here, the ordinary dead—we don't want our kings returned. Death is the only country we have, and they ruled it badly enough when they were alive.*

Then, like a tide going out, they withdrew. The pressure behind his eyes eased. The candle steadied.

Across the room, the oilcloth bundle seemed no different than before. But Sable found himself looking at it the way Odile had taught him to look at a trade that seemed too good: *When the price is perfect, Brightwater, the product is lying.*

* * *

Toward the small hours, Pip woke.

Not all the way—a surfacing, like a swimmer rising just far enough to breathe. Her eyes opened to slits, unfocused, finding the candlelight and then his face.

"Sable." Her voice was a rasp, barely there. "Did we get it back?"

"We got it back." He leaned close so she wouldn't strain. "It's safe. You're safe."

"Corvin?"

"Gone. Ran to Vale, Brannoc thinks. We'll deal with him later."

Her mouth twitched toward a grin and didn't make it. "Took a knife," she said, with a strange, wondering pride.

"You took a knife," Sable agreed, and heard his own voice crack on the words. He pressed a cup of water to her lips and she drank a little, obedient, then sagged back.

"Worth it," she whispered.

"It was not worth it. Nothing about this is worth—" He stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "Pip. Listen to me. Next time, you run. That's your job. Running. You're the fastest thing in this city and your job is to run, and if you ever throw yourself at a blade for me again I will—I don't know what I'll do, but it will be dramatic and embarrassing for us both."

"'M not letting them take the bells," she murmured. Her eyes were closing again. "Not letting them take *you*."

And there it was, laid out plain as a ledger line: the reason she'd done it. Not the relic. Him. This feral, half-starved scrap of a girl who'd sold her name for bread money and spent years being nobody's anything had decided, somewhere along the way, that Sable Galloway was hers to protect.

He took her hand. It was hot—too hot, fever warming up its engines—and her fingers curled weakly around his.

"I'm here," he said. "Sleep. I'll be here when you wake."

"Promise?"

"I keep ledgers," he said. "Everything I promise goes in the book."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face, and she slid back down into sleep, and Sable sat holding her hand in the candlelight and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what the Court had to offer him. Not power. Not knowledge. *Him, whole.* His hearing restored, his friends healed, his debts paid. They had watched him sit at this bedside and they knew precisely which levers to pull.

And the dead of the market had just told him those same hands had lit the first fire at a place called Ashfall.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the high slit window. Brannoc appeared with broth and a face like a closed fist, checked Pip's bandage, grunted the grunt of a man refusing to hope.

"Fever's up," he said. "Not critical. Yet."

"She'll keep," Sable said. "You said so yourself."

"I said *if*. Soldiers learn to live inside ifs." Brannoc set the broth down and stood a moment, looking at the wrapped relic with the expression of a man looking at an unexploded shell. "It sang to me last night. While I watched the door. Little threads of it, coming through the stone." His jaw worked. "It sang about my village, Sable. About the men I burned. Said it could bring them back. Give them back to me, whole and forgiven." He spat, precise and contemptuous, at the floor between himself and the bundle. "I don't want them back forgiven. I want them back, period. And it can't do that. Which means everything else it offers is the same kind of lie."

Sable looked at him. "The dead told me something last night."

Brannoc's eyebrows rose.

"That the Court started the war. That Ashfall—" he stumbled on the word, new to his mouth, "—that they burned it first. Before the crown ever raised a hand."

The old soldier was silent a long time. When he spoke, his voice was flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice. "Ashfall was a freehold. One of theirs. It turned crown-loyal early in the war—or the histories say so—and the Court made an example of it. My grandmother's songs mention it. The songs say the sky rained burning feathers for a day and a night." He met Sable's eyes. "I always thought the songs were embellishment. Songs usually are." He nodded at the bundle. "Ask it. Tonight, when it's singing to you. Ask it about Ashfall and watch what it does with the melody."

* * *

He waited until full dark, until Brannoc had taken Pip-watch and the Undercroft had settled into its creaking rhythms, until he could unwrap the relic in the little storeroom off the main tunnel with the door shut and the lamp turned low.

Nine voices rose to meet him like warm water. *Sable. Beloved carver. How fares the child?*

"Healing," Sable said. "Thanks to you, you'll say."

*Thanks to what we may yet do,* they corrected gently, and the melody swelled with tenderness, with patience, with the vast condescending love of parents for a slow child. *Speak your heart. We feel it heavy in you.*

"I have a question."

*Anything.*

"Ashfall."

And there—it was subtle, a listener had to be a listener to catch it—but the harmony flinched. Nine voices, and for half a heartbeat one of them went silent, and the chord that remained had a hole in it shaped like a confession.

*Ashfall,* they repeated smoothly, too smoothly, the melody reknitting itself. *A tragedy of the war. The crown's forces burned it and blamed us—*

"One of the voices stopped," Sable said quietly. "Just now. When I said the name. One of your nine went quiet, and the other eight covered for it." He leaned forward. "Which one of you was it? Which one of you lit the fire?"

The song stopped altogether.

In the silence that followed, Sable felt the relic become something it had never been with him before: *cautious*. A great beast realizing the small creature in front of it had seen the teeth.

*You have been speaking with the lesser dead,* the voices said at last, and the tenderness was gone, replaced by something colder and infinitely more ancient. *The chaff. The unremembered. They envy us, little carver. They have always envied us. Would you take the word of eel-sellers and ditch-diggers over the testimony of kings?*

"Yes," Sable said. "Every time. Kings lie for the crown. Eel-sellers only lie about the size of the catch."

Another silence, longer. When the voices came back, they had rebuilt the warmth, brick by careful brick, but he could see the mortar lines now. He would always see them.

*The past is a large country,* they said, *and every map of it was drawn by a survivor. Judge us by what we offer, not by what the envious whisper. The child heals or dies by tomorrow's dusk. The woman rots in Vale's cells. Your hearing frays with every voice you refuse. We are patient, Sable, but the world is not. Choose soon.*

The song faded to embers. Sable sat in the lamplight with his hands shaking—not with fear, but with the specific fury of a man who has caught a merchant mid-falsification and knows the whole ledger is rotten.

One voice had gone silent. One of the nine had burned Ashfall, and the other eight had sung over the gap for a thousand years.

He wrapped the relic slowly, thinking of Odile in her cell, of Pip's fever climbing toward tomorrow's dusk, of Brannoc's village and the forgiveness no one should be offered secondhand. Thinking of the Court's arithmetic: *the child heals or dies by tomorrow's dusk*, as if Pip's life were a coin they'd minted.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would go to Maren Vale.

The thought arrived fully formed and terrible, and he let it stand. Because Vale had questions, and Vale had a gift for truth, and above all Vale had Odile—and if anyone in this city knew what had really happened at a place called Ashfall, it was the Inquisition's own archives.

Behind him, through the wall, he heard Brannoc softly singing to Pip—one of the old soldiers' songs, tuneless and low. And beneath it, faint as a hairline crack, the relic began to hum again, patient as rot, keeping its own count of the hours until dusk.
