## 19. Odile in Chains

The sentence came down at the ninth bell of a gray morning, and Maren Vale stood in the back of the Assize Hall and felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*By order of Lord Commander Hesse, the prisoner Odile Vess, confessed trafficker with the dead, is remanded to the Pyre Yard. Execution to be conducted publicly, that the people may witness the crown's mercy and its justice.*

Mercy and justice. The words had always sat together badly in Maren's mouth, like two coins of different kingdoms. She had heard them spoken over a hundred sentences now—over bone-singers and ash-readers, over a girl of sixteen who could make bread rise by humming to it—and every time she had written her reports in a steady hand and told herself the machinery was larger than she was, that one wheel turning could not stop the mill.

But Odile Vess was not a girl who made bread rise. Odile Vess had looked at Maren across an iron table for three hours and given her nothing but riddles wrapped in courtesy, and then, on the last night, had hummed a lullaby through the bars of her cell—a lullaby about a boat going out past the harbor light, about coming home when the tide turned. A lullaby only one other person in all the world had ever sung to Maren Vale, and that person had been dead eleven years.

Maren had not slept since.

She stood in the Assize Hall's cold light with her hands folded behind her back, watching the clerk read the sentence in his dry paper voice, and she understood—with the terrible clarity that comes from too little sleep—that she was going to do something unforgivable or something unprecedented, and that she could no longer tell the difference between them.

"Vale." Commander Hesse's voice found her before she could slip away. He was waiting at the edge of the dais, a broad man gone soft at the middle, wearing his authority the way other men wore fur against winter. "A word."

She crossed to him. Around them the hall emptied, clerks and witnesses draining toward their lunches, their errands, their small unhaunted lives.

"The Vess woman," Hesse said. "You've had her three days without result. I'm reassigning the interrogation."

"To whom?"

"To no one. The sentence stands. What use is more questioning? We have what we need." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Confession enough. Names enough. She gave us the guild, didn't she?"

"She gave us the guild," Maren said carefully, "and nothing else. No names of associates. No locations of hidden gifted. No—" *No word of the relic,* she did not say. *No word of the bone-carver Galloway.* "Three days, Commander, and she has not broken. That means there is more to break."

Hesse studied her. He had a way of looking at his officers as though weighing them for market, and Maren had learned early to keep her face at the price she wanted him to see: competent, loyal, slightly humorless, no ambitions beyond the work.

"You've grown attached," he said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I've grown *frustrated*," Maren said. "She knows things we need. The Undercroft network. The smugglers' routes. If half of what the informants report is true, there are hundreds of the gifted under this city, and Vess is the thread that pulls it all loose. Burning her now is burning a map because we're tired of reading it."

It was a good argument. It was even mostly true. Maren watched it land and watched Hese resist it, his jaw working, and she pressed before he could find his footing.

"Give me until the execution. Two days. Let me conduct the final interrogation personally—if she breaks, you gain everything; if she doesn't, the pyre loses nothing but two days of wood."

"And why should the final interrogation be yours? You've had your chance."

Because I need time I cannot explain, Maren thought. Because I need to stand in front of that woman and ask her how she knew my mother's lullaby, and I cannot ask that question in front of anyone. Because somewhere between the bone-singer Tam's empty eyes and the falsified report in my own handwriting, I stopped being certain which side of this work I am standing on, and I am not ready to find out.

What she said was: "Because prisoners break differently for the officer who will watch them burn. It focuses the mind. Mine and hers."

Hesse laughed—a short, ugly sound. "That's the first honest thing you've said all week, Vale. Very well. Two days. But hear me." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register he used for threats he wanted deniable. "If you come to me at the end of those two days with nothing but more riddles, I will conclude that the problem is not the prisoner. Do you understand me?"

Perfectly, Maren thought. "Yes, Commander."

* * *

The cells beneath the Assize Hall were cut from the city's old bones—foundations older than the crown, older than the war, black stone that drank torchlight and gave back nothing. Maren's boots rang on the steps, and each echo sounded to her like a bell counting down.

Two guards flanked the corridor outside the special cell. She dismissed them with a flick of two fingers and the weight of her rank, and waited until their footsteps faded up the stairs before she unlocked the door.

Odile Vess sat on the cot with her back straight and her hands folded in her lap, as though she were receiving visitors in a parlor rather than awaiting a fire. They had taken her guild rings and her fine gray coat and left her in prisoner's wool, but they had not managed to take the thing that made her Odile Vess—the stillness, the gravity, the sense that she was the one doing the confining.

"Inquisitor," she said pleasantly. "Come to gloat?"

"Come to ask you a question I should have asked days ago."

"Oh?" One white eyebrow rose. "I warn you, I answer most questions with riddles. It's a failing of age."

Maren pulled the room's single stool closer and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that the words would not carry even if the walls had ears. "The lullaby," she said. "*Row out past the harbor light, the tide will turn, the tide will turn.* Where did you learn it?"

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—the first true expression Maren had seen on her in three days of interrogation. Not surprise exactly. Recognition. As if a card had been turned over that she'd been waiting for.

"That's not a question about the guild," Odile said softly. "Or the Undercroft. Or the boy Galloway."

"No."

"No, it isn't." Odile unfolded her hands, refolded them. For a long moment she simply looked at Maren, and Maren felt the strange sensation of being read—not her rank, not her file, but her, the tired woman underneath, the one who hadn't slept, the one whose gift itched under her skin like a splinter worked too deep. "Your mother sang it to you," Odile said. "In a kitchen that smelled of cedar smoke. You were small. There was a blue bowl on the table. Am I close?"

Maren's throat closed. She made herself breathe. "How do you know that?"

"Because I heard it secondhand, Inquisitor, from someone who heard it firsthand." Odile leaned forward, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Your mother was gifted. Did you know that? A truth-hearer, like her daughter after her. And before the purges came to her coast, she hid with people like me. People who kept safehouses. People who remembered songs." She paused. "She sang that lullaby to her own child, and she sang it to frightened strangers in a cellar in Corvane, and both times she meant it: *the tide will turn.*"

The cell was very quiet. Somewhere above, muffled by stone, the city went about its morning.

"My mother wasn't gifted," Maren said. It came out flat, automatic, the sentence she had carried for thirty years like a stone in her shoe.

"No," Odile agreed gently. "And you don't touch a hand and know whether a man is lying. And you haven't spent this whole hunt feeling every false confession crawl over your skin like ants. Of course not."

Maren stood abruptly. Her legs felt wrong, too long, borrowed. She walked to the wall and put her palm flat against the cold stone, and the stone did nothing, and that was somehow worse.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she asked the wall. "Why not during the interrogation? Why give me nothing for three days and then—this?"

"Because during the interrogation you were the Inquisition asking questions for the record. Now you're a daughter asking a question for yourself." Odile settled back against the wall. "And because I have two days left, Inquisitor, and I intend to spend them well. Ask me your real questions. The ones you've been swallowing."

Maren turned around slowly. "You'll answer them?"

"Some. Riddles where answers would be dangerous. Truth where truth might help." A thin smile. "I'm told I'm running out of time to be difficult."

* * *

They talked for four hours.

Not about the guild's ledgers, not about names or routes or the locations of hidden souls—every time Maren circled near those waters, Odile deflected into metaphor so elaborate it became its own kind of fortress. But around the fortress, in the spaces between, Odile said things that rearranged the furniture of Maren's mind and left it in disarray.

She spoke of the Gift-Hunt's architecture—who had designed it, who profited from it, how fear was a currency and the Inquisition minted it by the pound. She spoke of the last suppression, forty years gone, and of villages that had burned with the gifted inside them, and of how the histories recorded those villages as casualties of the gifted's own violence. "We write the ledger," she said, "and then we burn the ledger, and then we swear there was never a ledger at all. Your crown has been keeping books that way for a century, Inquisitor. You've just never been shown the column where the numbers don't add up."

And once—only once—she spoke of Sable Galloway.

"He's not what your files say," she said. "Your files say trafficker. Bone-witch. Enemy of the crown." She shook her head slowly. "He's a man who spends his nights carving charms for grieving widows so they can say goodbye, and his days pretending to be a bookkeeper because being known would kill him. He hears the dead, Inquisitor. All of them. Every day. Do you understand what that costs? He has never once used it for power. Not once. And now your commander wants him because he's the key to a door the crown should leave shut forever."

"What door?"

Odile smiled her riddle-smile. "Ah. There's the wall of my fortress. You'll have to siege that one yourself."

When Maren finally climbed the stairs back into the gray daylight, her hands were shaking, and she stood in the yard a long moment letting the cold air scour her face.

Two days. She had asked for two days, and she had told herself it was strategy—time to extract intelligence, time to serve the hunt. But walking back across the yard, past the scaffold frames and the stacked pyre-wood already piled like cordwood beside the stake, she admitted the truth she'd been carrying like contraband:

She wasn't stalling to break Odile Vess.

She was stalling because she could not yet name what she was going to do instead.

* * *

That evening she went to the records annex, a vaulted room smelling of dust and lamp oil where the Inquisition kept its confessions, its sentences, its careful history of everyone it had ever broken. She showed her seal to the night clerk and asked for the files on the coastal purges, twenty years back. Corvane district.

The clerk brought her six ledgers. She read until the lamps burned low.

She found her mother's village. She found the date. She found the entry—three lines in a clerk's indifferent hand: *Site cleansed. Eleven traffickers with the dead executed. No further action required.*

Eleven. Her mother had been one of eleven. Maren had grown up on the story her aunt told—that fever took the village, that her mother died in bed, that the Inquisition had never come near their coast. A kind story. A story built like a wall around a grave.

And beneath the entry, in the margin, in different ink, someone had added a note that the official record had never intended anyone to read: *Survivor reported: female child, approx. 6 years, removed prior. Disposition unknown.*

Removed prior. By whom?

Maren sat back in the creaking chair and pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Her aunt. Her aunt had come for her—her aunt, who never spoke of those years, who flinched at bonfires, who had raised her with a terror of discovery so complete it had become the shape of Maren's entire life. Someone had pulled a six-year-old girl out of a village marked for cleansing, and that girl had grown up to join the institution that lit the fires, and had risen high enough to light them herself.

*The tide will turn.*

She closed the ledger. The annex clock struck the hour—eleven bells—and she counted them the way she counted everything now, downward.

One day left.

* * *

She went to Hesse's office at dawn, before the building fully woke, and laid her request on his desk like a gambler laying down a losing hand.

"I want the execution delayed."

Hesse looked up from his breakfast—eggs, cold meat, the leisurely appetite of a man whose work never touched his hands. "Delayed."

"A week. Publicly announced as security preparation. Privately, I believe Vess is close to breaking, and I believe what she's holding is worth more than the spectacle of her death."

"Spectacle is the point, Vale." Hesse set down his fork. "You know that. Half the city thinks the gifted are myths or martyrs. A public burning teaches them otherwise. Fear is cheaper than soldiers." He wiped his mouth. "Besides, the order comes from above me. The Lord Protector's office confirmed it yesterday. There's interest in this one. High interest."

"Interest in a guild bookkeeper?"

"Interest in what she knows." Hesse's eyes narrowed, and for a moment the softness fell away and Maren saw the man underneath, the one who had signed the orders for Corvane's neighbors, for a dozen coasts, for the pyres that fed on fear and called it peace. "Which raises a question I've been chewing on, Vale. Three days with the prisoner and suddenly you want a week more. Suddenly you're full of arguments for her value. Tell me honestly"—he leaned forward, and his voice went quiet and pleasant, which was how he sounded when he was most dangerous—"has she gotten inside your head?"

Every instinct Maren possessed screamed at her to deflect, to laugh, to give him the cold professional mask he expected. Instead she heard herself say, "Everyone gets inside my head, Commander. That's rather the point of me."

It was the truth. She watched it strike him—watched him weigh it, unable to tell whether it was insolence or loyalty, and hating that he couldn't tell.

"Hm," he said at last. "Twelve hours. I'll move the execution to tomorrow evening. Use them well." He returned to his eggs. "And Vale—if you come to me again with delays, I'll start wondering whether the traitor in this building is sitting across my desk."

Maren collected herself, bowed, and walked out with her spine straight and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

Twelve hours. Not two days. Twelve hours.

* * *

She went down to the cells one last time.

Odile was awake—Maren suspected she was always awake—and she read Maren's face before Maren reached the bars.

"They refused," Odile said.

"Twelve hours. Tomorrow evening." Maren gripped the iron. "I couldn't get more. I tried."

"I know you tried." Odile rose from the cot and came to the bars, and up close Maren could see for the first time how tired she truly was, the cost of three days of stillness paid in the shadows under her eyes. "Listen to me, Inquisitor, because I won't waste our last hours on comfort. There is a young man who needs to hear what I have to say, and he isn't here, and you are. So you'll carry it."

"Carry what?"

"Tell him the relic must be unmade. Not freed. Not kept. Unmade." Odile's hands closed over Maren's on the bars, and her grip was startlingly strong. "He'll understand. He's the only one who can. And tell him—" Her voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in the marble. "Tell him I was proud of him before he ever carved anything worth being proud of. Tell him the listening was never his curse. The loneliness was. And the loneliness ends the moment he lets people stand next to him."

"Who is he?" Maren asked. "Galloway? Where do I find him?"

Odile smiled, and it was the saddest smile Maren had ever seen. "You'll find him, Inquisitor. Or he'll find you. Men like that are drawn to women carrying messages." She released Maren's hands. "Now go. You have twelve hours, and I suspect you're going to spend them deciding who you are. Choose quickly. The fire doesn't wait, and neither does whatever's coming for this city—I can feel it in my bones, and my bones are usually right."

"What's coming?"

Odile turned back toward her cot, folding her hands in her lap, composed as a queen on a throne that happened to be a cell.

"Bells," she said. "Can't you hear them starting?"

And Maren Vale, standing in the dark with her gift crawling under her skin like waking insects, realized with a lurch that she could—faint, far off, beneath the range of ordinary ears—a ringing that was not any bell of any church she knew, rising through the stones of the city like water through a cracked floor.

Somewhere out there, the dead were beginning to stir.

Twelve hours. She turned and ran for the stairs.
