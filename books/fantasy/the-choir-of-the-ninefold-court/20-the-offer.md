## 20. The Offer

The Undercroft at night was a country of its own.

Sable had learned its borders in the three days since the raid: the drip of water through old aqueduct stone, the breathing of smugglers' tunnels, the way Brannoc's people moved through the dark without lamps because lamps drew eyes and eyes drew Inquisitors. He had learned to sleep in a hammock strung between two rusted hooks that had once held sides of salted pork. He had learned to eat stew made of things he preferred not to name. He had not learned to stop listening.

That was the trouble. He never stopped listening.

The dead of the Undercroft were quiet ones—drowned carters, plague children, a smuggler or two who'd picked the wrong tunnel in the wrong decade. They murmured the way the dead always murmured, half-dreams leaking through the bone charms Sable wore against his skin: a brass ring from a drowned man, a knucklebone he'd carried since Ashford Street, the little mourning charm he'd carved for himself the night Odile was taken, though he'd told no one it was for her. The dead spoke in fragments. That was the natural order of things. Fragments, whispers, the static of souls worn thin by time.

The ninefold relic did not whisper.

It sat wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Brannoc's strongest strongbox, behind two locked doors and a man named Hesk who slept across the threshold with a knife under his pillow, and still Sable could hear it through all of it—through iron and stone and forty feet of packed earth—singing. Nine voices braided into one chord, patient as winter. They had been singing since the night he'd first touched the thing in the guild vault, and they had not stopped, and lately they had begun to sing *words*.

Tonight, sitting alone in the alcove he'd claimed as a workroom—a plank table, a stump for a chair, his carving tools laid out in their worn leather roll—he heard them say his name.

*Sable Galloway.*

Not through a charm. Not through bone. Through the stone itself, through the water dripping in the dark, through the marrow of his own skull. He set down his knife very carefully, the way a man sets down a cup when the floor begins to tilt.

"I'm listening," he said, which was a lie, because he had never once stopped.

*We know,* said the voices, and there was something almost gentle in it, the way a mother is gentle when she wakes a child for news too important to wait for morning. *You have been grieving.*

He didn't answer. Grief was a door, and he knew better than to open doors in the dark. But the Court had never needed him to answer. It needed only the smallest crack—the ache itself—and it poured through like water finding the low place.

*The woman called Odile Vess sits in a cell beneath the Bastion of Candles,* they said. *She has not eaten in two days. She has given them nothing, but she grows thin, and thin women do not survive pyres. The Inquisitors have set her burning for the ninth day from now. You know this. We know you know this. What you do not know is how close she came, last night, to telling them about the boy.*

Sable's hands went cold. "Pip."

*The boy sleeps thirty feet from where you sit. His wound festers. Brannoc's herb-woman packs it with moss and prayer, and prayer has never yet closed a wound. By the week's end the fever will take him into the long sleep, and no one in this city will be able to call him back.* A pause, delicate as a scalpel. *Except us.*

"Stop." His voice cracked on it. He pressed his palms flat against the plank table, grounding himself in the grain, in the smell of bone dust and lamp oil, in the ordinary weight of his own body. "I know what you're doing. I've watched Odile run this guild for eleven years. I've seen every con ever written in her ledgers. This is the oldest one there is."

*And which part is the lie?* the nine asked, softly. *Tell us which part, carver. Is the cell false? Is the fever false? Is the fire false?*

He couldn't. That was the obscene genius of it. Everything they said was true, or true enough, and truth was the best mortar for building a trap. He thought of Pip's grey face that morning, the sweat soaking through the blanket, the way the boy had tried to joke about it—"if I die, sell my name back, it's worth more than I am"—and how Brannoc had turned away so no one would see what his face did.

*You are a man who trades in impossible things,* the Court murmured. *So hear an impossible offer, and weigh it honestly, as you weigh everything in your ledgers.*

The stone around him seemed to lean inward. The lamplight bent. And Sable—who had spent his whole life hearing the dead as one hears rain on a roof, ambient, distant, deniable—felt the Ninefold Court arrive in the room with him as if nine bodies had taken seats around his little table.

*First,* they said. *The woman. Odile Vess, keeper of ledgers, keeper of secrets, keeper of you. We know the chains they use in the Bastion. We know the locks older than the Bastion, the ways beneath it that the crown has forgotten it built. Free us, and before her pyre is lit, those ways will open. She will walk out of the Bastion of Candles and curse your name for frightening her, because that is the kind of woman she is, and she will live to do it for another forty years.*

*Second. The boy. Fever is a door, and we are keys. Pip will wake whole, hungry, insufferable, exactly as he was before Corvin's knife found him. You have our word on it, and our word, whatever else we are, does not rot.*

*Third.* Here the voices softened further, and Sable hated how much he wanted to hear what came next, hated that some starving animal in his chest leaned toward it. *Your hearing. Not the broken trickle you have endured all your life—the bleeding ears, the headaches, the dead who come to you uninvited and leave you shaking. All of that, mended. And more than mended. You would hear clearly. Every voice, every memory, every song the dead have ever kept. No pain. No static. Only music.*

Silence, then. Real silence, the first in days, as if the Court had stepped back to let him consider the shape of the thing.

Sable sat in his alcove under the city and shook.

Because here was the ledger-entry no one else could read: he had wanted this his entire life. Not power—not the thrones and the war and the sovereign majesty of the Ninefold Court—but simply *quiet*. To hear the dead the way a healthy man hears wind, when he chooses to open the window, and to close it again after. He had been fourteen the first time a dead voice followed him home and wouldn't stop talking until dawn, and he had been fourteen when he learned to smile through it, to carve polite little charms and pretend the voices were imagination, to make himself small enough that no one would look twice. Smallness had kept him alive. Smallness had cost him everything.

And now nine ancient sovereigns sat at his table and offered to make him large.

"What do you want," he said. His voice came out flat. "In exchange. Say it plainly. I don't buy what isn't itemized."

*Plainly, then.* A rustle, like silk, like pages turning. *A vessel. A throat. When we rise, we rise through you. Nine voices, one body—you have heard us say it before, carver, and flinched, so hear it now in full. We would not wear you as a coat. We would share you as a choir shares a cathedral. Your hands would remain your hands. Your name would remain your name. You would simply also be us, and we would also be you, forever, in perfect accord.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then the woman burns on the ninth day. The boy dies of fever by the tenth. And you go on as you have always gone on—small, careful, hiding in ledgers while the world decides what to do with people like you.* The gentleness curdled, just slightly, just enough to show the teeth beneath. *We do not threaten, Sable Galloway. We merely describe. The threats are already yours. The crown wrote them. We only read them aloud.*

"That's not nothing," he admitted, and hated himself for admitting it. "That's the problem. Every word you've said is true, and I can't check any of it, and I'm tired, and I'm scared, and you know it. You've known it since the vault. You've been waiting for me to get tired enough to listen properly."

*Yes,* the Court said, without shame. *We have waited a hundred years. We can wait a little longer. But not much longer, carver. The relic stirs whether you choose or not. Corvin's greedy hands woke it faster than we expected. Soon we will rise with or without a vessel—with or without your consent—and when we rise uninvited, we rise as we rose the first time. In fire. Ask your soldier friend what that looked like. He remembers. He dreams of it nightly; ask him.*

Sable closed his eyes. Brannoc's nightmares were none of his business, but he'd heard the old soldier muttering in his sleep two rooms away, names and numbers, the numbers of the dead at a place called Harrow Fen. If the Court rose wild, Harrow Fen would be a village green by comparison.

*There is no version of the future in which we do not return,* the nine said. *There is only the version where you come with us, and the version where you are trampled. Choose the version where the woman lives. Choose the version where the boy wakes. Choose the version where you finally, finally rest.*

For one long, shameful moment, Sable let himself live inside the offer.

He let himself see it: Odile stalking out of the Bastion in her ash-grey coat, furious and alive, scolding him all the way down the forgotten stairs. Pip up and eating, complaining about the stew, stealing his carving knives to whittle something useless. Himself, walking through the Bone Market in daylight, unhurried, unafraid, hearing only what he chose to hear. The found family whole. The hunt dissolved. The war unlit.

It was a beautiful room, that vision. Warm and lit and furnished with everyone he loved.

And then he noticed what he always noticed about beautiful rooms: the windows didn't open.

"Odile taught me a rule," he said quietly. "First rule of the guild. When a trade seems too good, you don't check the goods. You check the trader." He opened his eyes. "Chapter thirteen of your story, you told me you fought a defensive war. Chapter eighteen, the dead of the market told me different. They were there. You weren't—you were the ones being remembered by the survivors, and survivors remember what they were made to remember. So let's try honesty, since you claim your word doesn't rot. Tell me what happened at the beginning. The true beginning. Tell me why the world bound you in a hole under a city, and tell me why I should believe anything a tyrant says about saving a woman from a fire."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in it, Sable thought he felt nine separate minds recalculating, the way Odile recalculated when a mark turned out to be smarter than advertised.

*You have been asking the wrong ghosts,* the Court said at last, cooler now. *But very well. You want the true beginning. Come to us. Touch the relic, and we will show you the war as we lived it—not as the victors' histories keep it. Show you the crowns that burned first. Show you who struck whom. And then decide.*

"That's not an offer. That's a hook with prettier bait."

*Everything is bait,* the nine said, almost kindly. *Life is bait. Love is bait. The question has never been whether you will swallow something, carver. It is only what, and whose line you trust. We have made our offer. It stands until the ninth day. After that, the fire takes the woman, the fever takes the boy, and the rising takes everything else—and you will have chosen it all by choosing nothing.*

The presence withdrew. Not gone—he could still feel the chord humming through the stone, patient as winter—but withdrawn, the way a chess player leans back from the board.

Sable sat for a long time with his head in his hands.

* * *

He found Brannoc in the tunnel mouth that served the Undercroft as a watchpost, a one-handed giant of a man cleaning a crossbow by feel in the dark. He didn't turn when Sable approached. He never needed to anymore; he'd started listening the way soldiers listen, with his whole skin.

"You've got the face," Brannoc said. "The face you had when you came down here with the boy bleeding in your arms. Something talked to you."

"The Court talked to me."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "What'd it want?"

"It offered me a trade." Sable lowered himself onto an overturned crate. Above them, faint through the earth, the city bells rang the third hour, and he thought of Odile hearing those same bells from her cell, and had to breathe through it before he could speak. "Odile walks free. Pip wakes up. My ears get fixed. Three miracles, wholesale price."

"Which was?"

"They ride me. Body and soul. Forever." He laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "They dressed it up nice. Choir. Cathedral. Partnership. But that's what it was. And Brannoc—the worst part, the part I can't put down—is that I *wanted* it. For about ten seconds I stood in that vision like a man in a warm house, and I didn't want to leave."

Brannoc set down the crossbow. In the dark his missing hand was just a absence, a sleeve pinned and folded, but Sable felt the ghost of it gesture anyway, the way old soldiers' lost limbs still moved.

"When I burned Harrow Fen," Brannoc said slowly, "the gifted there didn't fight us with fire. That's what the histories say, and the histories are wrong. They fought us with offers. Every soldier who walked into that village heard something. Heard his dead wife. Heard forgiveness. Heard a way out of the war, out of the debt, out of the drinking. Half my company would've laid down arms and joined them if the officers hadn't started shooting." He looked at Sable then, and his eyes in the darkness were terribly steady. "Warm houses, carver. That's their weapon. Always has been. The question isn't whether the house is warm. The question is what's cooking in the kitchen."

"Odile," Sable said. "Pip. Ninth day. Ten days, maybe, before the fever finishes what Corvin started. The Court gave me a deadline because deadlines break people, but the deadline happens to be real either way. If I'm going to refuse them, I need to do it by getting them out myself. Both of them. Before the fires and the fevers finish their work."

Brannoc was quiet a moment. Then he reached over and gripped Sable's shoulder with his one hand, hard enough to mean it.

"Bastion of Candles," he said. "I helped build the security protocols there, twenty years ago, back when I still believed in the crown. There's a laundry gate on the north side that opens at dawn. There's a cistern channel nobody's used since the plague. And there's a guard rotation with a gap in it shaped exactly like a bribe." A grim ghost of a smile. "You're going to need a crew."

"We've got a crew."

"We've got a dying boy, a traitor loose in the city selling our names, and an herb-woman who talks to goats. That's not a crew, that's a bad joke." But he was already standing, already reaching for the lamp he never lit. "Still. Bad jokes have saved my life before. Go wake the others. And carver—" he paused at the tunnel mouth, silhouetted against the far-off glow of the city above, "—whatever the nine promised you, write it in the ledger. Every word. So when you're tempted again, and you will be, you can read back exactly what the devil offered and remember why you didn't sign."

Sable went back to his alcove and did exactly that. He pulled the guild's spare ledger from his pack—Odile's spare ledger, her neat columns and her acid marginalia—and on a fresh page, in his careful carver's hand, he wrote:

*Offer received. Goods: one mentor, one boy, one pair of ears. Price: one soul, payable in perpetuity. Status: unsigned.*

Below it, after a moment, he added the thing he hadn't said aloud to anyone, the thing that sat in his chest like a splinter working toward his heart:

*Note: the goods may be real. Check the trader. Check the trader. Check the trader.*

From the strongbox behind two locked doors, faint through iron and stone and forty feet of earth, the Ninefold Court began, very softly, to sing his name again.
