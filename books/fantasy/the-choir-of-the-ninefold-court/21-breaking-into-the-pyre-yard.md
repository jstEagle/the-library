## 21. Breaking Into the Pyre Yard

Brannoc had spent twenty years learning how buildings died.

He'd learned it in the King's Own, before the hand, before the shame—breaching walls, burning barricades, reading a fortress the way a butcher reads an ox. Every structure had a spine, and every spine had a place where it could be broken. He had thought that knowledge buried with his old life, useless as his sword arm.

He was wrong. It turned out you could use a soldier's eyes for saving people too. Nobody had told him that. He suspected nobody had ever thought to try.

The pyre yard of the Inquisition sat on the eastern lip of Candlemere Hill, where the old gallows grounds had been since before the city had a name. The Inquisitors hadn't built anything new. They never did. They took what was already cruel and called it holy. The yard was a bowl of packed earth ringed by a wall of black granite, twelve feet high, topped with iron spikes that had been blessed so many times they'd gone dull and weeping with rust. One gate, south-facing, wide enough for a cart—the wood-cart that brought the fuel. Two watchtowers at the corners, each with a brazier, each manned by four men on rotation. A barracks beyond the north wall, close enough that reinforcements could arrive in under two hundred heartbeats.

Brannoc knew because he'd counted heartbeats outside walls before. You learned to count them when the counting was all that kept you from running.

"Say it again," Pip said. "Slower. With the part about the drain."

They were gathered in the back room of the Undertow, Brannoc's corner of the Undercroft—a low-ceilinged cellar that had been a smugglers' counting house once, then a fever ward, then nothing, then his. Maps covered the table: real ones, stolen ones, and three drawn from memory by people who'd never been inside the pyre yard but had stood on rooftops nearby and squinted. Sable sat at the far end, very still, the way he always sat lately, like a man listening to something no one else could hear. Which, Brannoc supposed, he was. Even now. Even with the relic wrapped in lead and chained in the strongbox beneath their feet, Sable's head tilted sometimes, catching echoes through the shielding like voices through a wall.

Odile Vess burned in six days.

That number sat in the middle of the room like a third person.

"The drain," Brannoc said again, and tapped the map where Candlemere's hillside met the old cistern works. "Before the city piped water up from the Serpent River, this whole quarter drank from rain-cisterns. When they abandoned them, they left the overflow channels open. There's one that runs under the east wall of the yard. The Inquisitors capped it with an iron grate."

"So we can't get through," said Wren, the guild's lockwoman, who had appeared three days ago smelling of river mud and refusing to say where she'd been. She was sharpening a knife as she talked, which Brannoc had learned was just how she listened.

"We can't get through *the grate*." He let that sit. "But the channel itself is wide enough for a thin man carrying nothing. And here's the thing about drains under a pyre yard." He looked around the table. "Ash has to go somewhere."

Pip grinned, sharp as a fishhook. "The clever bastards flush their sins downhill."

"They flush them into the Serpent outflow. Which means the channel is scoured regularly, which means it's clear, which means—" he moved his finger along the line "—a man could come up underneath the ash-pit itself, if the pit grate is old. And it is. I've seen it. Iron bars, hand-width apart, set when the wall was built. Forty years of rust and bone-ash eat iron faster than blessing preserves it."

"That's the way in," Sable said quietly. Not a question.

"That's the way in for one person. Small. Calm. Good in the dark." Brannoc didn't look at Pip. He looked at the map, because if he looked at the boy he'd have to think about what he was proposing, and thinking about it would stop him saying it.

"I'll do it," Pip said immediately.

"You don't know that I meant—"

"You absolutely meant me. Everyone's thinking it. Wren's too big, you're too big, Sable's—" Pip paused, and for once the boy's tongue failed him, some flicker of gentleness crossing his face before he recovered. "Sable's got other work. So it's me. It was always going to be me. I've been in worse holes than a drain, Brannoc. I lived in one."

* * *

The argument lasted until well past midnight, which Brannoc considered a victory, because he'd expected it to last five minutes.

It wasn't really an argument about the plan. The plan was sound—he'd built plans like it for generals who'd decorated themselves off the corpses that followed. The argument was about everything wrapped around the plan, the way gristle wraps around meat.

"You're asking a child to crawl under a killing ground alone," Wren said.

"I'm asking a professional to do the one job he's actually qualified for," Brannoc answered. "I've watched him go through the Undercroft ways. He moves quieter than I ever did with both hands and both causes."

"He's fourteen."

"He's fourteen and he's been feeding himself since he was ten. I'm not pretending that's right. I'm saying pretending otherwise gets people killed." He heard his own voice go flat and old, the parade-ground voice, and hated it, and used it anyway. "Here is what I know that none of you want to hear. Odile Vess dies in six days unless we take her out of that yard. There is no siege option. There is no negotiation option. The Inquisition does not trade; it burns. So we have one plan, and the plan has one door, and the door is small and dark and smells of ash. Somebody fits that door. That's not cruelty. Cruelty is standing here polishing our consciences while she burns because we couldn't stomach the shape of the work."

Silence. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of city, a bell rang the hour—one of the ordinary bells, the ones that only told time, that asked nothing of anyone.

Sable spoke without lifting his head. "She'd hate this plan."

"She'd hate it and then she'd improve it," Brannoc said. "And then she'd order us to run it exactly as written while claiming she'd changed nothing."

Something that was almost a laugh went around the table, thin and grateful. It was the first time any of them had laughed since the raid, Brannoc realized. Since the door came down and Odile put herself between the Inquisitors and her people like a wall made of riddles and contempt. Six days. He turned the number over again, feeling its edges.

"Then we make it better than she would," Sable said. He raised his head at last, and Brannoc saw the exhaustion in him, and under the exhaustion something harder, something being forged. "Every piece of it. If we're doing this, we do it the way she taught us—leave nothing to luck that you can leave to spite."

* * *

They worked for three days, and Brannoc discovered something that unsettled him more than any ghost story the guild had ever traded in: he was happy.

Not glad—happy was the wrong word, too bright. But there was a wholeness to the work that his body remembered from the army and his conscience had never been allowed to keep. The planning filled the hours. Every hour filled was an hour not spent staring at the ceiling remembering a village called Ashford and the smell of what he'd done there. Purpose, he was learning, was just guilt with somewhere to go.

Wren mapped the watch rotations by bribing a coal-heaver who delivered to the barracks—two coppers and a lie about a dead sister inside the walls. Four hours on, four off, towers staggered, changeover at the top of every even hour, and during changeover the tower men clustered together to pass the flask someone always smuggled up. Ninety seconds, maybe more if the flask was full. Ninety seconds where the east wall was watched by nobody.

Pip walked the streets above Candlemere for two days straight, wearing three different stolen faces, and came back with the forgotten ways: a collapsed stair behind the tannery that let out on the cistern road, a coal chute behind a shuttered chandler's shop that opened onto the hill's blind side, and—the crown of his collection—an old funeral tunnel, pre-Inquisition, that the mourners' guilds had used to bring coffins up the hill without climbing the processional stairs in front of the whole city. Sealed with brick, but brick, Pip announced with the pride of a general presenting conquered territory, was just a wall that hadn't met the right person yet.

"And the wood-cart," Sable said, on the third night. They were hunched over the map again, and the lamp flame leaned toward him, or seemed to. "The execution detail brings the fuel the morning of the burning. Cart comes through the gate, unloads, leaves. What if the cart doesn't leave empty?"

Brannoc stared at him. "You want to walk her out the front gate."

"In a coffin," Pip said, delighted. "Oh, that's *filthy*. The Inquisitors love coffins. They'd bless it on the way past."

"It fails ugly," Brannoc said. "If they search the cart—"

"They won't search it," Sable said. "Because the driver will be a real driver, paid real coin, who believes he's hauling an empty box. He can't tell us what he doesn't know. Vale interrogates everyone who comes near that yard—I've heard what she does to suspects. An innocent man passes that test because he *is* innocent. That's the trick of it. We don't beat her gift. We starve it."

Brannoc felt the plan settle into place around that idea like a keystone dropping into an arch. It was good. It was better than good—it was the kind of plan Odile would have pretended to find obvious. And it terrified him, because good plans still died, and he had buried enough of them.

"There's a second problem," he said. "The pit grate. Rust eats iron, but it also makes noise. Pip goes up into the ash-pit, pries the bars—and even quiet, even slow, that's minutes of metal-on-metal directly below the east tower's nose. We need the towers deaf as well as blind."

Wren stopped sharpening. "Fire," she said.

"No fire," Brannoc and Sable said together.

"A little fire," Wren insisted. "A cookfire complaint. A chimney smoking wrong in the tanners' row, downwind. Tower men are soldiers, and soldiers will always look at smoke before they look at silence. Give them a reason to lean the wrong way."

Brannoc chewed on it. It was inelegant. It was also correct. "A controlled burn. Wet straw, green wood—smoke without flame. Tanners' row has a dozen chimneys that smoke wrong on a calm day anyway. Nobody will look twice at the source." He marked it on the map. His hand, he noticed, was steady. Twenty years ago it would have shaken. Funny, the things fear did and didn't do anymore.

* * *

On the fourth night, Brannoc couldn't sleep, and found Sable in the Undertow's front room, sitting alone with a cup of tea gone cold in front of him.

The bone-carver looked up as he came in. In the lamplight his face had the translucent quality of a man who hadn't eaten properly in weeks. "Can't sleep either?"

"I sleep fine," Brannoc lied, and lowered himself onto the bench across the table, which groaned under him the way it always did. For a while neither of them spoke. The Undercroft breathed around them—dripping water, distant footsteps, the murmur of the hidden hundreds who lived below the city because the city above had decided they were crimes.

"I fought at Red Ford," Brannoc said finally. It came out before he'd decided to say it, the way old confessions do, escaping through a crack opened by tiredness. "Last suppression. Before your time. Before most people's time—they scrubbed it from the histories fast enough." He turned his cup, though there was nothing in it. "There was a village on the ford road. Gifted folk, the orders said. Heretics, the priest said. I was twenty-two and I had a torch because the man next to me had a torch and that was the entire reason." He looked up. "You know what I remember most? The singing. They sang while it burned. Not screaming. Singing. Like they thought it might reach somebody."

Sable said nothing. He had the stillness of a man who knew that listening was a whole profession.

"I told myself for years it was orders. Then I told myself it was ignorance—we didn't know, we couldn't have known. Both lies. The truth is I knew, and I did it, and I slept that night, because I was tired, and sleeping is easy when you've got a cause to set your conscience on." He exhaled slowly. "So when I tell you I know how to break into that pyre yard, understand what I mean. I know how those places work because I helped build the pattern. Everything the Inquisition does, we did first, with less ceremony. I'm not planning a rescue despite what I was. I'm planning it *with* what I was. It's the only use I've ever found for it."

"You could have kept that buried," Sable said quietly. "Most men would."

"Most men aren't sending a boy into an ash-pit in four days." Brannoc rubbed his face with his remaining hand. "Guilt's a debt, carver. You can ignore it, but it collects interest. This—" he gestured vaguely at the maps, the lamp, the whole crooked enterprise "—this is me paying some of it down. Won't clear the balance. Nothing clears it. But maybe it stops the interest."

Sable reached across the table and refilled Brannoc's cup from the pot, though the tea was cold and bitter and both of them knew it. It was such a small gesture. Brannoc found his throat tight around it anyway.

"Pip knows what he's walking into," Sable said. "I made sure. No softening it. He looked at me and said, 'Better than the tannery vats,' and then he stole my spoon." A ghost of a smile. "He's not a child we're sacrificing, Brannoc. He's a soldier we're deploying. There's a difference, and he drew it himself."

"Soldiers deserve officers who lose sleep over them."

"Then you're properly qualified."

* * *

The fifth night, they ran it end to end, every piece, every failure point, until the plan wore smooth as river stone.

Wren would set the smoldering straw in the tanners' row at the changeover hour, ninety seconds of leaning heads and watering eyes. Pip would go down the funeral tunnel, through the brick, along the overflow channel, up into the ash-pit, and wait in the dark among the dead men's leavings until the cart came. Sable and Brannoc would be at the chandler's coal chute with the cart-driver's replacement horse and a fast route down the hill through streets Pip had mapped and named after sweets. The coffin would ride out the gate under a blessing it didn't deserve. By dawn, Odile Vess would be in the Undercroft, swearing at them all, alive.

"And if it breaks," Wren said, in the silence after, "at any point—if the grate won't shift, if the cart's searched, if Vale is standing at that gate with her truth-touch—what's the signal? How do the rest of us know to run?"

Brannoc had an answer ready. He'd had it ready for days, and he hated it, and it was the only answer there was.

"Bells," he said. "If it goes wrong, Pip rings the yard bell. One long pull. Every Inquisitor within earshot runs to the bell—that's doctrine, that's drilled into them, trouble gathers at the alarm. It empties the gate for ten seconds. Ten seconds is enough for the rest of us to scatter, and it tells us everything we need to know." He met Sable's eyes across the table. "It also puts every eye in the yard on whoever pulled it."

Nobody said the rest. Nobody had to. They all understood that the signal for *abandon hope* and the signal for *save yourselves* were the same rope, and that the boy on the rope knew it too, and had volunteered anyway, with a grin, because that was who Pip was—someone who had learned young that the world took payment up front, and had decided to be cheerful about the exchange rate.

Sable closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he nodded once. "Then we make sure he never has to pull it."

"Amen," said Wren, who wasn't religious, and began packing her knives.

* * *

Later, alone, Brannoc stood at the Undertow's narrow window slit and looked up through forty feet of stone and street at the distant smudge of Candlemere Hill against the stars. Somewhere up there, in a cell probably smaller than this room, a woman who had once caught him stealing candles from her guild shop and instead of turning him in had hired him was waiting to burn. She'd told him once, early on, when he'd asked why she ran the Vellum Guild the way she did—why collect impossible things, why shelter impossible people—she'd said: *Because the world throws away anything it can't price, and someone has to keep the ledger.*

Tomorrow night they'd go collect her back.

He pressed his hand flat against the cold stone and made the promise the way soldiers make promises: silently, specifically, and knowing full well the world was under no obligation to honor it. *Six days becomes seven. She swears at us over breakfast. That's the whole of it.*

Below him, faint through the floor, he heard Pip laughing at something, quick and bright, a boy rehearsing being brave.

Above him, on the hill, the pyre yard waited with its stacked wood already drying in the wind.

And far off, at the edge of hearing—or perhaps only at the edge of imagination—Brannoc thought he heard bells. Not the city's bells. Lower. Older. Nine notes, layered, like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the world.

He told himself it was the wind in the cisterns.

He was almost able to believe it.
