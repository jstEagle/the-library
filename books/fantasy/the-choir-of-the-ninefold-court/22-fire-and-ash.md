## 22. Fire and Ash

The pyre yard smelled of pitch and old rain, and beneath both, faint as a scar, of burning hair.

Sable crouched in the shadow of the tannery wall with Brannoc's remaining hand heavy on his shoulder, and told himself the smell was memory. It was not. The Inquisition had been burning the gifted for three weeks now, and the yard had learned its trade. The scaffold stood at the yard's center, a platform of black oak with a stake and a stool and a ring of stones, and around it the wood was stacked high and soaked and waiting. Two days they had let it dry in the wind. Two days Odile Vess had sat in a cell beneath the Inquisitors' hall while the city decided what she was worth.

Tomorrow at dawn, the answer would be fire.

Tonight, she was still breathing, and that was the only arithmetic that mattered.

"Four guards on the gate," Brannoc murmured. He had a spyglass he'd taken off a dead officer years ago, and he used it the way he did everything else—economically, without waste. "Two on the scaffold. One walking the wall. The rest are in the barracks across the lane, which means we have perhaps ninety seconds between the first alarm and the second."

"Ninety seconds is generous," Sable said.

"Ninety seconds is what we have." Brannoc lowered the glass. His face, in the light of the distant watch-fires, was a map of old burns. "You don't have to come in, carver. I said I'd say it once. I've said it."

"You said it twice."

"Then I've wasted breath, and I won't do it a third time." He passed the spyglass to Pip, who took it with both hands the way he took everything—like it might be stolen back. The boy had insisted on coming, and Brannoc had insisted harder that he stay in the Undercroft, and Pip had won, the way he always won, by simply being where he intended to be when the argument ended.

"I know the yard," Pip whispered. "Ran messages through it two winters back, before the Inquisition. There's a drainage culvert under the east wall where the tanners used to flush the pits. It's barred, but the bar's rotten. I could've told you that for free, once." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white around the spyglass, and Sable saw that the boy was afraid and had come anyway, and loved him a little for it, and hated that love for what it might cost.

"Vale's not here," Brannoc said. "I checked the hall windows. Her light's dark."

"Maybe she sleeps," Sable said.

"Maybe she does. Inquisitors sleep like cats, one ear open. If she wakes, we run. Nobody fights Vale. Not for Odile. Not for anything." He said it flatly, a soldier's arithmetic, and Sable nodded because it was true and hated it because it was.

He touched the charm at his throat. He had carved it himself, years ago, a small knot of yew no bigger than a thumbnail, and it had never held a voice because he had never let it. It was the only thing he had ever made that was only wood. He held it now like a talisman against the thing he was, and the thing he was leaned close and whispered that the dead of this yard were many, and near, and that they knew the guards' names.

*Not now,* he told them. *Not tonight. Tonight I need my own eyes.*

The dead, for once, were kind. They withdrew like a tide.

"Positions," Brannoc said.

* * *

The culvert was exactly where Pip remembered, and exactly as rotten, and it broke with a sound like a snapped wishbone that Sable felt in his teeth. They froze in the dark of the tunnel, three men-shaped shadows in a throat of stone, listening to the yard above. A guard coughed. Somewhere a lantern hissed. Nothing else.

They came up through a grate behind the woodpile, of all absurd places—Odile's pyre, and they emerged at its foot like gravediggers rising into their own hole. Sable looked at the stacked faggots, the pitch-blackened stakes, and thought: *she is going to die on top of this wood, and I am standing in its shadow, and if we fail, this is the last thing she'll smell.*

"Cellar's under the hall," Pip breathed. "Steps down from the yard, iron door. I saw them carry her down through it." He was shaking. Sable put a hand on his shoulder and felt the tremor travel up his own arm and back down, and neither of them mentioned it.

Brannoc went first, because Brannoc always went first, a one-handed man with a soldier's silence and a knife he held like it was an old friend who'd come back from the war. The iron door was locked, and Brannoc looked at it, and then at Sable, and Sable understood and hated it: *your gift, carver. The dead know locks.*

He pressed his palm to the cold iron and listened.

The hall above had its dead, as all old buildings do—a servant who'd died of fever in the third-floor room, a magistrate whose ghost still rehearsed arguments no one heard. And beneath them, thinner, newer, the dead of the cells: the gifted who had been taken and questioned and burned, whose voices still clung to the stones like damp. One of them had been a bone-singer. Sable knew her voice. Marla, who used to sing to the fishmongers' dying catch so it wouldn't spoil, who had laughed at his careful invisibility and told him the grave was just another guild.

*Marla,* he thought. *The door. The lock. Tell me how it opens.*

And she did—because the dead are generous with small things, with keys and hinges and the weight of tumblers, because small things are all they have left to give. *Left of the hasp. A false stud. Press and turn, press and turn, the way the warden does it, the way I watched him do it through the crack in my door for six nights before they came for me.*

The lock opened like a sigh.

Inside, the cellar was a corridor of doors, and behind the third one, a voice Sable knew better than his own heartbeat said, "If that's a ghost, it walks heavily."

"Odile." His voice cracked on the second syllable, and he didn't care.

The door opened and she was there, and she was smaller than he remembered, which was absurd, because he had seen her three days ago through the bars of the cart, and three days of a cell should not shrink a woman. But it had. Her hair had gone fully white at the temples now, her wrists were ringed with the marks of irons, and her eyes—her eyes were exactly the same, dry and amused and terribly awake, the eyes of a woman who had been cataloguing her captors the way she catalogued inventory and had already priced them.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look like a woman sitting in a pyre yard."

"I look like a woman about to make a great deal of trouble for the Inquisition's laundry budget. Are you going to get me out of here, or did you come all this way to trade insults?" But her hand, when it found his in the dark, gripped hard, and held, and did not let go, and Sable understood that she was afraid, and that she had been afraid for three days, and that she had priced that too and paid it without complaint.

"Brannoc's above," he said. "Pip. We go out through the culvert, east wall, ninety seconds to the barracks' alarm. There's a boat—"

"I know the plan. I've heard you all rehearse it through the floor. The dead gossip, Sable, you know that." She was already moving, already limping toward the stairs, and he marveled at her, at the machine of her will, at the way she made even rescue sound like a scheduling dispute.

At the foot of the stairs she stopped. Her grip on his arm tightened.

"Listen," she said.

He listened. Above them, boots. Not the measured tread of the yard watch—running boots, many of them, and a bell beginning to ring, and a voice shouting a word he couldn't make out through the stone.

"They know," Odile said. "They've known since sundown. There was a man—guild face, I've seen him at the ledgers—came and looked at me through the bars and smiled. Corvin." She said the name like she was entering it in a ledger. "They moved up the burning. It's not dawn anymore, Sable. It's now."

* * *

They came up out of the cellar door into a yard full of torchlight.

Sable's first thought, absurdly, was that it looked like the Bone Market on festival night—all that fire, all those moving shapes. Then the shapes resolved into soldiers, a double ring of them, pikes low, and behind them on the scaffold the wood was already lit, flames climbing the faggots with an appetite that made his stomach turn, and he understood that this had never been an execution they were interrupting.

It was bait.

And they had swallowed it.

"Back," Brannoc roared, and his knife was out, and Pip was beside him with a smuggler's cudgel he'd held like a talisman all night, and the ring of pikes began to close like a hand.

Sable's mind did the arithmetic it always did, the guild arithmetic, the ledger of impossible things: nine soldiers, two blades, one boy, one lame woman, one carver whose only weapon was the voices of the dead. The sum was ruin. He reached for the dead of the yard anyway—*Marla, all of you, the guards, tell me where they'll step, tell me*—and the dead answered in a chorus, *left, the tall one favors his left knee, the one by the brazier is half-asleep, the officer will draw at the count of three*—and it was almost enough. Brannoc moved through the first gap like water finding a crack, and a pike went wide, and a soldier went down holding his opened forearm, and for one bright moment Sable thought the ledger might balance.

Then the second ring closed, and the fire on the scaffold roared up as if fed, and a voice from the yard's gate said, quietly, in a voice Sable had heard in nightmares for a week:

"Enough."

Maren Vale stood at the gate with her hood back and her hands empty, and her face in the torchlight was not triumph. It was the face of a woman watching a thing she had predicted happen anyway.

"Put it down," she said to Brannoc. "All of you. The yard is sealed, the street is sealed, and the commander is on his way with forty more. You have one chance to walk out of this. Take the boy and go. I will not offer it twice."

"You'll burn her," Sable said.

"I'll follow my orders," Vale said, and her voice caught on the word, a hairline fracture, and Sable—who heard the truth in everything, who had spent his whole life hearing what people didn't say—heard it now: she didn't want to. It changed nothing. It changed everything. It was the most useless knowledge in the world.

"Then we die here," Brannoc said, "and you can explain to your commander why you needed forty men for four."

"No," Odile said.

She said it the way she said everything—flatly, finally, the way she closed a ledger. She stepped forward, out from behind Sable, into the open, into the light of her own pyre, and Sable reached for her and she caught his wrist with those iron-marked hands and held him the way she had held him in the cellar, hard, deliberate, a grip with a message in it.

"Listen to me," she said. "We don't have time for pride. You were never going to get me over that wall, and every second you spend trying is a second they spend learning your faces, Pip's face, Brannoc's face. I am one old woman with bad knees and a talent for paperwork. You—" her grip tightened, "—you are carrying the most dangerous thing in this city in your head, and you have been dithering about it for a month, and I am done watching you dither."

"Odile—"

"Shut up and listen. It's the last lesson, so pay attention, because I don't repeat myself." Her eyes were bright and dry and absolutely clear. "The relic. The nine. You think the choice is free them or keep them. You think the Court is a door and you're standing at it wondering which side to open. You're wrong. There is no keeping them. There is no bargaining with them. There is no vessel, no partnership, no clever third path where everyone gets what they want and nobody bleeds. I've read the old ledgers, Sable. The founder's ledgers, the ones I burned the night the proclamation came. The Court does not share. It occupies. Every bargain is a hook, every gift is a leash, and the only thing they cannot survive—" her voice dropped, and the whole yard seemed to lean in to hear it, soldiers and Inquisitors alike, "—is being given back. Truly given back. Unmade. Not freed. Unmade."

The fire behind her climbed. The heat of it touched Sable's face.

"You carve," she said. "You've always carved. You unmake the same way, stroke by stroke, the way it was made, reversed. It will cost you. It may cost you everything. Do it anyway." She smiled then, the old dry smile, the one that had greeted him every morning for nine years across a desk of impossible things. "You were the best thing I ever traded for, Sable Galloway. Don't you dare waste the price."

"Odile, please—"

"Go," she said. "Now. While they're watching me."

And she turned from him—turned her back on him, on the escape, on the whole trembling ledger of what might have been—and walked toward the scaffold with her white-streaked head high and her ruined wrists folded in front of her like a woman going to a meeting she had scheduled herself.

"Odile!" Pip's voice, cracking. Brannoc's hand on Sable's chest, iron-hard, hauling him back as the pikes closed and Vale shouted orders she didn't want to give and the soldiers surged, and the last Sable saw of her was her profile against the fire, small and straight and utterly unafraid, and the last he heard was not a scream at all but her voice, pitched to carry, pitched for him alone out of all that noise, in the tone she used for closing the day's accounts:

*Unmade, Sable. Not freed.*

Then the smoke took her, and the yard roared, and something in Sable Galloway's chest went out like a lamp.

* * *

They ran. Brannoc broke them out through the tannery side where the wall watch had abandoned its post to watch the burning, and Pip led them into the culvert's black throat, and Sable went where he was pulled because his own legs had forgotten their trade. Behind them the bell rang and rang, and the firelight strobed orange against the tunnel walls, and the smell followed them down into the dark, pitch and rain and burning hair, and it would follow him, he knew, for the rest of his life.

They came up in the Undercroft an hour before dawn, and Brannoc barred the door, and Pip sat down on the floor and put his face in his hands and made no sound at all, which was worse than weeping.

Sable stood in the middle of the room with Odile's grip still ghosting his wrist, and did the only arithmetic left.

The Court was singing to him. It had been singing since the yard—softly, sweetly, patiently, the way it always did when he bled: *we can bring her back, carver. We have done greater things than one old woman. Open us. Wear us. Say the word.* And for one long, black moment, with the smoke still in his hair, he wanted to. God, he wanted to. He wanted it the way the drowning want air.

Then he heard her voice again, dry and final, closing the account.

*Unmade. Not freed.*

He took out his carving tools. He laid them on the table, all nine of them, in the order of the work, and his hands were steady, which surprised him, because nothing else in him was.

"We go down," he said. "All the way down. To the binding chamber. And I give them back—every one, voice by voice—whatever it costs."

Brannoc looked at the tools, and then at Sable's face, and whatever he saw there made the old soldier nod once, slow, like a man accepting terms.

Pip lifted his head from his hands. His eyes were red and hard as garnets. "Then we'd better move," he said, "because I heard the dead in the tunnel, Sable. All the way back. And they weren't talking to us."

Sable went cold. "What were they doing?"

"They were listening," Pip said. "Something's answering them. Something down there."

And beneath the floor, beneath the city, far down in the dark where nine thrones of bone waited, Sable felt the singing change key—no longer a lure now, no longer a bargain.

A summons.

They were done waiting to be asked.
