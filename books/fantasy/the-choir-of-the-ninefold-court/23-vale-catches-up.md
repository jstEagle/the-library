## 23. Vale Catches Up

Maren Vale had been following Sable Galloway for three days before she decided not to arrest him.

She told herself it was tactics. A man who had slipped a raid on the Vellum Guild, vanished into the Undercroft, and surfaced only to buy bread and bandages was not a man you took in an alley with two nervous novices at your back. He would have to be taken clean, or not at all. That was the professional explanation, and she repeated it to herself the way a sinner repeats a prayer—accurately, and without belief.

The truth was the lullaby.

Four nights ago, in the interrogation cell beneath the Inquisition's chapter house, Odile Vess had hummed seven notes through split lips while Maren's quill hovered over a blank confession. Seven notes that had not been sung in this world since her mother died with fever in a rented room in Corvassa, when Maren was nine. She had written nothing that night. She had signed Odile's transfer order instead—the pyre yard, three weeks hence—and told herself that was mercy, that a public burning was quicker than the question rooms.

She had not slept properly since.

Now she stood in the shadow of the tannery row above the Lamb's Canal, watching Sable Galloway count coins into a beggar's palm. He moved like a man who had learned to be furniture: unhurried, unremarkable, his bone-carver's apron folded away somewhere and replaced by a laborer's coat two sizes too big. But he touched everything. Doorposts, bridge rails, the canal wall as he passed—that constant, unconscious brushing of fingertips against surfaces, as if the world were a shelf of relics and he couldn't stop reading the spines.

*Gifted*, the reports said. *Auditor of the dead.* The word they used in the chapter house was *necromonger*, spat like a curse, but Maren had read enough of the old case files to know the taxonomy was nonsense. Galloway didn't raise the dead. He listened. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and no one above her rank wanted to hear it.

He finished with the beggar—an old woman whose hands were ruined by tannin—and turned up Tallow Street toward the rookeries. Alone. No Pip, no ex-soldier with the missing hand, none of the guild outcasts the informants had catalogued. As if he'd arranged to be alone.

Or as if someone had told him to be.

Maren followed.

* * *

He led her out of the rookeries entirely, which surprised her. Up through the weavers' quarter, past the shuttered dye-houses, to the old bell tower on Cinders Hill—the one that had burned in the war and never been rebuilt, its bronze long since stripped, leaving a stone shell open to the sky like a broken tooth. Children didn't even dare each other to go there anymore. Too many ghosts, the mothers said, and in this city that was no longer a figure of speech.

Sable climbed the rotting stair to the belfry floor and stood at the rail, looking out over the rooftops, his back to the stairwell. Waiting.

Maren considered her options for exactly as long as it took to admit she had none worth considering. She could call for the watch-pairs stationed at the hill's base—but by the time they climbed, whatever happened here would already have happened. She could draw her stave and take him now, alone, and deliver the Ninefold Court's vessel to Commander Hask in chains, and never learn what Odile Vess had refused to tell her with her last usable breath.

She climbed the stairs.

"Master Galloway." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "You knew I was behind you."

"Since the tannery row." He didn't turn. "Your boots have a cadence. Left foot drags a little on the heel. You favor it when you're tired."

"You've seen me walk?"

"I've heard you walk. Through a door, once, at the guild." Now he turned, and she saw his face properly for the first time—not the grainy sketch from the warrant, but a man perhaps ten years her senior, hollow-cheeked, with the particular pallor of someone who spent his life indoors with dead things. His eyes were steady. That unsettled her more than fear would have. "I assumed you'd come to arrest me. I picked somewhere with one staircase so you'd have to decide first."

"And if I'd brought six men?"

"Then I'd be in irons, and you'd be asking your questions in a room with candles and a table, and I'd be answering them the way Odile answered hers." Something crossed his face at the name—grief, quick and unguarded. "Which is to say: I wouldn't."

Maren came to the rail opposite him. Below, the city spread out in its evening grime and lamplight, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, and somewhere beyond the eastern districts the pyre yard where Odile Vess waited for her death. Three weeks. Eleven days, now.

"I'm going to ask you something," she said, "and I want you to understand what it costs me to ask it."

He said nothing. He was good at silence. She recognized the skill; she'd cultivated it herself across a hundred interrogations, the patience that made prisoners fill the quiet with confessions. But he wasn't filling anything. He was simply standing in it, comfortable, as though silence were a country he held citizenship in.

"The relic," she said. "The ninefold piece you carved. Every report, every informant, every scrap Corvin Ash sold us says the same thing: it contains nine voices. Sovereigns of the Court. And that they are *speaking* to you." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice of the chapter house. "Commander Hask believes they can be weaponized. Bound into an instrument, pointed at the gifted enclaves. He has requisitioned a warden-artificer from the capital to study the binding methods." She paused. "What do they actually want, Master Galloway? Not what the Church doctrine says they want. Not what Hask wants them to want. What did they say to you, in their own words?"

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the empty belfry, moaning softly around the stone arches where bells should have hung—a sound like a choir holding its breath.

"Why does it matter?" he asked finally. "You're an Inquisitor. Your orders don't change based on the answer."

"My orders haven't changed in eleven years," Maren said. "That's rather the problem."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It left her the way truth always left her—too fast, too plain, with no chance to dress it in the chapter house's language. Sable turned fully toward her then, and she watched him look at her the way he must have looked at relics: reading her, listening to some frequency beneath her words.

"You have it too," he said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Don't."

"The touch-for-truth. That's why your questions land differently than the other Inquisitors'. That's why Odile hummed to you instead of screaming at you." He tilted his head. "She knew. Didn't she. She looked at you across that table and knew exactly what you were, and she gave you the one thing she thought might reach you."

"Stop." The word cracked coming out of her. She gripped the rail. The stone was cold under her palms, cold the way all stone was cold, ordinary and mute, and she hated how much she suddenly wanted it to say something—anything—to break the moment open. "I am not here to discuss my—"

"Your gift," Sable finished gently. "Say the word. It doesn't burn you. Saying it is free."

Below them, a cart rattled over cobbles. A dog barked twice and quit. Maren became aware that her heart was going hard against her ribs, and that the last time it had done so was in that cell, at the seventh note of the lullaby.

"They'll find out about me eventually," she said. "It's the way of these things. Hask will demand deeper verification protocols, or Corvin will remember something useful to sell, or I'll hesitate one second too long at the wrong moment. And when they find out, there won't be a trial. There'll be a pyre with my name on it, and the man who lit it will cite my own case files as evidence of my cunning." She laughed, short and bitter. "So yes. Perhaps I want to know what the nine want. Because I have begun to suspect that everyone giving orders in this war is lying, and I have spent my whole life being the instrument other people's lies are played on."

Sable studied her for a long time. Then he sat down on the belfry rail itself, careless of the drop behind him, and gestured to the worn stone bench beside the stairwell.

"Sit down, Inquisitor. This takes a while, and you'll want to check my face while I talk. You'll be able to tell if I'm lying better than I can."

"That's not how it works. I don't get certainty. I get—" She searched for the word. "*Weather*. Pressure changes. A wrongness, like a room where someone has just stopped talking."

"A lie detector who gets weather," Sable said, and there was the ghost of a smile. "Odile would have liked you. Sit."

She sat. She did not draw her stave. She noted, distantly, that this was dereliction of duty, possibly treason, certainly the end of the career she had built out of discipline and grief—and she sat down anyway, because eleven days from now Odile Vess would burn, and Maren had begun to suspect that the only thing worse than complicity was complicity chosen blind.

* * *

He told her about the vault.

Not the raid—she'd read her own after-action report; she knew how Odile had opened the impossible door and shoved him through it, knew the sound the door made when it sealed behind him, a sound like a book closing. He told her about the nights before that, alone with the relic under lamplight, learning to separate the nine voices the way a musician separates chords into notes.

"There's a king who speaks in frost," he said. "Everything he says arrives already cold, already final. A queen who speaks in embers—she's warm right up until she isn't. A soldier-queen who counts things. Marches, losses, years. A twin pair who finish each other's sentences and hate being separated. A priestess who only speaks in questions. A smith whose voice sounds like hammering. And one—" he hesitated, and Maren felt the pressure shift in the air between them, the weather turning, "—one who hasn't spoken to me directly at all. Just listens. I think she's the oldest. I think she's the one who taught the others how to survive being bound."

"What do they say?" Maren asked. "When they speak. What do they promise?"

"Everything." He said it flatly, without seduction, which was somehow more frightening than a sales pitch. "That's the first thing you should understand. They promise everything because everything costs them nothing—they're dead, Inquisitor. Promises are the only currency they can mint. They offered me the location of every hidden gifted soul in this city. Every name. Yours, probably, if they've bothered to look. They framed it as protection: let us help you keep your people safe. Then they offered me Odile's freedom. Pip's healing. My hearing, restored past what it ever was." His hands, she noticed, were scarred in the pattern of a carver's—nicked knuckles, fine white lines across the fingers. They were very still in his lap. "And all they asked in return was me. A throat. A body to wear while they walked around deciding things."

"And you believed them?"

"I wanted to. That's different, and it's worse." He looked out at the city. "But I've been listening to the actual dead my whole life, Inquisitor. The small dead. The widow's husband complaining about his brother. The drowned boy in the canal wall asking if anyone found his boot. Dead people, real ones, are petty and sad and specific. These nine are none of those things. They're *arranged*. Everything they say is composed. And last week—" his jaw tightened, "—the dead of the Bone Market told me the war didn't start the way the Court remembers it. Or claims to remember it. They say the Court fired the first arrow. That the sovereigns burned the border provinces themselves, to frame the crown, to rally their own people. Half the world burned because nine people preferred a war to an election."

The wind gusted through the belfry, and Maren felt it move through her like a hand through wheat. She watched his face. She listened to the weather in the room.

Nothing turned. Nothing curdled. The air stayed level and clear as still water, and she understood, with a sensation like falling, that he was telling the truth—or at least that he believed every word, which for her gift amounted to the same thing.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "that the most dangerous objects in the world are liars."

"I'm telling you they're politicians. Which is worse, because politicians are dangerous *on purpose*." He turned to face her fully. "Now here's what I need to know from you, and then we're square. Hask has the artificer coming. Corvin sold him the relic's provenance, maybe more. What's the plan? Because if they intend to bind those nine into a weapon, they're not arming a sword. They're opening a door and putting a handle on it."

Maren weighed it. Eleven years of compartmentalization told her to give him nothing, to stand, to leave, to file her report and let the machinery grind forward. But the machinery's next product was a pyre with Odile Vess tied to it, and the one after that was a massacre in the Undercroft—she had seen the preliminary lists, the census maps with the smugglers' warrens circled in red ink, *estimated gifted population: four hundred, collateral acceptable*—and the weather in her chest had been turning foul for days.

"Hask doesn't believe the Court can be negotiated with," she said. "He believes it can be *harnessed*. The artificer arrives within the fortnight. After that, the plan is to force the binding using—" she hesitated, hating the words even now, "—using whoever the Court has already spoken to. As the anchor. The vessel. They don't need your consent, Galloway. There are rites for that. Old ones. The Church kept them from the war."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, she realized. Recognition. As if she had confirmed a suspicion he'd been carrying politely, the way one carries a diagnosis.

"They'll use me," he said quietly. "To reach the nine. And if the nine refuse—" 

"The rites don't require refusal to matter."

They sat with that. Below, the city lights thickened as full dark came on, and somewhere a temple bell rang the hour—one clean stroke, unhaunted, just bronze and physics, and Maren thought: *this is what bells are supposed to sound like. This is what we're defending, if we're defending anything.*

"If I help you," she said, and heard her own voice as if it belonged to a stranger, younger, standing in a doorway in Corvassa while her mother sang, "if I get you information, warn you when the moves come—I need something in return. Not for me." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. "Odile Vess. Eleven days. Find a way to get her out of the pyre yard, and I will burn my own career to the ground to cover it. But if she dies while I sit on my hands and follow procedure, then everything I am becomes exactly what the Church says I am, and I'd rather be dead than that."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Then he extended his hand across the space between them—carver's scars, ink stains, the hand of a man who made his living touching what others feared.

"Maren Vale," he said. "Do you know what the dead say about you? The ones I've passed in the streets near the chapter house. The executed ones, waiting in the walls." He almost smiled. "They say you're the only one who looks at their bodies afterward. Everyone else signs the forms. You *look*. They noticed."

She took his hand. Her gift registered nothing but warmth and callus and a pulse slightly too fast—which was, she supposed, another kind of truth.

"This isn't an alliance," she said, releasing him, standing, pulling the Inquisitor's gray cloak back around herself like armor. "I'm not defecting. I'm not one of your guild outcasts. I'm gathering facts, and when the facts contradict my orders, I will decide then what to do about it."

"Of course," Sable said solemnly. "Entirely provisional."

"It is."

"Then you should know the first fact." He rose too, and nodded eastward, toward the dark bulk of the chapter house district—and beyond it, faint on the night wind, a smell Maren had been trained not to notice: woodsmoke, tar, the sweet reek of prepared pyres. "The relic isn't where Hask thinks it is. Corvin stole it, yes—but Corvin never delivered it. Someone else took it off him between the Bone Market and the chapter house steps. Someone who's been carrying it around the city for two days, wrapped in a butcher's apron, trying to decide whether to sell it, free it, or throw it in the canal." His face, in the starlight, was grim. "And last night, Inquisitor, it started ringing. Audibly. In a crowded market. People heard it and *wept*, and didn't know why."

The wind came again through the empty belfry, through the arches built for bells, and for half a heartbeat Maren Vale would have sworn—would have testified, would have signed her name to it—that the wind carried harmonics. Nine of them. Faint. Patient. Like a choir tuning up in a room below the world.

"Where is it now?" she asked.

Sable Galloway looked at her, and for the first time since she'd known of him, he looked afraid.

"With Brannoc," he said. "And Brannoc has decided the only safe place left is the one place no one dares dig." He swallowed. "We're taking it down to the war-vault. Tonight."
