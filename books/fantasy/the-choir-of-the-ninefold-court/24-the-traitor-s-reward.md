## 24. The Traitor's Reward

Corvin Ash had learned long ago that the trick to selling a lie was to bury it in the middle of the truth.

The front of the story was true: he had been a ledger-keeper at the Vellum Guild for eleven years. He had handled impossible things. He had watched Sable Galloway—the quiet one, the bone-carver with his careful hands and his careful silences—slip out through the vault door while Inquisitors kicked in the front. All of that was true, and he delivered it to the man across the desk with the flat competence of a clerk reading inventory.

The middle was where the lie lived.

"The relic is being moved tonight," Corvin said. "Down. Into the old war-vault beneath the Undercroft. Galloway means to unmake it there—destroy it so no one can have it."

He watched Commander Aldous Vane take that in. Vane was not what Corvin had expected when he'd first imagined selling out his guild. He had imagined someone fat and soft, some administrator of cruelty who could be flattered and managed. Instead he got this: a lean man with grey at his temples and eyes like coins left too long in water, who listened without nodding, who never once interrupted, and whose office smelled of ink and cold iron rather than incense. The pyre-smoke from the city's burnings didn't reach this high up the Spire. Vane had made sure of that. He liked his air clean.

"Unmake it," Vane repeated. Not a question. A weighing.

"That's what I overheard." Also true, or near enough. He had heard Brannoc say *the war-vault*, had heard Sable say *tonight*. The rest—the unmaking—he had added himself, because a man does not sell a treasure; he sells a threat. And a threat about to be destroyed is worth more than a treasure sitting still.

"And you came to me," Vane said, "rather than to your guild. Why?"

Because Odile was dead. Because Pip was bleeding somewhere in the dark with a knife-wound Corvin's own carelessness had arranged. Because eleven years of ledgers and bottled laughter and saints' knucklebones had taught him exactly one lesson: everything has a price, and the fool is the one who doesn't set it himself. But he said none of that. He said:

"Because I've seen what happens to people who stay loyal to losing causes. I'd rather be on the winning side of history than the sentimental side of it."

Vane almost smiled. It was the kind of smile that happened entirely below the eyes, if it happened at all. "History," he said, "is written by whoever survives the paperwork."

* * *

They gave him a room in the Spire. Not a cell—a room, though the door locked from the outside and the window was a slit of grey light three fingers wide. Corvin told himself this was protection. A valuable informant needed protecting. He unpacked the small bag they'd allowed him to bring and arranged his things on the shelf with the precision of a man arranging a life: razor, spare collar, the silver letter-opener he'd lifted from Odile's desk the night the guild burned its papers, back before any of this began, back when betrayal had still been an idea instead of a trade.

The letter-opener had been the first thing he'd ever stolen from her. He'd told himself then it was practice.

A soldier brought him supper on a tin tray: bread, salted meat, small beer. The soldier would not meet his eye. That was interesting. Corvin had spent years reading people across a ledger desk—watching which traders flinched at prices, which ones lied about provenance, which ones were desperate enough to buy a door to nowhere—and he knew the difference between contempt and fear. This was neither. It was something worse. It was the look a man gives a thing that has been used.

"You're the one who sold them out," the soldier said, when he reached the door. Not accusing. Almost wondering. "The bone-carver. The one the bells are singing about."

"I'm the one who helped end it," Corvin said.

The soldier looked at him for a long moment, and then did the thing Corvin would think about for days afterward. He crossed himself—the old way, thumb to sternum, the way farmers did, the way people did when they thought something might be listening.

"Begging your pardon, sir," he said, and left.

* * *

In the morning they took him to the war-room.

He had expected a briefing. What he got was a cathedral of maps. The chamber at the heart of the Spire's third level had been built by some architect with more ambition than sense: a domed ceiling painted with constellations that no longer matched the sky, and spread across the floor, inlaid in brass and mother-of-pearl, a map of the entire city so detailed Corvin could find the alley behind the Vellum Guild, could find the grate Pip used to slip out to the rooftops, could find the bone market where a boy had taken a knife meant for another man.

Officers stood around the edges of the map like men around a gaming table, except no one was playing. They were waiting.

Vane stood at the center, on the map itself, his boots planted on the district called the Shambles, and Corvin understood immediately that this placement was deliberate. Men like Vane did nothing accidentally.

"Ash." Vane gestured him forward without looking. "Tell them what you told me."

Corvin told them. The relic. The nine voices. The descent into the war-vault planned for tonight. He kept his voice level, clerk-flat, and watched the officers as he spoke, because watching was the only skill he had left that was worth anything. Most of them wore the expressions of men hearing about weather. But two of them—two—went very still at the mention of the Ninefold Court, and one of those two, a woman with a scarred jaw, glanced at the painted constellations overhead as if checking whether the sky had changed.

"They mean to destroy it," Corvin finished. "You have until tonight."

"Destroying it would be a mercy," the scarred woman said quietly. "To everyone."

"It would be a waste," Vane said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Corvin heard the splash of it go all the way down. Waste. Not danger. Not abomination. Waste.

Vane turned at last and looked at Corvin directly, and Corvin felt the full weight of those coin-colored eyes and understood, with the particular clarity that comes too late, that he had misread this man from the beginning. He had thought he was selling information to a hunter. He was standing in front of a collector.

"Do you know what the Ninefold Court was?" Vane asked him.

"Sovereigns," Corvin said. "Old kings. Tyrants, depending on which histories—" 

"Histories." Vane smiled his below-the-eyes smile. "Written by the victors, Ash, and the victors were frightened men with torches. Nine rulers who held power over death itself, bound underground by cowards, and every generation since has been taught to spit at their names." He walked the edge of the map, boots ringing faintly on brass rivers. "I have read the sealed archives. Do you know what the Court offered the cities they ruled? No plague. No famine. The dead at peace. Order." He stopped. "And we burned half the world taking it from them, and called the burning salvation."

The scarred officer shifted. "Commander. With respect. The pyres—"

"The pyres are for the small fish." Vane said it without heat, the way a man says *the tide comes in*. "Bone-singers and charm-cutters, grief-eaters and hedge-witches. Necessary work. Housekeeping." He turned back to Corvin. "But the Court—that is not housekeeping. That is inheritance."

*It wants to use it,* Corvin thought. *Not destroy it. Not contain it. Use it.* And beneath the thought, arriving a heartbeat later like the second blow of a hammer, came the other thing, the thing he had been refusing to look at since the soldier crossed himself in the doorway: *and it will use me up getting it.*

He had assumed he was selling the relic to the Inquisition. Standing on the edge of that vast brass map, listening to a commander speak of tyrants as an inheritance, Corvin finally understood the shape of the transaction he had actually entered. He was not the seller. He had never been the seller. He was the goods.

"The vault approach," Vane was saying to his officers, all business now. "Ash tells us the Undercroft. Smugglers' territory. We don't send a column—we send a hand. Small. Quiet. Inquisitors Vale's former detail, the ones who can move without being seen." A pause. "Where is Vale?"

No one answered quickly. Corvin watched the silence travel around the room like a debt being passed hand to hand.

"She missed muster," the scarred woman said finally. "Two days running. Her quarters were cleared out. Personal effects gone."

Something flickered across Vane's face—not anger. Recalculation. The look of a merchant discovering a shipment has gone missing and adjusting the ledger accordingly. "Then she has chosen her side, and it changes nothing. She was one instrument. We have others." He looked down at the map, at the district of the Shambles, at the warren beneath it that no surveyor had ever properly charted. "Tonight the Court comes up out of the ground—or we go down and take it. Either way, by morning, the crown holds the oldest power in the world."

The officers murmured assent. The scarred woman did not murmur. Corvin noticed that too, and filed it away, the way he'd once filed away which traders lied about provenance. Old habits. The habits of a man who always wanted a second exit.

* * *

They let him walk back to his room alone, which was either trust or a message, and Corvin suspected it was a message: *you cannot leave. There is nowhere the door locks but here.*

He sat on the narrow bed and did the arithmetic he should have done weeks ago, in Odile's back office, with the silver letter-opener in his pocket and his future glittering in his imagination like bottled laughter catching lamplight.

Here is what he had believed he was buying: safety. Position. To be the man who ended the Gift-Hunt by delivering its greatest prize, rewarded with a post, a pension, a name in the new histories. Here is what he was actually holding: a room with a lock on the wrong side of the door, a commander who called tyrants an inheritance, and a piece of knowledge that was only valuable until tonight—after which Corvin Ash became a loose end with a good memory.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, the thing he kept turning over like a coin worn smooth: the soldier crossing himself. *The one the bells are singing about.* Ordinary people were hearing them now. The whole city, tuning like an instrument. And Vane wanted to put his hand on that instrument.

Corvin had betrayed his guild for money and survival, and he could live with that; he had made his peace with being that kind of man years ago, in small increments, each theft a step down a stair he'd told himself wasn't a stair. But there was a difference between selling a door to nowhere and handing a madman the key to the dead. Even a stair had a bottom.

He went to the window slit. Three fingers of grey light, and beyond it the city, spread out under a low bruised sky. Somewhere down there was the Undercroft, and Brannoc with his soldier's scars and his one hand, and Pip with his knife-wound and his stolen name, and Sable Galloway with his careful hands carrying nine dead sovereigns toward the one place in the world built to hold them. People he had eaten beside for years. People whose sins he knew because he'd recorded them in the ledgers, in his neat clerk's hand, right alongside his own.

Pip had taken a knife in the bone market because Corvin's greed had put the relic in play. He had told himself that was business. Collateral. The price of doing anything at all.

But the Court had been waking even then. He'd heard it, hadn't he? In the vault, the night he stole it—the hum in the bone, the way the carved faces seemed to follow him around the room. He'd told himself it was draft, imagination, guilt. Now the whole city heard bells, and the commander was talking about inheritance, and Corvin understood that the thing he had carried out of that vault in a sack had been awake the entire time, patient as interest accruing, and that everyone who touched it became part of somebody's design. The Court's. Or Vane's. Or—his stomach turned at the symmetry of it—Odile's, who had ordered it destroyed and died saying so, and whose riddles had scattered through the Inquisition like seed.

There was a knock at the door. Two knocks, precise, official.

Corvin stood. His heart was doing something stupid and human, hammering like a boy caught in the till. He smoothed his collar. Whatever came next, he would face it the way he'd faced every negotiation of his life: flat-voiced, watchful, already counting the exits.

The door opened. Not a soldier. An aide, young, breathless, holding a folded paper sealed with the Spire's black wax.

"Commander's compliments," the aide said. "He wants you downstairs within the hour. You'll be guiding the descent party yourself."

"Guiding," Corvin repeated. "Into the vault."

"You know the ways in. The Undercroft, the smugglers' runs." The aide's eyes skittered away from his, and Corvin saw it again—the look of a man regarding a thing that has been used. "The commander says no one else knows the approaches. He says you're the most valuable man in the Spire tonight."

*Most valuable,* Corvin thought, *and therefore most expendable, and both of us know which one I am by dawn.*

"Of course," he said aloud, clerk-flat. "Give me a moment."

The door shut. The lock turned. Corvin stood in his three fingers of grey light and looked at the silver letter-opener on the shelf, and at the slit of window far too narrow for a man, and at the locked door, and understood that he had exactly one move left on the board, and it was the same move he'd been making his whole rotten life: find the buyer before the current owner decides you're inventory.

Below him, muffled through stone, so faint he might have invented it, the city's bells began—wrongly, unsanctioned, at an hour when no bell was rung—to toll.

Nine times.

Then they stopped, all together, like a throat closing.

Corvin picked up the letter-opener and slid it into his sleeve, and went down to earn his reward.
