## 25. What the Dead Want

They gave him a room with no windows.

Brannoc had cleared out the old counting-house at the bottom of the Undercroft warren—a stone cell that smelled of tallow and rat droppings and the ghost of old ink—and set a single chair in the middle of it, facing the table where the relic lay wrapped in three layers of wool and one layer of iron chain.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Brannoc said from the doorway. He filled it the way he filled every doorway, one-armed and broad as a door himself, his missing hand's stump tucked into his belt like a habit he'd never broken. "The girl's still sleeping. You could wait until—"

"She'd tell me to stop stalling." Sable sat down. The chair creaked. "So would Odile. So would you, if you weren't busy pretending this isn't exactly what you want me to do."

Brannoc's jaw worked. He was a man who had burned a village of the gifted twenty years ago and had been chewing on it ever since, and Sable could see the two hungers fighting behind his eyes—the hunger for the Court to be monsters worth destroying, and the older, quieter hunger for them to be something else entirely. Something that would make the burning mean something different than it did.

"Chain stays on," Brannoc said finally. "And I'm outside the door."

"Wouldn't have it any other way."

The door closed. The bar fell. And Sable Galloway, bone-carver, keeper of impossible ledgers, sat alone in a windowless room with nine dead kings and queens and began, very carefully, to work.

* * *

He didn't unwrap the relic all at once. That was the first lesson of his craft: you never took the whole weight of a voice at once, any more than you'd drink a river in a single swallow. He peeled back the first layer of wool and felt the room change temperature—not colder, exactly, but *denser*, as though the air had grown a second skin.

The second layer. The bells began.

They were always bells, at first. That was how the Ninefold Court announced itself, how it had announced itself in the vault of the Vellum Guild a lifetime ago when his fingers had brushed carved bone and heard not the single thin voice of a mourning charm but nine voices braided together like the cables of a suspension bridge. Sovereigns. The old ones. The ones whose war had burned half the world and whose binding beneath the city had ended it.

The third layer came away. The iron chain he left—he wasn't a fool—but the last of the wool slid free, and there it was: a knucklebone no larger than a plum, carved so finely that the surface seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at it. His own work, from years ago, when he'd been young and careless and had thought a commission from a hooded stranger was just another entry in the ledger.

Nine voices rose around him like water filling a cup.

*Sable.* They always used his name now. They'd learned it, and they wore it the way courtiers wore a king's colors. *You've come to speak with us properly at last.*

"I've come to ask questions." He kept his hands flat on the table, on either side of the bone. A carver's discipline: don't touch what you're questioning until you know which of you is holding the knife. "And you're going to answer them plainly. No visions. No music. No showing me my mother's kitchen or Odile's garden or whatever else you've got in your pockets. Words. Plain words."

A ripple went through the voices—amusement, or something shaped like amusement. *You've grown suspicious.*

"I've grown *tired*." And that was true enough that it surprised him, coming out of his chest with weight behind it. "Every time I listen to you, I come away feeling like I've been handed a gift. And then a day later I find out the gift has a hook in it. The location of every gifted soul in this city—which would have painted targets on Pip and Brannoc and half the Undercroft if I'd used it. Visions of the old war—beautiful ones, where you're all martyrs and the crown are butchers. I believed that for two days. Then the dead of the Bone Market told me you lie about how the war started."

Silence. Not empty silence—the silence of a courtroom when the wrong thing has been said aloud.

"The dead gossip," one voice said at last. It was a woman's voice, low and resonant, the kind of voice that had once made rooms full of armed men sit down. He'd catalogued them over the weeks, the way he catalogued everything: the Empress Veyra, third of the nine, if the histories he'd stolen from the guild archive were right. "The dead say many things. They died confused, most of them. Confused people remember wars badly."

"Do they?" Sable leaned forward. "Then let's do this slowly. Let's do it the way I'd check a ledger. Line by line. Because I keep ledgers, Your Majesty. It's the one honest trade I have. And ledgers don't care who's listening—they only care whether the numbers add up."

* * *

He started with the simplest entry.

"The war. You say the crown attacked you first. That the Ninefold Court rose in defense. That's the story you showed me in your visions—crown soldiers at your gates, your people fleeing, you taking up arms because you had no choice."

*It is the truth,* said a man's voice, deep and patient, the one they sent out when they wanted to sound reasonable. Aurelian, maybe. Or the one wearing Aurelian's shape. *We have shown you—*

"No. No visions. Words." Sable pulled a scrap of paper toward him and dipped a pen. An absurd gesture, taking minutes in a room with nine ancient sovereigns, but it steadied him the way it always did. Numbers. Lines. Entries. "Here's my problem. I've spent the last week doing what Odile taught me—asking too many questions, finally—and I talked to the dead of the Bone Market. Ordinary dead. Shopkeepers and cutpurses and a dockhand who drowned forty years ago. None of them were alive during your war, but dead talk to dead, the way runners talk to runners, and memory drifts down through the layers like silt. And the silt says something different from your version."

He let the pause stretch. In the old days, before all this, he'd learned that the dead hated pauses more than anything. Silence made them fill space.

"The silt says the Ninefold Court demanded tribute," he said. "Bone-tithe. Every village under your dominion sent its dead to your vaults—not buried, *sent*. Rendered down. Because bound dead are power, and power was what you hoarded. The crown didn't march on you because it feared magic. It marched because you'd built an empire out of other people's grandparents and called it sovereignty."

The temperature in the room dropped for real this time. The candle flame stood up straight and rigid, like a soldier at attention.

*Careful, little carver,* said the Empress Veyra, and the silk in her voice had a blade under it now. *You handle things you do not understand.*

"I handle bones," Sable said. "I understand them fine. Here's another line item." He wrote as he spoke, partly for the record, mostly so he wouldn't have to look at the relic. "When the crown's armies broke your western gate—you showed me that vision, remember? The heroic last stand, your loyal dead throwing themselves against the siege lines—whose dead were they? Yours were already spent by then, according to the campaign records Brannoc remembers from his soldier days. The ones defending your walls were tithe-dead. Farmers' mothers. Fishermen's sons. People who never swore allegiance to you, dug up out of their graves because their villages owed you bones and you intended to collect."

*They served gladly.*

"They were *compelled*. That's what binding means. I should know—I've unbound enough charms to hear the difference between a voice that stays because it wants to and a voice that stays because it can't leave." He looked up, finally, and let himself meet the relic's surface, let the carved whorls seem to move under his gaze. "And that's the thing I keep coming back to. Every gift you've offered me has been a binding dressed up as a blessing. Save Odile—if I give you my throat. Heal Pip—if I give you my throat. Restore my hearing—my hearing was never yours to restore, it was mine to lose, and you know the difference better than anyone alive or dead, because binding is the only trick you know."

The nine voices spoke at once then, all of them, harmonized into something vast and beautiful and utterly inhuman, and the room shook with it, dust sifting from the ceiling, the iron chain humming on the table like a struck fork:

*WE OFFERED YOU MERCY.*

"You offered me a *throne*," Sable said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, because there was a truth in the crack too. "That's what a vessel is. That's what it's always been. You don't want a servant—you had millions of those, and look how that ended. You want a body. A living one, warm, with hands that can carve and a guild pass and friends in the Undercroft and a face the Inquisition hasn't finished hunting. You want to wear a man who can walk through the world you can't reach anymore. And you picked me because I'm lonely, and lonely men say yes to anyone who talks to them nicely."

The harmonized voice stopped. For a long moment there was nothing but the candle and his own breathing and the far-off drip of water somewhere in the Undercroft's bones.

Then, quietly, almost gently, the Empress Veyra again:

*And if we are everything you say? Tyrants. Tithe-takers. Liars. What will you do, Sable Galloway? Break us? You've held the tools. You've felt what we are. Nine voices, and each one of us is a cathedral. You cannot unmake cathedrals with a carving knife.*

"Odile said something before she died." His hands had gone still on the table. He hadn't meant to say it yet; it had been waiting behind his teeth for days. "She said the relic must be unmade, not freed. She died buying seconds for people she loved, and her last instruction to me was about *you*. She knew what you were. She always knew. She just hoped I'd be smart enough to see it before it saw me."

*The old woman was blind in both senses.*

"She was the sharpest thing in this city, and she trusted me with the worst job in it." Sable stood. His legs were unsteady—he'd been sitting too long in air that wanted to be soup—but he stood anyway, and he picked up his roll of carving tools from the corner, and he laid them out on the table beside the relic, one by one, the way he laid them out every morning of his working life. Gouge. Chisel. The fine hooked burin he'd used for mourning charms since he was nineteen years old.

Not today. Not yet. But soon, and they would see the tools, and they would know what the tools meant.

"So here's the final entry in tonight's ledger," he said. "I asked you here to find out what the dead want, and now I know, because you've never once lied about it—you've only lied about *yourselves*. The dead want rest. All of them, everywhere, always. The tithe-dead in your walls, the market dead in their cold rows, the widow's husband complaining about his boots—it's all the same want, underneath. Rest. And you nine are the one thing in this world standing between thousands of dead souls and the rest they've earned. You're not sovereigns. You're a dam. And dams get broken."

*You will die trying,* said the deep patient voice, and for the first time there was fear in it, real fear, the fear of something that had cheated death for a thousand years and knew exactly how thin the ice was. *The unmaking requires a vessel's strength. You'll pour yourself into the work until there's nothing left. Is that the mercy you preach? Leaving your found family a corpse?*

Sable paused with his hand on the burin.

"That's the first honest question you've asked me," he said. "I'll answer it when I've answered the others. Starting tomorrow. Starting with the one thing you've never explained."

He wrapped the relic back in its wool, layer by layer, and the bells faded, and the voices went with them—but not before he heard it, faint under the silk of their retreat, one voice murmuring to another in a language older than the city, and one word in it that his gift caught and held like a fishhook:

*Ashfall.*

The word the histories used for the last night of the war. The night the sky burned. The night, according to every chronicle he'd ever read, that the Court was *defeated*.

But the way the voice had said it—quick, furtive, the way guilty people say a name—it hadn't sounded like a defeat at all.

It had sounded like a plan they were still waiting to finish.

* * *

Brannoc was asleep against the doorframe when Sable came out, and woke the way soldiers wake, all at once, knife-hand already moving.

"Well?"

"They're tyrants." Sable leaned on the wall. The corridor tilted gently; he let it. "Liars, tithe-takers, the whole ugly ledger. But they're scared, Brannoc. Really scared, for the first time. And scared old powers do one of two things."

Brannoc got to his feet. "Run?"

"Sable shook his head. "Ashfall," he said. "They said it like a countdown."

Above them, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, the city went on with its evening—cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary noise of the living. And somewhere under all of it, so faint that Sable might have imagined it, might have invented it out of exhaustion and dread, a bell rang once.

Only once.

Like something checking that it still worked.
