## 26. Pip Wakes

The first thing Pip learned about waking up was that it hurt.

The second thing was that someone had been reading to him. Badly. Brannoc's voice, low and gravel-rough, stumbling over the words of some old soldier's memoir like a man crossing a river on wet stones. Pip kept his eyes shut and listened, because listening was free and moving was not.

"—and the Third Company held the ford until dusk," Brannoc read, "at which point—"

"You're doing all the voices wrong," Pip said.

The reading stopped. The silence after it was so loud Pip could hear the Undercroft breathing—the drip of water somewhere in the smugglers' tunnels, the tick of Brannoc's stove, the far-off murmur of the warren's other residents pretending very hard not to be listening at the curtain.

"Boy," Brannoc said, and his voice did something complicated, "you've been asleep for nine days."

"I heard." Pip opened his eyes. The ceiling of the sickroom was old ship timber, dark with age and smoke, and someone had hung a string of dried peppers above his bed for luck, which was a stupid thing to hang over a sickbed because it made him hungry every time he looked up. "I heard everything. That's the joke of it. Knife in the ribs and I just—lay there and listened."

He remembered it with terrible clarity. The Bone Market at night, lanterns swinging, Corvin's shadow running, Sable shouting his name—and then the knife, and then Sable's face above him, younger than Pip had ever seen it, scared in a way that made Pip want to laugh even now, even with the ache stitched into his side like a second seam.

"How bad?"

Brannoc set down the book. He'd taken off his harness, so his empty sleeve was pinned up neat against his shoulder, and without the leather and buckles he looked less like a fortress and more like what he was: a tired man with grey in his beard who had been sitting in a chair for nine days.

"Bad enough that Odile would've had opinions," Brannoc said. "Bad enough that Sable hasn't slept since they carried you in. Bad enough that I—" He stopped. Started again. "You nearly died, Pip."

"Yeah, well." Pip tried to shrug and immediately regretted it. "I've got a reputation as a bad investment. Can't have people thinking I've gone reliable."

But his voice cracked on *reliable*, and Brannoc pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness, and Pip lay back against the pillows and stared at the peppers and let himself be, for one more minute, a boy who had almost died and been allowed to make jokes about it.

* * *

They came to him in shifts, over the next two days, and Pip catalogued them the way he catalogued everything: by what they were hiding.

Sable came first, always first, slipping in before dawn with broth he'd made himself and pretending he hadn't. He sat close but not too close, the way you sit near a fire you're afraid of, and he talked about small things—the price of tallow, a rat in the storeroom he'd named Chancellor, the way the tunnels flooded when it rained—and never once about the relic, or the Court, or the knife, or the fact that when Pip closed his eyes he could still feel the cold going in under his ribs.

That was how Pip knew something was wrong. Not the avoiding—Sable avoided things professionally, it was practically his trade—but the *weight* of it. Sable moved through the Undercroft like a man carrying furniture on his back. Everyone stepped around him. Everyone spoke gently. And every decision, Pip noticed, every single one, ended the same way: *what does Sable think? What does Sable want? Is Sable safe?*

Brannoc came at midday, brought actual bread instead of gruel, and grumbled about supply lines and the rising cost of everything and the Inquisition patrols on the surface, three deep now, chalk marks appearing on doors in the districts above. He talked around the thing too. They all talked around the thing.

Vale came last, which surprised him. She stood in the doorway like she wasn't sure she was allowed indoors, this tall grim woman with her Inquisitor's coat replaced by a plain grey cloak, and she said, "I owe you a debt," in the tone of someone reading out a sentence of law.

"You don't," Pip said. "You owe Sable. I just got in the way."

"The knife was meant for him because he was protecting you." Vale's jaw tightened. "In my experience, debts don't care whose name is on them. They collect from whoever's standing nearest." She left a paper-wrapped bundle on the stool—sweet buns, still warm, stolen or bought or both—and was gone before he could thank her, which told Pip more about her than any confession could have.

And through all of it, underneath the visits and the broth and the careful voices, Pip listened the way he'd always listened, flat on his back with nothing to do but listen, to the sound of the Undercroft organizing itself around Sable Galloway like water around a stone.

It sounded, he thought, exactly like the way a city organizes itself around a king.

He didn't like kings.

* * *

On the third day he got up.

It took him most of an hour and involved language that would have gotten his ears boxed in any orphanage worth the name, but he got up, and he dressed, and he held onto the wall through the corridor and past the kitchen, where Marta the cook took one look at him and shoved a heel of bread into his hand without stopping her work, and he followed the sound of voices to the long room where they held their councils—which was to say, where Sable sat at the head of the table and everyone else sat along the sides and asked him questions.

Pip stood in the doorway and watched.

Sable had the relic's wrapping-cloth spread on the table—not the relic itself, that stayed locked in Brannoc's iron chest, but the diagrams Sable had drawn of it, the ninefold knot of bone and silver, annotated in his small precise hand. Brannoc stood at his shoulder. Vale sat opposite, arms crossed. Two of Brannoc's tunnel-runners hovered by the wall. And Sable was talking—quietly, wearily, in that voice he used when he wanted to be invisible even while speaking—about routes, about timing, about what *he* would do, what *he* would carry, where *he* would go when the time came.

"And if it wakes fully before we reach the vault," Sable was saying, "then I go in alone. It knows me. It'll listen to me. Nobody else needs to—"

"No," said Pip.

Every head turned. Sable's face did something painful—relief and fear fighting for the same ground.

"You should be in bed."

"Should I?" Pip came into the room. His side pulled with every step, a hot wire of pain, and he let them see it, let them see him walk anyway. "Funny thing. Nine days lying in that bed, and do you know what I heard? Everything. Every conversation outside my door. Every time somebody lowered their voice when they passed. And here's what I learned." He reached the table and put both hands flat on it, and his hands were shaking, and he let them shake. "This isn't a council. This is a man asking permission to die alone, and a room full of people too polite to tell him no."

"Pip—" Brannoc started.

"No, you don't get to *Pip* me either." He pointed at Brannoc without looking away from Sable. "You burned a village. You've decided your whole life is penance, so you follow whoever seems most guilty and call it purpose. And you—" now Vale, and she flinched like he'd struck her, "—you handed the Inquisition a map to a massacre because you were following orders, and now you're following Sable instead, and that's not the same as choosing. And *you*—" his finger came around to Sable at last, and his voice went rougher than he meant it to, "—you've spent your whole life being quiet so nobody would look at you, and now everybody's looking, and you're trying to carry all of us on your back because carrying us is easier than trusting us."

The silence rang.

Marta appeared in the doorway behind him, drawn by the shouting, took one look, and vanished again—which was the Undercroft way, and Pip loved it for that.

"Sable." Softer now. His side was screaming and he leaned harder on the table. "Nine days. You know what I thought about, lying there? I thought about the market. About you yelling my name. And I thought—if I die, he'll decide it was his fault, and he'll stop eating, and he'll start making decisions like a man who's already dead, and one day he'll walk into that vault alone because nobody stopped him, and we'll all be standing around afterward saying *we should have said something.*"

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what happened with Odile." The name landed like a dropped glass. Sable went white. Pip hated himself and kept going, because the wound was open either way and clean cuts healed faster. "She died buying you seconds because you were the plan. All of it. Every plan. You. And I know why—I know you think you're the only one who can hear them, the only one it wants, the only one who can end it. Maybe that's even true. But there's a difference between being the blade and being the hand that swings it, and right now you're trying to be both, and you're going to break, and when you break, everything breaks."

Sable looked down at the diagrams. His hands had gone still on the table, and Pip saw—for the first time, really saw—the tremor in them that had nothing to do with fear. Exhaustion. Grief. Nine kinds of weight.

"I don't know how to do it any other way," Sable said quietly.

"Well." Pip pulled out a chair and sat down, heavily, gratefully, because standing was becoming theoretical. "Lucky for you, I'm a professional at learning things fast and selling them cheap. Lesson one: family means you don't get to be a martyr. We voted. It was unanimous. You weren't there."

* * *

It did not become easy. Pip hadn't expected it to. But something shifted in the room that morning, some load-bearing wall knocked through, and what came through the gap was air.

Brannoc spoke first, haltingly, and it turned out he'd been sitting on a soldier's worth of knowledge about the war-vault approaches—old siege maps, decommissioned sally-ports, the way the Inquisition liked to seal a building before they breached it. He'd been waiting for Sable to ask. He'd have waited forever. Once asked, he talked for an hour, and the plan on the table changed shape entirely, became something with redundancy and fallbacks and more than one person who knew each step.

Vale spoke next, and it cost her visibly. She laid out what she knew of the commander's disposition, the patrol rotations, the pyre yards—and then she stopped, and her hands curled on the table, and she said, "There's something else. Something I've been carrying alone, because I thought it was mine to carry." And she told them about the order she'd found in the commander's papers—the cleansing of the Undercroft, the thousands marked for the pyres—and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and nobody looked away, and when she finished, Brannoc, who had every reason to hate her, pushed the water jug across the table to her like it was nothing, like it was just what you did.

Pip watched Sable watch all of it. Watched him unclench, degree by degree, like a fist remembering it could also be a hand.

By evening the plan had five authors instead of one. By nightfall it had contingencies. And when Sable finally said, "We should rest," nobody moved until they'd agreed on the first hour of tomorrow, and the second, and who would wake whom, and it was such a small thing, such an ordinary thing, deciding together, that Pip caught Brannoc blinking hard at the candle flame and pretended not to.

Later, when the others had gone, Sable lingered by Pip's chair.

"You could have died," Sable said. "Because of me."

"Nah." Pip grinned up at him, tired and crooked. "I could have died because Corvin's a coward and knives are cheap. You just happened to be standing there being worth dying for. Different thing entirely." He sobered. "Listen. When we go down there—into the vault, into whatever's waiting—you don't go alone. Swear it. Not because the Court wants you. Because we do."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and it was the first honest thing Pip had felt from him in nine days.

* * *

Pip woke before dawn to the sound of bells.

Not the cathedral bells—they'd been silent since the Court began stirring, the ringers too frightened to climb the towers. These came from everywhere and nowhere, thin and silvery, ringing up through the bones of the mountain itself, through the Undercroft floor, through the soles of his feet.

He limped to the corridor and found it already full. People stood in doorways in their nightclothes, faces tilted upward, listening. Old Mother Cassel had her hands pressed over her ears and was weeping. Down in the main hall, Brannoc stood beneath the great vent-shaft with his head bowed, and beside him Vale, and beside them Sable, who had gone the colour of ash.

"It's starting," Sable said. His voice carried down the corridor, quiet and terrible. "The relic's waking on its own. Corvin handled it too much—it doesn't need me anymore. It's calling the dead up through the whole city." He turned, and Pip saw that his hands were steady now, steady with purpose, and that he was already reaching for his coat. "The vault. Tonight. Before it finishes waking."

Above them, faint and far away, the bells rang on—and beneath the bells, so soft Pip might have imagined it, came another sound: footsteps. Thousands of them. Shuffling up out of the graveyards of the upper city, walking toward the light.
