## 27. The Commander's Gambit

The summons came at dawn, and Maren Vale knew before she opened the door what it would say.

She had learned to read the Inquisition the way sailors read weather—not by any single sign but by a pressure in the air, a stillness that preceded violence. The messenger was a boy she didn't recognize, which meant the order hadn't come through her own chain. Orders that came through your own chain could be delayed, misfiled, lost in the bureaucratic silt of the Bastion's lower floors. Orders delivered by strangers were orders meant to be obeyed before you could think.

The boy held out a folded paper sealed with black wax. She broke it standing in her doorway, still in her nightshift, and read it twice because the first time her eyes refused to accept the words as anything but a bad dream.

*By decree of Lord Commander Aldous Renne and the Holy Office of the Gift-Hunt: the district known as the Undercroft is declared a nest of unregistered practitioners. At noon tomorrow, the district will be cleansed. All entrances will be sealed. No quarter is to be given. No records are to be kept.*

No records are to be kept.

She stood there long enough that the boy shifted his weight and coughed. She sent him away with a coin she didn't remember taking from her purse, then closed the door and leaned against it and let the paper hang from her fingers like something dead.

The Undercroft. Brannoc's warren of smugglers' tunnels and forgotten cellars, where the city's unwanted had made a life out of the city's leavings. She had never been inside it—had only heard it named in interrogations, always with the same careful vagueness, the same instinctive shielding that people used for the places they loved. A thousand souls, maybe more. The old soldier who ran it. The children who ran messages through holes in the world too small for grown men to follow.

And every gifted soul who had ever fled there, drawn by rumor like moths to a lantern they prayed wouldn't burn them.

Her reports had led them there.

She walked to her washbasin and looked at herself in the small mirror above it, an exercise Odile Vess had once recommended with a riddling smile—*know the face you lie with, Inquisitor, or one day you'll forget whose it is.* The woman looking back at her had gray eyes and a straight spine and the composed expression of an officer of the Holy Office, and Maren wanted to put her fist through the glass.

Three months of reports. Careful, meticulous, damning reports. She had written them the way she wrote everything—precisely, completely, with the small falsifications buried so deep even she sometimes forgot they were there. *Suspect released for insufficient evidence.* *Bone-singer interrogated, found innocent, marked for observation.* And threaded between the true entries, the ones that should have been harmless: locations. Patterns. The observation that the guild's people scattered toward the river when pressed. That the runner boy moved goods through the old tannery district. That the ex-soldier Brannoc had been seen near the cistern gates.

She had told herself she was protecting them by controlling the narrative. She had told herself that a report filed by Maren Vale was a report softened by Maren Vale. She had written the truth in cipher inside her own head and lies on the page, and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that the page was all anyone read.

But Aldous Renne did not read reports the way she wrote them. He read them the way a butcher reads a map of cattle pens. Every location she'd noted, every pattern she'd traced, every careful half-truth she'd offered as misdirection—he had taken the bones of it and built a pyre.

They had used her mercy as a surveyor uses a compass. Her falsified reports hadn't hidden the gifted. They had *concentrated* them. By clearing suspects, by marking certain districts quiet, by noting where the hunt had found nothing—she had told the Inquisition, in her own precise hand, exactly where the hunted had gone to ground.

The Undercroft was quiet in her reports. The Undercroft was safe. The Undercroft was where nothing had ever been found.

So of course that was where everyone had run. Of course that was where they were now, a thousand candles burning in a cellar, and tomorrow at noon the Inquisition was going to pour oil over all of it and call it cleansing.

Maren sat down on the edge of her bed and put her head in her hands and did not pray, because she had stopped believing her prayers went anywhere except back into her own skull, where they curdled into excuses.

* * *

The war council convened at midmorning in the Map Room, a chamber at the top of the Bastion where the whole city lay spread below in painted miniature—the rooftops glazed with crushed lapis, the river a ribbon of silver leaf, the Undercroft rendered as a featureless gray smudge beneath the tannery quarter, as if the cartographer had preferred not to look at it closely.

Lord Commander Aldous Renne stood over the map the way he stood over everything: square-shouldered, silver-templed, his voice pitched at the exact register of reasonable certainty that made disagreement sound like madness. He was not a cruel man, Maren had once believed. He was worse than cruel. He was *clean*. He had the terrible tidiness of a man who had never once doubted that the world could be sorted into categories, and who considered the sorting a form of love.

"Sisters, brothers." He gestured at the gray smudge. "Tomorrow at noon, we end the longest infection this city has carried."

Around the table sat two dozen officers of the Gift-Hunt, their white-and-gray uniforms immaculate, their faces arranged in expressions of solemn resolve. Maren sat among them with her hands folded and her heartbeat loud enough that she feared someone would hear it.

"The Undercroft," Renne continued, "has sheltered unregistered practitioners for decades. Smugglers, bone-singers, hedge-witches, name-takers—the filth that flows downhill when the light of the Office shines above." He tapped the map, and his finger left a small dent in the soft paint. "Our intelligence confirms the presence of no fewer than eight hundred souls, likely more. Among them—" he paused, letting the room lean in, "—the fugitive Sable Galloway, the bone-carver who traffics with the Ninefold Court itself."

There it was. Her stomach turned over slowly, like a body rolling in water.

"You have all heard the bells," Renne said. "You have all felt the dead stirring in the ground like worms after rain. This is the work of the Ninefold Court, waking beneath our feet, and its servants are gathering in the dark under our city. Tomorrow we burn them out. Sealing squads take the twelve known entrances at dawn. At noon, the fires go in through the ventilation shafts. Anything that comes up the stairs dies on the stairs."

An officer near the end of the table—a young man Maren didn't know, fresh from the provinces, his face still soft—raised a hand halfway and let it fall. "Lord Commander. Eight hundred souls. Some of them must be... ordinary. Smugglers, yes, but children, laborers—"

"Then they chose their company poorly," Renne said, without heat, without pause, the way a man says *the tide comes in*. "The Office has never erred by being thorough, Brother Calloway. It has erred only by being late." He looked around the table, gathering their eyes the way a fire gathers sparks. "I know some of you feel hesitation. Hesitation is human. But ask yourselves: when the bells rang over your houses last week, when your mothers woke screaming from dreams not their own—was that not the enemy already among us? We are not beginning a war tomorrow. We are ending one that started without our consent."

Maren watched the young officer's doubt fold itself away, neat as a letter into an envelope. She watched the others nod. She nodded too, and hated the motion of her own neck.

"Vale." Renne's eyes found her, and she felt the particular cold of being seen by a man who trusted you. "Your reports have been instrumental. Your section identified the migration patterns, the concealment networks. The Office owes this victory to your diligence."

Every face turned toward her. She felt the praise land on her skin like ash—weightless, suffocating, everywhere.

"I only did my duty, Lord Commander," she said, and her voice came out level, and somewhere inside her something quietly tore along a seam that had been weakening for weeks.

"After tomorrow," Renne said, "there will be a great deal of paperwork. Confessions to sort. Survivors to process—if there are survivors." A thin smile. "I want your section leading the intake. You have a gift"—he said the word without irony, without knowing he was holding a knife to her throat—"for finding truth in frightened people."

*Yes,* she thought. *I do. That is precisely the problem.*

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she had done every day since she'd taken her vows: working. It was the only prayer she trusted anymore. She processed three suspects in the interrogation rooms below the Bastion, and each one she touched with bare hands under the pretense of checking for binding-marks, and each time her gift flared and showed her the plain truth of them.

A washerwoman who could make stains walk out of cloth and vanish. Innocent of everything except hiding. A ferryman who whispered to drowned men and brought back the names of the lost so their families could mourn properly. Innocent. A girl of perhaps fifteen whose gift was so small and so strange that Maren almost laughed through her despair—the child could make bread rise perfectly, every loaf, every time. That was all. Warm bread in a hungry city, and tomorrow the girl would burn for it if she stayed where the reports said she'd gone.

Maren falsified all three files. *Insufficient evidence. Released pending observation.* Her hand shook only on the third, and she steadied it by pressing it flat against the desk until the trembling stopped, and understood that she was practicing. Practicing for something larger. She just didn't yet know if the thing she was practicing for was defiance or complicity.

Because here was the arithmetic that gnawed her: if she ran to the Undercroft tonight and warned them, she saved a thousand lives and damned her own—and worse, she might save no one at all. Twelve entrances, sealed at dawn by squads she couldn't call off. If she warned them and the warning failed, she died uselessly in a tunnel with the people she'd failed. If she did nothing, she lived, and eight hundred people burned, and she lived afterward, years and years of afterward, wearing her own face.

And there was a third door, one she kept trying not to look at: Galloway. The bone-carver. If the Court was truly waking—if the dead were truly stirring—then the man who could hear nine ancient voices might be the only person alive who knew how to put them back to sleep. Renne wanted him burned as a servant of the enemy. Maren had stood close enough to him, in the moment before he vanished down the vault stair with Odile Vess's blood on his hands and grief in his mouth, to touch the truth of him: *he is not lying; he does not serve them; he is trying to stop them.*

She had let him go. She had written *fugitive, pursuit ongoing* in a report that detailed nothing, and she had told herself it was strategy.

It had not been strategy. It had been the first honest thing she'd done in years, dressed up as the last dishonest one.

* * *

Evening found her in the reliquary archive, three floors below the Map Room, where the Inquisition kept the confiscated relics of forty years of hunting. She had access—senior officers always did—and she moved down the rows of labeled drawers and lead-lined boxes with the ease of long habit, though she had never once come here for what she came for now.

She needed to understand what was waking. She needed to know if the bells over the city were the Court rising, or something else, or nothing—because if Galloway was right, if the nine sovereigns were liars wrapped in glamour, then Renne's massacre wasn't just murder. It was murder committed on a stage the Court itself had built, a slaughter that would feed the very thing it claimed to fight. Fear made the living clutch at ghosts. Ashes made martyrs. A thousand dead in the Undercroft would ring in the city's memory like a bell that never stopped tolling, and the Court would drink that ringing like wine.

She found the file she wanted near the back: *Ninefold Court — Historical Suppression, Vol. III.* The pages were brittle and smelled of smoke that had soaked into them a century ago. She read by lamplight while the archive's silence pressed against her ears, and what she read made her sit down slowly on a stool that groaned under her.

The original binding of the Court had not been done by the crown alone. It had been done by *gifted hands*—bone-singers and grave-listeners by the dozen, working with the armies of their day, because the sovereigns of the Ninefold Court had been tyrants even to their own kind. The gifted of that age had helped bury their own gods. And the price recorded in the margins, in a clerk's cramped hand: *the listeners gave their hearing to the work; none who bound the Nine heard birdsong again.*

Galloway meant to give his hearing. She understood it suddenly, wholly, the way her gift delivered truth—whole and undeniable. The man intended to unmake the relic voice by voice and pay for it with the sense that defined him, and the Inquisition's answer to that sacrifice was to burn a thousand innocents and call it victory.

Maren closed the book. In the dark of the archive, she spoke aloud, to no one, to the shelves of confiscated ghosts.

"I armed them."

The words echoed and died. Nothing answered, and for the first time in her adult life she was grateful for the silence, because she could not have borne a voice agreeing with her.

She thought of Odile Vess in her cell, humming a lullaby that only dead women should have known, smiling at Maren through the bars as if she could see the crack running through her and was patiently, lovingly waiting for it to widen. *Know the face you lie with, Inquisitor.* Odile was going to burn on Thursday unless something changed, and eight hundred strangers were going to burn tomorrow, and Maren Vale had written the maps for both pyres with her own meticulous hand.

She blew out the lamp.

In the darkness she made her decision the way she made all her decisions now—not with courage, which had never been hers, but with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own confession. There was no version of tomorrow morning in which she stood with the sealing squads. There was no version in which she filed one more clean report and slept at night. The seam had torn all the way through. What remained was only to decide what shape the rest of her life would take: a fugitive's, or a corpse's.

She took the historical volume under her arm. Then, moving through the sleeping Bastion on routes she'd memorized during a decade of inspections, she stopped at the records office and took something else: the operational ledger for tomorrow's action. Entrances, squad assignments, timing, the shafts designated for the fires. Everything. The whole anatomy of the massacre, bound in gray leather with the Office's seal upon it.

At the postern gate, the duty guard saluted her. "Late night, Sister Vale?"

"Records audit," she said. "The Commander wants the paperwork clean before the action."

"Aye. He's particular that way." The guard waved her through, and the gate swung shut behind her with a sound like a verdict.

The city at midnight was quiet in the wrong way—the hush of held breath, of dogs refusing to bark, of a place that sensed weather coming. Far off, beneath the river's murmur, Maren thought she heard it again: bells. Faint, layered, patient. Nine tones braided into one.

She pulled her cloak tight and walked toward the tannery district, toward the gray smudge on the map, toward the thousand lives her handwriting had condemned—carrying the Inquisition's plans in her satchel and her treason in her chest, knowing that somewhere below the streets a bone-carver was listening to dead kings, and that by dawn she would be kneeling in front of the man whose family she had helped scatter, offering him the only currency she had left.

The truth. And her life, such as it was, thrown in.

Behind her, the Bastion's towers rose black against the stars, and in the highest window of the Map Room a single lamp still burned—Lord Commander Renne, awake, reviewing his lists, counting his eight hundred like coins.

Tomorrow at noon, the fires were scheduled.

Maren intended to be underground long before then—and she intended to bring the war with her.
