## 28. Defection

The bells woke me before the sentry did.

Not the Court's bells—those had been silent since the market, since Pip's blood on the cobbles and Corvin's running feet. These were ordinary bells, the kind the city rang for fires and funerals, and they were ringing wrong. Too many of them. Too fast. I lay in my cot in the Undercroft's third gallery and listened to the dead of the deep tunnels murmur in their sleep, and I thought: something has changed above.

Then Brannoc was shaking my shoulder, one-handed, his grip hard enough to bruise.

"Up," he said. "We've got a visitor at the Salt Door. She's alone. She says her name is Maren Vale."

I sat up so fast the cot frame knocked the wall. "You let her—"

"I let her stand in the rain," Brannoc said. His face was grey as old tallow. "She's got a satchel over her shoulder and her hands where we can see them, and she's asking for you by name. So either this is a trap with your name stitched into it, or it's exactly what it looks like." He turned for the door, then stopped. "Either way, Sable—you're the one who talks to her."

* * *

The Salt Door opened onto a drainage culvert that smelled of brine and old iron, and Maren Vale stood in it the way she stood everywhere: perfectly still, as if she had made an arrangement with the world to be overlooked and the world had grudgingly agreed.

Rain ran off the hood of a plain oilskin cloak. No Inquisition grey beneath it. No badge. Her hands were empty and open at her sides, and there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to drown in.

"Bone-carver," she said.

"Inquisitor."

"Not anymore." She lifted the satchel slightly, like a merchant showing wares. "I've brought you everything. The campaign plans. The deployment orders. My own reports, annotated where I lied. And a confession, written out and signed, that would hang me twice over if anyone I used to serve ever read it." Her voice didn't shake. That frightened me more than shaking would have. "May I come in? It's cold, and what I have to say will take longer than you'll like."

Behind me, in the dark of the culvert mouth, I heard Brannoc's boots shift. Heard the click of a crossbow being quietly, deliberately cocked.

"You can say it here," I told her.

Something flickered across her face—not hurt, exactly. Recalculation. "Here, then." She reached into the satchel and drew out papers wrapped in oilcloth, laying them on a dry stone shelf one by one, careful as a surgeon laying out instruments. "This is the order for the cleansing of the Undercroft. Three hundred soldiers. Fire teams assigned by gallery. They intend to seal the exits first." She looked up at me, and her eyes were terrible in their steadiness. "They know about the Salt Door because I wrote about the Salt Door. In a report meant to establish that no gifted could hide there. I gave them the map, carver. Every tunnel I could find. I spent four months finding them."

The dead in the walls went quiet, listening. Even they wanted to hear this.

"Why?" I asked. It came out rougher than I intended. "Why bring us this now?"

"Because I believed I was hunting monsters," Vale said, "and then I met Odile Vess in an interrogation cell, and she hummed a lullaby only my mother knew, and I discovered that the monster was the man giving the orders." She folded her hands. "And because three days ago I verified—with my own gift, the one I've spent eleven years pretending not to have—that every name on the commander's list is innocent of anything except being born wrong. And then I falsified my report to say so, and he read it, and he ordered the pyres lit anyway." For the first time, her voice moved. Just a hairline crack, like ice on a pond. "He doesn't care whether you're guilty. Guilt was never the point. You're the point. You're fuel."

* * *

Brannoc did not wait for the family council.

He waited until Vale had been escorted—Pip's idea, and Pip insisted on doing it herself, walking behind the Inquisitor with a knife held low and casual as a conversation—to a dry side-gallery with a brazier and a guard at each end. Then he turned on me in the main passage, and his voice was the voice I'd heard him use once before, the night he told me about the village he'd burned.

"You vouched for her already. Don't think I didn't see it. Standing out there in the rain like a lost dog, and you looking at her like—" He stopped himself. Breathed through his nose. "Sable. She mapped our tunnels. Four months. Every person who died when the Inquisition finds a warren, dies holding a map she drew."

"She brought us the map back," I said. "Along with the orders to kill us."

"That's not absolution, that's inventory!"

"It's more than any of us has done." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, and I let it. "Brannoc. You burned a village. You've told me. You stood in front of me with your stump and your shame and you said *I did it because they told me it was mercy*, and I didn't ask you to hang for it. I asked you to help me carry it."

"This is different."

"How?"

"Because I was a soldier following orders, and she *chose*—"

"So did you," I said. "So did I, every year I kept Odile's ledgers and carved charms for people I knew were being watched, and said nothing, because silence was safer. We are all of us in this hole because we chose the safe thing until the safe thing started killing people." I stepped closer. The dead murmured against my skin, restless, and I ignored them. "She is standing in a room with two guards who hate her, having handed us the means to save three hundred lives, knowing we might kill her for it. That's not a trap, Brannoc. Traps don't hand you the knife first."

He stared at me for a long moment. Rain-sound filtered down from somewhere far above, soft as breathing.

"And if you're wrong?" he said finally. "If she walks out of here and puts a brand on the wall and we all burn?"

"Then it's on me," I said. "My vouching. My risk. Write it in the ledger if you want—I'll sign it."

Something in his jaw worked, ground, settled. He looked older than he had a month ago. We all did.

"One chance," he said. "One. And if she so much as looks at an exit wrong, I won't wait for a vote." He turned away, then paused. "Your mentor would have hanged her too, you know. Odile. She wasn't sentimental."

"No," I agreed. "But she'd have listened first. Listening was the whole point of her."

* * *

We convened in the long gallery—the whole strange, battered family of us. Pip sat cross-legged on a crate with her knife across her knees, watching Vale with the flat, unblinking attention of a cat watching a snake. Brannoc stood by the wall, arms folded, his missing hand's empty sleeve pinned up neat as parade dress. Two Undercroft runners hovered near the door. And Maren Vale sat on a bench like a woman before a tribunal, spine straight, hands loose, and told us everything.

She told it plainly, which I think was the only reason they let her keep talking.

She told us how she'd been recruited at nineteen, when an Inquisitor caught her touching a accused woman's wrist and reading truth off her skin like heat off a stove—and how instead of burning her, he'd offered her a choice: the pyre, or the grey coat. How she'd learned to bury her gift so deep she could almost pretend it wasn't there, and how the burying had become its own kind of lie, because every suspect she cleared secretly, every report she softened, had made her feel clean while the machine around her grew dirtier.

She told us about Odile.

That was the part where Pip stopped glaring and started listening.

"They sentenced her to burn," Vale said. "Public pyre, Ashmarket square, six days from now. The commander wants a spectacle—he wants the city to see a guild-mother die, wants the guilds to understand that no one is protected." She drew a folded paper from her breast and laid it on the crate between them. "Execution roster. Guard rotations. Pyre construction notes. The yard drains badly; the kindling will be damp; they'll use pitch." Her jaw tightened. "I requested to conduct the final interrogation personally. I told them there was a hidden network she'd only reveal to a sympathetic ear. There isn't. There's nothing left to interrogate. I bought four days, maybe five, and I don't know what I thought I'd do with them except stand between her and the fire for as long as standing works."

"Why?" Pip asked. Her voice cracked on it, young and furious. "Why her? Why stall for *her*, when you've burned—"

Vale didn't flinch. "Because she hummed a lullaby," she said, "that my mother sang to me before the fever took her. A lullaby from a village two hundred leagues north that Odile Vess has never seen. And when I asked her how she knew it, she smiled at me through the bars and said, *child, everyone's mother sang it once, before the Court fell silent. You've just forgotten.*" Vale's hands closed slowly in her lap. "I have spent eleven years telling myself the gifted were the enemy. One afternoon with that woman, and I couldn't remember why."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the deep stone, water dripped, counting seconds none of us wanted counted.

It was Brannoc who broke it, and his voice was gravel dragged over glass.

"The lullaby's real," he said. "My grandmother sang it. Before the suppression. Before I—" He stopped. Started again. "The commander. Halvard Renn. What does he actually want? Not the official want. The real one."

Vale looked at him for a long moment, and I watched her decide to answer honestly, and watched the cost of it move through her face.

"He wants the relic," she said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. I felt every head turn toward me, and I kept my eyes on Vale.

"Corvin reached him nine days ago," she went on. "Told him about the ninefold relic, about the Court, about the bone-carver who carries it—or carried it, or is it, the story changes depending on how much Corvin's had to drink. Renn doesn't believe in sovereigns. He believes in weapons. If the thing under the city can wake the dead, he wants it in Inquisition hands, pointed wherever he points it. The cleansing of the Undercroft isn't a purge, carver. It's a *search*. He thinks you're down here, and he thinks you'll lead him to it if he burns enough of the warren around you."

"And if he gets it," Brannoc said quietly, "he uses it."

"He'll try. And the thing about trying—" Vale's voice went very dry, "—is that according to Corvin, the relic doesn't need his permission anymore. It's waking on its own. Bells in districts that have no churches. Graves found open with nothing taken. My last night in the citadel, a clerk wept in the corridor because she could hear singing inside the walls, and everyone pretended she was mad, and I pretended hardest of all, because I could hear it too."

I felt it then, faint and awful, through the soles of my feet and the bones of my hands: the deep, slow tolling I had hoped was gone. Not the Court speaking. The Court *stirring*. Turning over in its sleep like something vast beneath the floor of the world, dreaming of thrones.

Pip saw my face. "Sable. What is it. What do you hear."

"It's waking up," I said. "Faster than I thought. Faster than it should." I looked at Vale—at the enemy who had walked into the dark carrying her own noose, offering it to us along with everything else. "How long do we have? Before Renn moves on the Undercroft?"

"Six days," she said. "Same day as the pyre. He means to burn us both in one morning—your people at dawn, your mentor at noon." She met my eyes, and there was no apology in her face, because she knew apologies were worthless, but there was something else. Something that had crawled a long way to get here. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to let me help. I know their protocols. Their signals. Which officers will follow orders into a massacre and which are already sick of it. Kill me if you need to afterward—I've written out my confession, it's all in the satchel—but let me be useful first."

Silence again. Then Pip stood up off her crate, knife still in hand, and walked over until she was standing directly in front of the Inquisitor, close enough to touch, looking up at her with those sharp street-quick eyes.

"You stalled for Odile," Pip said. "Four days. Why her and not us? You didn't know us. You'd never even met me, and I'm the most lovable one."

Vale almost smiled. It was a broken sort of smile, but it was real. "Because she asked me a question once, through the bars. She said: *Inquisitor, when you were a girl, and you hid your gift from yourself—did you hide it because you were ashamed of it, or because you were afraid of what they'd make you do with it?*" Vale's throat worked. "I've been answering that question for three weeks. This is the answer."

Pip looked at her a moment longer. Then she sheathed the knife, turned to face the rest of us, and threw her arms wide.

"Well," she said. "We're not leaving Odile in that yard. And we're sure as ashes not waiting for dawn on the sixth day. So somebody start talking fast, because as of right now we've got an ex-Inquisitor, a one-handed soldier, a ledger-runner, and a deafening bone-carver, and that's either a rescue crew or a joke, and I refuse to be a joke."

Brannoc made a sound that in another man might have been a laugh.

It was me they all looked to. Of course it was. And I thought of Odile, somewhere in a cell above us, humming lullabies to the woman sent to break her—turning even her captor, patient as water wearing down stone. I thought of what she'd say if she could see this gallery: *the ledger doesn't care who writes in it, boy, only that the sums come true.*

"We take her in," I said. "Fully. No leash, no second trial. Vale—if you betray us, you don't answer to Brannoc or to me. You answer to Pip, and she's meaner than both of us combined."

"Damn right," said Pip.

Vale bowed her head. When she raised it, her eyes were wet, and she let them be, and I understood that this too was a kind of courage—the letting.

"Then teach me your tunnels," she said. "We have six days."

But that night, lying awake in the third gallery while the others planned around a guttering lamp, I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone floor and felt it again, clearer now, unmistakable:

Nine heartbeats, deep beneath the city, keeping time.

And one of them had begun, very softly, to sing my name.
