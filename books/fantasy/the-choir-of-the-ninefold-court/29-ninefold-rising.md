## 29. Ninefold Rising

The bells began at dawn, and there were no bells.

Sable heard them from the cot in the Undercroft's deepest room, where the smugglers' tunnels met the old cistern and the air tasted of wet stone and tallow. He had been dreaming of Odile—of her hands, ink-stained, turning a page she would never let him read—and the dream tore open like wet vellum when the sound came through.

Not through his ears. Through everything else. Through the bones of his skull, the marrow of his arms, the small carved charm he still wore against his chest out of habit, though it had held no voice since the night he'd fled the guild.

He sat up in the dark. Pip was asleep on the next cot, bandaged shoulder rising and falling, her face slack with the particular exhaustion of someone who'd taken a knife for another person and hadn't yet decided how to feel about it. Brannoc was somewhere above, doing whatever Brannoc did in the hours before light—counting stores, sharpening things, standing watch over a warren full of people who had nowhere else to be.

The bells rang again, and Sable pressed his palms flat against the stone floor, and felt it: a tremor, deep and rhythmic, like a heartbeat under the city's skin.

*No,* he thought. *Not a heartbeat. A counting.*

Nine strokes. Then silence, long enough to doubt. Then nine strokes again.

He knew that rhythm. He had carved it into bone three years ago, working by lamplight in Odile's back room, following a pattern that had come to his hands unbidden—the way the best patterns always did, arriving like something remembered rather than invented. The knucklebone relic. The thing they should have destroyed when Odile first ordered it. The thing Corvin had stolen, sold, and lost to them again in a night market that still smelled of blood.

The thing that was now somewhere in the Undercroft, wrapped in three layers of wool and iron filings, singing loud enough to wake the dead.

Literally, Sable thought, and almost laughed, and could not.

* * *

By the time he climbed to the common hall—a vaulted cellar where forty-odd souls slept in rows like spoons in a drawer—the tremor had stopped being something only he could feel.

A woman named Tess, who mended nets and had never shown so much as a whisper of the gift, stood at the far wall with her head cocked, listening to nothing. An old man who'd spent thirty years hauling cargo was weeping quietly into his hands, saying over and over, "I hear my mother, I hear my mother, she's been dead twenty years." Two children huddled together whispering about music coming up through the floor.

And everywhere, threaded through it all, faint as breath on glass: bells.

"They started an hour ago," Brannoc said. He stood beside the cold hearth, one hand resting on the mantel where his axe leaned, his missing arm's empty sleeve pinned neat and soldierly. His voice was level, but Sable had learned to read the tension underneath it—the way a rope reads before it parts. "First just you gifted folk, I figured. Then the net-mender. Then the children." He turned. "It's your relic, isn't it."

"It's not mine."

"It came from your hands."

"Yes," Sable said. "Three years ago. And it slept until—" He stopped. Until Corvin pried open its wrappings in the dark of the vault. Until greedy fingers handled what should have been left alone. Until it was carried through the Bone Market in a thief's coat, drinking in the noise of ten thousand relics and the crowds that traded them, feeding.

"Until someone woke it," Sable finished.

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. Around them, the hall murmured with fear dressed up as conversation. Somewhere a baby cried, and beneath the cry, faint and terrible, a bell counted nine.

"So what does waking mean?" Brannoc asked. "For us. For the city. Plain words, carver. I've no patience for prophecy this morning."

Sable wished he had plain words. What he had was the Court's own voice, layered like bells, sovereigns of a war that had burned half the world, promising him knowledge and protection and wholeness if only he would open his throat and let them speak through it. What he had was the dead of the Bone Market whispering warnings even now, faint and fraying, that the nine lied about how the war began. What he had was a wound in the world shaped exactly like a knucklebone, and every hour it sang, more people heard it.

"It means it's choosing not to wait for me anymore," Sable said. "I thought—I told myself I had time. That I could decide slowly. Study it. Find the right way to unmake it without—" He gestured vaguely, at the hall, at the sleeping city above, at Pip's bandaged shoulder. "Without cost. But it's done waiting. Every hour it gets louder, and every hour more people can hear it, and eventually the Inquisition hears it too, and then it won't matter what any of us decide, because the commander will have his weapon and his pyres and his cleansing."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Then we stop deciding slowly."

"We go down," Sable said. "Into the war-vault. Where they were first bound. It's the only place the unmaking will hold—I've asked the dead, the ones the Court hasn't frightened into silence. The binding was made there; it can be ended there. Properly. All nine voices, given back to true death."

"And how far down is that?"

"Deeper than the Undercroft. Deeper than anything the smugglers dug. There are ways—old ways. Soldier ways." He met Brannoc's eyes. "Ways you might know."

Something moved behind Brannoc's face, old and ugly and familiar to him as his own hands. The village he'd burned. The song he'd heard rising from the ashes. "Aye," he said finally. "Maybe I do."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone and earth, the ninth bell rang—or the ninth stroke of the count, Sable couldn't tell anymore—and somewhere in the rows of sleepers, a woman began to hum along.

* * *

Pip found him in the workroom an hour later, sitting before the table where the relic lay unwrapped.

He hadn't meant to unwrap it. He had meant only to check the iron filings, to make sure the containment held. But the wool had come away in his hands like it wanted to, and now the knucklebone sat bare in the lamplight, and it was beautiful, and that was the whole horror of it. Nine grains of pale gold suspended in bone like seeds in ice, turning slowly, though nothing touched them. And around them, the voices—not speaking to him anymore, not tempting, not bargaining. Just singing. A choir rehearsing for a coronation.

"You shouldn't be near that," Pip said from the doorway. Her voice was rougher than usual; the knife wound had taken something out of her, or maybe given something. She crossed the room anyway, because Pip had never once in her life stayed where she was told. "Vale's reports said the Inquisition's moving on the Undercroft within days. You're down here having a nice sit with the doom-bone."

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"To what it does when I'm not fighting it." He rubbed his eyes. "That's the trick of it, Pip. For years I thought the gift was hearing the dead. It's not. The gift is *listening*—and most of the time, listening means knowing what something wants before it says it. This—" he nodded at the relic, "—wants to be found. Wants to be carried. Wants hands. Corvin gave it hands. Now it doesn't need me at all."

"That supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make us faster."

Pip dropped onto the stool across from him, wincing, and studied the relic with the flat pragmatic stare she brought to everything—stolen names, impossible ledgers, knives meant for other people. "So the bells. Everyone's hearing them. Even folk with no gift at all."

"Ordinary ears soon enough. Then everyone. The Court isn't calling to the gifted anymore. It's calling to *everyone*. Grief is universal, Pip. Everyone has someone they'd give anything to hear again." He thought of the old cargo-hauler weeping for his mother. "That's the army it's raising. Not soldiers. Mourners. Thousands of them, walking toward the sound, asking it to bring their dead back. And the Court will say yes. It will say yes to all of them, and what comes back wearing their loved ones' voices will be nine thrones wearing masks."

Pip was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its edge. "Odile," she said. "Her last words. Unmake it, not free it."

"Unmake it, not free it," Sable agreed.

"Then why are we still up here?"

Sable looked at her—at this sharp, half-feral kid who'd sold her name to survive and somehow kept her soul—and felt the full weight of what he'd been carrying alone, and how much of it he'd chosen to carry alone, and how angry she'd been about that, and how right she'd been.

"Because I keep thinking there's a version where nobody else bleeds," he said. "And there isn't. There's just versions where less of us does. So. We go down. Tonight. Before the Inquisition seals the upper tunnels, before the bells get loud enough to draw every mourner in the city into the streets between us and the door." He paused. "You're staying here."

Pip laughed—one short, incredulous bark. "Absolutely not."

"Pip—"

"You're going to walk into the place where a war started, carrying the thing that started it, to fight nine dead kings with a carving knife. And you want me to stay *here*?" She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the fever-bright stubbornness in her eyes. "I took a knife for you. That wasn't a loan, Sable. That was a purchase. I bought a seat on this disaster, and I'm going to use it."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue. And then, from the corridor, came the sound of running feet, and Brannoc filled the doorway with the particular set of his shoulders that meant the morning had gotten worse.

"Word from the lookouts," he said. "The streets above are filling. Not soldiers—civilians. Hundreds. Walking toward the Belltower district, all of them, quiet as a funeral. And the Inquisition's mustering at the gates of the Undercroft quarter." He paused. "There's more. The bells aren't counting nine anymore."

Sable went cold. "What are they counting?"

"Ten," Brannoc said. "They're counting ten."

Sable looked down at the relic, at the nine golden grains turning in their prison of bone, and understood, with the certainty of a man watching a door close, that the tenth bell was not a voice from inside the relic.

It was a summons. And it was ringing for him.

* * *

They gathered in the common hall as the light above ground failed—which light, no one could say, because none of them had seen the sky in two days. Vale's stolen plans lay spread across a trestle table, weighted with cups and stones: tunnel maps, garrison rotations, the commander's own hand annotating which sections of the Undercroft to flood first. Beside them, Brannoc had laid out a second set of maps, older, drawn in a soldier's careful hand on paper gone amber with age—maps of the war-vault approaches that no smuggler had ever charted, because no smuggler had ever come back.

Maren Vale stood at the edge of the group, still half-stranger, still half-enemy in Brannoc's eyes, her defector's papers folded in her coat like a confession she hadn't finished making. She had brought the last thing the Inquisition possessed that they needed: the exact hour the assault would begin.

"Dawn tomorrow," she said. "Two hundred picked men, fire teams behind them. The commander intends to burn the Undercroft whether or not he finds anyone in it. The bells have made the city afraid, and fear makes him bold." Her voice was steady. Her hands, Sable noticed, were not. "Whatever you're going to do, you do it tonight."

"Then we do it tonight," Sable said. He looked around the circle: Brannoc with his maps and his buried shame and his soldier's certainty that this time he could burn the right thing instead of the wrong one; Pip with her knife and her purchased seat; Vale with her truth-touch and her mutiny already smoldering in her chest; and beyond them, the faces of forty people who had trusted the Undercroft to keep them safe and were about to learn what it cost when safety ran out.

"The war-vault is three levels below the deepest cistern," Brannoc said, tapping the amber map. "There's one approach the old army used—a siege stair, cut for sappers. It's been sealed since the war. Sealed doors, collapsed sections, and—" his finger stopped on a mark, and his voice changed, "—a garrison post at the third landing. Whatever's left of the men who guarded this way down has been down there a long time."

"The dead stir anyway," Sable said quietly. "The whole city knows it. Those bells aren't just noise—they're a key turning in every lock death ever made. If there are soldiers on that stair, they'll be awake."

"Then we'd best hope they remember whose side they were on," Brannoc said.

Pip snorted. "Dead soldiers. Wonderful. Anyone want to bet they take bribes?"

"They took an oath," Brannoc said, and something in his tone closed the joke like a lid.

Vale stepped forward. "There's one more thing." She drew a folded paper from her coat—Inquisition seal broken, her own handwriting across the bottom. "My final report. I wrote it this morning. It says the Undercroft is empty, the gifted scattered beyond recovery, and the assault unnecessary. My commander read it, called it cowardice, and ordered the assault anyway." She laid it on the table. "But if you succeed—if the bells stop—this becomes evidence. That the danger was ending, and he chose fire regardless. Use it however serves."

Sable picked it up. The paper trembled slightly in his grip, and he realized the trembling wasn't his. It was the floor. The deep, slow, patient tremor of something vast shifting its weight after a thousand years of stillness.

Through the stone, through the marrow of him, the bells rang again—and this time he heard what Brannoc couldn't, what the mourners in the streets couldn't, what even the Court perhaps didn't know he could hear beneath the counting:

His own name.

Nine voices, one throat, calling him down.

Not tempting anymore. Not offering. Simply *calling*, the way a throne calls its heir, the way a debt calls its debtor, the way a grave calls the man who dug it.

Sable set down the report. He picked up his carving tools, wrapped them in oilcloth, and slung them across his back where they had hung through every chapter of this long descent—through guild halls and bone markets and pyre yards and the country of the grieving dead.

"Tonight, then," he said. "All the way down."

And above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the tenth bell rang once more—and this time, everyone in the room heard it.
