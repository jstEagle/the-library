## 30. The Long Way Down

Brannoc had spent twenty years learning every way a city could be hollowed out, and he still hated this one.

The entrance to the war-vault was not a door. It was an absence—a seam in the Undercroft's deepest gallery where the smugglers' maps simply stopped, where the candle flames leaned sideways as if bowing to something below. He'd known it was there since the war. Every soldier who'd fought in the suppression had known there were places beneath the places, and every one of them had learned to walk past those seams without looking down.

He looked down now. The dark looked back with the patience of something that had been waiting a long time and did not mind waiting longer.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," he said.

Sable Galloway stood at the edge of the seam with his carving tools wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a swaddled infant. The bone-carver had lost weight since the market—since Pip took the knife meant for him—and it sat wrong on him, made him look less like a man and more like one of his own unfinished works. "Because the relic is waking whether we want it or not," Sable said. "And because Odile said unmake, not free. And whatever unmaking means, it happens where they were first bound."

"And you know that how?"

"The dead told me." Sable said it flatly, without apology. "The ones in the market walls. They remember being marched down here in chains."

Brannoc grunted. Behind them, up through three levels of tunnels and two of cellars, the bells were still ringing. Not church bells—the city's bells hadn't rung in years, their towers sealed by Inquisition decree—but the deep bells, the ones under the ground, the ones no founder had ever cast. Ordinary people heard them now. Bakers heard them. Children heard them. And the dead were walking in the Warrens, slow and aimless as sleepwalkers, opening doors that had been shut for centuries, and the Inquisition had answered with fire and proclamation: *cleanse the Undercroft, purge the gifted, burn what stirs.*

Three thousand souls hiding in Brannoc's warren. Maybe more. All of them now standing between a crown's pyres and nine dead kings climbing out of their graves.

"So we go down," Brannoc said, "into the hole where the last war started, carrying the thing that starts wars, while soldiers come from above and corpses come from everywhere else."

"That's the shape of it."

"I've had better plans. I once charged a fortified ridge with forty men and a prayer." He rolled his shoulders, felt the old ache in the stump of his right arm where the hand used to be. "We won that one, though. Barely."

Pip came skidding around the corner of the gallery, lantern swinging, breath ragged. The boy's wound was bandaged under his coat—he should have been lying down, and everyone had told him so, and he had ignored everyone with the particular genius of the very young. "They're in the Warrens," Pip gasped. "Inquisition column, maybe sixty, torches and sappers. They're sealing exits as they go. Corvin told them about the Undercroft before he ran off, and now they're—" He stopped, swallowed. "There are people above us, Brannoc. Families. They're bricking people into the tunnels."

Brannoc closed his eye. Twenty years ago he had stood in a village called Ashenmere and watched men do exactly that—not brick, but fire, but the principle was identical: seal the gifted away from the world of the living, call it mercy, call it order, call it anything except what it was. He had held the torch himself. He had told himself for two decades that he'd had no choice, that orders were orders, that the gifted were dangerous, and every year the story had fit worse, like a boot gone tight with rot.

Now here was the same army, the same logic, coming down the same kind of stairs after the same kind of people, and this time Brannoc was on the other side of the door.

"We move now," he said. "All of us. Anyone in the lower galleries comes too or goes up and takes their chances with the pyres. I'm not leaving anyone below me when I'm below."

* * *

The seam opened at a touch of Sable's blood—one drop on old bone, a whisper in a language none of them spoke, and the wall exhaled and became a stairway.

It went down farther than the city had any right to be deep. Brannoc counted steps out of habit, soldier's discipline, and stopped counting at four hundred because the number had stopped meaning anything. The air changed as they descended. It grew older. That was the only word for it—the air tasted of smoke that had finished burning a thousand years ago, of iron and incense and the sweetish reek of mass graves. Their lanterns burned low and blue-edged, and the shadows they threw were wrong, moving a half-beat behind the hands that made them.

Vale walked near the front. She'd been with them six days now, long enough for Brannoc to stop reaching for his knife when she spoke and not nearly long enough to forgive her. She carried her Inquisitor's coat folded under one arm like a shed skin and she kept glancing at the walls with an expression Brannoc recognized from funerals.

"You've been down here before," he said to her. Not a question.

"In training. They showed us the vault mouth and told us it was sealed forever." Her voice echoed strangely, arriving at his ear a moment after her lips moved. "They told us a lot of things."

"They tell soldiers things too." He let that sit between them. "You wrote reports, Vale. Names, locations. My people. You armed the cleansing."

"Yes." No flinch, no excuse. He almost respected it. "And I'm here to stand between your people and the men following my reports. If that's not enough, put me at the rear. I'll be the first thing they meet."

"It's not enough," Brannoc said. "But it'll do for tonight."

Behind them all, faint and getting fainter, came the sound of masonry being broken open far above. The Inquisition had found the seam's upper reaches—or something had shown them. Brannoc didn't let himself think about which of his own people might have traded that knowledge for a pardon. There'd been a Corvin in every company he'd ever served in. There was always one.

Sable stopped so suddenly that Pip walked into his back.

"What is it?" Vale's hand went to her blade.

"He's listening," Pip said quietly. He'd learned that much in a week. "Give him room."

Sable stood with his head tilted, the way a man tilts toward distant music, and Brannoc watched his face go through fear and then something worse than fear—recognition. "The dead of the vault," Sable murmured. "Thousands of them. They're not restless like the ones above. They're *arranged*. Ranks and files. Someone kept them ordered down here, even in death." His eyes found Brannoc's, and Brannoc saw the question in them before it was asked, and hated it.

"You were a soldier in the suppression," Sable said. "What did they tell you about the Ninefold Court? What did they say happened at the end of the war?"

Brannoc's hand—the living one—curled into a fist. "They said the Court was evil. Tyrants, monsters, that the world got lucky when the old kings bound them. They gave us medals and a holiday and told us never to speak of it." He paused. The words came up from somewhere deeper than memory, from the place where Ashenmere lived. "And they told us the binding required a price. A garrison. Living soldiers, stationed underground, generation after generation, to keep the seals fed."

"Fed with what?" Vale asked.

"With themselves," Brannoc said. "That's why my grandfather's father had a brother nobody talked about. That's why whole regiments just—went missing, in the histories. They weren't missing. They were *here*." He looked down the stairwell, into the blue-dark, and understood for the first time what the bells were saying, what the stirring dead wanted, and it was not conquest. It was relief. It was *let us stop*. "Gods. We weren't guarding a prison. We were the prison."

No one spoke for a long moment. The lanterns guttered. Somewhere below, vast and patient, something turned over in its sleep.

* * *

The stair ended at a gate.

Not stone—bone. Femurs and ribs and skulls fused into a lattice wide enough for ten men abreast, and every skull faced outward, watching the approach, and every skull had been a soldier once. Brannoc knew the make of them. Standard-issue helms grown into the bone, rank insignias of regiments struck from the rolls two hundred years before his birth. The garrison. The feeding line. His inheritance.

"Well," Pip said, in the small voice he used when big words had failed him, "that's cheerful."

Sable stepped forward and laid his palm flat against the gate. "They're still in there," he whispered. "Still at their posts. They can't leave until the Court leaves. They're asking—" His voice cracked. "They've been asking for two hundred years, and no one could hear them."

"I can hear them," Vale said softly. Everyone turned. She stood pale in the lantern light, both hands pressed over her ears, though her gift had never needed ears. "Truth. The whole gate is made of truth. It's deafening."

Brannoc put his shoulder to the bone lattice, expecting resistance, and the gate sighed open like an old man granted leave to rest. Beyond lay the war-vault proper, and even Brannoc, who had seen battlefields stacked with dead, stopped breathing for a moment.

A cavern the size of a cathedral district. Nine thrones of black bone arranged in a crescent at its heart, each one taller than a windmill, each one draped in chains thick as ship's cable—and the chains were singing. That was the bells. That had always been the bells. The seals themselves, vibrating with strain, ringing their warning down through the bones of the city for anyone with grace enough or curse enough to hear.

And between the thrones and the gate, filling the floor in perfect ranks, knelt the dead. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Soldiers and prisoners and the nameless fed to the seals across centuries, kneeling in formation, heads bowed, waiting for an order that had never come.

"They're not attacking," Vale breathed. "The dead in the city—they weren't rising to conquer. They were rising because the seals are failing and they can finally *move*."

"Then why does the Inquisition think the world's ending?" Pip asked.

"Because a world built on a lie ends twice," Brannoc said. "Once when the lie breaks, and once when the men who told it start shooting." Far above, muffled by half a mile of stone, he heard the crack of a sapper's charge. The Inquisition was coming down. And behind them, in the tunnels, the sealed families, the bricked doors, the pyres already lit in the Warrens square—he'd heard them from the third landing, smelled the smoke.

Old wounds reopened wasn't the half of it. Old wounds had never closed. They'd just been buried, like everything else, and now the burial ground was waking up and demanding an accounting.

"Sable." He turned to the carver, who stood staring at the nine thrones with his tools clutched to his chest, his face lit blue and terrible. "Whatever you're going to do—do you know how yet?"

"No," Sable said honestly. "But I know where to start. The binding was made by carvers. Bone-work, seal-work, the same craft I was taught." He unwrapped the oilcloth with steady hands. "Odile taught me. Which means Odile's teacher taught her. Which means the craft came *from here*, from whoever bound the Court in the first place. I'm not breaking new ground, Brannoc. I'm finishing someone's work."

"Then finish fast," Brannoc said, "because we have company."

He pointed with his stump, an old habit, up the stairwell they'd descended. Torchlight flickered there now, weak and orange against the blue gloom. Voices. Boot-heels. The Inquisition vanguard, descending into a place their own church had declared sealed forever, led—Brannoc would have bet his remaining hand on it—by a commander who intended to drag the ninefold relic out of the dark and point it at his enemies.

And from the other side of the cavern, from galleries none of their maps had ever charted, came a second sound: the shuffling, patient tread of the risen dead of the outer warrens, drawn home at last to the place they'd been fed into the dark.

Between the two, one lame-handed ex-soldier, one wounded street thief, one defected Inquisitor, and a bone-carver with a set of chisels and the fate of the world in his oilcloth bundle.

"Pip," Brannoc said. "How many forgotten ways does this place have?"

The boy grinned, wild and white in the lantern light. "More than the Inquisition's ever found. Fewer than the dead know. Pick a direction."

"Then pick one that buys the carver time." Brannoc drew his knife and turned to face the torchlight coming down the stairs, and thought of Ashenmere, and thought: *not this time. Not again. This time I hold the door.*
