## 32. The Binding Chamber

The last stair was not a stair.

It had been one once—Sable could see the ghost of it in the stone, the worn hollows where feet had pressed a hundred generations of feet—but the Court's long dreaming had warped it, and now the steps descended in a spiral that turned against itself, so that every dozen paces brought them past their own footprints from moments before. Pip went pale and gripped Brannoc's sleeve. Vale, walking behind with her hands bound loosely in front of her by her own request—"so you'll know I'm not leading you into an ambush," she'd said, and Sable had chosen to believe her—counted under her breath until the numbers stopped meaning anything.

Then the spiral let go of them, and they were through.

The chamber opened like a held breath finally released.

It was vast beyond reason—a cathedral turned inside out, its ribs of black stone arching overhead into darkness no torch reached. The air tasted of cold iron and old incense and something sweeter beneath, something like the smell of a room where someone has just died. Water dripped somewhere far off, each drop landing with the weight of a bell.

And there were the thrones.

Nine of them, arranged in a crescent on a dais of fused bone, facing not outward toward a court of supplicants but inward, toward one another—as if the sovereigns who sat in them had never cared for subjects at all, only for each other's faces. Each throne had been carved from a different bone. A femur broad as a rowboat. A skull the size of a wagon, its crown open like a broken egg. A spine coiled upright, vertebra upon vertebra, still articulated, still *waiting*. They were beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful: with total indifference to what stood beneath them.

Sable's ears filled with bells.

Not the distant ringing he had carried since the Bone Market—the muffled, persistent chime of the relic waking in Corvin's greedy hands. This was close. This was *here*. Nine voices braided into a single chord, and the chord had been waiting for him the way a door waits for a key.

"You hear it," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. The big man stood with his single hand resting on the axe at his belt, his scarred face tilted up toward the thrones, and something in his expression was twenty years younger and infinitely worse. "Gods below. I heard it once before, the night we burned Thornhollow. Thought it was the screaming. It wasn't." He swallowed. "It was this. It was always this."

"The relic," Vale said sharply. "Where is Corvin?"

"Already here," said the dark between the thrones.

Corvin stepped out of it as though the shadow had been holding him, and Sable's stomach dropped. He looked *wrong*—not hurt, not frightened, but polished, like a man who had been scrubbed from the inside. His eyes had a lacquered shine. In his arms he cradled the ninefold relic, and it blazed against his chest with a light that cast no shadows, and he was smiling the way converts smile.

"I was going to sell it," Corvin said conversationally, walking toward the thrones as if giving a tour. "That was the whole plan. Simple. Sensible. But it talks, Sable. Did you know it talks? Not the way you hear things—all your sad little dead people with their complaints and their lullabies. It *reasons*." He turned, and the light from the relic washed his face bone-white. "It made me understand that I've been thinking too small my entire life."

"Put it down," Brannoc rumbled.

"Why?" Corvin spread his arms, relic and all, presenting the chamber like a gift. "Look where we are. Look what's waiting. You think the Inquisition's commander wants to *destroy* this? He wants to hold it up like a lantern and burn the world clean. And you lot want to smash it out of guilt. Both of you are wasting the only miracle anyone will ever be offered." His voice softened into something almost tender. "They're awake now. All nine. They've been listening to us come down the stairs. And they have an offer."

"They always have an offer," Sable said quietly.

His voice came out steadier than he felt. Inside him, everything was shaking. Because he knew this place—not from memory, but from the voices, from years of half-heard fragments leaking out of the relic he'd carved with his own ignorant hands. He knew that the dais had run red once. He knew that the thrones had been carved from the bodies of the Court's own enemies, and that the ninth throne—that one, the smallest, nearly humble, set slightly apart—had been carved from something else entirely.

He walked forward before he decided to walk forward. Brannoc caught at his shoulder; Sable shook him off gently.

"Sable." Vale's voice, low. "Whatever they show you—remember it's a salesroom. Everything in it is staged."

"I know," Sable said. "I'm a tradesman. Let me look at the goods."

He climbed the dais alone.

Up close, the thrones hummed. He could feel it in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his palms, in the fine bones of his inner ear where his whole cursed life lived. The relic in Corvin's arms flared brighter with every step he took, and the ninefold chord resolved, slowly, sweetly, into words.

*Little carver,* said the first voice, deep as a landslide. *You came.*

"I came to unmake you," Sable said.

*Yes,* said a second voice, silken, amused. *They all say that. The founder said it too, standing exactly where you stand. He wept while he said it. Do you know why he wept?*

"Because you lied to him."

*Because we told him the truth,* said a third voice, a woman's voice, warm as a hearth, *and the truth was heavier than any lie we might have forged.* A pause, delicate as a knife laid down on silk. *But you didn't come down those terrible stairs for truth or lies. Shall we stop pretending, little carver? You came because of the old woman.*

The chamber changed.

There was no transition—one heartbeat the black vault, the next a kitchen Sable knew better than his own hands. Odile Vess's kitchen above the guild, with its crooked chimney and its kettle that whistled flat and her handwriting covering every surface in notes to herself she'd never read again. And there she was, at the table, bent over a ledger, her spectacles pushed up into her gray hair, muttering at the columns the way she always muttered, and when she looked up and saw him her whole face did the thing it did—surprise folding into exasperation folding into a warmth she'd have denied under torture.

"There you are," Odile said. "You're late. Sit down before the tea goes bitter."

Behind him, he heard Pip make a small wounded sound. He heard Brannoc swear. He heard Vale say, very evenly, "It isn't real. None of you look at it. That's how it works—it shows you the door you can't help walking through."

Sable didn't turn around. He looked at Odile—at the ink stain on her left knuckle that she'd had for thirty years, at the way her spectacles sat crooked, at the exact cadence of her voice—and he made himself do the thing he had done ten thousand times across a workbench: listen past the surface.

The kitchen had no smell.

Every detail was perfect except that. No tallow, no tea, no woodsmoke, no dust. A kitchen without smell was a painting of a kitchen. A woman without a scent was—

"She's dead," Sable said aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle, and he hated himself for the crack, and he kept talking anyway. "You took her death out of the world and you're wearing it like a coat. Get out of her."

Odile sighed, and the kitchen sighed with her, walls flexing like lungs, and then she was gone and the chamber was back, and the nine voices laughed together—a sound like ice calving off a glacier, like applause in an empty theater.

*Good,* said the deep voice, and there was genuine approval in it. *Strong. The founder broke faster. But we are not cruel, little carver. We never offer counterfeits to those who can tell the difference. So let us offer you the true thing instead.*

The thrones began to glow.

Light rose out of the bone like heat shimmer, and the chamber filled with visions—not illusions this time, or not only. Sable felt the difference in his bones. These were memories, real ones, poured out of the thrones themselves: the Ninefold Court at the height of its power, nine sovereigns standing over a world map drawn on the flayed skin of a mountain, and the world was *whole*. No burned half. No Ashlands where the old kingdoms had been. Cities with spires of green glass. Rivers running clear. The gifted walking openly in the streets, honored, crowned, *safe*.

*This was ours,* the voices sang together. *This can be yours again. Give us passage. Be our vessel—our throat, our hands, our door back into the world—and we will undo it. Every pyre. Every purge. Every year of hiding. We will reach back along the roots of time and unburn the half of the world your crowns destroyed.*

"And the price?" Sable asked. His mouth was dry. The vision was gorgeous. That was the horror of it—it wasn't a cheap trick, it was a *masterwork*, and some traitor part of him wanted to live inside it forever.

*Your body, freely given,* said the hearth-warm voice. *We ask nothing we don't name plainly. You would not suffer. You would watch. You would sit beside us in the choir and sing the world right.*

"And me?" Sable said. "What happens to *me*?"

Silence, brief, honest.

*You would be more than you are,* the voices said, which was not an answer, and they knew it was not an answer, and the knowing rang through the chamber like a struck bell.

"That's what I thought." Sable turned away from the vision. It clung to him—he felt it pulling at the back of his skull like a hook in cloth—and he forced his eyes down to the floor, to the plain black stone, and he thought about ledgers. Columns. Numbers that balanced. *Everything has a cost recorded somewhere. Find the line item.*

He found it.

Because the visions kept flowing, and the Court, drunk on its own generosity, showed him the rest: Odile alive—truly alive, rewound, the pyre unlit, her heart beating again in a world remade. Pip's wound never dealt. Brannoc's hand restored, his village unburned, the thousand ghosts of Thornhollow released from his memory like birds. Vale's mother, humming her lullaby, alive and singing. Everyone healed. Everyone saved. Everyone *owed*.

And underneath it, faint as watermarks, Sable saw the other ledger. The one written small. The millions who lived in the remade world only because the Court willed it—who would live at their pleasure, in their image, subject to their mercy, forever. A world saved the way a man saves a coin: by owning it.

"No," Sable said.

The visions flickered.

"Little carver—" the deep voice began, gentler now, gentler than anything that size had any right to be.

"No." Louder. His voice bounced off the ribbed dark. "I've kept ledgers for fifteen years. I know a debt when it's dressed as a gift. You don't want a vessel—you want a *hostage*. You want the world back, and you want us grateful, and gratitude is just a softer word for chains." He looked up at the thrones, all nine, and his fear fell away from him all at once, the way a coat falls when you finally stop shrugging to keep it on. "You started the war. I've talked to your soldiers. I've heard your victims. The dead remember what the thrones forgot to mention. You burned the world first, and then you wrote yourselves as the fire brigade."

The chamber went very quiet.

Even Corvin's smile had frozen on his face. He was looking at Sable with something like panic, the polish cracking, because he had staked everything on the offer being irresistible and here was a bone-carver refusing paradise in front of witnesses.

*Sable.* The hearth-warm voice again, and now the warmth was gone from it, and what remained was older and colder and far more honest. *Consider what you refuse. The girl bleeds even now in the Undercroft. The old woman is ash in a pit. Your ears fill with silence a little more each day—you felt it on the stairs, didn't you? The quiet growing in you like frost. We can restore all of it. We are the only ones who can. Refuse us, and you choose the ash. You choose the empty chair by the kettle. You choose to grow deaf in a world that never says thank you.*

"I know," Sable said softly.

And he found—to his own surprise, standing in the house of the oldest evil in the world—that he meant it kindly. That was the worst discovery of his life: standing in front of nine ancient tyrants and feeling sorry for them. Because he had listened to the dead his whole life, and he knew what they sounded like when they were afraid, and these nine, for all their thrones and their visions and their gorgeous lying light, sounded exactly like every ghost he'd ever laid to rest.

Terrified of the quiet.

Terrified of ending.

"You were people once," he said. "Before the thrones. Before the war. Someone carved your names somewhere, and someone grieved you, and then you couldn't bear to be grieved, so you made yourselves into this." He stepped closer to the nearest throne, the femur one, and laid his palm flat against the cold bone, and the whole chamber shuddered. "I'm a carver. I know what happens to bone when you try to make it hold more than it was made to hold. It cracks. You've been cracking for a thousand years. Let me finish the job gently."

*Never,* said all nine voices at once, and the tenderness burned away, and what looked out of the thrones then was the thing Brannoc had seen over the burning village, the thing the founder had died fleeing, the thing the Inquisition had been built—in its rotten, twisted way—to fight:

Hunger.

The light in the chamber turned the color of an infected wound. The thrones rocked on their dais. Somewhere above them, impossibly far above, the city itself groaned, and Sable knew that outside, in the streets, the dead were rising from their shallow graves and ordinary people were dropping to their knees in the markets, hands over their ears, hearing bells that weren't there.

The Court was done asking.

"It's waking fully," Vale shouted, and her bound hands were already working at the knots. "Sable, whatever you're going to do—"

"I need my tools," Sable said.

Brannoc was already shrugging off his pack. He'd carried them the whole way down without being asked—the roll of oiled leather, the carving knives, the little hooked burin Sable had used since he was twelve years old. He tossed them across the dais, and Sable caught them against his chest, and the leather was warm from riding against the big man's back, and that small human warmth in that enormous cold place nearly undid him.

*You cannot unmake us,* the Court said, and for the first time there was something new in the ninefold voice, and Sable recognized it with a strange, calm sorrow.

Fear.

*We are bound into the root of the world. The founder tried. Armies tried. Gods tried. What will you carve us with, little carver? What do you think your knives are against—*

"Not my knives," Sable said, kneeling at the base of the ninth throne—the small one, the humble one, the one set apart—and unfolding the leather roll with steady hands. "My hearing."

He pressed the burin's tip to the bone, closed his eyes, and reached for the first voice.

It fought him. Of course it fought him—nine ancient wills slamming against his mind like a sea against a seawall, and blood ran warm from his nose, and behind him he heard Pip screaming his name and Brannoc roaring and steel clearing a scabbard as Corvin, betrayed by his gods' fear, made his own desperate choice. But Sable had spent his whole life doing one impossible thing: listening politely to the dead while they screamed, and raged, and begged, and lied, and never once letting them take the wheel.

He found the first sovereign's true name in the deepest layer of the song, the name from before the thrones, the name some grieving hand had carved a thousand years ago.

And he began, carefully, lovingly, to cut it free.

The first bell shattered.

The sound it made was not a sound—it was the *end* of a sound, a silence with edges, and the chamber lurched, and one throne of ancient bone split from crown to base with a crack that Sable felt in his own teeth, in his own ears, in the place where his hearing lived.

Eight voices screamed as one.

Sable spat blood, adjusted his grip on the burin, and reached for the second.
