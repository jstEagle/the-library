## 33. What Sable Chooses

The thrones were waiting.

Nine of them, arranged in a crescent around the heart of the war-vault, carved from bone so old it had gone the color of candlewax. They had been waiting a long time—Sable could feel that in his teeth, in the marrow of him, the way you can feel a held breath. The Court had sat in this chamber when the world was younger and crueler, and they had left their shapes behind like a hand pressed into wax, and now the wax was softening, and they wanted to press again.

He stood at the center of the crescent with his carving tools spread on a cloth at his feet, and above him the ninefold relic turned slowly in the air, turning without being touched, singing without sound.

*Say yes,* said the Voice of the First Throne, and it was warm as bread, warm as Odile's kitchen had been on winter mornings before everything burned. *Say yes, little carver, and the woman who died for you will not have died.*

Behind him, he heard Brannoc's boots shift on the stone. Pip's breath, quick and shallow. Vale somewhere near the stair, her hand no doubt resting on a weapon she was no longer sure she had the right to draw. His family, such as it was, gathered in the dark to watch him decide.

*You are grieving,* the Court said, all nine voices braided into one gentle chord. *Grief is a door. We are only asking to walk through it.*

"No," Sable said.

The word came out small in that vast chamber. He almost laughed at how small. He had rehearsed it for three days—for the whole descent, really, for the whole terrible year—and it still came out sounding like a boy's answer, flat and stubborn and afraid.

But he said it again. "No."

The turning of the relic slowed.

*Consider what you refuse,* said the Second Voice, and this one was cold, and honest in its coldness, and Sable found he liked it better than the warmth. The warmth had always been the lie. *We offer you the dead woman breathing. We offer you the boy whole, the knife-wound unwritten. We offer you your ears full of song until the end of your days. And you say* no *as though we offered you ash.*

"You did offer me ash," Sable said. "Just dressed up."

He crouched and picked up his smallest knife—the one he'd used for mourning charms, for the fine work inside the ear-bone of a sparrow, the one Odile had given him with the handle worn smooth by her own grip before it was ever his. The steel caught the light of Brannoc's lantern and threw it back thin and bright.

"I've spent my whole life listening to the dead," he said. "Do you know what I learned? Not wisdom. Not secrets worth the having. I learned that the dead want what they wanted when they lived—to keep going, to keep holding, to keep their hands closed around things that were never theirs to hold." He looked up at the turning relic, at the nine lights coiling within it like glowworms in a jar. "You were sovereigns. You're still acting like it. Even bound, even buried, even sung down to a whisper—you can't stop reaching for thrones."

*We kept the peace,* said the Third Voice.

"You started the war." His voice didn't shake. That surprised him. "I heard them, in the market. The dead who remember. Not your version—the true one. You burned half the world because the world stopped kneeling. And now you're offering to unburn it if I'll just be a good vessel and hold you while you do it all again." He shook his head slowly. "I've read enough ledgers to know a bad bargain. This is the worst one ever written."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of nine ancient minds recalculating, and Sable felt it press against him like deep water.

Then the warmth came back, and this time there was something underneath it, something with teeth.

*Then we will take.*

* * *

The chamber changed.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next—the bone thrones flaring with pale light, the walls shedding dust in long curtains, the air filling with bells. Not the sweet bells of the Court's earlier music but the war-bells, the ones from the visions, tolling over burning fields, and beneath them the voices of the dead rose like floodwater: soldiers and farmers and children, the ten thousand thousand dead of the Ninefold War, all of them pulled up out of their graves by the relic's waking, all of them howling through stone and earth toward the chamber where their old masters stood on the edge of return.

"They're coming," Pip said, backing toward the wall. "Sable, they're *coming*, I can hear them through the rock—"

"Then I'd better work fast."

Sable set the relic down on the floor at the center of the crescent, and it fought him. It thrashed against his hands like a landed fish, light bursting from its seams, and the voices poured over him—not persuasion now, not bargains, but raw assault, every grief he'd ever carried dragged up and played at full volume. Odile's laugh. His mother's humming, which he hadn't remembered in twenty years and which broke something in him just to hear. Pip crying in an alley at seven years old, selling his name to a stranger for a bowl of soup.

*You cannot carve us,* the Court said. *We were bound by nine archmages working a year and a day. What are you? A clerk with knives.*

"A clerk," Sable agreed, bracing the relic against his knee, "who keeps very good records."

And he set his knife to the first seam, and began to unmake them.

* * *

It was nothing like carving.

Carving was conversation. Carving was listening to what the material wanted and helping it become itself. This was the opposite of everything he had ever done with his hands: he was listening to what the relic desperately did not want, and forcing it there.

The first seam opened with a sound like a sigh.

And out of it came a voice—one voice alone, unbraided from the nine for the first time in centuries—and Sable knew it instantly, because he had heard it in every vision, in every temptation: the First Sovereign, the eldest, the one whose warmth had been the lure. It rose out of the crack like smoke from a snuffed candle, and for a moment it stood in the air before him, made of light and grievance, wearing the shape it had worn in life: a tall figure crowned with a circlet of knucklebones.

*Wait,* it said. And the majesty was gone from it. What remained was only old, and tired, and terribly unwilling. *Wait. I felt the sun. For one moment, through the boy's dreams—I felt the sun on my face again after a thousand years under the stone. Do you know what you take from me?*

"Yes," Sable said. His hands were steady. He marveled at that. "I know exactly what I'm taking. I've listened to the dead my whole life. Every one of them wanted more time. None of them got to have it. That's what makes them the dead." He lifted the knife. "You had your reign. It's someone else's morning now."

*It isn't fair.*

"No," Sable said gently. "It isn't. It never was. Not for any of us."

He cut.

The seam parted fully, and the First Sovereign came apart like fog in wind, like a knot untied, like a note allowed at last to finish fading. And where it had been, there was—nothing. True nothing. Not the hungry nothing of the grave, not the waiting nothing of the bound, but the clean nothing of an ending accepted. Sable felt it go the way you feel a splinter leave a wound: a small sharp pain, and then an ease so profound it was nearly joy.

Somewhere far above, through a mile of stone, he felt a bell stop ringing.

"One," he said aloud.

*Sable.* Brannoc's voice, rough at the edge. The ex-soldier had planted himself at the mouth of the stair with his single hand wrapped around a hatchet, facing the darkness where the dead of the city were pouring downward. "Whatever you're doing—"

"Keep doing it," Sable said. "Don't let anything reach me. I need maybe—" He touched the second seam, gauged the resistance, the way you gauge a joint before you break it down. "I need as long as it takes."

"Comforting," Pip muttered, and took up position beside Brannoc with a market-knife in her fist, and Vale moved to stand with them, and Sable stopped hearing them after that, because the second voice was rising out of the second seam, and it was screaming.

* * *

They did not go gently, one by one. That was the thing no one would ever write correctly in the histories, if anyone wrote histories of this night at all. The songs would say the Ninefold Court passed into death with dignity, that the last of the old sovereigns bowed their heads to the carver's blade. But Sable was there, and he would remember truly: they screamed, they begged, they threatened, they wept, they lied right up until the moment the lie stopped mattering.

The Fourth Sovereign offered him the secret of unaging flesh. The Fifth told him Odile's soul was not yet scattered—that it lingered, that it could be called back, that only he had the craft to do it—and Sable's hands went still for three full seconds, three seconds that cost him blood, because the relic bit him when he hesitated, biting like a trapped animal, and the pain brought him back to himself.

"Liar," he whispered. "She's gone where you can't follow. Thank the gods."

The Sixth Sovereign tried a different door entirely. It wore Vale's mother's face, and sang the lullaby, and Sable heard Vale make a sound behind him like a wire snapping, and then he heard her say, clear and cold across the chamber: "That's not her. My mother never once asked me for anything. Whatever you are, you don't even understand love well enough to counterfeit it."

The Sixth Sovereign faltered. And in its faltering, Sable found the seam, and cut.

Voice by voice. Seam by seam. His knees ached against the stone. His fingers were slicked red—the relic had grown teeth along its fractures, or perhaps it had always had them—but he worked the way Odile had taught him to work, slow and certain, each cut considered, each cut final. *Never rush the finishing,* she used to say, standing over his shoulder in the guild workshop, smelling of ink and pipe smoke. *The last pass of the blade is the one they'll see forever.*

The seventh voice went out quietly, almost gratefully, whispering *thank you* in a language dead a thousand years, and Sable chose to believe it.

The eighth fought hardest of all. It tore free of the relic's body as a shape of light and stood over him, vast, crowned, terrible, and for one moment the whole chamber filled with the vision it wanted him to see: the world remade, the gifted honored, the pyres cold, Odile alive and laughing, Pip dancing on a table, every wound of the last year unwritten, undone, forgotten—

"It's beautiful," Sable admitted. His voice cracked. "That's the cruelest thing about you. Everything you offer is beautiful."

*Then take it.*

"I can't. Because it's built on top of everyone else's grave, and I'd be the one keeping the ledger." He raised the knife. "I'd rather be deaf and honest than hear everything and own nothing."

He cut, and the Eighth Sovereign shattered into motes, and the motes went out, and the chamber rang with a silence deeper than any silence Sable had ever known.

One voice remained.

* * *

The Ninth did not scream. The Ninth did not bargain. When the last seam lay open before Sable's knife, the last sovereign simply gathered itself at the mouth of the fracture—smaller than the others, dimmer, worn down by centuries of binding into something almost humble—and looked at him with what might have been curiosity.

*You could have been one of us,* it said.

"No," Sable said. "I couldn't. That was always the difference. You thought power was the point. I think it's just a tool, and tools get put away."

*We were afraid,* the Ninth said. And it sounded, suddenly, like exactly what it was: the last ember of a person, a thousand years dead, afraid of the dark it was about to enter alone. *Is that so unforgivable? To be afraid of ending?*

Sable lowered his knife a fraction. He thought of every ghost he had ever soothed, every widow's charm, every murmured comfort passed from the living to the dead and back again. He thought of the dead man in the mourning charm, years ago, complaining about the weather in the churchyard, wanting only to be heard a little longer.

"No," he said. "It's the most forgivable thing there is. But fear doesn't get to rule. Not yours. Not mine." He rested the blade against the final seam, tenderly, the way you touch a closing eye. "Go on. The sun's still up there. Someone else is living in the morning now, and it's a good morning. I promise you. I keep good records."

The Ninth Sovereign was quiet a long moment.

*Yes,* it said at last. *All right.*

And it let him cut.

The last seam parted without resistance, and the ninth voice faded—not torn away but released, a note finally permitted to resolve—and the ninefold relic came apart in Sable's hands like a loaf broken at supper, crumbling into bone-dust and ordinary fragments, into nothing older than yesterday. The light went out of it. The weight went out of it. What remained in his palms weighed less than a sparrow's skull.

And the silence came down.

It came down through a mile of stone, through the roots of the city, through the Undercroft and the Bone Market and the pyre yard and the palace itself—a silence like snowfall, like a fever breaking, like ten thousand held breaths released at once. Sable felt the dead of the city settle. Not vanish—they were still there, the way the departed are always there in the grain of a place—but *settled*, lying down at last after a week of being dragged upright by the Court's waking, grateful, murmuring their thanks in voices already going soft, going home, going quiet.

*Thank you,* said a soldier who had died eight hundred years ago.

*Thank you,* said a child of the plague years.

*Thank you,* said the dead of the Bone Market, and the words washed over Sable like warm water, and he realized his face was wet, and realized he was laughing, or sobbing, or both.

"Sable?" Pip's voice, very small. "Is it—is it done?"

Sable looked down at his hands. The dust of the relic was already sifting away between his fingers, and with it, he understood, with a calm that felt like standing at the edge of a high place, went everything else. The murmur of the dead, which had been the weather of his life since childhood, was receding like a tide going out. He could feel it going—feel the last whispers thinning, gentling, and then, one by one, ceasing altogether, until the world was a place with only one kind of voice in it.

The living. Just the living.

"It's done," he said. And then, because his family was staring at him, and because Odile would have wanted the ledger balanced: "It's done. They're gone. All nine. Truly gone."

Brannoc let out a breath that seemed to come up from his boots. Vale sank against the wall, both hands over her face. Pip crossed the chamber at a run and dropped to her knees beside him and grabbed his shoulders and shook him, hard, shouting something—*you idiot, you absolute idiot, don't you ever*—and Sable smiled at her and meant to answer.

But the chamber tilted.

The lantern-light smeared sideways. The stone came up to meet him, patient and enormous, and the last thing he registered—faintly, absurdly, from a great distance—was the quality of the silence inside his own skull. Total. Unbroken. Deaf as the grave and twice as peaceful.

His hearing was gone. And so, he thought, drifting down into the dark, was quite possibly everything else—

Above them, faint even through a mile of rock, the pyre-yard bells began to ring.
