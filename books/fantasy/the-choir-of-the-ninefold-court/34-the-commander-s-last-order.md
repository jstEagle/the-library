## 34. The Commander's Last Order

The pyre yard smelled of pitch and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Maren Vale stood at the edge of the scaffold platform with her hands folded behind her back the way she'd been taught in her first year of service—hands hidden, spine straight, face a wall. Below her, the Undercroft's captured spilled across the packed earth in rows: bone-singers and charm-cutters, a woman who could find water with her palms, two children whose only crime was a grandmother who'd whispered to moths. The Inquisition had taken four hundred souls from the warrens in three days. Four hundred. She had signed the transport orders herself.

*My reports armed this,* she thought, and the thought had teeth now, had been chewing on her for a week.

Commander Aldous Renk stood at the rail above her, a broad man gone soft at the edges, his gray uniform immaculate despite the ash drifting from the unlit pyres. Nine stacks of kindling and oil-soaked timber ringed the yard like a crown of thorns laid flat. He had wanted more. He had wanted the whole city watching.

"You look pale, Vale," he said without turning. "Eat something."

"I ate, Commander."

"Liar." He said it pleasantly, the way a man comments on weather. "You've been lying to me for weeks. Small lies. Omissions. I let them go because your conviction numbers were good." He turned then, and his eyes were pale as river ice. "But this morning I read your interrogation transcripts again. All of them. And do you know what I noticed?"

Maren kept her face still. Beneath her folded hands, her fingertips pressed against each other, and she felt the old hum rise in them—the truth-touch, the gift she had buried so deep for eleven years that sometimes she convinced herself it was dead.

*Don't,* she told herself. *Not here. Not now.*

"The Vess woman," Renk continued. "Dead before we got anything useful. The bone-carver escaped a locked vault through a door that doesn't exist. Your informants keep dying or going quiet. And every suspect you cleared—" he smiled, thin as a knife's edge, "—every single one you personally interviewed walked free. Curious arithmetic, Vale."

"Careful work isn't a crime."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But it's about to become irrelevant." He gestured at the yard, at the prisoners, at the nine cold pyres. "The relic is in our possession. Corvin delivered it at dawn."

Maren's stomach dropped. *Corvin.* The guild traitor, the man with ink-stained cuffs and a merchant's smile, who had sold out everyone who'd ever fed him. She had seen him once through a doorway, wringing his hands, asking when he'd be paid.

"It's waking," Renk said, and for the first time something moved behind his eyes—not fear. Appetite. "Our artificers say it sings. Says it contains nine sovereigns of the old Court, and that whoever holds it can command the dead." He spread his hands. "Do you understand what that means? No more hunts. No more ledgers, no more interrogations, no more rooting through cellars for hedge-witches. One instrument, and every gifted soul in the world becomes a servant of the crown. Or ash. Their choice."

"That's not how the reports described it," Maren said carefully. "The relic resists handling. It burned two of Corvin's men."

"And it will burn a hundred more if it must, until we learn its language." Renk stepped closer, lowering his voice to something almost confiding. "You've been useful, Vale. More than useful. Which is why I'm telling you plainly: the order comes from the Archon's own seal. Tonight, the pyres light. All of them. The Undercroft dies as an example, the relic wakes as a weapon, and the age of hunting ends because there'll be nothing left to hunt but what serves us."

*There it is.* The thing she had felt circling for days, the shape beneath the paperwork. She thought of Odile Vess in her cell, humming a lullaby that no living person should have known—a lullaby Maren's mother had sung before the fever took her, before Maren had learned to stop hearing the truth in people's skin. She thought of the bone-carver Galloway standing in the rain outside the guild, saying *they were tyrants, they started the war*, and her gift confirming it down to the marrow.

She thought of the falsified report in her own hand, the innocent dyer's apprentice she'd marked *cleansed* instead of *condemned*, and how small that sin looked now beside the four hundred below her.

"When?" she asked.

"Dusk. Two hours." Renk checked a pocket watch he did not need; the sun told the time fine. "I want you on the platform. The troops respect you. When they see you standing witness, there'll be no hesitation."

"Yes, Commander," Maren said.

He studied her a moment too long. Then he nodded and turned back to the rail, and Maren walked down the platform stairs with her legs steady and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

* * *

She found him in the records tent, hunched over the manifest, and she did not knock.

Sergeant Ivo Kess was twenty-six years old, earnest, devout, and the only Inquisitor under her command whose reports she had never once needed to verify. He looked up, saw her face, and set down his pen.

"They're lighting tonight," he said. Not a question. Word traveled through the camp faster than orders did.

"Yes."

"Four hundred people." His jaw worked. "Half of them are children, Vale. There's a boy in cage nine who draws pictures in the dirt. Little houses. He showed me one yesterday. He asked if I had a house."

Maren closed the tent flap behind her. For a long moment she just breathed, tasting canvas dust and lamp oil, gathering the words she had never said aloud to anyone.

"Ivo. What I'm about to tell you will end both our lives or save four hundred. I need you to decide which before I finish speaking."

He went very still. "Tell me."

"My mother was a truth-hearer. Village wise-woman, two counties over. People brought her disputes and she'd hold their wrists and know who lied. The Inquisition came when I was nine. They didn't burn her—they were subtler then. They took her for questioning and returned her wrong. Empty. She died within the year, and I grew up learning to hide the same thing she died of." Maren held up her right hand, palm out. "I have the gift, Ivo. I've used it on every interrogation I've ever run. That's why my numbers are clean. That's why your commander trusts me. And that's why I know—*know*—that the bone-carver told the truth about the relic. The nine sovereigns were tyrants. They began the war to keep their thrones. And whatever Renk thinks he's unleashing tonight, it will not serve the crown. It will serve itself."

Kess stared at her hand as though it might bite. Then, slowly, he looked up at her face, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd seen a hundred suspects do: the cost of believing her against the cost of calling her a liar.

"You're confessing," he said. "To me. Why?"

"Because in two hours I'm going to stand in front of the assembled Inquisition and refuse an order sealed by the Archon, and I will not survive that alone. I need witnesses who knew me before. I need someone to remember that I was thorough. That I was fair." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated it and let it happen. "That's all any of us get, in the end. To be remembered fairly."

Kess was quiet for a long time. Outside, a soldier laughed at some joke; somewhere a horse stamped; the ordinary sounds of an army preparing to murder children.

"The boy in cage nine," Kess said finally. "If I help you, can you get him out?"

"I can try."

"Then I'm yours." He stood, and squared his uniform, and looked suddenly much older than twenty-six. "What do you need?"

"A miracle," Maren said. "Or failing that, a distraction."

* * *

Dusk came down purple and cold, and the pyre yard filled with torchlight.

Renk had assembled the full garrison: two hundred Inquisitors in ranks, halberds grounded, faces lit orange from below. The prisoners had been bound to stakes already—she hadn't known that, hadn't been able to stop it—and the sight of them, four hundred small flames waiting to be lit, made something in Maren's chest go quiet and hard as river stone.

She took her place on the platform at Renk's left hand. Sergeant Kess stood among the rank officers, third row, where she could see the edge of his shoulder. In his sleeve, folded small enough to hide in a palm, was the paper she'd given him at noon: a writ of transfer, forged in the Commander's own cipher, ordering forty prisoners—priorities of interrogation—to be moved to the citadel cells before the burning. Forty. It wasn't enough. It would have to be a beginning.

Renk raised his arms.

"Sons and daughters of the Inquisition!" His voice carried beautifully; he'd practiced. "For a thousand years we have hunted the corruption that hides among us! Tonight, the hunt ends—not in retreat, but in triumph! The relic of the Ninefold Court rests in our keeping, and by dawn, the power of the old tyrants will be the power of the crown!" He drew a torch from the brazier beside him. Firelight crawled up his face and made it monstrous and joyful. "Light the pyres!"

Two hundred voices roared assent. Torches dipped toward the nearest stacks.

"No."

The word came out of Maren before she had decided to speak it—no, that was a lie, she had decided it in the records tent at noon, in the rain outside the guild weeks ago, perhaps in her mother's kitchen at nine years old—but it still surprised her, the way a bone breaks surprising the body that carried it so long.

The yard went silent. Two hundred heads turned. Renk lowered his torch slowly and looked at her with something between amusement and pity.

"Inquisitor Vale. Do you have something to add?"

"I have evidence," Maren said, and her voice rang clear across the yard, and she marveled distantly at how steady it was, how the fear had burned away and left only the truth underneath, which was, after all, the only thing she'd ever been able to touch cleanly. "Evidence that the relic you intend to wield is not a weapon of the crown. Evidence that the prisoners below us include children whose only gift is inherited grief. Evidence that this order"—she lifted the Archon's decree, the one she'd asked to examine at midday and never returned—"was sealed on intelligence I falsified. By me. Because I could not bring myself to write the truth: that the woman I interrogated was innocent, and I knew it, and I hid it."

A murmur ran through the ranks like wind through wheat.

"Vale." Renk's voice dropped into the register he used for broken subordinates. "You are ill. Step down. We'll speak after—"

"I am not ill." She stepped forward to the platform's edge, where every eye in the yard could reach her. "I am a truth-hearer. I have been since birth. My mother was one too, and your Inquisition hollowed her out and called it mercy. Everything I have done in eleven years of service, I did hiding inside the machine that killed her, telling myself I could soften it from within." She laughed, short and bitter. "Tonight I stopped lying to myself. So hear me, all of you: I have touched the skin of every prisoner in this yard. I know their truths. And I am telling you that Commander Renk's great weapon is nine dead tyrants wearing the mask of salvation, and that the first thing it will devour is the hand that holds it."

"Seize her," Renk said quietly.

No one moved.

Maren saw why in the faces of the front rank—young Ivo Kess among them, his hand resting on the shoulder of the soldier beside him, and beyond them, spreading outward like frost across a window, the particular stillness of people who had spent years burning strangers and had never once been asked to look at what they burned. A woman in the third row had begun, very softly, to cry.

"Seize her!" Renk shouted, and thrust his torch toward the nearest pyre. "Light it! Light them all!"

The torch-bearer at stack one looked at the stake, at the old man bound to it—a charm-cutter, Maren knew, whose gift was making bread rise—and did not move.

And then the bells began.

Not the cathedral bells. Something deeper, older, rising out of the ground itself, out of the relic in its iron case in the Commander's tent, out of every grave and bone-heap and forgotten battlefield within ten miles of the city. The sound rolled across the pyre yard and every torch guttered, and Maren felt the truth-touch flare in her fingers without being summoned, screaming at her: *true, true, all of it true, the dead are waking, the ninth hour is come.*

In the sudden dark and ringing air, Renk stood alone on his platform with a torch that would not stay lit, shouting orders to an army that had stopped listening.

Maren Vale stepped off the platform, walked to the nearest stake, and drew her belt-knife to cut the first prisoner free.

Behind her, she heard Kess's voice, clear and carrying, giving the counter-order in the Commander's own cipher: *"Transfer detail! Forty to the citadel—move!"*

And beneath everything, beneath the bells and the weeping and the crackle of torches refusing flame, she heard—or imagined she heard, though her gift swore otherwise—a voice like dry leaves, amused and ancient, saying:

*Too late, little truth-teller. But thank you for opening the door.*

The bells swelled toward midnight, and the ground beneath the pyre yard began, very gently, to breathe.
