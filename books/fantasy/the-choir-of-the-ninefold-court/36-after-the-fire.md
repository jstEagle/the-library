## 36. After the Fire

The smoke took four days to leave the city, and Brannoc spent every one of them counting what was left.

He did it the way he'd once counted a company after a bad engagement—by rote, by lamplight, by the names on his tongue. Forty-one souls sleeping in the Undercroft's long gallery. Nine too hurt to walk unaided. Three dead: Marla of the tallow-candles, who had sung to her wicks; old Tam, who had never been gifted at all and had simply refused to leave; and a child whose name nobody knew, found at the mouth of the smugglers' stair with soot in her lungs and no one claiming her.

Brannoc wrote all of it down in a ledger he'd taken from the ruins of the Vellum Guild, because someone had to, and because writing kept his one hand busy enough that it couldn't shake.

The pyres had never been lit. That was the miracle everyone kept tripping over. The commander had given the order—Brannoc had heard it secondhand, third-hand, from a dozen mouths by now—and Inquisitor Maren Vale had stood up in front of the assembled Order and said *no* in a voice that carried to the back of the yard, and then she had shown them why she had the right to say it. She had touched the commander's own clerk, there before everyone, and spoken aloud what lived in the man's heart: that he had forged half the evidence in the Gift-Hunt's ledgers, and that he wept at night for his sister, whom he had denounced himself to keep his post.

After that, the Inquisition had not fractured so much as shattered, like a dropped plate. Half the Order had arrested the other half. The commander had been taken to the Citadel in irons, protesting that he alone had been faithful, which Brannoc supposed was even true, in the way that a knife is faithful to the hand that holds it.

And the city, meanwhile, had done what cities do. It had swept its streets. It had reopened its markets. The bone market stalls were back up under their awnings, though the sellers had quietly stopped stocking anything carved from grave-ivory, and the criers had new proclamations—*the Register is dissolved, the Hunt is ended, all writs of cleansing are void*—read in voices that hadn't quite decided whether they believed themselves.

Brannoc believed nothing until he saw it with his own eye. So he went and saw.

* * *

The hearing was held in the Hall of Reckoning, which had been built for trials and had never once, in Brannoc's memory, held one worth the name. Now the benches were packed with citizens, and the gallery above with clerks, and the air itself seemed to lean forward.

Vale testified for six hours.

Brannoc sat where he could see her face, which was a mistake, because her face was the worst part. She didn't perform grief or outrage. She read from her own reports—the ones she had falsified, the ones she had written honestly, the ones she had written honestly and then watched become instruments—and she named each lie as a lie, hers first. She told them about the touch for truth she had buried for eleven years. She told them about Odile Vess, and how Odile had hummed a lullaby through two days of questioning without ever once answering, and how Vale had gone home afterward and been sick in a gutter like a drunk because she had finally understood that the woman had known about her mother somehow, had known, and had chosen mercy in the only language left to her.

"I was the best hunter they had," Vale said, "because I never doubted I was right. I want that entered into the record. Not my doubts. My certainty. The certainty was the crime."

A clerk near Brannoc whispered to his neighbor, *"She's hanging herself."*

"She's testifying," the other whispered back. "There's a difference. Barely."

When it was done, when the new tribunal—three magistrates, one of them visibly gifted, which would have been unthinkable a season ago—dismissed her pending judgment, Brannoc caught her at the side door where the light came in gray and cold.

"You could have lied," he said. "Half-truthed your way through it. Kept your rank."

"Rank," Vale said, as if tasting something spoiled. She looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. "How is he? Sable. They say he can't hear anything anymore. They say he nearly died."

"He's stubborn. Same thing, mostly." Brannoc shifted his weight onto his good leg. He hadn't come to trade pleasantries. "You'll be sentenced. Could go either way, from what the lawyers mutter. If it goes badly—if they send you somewhere—I'll tell the boy you stood up straight. That's worth knowing, where you're going."

Something moved behind her eyes, quick and guarded. "That's almost kind, Brannoc."

"Don't spread it around."

"I won't." She pulled her cloak tighter. "The Undercroft. Is it true you're not rebuilding the resistance?"

"We were never a resistance. We were a hole people hid in." He looked out the door at the street, at the ordinary traffic of carts and fishwives and children running hoops. "Holes aren't living. I've done hiding. I'm done."

"And if they come again?" she asked quietly. "If in ten years some new commander reads the old ledgers and gets ideas?"

Brannoc thought about the ledger on his table, forty-one names, three crossed out. He thought about the village he had burned thirty years ago, and how the only thing worse than remembering it was building your whole life so you'd never have to.

"Then they'll find a neighborhood," he said. "With neighbors. Who know each other's faces and owe each other bread and sit on each other's doorsteps. You can burn a resistance out. Nobody's ever managed to burn out a place where people actually live." He shrugged with the shoulder that still worked. "Took me thirty years and one very stubborn bone-carver to figure that out. But I was always slow."

Vale studied him for a long moment. "If the sentence goes lightly," she said, "I'll need work. No Order will take me now. I know things about documents and ledgers and the inside of institutions that most people learn only from the wrong end."

"The guild's reopening," Brannoc said. "Under Pip, gods help us all. Talk to him. He'll either hire you or try to sell you something. Possibly both."

* * *

The Undercroft had been a warren when Brannoc found it: smugglers' tunnels and forgotten cellars, dark in the wrong places, damp in all of them. He had spent the week after the fire walking it end to end with a lantern and a carpenter's chalk, and what he had seen, finally, after years of seeing only walls, was a district.

It had streets—or could. The main gallery ran half a mile, wide enough for carts if you widened the low arches. There were chambers off it that had been storerooms, barracks, a chapel to some god whose name the chalk marks had worn away. There was water—a spring-fed cistern the smugglers had kept sweet, and a drainage run that connected, via routes Brannoc knew better than he wanted to, to the river. There was even sky, in three places, where old ventilation shafts opened into alleys above, and one of those shafts was wide enough for stairs.

So he made stairs.

He hired whoever came—gifted and ungifted alike, because the distinction had died in the smoke along with everything else, and good riddance to it. A mason whose hands could soften stone like wax, working beside a mason whose hands were just hands, and neither of them remarking on it, which was itself a kind of miracle. A woman who grew moss and lichen in patterns, carpeting the wet walls with green instead of rot. Two brothers who could find any lost thing, put to work locating the old property deeds for the buildings above the shafts, which turned out to belong to a dozen landlords, most of them absentee, several of them dead, one of them a fiction.

"We're squatting," Pip said delightedly, arriving with a wheelbarrow of looted office supplies and an expression of pure joy. "We're the largest organized squat in the city's history. Do you know what this does to property law? Do you know who I get to argue with?"

"It's not squatting if we improve the land," Brannoc said.

"That is *not* how property law works."

"It's how mine works."

Pip grinned and dumped the wheelbarrow. Somewhere in the last months the boy had stopped flinching when raised voices happened, and started raising his own, and Brannoc counted that among the week's small victories.

They argued constantly, the whole crew of them, and Brannoc came to understand that the arguing was the point. When the gifted had hidden, decisions got made in whispers by whoever was most frightened. Now decisions got made loudly, at meetings in the old chapel chamber, by anyone who showed up—which was everyone, because the meetings had food. The first vote was over the name. Someone proposed *Newhaven*. Someone else proposed *the Warren*. An ancient tunnel-widow called Mother Quill stood up and said, "It's the Undercroft. It's always been the Undercroft. You don't rename a place just because it stopped being ashamed of itself," and the vote wasn't even close.

The second vote was harder. A family from the Tanners' Row fires—ungifted, refugees of a different purge two generations back—asked for space. Some of the gifted wanted the district kept for their own, a sanctuary, a fortress against next time. The argument ran three hours. In the end it was Marla's sister—not gifted, never gifted—who settled it, standing where Marla used to stand. "My sister hid down here for nine years," she said, "and the loneliest part wasn't the fear. It was that nobody up top knew her name. If we build walls, we build the next hunt ourselves." They voted the family in. Then they voted in a rule, chalked on the chapel wall where everyone could see it: *No doors locked against neighbors.*

Sable came down twice that week. The first time he stood in the main gallery with his head tilted, listening to nothing, and Brannoc watched him do it and understood what it cost—the habit of a lifetime reaching for voices that weren't there anymore. He didn't say anything about it. Neither did Sable. Instead Sable walked the length of the district, touching the walls, and at the end of it he said, "The acoustics in the chapel are extraordinary. Whatever you hold in there will sound beautiful," and went home to carve.

The second time, he brought a box of charms. Small ones, plain ones, wood and bone and river-clay. "For the doorways," he said. "They don't do anything. They're just—" he searched for the word, and Brannoc waited, because he'd learned the boy needed time to find words now, "—they're just *meant well*. Memory, mostly. People like having something at the threshold that remembers them."

Brannoc hung his above his own door, in the room he'd claimed at the gallery's end, and if he touched it sometimes on his way past, in the dark, the way a man touches a comrade's shoulder, that was between him and the doorway.

* * *

On the seventh day, the tribunal's verdict came down, and Brannoc read it aloud in the chapel because half the district refused to believe news until they heard it from someone they trusted.

*Suspended sentence. Stripped of rank and title. Restitution in service: five years, assigned to the Office of Public Records, under supervision.* And beneath the formal language, one line that had clearly cost somebody a fight: *The tribunal notes for the record that the testimony of Inquisitor Vale was material to the dissolution of the Gift-Hunt, and that courage, however late, remains courage.*

"They gave her a desk job," Pip said, scandalized. "Five years of filing. That's either mercy or the cruelest thing I've ever heard."

"Both," Brannoc said. "Same as most sentences."

He walked up the new stairs—it still pleased him, the stairs, solid oak, wide enough for two—and out through the ventilation shaft into the evening street, because he'd promised himself he'd climb into the daylight every day, no exceptions, no matter how much the old soldier in him muttered about exposure. The city smelled of rain coming. Lamps were being lit. Above the rooftops, the bells of the Citadel rang the hour, and Brannoc noticed—he'd been noticing for days—that people no longer flinched at bell-sound. For a season there, after the Court's rising, after the stirring dead, bells had meant something else entirely. Now they were just bells again. Time passing. Nothing more.

Vale was waiting at the corner of the shaft's alley, out of uniform, in a coat that had seen better decades, holding a document case like a shield.

"Suspended sentence," Brannoc said.

"You heard."

"Whole district heard. Pip's already composing a welcome speech. It has jokes in it. I'd brace yourself."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "I'm not here about the guild. Or—I am, partly. Later." She glanced up and down the alley, an old habit, checking sightlines. "Brannoc. Before I start filing papers for five years, there's something you should have. Something I pulled from the commander's effects before the tribunal sealed them." She held out the case. "His private correspondence. With the Citadel. Dated from *before* the proclamation—before the Gift-Hunt was ever announced."

Brannoc took the case. It was heavier than paper had any right to feel. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the Hunt wasn't panic. It wasn't fear after the relic woke. The orders were written eight months before anyone in this city heard a single bell." Her voice stayed level, but her knuckles were white on her own sleeves. "Someone at the Citadel knew the Ninefold Court was going to wake. Knew it, planned for it, and used the panic to do what they'd wanted to do all along. And the correspondence suggests they weren't the only ones who knew." She met his eye. "There are letters here from outside the city, Brannoc. From across the sea. From places that burned half the world once and called it a war."

Rain began to fall, soft and steady, hissing on the lamp-glass overhead. Down the new stairs, faintly, Brannoc could hear the sounds of his neighborhood—hammering, arguing, someone laughing, someone singing badly—rising up through the earth like warmth through floorboards.

"Show me the letters," he said.
