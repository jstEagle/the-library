## 37. Learning Silence

The silence came in layers, like snow.

The first layer was the loudest: the absence of bells. For nine days after he climbed out of the war-vault—carried, half of it, on Brannoc's back and Pip's curses—the city had rung with a sound no one else could hear. The Court dying. Nine voices going out one by one like candles pinched between wet fingers, each one screaming a little, or singing, or in the case of the fourth sovereign—the one who had called herself the Lady of Salt—simply saying *thank you* before the dark took her.

Sable had lain in the cot beneath the Undercroft and listened to all of it, because listening was the only thing he knew how to do.

Then the ninth voice faded, and there was nothing.

Not quiet. Quiet was a thing he'd known his whole life—the hush of a workshop at dawn, the held breath of a vault. This was different. This was *silence*, the kind the dead left behind when they finally stopped needing him. He lay in the cot and turned his head on the pillow and heard—truly heard, with ears that worked like anyone's ears—the drip of water down in Brannoc's cisterns, the far-off argument of gulls over the Bone Market, Pip snoring in the next room with the magnificent disregard of the living.

It should have been a relief. It was, mostly. But underneath the relief was a hollow place shaped exactly like the voices, and some mornings he woke reaching for them the way a man reaches for a limb that isn't there anymore.

On the tenth day, he got up, found his carving knives where Odile's people had packed them—he still couldn't say her name without his chest doing something complicated—and went back to work.

* * *

The workshop they'd given him was a converted storeroom off the new guildhouse, which was not really a guildhouse yet but a warehouse with ambitions. Someone had put a window in it. Sable suspected Pip, who had opinions about windows the way other people had opinions about gods. Through it he could see the rooftops of the Undercroft district climbing the hill, patched and smoking and stubbornly alive, and beyond them the spire of the Inquisition's old chapterhouse, empty now, its banners cut down.

He sat at the bench and picked up a piece of yew—a good piece, dense and pale, from a tree that had grown on the grave-mound outside the walls where nothing else would grow—and held it in both hands, and waited.

That was the ritual. That had always been the ritual. You held the material, you opened yourself the way Odile had taught him to open a ledger—calmly, without flinching—and you waited for the voice to arrive. Every piece of bone or wood or stone carried something. A splinter of coffin-lid remembered the man it had boxed. River stone remembered drowning. Yew remembered everything, because yew grew where grief planted it.

He waited.

Nothing came.

He turned the wood over. Ran his thumb along the grain. Closed his eyes and did the thing he had never once taught anyone to do, the thing that had made him valuable to the Vellum Guild and invisible to everyone else: he reached.

And reached.

And touched nothing but wood. Warm from his hands, smelling faintly of resin, utterly, perfectly, ordinary. A dead thing that had never been anything but dead, that had never stood up inside his skull and started complaining about the cold.

Sable set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might break, and discovered his own hands were shaking.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty room. His voice sounded enormous without the dead under it. "Right."

* * *

Pip found him an hour later, still sitting there, the yew untouched, three whittled shavings on the floor and none on the bench.

"You're doing it wrong," Pip announced, dropping onto the stool across from him with the boneless authority of someone who now technically ran a trading concern and had not yet learned what humility was for.

"I'm not doing anything."

"Exactly. You've been staring at that stick since breakfast. Tess brought you bread. You didn't eat it. That's two sins against the household already, and it's not even noon." Pip leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied him with those quick market-street eyes that missed less than anyone gave them credit for. "You miss it."

Sable looked at the window. At the hill. At the empty spire.

"No," he said, and heard the lie even without a gift for truth.

Pip waited. Pip was very good at waiting; it was how you survived as a runner, knowing when to talk and when to let silence do the lifting. The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Brannoc was hammering something, the steady one-armed rhythm he'd taught himself, patient as tide.

"I don't know what I miss," Sable said finally. "That's the truth of it. I spent thirty years wishing they'd shut up. Every charm I carved, every relic, every saint's knucklebone—I was a door, Pip. Things walked through me constantly and I never got to close it." He turned the yew over again. "And now it's closed. And I keep standing in front of it like a fool, knocking."

"Do you want it open?"

The question landed harder than Pip probably intended—or maybe exactly as hard as Pip intended, because the boy had always known where the soft places were. Sable thought about answering honestly and found he didn't know what honesty looked like yet. He was a man rebuilding his identity out of unfamiliar timber.

"I want—" He stopped. Started again. "I want to carve something and have it be only what it is. Does that make sense? All my life, everything I made was a vessel. A container. People didn't buy my work; they bought whoever was living in it. And I told myself that was service. That I was giving the dead somewhere warm to stay." His thumb found the grain again, traced it. "But some part of me, I think, liked being needed. Even by ghosts. Especially by ghosts. Ghosts don't leave you. Ghosts can't look at your life and decide they've got better options."

Pip was quiet for a moment. Then, with the brutal gentleness that was becoming his signature: "The living can't either, if you don't let 'em try."

Sable laughed—short, surprised, real. "When did you get wise?"

"Bought it off a peddler in the Undercroft. Fair price. Two coppers and a promise to stop calling him a peddler." Pip stood, snagged the bread off the shelf, tore it in half, and shoved one piece into Sable's hands. "Eat. Then carve something. Not for a ghost. Not for a customer. Just—something. See what happens."

"What happens if nothing happens?"

"Then you'll have made a stick." Pip grinned, and it was the grin of a boy who had sold his own name once and bought a better one since. "Worst case, it's a very nice stick."

* * *

He ate the bread. Then he sat with the yew until the light through the window went long and gold, and he began, for the first time in his life, to carve without listening.

It was harder than he expected. Not the craft—that lived in his hands, deeper than the gift ever had—but the habit of it. Thirty years of carving *around* a presence, shaping the charm to fit the voice the way a tailor fits a coat to a body. Leave this hollow for the dead man's grievance. Smooth this curve so the widow's prayer has somewhere to rest. Every piece he'd ever made had been architecture for an occupant.

Now there was no occupant. Now there was only the shape itself, and his hands kept hesitating at the places where he would have made room.

He caught himself four times leaving a cavity where no voice would ever live, and four times he filled it in, and by the fifth time his eyes were burning and he had to set the knife down and press the heels of his hands against them and breathe.

Grief, he was learning, was not only for the dead you loved. It was also for the dead you'd resented, and the self you'd been with them, and the strange terrible comfort of being a door that everyone needed and no one thanked.

Odile would have said something dry and devastating right now. Something about ledgers, probably. About how every account balances eventually, and you don't get to choose which column the cost lands in.

He carved her instead. He didn't plan to. His hands simply went there, the way water goes downhill. The yew became a small figure—not a likeness, he wasn't skilled enough for likeness and wouldn't insult her with the attempt—but a suggestion. A woman standing straight, chin up, one hand extended as though handing you something you hadn't asked for and absolutely required. He gave her a ledger tucked under one arm. He gave her a mouth curved at one corner, the exact geometry of every time she'd caught him asking too few questions.

No hollows. No chambers for a soul to roost in. Just the shape of her, holding.

When he finished, the workshop was dark except for the last stripe of window-light lying across the bench like a blade. He turned the little figure in his fingers.

It weighed almost nothing.

And it held—nothing. No murmur, no memory, no faint complaint of the grave-wood. It was only yew, and skill, and love, and it was the first thing he had ever made that was completely, unreservedly himself.

Sable sat with it a long time.

Then, terrifyingly, quietly, in the deepest chamber of himself where the nine had once sung: he realized he didn't miss being needed by ghosts. Not even a little. What he missed was being *certain*. The voices had made him necessary. Without them he was just a man with sharp tools and a name he was only now learning to sign.

It felt like falling. It felt, also, like ground approaching.

* * *

He hung the charm above the door of the workshop on a leather cord, where everyone who entered would pass beneath a woman handing them something they hadn't asked for. Tess noticed it first and asked who it was; he said, "My teacher," and she nodded and touched it reverently on her way past, and Sable understood that it was working already—that a thing could hold nothing but memory and still do the work of a blessing.

Brannoc came by that evening, drawn by whatever instinct brought him around when the house was too quiet. He stood in the doorway a while, filling most of it, his empty sleeve pinned and folded with soldier neatness.

"Heard you carved today," he said.

"Heard correctly."

"Good." Brannoc's gaze went up to the charm, lingered, came down. There was a question in his face that had been there since the war-vault, since the binding chamber, since the moment Sable had knelt among nine thrones of bone and taken a knife to eternity. Brannoc had fought the gifted. Had burned a village of them. Had spent twenty years paying for it in the coin of other people's safety. He understood sacrifice from the inside, and Sable knew he wanted to ask what it had cost, and knew he wouldn't, because soldiers learn which wounds you don't probe.

"It's quieter in my head than it's been since I was six years old," Sable offered.

"Is that good?"

"Yes." And then, because it was Brannoc, and because the family they were building ran on a currency of confessed truths: "Also no. Both. I think it's going to be both for a while."

Brannoc nodded slowly, as if this matched something in his own ledgers. "Both lasts longer than you'd think," he said. "But it does become mostly yes. Given time." He tapped the doorframe once with his knuckles—a soldier's knock, a sentry's all-clear—and left him to the lamplight.

* * *

He slept that night without dreaming of thrones, which was new, and woke before dawn with his hands aching to work, which was older than memory.

The workshop was cold and blue with early light. He lit the lamp, laid out his knives, and set before himself a fresh piece of yew—and then, on impulse, the small hoard he'd been keeping in a drawer: offcuts and scraps, a sliver of the pyre-wood from Odile's burning that Tess had smuggled home without telling anyone, a chip of the vault door, a curl of shavings from the ninefold relic itself, saved from the binding chamber floor and wrapped in oilcloth like the dangerous treasure it was.

He unwrapped the relic-shaving last.

It was grey as ash and no bigger than a thumbnail, and when he held it up to the light, he waited—out of habit, out of thirty years of ritual—for the voices.

Silence. True silence, all the way down. Whatever had lived in the ninefold relic was gone past any reaching, past any door, and the shaving was only what it appeared to be: a fragment of something ancient, unmade, free.

Sable exhaled a breath he felt he'd been holding for nine days and forty years.

And that was when he heard, with his ordinary ears, footsteps on the stair—two sets, one light and quick, one heavy and deliberate—and a knock at his door before sunrise, which in the months since the fire had only ever meant one thing:

News.

Pip opened the door without waiting, cheeks flushed from the cold climb, and behind him stood a woman in a plain grey traveling cloak with her hood back and frost in her hair, and Sable's heart performed a complicated maneuver, because the last time he had seen Maren Vale she had been standing in a shattered chapterhouse with her gift bared to the whole Inquisition, and no one had known which way the wind would take her.

She looked thinner. She looked tired. She looked, he thought, like a woman who had walked a long way to say something difficult.

"Sable Galloway," she said. "I need to show you something. It's about Odile."
