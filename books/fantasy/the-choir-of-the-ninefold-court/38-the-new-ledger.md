## 38. The New Ledger

Pip had stolen a lot of things in his life. A name, first, off a dead boy in Ropewalk Lane when he was six and needed one that didn't come with debts attached. Bread, obviously. A silver hairpin once, which had turned out to be cursed and had cost him three days of sneezing moths. But he had never stolen anything as large as the Vellum Guild.

That was what it felt like, anyway, standing in the doorway on reopening morning with the key Odile had left him—left *him*, of all people, scrawled on a scrap of vellum tucked into her will like a joke she'd planned years in advance—looking at a hall of dust and empty shelves and one broken window that Brannoc kept promising to fix and never fixing.

"You're grinning," said Sable behind him.

"I'm not."

"Your whole face is doing it. It's very undignified."

"It's called *optimism*, you miserable bone-carver, and I'll thank you not to shame me for it." Pip pushed the door wide and let the morning in. Sunlight came through the smoke-stained glass in long amber bars and lit up the dust like it was something precious instead of something that would need sweeping for a week. "Look at it. Just look at it. We're going to be rich."

"We are going to be solvent," Sable corrected. "There's a difference, and it's roughly the difference between a candle and a house fire."

"House fires make light too." Pip stepped inside, boots ringing on the old floorboards, and put both hands on the counter where Odile used to stand. The wood was worn smooth in two places—one from her elbows, one from where she'd drummed her fingers when she thought no one was counting. He'd counted. He'd counted everything about this place since he was nine years old and she'd caught him lifting a ledger page to sell to a rival broker and, instead of turning him over to the watch, had handed him a broom and said, *If you're going to take things from this guild, at least sweep while you're here.*

He'd swept for eleven years.

Now the guild was his. Well—theirs. That was the part that still made his chest do something complicated and embarrassing whenever he thought about it directly, so mostly he didn't think about it directly, and instead did useful things like shouting orders at people who outranked him in every way except this one.

"Brannoc! The window!"

"It's being fixed," Brannoc rumbled from somewhere near the back, where he was doing something loud with a hammer and only one hand, which should have been impossible but wasn't, because Brannoc had made an entire life out of doing impossible things slowly and without complaint.

"It's been 'being fixed' since the thaw."

"And it'll be fixed when it's fixed. Some things can't be rushed." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone he used when he was saying something true: "Most things, actually."

Pip let that sit. They all let things sit these days. Grief had taught them that much—Odile's name carved into the memorial stone by the pyre yard, and the space at the counter where nobody stood, and the way Sable sometimes reached for a voice that wasn't there anymore and found, for the first time in his life, nothing. Silence everywhere. Pip couldn't imagine it. Pip heard too much if anything—the city talked to him constantly, all its gossip and grift and small hungers—and he figured losing that would be like going deaf twice.

But Sable seemed lighter under it. That was the strange thing. Like a man who'd set down a pack so heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying it, and now kept reaching for straps that weren't there and finding, each time, that his shoulders were free.

"Right," Pip said, clapping his hands, which produced more dust than applause. "Inventory. We open in three days and we have, currently, the following stock: nothing."

"Not nothing," Sable said. He came around the counter and set down a crate that clinked. Inside, packed in straw, were charms—dozens of them, small bone carvings polished smooth, each one tagged in Sable's careful hand. Mourning pieces. Memory-keepers. Little birds and little doors and little hands, carved to hold nothing but the shape of remembering.

"No voices?" Pip asked, though he knew the answer. He asked anyway, because asking was how you took care of someone without making it a whole ceremony about it.

"No voices. Nothing but memory. That's the whole trade now." Sable's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "Merely possible things."

"That's the worst business model I've ever heard."

"It's the best one. Everything else is illegal."

* * *

The first customer came before they'd even finished hanging the sign.

She was a fishwife named Dalla with forearms like dock rope, and she wanted a charm for her son who'd gone down with the *Kestrel* two winters past, and she'd heard—everyone had heard, the story had grown legs and crossed the city twice—that the Vellum Guild could make the dead quiet enough to sleep.

Pip met her at the counter, because meeting customers was his job now, apparently, along with inventory and accounts and hiring and firing and fixing the window himself after Brannoc's third broken promise.

"Dalla," he said. "Right. You want the quiet kind."

"The what?"

"The kind that doesn't talk back." He pulled a tray from under the counter and laid it out. "Old guild, we'd have sold you a listening piece. Dead man's voice in a bit of rib, whispering at you forever. Very popular. Also—" he leaned in, confiding, "—very haunted, and half of them lie, and one of them started a war once, so we've pivoted."

Dalla stared at him. "You're mad."

"Frequently. But look." He picked up one of Sable's carvings—a small boat, no bigger than a thumb, carved from riverbone and worn soft at the edges where Sable's hands had worried it. "This doesn't hold your boy. Nothing holds your boy; he's gone where things go. What this holds is *you holding him*. Every time you miss him, you hold this, and the missing has somewhere to live besides your chest. That's all. That's the whole trick. It's just wood and bone shaped like permission."

Dalla looked at the little boat for a long time. Then she looked at Pip, and her eyes were wet and furious about it. "How much?"

"For you? Three coppers."

"It's worth ten times that."

"It's worth exactly three coppers, because grief shouldn't be a luxury good, and if you tell anyone I said that I'll deny it and charge you five next time."

She laughed—a rough, startled bark—and paid him four because she was stubborn, and left with the boat closed in her fist like something alive.

Pip watched her go and felt the strangest sensation in his ribs, which took him a moment to identify as pride. Not his own pride, even. Borrowed pride. Pride on behalf of a dead woman who'd taught a street thief to sweep and a broken soldier to build and a silent carver to speak.

"She'll tell everyone," Sable said, appearing at his elbow with the particular stealth of a man who'd spent years being invisible on purpose.

"That's the plan. Word of mouth is free advertising."

"You gave away a ten-copper charm for four."

"I *invested* a ten-copper charm for four. There's a difference, and it's roughly the difference between a candle and a house fire."

Sable laughed. Actually laughed, short and rusty, like a hinge that hadn't turned in a year. Pip decided on the spot that getting that sound out of him regularly was now an official duty of management.

* * *

By the end of the first week, the ledger had entries.

Pip wrote them himself, in his terrible handwriting, because Odile's ledgers had always been beautiful and someone had to break the tradition or it would haunt the place. *One memory-charm, sold to Dalla the fishwife, 4 cp.* *One commission, wedding tokens, the Tanner twins (both of them, to each other, finally, gods bless), 2 sp.* *One trade: a jar of last summer's thunderstorm, acquired from the weather-witch on Candle Street, in exchange for a carving of a door that opens inward on winter nights.* *One item, unsold, shelved indefinitely: a map of every place in the city where the sun hits at the hour you most need it. Priceless. Not for sale. Display only.*

That last one was Sable's idea. He'd carved a frame for it and everything.

The guild filled up slowly, the way a house fills when people actually live in it instead of just hiding in it. Brannoc fixed the window on day nine and then immediately broke a shelf, so the net progress was disputed. He also built a new counter at Pip's height, which meant Pip no longer had to stand on a stool to see over it, and pretended this was a structural necessity rather than kindness, and Pip let him pretend, because that was what family was—letting people be kind in ways they could survive admitting.

And on the tenth day, in the grey hour before opening, when the fog was still tangled in the streets and the lamps hadn't been snuffed, someone knocked on the door.

Three knocks. Precise. Even. The knock of a person who had spent her life making sure every sound she made was exactly intended.

Pip looked up from the ledger. Across the hall, Sable went very still over his carving bench. Brannoc's hammer stopped mid-swing.

Maren Vale stood outside the glass, hood down, and she looked—Pip searched for the word and settled on *unarmored*. No Inquisition grey. No badge. Just a plain wool coat and a face that had lost whatever it was that used to hold it together, and gained something else in its place, something rawer and possibly better.

She'd testified, they all knew. Stood up in front of the fractured remains of the Inquisition's tribunal and told the truth about the massacre order, about the pyres she'd refused to light, about the gift she'd buried her whole life and finally dug up and held to the light like a confession. They'd stripped her rank and her warrant and her name from the rolls, and the city, in its fickle way, had made her a story—half villain, half saint, depending on which tavern you drank in.

She waited on the step. She didn't pound. She didn't leave. She just stood there in the fog like a question that had walked a long way to be asked.

Pip looked at Sable. Sable looked at Brannoc. Brannoc's jaw did the thing it did when old war memories were walking through it, because the last time he'd seen Maren Vale up close she'd been hunting them, and some wounds don't care about testimony.

"I'll get it," Pip said, because apparently that was also his job now. Door-opener. Keeper of thresholds. He'd stolen a name once; he could lend the guild's.

He opened the door. The fog came in around her boots.

"Guild's open," he said. "Mostly. The window's fixed but the shelf situation is ongoing."

Vale's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might eventually be excavated. "I'm not here to trade."

"Everyone says that. Everyone trades. Last week a bishop traded me his doubt for a carved sparrow, and I don't even know what I'm going to do with a jar of episcopal doubt, but it's on the shelf."

"Pip," Sable said quietly, from across the room. Not a warning. More like a hand on a shoulder.

Vale looked past him, into the hall—at the counter, at the crates, at the ledger lying open with its crooked honest handwriting, at the little framed map of sunlight. She looked a long time, the way people had started looking at ordinary things lately, like the world had nearly ended and everything mundane had become suddenly worth memorizing.

"I don't have a trade," she said. "I don't have a craft. I can't carve, I can't build, I can barely cook. What I have is—" she stopped, and started again, and Pip realized with a small shock that she was nervous, actually nervous, this woman who had once emptied an entire guildhall with her presence alone. "I know how to find things. People, mostly. I know how to listen to what they're not saying. And I know how the Inquisition thinks, what's left of it, and which corners of it are still dangerous. I thought—" Her hands curled at her sides, then deliberately opened. "I thought maybe you needed someone who knows how to hunt, so the hunting stays away from you. Or someone who can verify a trade is honest, because my gift works fine and it turns out honesty is in short supply. Or failing all that, someone to sweep."

The silence stretched. Outside, the fog was thinning; somewhere a cart rattled past, and the city began its usual argument with the morning.

Brannoc set down his hammer. The sound of it was very loud.

"She burned villages," he said. Not cruelly. Just laying it on the counter like a coin that had to be counted.

"Yes," Vale said. "Not personally. But I carried the orders, and I didn't ask what was in the wagons, and not asking is its own kind of lighting the match. I'm not asking you to forget that. I'm asking you to let me work next to it."

More silence. Pip's heart was doing something inconvenient. Because he remembered her, was the thing—he remembered her voice through the wall of the Undercroft, cold and certain, reading names off a list. And he also remembered Odile humming a lullaby in chains to crack a hunter's certainty, and he remembered that certainty cracking, and he remembered Sable standing in front of them all in the binding chamber saying *mercy is a risk, and we take risks, that's the whole family business.*

Sable came around the counter. He walked slowly, the way he did everything now, and stopped in front of Vale, and for a moment neither of them spoke—two people who had spent a year trying to destroy each other, standing in a shop that sold memories without voices.

"There's a broom in the back," Sable said.

Vale blinked. "What?"

"Odile's rule. If you're going to take something from this guild, sweep while you're here." He nodded toward the corner where the broom leaned, handle worn smooth by eleven years of a thief's hands. "You want to take a place here. So. Sweep."

Something moved through Vale's face—something that had been locked behind her ribs since a lullaby in a cell, maybe longer, maybe since childhood, maybe always—and she bowed her head, and when she lifted it again her eyes were bright and steady.

"I'll sweep," she said.

"Sweeping starts at dawn," Pip announced, seizing the ledger and his destiny with both hands. "Pay is terrible, hours are worse, and the manager is insufferable, so you'll fit right in. Brannoc, show her where the buckets live. Sable, stop looking moved, it's bad for morale. We open in an hour and I refuse to greet our first customer with visible emotion, it sets a precedent."

They scattered to their tasks—Brannoc gruffly pointing, Vale following with a bucket like a knight accepting a strange new quest, Sable returning to his bench where the little bone birds waited to be given nothing but memory.

Pip stood at his counter, at his height, no stool required, and opened the ledger to a fresh page. At the top, in his terrible handwriting, he wrote:

*Day ten. Staff: four. Stock: possible. Debts: forgiven. Future: pending.*

Then, after a moment, he added one more line, small, in the corner, where only he would ever read it:

*Odile—you were right about everyone. Don't tell Sable I said so.*

The bell above the door chimed. First customer of the day.

Pip smiled, and got to work.
