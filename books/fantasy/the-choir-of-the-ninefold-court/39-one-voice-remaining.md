## 39. One Voice Remaining

The frost had come early that year, and it had come to the graveyard first.

Sable noticed it as he climbed the hill above the Tanners' Quarter, where the city's dead were laid out in rows like ledgers waiting to be balanced. Frost silvered every headstone, every kerbstone, every blade of grass gone stiff and brittle in the cold. The whole hill glittered under a sky the color of unbleached vellum. It was beautiful in the way that only things which did not care about him could be beautiful.

He carried a chisel wrapped in cloth, a mallet, and a small paper bag of honey cakes from the stall by the Bone Market—the good ones, with the burnt edges Pip called *the best part* and everyone else called *ruined*. Odile would have eaten them all without comment and then stolen his tea.

A year since the pyre yard. A year since her last words had gone into his ear like a splinter he'd never worked free: *Unmake it, boy. Not free. Unmake.* A year since he'd knelt in the binding chamber with his hands shaking around his carving tools and given nine sovereigns back to true death, voice by voice, until the last bell faded and the silence came down over the city like snow.

He still wasn't sure whether the silence had taken his hearing or given it back to him changed. The chirurgeons said his ears were sound. The world said otherwise. He heard carts and gulls and Pip shouting across the guildhall, but underneath it all there was a quiet so complete it felt less like an absence than a presence—something vast and patient sitting in the room with him, saying nothing.

He had stopped asking what it was. That was the year's other lesson.

Odile's grave stood at the top of the hill, where the ground fell away toward the river. She'd bought the plot herself, decades ago, back when she was young enough to find mortality funny. *Best view in the city,* she'd told him once, tapping the deed against her knuckles. *And nobody talks.*

He'd carved her marker himself. It had taken him three weeks, because for the first time in his life he was carving stone without listening to anything, and his hands kept hesitating at the places where instinct used to be. No ghost of hers whispered suggestions. No dead man's memory guided the chisel. Just Sable Galloway, alone with the grain of the granite, doing the work the slow way, the human way.

The stone read:

**ODILE VESS**
*She asked too few questions of no one,
and too many of everyone.*
Keeper of the Vellum Guild.
Mentor. Tyrant. Home.

Pip had insisted on *Tyrant.* Brannoc had insisted on *Home.* Sable had refused to choose between them, because choosing between them would have been a lie either way.

He reached the grave and stopped.

Something was wrong with the stone.

Not damaged—nothing so simple. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved a chair two inches while you were out. He stood very still, breath fogging, and looked.

At the base of the marker, where the granite met the frost-stiff grass, there was a carving.

It was fresh. The cut edges were pale and sharp, unweathered, untouched by frost—as though the stone itself had not yet decided to accept them. And the style—

Sable knew that style the way he knew his own heartbeat. The deep confident gouges. The way each line swelled and tapered like a breath being drawn. The little hooked terminals she used to put on everything, the ones the apprentices at the old guildhall had spent years failing to copy. Odile had carved bone and wood and ivory for forty years, and her hand was in this work as surely as her name was not.

Because there was no name. There were only words, cut in her unmistakable script:

**YOU ASKED TOO FEW QUESTIONS OF THE DEAD
AND TOO MANY OF YOURSELF.
I SAW WHAT YOU CHOSE IN THE DARK UNDER THE CITY.
I WOULD HAVE CHOSEN SLOWER AND WORSE.
THE GUILD IS NOT A BUILDING, BOY. IT NEVER WAS.
EAT THE CAKES WHILE THEY'RE STILL WARM.
—O.**

Sable sat down in the frost without feeling the cold soak through his coat.

He read it again. Then again, moving his lips, hearing her voice in the cadence even though no voice remained anywhere in the world for him to hear. The scolding rhythm of it. The way she saved the tender thing for last and buried it under a joke about cakes, because Odile Vess would have bitten off her own tongue before letting tenderness stand in a sentence unguarded.

*I saw what you chose in the dark under the city.*

But she hadn't been there. She had died in the pyre yard with fire climbing her dress and Vale's hand reaching through the smoke, buying them eleven seconds with her life. Sable had counted the seconds afterward, obsessively, the way a man worries a loose tooth. Eleven seconds. Long enough for Brannoc to drag him behind the wall. Not long enough for anything else.

And yet.

He put his palm flat against the carving. The stone was cold, colder than the morning justified, and beneath the cold there was something else—a faint vibration, so slight he might have imagined it, like the memory of a bell long after the ringing stops. His scarred fingertips, the fingers that had spent thirty years reading voices out of bone, pressed harder.

Nothing spoke. Nothing answered. There was no whisper of the dead, no layered chorus, no sovereign purring promises from inside the stone. Whatever he had shattered in the war-vault had stayed shattered. He was almost certain of that now. Almost.

So how?

He turned the question over the way he used to turn relics, looking for the seam. Possibilities arranged themselves like tools on a bench.

She could have left instructions before she died. A letter, a commission, a payment to some stonemason to cut the words after her execution. But no mason alive carved like that. He'd spent a decade watching her work; he'd have known a forgery the way you know a stranger wearing your mother's coat. And the frost—frost clung to every surface on the hill except those fresh-cut lines, as if the stone remembered the chisel as warmth.

She could have left it unfinished and someone finished it. Who? Brannoc couldn't carve a straight line. Pip could carve, after a fashion, but Pip's letters ran sideways like a drunk walking home, and Pip had wept for three days when they buried her and would never have managed her hand sober or otherwise.

Or—or the thing he didn't want to say, the thing that sat in his chest like a coal: that some part of Odile Vess had known what she was, all those years running a guild that traded in impossible things, and had left one last impossible thing behind, addressed to the one person who would recognize both the style and the message. That love, given enough time and enough stubbornness, might learn to do what magic does.

He thought about the lullaby. Vale had told him about it once, late, over bad wine in the rebuilt guildhall—how Odile, chained in her cell, had hummed a song that only Vale's dead mother knew, and how it had cracked the Inquisitor open like a nut. They had never resolved what that meant. Whether Odile had trafficked with Vale's mother's ghost, or heard it through some relic, or simply been the kind of woman who collected other people's griefs the way she collected debts. They had agreed, finally, to stop asking.

Maybe that was the whole of it. Maybe the world held a residue that wasn't quite gift and wasn't quite chance—the imprint of people who had loved hard enough to leave marks. Maybe the dead didn't need voices to speak. Maybe they just needed someone who knew their handwriting.

Sable laughed. It came out ragged, and it turned halfway through into something else, and he let it, because there was no one on the hill to see and because Odile had always despised dignity in private moments. *Dignity is for customers,* she used to say. *In here, we're just people stealing bread from the impossible.*

He cried for a while, kneeling in the frost with his forehead against the cold granite, and the vast patient silence sat beside him and said nothing, and for once its silence felt like company instead of wound.

When he was done, he unwrapped the honey cakes and found they were still faintly warm. He had bought them more than an hour ago. He looked at the paper bag for a long moment.

Then he ate one, and it was perfect, burnt edges and all, and he decided—he made the decision deliberately, the way you set a bone—that he was not going to ask about that either.

* * *

He stayed until the sun cleared the rooftops of the Tanners' Quarter and the frost began to sweat off the stones. He told her about the guild. About Pip's ledger system, which violated arithmetic, several laws, and possibly God, but somehow balanced. About Brannoc teaching carving classes to Undercroft kids with his single hand and pretending he hated every minute. About Vale coming by on rest-days, awkward as a cat in a formal garden, asking questions about trade routes that were really questions about whether she was allowed to belong anywhere anymore.

"She asked for a place," he said. "Formally. Stood in the hall with her hat in her hands like a girl at a hiring fair. Pip made her sign the ledger twice because she enjoyed watching her do it." He turned a cake over in his fingers. "You'd have hated it. You'd have loved it. Same thing, with you."

The stone said nothing. The stone was a stone. But the carving caught the light as the sun moved, and for half a heartbeat the hooked terminals of her letters seemed to gleam like eyes amused at him from the edge of the world.

"I'm going to sign my work," he told her. "My own name. Starting today. I've been thinking about it for months and finding reasons, and I've run out of reasons, so I expect that was your doing too."

He rose, knees cracking, and brushed the frost from his coat. Before he left he touched the carving once more—lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child—and felt nothing but stone, and was grateful, and was bereaved, and understood that both of those could live in the same hand at the same time. That was the lesson, maybe. The last one. Grief didn't close. It just learned to carry weight.

He went down the hill without looking back, because looking back was for men who needed permission, and he had it now, cut in stone in a dead woman's hand: pride, and permission, and a joke about cakes.

* * *

The Vellum Guild smelled of wood shavings, ink, and Pip's disastrous attempt at spiced cider, which had been simmering since dawn and now threatened the structural integrity of the building. Sable came in stamping frost off his boots and found the main hall in its usual state of controlled catastrophe: apprentices hauling crates of stock, Brannoc arguing with a supplier about the price of walnut, Vale perched on a stool by the window cataloguing acquisitions with the grim precision of a woman filing reports for a government that no longer existed.

"Well?" Pip dropped down from the counting desk, where she had absolutely not been standing, because standing on the counting desk was forbidden. Her eyes went over his face with the ruthless speed of a street kid reading a mark. "You look like weather. Good weather or bad?"

"Good," Sable said, and found it was true. "It's her. Her hand, her words. Fresh-cut." He set the remaining honey cakes on the counter, and Pip took one automatically, and then went very still, looking at the bag, looking at him.

"They were warm," she said.

"They were warm."

Brannoc had come over quietly, the way big men move when they don't want to interrupt something. He looked at the bag, then at Sable, and something passed over his face—not surprise. Recognition. "My grandmother's grave," he said slowly. "Up past the salt marshes. Every year on her day, the moss on her stone grows in a pattern. A knot she used to braid into my hair." He shrugged, embarrassed, a soldier unused to talking about such things. "Never told anyone. Never seemed like the kind of thing that survived being explained."

"Maybe it doesn't need explaining," Vale said from the window, without looking up. "Maybe that's the point of it."

Pip snorted, but gently. "The great bone-carver takes a year to figure out what I knew at twelve. Dead people don't leave. They just stop making noise about it." She bit the cake, got the burnt edge, and pointed the rest at him like a scepter. "So. Are we done? With the brooding? Because I've drafted the winter inventory and it's a masterpiece and it needs your signature, and also Corvin's old storeroom is haunted, probably, or full of rats, same thing, and I want hazard pay."

"You want—" Sable blinked. "You want me to pay you to deal with rats in a room you already get paid to manage?"

"Hazard pay," Pip confirmed. "Family rates."

And there it was, the word, dropped as casually as she dropped everything, and Sable felt it land in his chest and settle somewhere it had been trying to reach for a year. Family rates. He looked around the hall—at Brannoc already drifting back to his argument, at Vale hiding a smile behind her catalogue, at Pip standing there with cake crumbs on her chin and the whole chaotic future of the guild in her grubby competent hands—and he thought: *the guild is not a building, boy. It never was.*

He fetched his ledger. He signed Pip's winter inventory—after correcting three entries that would have bankrupted them by spring—and then he turned to the last page, the one he'd been avoiding, where the commission book waited for new work.

There was a job waiting. Had been waiting for six days, according to Pip's marginal notes, which grew increasingly sarcastic as they descended the page. A widow in the Weavers' Quarter. Her husband had been a bell-founder, died in the autumn fevers, and she wanted a mourning charm carved for his grave.

Ordinarily Sable would carve it listening—had carved a thousand such charms, catching the last warm echo of a voice before it scattered. But there were no voices now. There was only him, and the stone, and whatever a man could put into a thing with nothing but his own two hands and his own one heart.

He wrote the entry in the ledger. Then, below it, in a clear deliberate hand, for the first time in his career, he signed it:

*Sable Galloway.*

His name looked strange on the page. It looked like the beginning of something.

"Pip," he said, closing the book. "Get my travel cloak. And tell Brannoc I'll need the cart—there's a widow in the Weavers' Quarter who's been waiting six days, and I intend to hear an apology about that from whoever let it sit."

"That was me," Pip said cheerfully. "Hazard pay."

"Out of your wages."

"We don't pay wages!"

"Exactly."

She was laughing as she ran for the cloak, and the sound filled the guildhall the way bells used to fill the city, and Sable Galloway picked up his tools—clean, sharp, holding nothing but memory and skill—and went out into the cold bright morning to do the only work that had ever truly been his.

Behind him, on the counter, the last honey cake sat in its paper bag, still warm, though no one had lit a fire near it all day.

He chose not to ask.
