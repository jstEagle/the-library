## 40. The Choir Dispersed

The workshop smelled of cedar shavings and beeswax and, faintly, of the river that ran beneath the Vellum Guild's new foundations—clean water now, or clean enough, carrying away the last of the ash from a pyre yard no one spoke of anymore except in the way people speak of weather from another year.

Sable Galloway stood at the long bench by the eastern window and watched his three apprentices fail.

Not badly. That was the point of them. He had chosen them badly on purpose, in a manner of speaking—chosen hands that trembled, eyes that wandered, tempers that flared at the first crooked line—because he had learned, over a year of teaching, that the ones who carved too easily were the ones who listened without meaning to. The gift did not die when the Court died. It only went quiet, like a bell with its clapper drawn, and quiet things could be woken. He knew that better than anyone alive.

"Again," he said to the youngest, a girl named Tansy whose father had sold fish off the Bone Market stalls back when the market still sold bones. "You're rushing the grain. The bone remembers being part of something. You have to ask it politely."

Tansy scowled at her half-carved mourning charm—a small round thing meant to hold nothing but the shape of grief, so the wearer could set their sorrow down for an hour at a time and pick it up again after. "It doesn't say anything."

"No," Sable said. "It doesn't. That's what we're practicing."

He said it lightly, but the words sat in his chest like a stone set into a wall—fitted, deliberate, load-bearing. *That's what we're practicing.* A year ago he would have said *listen for it.* A year ago he would have closed his eyes and let the dead man's voice come up through the marrow like water through chalk, and he would have known exactly which words the widow needed to hear, and he would have carried that knowledge home alone at night like a debt he could never finish paying.

Now there was only the rasp of steel on bone, and the morning light, and three apprentices who thought their master was merely fussy.

*You don't miss it.*

That was the terrifying part. He had expected to miss it the way a man misses a limb—the phantom ache of nine voices gone silent all at once, the great choir of the city's dead dispersed like smoke. For the first weeks after the Binding Chamber, after he had knelt on cold stone and unmade sovereigns one by one with his carving tools until his hands bled and the ninth voice faded like a lamp guttering out, he had waited for the silence to crush him.

It hadn't. The silence had turned out to be a room with the windows open.

He picked up his own work—a commission from the Undercroft, from Brannoc's neighborhood association, though Brannoc refused to call it that. A memorial plaque for the corner where Odile Vess had died buying them eleven seconds. Eleven seconds was what Brannoc had calculated, afterward, in the flat voice of a soldier doing arithmetic on the dead: *she gave us eleven seconds, boy, and we spent every one of them getting you out.* The plaque was to be oak, not bone—Odile had hated bone-work done carelessly—but Sable had carved a small vellum scroll into its face, because the guild had been her life before it was anyone else's.

He was carving her initials into the scroll's seal when Pip burst through the door without knocking, which was how Pip did everything.

"—and I told him, I said, Master Corvin's ledger is *closed*, sir, permanently, in the sense of *buried*, and if he wants impossible things he can take it up with the river, because that's where the last person who tried ended up—" Pip stopped, took in the workshop, the apprentices, the shavings, and lowered his voice to a stage whisper that carried perfectly to the far wall. "Is this a teaching moment? Should I come back?"

"You live here," Sable said. "Technically you live here more than I do. Brannoc says you've claimed the good cot."

"Brannoc says the good cot belongs to whoever does the ledgers, and I do the ledgers." Pip dropped onto the bench beside Tansy, stole a shaving, and began folding it into some kind of bird. He was sixteen now, or seventeen—he'd never been sure himself—and he had grown three inches and acquired a scar along his jaw from the knife that had been meant for Sable's heart. It made him look dashing, he said. It made Sable's chest tighten every time he saw it, though less than it used to. Time did that. Time and the sight of the boy laughing.

"The guild took a trade this morning," Pip announced, producing the ledger with a flourish. "Listen to this. Bottled *rain*, from the summer of the Bells. Actual rain, in a bottle, sworn authentic. A widow from Harrow Lane traded it for a memory-charm of her husband's laugh."

Sable set down his knife. "And what did you record as the price?"

"Two coppers and a promise she gets first pick of the spring charms." Pip grinned. "Odile would have called it charity."

"Odile would have called it *good business*," Sable corrected. "Charity is when you don't write it down."

The apprentices laughed. Even Tansy, who was solemn the way only children who have seen fires are solemn. And Sable felt it again, that thing he had no better word for than *keptness*—the sensation of being held inside something larger than himself, a vessel at last, though not the kind the Ninefold Court had wanted to make of him.

* * *

In the afternoon he walked up through the city to the grave.

The way took him past the old Inquisition hall, which was now a courthouse with its iron doors pried open and replaced with glass, so that anyone passing could see the proceedings within. Vale had fought for that. *Justice you can't watch is just punishment wearing a mask,* she'd said, standing in front of the assembled remnant of the Order with her gift laid bare and her career already ash, and enough of them had been young enough, or tired enough, or honest enough to believe her. She testified for nine days about the massacre order. On the tenth day the commander was taken away in irons, and on the eleventh day Maren Vale resigned her rank in a letter of four sentences, the last of which read simply: *I know what truth sounds like, and none of it was spoken in that building.*

She worked as an advocate now, for the gifted who came out of hiding to register under the new Accords. There were more of them every season. The Gift-Hunt proclamations had been pulled down from every post in the city; children peeled the last scraps of them for the pretty red wax seals.

The city remembered the Ninefold Court the way it remembered a fever—as a warning, not a longing. That had surprised Sable most of all. He had braced for myths, for cults, for some fool in a cellar trying to piece the relic together from memory and rumor. Instead the storytellers in the market squares told it straight: nine kings and queens who would not stay dead, who sang so sweetly that a bone-carver nearly gave them the world, and who were given instead the true death they had fled for a thousand years. *And the carver lost his hearing for it,* the tellers always added, leaning in, *and he said it was a fair price, and the frightening thing is that he meant it.*

They always looked around at the crowd when they said that last part, as if expecting Sable Galloway to materialize and object.

He never did. He bought chestnuts from the roaster by the fountain and kept walking.

The graveyard on the hill had filled in the year since the fires—not with the burned, whose names were cut into a single long stone in the Undercroft square, but with ordinary dead, the city resuming its ancient habit of outliving itself. Odile's plot sat at the top, where the wind came through, because Odile had once said that any afterlife worth having required a draft.

Sable stood before the headstone a long moment before he saw it.

His breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door opens onto winter.

There was fresh carving on the stone. Not weathering, not moss—carving, crisp-edged, recent, done with a fine chisel by someone who understood that stone is just slow bone. Three words in Odile's own hand, in her own style, with her particular way of cutting the serifs at a slight upward angle, as if even her letters were raising an eyebrow:

**WELL DONE. NOW LIVE.**

He touched the grooves. Cold stone. Nothing rose to meet him—no voice, no whisper, no ghost of a ghost. Whatever had held the dead close to the world had loosened its grip the day he unmade the ninth throne, and if anything remained of Odile Vess in the ground beneath his feet, it kept its own counsel.

But the carving was real. Someone had climbed this hill with chisels and mallets and Odile's letters memorized, and left a message for one man in a city of thousands.

Brannoc, who visited every week with pipe-smoke and complaints? Pip, who couldn't carve a straight line to save his life? Vale, who had stood at this grave once in her gray traveling coat and said nothing at all? Any of them. All of them. Or—or the other possibility, the one he had stopped permitting himself to name, the possibility that love was a kind of craft and craft was a kind of persistence and some things outlasted even the silence he had purchased with his hearing.

A year ago he would have needed to know. A year ago the not-knowing would have eaten him alive, and he would have gone hunting through every relic in the guild for one thread of her voice.

Sable Galloway laughed—one short, startled sound that scattered a crow off the neighboring stone—and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"All right," he told the headstone. "Message received."

* * *

He stayed until the light went gold, then walked back down through streets that smelled of bread and tar and the first cold promise of autumn. At the foot of the hill he passed the market square, where a storyteller was just reaching the end of the tale—the unmaking, the ninth voice fading, the bells going quiet over the city—and Sable paused, as he always did, at the part where the carver signed his work.

Because that was the detail the storytellers had gotten right, the one true thing they'd somehow learned: that for thirty years, Sable Galloway had hidden his mark inside his carvings, a tiny spiral tucked into the grain where only another carver would ever find it, the signature of a man who could not afford to exist. And that in the Binding Chamber, kneeling among nine empty thrones with blood on his hands and silence filling his skull like snow, he had understood that invisibility had been its own kind of death—the slowest of the nine deaths available to him—and that a man who gives everything should at least get to keep his name.

Every piece that came out of the Vellum Guild's workshop now bore the mark openly, cut deep where anyone could see it: **S. GALLOWAY**, and beneath it, smaller, the guild's new motto, invented by Pip during a late night of bad wine and ratified by acclamation:

*Traders in Merely Possible Things.*

Back at the workshop, the apprentices had swept the floor and lit the lamps, and Pip was arguing with Brannoc in the doorway about whether the guild could afford a second apprentice bench, and Vale's silhouette had appeared at the end of the lane with her satchel of case papers, meaning she'd stay for supper and pretend she hadn't planned to.

Sable stood a moment in the doorway, looking at all of it—at the lamplight and the argument and the smell of Brannoc's pipe, at the family that had assembled itself out of outcasts and defectors and a street kid with a stolen name he'd finally, last spring, legally changed to the one he'd chosen for himself.

Then he went inside, picked up his knife, and got back to work.

The bone lay warm under his hands. It said nothing at all.

He found that he had plenty to say to it anyway.
