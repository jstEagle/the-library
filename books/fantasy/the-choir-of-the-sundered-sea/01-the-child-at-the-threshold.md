## 1. The Child at the Threshold

The rain had been falling for three days, and Astrid Ravensworth had stopped believing it would ever stop.

They stood in the doorway of the waystation at Thornfell Pass, watching the water carve channels through the mud of the yard, and thought—not for the first time—that this was a stupid place to build a road, a stupider place to guard one, and the stupidest place in all the Nine Holds to spend the last week of a contract that had already gone on four months too long.

Behind them, the hearth crackled. The merchant's factor snored in his chair, a half-empty cup of wine balanced against his chest. Two hired guards played dice by the fire, cheating each other with the lazy competence of men who'd been paid too well for too long. Outside, the pass howled.

Astrid checked the sky out of habit. Low cloud, no moon visible, which meant nothing anyway. They hadn't looked for the moon in years. Looking was a prayer's first cousin, and prayers were a habit they'd been trying to break since the god went quiet.

*Vael*, their god's name had been. Vael of the Open Hand, patron of mercenaries and travelers and anyone whose life fit badly inside a kingdom's borders. You didn't build temples to Vael. You built shrines at crossroads, and you left a coin or a knife or a drop of blood, and you said the words—*keep my hand steady, keep my road open*—and for thirty-one years, Astrid had felt the answer like a second pulse under their own.

Then, six years ago, mid-winter, mid-contract: silence.

Not absence. That was the thing nobody understood. Astrid had prayed to dead gods before, back when they still bothered, and praying to a dead god felt like shouting into an empty room. Praying to Vael felt like standing outside a door with someone moving behind it. Someone who could hear you. Someone choosing not to answer.

Astrid had stopped knocking eventually. But they never quite stopped standing near the door.

"Ravensworth." One of the dice players, a thick-necked man called Odo, jerked his chin toward the window. "Something's in the yard."

"There's always something in the yard. It's called weather."

"It moved."

Astrid sighed, took their sword down from where it leaned against the lintel, and stepped into the rain.

* * *

The yard was a churned brown mess under the grey wash of evening, and whatever Odo had seen was gone—or seemed to be, until Astrid crossed halfway to the gate and heard it.

A sound from beside the woodpile. Small. Rhythmic. Not crying, exactly. Crying wanted something. This was the sound of someone who had learned that wanting got you nothing, and was making noise anyway because the body demanded it.

Astrid rounded the woodpile with the sword low and found a child.

Mud-soaked didn't cover it. The child was *made* of mud, more or less—a small shape maybe seven or eight years old, wrapped in what might once have been a wool blanket, now a sodden brown shell. Bare feet, blue-white with cold. Hair matted flat. And eyes that came up and fixed on Astrid with a look that made the mercenary's hand go loose on the grip, because it wasn't fear. Fear, Astrid knew every flavor of. This was assessment. The child was looking at Astrid the way Astrid looked at a contract: weighing terms.

"You alone?" Astrid asked.

The child said something.

It wasn't any language Astrid knew, and Astrid knew most of them—the trade-tongue, High and Low Reaves, the river-cant of the delta smugglers, enough Holdish to bargain, enough of the steppe speech to insult a man's horse. This was none of those. It was older than any of them; Astrid couldn't have said how they knew that, only that the sounds had the weight of things worn smooth by centuries of mouths. Like river stones. Like the sea floor.

It was also, unmistakably, a question.

"I don't understand you." Astrid crouched, slow, keeping the sword flat along their forearm where the child could see it. Old trick. *I'm armed but I'm not aiming.* "Are there others? Did someone leave you here?"

More of the language, faster now, and the child's hands came up and shaped something in the air—a curve, a depth, a falling—and then pressed flat, palm down, over and over, with an urgency that needed no translation.

*Down. Something down.*

"Yeah," Astrid said. "Lots of things are down. Ground, for one."

The child stared at them. Then, deliberately, it reached out and touched Astrid's boot, and said one word—one short, hard syllable—and Astrid felt it land somewhere beneath the ribs, in the old hollow where Vael's voice used to live.

For one heartbeat, absurdly, impossibly, the hollow *echoed*.

Astrid stood up fast. Rain, exhaustion, bad wine. That was all that was. "Inside," they said. "You'll die out here, and I've got enough trouble without a corpse on the ledger."

The child didn't move. Astrid held out a hand. The child looked at it for a long moment, then took it—small fingers, freezing, gripping with surprising strength—and let itself be led across the yard like a small drowned thing hauled ashore.

* * *

Odo laughed when he saw them. The factor woke, sputtered, complained about vermin and liability, and was bought off with a look and a promise itemized on Astrid's own contract margin. The innkeeper's widow, Marta, who ran the waystation kitchen and feared nothing on this earth except unpaid bills, took one look at the child and started issuing orders—bath, broth, dry clothes from the chest she kept for stranded pilgrims—and Astrid, who had spent twenty years giving orders and taking them and mostly ignoring both, found themselves obeying.

By full dark the child sat by the hearth in a shirt three sizes too big, holding a bowl of barley broth in both hands and eating with the controlled ferocity of someone who did not trust food to stay offered. Marta had scrubbed the mud away and revealed a face that was ordinary enough—sharp chin, scabbed knee, a healing cut above one brow—except for the eyes, which stayed wrong. Too steady. Too old. Eyes like deep water.

"What do we do with it?" Odo asked.

"*Them*," said Marta.

"What do we do with *them*, then. There's no villages up-pass. Nobody comes through Thornfell in this season unless they're mad or paid."

"Maybe they're paid," Astrid said quietly, and the thought settled over the room like a change in pressure. Nobody walked into the high passes in the wet season. Nobody walked anywhere barefoot. Somebody had brought this child up the mountain, or lost them on the way, and either possibility had teeth.

The child looked up from the broth. Looked at Astrid. And spoke again—that same hard syllable from the yard, then a run of the smooth old words, softening at the end almost like a question, almost like a plea.

"What's it saying?"

"How should I know?" Astrid said. But they knew. The way you know the shape of a lie, the way you know a blade coming for your back. The child was saying *you*. Saying it the way people said the names of gods.

Later, when the fire burned low and the others slept, Astrid sat across the hearth from the child and tried, feeling like a fool, the old formulas. Trade-tongue. Reaves. River-cant. The child watched each attempt with polite, unnerving patience, the way you'd watch a dog try to open a latch.

At last the child set down the empty bowl, crawled close, and put one muddy-damp hand flat on Astrid's chest, over the sternum, over the hollow.

And said a word.

It was not the hard syllable from the yard. It was longer, softer, and it went through Astrid like a bell struck inside the bone. For half a breath, the silence behind the door—the six-year silence, the silence Astrid had built their whole hardened life around—cracked. Just cracked, just a seam of light and sound, and through it came something vast and drowning and *glad*, the way the sea is glad, indifferent as weather—

Then it closed. Silence again. The fire popped. The child withdrew its hand and looked at Astrid with those deep-water eyes, and yawned, enormous and unguarded and suddenly, horribly, just a child's yawn, and curled up on the hearthstone like a cat and was asleep between one breath and the next.

Astrid sat very still for a long time.

They should wake Odo. Should bar the door, post a watch, start planning. Somebody had carried this child into the mountains for a reason, and reasons like that came looking for their property. Astrid had buried enough of their past to know how the past collected debts.

Instead they pulled their own cloak off and laid it over the sleeping child, tucking it with a clumsiness that would have gotten them laughed out of any company they'd ever ridden with.

"Don't make me regret this," Astrid told the small sleeping shape.

The child, naturally, did not answer. But as Astrid banked the fire and took up their sword and settled into the chair by the door for a watch they weren't contracted to keep, they could have sworn—could have sworn on the silent name of Vael—that somewhere far below the mountain, far below even the sea, something turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to count the moons.

Three days later, when the rain finally broke and the first riders came up the pass wearing the crown's grey and silver, Astrid would understand exactly how much trouble a mud-soaked child could be.

But that night, they only kept watch, and for the first time in six years, they prayed.

Just to see if anyone was listening.
