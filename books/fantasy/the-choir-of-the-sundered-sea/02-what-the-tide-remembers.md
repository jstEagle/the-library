## 2. What the Tide Remembers

Ysolde Fenwick had spent eleven years translating the dead, and in all that time the dead had never once answered back.

She kept her study above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, a room that smelled of tallow and old vellum, where the afternoon light came through salt-fogged windows the color of weak tea. Her colleagues at the Royal Collegium had called her work a curiosity at best — *the linguistic equivalent of grave-robbing*, Provost Halloway had said, with the particular cruelty of a man who had never been wrong about anything important. They studied living tongues. Languages with armies and trade fleets behind them. The Sundered dialect had no speakers, no homeland, no future. It had drowned with everything else.

Which was why, when Astrid Ravensworth had appeared at her door three days ago with a mud-streaked child and an expression like a drawn blade, Ysolde had assumed it was a joke. Or a robbery. Possibly both.

Now she sat across from the child at her own writing desk, a slate between them, and watched the impossible happen.

"Say it again," she whispered. "Slowly. Please."

The child — Nell, the mercenary called her, though Ysolde suspected the name was borrowed rather than given — looked up. She was perhaps seven years old, small for it, with eyes the flat grey of deep water and hair that had probably been fair before the road got hold of it. She spoke the word again.

*Vessu-kaal. Vessu-kaal im-morra.*

Ysolde's pen hovered over the page. Her hands were not steady. Eleven years of conjugating a language no one had spoken since the sea took a kingdom, and here was a child of seven using its subjunctive mood correctly. Not parroting — she'd tested that first, cruelly, offering nonsense syllables to see if the girl would repeat them. Nell had only frowned, the way a scholar frowns at bad grammar, and corrected her.

Corrected *her*. Ysolde Fenwick, who had written the only surviving grammar of the Sundered tongue, corrected by a child who couldn't have been alive when the last native speaker died.

Unless.

No. She wasn't ready for *unless* yet.

"The second word," Ysolde said. "Im-morra. Do you know what it means?"

Nell's face did something complicated. Children's faces were supposed to be simple — hunger, sleep, joy, grief, all worn openly. But this child's face moved like weather over water, showing depths without revealing them. She pointed one grimy finger at the floor. Then, after a moment, down through the floor. Then further, hand pressing against the boards as if she could feel something beneath the city itself, beneath the mountain, far away.

*Below,* Ysolde wrote on the slate. *Far below.*

The word meant below. It also meant *forgotten*, depending on which of the four fragmentary texts you trusted — and Ysolde had spent two years arguing in footnotes that the distinction mattered, that the old speakers had understood forgetting and depth to be the same act, the same direction. Down was where things went when they were unremembered. The sea was where the world put what it wanted to lose.

She had thought this was poetry. An academic conceit. A pretty observation for a monograph no one would read.

The child pressed her palm flat against the floorboards and said, very clearly, in a voice too old for her throat:

*Vessu-kaal im-morra. Senneth var. Senneth var.*

And then she began to count.

Ysolde recognized the numbers immediately — any student of hers could have recited them. One. Two. Three. The child counted to twelve and stopped, and her whole body went rigid, and she made a sound Ysolde had never heard a human being make: a low, keening note that seemed to come from somewhere behind her ribs, and her eyes rolled back, and for three terrible seconds she was somewhere else entirely.

Then it passed, and Nell blinked, and asked — in accented but perfectly serviceable Common — whether there was anything to eat.

* * *

Astrid Ravensworth arrived at sundown, smelling of horse and rain, and stood in Ysolde's doorway radiating the specific impatience of someone who did not trust scholars, or cities, or doors that closed.

"Well?" the mercenary said.

"You'd better sit down."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to be sitting." Ysolde set out her notes — three days of frantic transcription, pages of phonetics, her working lexicon annotated in margins gone wild. "The child is speaking Old Sundered. Not a creole, not a trader's pidgin, not the liturgical fragments the coastal temples mangled into their drowning rites. The genuine article. Grammatically complete. Fluent."

"That's not possible."

"No," Ysolde agreed. "It isn't. And yet." She pulled the slate toward her and began to write as she talked, because some thoughts only came clear through the hand. "I've catalogued perhaps six hundred words of this language from four surviving texts. The Drowning Psalter. Half a merchant's ledger recovered off Cape Verren. Two funerary steles. Fragments. My life's work is a jigsaw puzzle missing nine-tenths of its pieces." She looked up. "Your child just used words I've never seen. Inflections I theorized might exist and was told I was inventing. She declined a verb form today that I have argued about in journals for six years. She's right. I was right. We both were, and I have never been so unhappy to be correct."

Astrid's jaw tightened. "What is she saying? The words she keeps repeating."

"That's the part you'll want to sit for."

"*Tell me.*"

Ysolde told her.

*Vessu-kaal* — the seal, the binding, the great knot. In the Drowning Psalter it appeared exactly once, in a passage the temple scribes had translated as a blessing: *that which is knotted beneath shall remain knotted.* Pious nonsense, Ysolde had always assumed. But Nell didn't use it as a blessing. She used it the way you'd use the word *dam*. Something holding something else back.

*Im-morra* — below, forgotten, beneath.

*Senneth var* — and here Ysolde's voice actually failed her for a moment, because the phrase appeared in all four texts, always at the end, always unfinished, and she had spent a decade assuming it was a ritual formula, a closing benediction. *Senneth var.* The turning. The turning of the moon.

"She counts to twelve," Ysolde said. "Every time. She counts twelve moons and then she stops and she — " Ysolde gestured vaguely at her own head, inadequate to the task. "She goes elsewhere. And when she comes back, she starts again at one. Except tonight. Tonight she counted thirteen."

The silence in the room had weight. Somewhere below them, the chandler was banking his fires; the smell of hot wax rose through the floorboards, ordinary and warm and utterly beside the point.

"There is no thirteenth moon," Astrid said finally.

"There are thirteen lunar turns between now and midwinter. I checked the almanac twice." Ysolde turned the slate around. On it she had written the translation she'd been avoiding for a full day, the one sitting in her notes like a splinter working toward the heart:

*THE SEAL BELOW IS TURNING LOOSE. EACH MOON THAT TURNS, THE KNOT SLIPS. WHEN THE THIRTEENTH MOON TURNS, THE KNOT FAILS.*

"And before you say it," she added, "yes. I know how it sounds. Yes, I've considered that I'm pattern-matching noise into prophecy — scholars do it constantly, we're vain creatures, we find our life's work in tea leaves. But the grammar is consistent. The tense is future-imperative, which doesn't even exist in Common; it's a construction for *this will happen and nothing can prevent it*. She isn't warning us, Ravensworth. Warnings can be heeded. She's reporting."

"Reporting what?"

"A countdown." Ysolde heard her own voice go thin. "Twelve turns gone. One remaining."

* * *

They worked through the night, because neither of them could imagine sleeping.

Astrid sat by the window with a whetstone and a sword she never quite finished sharpening, and Nell slept curled in Ysolde's reading chair like a cat who had decided the household was acceptable, and Ysolde filled page after page while the candle burned down through its inches.

It was near the black hour when Nell woke screaming.

Not crying — screaming, in Old Sundered, a torrent of it, words pouring out of her faster than Ysolde could follow, faster than anyone could follow, the child bolt upright in the chair with her hands pressed over her ears as though the sound were coming from inside her skull. Astrid reached her first. Ysolde would remember that later: the mercenary moving without hesitation, gathering the flailing child in against armor and scarred leather, murmuring nothing in particular, just sound, just a human voice making human noises until the tide of it receded.

When Nell went still, she was gripping Astrid's wrist hard enough to leave marks. And she said, slowly, deliberately, looking directly at Ysolde as if she knew exactly which of them needed to understand:

*Kaal-senneth morrathen. Vessu-kaal. Im-morra. Senneth var.*

Then, in Common, in a small cracked voice:

"He dreams under the water. He is almost awake. The moon will let him out."

Ysolde's pen had stopped moving. She stared at the child. "Who?" she managed. "Who dreams?"

Nell's brow furrowed — the frustration of a child reaching for a word that lived deeper than vocabulary. She opened her mouth, closed it, then raised one hand and touched, gently, the place above her own heart.

And then she touched the same place on Astrid, and said a single word.

Ysolde knew that word. It appeared twice in the Drowning Psalter, once in a hymn and once in a curse, and the temple translators had rendered it *god* in the hymn and *devil* in the curse, and Ysolde had built an entire unpublished lecture on the contradiction.

The mercenary went absolutely still. Ysolde watched it happen — watched twelve years of professional detachment collide with something older and less scholarly on Astrid Ravensworth's face. Everyone knew the stories about Ravensworth, the way everyone knew stories about famous blades: the mercenary whose god had gone silent mid-prayer a decade ago and never answered again, who still paid tithe to an empty altar, who still wore the god's sigil stamped into their sword pommel like a widow wearing a wedding ring.

"What did she say?" Astrid asked. Very quietly. Very evenly. The voice of someone standing at the edge of something.

Ysolde told her.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell began to ring. Just the hour bell. Just midnight, ordinary and mortal. But Nell lifted her head at the sound, and her grey eyes went wide and fixed, and she began to count again — *one, two, three* — and this time she did not stop at twelve.

She stopped at thirteen. And smiled.

Ysolde did not sleep again that night. At dawn she sent three letters: one to the Collegium archive, one to a former student serving in the Temple of Records, and one — after a long argument with herself that she lost — to the office of the Crown Inquisitors, requesting access to sealed materials pertaining to the Sundered Coast.

She addressed the third letter carefully. Formally. With every courtesy.

Three days later, instead of a reply, riders came through the mountain passes asking questions about a child.

Ysolde Fenwick stood at her window watching the dust of them rise along the far road, and understood, with the cold clarity that comes too late, exactly what she had done — and that the countdown she had transcribed was not the only clock now running.
