## 3. Inquisitors at Thornfell

The bells started at dawn, and Astrid Ravensworth knew before they finished ringing that a decade of careful work was about to burn.

Three bells meant riders on the pass road. Four meant soldiers. Five — five had never been rung in Astrid's lifetime. The old bell-keeper at Thornfell, a half-blind woman named Maren who owed Astrid more money than she'd ever admit, rang five and then kept ringing, wild and arrhythmic, until someone up the mountain made her stop.

Astrid was already dressed. They'd slept in their boots for three nights now, since the child arrived, since the dreams started — not the child's dreams, but Astrid's own, of black water rising through floorboards that didn't exist. They stood at the window of the waystation with a cup of cold barley tea going untouched in their hand and watched the pass road below.

Six riders in grey-and-silver. No banners. Inquisitors traveled without banners the way wolves traveled without invitations.

Behind them came forty crown soldiers in column, pikes catching the early light, and behind *them* came something worse: a wagon with a cage on it, iron-barred, draped in canvas that did nothing to hide what it was. Astrid had seen that wagon once before, eleven years ago, on the coast road south of Vhalen's Reach. They'd been young enough then to believe the thing inside it deserved to be there.

They were not young enough to believe it now.

"You see them." It wasn't a question. The child stood at Astrid's hip, barefoot on cold stone, watching the road with an expression no child should own — flat, ancient, patient. Nell. Astrid had given her the name two nights ago because calling her *girl* or *it* had begun to feel like a wound neither of them could afford. "You see them," Astrid said. "Good. Then you know we're leaving."

Nell said nothing. She rarely spoke anything Astrid understood, though the dead tongue came out of her in her sleep like water finding its old channels. But she reached up and took Astrid's hand, small fingers closing around scarred knuckles, and Astrid felt that grip land somewhere beneath the ribs where no blade had ever reached.

*Don't,* Astrid told themselves. *Don't do this. You've carried contracts, cargo, corpses. You don't carry children.*

The thought lasted exactly as long as it took the lead rider to reach the waystation's outer gate.

* * *

Maren let them in at the front, all apology and cataract-clouded eyes, playing the fool so well that Astrid almost believed her. Astrid used the time to move through the back corridors they'd built into Thornfell's bones over ten years — the smuggler's architecture, false walls and stairways that went nowhere except somewhere else. The child rode against Astrid's chest in a sling meant for contraband, which felt, Astrid thought grimly, about right.

In the cellar behind the third wine rack, old Doss was waiting. Doss had run the mountain routes since before Astrid could grow a beard, a man shaped like a question mark and twice as bent, who knew every goat track from Thornfell to the sea.

"They're asking after a child," Doss said without preamble. "Grey-cloaks have a drawing. Bad likeness, but a likeness." He looked at the bundle on Astrid's chest and his face did something complicated. "That's her, then."

"That's her."

"And you mean to take her over the passes."

"I mean to get her out alive. The passes are one option."

Doss spat. "The passes are the only option, girl, and you know it. Coast road's sealed, river crossings are watched since the moon turned wrong. But hear me." He gripped Astrid's wrist with fingers like root wood. "There are eleven people who can move you through those mountains. Eleven. And every one of them has family, debts, or fears. Grey-cloaks pay in all three currencies. By tomorrow night I'll know which of my eleven are still mine, and by then—"

"By then I'll be gone." Astrid set the child down gently and took Doss's hands in both of theirs. "Old man. Ten years you've covered for me. Ten years of routes and lies and looking the other way. If I take this road, I burn every name you ever gave me. Every contact from here to the Sundered Sea. The network dies with me walking out of it."

Doss was quiet a long moment. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the floorboards — measured, unhurried, the walk of men paid by the day and certain of the night.

"Boy," he said — he'd called Astrid *boy* for twenty years and would call them *boy* for twenty more, whatever the priests said about such things — "when I was younger than you, I moved a family of three through these same passes. Fleeing the Reach purges. Mother, father, a little one with fever. I got them across. All three." His voice caught on nothing visible. "I have spent thirty years telling myself that counts for something. Don't you dare make me spend thirty more learning whether it does. Go. Take the high route past the Widow's Teeth. And take this." He pressed a folded paper into Astrid's palm — names, routes, signs and countersigns, a decade of trust in ink. "Burn it when you're clear. Not before. And Astrid—" he almost smiled, "—the child. When she looks at you like that. That's not gratitude. That's recognition. Gods help you both."

* * *

They nearly died twice before noon.

The first time was at the Widow's Teeth themselves, the twin stone spires where the trail narrowed to a ledge a mule would refuse. Astrid heard them before seeing them — the particular silence of men trying not to be heard — and pressed Nell into a crevice behind a fallen slab with a whispered *don't breathe*, and then four grey-cloaks came around the bend with crossbows slung and a sketch held between them.

Astrid killed none of them. It was close. The nearest man turned at the wrong moment and found Astrid's dagger at his throat instead, and Astrid held him there, feeling his pulse hammer against the flat of the blade, and said, very quietly, "You have a wife in Lowmark. Red hair. I carried her sister's letters for two years. Walk back down this trail and tell your captain you found goat tracks and nothing else, and I will never be a story you tell."

The man walked back down the trail. Whether from fear, mercy, or the letters, Astrid never learned. Astrid's hands shook for a mile afterward, and Nell watched the shaking with that same terrible patience, and finally reached out and put her hand flat against Astrid's forearm until it stopped.

The second time was worse, because it wasn't soldiers. It was Tam Vess.

Tam had run grain and gunpowder through the eastern valleys for as long as Astrid had run anything. He'd taught Astrid how to read a bounty notice for hidden clauses, how to bribe a toll-keeper with flattery instead of coin. He stood in the middle of the high trail with six armed men behind him and tears freezing in his beard, holding the same bad likeness of the child that every grey-cloak in the mountains seemed to carry.

"Astrid." His voice broke on the name. "They took my brother. Two nights ago. They say they'll return him when the vessel is returned to the crown. Astrid, he's *seventeen*."

"I know." Astrid stepped forward slowly, hands open. Behind them, tucked into the rocks, Nell watched. "Tam. Look at me. Your brother is already returned or already dead — the grey-cloaks don't trade, they consume. You know that. You've *seen* that."

"I can't—" His hand shook around the sketch. "Ten years, Astrid. You're my—"

"Don't say it." Something cracked in Astrid's chest, clean as green wood. "If you say it, you'll have to choose, and I'm asking you not to choose. Turn around. Count to a hundred. Tell them you lost the trail in the scree. I'll be gone either way, Tam. The only question is whether you have to live with what you did."

He stood there a long time, snow gathering on his shoulders, an old friend becoming a stranger becoming something else again — and then he turned around, and counted, and shouted to his men that the trail was washed out, and Astrid led the child past him at a distance of twenty feet that might as well have been twenty years.

Nell tugged Astrid's sleeve as they climbed. She pointed back at Tam's diminishing figure, then at Astrid's chest, then made a sound — not the dead tongue this time, but something simpler, older than language. A question.

"No," Astrid said roughly. "It doesn't stop hurting. You just get to decide what it hurts *for*."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, and Astrid laughed — a cracked, unexpected sound, the first in weeks — and the mountains swallowed them both.

* * *

They made the far shelter at dusk: a smuggler's cache carved into the rock above the tree line, stocked with hardtack and lamp oil and a decade of Doss's paranoia. Astrid fed the child dried plums and watched her eat with the focused ferocity of someone who remembered hunger the way other people remembered prayers.

Then Astrid took out Doss's paper and read it by lamplight. Eleven names. Eleven lifelines threaded through the mountains. By morning, grey-cloaks would hold Tam Vess's sketch and Maren's description and the shape of everything Astrid had been. Every name on that page was a door the inquisitors would kick in, hunting them.

Astrid touched the flame of the lamp to the corner and watched a decade of trust curl into ash.

When it was done, Nell was watching. She picked up a fleck of burnt paper from the stone floor, turned it over in her small fingers, and then — deliberately, her eyes on Astrid's the whole time — she spoke.

Not the dead tongue. Common speech, rough and half-formed, a voice like a hinge unused for years:

"Burned... for me."

"Yes," Astrid said.

"Why?"

And Astrid, who had spent a lifetime keeping oaths to gods and crowns and men, and who had watched every one of them go silent or rotten, heard the answer come from somewhere deeper than any oath had ever lived.

"Because some things aren't owed," Astrid said. "Some things you just *do*."

Nell studied them for a long moment. Then she leaned against Astrid's shoulder, heavy with sleep, and murmured something in the drowned language — soft, urgent, repeated twice — and even without Ysolde Fenwick's scholarship, Astrid felt the shape of it settle into their bones like a key turning:

*Thirteen moons. Twelve gone.*

Far below, past the passes, past the sleeping capital, the sea waited. And somewhere behind them, torchlight moved along the high trail, following the ash.
