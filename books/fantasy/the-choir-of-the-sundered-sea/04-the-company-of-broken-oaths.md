## 4. The Company of Broken Oaths

Brannoc had been a soldier for eleven years and a deserter for three, and in all that time he had learned one rule that never failed: when someone asks you to meet them at a crossroads shrine at midnight, bring your own wine.

He brought two bottles. He was glad of it by the time the third hour passed and the shrine's cracked saint stared down at him with her chipped smile, and the road stayed empty, and the wine got better the longer he waited, which was the only kind of miracle he still believed in.

Then Astrid Ravensworth came out of the dark like a debt come due.

They looked worse than Brannoc remembered — thinner, ash-smudged, a fresh burn along one forearm wrapped in dirty linen. But they walked the same way they always had, like the ground owed them passage and knew it. And tucked against their hip, swaddled in a smuggler's oilcloth, was a child.

"Ravensworth." Brannoc stood, joints cracking. "You look like something the tide spat back."

"It did." Astrid glanced at the bottles. "Is that the good stuff or the stuff you drink when you're pretending not to cry?"

"There's a difference?"

"Not with you."

He handed one over anyway. They drank standing up, in the old way, and for a moment it was three years ago and a different war, and neither of them had broken anything yet.

"I need six people," Astrid said. "I have a scholar who talks too much, a child who talks in a dead language, and a price on all our heads. I need people who can fight, people who can lie, and people who don't ask questions until it's safe to answer them."

"That's not six people. That's maybe two."

"I know where to find the rest." Astrid looked at him over the bottle's mouth. "You first."

Brannoc laughed, because the alternative was nothing at all. "You want me on this mad errand. You know what I am."

"I know what you were. A sergeant who wouldn't burn a village because a boy with a seal told him to. I was there, Brannoc. I've never once thought you were wrong."

The words landed harder than he expected. He turned away, looked at the chipped saint, at the child watching him from inside the oilcloth with eyes too old and too pale, like sea-glass.

"What's the job?" he said.

Astrid told him. All of it — the drowned god, the seal, the moons, the inquisitors, the thirteenth moon coming fast as a knife thrown downhill. It should have sounded insane. It didn't, and that frightened Brannoc more than anything, because he had spent three years learning to hear silence where his god used to be, and everything Astrid described fit inside that silence like a hand fits a glove.

"The child," he said finally. "She's the key?"

"She's Nell," Astrid said, in a voice that closed the subject. "Everything else is secondary."

* * *

Sera Ilva found them, rather than the other way around.

They were two days south, holed up in a drover's barn with hay up to their chins, when the former priest of the Drowned Tide walked in through the wrong door — the one facing the cliff — carrying a satchel of stolen relics and a face like a woman who had already done all her crying years ago and found it inefficient.

"You're gathering them," she said, without greeting. "The broken ones. I heard it in Vell's Landing from a man who sells information worse than he sells fish."

"And you came anyway."

"I came because you'll fail without me." She set down the satchel. Inside, Brannoc saw, were relics of a god whose name no one said anymore — a god whose temples had gone quiet, whose priests had scattered or drunk themselves into the sea. "I held the last living congregation of the Silent Order. Forty souls. I watched every one of them stop praying, one by one, because what's the point of prayer when there's no one listening? I kept faith with an empty room for nine years." Her jaw tightened. "If there is even a chance that the silence has a *cause* — that someone took our god's voice — then I will walk into the sea itself before I let that stand."

Nobody asked about the disgrace. Nobody needed to. Brannoc recognized the shape of it: somewhere in her past was an oath, and somewhere beside that oath was the moment she'd chosen truth over obedience, and institutions never forgive that arithmetic.

Nell crawled out of the hay and studied Sera Ilva with that unnerving, ancient stare. Then the child said something — three syllables, soft, in the drowned tongue — and the priest went white as bone.

"What did she say?" Astrid asked.

"I don't—" Sera Ilva stopped. Started again. "It's a blessing. An old one. The kind we used before the Order fell silent." She knelt slowly, like her knees had opinions. "Child, where did you learn—"

Nell just smiled, and patted the priest's head once, the way you'd pat a dog that had finally come home.

* * *

The twins were harder.

Pip and Wren operated out of a flooded quarry two days east, a hole in the world where smugglers stashed goods and worse things stashed smugglers. Getting in required Ysolde Fenwick to recite a passphrase she'd extracted from a drunk fence, getting out would require luck, and both twins met them at the quarry's edge with knives drawn and identical expressions of professional contempt.

"They're children," Sera Ilva whispered, horrified.

"They're nineteen," Brannoc said. "And they've been stealing since before you could hold a censer. Look at their hands."

Pip grinned at them from atop a rusted crane arm. Wren didn't grin. Wren looked at Astrid for a long, long moment, and then at Nell, and something moved behind her eyes that Brannoc couldn't read — recognition, maybe, or its opposite.

"Heard about you," Pip called down. "The mercenary with the magic baby and the crown's whole inquisition on their arse. We're flattered, truly, but we don't do suicide jobs."

"This isn't a job," Astrid said. "There's no pay."

"Even worse!"

"There's this instead." Astrid reached into their coat and drew out a folded paper — a writ, Brannoc saw, bearing a seal that made even the twins go still. A pardon. Full restoration of name and citizenship, signed by someone very high up, offered by someone very desperate to anyone who could deliver the vessel alive.

"We're not delivering her anywhere," Astrid said. "But the inquisitors are offering these to everyone. And I'm offering you the only thing worth more than a pardon." They paused. "A reason."

Wren climbed down. She moved like water finding its level, silent on rusted iron, and stopped an arm's length from Astrid. Up close, Brannoc could see what the twins' swagger usually hid: Wren's wrists bore old scars where manacles had been, and her eyes had the flat patience of someone who had survived something and intended to survive everything else.

"My name was taken when I was eight," she said. "Court record, erased. As far as the kingdom knows, Wren was never born. Pip and I don't exist, which is useful for theft and hell for everything else." She tilted her head. "You're saying if we help you drown a god or save one or whatever this is, we get to exist?"

"I'm saying you already exist. The kingdom's the one lying."

Wren looked at her brother. Pip shrugged elaborately, which between the twins apparently meant *yes, obviously, we were always going.*

"Fine," Wren said. "But I want it noted that this is a terrible idea, and when we die in a flooded cave, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted," Astrid said.

That made five. Ysolde counted herself as six, loudly and twice, and nobody argued, mostly because arguing with Ysolde was like arguing with weather.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of an old watchtower above the river valley, and Brannoc built the fire because building fires was the one thing he still did the army's way, properly, with patience. Nell sat close to the flames and fed them twigs with the solemnity of a priest at an altar. Above them, the moon hung fat and white, twelve nights from full.

"Thirteen moons," Ysolde murmured, sketching calculations in the dirt. "Twelve gone. One left. We have perhaps ten days of travel if nothing goes wrong, and everything will go wrong."

"Cheerful," said Pip.

"I contain multitudes."

It was Sera Ilva who started it, though Brannoc would remember afterward that it felt inevitable, like the fire had been built for exactly this. She was mending a strap on her satchel, and she said, quietly, to no one in particular:

"When the Order fell silent, I made a vow. That I would find out why or die asking. And then I spent nine years breaking that vow, because finding out meant leaving my congregation, and leaving them felt like a second silence." She tied off the strap. "So. That's mine. An oath I broke by keeping it too long in the wrong place."

The fire crackled. Nobody spoke. And then, one by one, the way stones fall, the others followed.

Pip went first, grinning, but the grin didn't reach his eyes. "We swore an oath when we were kids. Both of us, blood and spit, over a stolen loaf. That whatever happened, we'd never let them take each other. Not the magistrates, not the guilds, nobody." He nudged his sister. "Then the Guild of the Pale Hand offered Wren a place, real work, protection, and she took it to keep me fed, and I hated her for a year because I thought she'd chosen them over me. Oath held. Barely. But it cost us everything else."

Wren said nothing for a long time. When she spoke, she watched the fire. "Mine's shorter. I swore I'd never trust anyone who gave me something for free." She glanced at Astrid. "Working on it."

Ysolde cleared her throat. "I swore an oath of scholarship. To record only what can be proven. Then I translated the child's first warning, and I *knew* it was true, and knowing cannot be proven, and I have been living in violation of my discipline ever since." She smiled thinly. "Academia is crueler than any inquisition."

Then they all looked at Brannoc, because the fire was warm and the night was long and there was nowhere left to hide.

He turned his cup in his hands. "Twenty-two years in the king's army. Swore the oath at sixteen, same as everyone. Crown and country, protect the small folk, all of it." He drank. "Third year of the border war, we got orders. Burn a valley. Every farm, every granary. Starve a rebellion out through its children's bellies. And I stood there with a torch in my hand and I thought about the oath — the real one, underneath the words they make you say. *Protect the small folk.*" His voice went rough. "I put the torch down. Told my captain no. Spent a night in irons, and then I walked, and I've been walking for three years, and every winter I wonder if I did the right thing, and every spring I know I did, and neither one of those feelings ever shuts up."

Silence again. But it was a different silence now — not the godless kind, but the kind that comes after something true has been said in company. Brannoc looked around the fire at these strangers: a priest without a god, thieves without names, a scholar without a discipline, a deserter without a country. Broken oaths, every one. And yet sitting here, in the ruin of all their vows, they were somehow more whole than any garrison he'd ever served in.

Nell tugged his sleeve. He looked down. She pointed at the fire, then at each of them in turn — the priest, the twins, the scholar, him, Astrid — and said a word in the drowned tongue. Then she said it again, patiently, the way you'd teach a slow student.

"What's she saying?" Pip whispered.

Ysolde had gone very still. "*Choir,*" she translated softly. "She's calling us a choir. Broken voices, she means. Voices that broke and kept singing anyway."

Above them the moon climbed, one night closer to full, and far off down the valley a dog began to bark — not at wolves, Brannoc thought, not at anything natural. At torchlight moving along the far ridge, patient as a tide.

Astrid rose, unhurried, and looked north.

"Get some sleep," they said. "We ride before dawn. And Brannoc — you'd best teach these people how soldiers move. Because I think we're being followed, and this time I don't intend to run."
