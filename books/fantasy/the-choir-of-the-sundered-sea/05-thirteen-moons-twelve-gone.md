## 5. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Gone

Nell dreamed in the water again.

That was how she thought of it — *the water* — though there was no water anywhere near Thornfell's ruined watchtower except the rain that came through the roof and pooled in the dented copper pan Astrid had set out to catch it. But when she slept, she sank. That was the only word for it. She went down and down through cold green light, past things with too many arms, past towers made of coral and bone, down to a floor of black sand where something enormous waited.

She wasn't afraid of it. Not at first. In the first dreams, before Thornfell, before the fire with all the strangers telling their sad stories, the enormous thing had been kind. It had shown her fish that glowed like fallen stars. It had let her ride on its back through streets where drowned people walked, still going about their days, buying bread from stalls that sold nothing, because bread does not keep underwater and neither do the dead.

But lately the dreams had changed.

Lately there was a crown.

* * *

She woke screaming on the fourth night after the fire.

Astrid was there before her eyes were fully open — Astrid was always there fast, moving quiet for such a big person, knife already out, scanning the dark doorway for whatever had frightened her. When they saw nothing, they put the knife away and sat on the edge of Nell's pallet, which was really just blankets heaped in the corner farthest from the draft.

"Bad dream," Astrid said. Not a question. They'd gotten good at reading her, these last weeks. Or maybe she'd gotten good at being read. The words were coming easier now, the common tongue arranging itself in her mouth like borrowed clothes that were starting to fit.

Nell nodded. Then shook her head. Then did both at once, because it was a bad dream but it was also more than a dream, and she didn't have the words for *more than a dream* in either language.

"Show me," Astrid said.

So she took their hand — rough palm, old scars, a warmth she'd stopped flinching from — and pressed it flat against her own forehead, the way you'd press an ear to a door. And she let the dream come up through her skin.

Astrid went very still.

* * *

In the morning, Ysolde spread out her papers by the fire and made Nell say everything twice: once in the dead tongue, once in common, while the scholar wrote so fast her pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"The crown," Ysolde said. "Describe the crown again."

Nell held her hands over her head, fingers curved, making the shape. "Gold. But wrong gold. Like gold that got sick." She frowned, hunting for it. "It has teeth. Little teeth, all around the inside, and they go into his head."

"Whose head?"

"The sitting man." That was what she called him, because in the dream he was always sitting, always on a chair too big for him, in a room full of tall windows and people bowing. "The tired man. He prays. All day he prays, in my language, but he says the words wrong, like—" she searched, "—like singing a song you heard through a wall."

Ysolde's pen had stopped moving. Brannoc, who was mending a strap by the fire, looked up. Even Pip and Wren, who were supposed to be keeping watch and were instead playing a game of tiles they'd definitely stolen somewhere, went quiet.

"Nell," Ysolde said gently. "The man who prays. What does he pray *to*?"

And here was the part Nell had been carrying alone for three nights, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Because she knew the answer. She'd known it since the first time she dreamed the sitting man, and she hadn't told anyone, because telling would make it real, and if it became real then the enormous thing under the water would know she'd told, and —

But Astrid was watching her. And Astrid had carried her through the snow when she couldn't walk. And Astrid had burned bridges and blown secrets and spent a whole life's worth of favors just to keep her safe, and lying to Astrid felt like lying to the sun.

"He prays to the one behind him," Nell said. "There's someone inside the sitting man. Behind his eyes. And the one inside is—" Her voice wobbled. She pushed through it, the way Astrid had taught her to push through fear on the mountain paths. "*Screaming.*"

"Screaming," Sera Ilva repeated softly.

"Not angry-screaming." Nell wrapped her arms around her own knees. "Trapped-screaming. Like a bird in a jar. And I know its voice." She looked up, straight at Astrid, because this was the worst part and the worst part belonged to them. "It's your god. The one that went quiet. The one you talk to even though it never talks back. It's been talking back the whole time. It's been screaming back. You just couldn't hear it, because it's inside the king, and the king is wearing it like—"

"Like a mask," Astrid said.

Their voice came out strange. Flat and far away, like a voice shouting across a valley. Nell watched their face do something complicated — the hard face cracking in places no one was supposed to see.

"Astrid?" Brannoc said.

"My god answered prayers for thirty years," Astrid said slowly. "Then, eleven years ago, the night the old king died and the new one was crowned — silence. Everyone said the god had abandoned us. Turned its face away. There were priests who—" They stopped. Started again. "I built my whole life on that silence. Every contract, every mile of road, every prayer I stopped saying. And you're telling me it was screaming the entire time?"

"I'm telling you what the child dreams," Ysolde said carefully. "Which may be memory. Which may be the drowned god's own knowledge, leaking through the seal into the nearest vessel it can find." She looked at Nell, and her eyes were wet, and Nell didn't understand why until she spoke again. "You're not dreaming, little one. You're *remembering*. Things that happened before you were born. Things happening right now, in a palace three hundred miles south of here."

"The seal," Brannoc said. He'd set down the strap. "Twelve moons gone. One left. If the crowned thing is feeding on the king's prayers — using the king as a — a spout, a mouthpiece —"

"Then it isn't waiting for the thirteenth moon," Wren said. She'd gone pale under her freckles. Pip's hand had found hers without either twin seeming to notice. "It's getting ready."

* * *

That night Nell dreamed the water again, and this time she tried not to sink.

She dug her fingers into the black sand. She thought about Astrid's hand on her forehead, warm and scarred. She thought about the copper pan catching rain in the ruined tower, and the way Brannoc hummed when he worked, off-key and unashamed, and the way Pip and Wren finished each other's insults, and the way Ysolde said *little one* like it was a title worth having.

*I don't want to see anymore,* she told the enormous thing. *I'm just a kid.*

The enormous thing turned its attention toward her, and even now, even knowing what she knew, some animal part of her wanted to bask in it, the way you'd bask in sun through deep water. It was so *vast*. Its loneliness was vaster.

*Little key,* it said, in the dead tongue, in her grandmother's voice, in the voice of every drowned person walking the flooded streets. *They will take you to the throne. That is why they keep you. That is why they are kind.*

*Nobody keeps me,* Nell said. *I stay because I want to.*

*Kindness is a current,* the thing said. *It carries you whether you consent or no. And at the end of the current is the lock, and the lock is your shape, little key, and the crowned one knows it now. It felt you the moment you began to remember. It is sending its hounds.*

*Hounds?*

*Grey coats. Silver chains. Words like knives folded into paper.* The vastness rippled, and for one terrible moment Nell saw through its eyes: a map of mountains, a red line drawn through the passes toward Thornfell, and figures moving along it — many figures, moving fast, wearing the sunburst badges of the crown's inquisition. *They come for the vessel. They come to put you in a jar of your own, little key, and carry you south, and pour you into the lock.*

Nell woke with her heart slamming and the taste of salt in her mouth, though the nearest sea was weeks away.

The tower was dark. Rain ticked into the copper pan. And downstairs, faintly, she could hear Astrid pacing — the particular heavy-soft rhythm of boots on stone that meant Astrid wasn't sleeping either, was maybe standing at the arrow-slit window looking north, or south, or wherever you look when you're waiting for trouble you can't yet name.

Nell got up. Bare feet on cold rock. Down the stairs, one hand on the wall.

Astrid turned when she came in. Firelight caught the grey in their hair and the question already forming on their face.

"You're crying," Astrid said. "What is it? What did you see?"

Nell wiped her nose on her sleeve. She made herself stand up straight, the way the company stood, the way people stood when they'd sworn themselves to something even if they'd never said the words out loud.

"Hounds," she said. "Coming up the mountain. And Astrid — we have to go *tonight*."
