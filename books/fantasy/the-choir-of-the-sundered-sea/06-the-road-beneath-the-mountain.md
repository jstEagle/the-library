## 6. The Road Beneath the Mountain

Pip had stolen from kings, priests, and one very memorable dowager who kept her fortune in a hollowed-out hymnal, but he'd never stolen from the sea before.

"You're sure this is the way," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question for three days, which was how Brannoc asked questions when he was scared.

Nell stood at the mouth of the old smuggler road with her hand flat against the wet stone, and she said a word in the dead tongue that made Sera Ilva flinch like she'd been struck.

"She says the water remembers us," Ysolde translated softly. "She says it's been waiting."

"Wonderful," said Wren. "I love being expected."

Pip grinned at her because that was his job — grin first, worry later, worry in private where nobody could bill him for it. He and Wren had been twins long enough to know that fear was just excitement wearing a bad coat, and if you laughed at it loud enough it sometimes left without taking anything.

The road went down.

That was the whole truth of it. The smugglers of Thornfell's passes had carved their road beneath the mountain a century ago, back when salt was worth more than silver and the crown's men couldn't be bothered to crawl through the dark after it. Now the dark came to meet you halfway. Torches caught on things that glistened. The air tasted of iron and old fish. And everywhere, everywhere, the sound of water moving in places water had no business being.

"Sea's been creeping inland for years," Brannoc said, crouching at a pool that spread across the road like spilled wine. "My grandfather ran this route. Said you could walk it dry-shod with a candle." He dipped two fingers in, tasted, spat. "Salt."

"The seal is failing," Sera Ilva said. She said it the way she said everything now — quietly, like a woman reading her own last rites. "Every moon, a little more sea gets through. Every moon, a little more of *him* gets through with it."

"Him being the drowned god," Wren said.

"Him being the thing the drowned god became," Ysolde corrected, and then looked sorry she had, because Nell was watching her with those enormous eyes that took in everything and gave back nothing a child should have to carry.

Pip hoisted Nell onto his shoulders. It was practical — the pools were deepening, the child was small, and his neck was free real estate. It was also, though he'd have denied it under torture, because he liked the weight of her. She gripped his hair with both fists like reins and went solemnly wherever he carried her, and somewhere over the past weeks his heart had grown a Nell-shaped room in it that he didn't remember building.

"Right," he said. "Onward and downward. Anyone who drowns does it quietly. I'm trying to concentrate."

* * *

They walked the drowned road for a day and a night, or what passed for them down there, where torches burned low and time went soft as soaked bread.

Nell dreamed twice.

The first time she only whimpered, and Astrid held her until dawn-or-what-passed-for-it, and in the morning the girl drew shapes in the silt: a spiral, a door, a crown. Ysolde copied them into her journal with hands that weren't quite steady.

The second time she screamed.

It tore out of her like something escaping, high and wrong, and every torch in the cavern guttered at once as if the flame itself had turned to look. When they got her calmed — Astrid murmuring, Sera Ilva singing some half-remembered hymn in a language even older than Nell's — the girl grabbed Ysolde's sleeve and spoke fast and desperate, a flood of the dead tongue, and Ysolde listened with her face going greyer by the word.

"What?" Astrid demanded. "What did she say?"

Ysolde swallowed. "She dreamed the throne room again. But this time there were people in it. Soldiers in white and grey." Her eyes came up. "Inquisitors. She says they're already below us. She says they've been walking this road since before we entered it."

Silence, except the water, always the water.

"They knew about the road," Brannoc said flatly. "Which means someone talked."

Wren went very still. Pip saw it — he always saw it, twin-sight, the little tells no one else caught — the way her hand drifted toward her belt knife and stopped, the way her breathing changed. He filed it away in the locked drawer where he kept things he wasn't ready to know.

"No one talked," Astrid said. "They've been hunting the vessel since Thornfell. They'd find the old routes eventually. We move faster, we move quieter, and we get ahead of them." A pause. "Nobody accuses anybody. Not down here. Down here we're all each other has."

But Pip watched Wren not look at anyone, and he kept the drawer locked, and hated himself a little for locking it.

* * *

The ambush came at the flooded gallery, where the road widened into a cathedral of stone and the ceiling hung with stalactites like the ribs of some enormous drowned beast. The water here was waist-deep and black and utterly still, and stillness, Pip had learned young, was the most expensive thing in any market — because somebody always paid for it.

He felt it before he heard it. A wrongness in the echo. His own splash coming back a half-beat late, doubled.

"*Down,*" he shouted, and the world exploded.

Crossbow bolts hissed out of the dark galleries above. Brannoc roared and hauled Nell behind his shield like a turtle hauling its shell. Astrid moved the way Astrid moved — no wasted motion, blade out and body already answering — and then the white-and-grey shapes came down the far slope, wading, six of them, seven, chainmail glinting under the torchlight like fish scales.

"Inquisitors!" Ysolde's voice cracked. "They're herding us toward—"

Toward the deep channel. Pip saw it a heartbeat too late: the gallery floor shelved away on their side, dropping off into water black and bottomless, and the inquisitors' whole plan was simply to push. No need to kill a company when the mountain would do it for you.

A bolt took Brannoc in the shoulder and spun him; Astrid caught him by the collar. Wren was already swimming for the far shelf, knife in her teeth, and Pip — Pip had Nell's wrist and no footing and the channel sucking at his legs like a patient, enormous mouth.

Then Sera Ilva stepped forward into the deepest water, raised both arms, and began to pray.

Not quietly. Not like a woman reading last rites. She prayed the way she must have prayed before the disgrace, before the exile, with her whole ruined voice, in the old liturgy of the silent god, and the words rolled across the black water and *stuck* to it. Pip felt it in his teeth. The channel's pull faltered. The inquisitors staggered as if the water had thickened around their legs.

"SERA!" Astrid bellowed. "Swim! *Now!*"

She turned, and smiled at him — at all of them, at the ragged oath-broken lot of them she'd confessed her sins beside on the first night, when strangers became something like family — and Pip understood, with a thief's instant arithmetic, exactly what she was doing.

Prayer was a door, she'd told them once. You opened it from your side. Somebody had to hold it open.

"Ilva, *no*—"

"The god is not silent down here," she said, and her voice was already changing, going strange and resonant, as if something vast had begun to speak through her the way wind speaks through a reed. "Can't you hear it? It was never gone. It was only ever *waiting*—" 

The water rose up.

Not violently. That was the terrible part. It rose the way a hand rises to cup a candle flame, gently, deliberately, and closed over her, and the prayer went on for three more seconds from underneath the surface, resonant and calm, and then stopped.

The channel went still. The inquisitors fled — actually fled, scrambling back up the slope, and Pip would think about that later, how the king's own hunters ran from what they'd helped wake.

Astrid dove. Brannoc dove. Pip dove, though he swam like a cat and knew it. They searched the black water until their lungs burned and their hands went numb, and found nothing but cold, and the silence, and the silence, and the silence.

When they finally dragged themselves onto the far shelf, Nell crawled to the water's edge and put her small hand on the surface. She spoke one word of the dead tongue, very softly.

Ysolde translated it with tears running freely down her face. "*Given.*"

* * *

They burned nothing, because there was nothing to burn. They buried her as best they could — a cairn of wet stones at the gallery's edge, above the waterline, with Sera Ilva's broken prayer-beads laid on top. Astrid stood over it a long time without speaking, and Pip, who made his living reading faces, read nothing there at all, and that frightened him more than the ambush had.

It was Nell who finally moved. She pressed her palm flat against the cairn, the way she'd pressed it against the mountain's mouth, and said something long and low and flowing.

"What is it, love?" Ysolde whispered.

The girl looked up at Astrid. At all of them. And answered in trade-tongue, her first full sentence in it, rough and halting and absolutely clear:

"She is not gone. She is *ahead*. Walking in the water. She says—" Nell frowned, hunting for the words, and found them. "She says the door is open now. She says hurry."

Pip looked at the black channel, at the road descending beyond it into dark deeper than any dark he'd ever picked a pocket in, and felt the grief in his chest turn over and harden into something with an edge.

"Well," he said, and his voice only shook a little. "You heard the lady. Hurry."

Behind them, far up the flooded gallery, something in the water sighed — long, vast, and unmistakably awake — and the company of broken oaths went down together into the dark.
