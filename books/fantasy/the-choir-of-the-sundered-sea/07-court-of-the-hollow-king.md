## 7. Court of the Hollow King

Ysolde Fenwick had spent her whole life wanting to be wrong.

It was a strange ambition for a scholar, and she had never confessed it to anyone — not to the colleagues who'd watched her expelled from the Athenaeum of Corvane, not to Astrid, who thought her a woman of unshakable nerve. But it was true. Every discovery she had ever made had cost someone something, and so she had learned to pray, in her dry academic way, that each new theory would collapse under scrutiny like a bad translation.

Standing in the gallery above the throne room of Vael Corath, watching the King of the Sundered Coast recite prayers in a language dead for nine hundred years, Ysolde wanted very badly to be wrong.

She was not wrong.

* * *

They had entered the capital three days before, folded into a river barge loaded with salted eels and cheap wine, hidden beneath tarps that stank of brine and Pip's complaints. The plan was simple, as Brannoc laid it out: cross the city by night, buy passage on a coastal trader out of the harbor at Greywater Stair, and be gone before the thirteenth moon rose. The drowned throne lay two days' sail south, past the teeth of the Sundered Reef. Simple. A smuggler's errand with extra steps.

But Nell had woken on the second night screaming words none of them understood except Ysolde, who sat up with her heart hammering because she did understand them now, all of them, the child's tongue having colonized her dreams the way ivy colonizes a wall.

*The crown is hungry,* Nell had said, in the old speech, tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face. *The crown is hungry and it eats from his mouth.*

Astrid had held the girl until she slept again, and then looked across the fire at Ysolde with an expression that asked a question neither of them wanted answered.

So they had changed the plan. Which is how Ysolde came to be crouched behind a screen of carved alabaster in the servants' gallery of the royal chapel-palace, wearing a laundress's smock three sizes too big, watching a king feed himself to something.

* * *

The throne room had been built as a place of worship once; you could see it in the bones of the architecture, in the ribs of stone arcing overhead like the inside of a whale, in the way every line of the floor drew the eye toward the dais. Later kings had gilded it, armored it, hung banners from the vaulting. But the last king — Aldric IV, the Peaceful, the Pious, the man whose face was stamped on every coin Ysolde had ever counted — had stripped it bare. No banners. No courtiers. Just the empty hall, the empty throne, and the king kneeling before both, alone, at midnight, praying.

His voice carried in the vastness, thin and worn as an old rope.

Ysolde knew the language he spoke. She had spent eleven years cataloguing its fragments — grave-goods inscriptions, the cracked bell of Harrowmere, half a hymn preserved in a monastery marginalia. It was called Seimhach in the oldest texts, though no one alive could say what the word meant. The tongue of the people who had lived along the Sundered Sea before the sea took them. The tongue of the drowned god's first congregation.

And here it was, pouring from the mouth of a living king, fluent and perfect and wrong.

Because he didn't know what he was saying. That was the horror of it, the thing that made Ysolde's hands shake against the alabaster. She had expected possession, perhaps — some theatrical thing, black eyes, a voice like a landslide. Instead there was only a tired man in a plain white shift, kneeling on cold stone, reciting syllables he could not possibly understand with the blank fluency of a sleepwalker. His eyes were open. They were the pale, unfocused eyes of a man looking at nothing, or at something very far away, or at something looking back at him through the back of his own skull.

Beside her in the dark, Astrid breathed slowly and deliberately, the way they did when they were keeping themselves still by force. Below, wedged between a pillar and a cistern where no guard would think to look, Nell was supposed to be sleeping under Wren's watchful arm. Ysolde hoped she was. The child heard this language in her bones, and Ysolde could not bear to imagine what it sounded like, coming from that mouth.

* * *

She had translated the prayers already. That was the worst part. She had done it two nights ago, by candlelight, working from the fragments Nell murmured in her sleep, and when she finished she had closed her notebook and sat very still for a long time, because there was no way to unknow it.

It was a litany of offering. An old one — she recognized the structure from funerary texts, the call-and-response shape of a rite meant to sustain the dead through their long journey. *We give you breath. We give you name. We give you the warmth of our remembering.* Except this version had been inverted, corrupted, turned inside out like a glove. It was not the congregation feeding the dead.

It was the dead feeding.

Every word the king spoke was a small surrender. Breath, given. Name, given. Warmth, memory, will — given, given, given. And something below the palace, below the city, below the seabed itself, drank each gift down through whatever channel connected a hollow man on a throne to a drowned god under the sea.

*He doesn't know,* Ysolde had written in her notebook, and underlined it twice, and hated how much it sounded like an excuse.

* * *

The king finished the litany. He rose — stiffly, like an old man, though he couldn't have been past fifty — and stood before the empty throne for a long moment. And then he did something that made Astrid's hand close around Ysolde's wrist hard enough to bruise.

He wept.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. He simply stood there in the vast dark hall and cried the way men cry when no one is permitted to see them: silently, helplessly, his shoulders shaking, tears running off his jaw onto the white shift. And as he wept, his lips kept moving, whispering — and Ysolde caught the words, because they were not Seimhach anymore but plain coastal common, the language of markets and kitchens and mothers.

*"I don't want to,"* the king whispered. *"Please. I don't want to. Let me stop. Please let me stop."*

Then his spine straightened, his face went smooth and vacant as a washed slate, and he knelt again and began the litany over from the beginning.

Ysolde discovered she was crying too. She wiped her face angrily with the sleeve of the stolen smock. Beside her, Astrid stared down at the kneeling king with an expression Ysolde had seen only once before — on the night they'd told her about the god that had gone silent, the mercenary's god, the one whose absence had hollowed them out year by year like rot in a beam.

"You recognize it," Ysolde whispered.

"The silence." Astrid's voice was gravel. "That's what it looks like from the outside. I always wondered." A pause. "I prayed into that silence for six years and thought my god had abandoned me. Thought I'd failed some test. Thought—" They stopped. Swallowed. "That thing down there isn't just eating him. It's *wearing* him. And he's awake inside somewhere. He's awake and he can't stop."

"The litany doesn't just feed it," Ysolde said. Her scholar's mind was moving now, cold and fast, laying pieces side by side the way she laid out manuscript fragments. "It maintains something. A seal has to be fed or it fails — that's what Nell's been saying since the beginning, that's why the moons matter. The crown isn't the prison. The crown is the *lock mechanism*. The king is the keyhole, and the thing beneath the sea has threaded itself through him like thread through a needle. If he dies—"

"The seal breaks."

"If he dies, if he stops praying, if anyone cuts that thread carelessly—" She looked at Astrid. "Everything your inquisitors have done, everything the crown has hunted Nell for — they're not trying to free the drowned god. They're trying to keep the meal coming. Because the thing on the throne and the thing under the sea are the same creature, and it has arranged itself a kingdom that worships it without knowing, guarded by soldiers who think they're protecting the realm."

Below them, the king began the litany a third time.

* * *

They should have left then. That was the plan — see, understand, withdraw. Brannoc would be waiting at the eel-house with the twins. Every minute in the palace was a minute closer to the dawn patrols, the changing of the guard, the hundred small chances of discovery.

But Ysolde lingered one moment longer, because the scholar in her wanted one more look at the throne itself — and in that moment she saw it.

From the gallery, at this angle, with the moonlight falling through the high windows, the empty throne cast a shadow. Thrones cast shadows; that was nothing. But the shadow on the dais floor did not match the throne. It was larger. It was shaped wrong — a sprawl of darkness like a drowned man's hair spreading through water, like fingers, like roots, and it moved. Slowly. Breathing.

And at its center, where the shadow pooled thickest, Ysolde saw — or thought she saw, and would spend the rest of her life certain she had seen — a second figure seated within the king's shadow. Small. Waiting. Patient as tide.

Its head turned. It looked up at the gallery.

Ysolde stumbled back from the alabaster screen, heart slamming, and grabbed Astrid's arm. "We have to go. Now. It saw me. Astrid — *it saw me.*"

They went, fast and silent, down the servants' stair, through the laundry corridors that smelled of lye and steam, out through the gate Wren had bribed open with a purse of clipped coins. Only when they were three streets away, swallowed by the sleeping city, did Ysolde realize she was still clutching her notebook, and that her hand had torn a page nearly in half.

At the eel-house, Brannoc read their faces and didn't ask. Pip poured four cups of raw spirit. Nell sat up among the sacking, wide awake, and looked at Ysolde with those ancient eyes and said, in the dead tongue, softly:

*You saw it looking back.*

"Yes," Ysolde said, in the same tongue, her accent terrible and the child's solemn nod forgiving her anyway. "Yes, I did."

Nell reached out and took Ysolde's hand, small fingers closing around hers, and said something else — and this time the words were new, words Ysolde had never translated, words that arrived in her understanding whole and terrible:

*It knows your name now. All of yours. We must reach the water before the moon, or it will start calling.*

Outside, over the rooftops of Vael Corath, the twelfth moon was setting — and somewhere beyond the reef, the thirteenth was already rising, dark and patient, waiting for its turn.
