## 8. The Betrayal of Names

The inquisitor found Wren at the well in Harrowmere, where she'd gone alone to fill the water skins and lie to herself about not being afraid.

She knew what he was before he said a word. Inquisitors dressed like clerks — grey wool, soft boots, ink-stained fingers — but they stood like men who had never once needed to run. This one was young, with a kind face that made it worse.

"Wren," he said.

Not *girl*. Not *thief*. Her name, spoken gently, the way you'd speak to a bird you didn't want to startle.

"That's not my name," she said. "That's the name they gave me."

"I know." He set a leather folio on the well's stone lip and opened it. Inside, in a clerk's careful hand: a ledger page. A line item. *One female child, age six, sold to the Guild of Quiet Hands, name surrendered per contract.* Below it, in faded red wax, her mother's thumbprint.

Wren's mouth went dry. She'd spent eleven years telling herself there had never been a name to lose. That Pip and she had been born blank, like coins worn smooth. And here it was, in government ink, proof that she had been someone before she was property.

"Names can be restored," the inquisitor said. "By writ of the Crown. Your sister's too. You'd be *someone* again. Legally. In every church record, every toll book, every door that's ever been shut to you."

"And what does the Crown want for it?"

He smiled, sad and patient. "You know the route your company is taking through the Greymarsh. That's all. No one will be hurt. We only need to find the vessel before others do — bad men, worse than us — and keep it safe."

*Keep it safe.* Everyone who wanted Nell used those words. Wren had learned by now that safe was the most dangerous word in any language.

She looked at the ledger page a long time. At the thumbprint. Her mother had pressed it there, or someone had pressed her mother's hand there; after eleven years she couldn't tell the difference anymore, and maybe there wasn't one.

"The marsh road," Wren said. "Past the drowned chapel. There's a causeway under the waterline — old smuggler work. Astrid means to take it tomorrow night, at low tide."

The inquisitor wrote it down. Then he tore out the ledger page with her name on it and held it out.

"This stays with us until the writ is signed. Come to the chapel of Saint Verren in three days. Bring nothing. You'll leave with everything."

Wren took the page because her hands were already moving, thief's hands, hands that took things before her heart could vote. Then she walked back to the inn with water slopping over her knuckles and the paper burning a hole against her ribs, and she did not tell anyone, and she hated how easy it had been, how natural, like a door she'd been walking past her whole life and had simply never pushed.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. Pip slept beside her the way she always did, curled tight as a fist, and Wren lay listening to her breathe and doing arithmetic she couldn't finish.

A name. A legal name. Doors opening. Pip saying *yes, that's me* at last instead of flinching when clerks asked. Wasn't that worth one road? One route? The inquisitors hadn't even wanted Nell hurt. They'd said *safe*. Maybe they really would keep her safe. Maybe the crown was the only thing big enough to protect a child everyone else wanted to use.

Maybe.

Or maybe Wren just wanted it to be true, because wanting it made the theft smaller.

Near dawn she heard Astrid moving downstairs, checking the windows the way they always did, and she heard them pause outside the room she shared with Pip, and stand there a moment, and move on. Trusting. That was the thing about Astrid nobody believed until they saw it: a mercenary with a dead god and a decade of blood debts, and they trusted like a man with no enemies left to make.

Wren got up before light and went to Saint Verren's.

* * *

The chapel was wrong.

She understood it in pieces, the way you understand a rigged game — first the small things, then the shape of the whole cheat. The inquisitors waiting inside weren't in grey wool. They wore white now, ceremonial, and the chapel's saint had been draped in cloth, and beneath the cloth the statue's face had been chiseled away. Not broken. *Removed.* Carefully, lovingly, the way you'd remove a mask.

And they were praying. A dozen of them, kneeling, reciting something in a language Wren didn't know — except she did know it, a little, from Nell. The child murmured it in her sleep sometimes, words like water over stones. Nell's dead tongue. The tongue of the Sundered Sea.

They were praying to the thing under the sea. The thing the seal held back. The thing the king fed with prayers from his hollow throne.

Inquisitors served the crown. Wren had assumed that meant the king. But standing in that stripped chapel, listening to grown men chant the drowning god's name, she understood: the crown was just the handle. Something else held the blade.

An older inquisitor detached from the kneelers and came to her, holding a folded writ. "Your name, restored," he said warmly. "Both sisters. The Crown is generous to its friends."

"Who is 'the Crown'?" Wren asked. "Really."

The warmth didn't falter, which was its own answer. "The Crown is eternal," he said. "Kings are temporary. Sign here."

He turned the writ around. And there, at the bottom, where her mother's thumbprint had been in the ledger — where her own name should go — the space wasn't blank. It was pre-filled. Written in that same clerk's careful hand, in ink that shimmered faintly wrong, like oil on water:

*Wren, daughter of the Drowned, given back to the sea.*

Not restored. *Returned.* They weren't giving her a name. They were writing her into the god's ledger, the way her mother's thumbprint had once written her into the Guild's. Every name in this kingdom was a chain, and the inquisitors were simply the link-smiths, and the thing wearing the king collected the chains.

"You lied," Wren said.

"We told you exactly what we're doing," the old inquisitor said, still kind, still patient. "We're returning stolen property. All of it. The vessel. The names. Everything the Sundered Sea lost when it was sealed away. You'd be part of putting the world right."

Behind him, the faceless saint watched with its absence of eyes, and Wren thought about Nell asleep against Ysolde's shoulder, murmuring warnings in a language older than sin. About Astrid standing outside their door each night. About Brannoc confessing his desertion with his head down like a man expecting a blow. About Sera Ilva, dead in the flooded dark so the rest of them could walk.

They hadn't put the world right. None of them. They'd just failed honestly, together, and somehow that had become the truest thing Wren had ever belonged to.

"No," she said.

The kindness finally dropped. "You've already given us the route."

"Then you don't need me." She backed toward the door. "But hear this for free: the causeway floods an hour before high tide, not after. If your men take it at the time I gave you, the sea takes them. That's not a warning. That's arithmetic."

She ran. Behind her, no one followed — they had a route, they had a schedule, they had a god to serve, and gods' servants never chase what they believe is already caught.

* * *

She came back to the inn as the sun cleared the rooftops, soaked in sweat, and found them all at breakfast like a painting of a family: Astrid sharpening a knife that didn't need it, Brannoc eating enough for three, Ysolde annotating a map, Nell drawing in spilled porridge, Pip watching the door because Pip always watched the door.

Pip saw her face first. "What did you do?"

Wren stood in the doorway with river-mud still on her boots and said it plainly, because she'd learned on the walk back that plainness was the only coin she had left.

"I sold your route to the inquisitors. For our names. It was a trap and I walked into it and then I walked out, and now they're coming, and I'm sorry, and I'll spend the rest of my life being sorry if you let me stay."

Silence. The knife stopped moving under Astrid's whetstone.

It was Nell who reacted first. The child slid off her bench, crossed the room, and took Wren's hand — the one still clutching the torn ledger page, crumpled now, her mother's thumbprint crushed to a smudge. Nell looked at it, then up at Wren, and said something in the dead tongue, soft, and Ysolde translated in a voice gone rough:

"She says: *a name taken is a wound. A name given is a door. You gave yours back.*"

"They were going to give it *to the sea*," Wren said. "To him. To the thing under—"

"I know what they were going to do." Astrid rose. Their face was unreadable, which from Astrid meant fury being kept on a very short leash. "I want to hit something. Instead I'm going to ask you one question, and your whole life depends on the answer. Why did you come back?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Thought about the faceless saint, the chanting, the pre-written name. Thought about Pip asleep beside her, curled like a fist.

"Because they told the truth," she said finally, "and the truth was worse than any lie. And because—" Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Because you people are the only ones who ever let me fail without charging me for it."

Astrid looked at her a long moment. Then they picked up the map from under Ysolde's elbow and tore it in half.

"Good," Astrid said. "Because the route you sold them was already dead. We changed it two days ago — you'd have known if you'd been at supper instead of skulking at wells." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "You betrayed us to a lie, Wren. Congratulations. You're the only honest one among us."

Brannoc laughed, sudden and huge, and pulled out a chair. Pip didn't laugh. Pip crossed the room, took Wren's other hand, and squeezed it hard enough to hurt, which between the twins had always meant *forgiven*, and also *never again*, and also *I love you*, all in one grip.

"Now," Astrid said, spreading the ruined halves of the map flat, "the inquisitors will be on the marsh road tonight, hunting a causeway that isn't there. Which means the real road — the deep one, under the mountain, the one Nell's dreams have been screaming about — will be unguarded." They looked around the table, at each of them, lingering last on Wren. "Three days to the coast. Thirteen days to the thirteenth moon. Eat fast."

Nell tugged Wren's sleeve. When Wren bent down, the child whispered — not in the dead tongue this time, but in plain trade-speech, learned word by word over weeks of fireside lessons:

"He knows your name now. The one under the sea. He says he's keeping it."

Wren's blood went cold. But Astrid was already shouldering their pack, and outside the window the thirteenth moon's pale twin hung visible even in daylight, thin as a paring of bone.

"Then he can keep it," Wren said, and followed her family out the door.
