## 9. The Drowned Throne

The sea did not want them there.

Astrid felt it in the water itself — a pressure against the chest, a cold that climbed through boot leather and mail and settled behind the ribs like a hand. The smuggler road had ended a mile back, drowned in black water, and now the company waded shoulder-deep through the flooded cavern while the thirteenth moon bled its light down through cracks in the rock above.

Nell rode on Brannoc's shoulders, small fists knotted in his hair, humming the dead tongue under her breath. The song had changed since the capital. It was no longer a warning. Astrid couldn't have said how they knew that, only that the tune had the shape of a door opening.

"Air pocket ahead," Ysolde called from the point, her voice thin and strange in the cavern's throat. She held her lantern high, and the light caught on stone pillars rising out of the water like the ribs of something enormous. "The dreams show stairs beyond. Dry stairs."

"And after the stairs?" Pip asked. He was trying for levity and missing by a mile. Wren swam beside him, one arm hooked through his pack strap, and she didn't joke at all. She hadn't since Thornfell. Since the inquisitors' camp and the name they'd offered her and the thing she'd learned wearing their faces.

"After the stairs," Ysolde said quietly, "the throne."

They climbed. The water fell away in stages, and then they were walking, dripping, through a corridor of worked stone so old the carvings had worn to suggestions — figures with crowns of kelp, figures kneeling, figures with their mouths open in what might have been song or screaming. Sera Ilva would have known. Sera Ilva would have read every glyph aloud and wept over half of them. Astrid touched the hilt of their sword where the priest's blessing-charm still hung and kept walking.

The corridor ended at a wall of water.

Not falling. Standing. A curtain of it, floor to ceiling, black as ink and moving without sound, filling the archway like a held breath. Beyond it, faint as memory, gold light pulsed.

"That's not possible," Brannoc said. The deserter's hands were steady on Nell's ankles, but his voice wasn't. "Water doesn't stand."

"It does here," Nell said.

It was the longest sentence she'd spoken in weeks. Her voice came out layered, doubled — the child's clear pipe underneath, and beneath *that*, something vast and patient and drowning. Astrid's god had gone silent seven years ago, mid-prayer, mid-sentence, and Astrid had spent those years telling themselves the silence meant absence. Standing before the standing water, hearing that doubled voice, they understood for the first time that the silence had never been absence at all.

It had been captivity.

"Nell," Astrid said, crouching to her eye level. "When we go through — whose eyes are you seeing with?"

The child looked at them with an expression far too old for her face. "Both," she said. "That's why I'm the key." Then, smaller, just Nell: "Don't let go of me. Whoever's talking."

"I won't."

* * *

The water parted around them like a wound closing, and the throne room took them.

It was vast beyond sense — a hall drowned and yet breathable, the air salt-thick but real, the ceiling lost in darkness where pale shapes drifted like jellyfish or souls. Gold light came from the throne itself: a chair of coral and drowned iron on a dais of black stone, and upon it sat the king.

Except it was not the king. Not anymore. The body wore the crown and the royal face Astrid had seen on coin and wanted poster both, but the face had gone soft and hollow, wax melting slowly around something underneath. The king's mouth moved. Prayers, in the dead tongue. The same words Nell hummed. And with each syllable the gold light pulsed brighter, and the drifting shapes overhead drifted closer, and Astrid felt the pressure return — the sea leaning in to feed.

"He doesn't know," Ysolde whispered. "Gods below, he's been praying himself into it for thirty years. He thinks he's devout."

"He is devout," said the king's mouth, without the king's voice.

The crowned thing rose. It moved wrong, joints lagging a half-beat behind intention, like a puppet remembering its strings. The hollow eyes found Nell first, and hunger moved across the ruined face like weather. Then they found Astrid, and stopped.

And the voice that came out was not the thing's voice either.

*"Ravensworth."*

Seven years of silence. Seven years of prayers thrown into a well with no bottom. Astrid's knees nearly went. They locked them. Drew steel. Around them the company spread into a crescent — Brannoc with his axe, Pip and Wren flanking, Ysolde clutching her translations like a shield, Nell gripping Astrid's belt with both fists.

"You're in there," Astrid said. Their voice cracked and they let it. "You've been in there. All this time. Inside *him*."

*"Inside the crown,"* the god said through the king's mouth, and the king's face wept seawater without the king knowing. *"The crown is the seal. They built it wrong, Ravensworth. Your ancestors. They meant to bind the drowned one, and they bound me instead — bound me into the binding, made me the lock, and set the lock on a living head. Every king dies. Every heir is crowned. I pass from prison to prison, praying through their mouths for someone to break me free, and every prayer I speak feeds the thing I'm holding shut."*

The horror of it landed slow, the way deep water lands. Astrid thought of every silent year. Of the altar dusted and relit and dusted again. Of cursing a god for abandonment.

"You could have told us. Somehow. A dream. A sign."

*"I tried. The drowned one eats my words. Everything I am goes into holding this seal, and what leaks through comes out as silence."* The hollow eyes closed. Opened. *"Until her."* The gaze slid to Nell. *"She was made to be the next key. The vessel. When the thirteenth moon sets, the seal turns, and either she takes my place — a child chained to a crown forever — or the drowned one rises, and the Sundered Sea comes inland until nothing dry remains."*

"No third option?" Wren asked bitterly. "There's always a third option. We've all broken oaths finding them."

The god was quiet a long moment. The gold light dimmed, steadied, dimmed.

*"There is a third option,"* it said at last, and Astrid heard, beneath the vastness, something that sounded almost like shame. *"But it is not free. And it must be given, not taken. That is the oldest law, older than the seal: a binding holds until someone pays it a self. A name, freely surrendered. A whole life, poured into the lock. Then the prisoner walks out and the payer—"*

It stopped.

"The payer what?" Astrid said.

*"I don't know,"* the god said. *"No one has ever done it."*

* * *

The moonlight through the ceiling-cracks was turning from silver to red. The thirteenth moon, setting. The drifting shapes overhead had begun to descend, slow as snowfall, and where they touched the water the water boiled silently.

Brannoc spoke first. "We fight. We take the crown off his head and we run."

"You touch the crown, the seal breaks unbound," Ysolde said. "Unpaid. The drowned one rises *now*, with no lock at all. My translations are clear."

"Then we put Nell on the throne," Pip said, and immediately looked sick for saying it. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm thinking out loud—"

"You're not putting anyone on any throne," Astrid said, and heard their own voice go flat and certain, the mercenary voice, the contract voice. Except this was no contract. This was the opposite of every contract they'd ever signed.

They looked at the god wearing the king. Their god. Silent seven years, imprisoned thirty times thirty more, begging through a stranger's mouth. And they looked at the king himself — the man inside the mask, hollow-eyed, weeping salt he didn't feel, who had prayed himself into damnation believing it was devotion. Two prisoners. One crown.

*Astrid Ravensworth.* The name their mother gave them. The name sworn over altars and contracts and graves. The name that had meant something once, before the silence, before the road, before a mud-soaked child had knocked on their door speaking the end of the world.

If a name could open the lock — then a name could also be the key turned willingly. Not the child. Not another chain. An ending.

"Sera Ilva used to say," Astrid said quietly, "that oaths are just names we give away in advance." They drew the blessing-charm from their sword hilt. Kissed it, the way the priest had taught them, the one time they'd ever allowed it. "I'm done giving my name away to things that don't answer."

They stepped forward onto the dais.

"Astrid—" Brannoc's hand on their arm.

"Don't follow me up. Any of you. Whatever happens, keep hold of Nell."

The crowned thing watched them come. Through the king's mouth, their god whispered, *"You don't know what you'd be paying. You don't know what's left after."*

"No," Astrid agreed. "Same as when I took my first contract. Same as every day since your silence." They reached the throne. Looked up into the hollow eyes, and past them, deep down, at the flicker that had answered prayers once — that had *known* them, before. "But I know what's left if I don't. And I'm tired, old friend. I'm so tired of talking to a well."

The red moonlight touched the crown.

Astrid put both hands on the sides of the king's face — gently, the way you'd steady a frightened animal — leaned close, and gave the god their name.

Not spoke it. *Gave* it. Poured it out the way you'd pour out blood, the way you'd pour out everything, mother-tongue and oath-name and the word strangers shouted across battlefields, all of it flowing out through the palms and into the seal—

—and the crown screamed.

Gold light went white. The standing water in the doorway collapsed inward. Somewhere very far away Astrid heard Nell singing, loud now, triumphant, the dead tongue alive in her mouth, and heard the god's voice rise out of the king like a diver breaking surface — vast, freed, *speaking* —

—and Astrid Ravensworth felt their own name dissolve like salt in the sea, and did not know, anymore, who was kneeling on the dais, or why the drowned throne was glowing, or why the child was running toward them crying a word that used to mean *home* —

The last of the red moonlight went out.

In the dark, something ancient drew its first free breath in three thousand years, and said, in a brand-new voice:

"*Thank you.*"

Then the sea stood up.
