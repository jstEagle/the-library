## 10. A Name Given Freely

Nell had never seen the sea be still.

It was still now. The thirteenth moon hung fat and white above the drowned throne room, and the water that filled the great hall lay flat as poured glass, holding the moon whole in its surface like a coin held in a palm. Nell stood on the last dry stair with Brannoc's huge hand resting on her shoulder, and she looked down at the moon floating there, and she thought: *it is waiting to be spent.*

The words came to her in the dead tongue first, then in the living one, the way all her thoughts came now. Two languages braided together, like two streams running into one riverbed.

Below, at the edge of the flooded hall, Astrid Ravensworth stood ankle-deep in the still water. The crowned thing sat upon its throne of black coral at the center — the king's body, the king's face, but the eyes were wrong, deep as wells, and behind them something vast turned over and over like a whale circling a cage too small for it.

*Let me out,* it said through the king's mouth, in the king's voice, which was the worst part. *Little soldier. Little oath-keeper. You prayed to me for ten years. You starved yourself on my silence. Give me the crown and I will give you back your god.*

Nell felt Ysolde's fingers tighten around her own. The scholar was crying without any noise at all, tears sliding down her face into her open mouth, and she did not taste them.

Astrid said nothing. Astrid drew their sword — not to fight, Nell understood suddenly, but because some people need their hands full when they are about to do the bravest thing they will ever do — and waded forward into the glass-still water, step by step, until the moon broke apart around their legs.

"No," Astrid said. "You don't get my crown. You don't get his."

*Then what?*

"Me." Astrid stopped waist-deep. "I give you me. Not my service. Not my oath. Oaths are chains, and you've worn enough chains." They lifted their sword and drove it point-down into the water, and let go of it, and it sank. "I give you my name. Freely. No bargain. No debt. Take it and be unbound from him, and from this throne, and from every seal your priests ever carved. Take it and speak."

The thing on the throne went very quiet.

And Nell, who carried the drowned god's memories folded up inside her small chest like letters never sent, knew what Astrid did not: that a name given freely was the one key no lock could refuse. That seals were made of debts, and debts made of fear, and fear had no hold on a gift.

*Astrid,* the king's mouth said, tasting it. *Ravensworth.*

"No," Astrid said softly. "Not anymore. Just Astrid. Just the name. It's yours. I'm nobody's."

The water began to sing.

That was the only word Nell would ever find for it, afterward, when people asked and she tried to explain. The sea began to sing — a low note that rose up through the flooded hall, through the black coral, through the bones of the mountain itself, and the crowned thing opened its mouth and the king screamed and the scream became something else, became a voice, became *the* voice, the voice that had been silent for ten years and a hundred before that, and it spoke its first free word in an age:

*"Astrid."*

Saying it back. Holding it. Like a parent saying a child's name for the first time.

The crown cracked.

Nell watched it happen — watched the black iron split along its seam like ice in spring thaw, watched the light pour out of the king's eyes, watched the vast caged thing unfold upward through the water and out through the broken roof of the throne room and up, up, into the night, into the moonlight, becoming weather, becoming wind, becoming the long slow voice of the tide that would never again be silent. And where it passed, the king slumped forward on his coral throne, just a man now, hollow-eyed and weeping, alive, unmasked, small.

The still water shivered. Then it moved. Then the tide came home.

* * *

They carried the king out themselves. Nobody else would touch him.

Brannoc bore him up the smuggler road on his back like a sack of grain, grumbling the whole way, because Brannoc believed grumbling was how you showed love. Sera Ilva's grave-marker — a priest's knotted cord, weighted with stones — they left at the cavern's mouth, where the sea could reach it. Pip cried about that and pretended not to, and Wren put her arm through Pip's arm and neither of them said anything, which between the twins was the same as saying everything.

Ysolde walked beside Nell the whole way, translating the world for her even though Nell no longer needed it — naming trees, naming birds, naming stars — because Ysolde had discovered she loved the sound of the dead tongue in a living mouth, and because Nell had discovered she loved being someone's wonder instead of someone's weapon.

The king wept the entire journey. He kept asking what year it was. He kept asking if anyone had been hurt. When they told him about Sera Ilva he asked for her name, all of it, and said it aloud three times, carefully, like setting something down gently.

"He'll abdicate," Ysolde predicted, somewhere past the mountain passes. "Or they'll hang him. Either way, the crown is finished as a prison. There's nothing left inside it to chain."

"There never was," Nell said, in both languages at once, and Ysolde wrote it down.

* * *

The ship was called the *Mercy of Small Debts*, which Pip swore was funny and Wren swore was not, and they sailed south along the coast with the thirteenth moon sinking behind them and the sea talking the whole way.

Talking. That was the change everyone felt and no one could say plainly. The tide had a voice now. Fishermen would hear it within the season; priests would argue about it for a century. But Nell heard it immediately, because the voice spoke in the dead tongue, and the dead tongue was hers, and the voice had learned its first new word from someone she loved.

It said *Astrid* whenever the waves broke. Softly. Over and over. Like practicing.

Astrid stood at the rail most of those first days, watching the water, and Nell climbed up onto the rail beside them because Brannoc yelled at her for it and that meant it was safe.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "Your name?"

Astrid considered this for a long time. The wind moved their hair, which was going grey at the temples, which Nell had never noticed before.

"I spent ten years praying to something that couldn't answer," Astrid said. "Turns out it wasn't ignoring me. It was drowning. And the only rope I had was my own name." A shrug. "Names are for calling across distances. I'm not far from anything anymore. I'm right here." They looked down at her. "Besides. You can call me whatever you like. You're the only one who speaks both my languages now."

Nell thought about that. She thought very hard, the way she thought about everything, twice, once in each tongue.

"Astrid," she said finally. "Just Astrid."

"Yeah," Astrid said, and smiled, and it was the smile of someone whose old life had dissolved and whose new one fit better. "Yeah, that'll do."

* * *

On the fourth day home, Nell laughed.

She was in the rigging — forbidden, ignored — and Pip was below pretending not to catch her, and Wren was stealing bread from the galley, and Brannoc was yelling at all three of them, and Ysolde was reading aloud from her notes and getting the pronunciation wrong on purpose to make Nell correct her, and the sun was out, and the sea said *Astrid, Astrid* against the hull, and Nell threw back her head and laughed in two living languages at once, high and wild and entirely her own.

Nobody had ever heard her laugh before. The whole ship stopped. Even the sea seemed to pause between words.

Then Brannoc laughed too, huge and rolling, and Pip, and Wren, and Ysolde set down her notes, and Astrid tipped their head back against the mast and laughed up at the sky like a person who had finally put down something heavy.

Nell laughed until she cried, and cried until she laughed again, and understood — in the way children understand things that take scholars whole books to miss — that this was what a name really was. Not a chain. Not a key. Not a word on a seal or a debt or an oath.

A name was the sound the people who loved you made when you came into the room.

She had one now. She had several. She was making more.

Far off behind them, the drowned throne room filled slowly with clean seawater, and the seals dissolved like salt, and the god that had been a prisoner and then a storm and then a voice in the tide settled into the long work of learning to answer prayers again — beginning, always, with the same one:

*Astrid.*

*Answered,* said the sea.
