## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The undercroft smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular cold that lives in stone below the waterline. Coral Brightwater had long ago stopped noticing it, which was its own kind of comfort — a man who notices nothing about his surroundings is a man who has made his peace with them.

He trimmed the lamp, squared the night's receipts on the counting table, and opened the Ledger of Small Impossibilities to a fresh page.

The guild's ledgers came in ranks and hierarchies, like soldiers or saints. There was the Grand Register of Holdings, fat as a scripture, which recorded what the Guild of Impossible Things owned: eleven relics of dubious provenance, three doors that opened onto places that did not exist, a chandelier that wept real pearls when lied to beneath it. There was the Book of Debts, thinner and more dangerous. And there was this — the ledger Coral loved best — in which were recorded not holdings but *transactions*: the small impossibilities that passed through the guild's hands each day, bought and sold and bartered by people whose lives required a little more wonder than the world was willing to give them for free.

He dipped his pen. He wrote, in his careful hand — the hand Vess had once said could mend a broken sentence the way a mason mends a wall:

*Entry the first. One bottle of thunder, captured from the storm over Tanner's Row on the third of Rainsmonth. Sold by the widow Osk, who caught it herself with her late husband's bell-jar and would not be talked below four silver. Purchased by a midwife of the Salt Quarter, who says a house where thunder has been kept is a house where difficult births go easier. Price: four silver, one jar of plum preserves, accepted against guild policy because the preserves were excellent.*

Coral paused, considered, and added: *Note: the thunder rattles when it rains. It knows its own weather. This is either charming or sad, and I have not decided which.*

That was the sort of note Guildmaster Vess pretended to disapprove of and secretly read aloud at council meetings when she thought no one was watching her face.

* * *

The trades had come slow tonight, as they always did in the deep part of autumn, when the city drew inward like a fist closing. But slow was not empty. By the time the bells of the low quarter rang the hour past midnight, Coral had catalogued seven entries, and he read back over them now with the small, private satisfaction of a man surveying a wall he has built true.

A borrowed shadow — returned by a lamplighter who had rented it out during the festival months to a woman who wanted to dance twice at once, and who now reported that his shadow had "picked up habits." Coral entered the rental fee, the damage deposit (forfeited; the shadow had learned to point at things before its owner looked at them), and a note: *shadows remember more than we think. Recommend the guild stop renting them out, which recommendation will be ignored, because the fees are good.*

A left-handed key, traded by a locksmith who swore it opened only doors that had been locked in anger. A promise, written down and folded seven times, sold by a man who could no longer afford to keep it. A single snowflake from a winter forty years gone, preserved in salt, purchased by an old woman who had been a child in that winter and wanted, she said, to hold one piece of it again before her hands forgot how.

Coral wrote that last entry slowly. There was something in it that sat wrong with him, though he could not have said what. Not wrong like an error in the sums. Wrong like a splinter too deep to find.

*Price: two silver,* he wrote. *Buyer's remark, recorded verbatim: "It isn't the snow I want. It's being someone who remembers it."*

He sat back and looked at that line for a long time.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above him, through six floors of the guildhouse, the building settled and sighed the way old buildings do, timbers easing against one another like tired travelers sharing a bench. Coral knew every sound this place made. He had been brought here — so the story went — as a foundling of perhaps four years, left on the guild steps with nothing but a name stitched into his collar and a wooden practice sword no bigger than a finger. The guild raised him the way a river raises a stone: patiently, turning him over and over until all his rough edges had a story attached.

And they had given him wonders. That was the thing he could never explain to people outside these walls. Not the wonders themselves — anyone could gawk at a bottled storm or a door to nowhere. The guild gave him the *accounting* of wonders, which was better. It gave him a world where everything strange had a place in a book, a date, a price, a provenance. Nothing needed to frighten him if he could write it down correctly.

He was, by any measure, content.

He picked up the pen again, and the restlessness moved through him like water through a floorboard — there and gone, leaving only the knowledge that it had been there.

* * *

Near dawn, footsteps on the undercroft stair. Coral knew them before he saw her: Sera Quill, archivist of the ninth stair, whose walk had a hitch in it from a childhood fall she refused to discuss and whose tread on stone was somehow both careful and impatient, as though the stairs were slowing her down personally.

"You're still here," she said, arriving with a basket of returned folios on her hip and candle-smoke in her hair. "Vess asked after you at supper."

"Vess asks after me at every supper. It's how she checks I'm still eating."

"And are you?"

He gestured at the plum preserves, which he had, in fact, opened. Sera peered into the ledger over his shoulder — close enough that he could smell the dust of the upper archives on her, paper and lavender and something warmer underneath — and read the night's entries the way she read everything, quickly and completely, missing nothing.

"The shadow entry," she said. "You wrote 'recommend the guild stop renting them out.' You know Marr's office reads our recommendations now."

"Inquisitor Halven Marr," Coral said, "can read whatever he likes. A shadow that points at things before its owner looks is a hazard, and I'll put it in writing."

"Mm." She set down the basket. For a moment she stood there in the lamplight, and Coral had the odd, unbidden thought that she looked like someone deciding whether to say a thing, and deciding against it, and filing the unsaid thing away somewhere safe. She did that often. He had never asked why. Some part of him suspected he didn't want the answer, which was a strange way to feel about your closest friend.

"Go to sleep, Brightwater," she said instead. "Even impossible things need rest."

"They don't, actually. That's rather the definition."

"Then sleep anyway. You're possible, and you look terrible."

She gathered her basket and climbed back toward the daylight and the ninth stair, and Coral watched the space where she had been for longer than a man watches empty air, if he is content, if he is fine, if nothing is missing.

* * *

He should have closed the ledger then. Instead he turned back one page, then five, then fifty, idly rereading — a habit Vess called *auditing backward*, and which she tolerated because, she said, a man who doesn't reread his own entries will eventually lie to himself in ink.

The trades of years past scrolled under his thumb. Bottled laughter from a funeral, which had cured a mute child. A debt of gratitude, physically heavy, carried in a wheelbarrow. An hour that had fallen out of somebody's life and been sold intact, still warm.

All of it other people's wonders. All of it passing through his hands like water through a lock — lifting him a little, then moving on, leaving him exactly where he stood.

Coral looked down at the ledger, at his own neat hand recording a century of other lives' magic, and felt the splinter again, deeper now, closer to something true: that he had spent his whole life cataloguing impossibilities without ever once touching one. That the guild had taught him to write down miracles in such beautiful handwriting that he'd mistaken transcription for living.

He blew out the lamp.

And in the dark of the undercroft, in the silence after, he heard it — faint, unmistakable, coming from the direction of the sealed prophecy vault two rooms away, where no lamp burned and no one had business being: the soft, dry sound of a page turning.

Alone. In a locked room. Where the books had not been touched in a hundred years.

Coral stood very still in the dark, listening, and told himself it was the building settling, the way old buildings do.

He did not believe himself.
