## 2. A Date Written Before Tonight

The prophecy vault smelled of cold iron and old rain.

Coral Brightwater had always thought that was a strange thing for a room to smell like — rain that had fallen before anyone alive remembered falling — but the vault kept its own weather, its own seasons, sealed behind three doors of increasing stubbornness. The first door was oak banded in bronze. The second was stone that weighed more than it should have, as though it had been quarried from somewhere heavier than the world. The third door was only ever unlocked by the Guildmaster's key, and tonight, for the first time in his eleven years of service, Coral carried it himself.

It sat in his breast pocket like a coal that refused to go out.

*Annual audit, all holdings, witnessed and countersigned.* Vess had written the order herself, in her own hand, which was unusual. Vess delegated audits the way mountains delegated erosion. But her note had been brief and her eyes, when she'd handed him the key at dusk, had been careful in a way he couldn't name. *Count everything twice,* she'd said. *And Coral — if anything seems wrong, bring it to me before you bring it to anyone.*

He had laughed and asked what could possibly be wrong with a room no one entered.

She hadn't laughed back.

Now he stood in the heart of the vault with a lantern turned low and a ledger open on his knee, and the silence here was not empty the way the undercroft's silence was empty. The undercroft below was full of small wonders waiting to be traded — bottled thunder ticking softly on its shelf, the borrowed shadow folded into its cedar box, dreaming someone else's dreams until its owner came to reclaim it. That silence was companionable. This one listened.

The vault was a long chamber cut into the guildhouse's oldest bones, shelved floor to ceiling with books that were not for sale, not for reading, and — according to the charter — not entirely for believing. The Guild of Impossible Things dealt in wonders the way other merchants dealt in wool: honestly, at fair prices, with receipts. But prophecies were different stock. Prophecies were inventory that read you back.

Coral worked methodically, because method was the only prayer he trusted. Shelf by shelf, spine by spine, he matched each volume against its entry in the master ledger, checking seals, checking wards, checking the faint silver thread of preservation-magic that ran through every binding like a vein of ore. *One hundred and forty volumes, accounted. One hundred and forty-one, accounted. One hundred and forty-two—*

His lantern guttered.

Not went out — guttered, the flame leaning hard toward the far shelf as if pulled on a string, then righting itself with a little offended sputter. Coral frowned at it. He relit nothing; there was nothing to relight. He simply stood in the cold iron-and-rain smell and felt the first thin wire of unease draw itself tight across his shoulders.

The flame leaned again when he took a step toward the far shelf.

"Professional calm," he murmured to himself, which was something his first master at the guild used to say — *a swordspeaker's hands are steady or they are useless, Brightwater, so make your calm professional even when it isn't real.* He was not swordspeaking now. There was no blade in the vault; blades were forbidden past the second door, an old rule everyone assumed was about fire risk. His hands were steady anyway. Mostly.

The book at the end of the far shelf did not look remarkable.

That was the first wrongness. Every volume in the vault wore its importance openly — chained, warded, boxed in lead and glass, labeled in three scripts. This one sat unchained, unboxed, bound in plain grey leather gone soft as moth wing at the corners, its title stamped in faded gilt: *Annals of the Weeping Century.* No ward-thread in its spine. No seal on its cover. It looked less like a treasure than like something left behind on purpose, the way one leaves a lamp lit in a window.

Its entry in the master ledger was equally unremarkable, and that was the second wrongness. *Acquired prior to current registry. Contents: local histories, disputed. Value: negligible. Handling: gloves recommended.*

Gloves recommended. Not required. In a vault where a single unwarded page could cost a reader her shadow, the ledger said *gloves recommended*, in the same ink and the same hand as entries describing crates of haunted buttons.

Coral pulled on his gloves anyway. Then, because he was who he was — meticulous, thorough, the man who counted everything twice — he lifted the grey book down and opened it to the registry ribbon and began, professionally, calmly, to audit.

* * *

The early pages were exactly what the entry promised. Local histories, disputed. Rainfall records from a century ago. Grain tallies. A long, bitter argument between two extinct villages about the true course of a river that had since changed its mind. Coral read quickly, ticking each entry against nothing, because there was nothing to tick against — this book answered to no other record. It was its own witness. He noted that in his margin and moved on, and the vault breathed around him, and the lantern flame leaned gently toward the pages like a curious cat.

Then the handwriting changed.

It happened between one leaf and the next, the way weather changes. The earlier entries had been written in a round, patient clerk's hand, the kind of script belonging to someone who expected to be alive a long time and mostly correct. The new hand was narrower. Faster. Pressed hard enough into the paper that the letters showed through on the reverse side like scars. And the subject changed too — no more grain, no more rivers. The new hand wrote about the Wood.

*The trees wept resin again this spring. The villagers say the spirits are dreaming of the war. I say the spirits do not dream. I say they remember, and remembering is worse.*

Coral slowed. Outside the vault, somewhere far above, the guildhouse settled into its nighttime creaks — stairs sighing, pipes muttering, the great clock in the entry hall clearing its throat. Down here none of it mattered. Down here there was only the narrow, scarred handwriting and the smell of rain that had never fallen in his lifetime.

He turned pages. The annalist — whoever they had been — grew stranger as the years advanced. Entries shortened. Sentences fractured. Once, mid-entry, the writing simply stopped for two full pages and resumed with: *Forgive the gap. I did not want to write down what I saw, and then I understood that not writing it down is how these things are lost, and lost things come back wrong.*

*These things,* Coral thought. *Which things?*

The dates marched forward through the century, and Coral tracked them out of habit, marking intervals in his own ledger, building the little architecture of time that made history feel like a room instead of a fog. Forty years in. Sixty. Eighty. The annalist's hand deteriorated into something almost feverish, entries shrinking to lines, to fragments:

*The Wood is quieter. That is not peace.*

*A child in the lower town spoke a word in her sleep and every candle in the street went out. They burned her mother's house for it. I have written to the Guild. The Guild has written back that some inventories are better lost.*

*They are wrong. Lost things come back wrong. I will keep the record. Someone must keep the record.*

Ninety years. Ninety-five. The fragments came slower now, years apart, as if the writer were dying by degrees and rationing their ink. Coral found he had stopped sitting properly on his stool and was leaning over the book with his elbows on the reading desk, his breath fogging faintly in air that should not have been cold enough to fog. The lantern flame lay flat toward the pages, streaming like a banner in wind that did not exist.

Ninety-nine years ago. The penmanship steadied — suddenly, completely, as if the writer had gathered themselves for one final act of precision. The entry was longer than any in twenty pages. Coral read it once at speed, then forced himself to go back and read it again slowly, the way he audited, line by line, because surely he had misread, surely the ink had smeared, surely—

*In the hundredth year I set down what I have withheld, so that the record may be whole. The Guild keeps among its holdings a last oath-bound swordspeaker, hidden in plain sight, raised as one of their own. He does not know what he is. When the hundredth year turns, he will stand in this vault, on this night, and open this page, and read these words. I know this because the Wood told me, and the Wood does not lie; it only weeps.*

*To the swordspeaker who reads this: today is the day. Check the date. Check it against your own heart's calendar, the one that tells you what day it is without asking anyone. They will match. They have always been going to match.*

Coral's hands were still steady. He noticed that from very far away, the way one notices the behavior of strangers. He turned his own ledger around, drew it close, and wrote the date at the top of a fresh line in his neatest professional hand.

Then he looked up at the vault's chronometer — a brass ring of interlocking gears mounted above the third door, wound weekly, accurate to the minute, the one clock in the guildhouse no one ever argued with.

The dates matched.

Of course they matched. That was the entire obscene point. He sat with the matching of them the way one sits with a diagnosis, letting it settle into the body, feeling for the edges of it. A century ago a dying archivist had written tonight's date into a book and shelved it in a vault and gone into whatever dark waits after the last sentence, trusting — *knowing* — that a man not yet born would sit exactly here and read it exactly now.

*He will stand in this vault, on this night, and open this page.*

Coral became aware, distantly, that he was standing. He had no memory of rising. The lantern streamed its flat banner of light across the desk. The iron-and-rain smell had thickened, and beneath it now was something green — sap, crushed leaves, the deep wet breath of a forest — though the nearest living thing was forty feet of stone away in every direction.

There was more. Of course there was more. The entry continued, the narrow hand pressing harder, the letters going ragged at the ends as if the writer's certainty outran their strength:

*You will want to stop reading. Stop anyway. What follows is the only kindness I have left to give, and kindness delayed a century is still kindness.*

*The page after this one is sealed. I sealed it myself, with blood and grief, because it names what you carry and what you must choose, and I would not have it read by chance or by children or by the usurper's men. It opens for one thing only. You already know what. Your blood has always known. That is why your oaths sing the way they do, why the blades listen to you as they listen to no one else. You are not merely a speaker of swords. You are the last leaf of a tree the world tried to burn, and the sap in you remembers fire.*

*When you are ready — and you will not be ready, readiness has nothing to do with it — open the next page.*

*Or don't. The Wood will open it for you. It has been opening toward you your whole life.*

Coral read the entry a third time. Then a fourth. Professional calm, his first master called it; make the calm professional even when it isn't real. His hands did not shake. His hands were perfect. Somewhere inside his chest, behind the sternum, something that had been quietly humming his entire life — the thing that let him speak to steel and be answered — had gone silent, attentive, the way a held breath is silent.

He checked the date again. Brass gears did not lie. Ink did not lie; ink was the whole religion of his life, the one faith the Guild taught and the one faith he had never doubted. Ledgers balanced. Records held. The past stayed past, obedient, filed, harmless.

Except the past, it appeared, had been writing him letters.

"No," he said aloud, and the vault took the word and gave nothing back, and saying it helped not at all, because denial was not an audit finding. Denial did not balance. He had spent eleven years learning to trust the record over the rumor, the receipt over the memory, the ink over the wish. The record said he would be here. He was here. The arithmetic was flawless and he hated it with his whole meticulous heart.

*Hidden in plain sight, raised as one of their own. He does not know what he is.*

Vess's face rose unbidden: the careful eyes at dusk, the key pressed into his palm, *count everything twice, and if anything seems wrong, bring it to me before you bring it to anyone.* Had her voice caught, just slightly, on *anything*? Had she known — had she always known — what waited on this shelf for this night? The Guildmaster who never delegated audits had handed him this one. The thought arrived cold and complete, like a coin dropped down a well: *she sent me to find it, or she sent me hoping I wouldn't.*

Both possibilities were betrayals of some size. He set the thought aside with difficulty, the way one sets down something hot.

The grey book lay open before him, and the next page waited.

He could see its edge — a sliver of parchment fused to the page before it, sealed edge to edge in a film of something amber-dark and translucent. Resin. Sap, he corrected himself, with a lurch that had nothing to do with his stomach. Tree-tears, the woodcutters called it. It had soaked through and hardened into a seal smooth as glass, and beneath it, just barely, just barely visible, the ghost of writing showed through like a body under ice.

He touched it with one gloved fingertip. The seal was warm.

Everything else in the vault was cellar-cold. The seal was warm as skin, warm as a cheek, and under his glove it seemed to press back against his finger with the faintest pulse — once, twice — patient as a heartbeat, unhurried as a tree.

Coral withdrew his hand. He closed the grey book gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and stood in the listening silence with his ledger hugged to his chest, and made himself think like an auditor instead of a man.

Facts. One: the annals contained an entry dated to tonight, describing him, accurately, a century in advance. Two: the following page was sealed with living resin and readable only — allegedly — by his blood. Three: the Guild's registry had mislabeled the volume as negligible, which meant either gross negligence by people who were never negligent, or deliberate concealment by people who were very good at it. Four: Vess had given him this audit, this night, this key.

Five: he had promised to bring anything wrong to her before anyone.

He looked at the book. He looked at the third door, and beyond it the stairs, and beyond those the warm lamplit ordinary world where Sera was probably still at her desk on the ninth stair, chewing her pen, and Vess was probably still awake over her maps, and tomorrow the ledgers would balance and the wonder-trades would run and everything would continue being the life he had, the good life, the life built patiently out of other people's miracles.

The lantern flame leaned toward the book.

Coral Brightwater picked up his pen, and in his own ledger, in his own neat professional hand, beneath the date he had copied from the brass clock, he wrote the only honest entry available to him:

*Item 143. One (1) volume, Annals of the Weeping Century. Condition: sealed. Discrepancy: the future appears to have been filed under the past. Resolution: pending.*

He blotted it. He squared the pages. And then he stood for a long moment with his hand resting flat on the grey cover, feeling the warmth of it through the leather, feeling the slow patient pulse beneath, like something enormous asleep just under the floor of the world.

*Open the next page. Or don't. The Wood will open it for you.*

"Not tonight," he told it quietly.

The seal pulsed once, warm against his palm, and he could not have sworn — would never have sworn, not in any ledger, not under any oath — whether it was a heartbeat or an answer.
