## 4. Sera of the Ninth Stair

Sera Quill counted the stairs the way other people said prayers.

Nine flights from the guild's front hall to the archive loft, each one worn into a shallow bowl by two centuries of clerks' feet, and she had climbed them so many times that her body knew the rhythm without her mind's consent. Nine flights up, nine down, and somewhere in between — usually on the seventh, where the banister had a knot shaped like a sleeping fox — her thoughts always drifted to Coral Brightwater.

Tonight they drifted earlier than usual. On the fourth stair, in fact, which was unprecedented, and she chose not to examine why.

The undercroft lamps were still burning below her. She could see their glow pooling on the landing like spilled honey, and beneath it, faintly, the scratch of a nib on paper. That was wrong. The ledgers were closed at dusk; that was the first rule Sera had learned when she came to the Guild of Impossible Things at fourteen, orphaned and sharp-tongued and desperate for a roof that did not leak. *The books sleep when the city sleeps,* old Archivist Maren had told her, tapping the ledger-room door with one knuckle as if it were a living thing that needed rest. *A book kept awake past its hour starts dreaming, and dreams get into the ink.*

Someone was keeping a book awake.

She knew who before she reached the bottom step. There was only one person in the guild who worked past closing with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his collar unbuttoned, looking less like an archivist than like a man slowly drowning in beautiful weather. Coral sat at the long oak table with three lamps lit around him — greedy with light, which was unlike him — and he did not hear her come down the last flight, because he never heard anyone come down the last flight. His whole attention had gone into the page before him the way water goes into roots: completely, silently, and with no intention of ever coming back out.

Sera stopped in the doorway and watched him for a moment longer than propriety allowed.

He was writing. His hand moved with the particular care he reserved for entries that mattered — the small, deliberate strokes of a man copying scripture — and yet something about the motion was off, some hesitation between strokes, a fractional lifting of the nib where there should have been none. Sera had spent eleven years reading hands across tables. She knew what honest ink looked like.

This was not honest ink.

"Coral," she said.

He flinched. Actually flinched — his shoulder jerking up toward his ear, his hand smearing a half-formed word into a grey ghost — and then he looked up at her with an expression she had never seen on him before. Not guilt exactly. Guilt she would have known how to handle; guilt was a currency the guild traded in daily, along with bottled thunder and borrowed shadows and every other impossible thing that passed through their books. This was closer to fear, and fear looked obscene on Coral Brightwater's face, the way a crack looks obscene in a window you've leaned against a thousand times.

"Sera." He said her name like a man setting down something heavy. "You're late."

"I'm never late. You're early. Those are different crimes." She came into the room properly, letting her boots announce her, giving him time to arrange whatever it was he needed to arrange. He used the time badly. His palm slid sideways over the open ledger in a gesture of concealment so clumsy that a first-year apprentice would have blushed to attempt it. "What are we hiding tonight?"

"Nothing."

"Mm." She pulled out the chair opposite his — the one with the wobbly leg she'd been meaning to report to the carpenters for six years and never had, because the wobble meant the chair was hers, and she was sentimental about territories — and sat. "Show me nothing, then."

For a long moment he didn't move. The lamps hissed softly. Somewhere above them, through the stone, the city went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bell of Vespermark tolling the hour in its cracked voice. Sera waited. Waiting was the oldest trick in her inventory, and she had learned it from watching Coral wait, because Coral could outwait a glacier when he decided something mattered.

"Please," he said finally, and it was the *please* that undid her, not the request. Coral did not beg. Coral apologized, negotiated, charmed, endured — but begging required a belief that the world might actually refuse him, and until recently Sera would have sworn he'd never entertained such a belief in his life.

She looked at the ledger. He didn't stop her. That was its own answer.

It was the Night Ledger, the great master volume of the undercroft, the book in which every impossible trade was recorded in the guild's own hand — and the entry dated for this evening was written in Coral's neat, upright script, recording a transaction Sera knew had never happened. *Received into custody: one vial, river-water, from the Ashfall tributary, provenance attested by W. Corren.* She knew W. Corren. Willet Corren had been dead for four months, drowned in the canal behind the tannery, and his attestations had died with him. More than that: the Ashfall tributary ran through the Weeping Wood's outer groves, and no one — *no one* — brought anything out of the Weeping Wood anymore. The guild hadn't accepted Wood-trade in a generation. Every archivist learned that rule alongside the alphabet.

"You forged an entry," she said quietly. It wasn't a question, but she made it one anyway, because questions gave him room. "Why?"

His jaw tightened. "You know I can't—"

"No, I don't know. That's rather the point of asking." She kept her voice level, almost bored, though her heart had begun doing something complicated against her ribs. "Coral. Eleven years. I have covered for you twice — once when you misfiled the Meridian Contract under F for *fish*, and once when you told Guildmaster Vess the fire in the map room was my fault, which it was not, and which I forgave because you cried, and I am weak against men who cry. But those were mistakes. This is ink. Deliberate ink. In the Night Ledger."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. Up close, in the lamplight, she could see what the distance had hidden: he looked like a man who hadn't slept in days. There was a bruised crescent under each eye, and his lips were dry, and his left hand — his sword-hand, though he'd never carried anything but a quill since she'd known him — lay flat on the table as if pinning something down. "Because the Coral I know triple-checks his dates and alphabetizes his spice rack. The Coral I know once wrote a formal letter of apology to a book he'd dog-eared. Something has frightened you, and you're lying to the ledger about it, and lying to the ledger is—" she heard her own voice drop, "—it's lying to the guild, Coral. If Vess sees this—"

"She won't see it."

"She audits everything. You know that better than—"

"*She won't see it.*"

The words came out with a force that made the lamp flames lean. Sera sat back. For one strange heartbeat the light in the room seemed to gutter toward him, all three flames bending a degree off true, and then they righted themselves, and she decided she had imagined it, because the alternative was a thought she wasn't ready to think.

Coral closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the force was gone, folded away, and what remained was worse: exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion, a kind of terrible tenderness aimed directly at her.

"I can't tell you," he said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because the fewer people who know, the safer they are. That's not a riddle, Sera, it's just arithmetic."

"And me? Am I safe, in your arithmetic?"

He didn't answer. The silence answered for him, and it was not the answer she wanted, and she found herself furious at him for it — furious and, beneath the fury, something warmer and far more dangerous, which was the exact shape of the thought *he is trying to protect me* wearing a disguise and pretending to be anger.

* * *

They had met on these stairs, technically. Or near them. Her second week in the guild, fourteen years old and drowning in the catalog system, she had sat down on the seventh step — the one with the fox-knot — and cried with the efficient, furious crying of a girl who refused to be caught at it. And Coral, sixteen and already solemn as a church mouse, had come down the stairs carrying a stack of folios, seen her, and simply sat down beside her without a word. He'd opened one of the folios on his knees, turned to a page at random, and read aloud.

It was a trade record. A completely ordinary trade record. *One memory of a summer afternoon, sold by a dying woman to buy her grandson a winter coat.* He read it in his flat, careful voice, no drama in it at all, and somehow that was the thing — the utter ordinariness of it, the fact that here was a place where a summer afternoon could be weighed against a coat and both parties walked away satisfied — and Sera had stopped crying, and looked at him, and said, "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard," and he'd said, "Yes. Isn't it wonderful that someone wrote it down," and that had been that. Eleven years of that.

She had loved him for approximately ten of them, and had told him for zero, and the gap between those numbers had become so familiar it fit her like the wobbly chair fit her: uncomfortable, hers, load-bearing.

Now she stood at the table while he re-inked the smeared word, and she understood with sudden clarity that the gap was about to close or kill her.

"Here's what happens," she said. "You tell me nothing. Fine. I respect arithmetic. But the entry stays."

His head came up sharply. "Sera—"

"The entry *stays*. Listen to me." She planted both palms on the table. "If you alter it further, if you keep working on it, you'll make it worse. Forged entries are like lies — the first one's clean, every repair shows. Right now it's passable. Corren's dead, no one will query his attestation, and the Ashfall reference reads as clerical error rather than intent, because honestly, who would believe we traded in Wood-goods? It's too absurd to suspect." She saw him register the logic, saw the tension in his shoulders shift by a degree. "But if Vess pulls the Night Ledger tomorrow and finds fresh ink over dried, or a date corrected, or your handwriting *tired* — tired handwriting is a confession, Coral, everyone knows yours goes narrow when you're frightened — then you're finished. And whatever it is you're protecting by lying, it dies with you. So. The entry stays. You close the book. You go upstairs. You eat something. And tomorrow you act like a man whose conscience is boring."

He stared at her. "When did you learn all that?"

"When you taught me to audit, you absolute disaster. Year three. You talked for two hours about ink aging and I took notes because I was in love with you and too stupid to say so." The words were out before she could weigh them — true words, unweighed, the most dangerous kind — and the silence that followed had a texture she felt on her skin.

Coral went very still. "Sera."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the face. Don't be kind about it. I'm not asking for anything. I'm telling you why I'm covering for you, so you understand it isn't charity. It's—" she searched for the word and hated every candidate, "—investment. Now close the book."

He closed the book. His hand lingered on the cover, and when he spoke, his voice was low and rough. "I would tell you. If I could. I need you to believe that."

"I believe it."

"And Sera — what you said. I..." He stopped. Started again. Stopped. Coral Brightwater, who could speak oaths into steel and make blades obey, could not finish a sentence aimed at her heart, and some cruel, tender part of her wanted to laugh and weep in equal measure. Finally he said only: "Thank you. For not asking why."

"That," Sera said, turning toward the stairs before her face could betray anything further, "is the first lie I've told you tonight. I'm not not asking why. I'm saving it." She paused on the bottom step, one hand on the rail, and looked back at him sitting alone in his island of lamplight with the closed ledger in front of him like a grave. "One day soon you're going to tell me everything, Coral Brightwater, because you're going to realize that arithmetic works both ways — and the safest place for a dangerous secret is in the keeping of someone who already loves you past the point of good judgment."

She climbed. Seven steps up, she heard him blow out the first lamp. On the eighth, the second. At the top of the ninth she stopped, in the dark, and admitted the thing she had been circling all evening the way a moth circles a flame it intends to die in:

She would have burned the Night Ledger itself, if he'd asked. Every record, every oath, two hundred years of the guild's careful memory — she would have fed it all to the stove and held the door shut while it screamed, if holding the door meant standing between Coral Brightwater and whatever was hunting him.

And that, she thought, climbing the last steps into the cold air of the loft, was the most dangerous entry of all. Not the one in the book below. The one she had just written in herself, in ink that would never age, never fade, and never, ever come out.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she heard the third lamp go out — and then, after a long moment, a sound she had never heard Coral make: a single low word, spoken to no one, in a language that was not any language the guild kept books on.

It sounded, she thought, almost like a tree learning to talk.
