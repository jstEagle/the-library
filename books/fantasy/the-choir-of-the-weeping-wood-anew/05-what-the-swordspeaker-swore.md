## 5. What the Swordspeaker Swore

The proving hall beneath the Guild of Impossible Things had no windows, and Coral Brightwater had always thought that was the point. Wonder did not need daylight. It needed stone, and silence, and the particular hush of a room that had heard ten thousand oaths and kept every one of them the way a riverbed keeps water — shaped by them, defined by them, holding them without complaint.

He came down an hour before dawn, as he always did on speaking days, because the first oath of the morning belonged to him alone. The other swordspeakers were gone now, all of them. Dead, scattered, or retired into the quiet trades of appraisal and authentication, their voices gone soft with disuse. Coral was the last who still came to the rail of black iron at the hall's center and unbound a blade and spoke to it as one speaks to a thing with ears.

The blade waiting for him this morning was a modest one: a short sword called *Kindness*, bound three years ago from a widow in the Saltmarket whose husband had carried it through the Border Wars and then, one winter, walked into the sea. It had wept saltwater into its scabbard ever since. The Guild had paid her fairly. The ledger entry was Coral's own, dated, witnessed, annotated in his small exact hand: *Item 4,117. One short sword, haunted lightly. Grieves. Requires speaking twice monthly.*

He set it on the rail and laid his palms flat against the cold iron on either side of it, and he felt the faint tremor come up through the metal, the way you feel a cat purring through a floorboard.

"Good morning," he said.

The blade shivered.

Swordspeech was not command, whatever the old manuals pretended. That was the first lie they scrubbed out of you in your novitiate years, before they let you stand at the rail. A bound blade was not a servant. It was a grief, or a rage, or a promise that had outlived its maker, folded into steel and left there like a letter no one would deliver. The speaker's work was not to order it about. It was to *witness* it — to speak aloud the terms of its binding, oath by oath, until the steel remembered what it was for and agreed, briefly, willingly, to be a sword again instead of a wound.

So Coral began, as he always began, with the widow's husband's name.

"Your bearer was Tomas Vell," he said, and his voice dropped into the register the guild had trained into him, low and even, a voice for holding things. "You were forged for him by his father. You were given to him at nineteen, with the words *keep it kind*. You slept at his hip through eleven years of war and never drank deeper than the arm."

*Kindness* went still under his hands. Stillness, in a bound blade, was attention.

"You held the line at Carrow Ford." His voice did not waver; it had been trained not to, and besides, he had said these words forty times now, and grief repeated well became liturgy. "You were sheathed when he wrote his last letter home. You were on the beach when he walked into the water, and you could not follow, because he told you to stay."

The tremor in the steel changed. It always changed here. It went from the fluttering of a trapped moth to something slower, deeper, a pulse.

"And so you stayed," Coral said. "And staying is your oath. And I am here to tell you that the oath is kept."

This was the heart of it, the part the manuals called *the Confirmation*, and the part Coral privately thought of as the mercy. Every binding ended in a promise made to the blade rather than extracted from it, and the promise was always the same in shape if never in wording: *you are not forgotten; your keeping is witnessed; you may rest.* He drew breath for it now, the familiar breath, the one that sat in his chest like a key sitting in a lock—

—and the hall listened back.

There was no other way to say it, though he tried, standing there in the grey dark with his palms on the iron, to find a better one. The Guild's undercroft had acoustics like a held breath; every word spoken at the rail came back to the speaker softened and delayed, the way sound returns from snow. But this was not echo. This was attention. It pressed against the edges of the room the way weather presses against a window, and it arrived at the precise moment his breath did, as though something vast and patient had been waiting — had perhaps been waiting for a very long time — for exactly this sentence, in exactly this voice, and had leaned in to hear how he would finish it.

Coral finished it anyway. He was a guildsman, and guildsmen completed their entries.

"*You are not forgotten,*" he said. "*Your keeping is witnessed. You may rest.*"

The word *rest* left his mouth and did not fall to the floor.

That was the second impossible thing, and Coral Brightwater, keeper of ledgers, appraiser of impossibilities, knew an impossible thing when it happened six feet from his face. Words fell. Sound obeyed weight, eventually, even in a vaulted room. But *rest* hung in the air above the blade like a mote of dust caught in a shaft of light that had no source, and while it hung, the whole hall changed temperature. Not colder. Warmer — the warmth of a room where many people are standing very close and saying nothing. He felt it against the back of his neck. He felt it in the iron under his palms, which had gone from trembling to ringing, a single clear note below hearing, the way a tuning fork rings after the strike is over.

Then the light-mote that had been his word sank, gently, the way a leaf settles onto water, and went into the steel, and *Kindness* sighed.

Blades did not sigh. Blades rang, rattled, keened, wept brine. They did not sigh — that small human exhalation of a burden set down — and yet Coral heard it, unmistakably, and felt the sword go quiet under his hands in a way it had never gone quiet in three years of twice-monthly speakings. Not the stillness of attention. The stillness of sleep.

He stood there a long moment with his hands on the rail, listening to the silence where the hall's listening had been. It had withdrawn — or been withdrawn — but it left a shape behind, the way a body leaves a shape in a bed.

"Well," Coral said to the sleeping sword, because speaking aloud was how he thought, and because the alternative was to admit to himself that his skin was crawling. "That's new."

* * *

He should have logged it. That was the discipline; that was the whole architecture of his life. *An unrecorded wonder is a wonder wasted,* Vess had told him when he was nine years old and newly inked into the guild rolls, and he had built his entire self on that sentence the way a barnacle builds on a piling. So he climbed the stairs to the scriptorium with the morning bell still two hours off, took down the day-book, uncapped his pen, and wrote:

*Item 4,117, speaking the 24th. Blade confirmed and rested per standard rite. Note: confirmation observed anomalous resonance—*

His pen stopped.

Because what would he write next? *Resonance* implied physics, measurement, a phenomenon with edges. What he had felt had no edges. It had leaned in. It had *listened*, and not the passive listening of walls. Something in the deep stone of the Guild — or beyond it, or beneath it, or threaded through it the way mycelium threads through soil — had heard the last swordspeaker begin the oldest sentence in his order's liturgy, and had wanted, urgently, to know how it ended.

And then there was the page. The fused page. Three nights ago now, in the prophecy vault, he had stood with his lamp over a book a century old and read today's date written in faded iron-gall ink, describing him — *the last swordspeaker opening the sealed page* — in words that had been drying on vellum since before his grandmother was born. He had reported it to Vess, as duty required. She had looked at him with an expression he was only now, walking its contours over and over in his memory, learning to read: not surprise. Recognition. Grief wearing the mask of calm. *Seal the vault, Coral. Some pages are fused for a reason.* And he had obeyed, because he was Coral Brightwater and obedience was the load-bearing wall of everything he was.

But obedience, he was discovering, sat poorly in a man who had felt the world lean in to hear him speak.

He scraped the half-line from the day-book with a knife, dried the ghost of it with blotting sand, and wrote instead: *Item 4,117, speaking the 24th. Blade confirmed and rested. No anomalies.* The lie sat on the page looking exactly like truth, which was, he supposed, the entire business model of the Guild of Impossible Things, and he hated it, and he wrote it anyway, and he understood in that moment — cleanly, terribly — that this was the second lie he had told the ledgers in three days, and that lies, like debts, compounded.

He capped the ink. He sat in the empty scriptorium while the sky outside the high windows went from slate to pearl, and he listened to the building around him: the tick of the great case clock, the settling of old timber, the far-off clatter of the kitchens waking. All the ordinary sounds of the only home he had ever known. And underneath them, so faint he could have imagined it, that sub-audible ring again, one long sustained note, as if somewhere far below the proving hall something enormous had struck itself against something else enormous and was still vibrating.

*What am I?* he asked the silence, and the silence, courteously, declined to answer.

* * *

The morning's docket gave him no refuge. Two authentications, a valuation, and a speaking — a heavy one, a great two-handed grave-blade called *Orison* that the Guild had held for sixty years and spoke quarterly, its binding so old that the original oaths had to be reconstructed from fragments, which made every speaking part liturgy and part archaeology. Coral worked through the authentications with his hands and let his mind run loose behind them, touching relics, murmuring provenances, while some quieter chamber of him turned over and over the fact of the listening hall.

It was during the Orison speaking, mid-morning, that the pattern announced itself.

He stood at the rail with the great blade bare before him, its steel clouded with age like ice with frost, and he began the reconstruction as he always did, feeding it the recovered fragments in their proper order: *bearer's name, forge-oath, battle-oaths, the long middle years, the death-oath, the Confirmation.* And at each fragment, he felt it again — the leaning-in, the pressure of attention, arriving earlier each time. At the third oath it was already there. By the fifth, the air above the rail had that warm crowded quality, and the lamp flames in their brackets along the wall had all bent toward him together, gently, like grass bending toward a door being opened.

And *Orison* — sixty years silent between speakings, sixty years of quarterly liturgy performed by four different speakers across two generations — *answered early.*

Coral said, "*You were forged in the year of the Ash Winter—*" and before he could reach the next line, the blade hummed the next line's pitch. Not words. Steel could not hold words. But pitch, tone, the rising cadence of a sentence it had heard spoken so many times it knew the shape of the voice that should follow — it offered the shape to him like a hand offering a rope.

His arms prickled from wrist to shoulder. He took the offered cadence and rode it, finishing the fragment, and the blade hummed assent, and the listening presence around them both deepened — and Coral, standing in the amber lamplight with a dead man's sword singing harmony with him, felt something crack open in his chest that was not fear.

It was wonder. Raw, unledgered, unpriced wonder — the thing the Guild traded in and he had catalogued for twenty years without once, he realized now, *feeling*. Every item in every crate downstairs was someone else's miracle, authenticated, valued, shelved. And here was his own, arriving unbidden, unsold, unwitnessed except by whatever vast patient thing filled the room like tide fills a cave: his power, his inherited, dwindling, last-of-his-order power, was not faltering the way he'd feared. It was *answering*. Something had called, in a century-old book with a fused page, and everything in him that was swordspeaker was turning toward the call like a compass needle finding north.

"The Confirmation," he whispered, and his voice shook, and he let it.

He spoke the old closing to *Orison* — *you are not forgotten; your keeping is witnessed; you may rest* — and the words did not hang in the air this time. They *went*. He felt them leave him like heat leaving an open door, streaming out of his chest and down through the rail, through the iron, through the bedrock, down and outward in a direction he could not name but his body understood the way a migrating bird understands south. The lamp flames stood up straight. The hall exhaled. And *Orison* settled into a rest so profound that for the first time in sixty years of quarterly speakings, the grave-blade did not resume its low grieving hum when the rite ended.

Silence, then. Real silence, spent and clean.

Coral stood at the rail with his palms flat on the iron and his heart going like a hammer, and he thought, with the strange clarity that arrives at the exact center of dread: *I am being readied for something. My voice is being tuned.*

* * *

He found Vess at noon in the map room, and he might have told her everything — the hanging word, the answering blade, the compass-needle pull in his chest — because she was Vess, because she had raised him, because the load-bearing wall of his life was still, despite everything, obedience and trust.

But she was standing at the great table with a courier's satchel open in front of her, and her face was the face she wore for bad weather, and beside the satchel lay a letter bearing a wax seal he had seen only in the authentication files: the double-keyed seal of the capital's Office of Inquiry, pressed deep and clean, the wax still bright.

"Auditors," Vess said, without looking up. "From the usurper's court. They arrive tomorrow at dawn, and they want every prophecy holding in our vaults, and they carry papers signed with our own stolen guild-seal to make it lawful." She finally raised her eyes to him, grey and level and older than he had ever let himself see. "Coral. Before they come, I need to know exactly what you touched in the vault."

The compass needle in his chest swung, slow and sure, toward the fused page, toward the listening dark, toward whatever was waiting at the end of the sentence his blood had begun a hundred years before he was born.

"Everything," Coral said. "I touched everything."
