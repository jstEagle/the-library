## 6. The Usurper's Auditors

Inquisitor Halven Marr did not believe in dawn. He believed in the hour before it — the grey interval when the world was neither one thing nor another, when watchmen dozed and gatekeepers dreamed of their beds and every door in a city stood half-open to whatever wished to come through it. It was in that hour that he had always done his best work, and so it was in that hour that he came down from the mountains with twelve soldiers and a wagon of empty ledgers, and halted his company at the treeline above the town of Vellharrow, where the Guild of Impossible Things kept its crooked tower against the sky like a knife left standing in a table.

The Weeping Wood lay below him on the far side of the valley, black under the last of the night, and even at this distance he could hear it — or imagined he could — a sound like weeping carried on the wind, thin as wire. The trees there bled sap instead of resin, sap that ran silver-pale down their bark in long slow tears, and the peasants would not cut them for timber and the carters would not drive through them after dark, and Halven Marr had spent eleven years learning not to believe in the things peasants would not do.

He dismounted. His boots found the frost-stiff grass. Behind him, the twelve soldiers waited in the disciplined silence he had drilled into them, and the wagon creaked as the horses shifted, and somewhere below, a cock crowed once and thought better of it.

"Captain Ossen," he said.

"Sir." The captain came up beside him, breath steaming.

"What do you see?"

Ossen squinted down at the town. "A guildhouse, sir. Chimneys lit. A watchman on the wall who's asleep."

"I see a town," Halven said, "that has been permitted to forget what it is. Look at the roofs. Not one banner. Not one portrait of the Regent Sovereign facing the street. Eleven years since the succession, and they still fly nothing but their own weathercocks." He said it without heat, the way a man might note a stain on a collar. "Forgetfulness is a kind of treason, Captain. It simply takes longer to hang."

"Yes, sir."

Halven turned up his collar against the mountain wind. He was a narrow man, dry as an old ledger's spine, with a face that people struggled afterward to describe — not because it was plain but because it declined to be remembered. Witnesses gave contradictory accounts of his eye color. Sketch artists produced three different men. This was not sorcery; it was only that Halven Marr looked at people the way an auditor looks at columns of figures, and no one likes being looked at that way, and the mind protects itself by forgetting.

He had ridden nine days from the capital with papers signed by the Regent Sovereign's own chancery, and those papers bore a seal that made his mouth go dry every time he looked at it, though he would have died before showing it: the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things itself, stolen in the first week of the usurpation, pressed into wax by clerks who had never seen the guildhouse it belonged to. The usurper's court used it the way a poacher uses a landowner's whistle. *Come along, come along, everything here is lawful.* And doors opened, because a seal is a promise, and most people never learn to check whose mouth the promise comes out of.

"Sir," Ossen said carefully, "the brief. If I might review it once more."

"You may not. You know your part." Halven began walking, and the soldiers fell in behind him, boots crunching frost. "We are auditors conducting a routine inventory of esoteric holdings under emergency statute. We are polite. We are patient. We are *boring*, Captain, until the moment we are not. Do you understand? Fear makes people hide things. Boredom makes them hand them over."

"And if they refuse?"

"They won't." Halven smiled, which on his face was less an expression than a change in the light. "Guilds survive by being indispensable to whoever holds power. They will open their vaults and count their wonders for us and thank us for the privilege. That is what guilds are *for*."

They went down through the sleeping streets, past shuttered market stalls and a fountain wearing a skin of ice, and the guildhouse rose ahead of them with its impossible architecture — walls that leaned where walls should fall, windows set at angles that argued with the sunrise, a door of black oak carved with the guild's motto in letters that were said to rearrange themselves when read by liars. Halven had read accounts of that door. He intended to test it.

The watchman on the wall woke as they arrived, fumbled for a horn, and produced instead a series of sounds like a goose being murdered. Lights bloomed in upper windows. By the time Halven reached the foot of the steps, the great doors were already opening, and Guildmaster Vess stood in the gap between them with a lamp in her hand and her grey hair bound back like a woman preparing for surgery.

She was smaller than her reputation. Halven noted this with satisfaction. Reputations inflated; bodies did not.

"Guildmaster Vess." He inclined his head precisely enough to be courteous and not one degree further. "Halven Marr, Inquisitor-Auditor, Office of Esoteric Holdings, by warrant of the Regent Sovereign's chancery." He held out the papers and let her take them, watched her eyes move across the seal in the wax, watched the small stillness pass through her like a stone dropped down a well. There it was. She recognized her own guild's mark in a stranger's mouth. Good. Let her feel it.

"You've brought soldiers," she said.

"Auditors require protection on the roads. These are uncertain times." He gestured, and the twelve filed past him into the entry hall with the wagon-loads of empty ledgers following, and he watched Vess watch them, and he watched her decide — visibly, deliberately, the way a gambler decides — to be gracious about it.

"Then you'll want tea," she said. "And warmth. Come through."

* * *

They put him in the Map Room, because of course they did — a long chamber hung with charts of territories that no longer existed, drawn by cartographers who had mapped places that had since decided to stop existing, which was the sort of trade the guild had always done. Tea came. Bread and honey came. A fire was laid with a efficiency that suggested the chimney had been swept in anticipation of guests, and Halven sat with his hands around the cup and let the warmth soak into him while Vess took the chair opposite and folded her hands and became, with visible effort, a woman with nothing to hide.

"Eleven years," Halven said pleasantly, "since the capital last sent anyone. I confess I expected more resistance to our arrival. Or less courtesy."

"The guild serves whoever keeps the roads safe," Vess said. "That has always been our policy. Kings, regents, usurpers — the ledgers don't ask our opinion."

"No. Ledgers rarely do." He sipped the tea. It was very good tea, and he distrusted it accordingly. "Tell me about your prophecy holdings, Guildmaster."

"We keep a modest collection. Almanacs, astrological tables, the usual fortune-teller's stock. Perhaps two hundred volumes."

"Two hundred." He let the number sit between them like a coin he was deciding whether to bite. "My predecessor's files suggest rather more. There was a survey done forty years ago — before either of our tenures — that catalogued some six hundred items in this building alone, including several classified as *sealed*. Do you know what the sealed classification means, Guildmaster?"

"It means the guild judged them too dangerous to sell and too valuable to burn."

"It means," Halven said softly, "that someone in this building once believed the future could be owned. And the Regent Sovereign takes a simple view of such things. The future belongs to the state. Anything else is counterfeiting."

Vess's face did something very small — a tightening around the eyes, gone almost before it began. Halven had spent eleven years training himself to catch such things, the way a fisherman trains himself to catch the flick of a fin beneath dark water. He filed it away without letting his expression change.

"You'll have your inventory," she said. "Every volume, every scroll, every horoscope some hedge-witch sold my predecessor. My archivists will begin at once."

"Wonderful." He drank his tea. "One more matter, purely administrative. Your guild registers. I'll need the current roster. Names, trades, dates of admission."

Something moved behind her eyes then, quick as a mouse behind a wainscot. "The roster is public record. Any member may consult it."

"But outsiders may not."

"Outsiders," Vess said, "have historically been the reason the rule exists."

Halven laughed — a dry, genuine sound, surprising even himself. "Well played, Guildmaster. Very well. I'll content myself with the prophecy holdings today. The roster can wait for tomorrow. Or the day after." He set down his cup. "I should tell you — I'm told I'm a patient man, though I've never understood the compliment. Patience is just appetite with good posture. I will sit in this room, and drink this excellent tea, and audit your shelves one spine at a time for as many weeks as it pleases me. And while I sit, I will think. Thinking is my trade, Guildmaster. I am told the guild deals in impossible things. So do I. I collect them."

He rose. "Shall we begin with the vault?"

* * *

The descent into the undercroft took four turns of stairs and ended at a door of banded iron that Vess unlocked herself, refusing all offers of assistance, and Halven noted that too — the way her thumb lingered on the key, the way her shoulders squared as the lock turned, a woman bracing herself the way one braces before opening a letter one already knows the contents of.

Inside, the vault breathed cold and dust and older smells beneath them — vellum, ink, wax, and something faintly sweet and wrong, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Lamps guttered alight along the shelves as they entered, wicks catching without hands, and Halven felt the hair rise on his forearms and despised the feeling.

"Preservation charms," Vess said, watching him notice. "The guild's own work. Nothing here rots."

"Nothing here *changes*, you mean."

"If you prefer."

The audit began. Halven worked the way water works, finding every crack. He had brought four clerks of his own — quiet men with quiet hands — and they moved down the shelves pulling volumes, checking titles against the forty-year-old survey, calling numbers in flat voices while Vess's archivists fetched and answered and grew progressively wearier. Halven himself touched little. He walked. He looked. He asked questions that seemed idle and were not.

"This case is empty," he observed, pausing at a shelf near the vault's heart, where the air seemed densest. "Dust everywhere else, but here the shelf has been wiped. Recently, I'd say. Within the month."

"Inventory cleaning," said an archivist.

"Naturally." He ran one finger along the wood and examined it. "And this label, here — *Prophecy, Sealed Class, Pending Review*. The ink is fresh. Fresher than the dust would allow, if the book had sat here awaiting review for any length of time." He turned his head and smiled at the archivist, a young man with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of someone who had not slept. "When was the review conducted?"

"I—"

"The guild reviews sealed holdings annually," Vess said smoothly, arriving between them like a wall arriving. "It's in the charter. You're welcome to read it."

"Oh, I intend to read everything." Halven stepped back, hands clasped behind him, the picture of reasonableness. "I find, Guildmaster, that buildings talk. Shelves talk. Dust talks — dust is the most honest historian I know. People lie; dust merely accumulates. And the dust in this vault tells me that within the last month, at least one item of the sealed class was removed, examined, and returned — or not returned." He let the last words drop gently, like stones into still water. "Which is not an accusation. Merely an observation. Audits are made of observations."

"Then observe," Vess said, "and be done."

They were three hours in the vault. Halven counted, catalogued, and missed nothing on purpose, because missing things on purpose was how one learned what others considered worth hiding. Twice he saw the young archivist — Quill, the roster-less roster had eventually surrendered her name through a slip of Vess's tongue — glance toward a particular aisle with a frequency that exceeded chance. Twice he saw the guildmaster position herself between his clerks and the same aisle. And once, passing that aisle himself, he paused and drew a slow breath through his nose, and caught it: blood. Old iron and salt, the smell of blood soaked into paper, faint as a ghost but present, recent, *wrong*.

Someone had bled in this vault. Recently. On or near a book.

He said nothing at all. He thanked Vess for her hospitality. He requested a room for the night, and dinner, and the continued cooperation of her staff, and he smiled his light-changing smile, and upstairs, as the soldiers billeted in the guest wing and the clerks sharpened their pens, Halven Marr stood at a window overlooking the dark shape of the Weeping Wood and allowed himself, at last, to think.

* * *

The trouble with the swordspeakers, he reflected, watching the trees, was that everyone assumed they had died out because they had been defeated.

The histories said so. The usurper's histories said so loudest: the order broken at the Battle of the Ash Ford, its masters executed, its blades melted, its art extinguished like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Halven had read those histories until he could recite them, and then he had done what he did with all histories — he had audited them. And audits, he had found, are made of absences as much as presences. What struck him was not what the records contained but what they lacked: no list of the order's members at its fall. No accounting of the apprentices. Every other purged institution in the capital's archives came with rolls and rosters, names crossed out in red — but the swordspeakers' final census was missing entire. Torn out. Burned, perhaps. Or *kept*, by someone, somewhere, for reasons that had nothing to do with history.

An extinction with no body count was not an extinction. It was a disappearance. And disappearances, in Halven's experience, resolved themselves in one of two ways: into graves, or into children.

There had been a report, eleven years ago — buried, misfiled, the kind of document that surfaces only when a clerk dies and his successor cleans the drawer. A midwife in a river town, questioned in an unrelated matter of forged birth-records, who had mentioned in passing that she had once been paid an extraordinary sum to deliver a child into hiding, a child whose mother had been "of the blade-blood." The midwife had been hanged for the forgeries. The child had been a footnote. Footnotes, Halven knew, were where civilizations hid their living secrets.

He had spent eleven years pulling that single thread through the fabric of the provinces, patiently, invisibly, and the thread had led him — as all threads eventually led — to guilds. Because where does one hide a dangerous child? Among people whose trade is impossibility. Where does one bury a bloodline? In a ledger, between a bottled thunderstorm and a borrowed shadow, where no auditor thinks to look.

And now the chancery had finally given him what he'd needed all along: lawful pretext. An audit of prophecy holdings. Because prophecies, unlike people, could not lie about what they had seen — and somewhere in this crooked tower, he was certain, there was a book that had already written down the answer to a question Halven had been asking for a decade.

*The last swordspeaker.* He turned the phrase over like a coin. The reports from the capital spoke of unrest in the old forest provinces, of trees that whispered, of a Wood that had begun — the peasants swore it — to *move*. Superstition, the chancery said. But Halven had audited superstition long enough to know it was usually memory wearing rags. The Wood was waking. Things woke for reasons. And the oldest records agreed on one point with suspicious unanimity: the Wood slept only so long as no true name was spoken over it, and only the blade-blood carried the true name.

Below him, the valley lay dark, and the Weeping Wood stood at its edge like a congregation of mourners, and the wind came up the slope carrying that thin, wire-fine sound that might have been weeping and might have been wind and might have been neither.

"Sir?" Captain Ossen, at the door. "The guildmaster's staff has prepared supper. Also — the young archivist, the one called Quill. She asked whether you'd require anything further tonight. Tea. Papers. She was... helpful."

"Was she." Halven did not turn from the window. Helpful people interested him enormously; helpfulness was nearly always a transaction with the price written on the other side of the page. "Send my compliments and decline. And Ossen — tonight, I want two men walking the corridors. Quietly. I want to know which rooms stay lit past midnight, and which doors are locked that weren't locked yesterday, and if anyone goes down to the vault, I want their name before their second step."

"And if it's the guildmaster herself?"

"Then we shall have a fascinating conversation at breakfast." He watched the dark trees. Somewhere in that guildhouse, a book with his guild's stolen seal on its cover — no, that was wrong, the seal was on the *papers*, the seal was his leash and his license both — somewhere in this building was a page fused shut by hands a century dead, waiting for one specific reader, and the dust said the reader had already come, and the blood-smell said the reading had already begun, and the fresh ink on the shelf-label said someone was trying very hard to make a missing book look like a present absence.

People thought auditors hunted for what was there. Amateurs. Auditors hunted for what was *almost* there — the gap in the column, the year with no entries, the book-shaped hole in the dust.

"Ossen," he said. "Tomorrow morning, I want the full guild roster on my desk. Every name. Every date of admission. Especially admissions from eleven years ago — and especially any member registered as a foundling."

"Yes, sir." A pause. "You believe the swordspeaker's here, sir. Don't you. In this guild."

Halven smiled at the Weeping Wood, at the thousand pale tears running down the dark bark, at the whole listening dark of it, and felt — not for the first time, but more strongly than ever before — the peculiar sensation of being *watched back*.

"I believe," he said, "that everything in this valley is holding its breath. And things only hold their breath, Captain, when they're afraid of being heard."

Behind him, down in the warm belly of the guildhouse, he heard the front doors open and close — someone going out into the night, footsteps soft on frost, moving not toward the town but toward the treeline, toward the Wood, quick and quiet and desperate.

Halven Marr did not turn around. He simply began, silently, to count the seconds between the door's closing and the moment he would ring for Captain Ossen — and to wonder, with the patience of a man who had waited eleven years and could wait eleven more, exactly which of them had just run, and what, precisely, they had taken with them into the trees.
