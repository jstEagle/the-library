## 7. The Fused Page Opens

The crate smelled of cardamom and old rope.

Coral had chosen it deliberately — a shipment of spice-paper bound for the coastal houses, the kind of cargo no auditor would waste an hour on. Bottled thunder drew eyes. Borrowed shadows drew eyes. Dried lichen and pressed flowers from some minor guildhouse in the hill country drew nothing at all. He had buried the fused book beneath six layers of packing straw, wrapped it in oilcloth, and told himself that was enough. Told himself it twice, which was once more than he needed, which meant it wasn't.

The undercroft was empty at this hour. The night-bell had rung its third peal; the lamplighters were making their rounds above, and their footsteps came through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat. Coral sat cross-legged beside the crate with a shuttered lantern turned low, and he did not open it for a long time. He simply listened to the building breathe around him — the tick of settling timber, the far-off sigh of the aqueduct, the small industrious sounds of mice in the grain stores. A life built of ordinary noises. He had always found it comforting before tonight.

Now every sound felt like a held breath.

*You are being ridiculous,* he told himself, in the crisp internal voice he used when correcting a misfiled entry. *A book is a book. Pages are pages. Whatever is written there was written a hundred years ago by someone who wanted to frighten an archivist, and it has frightened you, and that is the whole of the miracle.*

But his hands, when he finally reached into the straw, were not steady.

* * *

He unwrapped the oilcloth on his knees, in the lantern's narrow cone of light, the way one handles a thing that might be sleeping. The book was smaller than he remembered — memory does that, swelling what frightens us — bound in grey leather gone soft as bark, its corners worn round as river stones. No title. No guild seal. Only the faint impression on the cover where a stamp had once been scraped away, leaving a ghost of letters too shallow to read.

He opened it to the first page, the one he had already read, the one that had ruined the shape of his evening three nights ago:

*On this day, in the year the ledgers call 1147, the last swordspeaker opens the sealed page.*

Today's date. Inked a century before he was born, in a hand that slanted leftward, hurried, as though the writer had been cold or afraid or both. Coral had audited ten thousand entries in his years below the Guild of Impossible Things. He knew forgery the way a fisherman knows rivers — every current, every eddy, every place where a lie liked to hide. Paper aged falsely showed itself in the fibers. Ink aged falsely bloomed the wrong colors under lamp-oil. This page passed every test he knew. It was genuinely old. Genuinely written. And it was waiting for him the way a trap waits, patient and specific, down to the hour.

And the second page — the second page was fused shut.

Not glued. Not waxed. Fused, as if the paper itself had decided to become one sheet, two surfaces melted into a single skin with no seam, no edge to catch a fingernail, no honest way in. He had tried a bone folder, a warm knife, steam. The page had regarded these attentions with the serene indifference of stone. Books did not do this. Books were made of dead things — flax, rag, calf, soot — and dead things did not refuse you. Refusal was a property of the living.

Which meant, Coral thought, turning the book over slowly in his hands, that either the page was not dead, or he was wrong about what books were.

He set the lantern closer. Its light lay along the fused surface and he saw something he had missed before: not smoothness, exactly, but texture — a grain running through the sealed page like the grain of wood, fine whorls circling outward from a point just off-center, as though the page had grown rather than been pressed. As though somewhere inside the paper there was a knot, a heart, a ring-counting center, and everything else had closed over it the way a tree closes over a wound.

His thumb rested against the page's edge without his deciding it.

"Right," he said aloud, because the undercroft was empty and saying things aloud made them smaller. "One more attempt. Properly documented." He took out his pocket ledger — the little one, the personal one, the one where he recorded trades too strange for the official books — and wrote, in his neat archivist's hand: *Attempted unsealing, fourth. Method: patience.* He dated it. He almost laughed at himself. Even now, even alone, even afraid, he kept the books. Perhaps especially then. The habit was older than his fear and would outlast it, whatever it turned out to be.

Then he bent the corner of the fused page — gently, only to test the give —

— and the paper bit him.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible part, how undramatic it was. A papercut, the smallest kind, a bright hairline sting across the pad of his thumb, the sort of wound he'd collected a hundred times in twenty years among ledgers. A bead of blood welled up, absurdly red against the grey page, and he put his thumb instinctively to his mouth the way any person does, and for one heartbeat nothing happened at all.

In the second heartbeat, the blood sank into the paper.

Not soaked. Sank — drawn inward, the drop narrowing, thinning, threading down into the page's grain the way water goes down into dry earth, and where it went the whorls darkened, ring by ring by ring, spreading outward from the point of contact like frost flowering across a window, except the color was not white but deep living brown, the brown of heartwood, of sapwood, of the rings Wren — no. Of trees. The brown of trees.

"Oh," said Coral, very quietly, to no one.

The page opened.

There was no tearing, no splitting. The two surfaces simply remembered they had been two, and parted along a line that had been waiting in them all along, soft as a leaf letting go. The air of the undercroft moved — he felt it on his face before he understood why, a breath of wind in a windowless room, carrying a smell that did not belong beneath the city: rain on bark, green rot, something sweet and wounded. The smell of a forest after a storm. The smell, he realized with a vertigo that arrived a half-second behind the knowing, of the Weeping Wood, which stood forty miles north and had never once sent its weather down to the capital.

The new page was written in the same slanted, hurried hand. But the ink here was different — darker, glossy, and it caught the lantern light the way sap catches light. He read it the way he read everything, first fast and then slow, and the fast reading was a mistake, because the words went into him whole, unguarded, like a splinter entering skin:

*The swordspeaker flees through the weeping trees, and the trees know his footfall, and the hour of the speaking is at hand. What was bound in blood will be loosed in blood. Let him count the cost before he counts the leaves.*

Below that, the ink changed. The hurried hand gave way to another — rounder, calmer, added later, the way marginalia is added later — and this second hand had written only four words, pressed hard enough into the paper that he could feel them with a fingertip, braille-deep:

*We are still here.*

* * *

Coral sat very still for a long time.

The lantern flame leaned and righted itself, leaned and righted itself, though there was no draft. The wind-smell strengthened. Somewhere above, the lamplighters' footsteps had stopped, and the silence they left behind had a texture to it, a listening quality, as if the whole guildhouse had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it.

He should document this. The thought arrived on schedule, faithful as the night-bell. He reached for his pocket ledger and got as far as uncapping his pen, and then his hand stopped, hovering, and he understood that he could not write it down. Not because it was forbidden — Vess had forbidden plenty, and he had found his ways around her prohibitions already — but because writing it would make it an entry, and entries lived in ledgers, and ledgers were orderly, and this was not orderly. This was the opposite of orderly. This was a date that had waited a hundred years for his thumb to bleed on the correct night, and a page that drank his blood like a root drinking rain, and a sentence that described — he checked the date again, uselessly, a third time, a fourth — this hour. Not this day. This hour. *The hour of the speaking is at hand.* He had opened the page at the third bell. The prophecy had known. The prophecy had been keeping time with him, patient as an auditor, exact as an auditor, and Coral Brightwater, who trusted nothing he could not enter in a ledger, had just discovered that he was himself an entry in someone else's.

The vertigo came properly then, rolling up through the floor.

He gripped the crate's edge. The planks were trembling — actually trembling, a fine shiver in the wood, and he thought at first it was the aqueduct, the great water-works shuddering on their schedule, but the schedule was wrong, the rhythm was wrong, the tremor rose and fell like breathing. Like many breaths. He put his palm flat against the plank and the vibration climbed into his arm and settled behind his sternum, and it was not unpleasant. That was the worst discovery of the night so far: it was not unpleasant. It felt like recognition. It felt like the moment in a crowded market when, across fifty strangers, you catch the eye of the one person you were hoping for.

Outside the high slit-window, the city bells began to ring — not the night-bell, not any hour he knew. All of them at once, every tower, discordant, urgent, and then, mid-peal, they stopped together, all of them, as if a hand had been laid flat across the whole ringing city.

And then the dogs began. Every dog in the lower quarters, howling at nothing, at the north, at the sky.

Coral blew out the lantern.

He did not decide to; his body decided, some old animal prudence older than the guild, older than his oaths. In the sudden dark the fused book glowed — faintly, terribly, the whorl-lines in the opened page holding a light of their own, dim amber, the color of sap lit from within, the color of the lamps the guild sold to people who needed light where no fire could live. The light pulsed. With his pulse. He watched it quicken as his fear quickened and hated it and could not look away.

"The trees know my footfall," he said to the dark, testing the sentence, weighing it like a coin he suspected was clipped. "I haven't taken a step."

But even as he said it he was standing, and even as he stood he was wrapping the book — the oilcloth seemed to cling to it reluctantly, the knots fighting his fingers — and even as he buried it again in the straw he knew, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own handwriting, that hiding it had stopped meaning anything. You cannot hide a thing that is happening. Prophecy had stopped being text. It had become weather. It was in the trembling planks and the silenced bells and the forest-smell filling a cellar forty miles from any forest, and weather does not stay in crates, and it does not wait for audits.

Above him, boots. Many boots, crossing the counting-house floor in the pattern soldiers make — not the loose scatter of guildmates, but ranks, files, purpose. Halven Marr had come back. Or Halven Marr had never left. Either way, the inquisitor was between Coral and every stair he knew, and the undercroft had one other exit, the sally-door, the old smugglers' door in the foundation wall that the guild pretended not to have and every guildmate used at least once a year.

He was moving before he finished the thought, crate repacked, ledger pocketed, the book's glow fading behind him like a lamp carried away down a long corridor. At the sally-door he paused with his hand on the bar, and the pause cost him, because in it he heard the doors above open, and a voice he knew — Halven's voice, unhurried, courteous, the voice of a man who has never once needed to raise it — say something he couldn't make out, followed by Guildmaster Vess's voice, higher than he had ever heard it.

They were buying him time. Or spending him. With Vess he could no longer tell the difference, and grief for that — real grief, sudden and sharp — nearly kept him standing there to be found.

The bar lifted. Cold air poured in, smelling of the canal, and beyond the canal, faint under the city's stink, unmistakable now, the green wounded sweetness of the Weeping Wood.

The night sky over the northern rooftops was wrong. Clouds stood over the forest in a column, unmoving, turning slowly like something stirred, and lightning flickered inside them without thunder — silent lightning, patient lightning, the kind the guild bottled and sold in jars labeled *improbable*. The Wood was doing it itself. The Wood was bottling its own weather, and aiming it south.

Coral stepped out into the alley, pulled his hood up, and ran.

His feet found the old routes without consulting him — the cut-through behind the tanneries, the stair down to the towpath, the low bridge where the watch never looked. He had walked them a thousand times and never noticed, and now he understood that he had never noticed because he had never needed to flee before, and the knowledge of escape routes had been sitting in his legs all along like a letter unopened. His body knew things his ledgers didn't. His body had been keeping its own records.

At the north gate — barred for the night, guarded by two men whose spears suddenly seemed very long — he did not use the gate. There was a gap in the wall where the masons had never finished, behind the dyer's sheds, a crawl of broken brick that children used and adults forgot. He went through it tearing his sleeve and skinning both palms and did not care, and came out on the downs beyond the walls with the whole sleeping capital at his back and the road north unrolling pale under the moon.

Forty miles. He had supplies for none of them. He had told no one. Sera's face crossed his mind — Sera on the ninth stair, Sera laughing at his careful handwriting, Sera who covered for him without asking why — and he almost turned back for her, and did not, because turning back was death and she would want him alive, and because he could not yet say the truer reason, which was that some part of him already suspected she was safer believing he was gone.

Behind him, the city bells began again, all together, and this time they did not stop.

Ahead, the Weeping Wood stood black against the stars, and as he ran — mile after mile, lungs burning, the road giving way to cart-track, cart-track to drover's path, drover's path to nothing with a name — he watched it change. The treeline, fixed his whole life, a constant on every map he had ever filed, was moving. Slowly, hugely, the way a glacier moves, the way a tide moves: the outer trees leaning southward, lifting their roots out of the earth and setting them down again a yard nearer the city, and nearer, and nearer. Trees did not walk. He had catalogued impossible things for eleven years and he had never once entered *a walking tree*, because some impossibilities were too large for the ledgers, and he had been wrong about that, wrong about the ledgers, wrong about what the word *ledger* had ever contained.

The wind coming off the Wood carried voices. Not words — not yet — but the shapes of words, the way a shell carries the shape of the sea. And threaded through them, so faint he could believe he imagined it, so familiar he knew he didn't, something whispering fragments of a tune. A lullaby. One he had hummed as a child in the guild nursery and never learned the name of, because no one in the guild would admit to teaching it to him.

The trees knew his footfall. He had argued with the prophecy about that, hours ago, in a cellar, over a line of ink.

Now the ground itself was arguing back.

* * *

Dawn found him at the forest's edge, bleeding, breathless, and utterly calm — the strange calm of a man who has run past the last point where running home is possible. The first trees stood over him like witnesses. Their bark glistened. From every seam of it, slow beads of sap gathered and fell, gathered and fell, weeping without wind, weeping without wound, and where the drops struck the ground the moss drank them and glowed.

Coral put his torn hand against the nearest trunk.

The tree sighed — a long, shivering exhalation, the sound of something that has been holding its breath for a hundred years — and every tear on every tree within sight fell at once, a silver rain with no cloud above it, and in the hush after, the Wood spoke its first clear word.

It said his name.

Not *Brightwater*. Not *Coral*. Some other name, older, longer, spoken in a voice like roots shifting underground — and the moment it touched his ears, the fused book, still wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs, grew warm as a living thing.
