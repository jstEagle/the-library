## 8. Into the Weeping Wood

The name the Wood spoke was not a word. It was a weather.

It moved through him the way a storm front moves through a valley — pressure first, then a ringing in his teeth, then the strange stillness at the center where everything holds itself very carefully and waits to see what breaks. Coral stood with his hand against the bleeding trunk and felt that name settle into his bones like water into dry ground, finding channels he had not known were there, filling them, claiming them.

*That is not my name,* he thought, and even as he thought it, he knew the thought was a lie told by a man who had lived thirty years inside a smaller house than the one he'd been born to.

Behind him — far behind, back through the darkening streets of Lowmarket, back past the shuttered counting-houses and the guildhall with its lamps burning late for people who were already hunting him — came the sound of boots. Not many yet. Six men, maybe eight, moving in the disciplined cadence Halven Marr drilled into everything he touched. They would have found the crate by now. They would have found the oilcloth, warm from his body, smelling of tallow and fear. They would have found the trail of blood from his torn palm, small dark coins dropped on the cobbles like an honest merchant's ledger of exactly where he had gone.

Coral Brightwater had kept accounts his whole life. He knew what his own ledger said.

He ran.

The treeline took him the way a mouth takes a word — completely, without chewing.

One moment there was road under his feet, gravel and ruts and the pale dust of cartwheels; the next there was root-lace and loam so old it had stopped being dirt and become something closer to memory. The light changed too. The last of the sunset came down through the canopy in long amber blades, and everywhere between them hung the slow silver fall of sap-tears, each one catching the light as it let go of its branch, so that running through the Wood was like running through a room where someone had shattered a chandelier and all the pieces were still falling, patiently, forever.

He heard the soldiers reach the treeline behind him and stop.

He heard them stop because the Wood did not want him to lose the sound. That was the first thing he understood, gasping, three hundred paces in, hands out before him against the gathering dark: the trees were *telling* him things. Not in words, not yet, not in anything so clean as words — but the nearest trunk shivered when the soldiers shifted their weight at the forest's edge, and the shiver ran down through the root-system under his feet and up through his boot-soles into the small bones of his ankles, and Coral, who had spent his life reading other people's handwriting, read it without trying.

*They are afraid to come in.*

Of course they were. Everyone was afraid of the Weeping Wood. Children in Lowmarket were warned off it with rhymes; caravans paid tolls to go around it; the guild's own ledgers recorded, in the flat ink of routine trade, the occasional fool who went in and did not come out. The Wood wept and whispered and sometimes, the stories said, walked. Halven's soldiers were brave men in the way that well-fed dogs are brave, and they stood at the treeline now like dogs at the edge of deep water.

Then a voice carried through the trees — Halven's voice, or one of his captains', pitched to carry, smooth as oiled slate:

"Swordspeaker! The Guild has surrendered its claim on you. Come out, and you will be treated as a witness, not a heretic." A pause, measured, generous. "Stay, and the Wood burns with you in it. We have tar enough for every tree."

The lie was beautifully made. Coral almost admired it. *The Guild has surrendered its claim on you* — which meant Vess had given him up, or been made to look as though she had, or was at this moment trading years of careful neutrality for the slim chance that Halven's patience outlasted his ambition. He thought of her face when she'd ordered him to seal the vault. *Forget it, Coral.* She had known. She had known for years, and she had chosen the kind lie, and the kind lie had just been sold to the man with the tar-carts.

The trees around him went very still.

Not quiet — still. There is a difference, and Coral felt it in his teeth. A quiet forest is a forest minding its own business. This was the stillness of a thousand living things turning their attention, all at once, toward the sound of fire being promised.

"Don't," Coral said aloud, to the trees, feeling immediately like a madman. "Don't do anything. Just — don't."

The stillness held. Then, slowly, reluctantly, like a dog lying back down, the Wood exhaled, and the tears began falling again, silver in the dusk, and somewhere above him a branch groaned and settled.

*It listens,* he thought. *It listened to me.*

He should have found that terrifying. He filed it away instead, in the place where he kept impossible trades and dates written a century early — the place that had been getting fuller all week and heavier, until it ached like a splinter working toward the surface.

* * *

He walked for hours, because running was noise and noise was death, and because some part of him — the part that had spoken the first word of command over a bound blade at nine years old and never once wondered *why him* — knew that depth was safer than distance here. The Wood did not want him gone. The Wood wanted him *in*.

The dark came down and did not behave like ordinary dark. Under the canopy there was no moon to speak of, no stars, only the black lattice of branches against a sky the color of deep water — and yet he could see. Faintly. The way one sees in a remembered room. The tears themselves gave off a pale ghost-light as they fell, and the sap that bled from every wound in every trunk glimmered like veins of quicksilver under bark, tracing patterns that were almost, but not quite, writing.

He tried not to look at the patterns. He looked anyway. He was an auditor; patterns were his native country.

And the whispers — the famous whispers of the Weeping Wood, the ones the rhymes warned children about — they were not wind. He had half expected wind, or owls, or the ordinary machinery of night forest. Instead the voices came from inside the trunks, low and layered, dozens of them braided together, and they were saying—

"—the blue door, mind the blue door, mind—"

"—count them twice, count them twice, the little one always counts twice—"

"—burned the porridge again, burned it, laughed about it, burned it—"

Coral stopped walking.

Those were not prophecies. Those were not warnings. Those were fragments of a childhood — *his* childhood, the blue door of the foundling-house on Tanner's Row, the way he had counted the orphanage's spoons twice every evening because the matron accused him once of losing one, the time he burned the porridge and laughed until he cried because he was seven and it was the worst thing that had ever happened to him.

No one alive knew those things. Vess might have known the porridge; Vess had raised him, after whatever fashion guildmasters raise foundlings. But the blue door, the spoons, the laughing — those were his alone, unrecorded, unwritten, locked in the one ledger no auditor ever audits.

The trees were reciting his life back to him.

"Who are you?" he whispered. His voice sounded obscene in the hush, loud and mortal. "What are you?"

The whispering paused — the whole Wood, pausing, the way a room pauses when someone says something true — and then, from everywhere and nowhere, soft as sap falling:

"*Ours.*"

One word. It went through him like cold water through cloth.

He walked on. What else does a man do, when the forest claims him? He walked on, and the fragments kept coming, gentler now, less insistent — snatches of his own past drifting between the trunks like smoke: his first swordspeaking, the oath-words hot in his mouth; the smell of the undercroft's candle-wax; Sera's laugh, Sera's name, Sera's voice saying *you count everything twice, Coral, except yourself* — and at that fragment the ache in his chest was so sudden and specific that he had to stop and put his hand against a trunk to steady himself, and the trunk bled a fresh tear onto his torn palm, and the sting of it was almost a comfort, because pain was simple and belonged to the world he understood.

The book against his ribs stayed warm. Warmer, as he went deeper. He could feel its heat through the oilcloth and his shirt, a small patient coal, and he found himself thinking of the fused page, the second page, the one his blood had opened: *the swordspeaker flees through the weeping trees.*

Flees. Present tense, a century old. He was not fleeing *through* the prophecy; he was walking along inside it, a figure moving through text, and somewhere ahead of him the next line was already written, waiting in ink he hadn't read yet.

Ahead, something waited. He could feel it the way you feel a listener in the next room — not hostile, not friendly, simply *attending*. And beneath the fear, beneath the exhaustion soaking through him like the damp through his boots, there was the other thing, the thing he could not say aloud, the thing that felt like treason against thirty years of guild-raised life:

It felt like going home.

The guildhall had never felt like home. He had told himself it did for decades — the familiar creak of the third stair, the smell of the ledger-room, Sera's lamp burning in the ninth-stair archive — but familiarity was not belonging; he had audited enough estates to know the difference between a house and a holding. The guild had *held* him. It had fed him, trained him, named him, taught him to bind his wonder into neat columns and his power into serviceable oaths. It had never once asked him who he was, because it had already decided, and the decision fit him the way a borrowed coat fits: adequately, and always slightly wrong at the shoulders.

This forest, this weeping, whispering, forbidden dark — it knew the shape of him. Knew his childhood. Knew his name, the true one, the one that rang in his bones like a struck bell. Terror walked beside him step for step, yes, cold hand on the back of his neck. But under the terror ran something older and warmer, a current under ice, and both of them together made a music he had no name for except, finally, at the edge of sleep-walking exhaustion:

*Belonging.*

* * *

Near midnight, the trees opened.

There was no warning to it — one stride he was shoulder-deep in close dark growth, the next he stepped into a clearing where the canopy pulled back and let the actual stars down, hundreds of them, cold and enormous. The clearing was round as a coin and utterly silent. No whispers here. No tears falling. The great trees that ringed it leaned slightly inward, like elders bending over a cradle, and at its center stood a single tree unlike any he had seen: younger, slender, its bark pale as birch and unmarked by any wound, its leaves silver on their undersides so that when the night breeze moved them, the whole tree shimmered like a shoal of fish.

No tears on this one. Every other tree in the Wood wept. This one had *stopped*, or had never started, and somehow that was the most unsettling thing he had seen all day.

At its base, the roots rose and knotted into something almost like a seat, almost like an altar, and on the ground before it lay objects — offerings, or leavings: a rusted sword-hilt green with age, a child's wooden bird, a clay cup, a strip of cloth embroidered in a pattern Coral's eye snagged on and could not leave, because he had seen that pattern before, stitched into the hem of a blanket he'd been found wrapped in, twenty-nine years ago, on the steps of the guildhall.

His breath went out of him slowly.

The book against his ribs flared hot — actually hot now, hot enough that he fumbled the oilcloth loose with shaking fingers — and in the starlight he saw that the fused page, the second page, the page his blood had opened, was no longer showing the words he'd read in the vault. The ink had moved. Ink did not move; ink was the one honest thing in the world, fixed and final, and yet there on the century-old vellum, in a hand that was unmistakably his own — his own cramped auditor's script, down to the way he crossed his sevens — new lines were writing themselves even as he watched:

*The swordspeaker comes to the still tree.*
*The swordspeaker asks the question he has carried since the blue door.*
*And the Wood sends one who remembers what he has forgotten.*

The pen-strokes finished. The ink dried. The clearing held its breath.

Coral stood in the ring of leaning elders with a self-writing prophecy warm against his chest and the wreckage of his certainties scattered around him like the offerings at the tree's feet, and he did the only thing left in his inventory. He asked.

"What am I?"

The words fell into the silence and did not echo. The Wood swallowed them whole.

And then, from the darkness beyond the ring of trees — from the direction of the deep Wood, the heart-Wood, the places even smugglers' maps marked with blank space — came footsteps. Unhurried. Bare feet on loam, he somehow knew, though he could not have said how. A figure resolved at the clearing's edge, backlit by the faint silver bleed of the wounded trees behind her, and she walked toward him across the starlit grass without hesitation, the way a person walks toward a door they have opened ten thousand times.

She was looking at him as if he were the answer to a question she had been asking for years.

"You're late," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the second word, and she pressed her hand briefly over her mouth as if to hold something in. When she lowered it, her eyes were bright and wet as the tears on the trees. "You're so late, and you don't remember me at all, do you? No. Of course not. They took that too."

She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see the leaf-veins of silver standing in her irises, close enough that the warmth coming off her was wrong for the night air, warmer, greener, like sun through a canopy.

"My name is Wren Ashvale," she said. "And I am the only person left in the world who knows your true name — besides the Wood, and besides you, though you don't know you know it." Her voice steadied, the way voices do when they have rehearsed a moment for a very long time. "I've come to teach it to you. And Coral—" she used the false name gently, the way one touches a bruise, "—before we begin, there is something I have to tell you about the woman you loved, and the life they cut out of you, and why every year on this night, I walk to this clearing and wait."

The tears on the ring of trees began to fall, all together, silently, like a congregation weeping at a wedding.

"And tonight," Wren Ashvale said, "you came."
