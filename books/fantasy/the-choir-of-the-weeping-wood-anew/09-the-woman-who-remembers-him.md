## 9. The Woman Who Remembers Him

Wren Ashvale had been counting the years in rings.

That was the old way of the wardens — the spirit-wardens who had once been many and were now, by her best reckoning, one. You pressed your palm to the bark of an elder tree and you read, the way a scholar reads a ledger, the slow annals of the Wood: the wet year, the burning year, the long gray century when nothing grew at the treeline because the world beyond had decided the forest was a thing to be spent. She had read those rings so often she could recite them. She had read them so often that when a new ring appeared — thin, frightened, written in sap that ran like tears down the heart-tree's face — she knew before any bird cried out that someone was coming.

Not soldiers. Soldiers rang wrong, like coins dropped on stone. This was something else. Something the roots had been waiting for with a patience older than her grief.

*He is coming,* the trees said, in the way trees say things, which is not words but pressure, a leaning of the whole green mind of the Wood toward a single point on the map. *The oath-tongue. The last one. He is coming home.*

Wren stood in the deep hollow where the birches grew white as bone and did not let herself hope. Hope was a habit she had buried twice already — once when the curse took the swordspeakers' order apart, and once, worse, when it took him. She had learned to live on memory the way other creatures lived on blood, and she had told herself, for a hundred years and then some, that this was enough.

But her hands were shaking. So much for enough.

She heard him before she saw him — crashing through the bracken with none of a soldier's economy, all elbows and breath, the way he had always moved when he thought no one who mattered was watching. A branch whipped back and caught his shoulder and he made a small wounded sound, undignified, human, and Wren had to press her knuckles to her mouth because that sound had followed her through a century of quiet like a bell rung once in an empty house.

He came around the birch with a drawn sword — of course he did; they never came to the Wood without the blade, even now, even penitent — and stopped dead when he saw her.

He was younger than she remembered. That was the first cruelty. The curse had kept her as she was, more or less, the way amber keeps a fly, but he had been allowed to be born, to grow, to become someone new, and the man standing in the fern-light with sap on his sleeves and terror in his eyes was not the man she had loved. He was the man she had loved *remade*, and she would have to learn him the way you learn a song transposed into a new key — every note familiar, everything shifted.

"You're not a soldier," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat and tried again, steadier, and she recognized the effort of it, the little discipline of calm he wore like borrowed clothing. "You're not one of Marr's."

"No," Wren said.

"The trees—" He looked up, and she watched him look up, watched the weeping birches lean toward him the way sunflowers lean toward light, watched him feel it and distrust what he felt. "They've been saying things. Or I think they have. I think I'm losing my mind, which under the circumstances might be the merciful outcome."

"What are they saying?"

His jaw worked. "My name," he said finally. "But not my name. Another one. Longer." He laughed, short and unhappy. "Which proves I've gone mad, because names don't work like that."

"Say it," Wren said. "The long one. Say it to me."

"I don't— I can't pronounce it. It's like trying to hold water." He dragged a hand through his hair, and there, at the hairline, above his left ear, she saw it: the faint pale mark, old as infancy, where the warden's sigil had been pressed into his skin to hide him from the inquisitors' ledgers. She had pressed it herself. Her hands had been smaller then, or time had made them seem so.

"You haven't gone mad," she said. "You've gone *remembered*. There's a difference, though I grant it feels the same from inside."

"Who are you?" He hadn't lowered the sword. Good. A man who lowered his sword for a stranger in a whispering wood deserved to die in it. "How do you know what the trees are saying? What *are* you?"

"Wren Ashvale," she said. "Spirit-warden. Last of the order, unless the order has grown without my notice, which the rings say it hasn't." She stepped closer, slowly, palms open, and the sword point tracked her like a nervous animal's eye. "And I know what the trees are saying because I taught them language, a very long time ago, along with a woman whose name you used to know and have forgotten, which is the way of curses. They take the sweetest things first."

Something moved behind his face — not recognition, recognition would come later or never, but its shadow, the flinch of a body remembering a blow the mind has filed away. "I don't know you," he said. And then, quieter, almost angry at himself: "Except that I do. Which is impossible."

"Most true things are," Wren said. "Sit down, Coral Brightwater. You've been running since sundown and the Wood will not let anything find you here that I do not permit. And I have a great deal to tell you, and very little night left in which to tell it."

* * *

He did not sit. He paced instead, a tight circuit between two birches, sword still bare, while Wren built a fire the old way — no flint, only a word in the tree-tongue held against a knot of deadwood until the wood remembered warmth. He watched her do it and she felt the watching change quality: from suspicion to hunger.

"Do that again," he said.

"No."

"It's swordspeech." His voice had gone strange. "Or near it. The grammar's different but the — the *shape* of it, the way you hold the word before you spend it — I know that shape. I've done that shape my whole life and I never asked where it came from. Oaths are just words. Everyone says oaths are just words with a guild seal on them." He stopped pacing. "Oaths aren't just words."

"No," Wren said. "Oaths are chains with pretty links. Sit down, Coral."

"Don't call me that."

"It's your name."

"It's the name on my ledger entries." But he sat, abruptly, as if his legs had been argued out from under him, and drew his knees up, and looked young again, and lost. "It's the name Vess gave me. Guildmaster Vess. She found me, I think — I was raised in the undercroft, I never asked about before. There wasn't a before. There was the guild, and the ledgers, and the trades nobody else could broker, and my voice, which makes blades obey." He turned the sword over in his hands without looking at it, the way a man turns over a tool he has begun to suspect. "Tonight I found today's date in a book a hundred years old. Written before tonight. About me. And the page after it was sealed, and when it opened it had my blood on it, and the trees outside the city started moving, and an inquisitor named Halven Marr wants me dead or owned, and I ran into the one forest in the world that whispers my name in a language I don't speak." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and he was too proud to wipe them. "So you'll understand if I don't sit calmly while a stranger tells me what I've forgotten."

"Yes," Wren said. "I understand exactly. I did the same thing, the first time someone told me what I was."

"And what was that?"

"A liar," she said, "who had believed her own lie for so long it had grown roots. Sit. Listen. I'll tell it in order, and the order is cruel, but cruelty in order is kinder than kindness scattered."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a nightjar called, three notes descending, and the birches answered with a sound like rain that never reached the ground.

"Before the guild," Wren said, "there was the Order of the Speaking Sword. Before the Order, there was the Wood entire — every forest from the Salt Reach to the Grey Fells one continuous green mind, and we who tended it were its wardens, its translators, its friends. We didn't command the spirits. We *conversed*. Do you understand the difference? Your swordspeech commands. It was built to command. It was carved out of our conversation the way a knife is carved out of a living tree — same material, opposite purpose."

"Carpenters don't apologize to chairs," Coral said quietly.

"No. They don't. And the speakers didn't apologize to us." She fed the fire another word, gently. "There was a war. Of course there was a war; there is always a war, and someone always profits, and this time it was the speakers. They bound the spirits into blades and engines and walls. They spoke the deep names and made the Wood build their cities. And when the spirits resisted, the speakers did the unforgivable arithmetic: they calculated how much forest a victorious army required, and they burned the rest. Three centuries of green. Ash to the horizon. I was there. I carried children out of groves that were screaming." Her voice did not shake; she had spent a hundred years sanding the shake out of it. "I am telling you this so you understand that when I say your order was founded in guilt, I am not insulting you. I am describing the only decent thing your ancestors ever did."

"The curse," Coral said. "The ledgers mention a curse. Nobody knows its terms."

"The terms were elegant. Curses usually are; hatred is a careful craftsman. The last of the speakers, dying, were given a choice by the Wood's remnant: die entirely, or serve. Bind your gift forever to penance. Speak only to protect, never to possess. Raise your heirs ignorant of what the gift was *for*, so that the knowledge of enslavement would rot unpassed. And the Wood, in turn, swore to sleep — to withhold itself from the world, to forgive nothing but to revenge nothing either, until a speaker came who could hold the true name in his mouth and choose, freely, with full knowledge, what to do with it." She met his eyes. "Every oath you have ever sworn, Coral Brightwater, every blade you have commanded, every impossible trade the guild has brokered with your voice — all of it was penance wearing livery. You were raised to be the installment payment on a debt you didn't know existed."

The silence went on so long that the fire had time to settle twice.

"All right," he said at last, very evenly, the way men speak when evenness is costing them everything. "All right. Say I believe you. Say the guild is a penitent's order and my life is somebody else's apology. What does that make *you* to me? Why me? Why here? Why does the forest know a name I've never heard?"

And there it was. The question she had rehearsed for a hundred years and would now fail to answer well, because rehearsal is nothing against his actual face.

"Because you've heard it," Wren said. "You just weren't allowed to keep it."

* * *

She told him about the life before the curse's last turn — carefully, in pieces, the way you feed a fire that must not flare. She told him about a warden and a speaker, an impossible friendship that the war had made inevitable and the peace had made possible: how they had met at a truce-ground where both their orders sent representatives to pretend at diplomacy, and had discovered within a season that they shared a heresy, namely that the other side were people. She told him about years spent teaching each other languages — he teaching her the grammar of command so she could better resist it, she teaching him the grammar of converse so he could better refuse it. She told him about a plan, half treason and half theology, to unmake the binding-names and free the bound, and how they had gotten as far as learning the Wood's true name together, syllable by syllable, trading it back and forth like lovers trade a secret neither should carry alone.

"He hid it in his blood," she said. "That was the only vault the inquisitors couldn't audit. The true name of the Weeping Wood, folded into a bloodline, passed down father to child in silence, waiting for an heir who would also inherit the choice. Because the name isn't a key, Coral. Keys just open things. The name is a *question*. The Wood asks it of whoever speaks it: *what is in your heart as you say me?* Answer with hate, and the spirits wake armed. Answer with love, and they wake free. Same word. Same mouth. Two worlds."

"And the heir," Coral said. His face had gone very still. "What happened to the heir?"

"The inquisitors found us," Wren said. "They always find us; that is the one thing they have never failed at. We ran. There was a child by then — weeks old, born in a hollow like this one, with birch leaves for a cradle. We split up, because a hunted pair runs slower than a hunted one, and the plan was—" Her voice did shake now, once, and she let it. "The plan was that I would draw them east and he would carry the child north to the guild, to the penitents, who owed our family protection from before the war and had never once failed to pay it. The last thing he said to me was that the boy would grow up safe and stupid and happy, knowing nothing, owing nothing, and that ignorance was the kindest inheritance he could give." She looked at the fire. "He was right about the safety. He was wrong about the rest. Ignorance is not kind. Ignorance is just a debt with the interest compounding."

Coral was not looking at the fire. He was looking at her with an expression she had seen only once before, across a truce-fire, on a younger man's face: the look of someone doing terrible arithmetic and arriving at a sum he cannot put down.

"The mark," he said. His hand rose, unbidden, to the pale scar above his ear. "Above my ear. I always thought it was a birthmark. Vess said it was a birthmark."

"It's a warden's sigil. It hides a bloodline from scrying. I pressed it there myself when you were nine days old." She said it plainly, because plainness was all the mercy left in the telling. "Your father got you to the guild. He didn't get himself anywhere. The inquisitors took him on the north road, and the guild recorded him as a nameless drowned traveler, and Vess — who was young then, and already guildmaster's hand, and braver than the office allowed — wrote you into the ledgers as a foundling. Coral Brightwater. A bright water to wash away the ash. She gave you a life made entirely of other people's wonders because she couldn't give you your own."

"That's why the prophecy has my date in it." He was on his feet now, moving, unable to be still, and the birches leaned after him. "A hundred years ago. Because it's not a prophecy, it's a *schedule*. Someone wrote down what would happen when the bloodline surfaced. Someone planned this." He rounded on her. "Was it you? Did you write me like a ledger entry? Did everyone — Vess, the guild, the *trees* — did everyone know what I was except me?"

"Yes," Wren said. "Everyone knew except you. That is what hiding is. It is a conspiracy of everyone against one person, committed lovingly, and it is a violence nonetheless, and I will not insult you by dressing it as anything else." She rose too, and the firelight moved on both of them, and the nightjar called again, closer. "Hate me for it if you need to. Hate Vess. Hate the dead. But hate us *after* you've heard the rest, because Halven Marr is burning the outer groves as we speak, and the question your whole life has been building toward is going to be asked of you whether you're ready or not."

"The name," he whispered. "The true name. I have it. In my blood." His hand went to his forearm as though he could feel the syllables swimming there. "And if I speak it—"

"Everything changes," Wren said. "One way or the other. That is the whole of the prophecy, Coral. Not a prediction. A warning label."

He turned away from her, toward the dark between the birches, and she watched his shoulders rise and fall, rise and fall, a man learning to breathe in a new atmosphere. And because she was weak — because a hundred years of counting rings had not cured her of being weak — Wren crossed the space between them and did the thing she had promised herself she would not do.

She touched his face. Just briefly. Just to confirm, the way you touch a doorframe in a house you dreamed.

He went rigid. Then, slowly, he turned his cheek into her palm, an animal gesture, involuntary, his body answering a question his mind had no records for. His eyes closed.

"I don't know you," he said again, barely a sound. "But I know — God help me, I know your hand. I know this exact — I've felt this before. Where would I have felt this before?"

"In another life," Wren said, "that ended badly, and is asking politely to begin again." She withdrew her hand before the touching became unbearable, and stepped back, and made her voice a warden's voice, steady as bark. "Sleep now. The Wood watches; nothing crosses this hollow. Tomorrow I show you the rings — the Wood's own ledger, the record of the burned century — and then, if you still have the stomach for it, we begin teaching you the name."

He lay down by the fire as if his bones had been unplugged, a swordspeaker felled by mere exhaustion, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way the truly spent sleep. Wren sat beside him and did not sleep at all. She listened to him breathe and matched her own breath to it, an old habit, a married habit, and above them the birches wept their slow sap, and far off to the south — too far for him to hear, not nearly far enough for her — the night filled with a smell like pitch and a sound like teeth.

They were burning the treeline. She had perhaps two days. Perhaps less.

Wren looked at the sleeping man who had been her husband, and her son's father, and her century's whole architecture, and who currently knew her not at all, and she made the only vow left available to her: *this time, whatever it costs, he chooses with his eyes open.*

Then the heart-tree shuddered, miles away, and every root in the Wood turned south, and Wren understood that two days had been optimism.
