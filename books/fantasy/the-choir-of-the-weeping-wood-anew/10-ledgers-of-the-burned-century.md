## 10. Ledgers of the Burned Century

Wren did not take him deeper into the Wood. She took him *down*.

The path began behind a curtain of willow-roots that parted for her the way a crowd parts for a queen — reluctantly, with whispers — and descended into the earth along a spiral of living stone. Coral had spent his life in undercrofts and vaults; he knew the smell of old paper, of dust and lamp-oil and the particular hush of places where records slept. This was nothing like that. The air below the Weeping Wood was warm and green and faintly sweet, like the inside of a struck bell, and it moved. It breathed.

"The guild keeps its ledgers in boxes," Wren said, her voice low, pitched to the dark as if she were afraid of waking something. "We keep ours alive."

"Ours," Coral repeated. "You count me among—"

"I count you among the guilty," she said, without cruelty. "That's different from among us. But you'll be both before morning."

He wanted to argue. He had been arguing with her since the moment she'd spoken his true name — *Vaelorin*, a word that had fit his mouth like a key fits a lock he'd never known he carried — and every argument had died somewhere between his chest and his tongue. So he said nothing and followed her down, one hand trailing the spiral wall, which was not stone at all but root, ancient and smooth as polished bone, and warm beneath his palm in a way that made his skin prickle. It felt, absurdly, like being touched back.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom had no floor he could find and no ceiling he could see. Rings of wood rose from the darkness in concentric circles, vast as mill-wheels stood on end, each one a cross-section of some tree so old its name had probably been spoken by no living mouth. They glowed faintly, the way the heartwood of the Weeping trees glowed when the sap ran high, and their surfaces were not blank.

They were written.

Coral stopped three steps into the chamber and forgot how to breathe.

He was a ledger-keeper. He had spent eleven years reading hands — the crabbed fist of Guildmaster Vess's predecessor, the elegant slant of Sera's entries, the drunken loops of traders who'd signed their impossible bargains after too much plum wine. He knew writing the way priests know prayer. And what covered these rings was writing, line upon line upon line of it, cut into the living wood in script that shifted as he watched, letters surfacing and sinking like fish rising to feed.

"What am I looking at?" His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"A record," Wren said. "The oldest one in the world. Every ring is a year. Every year remembers itself." She walked to the nearest ring and laid both palms flat against it, and the glow beneath her hands brightened, words swimming up toward her touch like trout toward bread. "When your guild writes a lie in ink, the ink can be burned. When we write truth in wood—" she glanced back at him, and the green light made her face strange, half spirit and half grief, "—the wood has to die first."

"And no one has killed them."

"No one has managed," she corrected gently. "Your order tried very hard once."

She drew her hands away and stepped aside, and the ring before them filled with light from edge to edge, and the century began.

* * *

Coral had learned the history of the swordspeakers from a book bound in grey calfskin, kept in the guild's inner library, which only oath-holders might open. He had read it a dozen times. He could have recited it: *In the age of ash, when the forest-spirits warred against humankind and blighted the fields and drowned the rivers, the First Speakers rose. With oaths of binding they chained the wild powers, and with chains they bought peace, and the peace was purchased justly, for the strong must bind the mad lest all things starve.* There had been more, in that elevated register the old histories affected, about honor and sacrifice and the long vigil. He had copied passages of it out in his own hand when he was nine years old, sitting cross-legged in Vess's office while she watched him with an expression he had never, until this week, thought to question.

The rings told it differently.

The first images were not words at all but impressions that bloomed behind his eyes when he looked too long — he understood, distantly, that the wood was showing him rather than telling him, because some things predated language. He saw a world green past imagining, forests that covered continents, and moving through them shapes of light and sap and storm: the spirits, free, neither kind nor cruel, the way weather is neither kind nor cruel. He saw human villages at the forest's edge, small and stubborn and afraid.

Then he saw the first speaker.

A woman, standing in a burning grove, holding a sword that sang. Her voice came through the rings not as sound but as *certainty*, a great cold weight of it pressing against the inside of Coral's skull, and the oaths she spoke were beautiful. That was the horror of it. They were beautiful the way Vess's forged histories were beautiful, cadenced and ringing, built to be remembered. *By my breath, be still. By my blood, be bound.* And the spirits — the vast, weather-hearted, unthinkably old spirits — *bent*. Not all at once. One grove, then a river-spirit, then a hill that had worn a crown of oak for ten thousand years, folded down into shapes small enough to serve.

"They didn't enslave them in a day," Wren said quietly. She had come to stand beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, and there was something unbearable in that — that she could stand beside him and watch this. "It took four generations. Each one believed they were finishing a mercy the last one started. Each one wrote their oaths a little tighter." Her hand found the ring again. "Look for the year the singing stopped."

He looked. The rings turned under his gaze like pages under a patient thumb, and the centuries scrolled, and everywhere the pattern repeated: green going grey, rivers learning to run on schedule, spirits shrunk into lamps and plow-beasts and door-gods, their true names locked away in the chests of men who called themselves keepers. The swordspeakers grew rich. Their order built halls of white stone. In one ring he saw the founding of a guild — not his guild, an older one, its seal a sword crossed with a quill — and watched it grow fat and proud, and watched it begin, slowly, to forget what it was keeping.

And then he found the year the singing stopped.

* * *

It began, as catastrophes do, with a small thing. A young speaker — the rings gave him no name, or gave it in a language Coral's blood almost recognized and could not quite reach — ordered a river-spirit to flood a valley. A rival lord's valley. The spirit obeyed; obedience was the whole of its existence now, the oath worn into it like a groove worn into a stair. But the valley held a grove where the spirit's mother-root still lived, one of the last free-standing elders, and the water drowned it, and the spirit, feeling its mother die through roots it no longer had, did something no bound thing had ever done.

It wept. And its grief was a name.

Coral pressed his hand to the ring without deciding to. The wood was cool now, cooler than it had been, and the vision poured up through his palm and into the place behind his eyes where Vaelorin lived, the name he hadn't known he carried, and for a moment he was the spirit, drowning in commanded water, screaming a word that no chain had ever thought to forbid because no one had dreamed a slave would dare speak the master's tongue—

The chamber shuddered. Every ring flared white.

"Coral." Wren's hands were on his shoulders, pulling him back. "Don't touch the memory bare-handed. You're not a reader. You're a *key*."

He staggered back from the ring, gasping, sap-bright spots swimming across his vision. Where his palm had rested, the wood bore the print of his hand, sunk a quarter-inch deep, and around it the script had gone frantic, lines of it peeling up off the surface and hanging in the air like startled birds before settling again.

"What happened in that year?" he managed. "After the spirit spoke?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the silence had texture. When she answered, she spoke to the rings rather than to him, the way people speak of the dead.

"The word it screamed was a fragment of the Wood's true name — the name your blood carries, the name the prophecy wants from you. Grief spoke it, and grief speaks truer than any oath. Every bound spirit within a hundred miles heard it, and every chain in the world cracked at once." She turned to face him. "The speakers felt it happen. All of them, in all their white halls. And they understood, in a single hour, that everything they had built rested on a silence — and that the silence had a voice, and the voice had a name, and the name was loose."

"So they went to war."

"They went to war," she agreed, "and called it the War of Liberation. You'll find it in your histories under that name, won't you? The noble last stand against the risen wilds." Her mouth twisted. "Here we call it the Burning. The speakers could not unlearn the name they'd heard — a fragment, only a fragment, but enough. If the whole name were ever spoken freely, by a voice that meant freedom instead of grief, every binding would fail forever. So they resolved that no voice would ever speak it at all. They hunted the free groves. They burned the elder trees, because elder trees remember names. And when the burning reached the heart of the Wood, the last free spirits did the only thing left to them."

"The curse," Coral said. His mouth was dry. "The curse on the speakers' bloodline."

"Not on the bloodline," Wren said. "*In* it. Look."

* * *

The final rings were the worst, because they were the most familiar.

He saw the last elder-tree fall, and saw the dying spirits gather what remained of themselves into the deep roots, and saw them work the only magic left to the defeated: they took the fragment of the true name — the fragment that had escaped into the world inside a grieving voice — and they hid it. Not in a vault. Not in a book. They hid it in the enemy's own children, braided into the bloodline of the speakers themselves, generation after generation, so that the name would sleep in the very hearts of the people sworn never to let it wake. A poison pill swallowed by a dynasty. A secret buried in the one place the jailers would never think to search: themselves.

"And then they waited," Wren said softly. "A hundred years. Two hundred. The speakers' order thinned. Their guild fell; yours rose from its ashes, keeping the same oaths without knowing why, guarding prophecies it never read. And the name slept in the blood, waiting for a child to be born empty enough of lies to carry it honestly." She looked at him then, fully, and her eyes were the green of the deepest rings. "You were born in the guild's own undercroft, Coral Brightwater. Vaelorin. Do you understand what that means? Your order didn't raise you despite what you are. They raised you *because* of it. Every oath you've ever sworn — every blade you've ever bound, every command you've spoken into the dark — those words were written by the guilty, to keep the guilty's peace. You have been speaking your jailers' language since you were six years old."

Coral sat down. He didn't decide to; his legs simply ended their argument with the rest of him. He sat on the warm root-floor of the oldest archive in the world, in the house of everything his order had burned, and he put his head in his hands, and the ledger-keeper in him — the part that had survived every other catastrophe of the past week by treating it as an audit — began, helplessly, to reconcile accounts.

*In the age of ash, when the forest-spirits warred against humankind...*

There had been no war. There had been weather, and fear, and men with beautiful voices.

*With oaths of binding they chained the wild powers.*

Chained. Yes. That word, at least, had been honest.

*The peace was purchased justly.*

He thought of the river-spirit drowning its mother-root on command. He thought of the print his own hand had left in the ring, and how the wood had flared — not in pain, he realized now. In *recognition*. The rings knew his blood. The archives of the murdered had opened for the heir of the murderers, and shown him everything, and asked for nothing.

Except, perhaps, everything.

"I swore my first oath at six," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's, scraped thin. "Vess held the blade. She said the words and I repeated them, and the steel listened to me, and everyone clapped." He laughed, and hated the sound. "Everyone clapped. I thought — my whole life, I thought I was the last of a noble line. The last keeper of a necessary prison." He lifted his head and looked at her. "What am I the last of, Wren? Say it plainly. I keep ledgers. I can bear a plain entry."

Wren knelt in front of him, slow, the way you kneel to something wounded that might bolt.

"You are the last of the penance," she said. "The spirits didn't hide the name in your blood to arm you. They hid it there so that someday, if the descendants of the jailers ever grew a conscience, the means of unbinding would already be inside their own bodies — carried unknowing, paid for unknowing, offered back. Your order was the crime, Coral. You are the apology. The curse and the cure wear the same skin, and the skin is yours." She hesitated, and he watched her choose honesty over comfort, and loved her a little for it, in the way one loves a hard winter. "But hear the rest of it plainly too. The name doesn't know mercy from malice. It does what the speaker's heart commands. A man full of rage could speak it and loose the spirits as an army. A man full of grief could speak it and unmake the bindings and himself together. Only a man whose heart holds love without hatred—" she stopped.

"Only then," Coral said, "it frees them."

"Only then."

He looked past her at the rings, at the centuries turning in their slow green light, at the print of his own hand sunk in the record of the drowned. Somewhere above them the Weeping Wood stood in the dark, bleeding sap like tears, whispering fragments of a childhood he'd never had in a city he'd always called home. Somewhere beyond the treeline, Halven Marr's soldiers were sharpening whatever soldiers sharpen. Somewhere in the city, Sera was lying to an inquisitor with a stolen seal, and Vess was sitting alone with a hundred years of forgery, and none of them knew yet what he now knew, which was that the ledger had finally balanced, and the debt had come due, and the debtor was him.

"Wren," he said. "Teach it to me."

Her breath caught. "Coral—"

"Not tonight. Not to use. But I've spent my life reading other people's records secondhand, trusting other people's hands." He stood, and his legs held, and the rings brightened as he rose, every line of ancient script leaning toward him like grass toward sun. "I want to read this one myself. Before anyone burns the library."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she rose too, and took his hand — the one that had left its print in the wood — and turned it palm-up, and pressed her thumb to the center of it, where the blood runs closest to the skin.

"Then we begin at the roots," she said, "and we go carefully. Because the first lesson, Vaelorin, is the one your order never learned." Her eyes held his, green and grave and full of a hundred years of watching. "The name listens back."

Far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of root and earth, the Weeping Wood began — very softly, very distinctly — to sing.
