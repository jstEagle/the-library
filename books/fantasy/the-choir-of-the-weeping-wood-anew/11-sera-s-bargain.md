## 11. Sera's Bargain

The Ninth Stair had always been Sera Quill's favorite place in the guild — the long spiral of worn stone that climbed from the undercroft to the archive galleries, lit by lamps that burned without oil because someone, a century ago, had traded for a flame that refused to die. She had spent ten years cataloguing wonders on that stair, and she had never once imagined she would stand on it deciding which wonder to sell.

She told herself it wasn't selling. That was the first lie, and she made herself say it plainly, standing in the dark with her hand flat against the cold stone: *You are going to sell them.*

Not all of them. Not the true ones.

But Halven Marr would not know the difference, and that was the whole architecture of what she was about to do — build a cathedral of lies so convincing that a man who smelled falsehood the way sharks smelled blood would walk through it singing hymns.

Coral had been gone three days. Three days since the fused page opened under his blood, since the Wood at the city's edge had begun to move like something waking from a long fever, since Vess had sealed the vault and the soldiers had come and Coral had run — run, Sera of all people knew he had run, because she had been the one who opened the postern gate and lied to two guardsmen with a smile she'd practiced in a mirror.

Three days, and no word. The Wood kept its own counsel. And every hour, Inquisitor Halven Marr sat in the guild's best parlor drinking their tea and asking his soft, patient questions, and every hour Vess's stalling grew thinner, more transparent, more like a candle burning down to its brass holder.

Sera descended the stair. She did not let herself think about Coral's face when he'd found the date written a hundred years before tonight. She did not let herself think about the way he'd said *Sera* when he came to her window at midnight, or how her hands had shaken on the gate bar, or how she had wanted — absurdly, unforgivably — to go with him into the dark trees.

If she went with him, no one would be left behind to buy him time.

That was the thought she carried down all nine turns of the stair, and it was the only clean thing in her.

* * *

Halven Marr received her in the parlor at the ninth hour of the evening, as though he had been expecting her, which of course he had. He sat by the fire with his boots crossed and his coat folded over one arm, and the guild's stolen seal glinted at his collar — the compass rose and open book, the mark Sera had sworn her oaths beneath at fourteen, now worn by a man who used it to pry things open.

"Archivist Quill." He did not rise. "I wondered how long you'd take."

"You wondered if I'd come at all."

"I wondered both." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. The tea is your guildmaster's blend. Chamomile and something bitter underneath. I've been trying to place the bitter note all day."

*Sovereign's bane,* Sera thought. *It's a mild sedative, and you've been drinking it for six hours.* She said nothing and sat, arranging her skirts with the composure of a woman who had rehearsed this conversation in front of a mirror until the rehearsal itself felt like a wound.

"I've come to make an arrangement," she said.

"Mm." Halven poured a second cup himself, unhurried, and pushed it across the low table toward her. Steam curled between them. "Guildmaster Vess makes arrangements too. She arranges delays. She arranges missing ledgers. She arranged, I believe, a very convenient fire in the prophecy annex eleven years ago that destroyed nothing anyone can prove mattered." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, warm at the edges, and it reached none of the way to his eyes. "You strike me as a woman who might arrange something else."

"And what is that?"

"The truth." He leaned back. "Or close enough to it."

Here it was, then. The door. Sera looked at the tea and did not touch it, and looked at Halven Marr across the steam, and understood with terrible clarity that everything depended on the next thirty seconds — on whether she could hate him convincingly enough to be believed.

Because that was the trick she had built in the mirror, the load-bearing wall of the whole cathedral: she could not come to him as a collaborator. Collaborators were bought, and bought things could be outbid. She had to come to him as a woman whose loyalty had already broken — broken ugly, broken with resentment in it — so that everything she gave him afterward would taste like spite rather than strategy.

"I'll tell you where the swordspeaker went," she said, and let her voice crack on the last word, just slightly, just enough. "And you're going to give me something first."

Halven's eyebrows rose a fraction. Genuine interest, or its excellent imitation. "Go on."

"A writ. Under the usurper's seal. Full pardon, named, for Sera Quill, archivist of the Guild of Impossible Things, for any crimes committed in aid of the crown's inquiry." She recited it like a ledger entry, because that was the only way she could say it without vomiting. "Signed. Dated. In my hand before I leave this room."

"You want insurance."

"I want *out*," Sera said, and here she didn't have to act; the loathing in it was real, only aimed, as all her loathing currently was, at herself. "Do you know what I've done in the past three days? I forged an accession record. I lied to my own guildmaster. I opened a gate I've locked every night for six years. If Coral Brightwater comes back, I hang beside him. If he doesn't come back, I hang alone. So yes, Inquisitor. Insurance. You want the last swordspeaker and I want to survive the finding of him. That's the trade."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the undying lamp on the Ninth Stair burned on, indifferent.

Halven set down his cup. "You love him," he said. Not a question.

The trap, laid so neatly she almost admired it. Deny it and he'd smell the lie; confirm it and he'd own her twice over. So Sera did neither. She laughed — short, bitter, real, dredged up from somewhere below the fear.

"Love him? Have you *met* him?" She shook her head. "He alphabetizes other people's miracles and apologizes to furniture. I've watched him spend four years pining after a woman who doesn't exist anymore and being too decent to notice me noticing. No, Inquisitor. I don't love Coral Brightwater. I'm *tired* of him. Tired of being the second-best thing in his life while he chases ghosts and prophecies and a destiny he never asked for. Tired of watching Vess look at him like — " She stopped. Let the silence do its work. Let him lean in.

"Like what?"

"Like he matters more than the rest of us put together." Sera met his eyes and held them, and behind her own eyes she built the false room brick by brick: the resentment, the smallness, the ambition curdled into spite. "You want to know why I'm here? Because when they find him — and they will, because the Wood is screaming loud enough to wake the dead quarter — I intend to still be standing. And I intend to be the one who tells the capital exactly how much the Guild of Impossible Things knew, and for how long, and who signed off on hiding it. There are promotions in that. Maybe a mastership. The guild will need new management once you've finished with it."

Something shifted in Halven's face. Not belief — belief would come later, fed and watered — but appetite. The look of a man hearing a price he found plausible because it confirmed what he already suspected about human nature.

"You'd burn your own guild."

"My guild is already burning," Sera said quietly. "I'm just choosing which side of the fire to stand on."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he drew a leather folio from inside his coat, uncapped a pen, and wrote — quickly, fluently, the seal pressed hard enough to bruise the wax. He slid the parchment across the table.

Sera read it twice. Every clause she'd demanded, plus one she hadn't: *services rendered in aid of the inquiry, nature and extent to be determined by the Inquisitor's sole judgment.*

There it was. The hook inside the bait. She signed anyway, because the alternative was hanging, and because a bargain with a devil was only fatal if you forgot it was one. Her signature looked steady. She was almost proud of that.

"Now," Halven said, folding the writ away, and his voice changed — dropped its parlor warmth and became the instrument underneath, the thing he actually was. "Where did the swordspeaker go?"

* * *

She gave him the truth the way a surgeon gives pain: precisely, and only as much as needed.

"He went east through the tanners' quarter, before dawn three days ago. Alone. On foot. Toward the Weeping Wood." She watched Halven absorb it, watched his fingers drum once against his knee. "Vess knows. She's known since the night he ran. She's been sending runners to the treeline villages to warn the woodcutters away from the roads — not to help Coral, mind. To keep the Wood quiet. She thinks if no one disturbs it, it will swallow him and the problem both, and in a year we'll all pretend the prophecy vault was empty."

"Does she know about the book?"

"She knows about the first page. Everyone senior knows about the first page — the date, the swordspeaker, the sealed page after it." Sera paused, and let the pause stretch just long enough to seem reluctant. This was the hinge, the false stone in the arch. "What she doesn't know is that I copied it. Before the sealing. Both pages."

Halven went very still. "The second page was fused shut."

"It was." Sera reached into her sleeve and produced a folded paper — her own careful forgery, aged with tea and ash, the script mimicking the century-old hand she knew better than her own heartbeat. She did not hand it over. Not yet. "My blood isn't swordspeaker's blood, Inquisitor. But paper remembers pressure, and I am very good with paper. I lifted the impression of the fused text weeks ago, when I first audited the vault. What's written there names the route. *The swordspeaker flees through the weeping trees; the heart answers at the third ford of the Ashen River.* Your soldiers are searching the north groves. They're wasting their time. He's south and east, at the fords, and every hour you spend beating the north woods, he gets closer to whatever the heart of that forest is."

She laid the forgery on the table between them and slid it across with two fingers, and hated herself with a completeness that felt almost religious. Every word of it was a lie braided through a truth — Coral *had* gone east, the fords *were* south and east, and the false page would send Halven's soldiers marching along a route that was plausible, searchable, and empty. Three days' march in the wrong direction. Perhaps four, if the weather turned.

Perhaps enough.

Halven read it. His eyes moved slowly, and Sera watched him the way she'd learned to watch rare documents — for the tremor, the hesitation, the place where the hand hesitated over a word. There. A flicker at *third ford.* He looked up.

"This is the fused text."

"That's the fused text."

"You understand that if this is false, the writ won't protect you. Nothing will. I'll take you apart with the same patience I'm taking apart your guild."

"If it's false," Sera said, "then I'll already be dead, because Coral Brightwater will have reached the heart of the Weeping Wood, and according to your own inquisitors' reports the last time anything spoke that name, the world burned for a hundred years." She stood, smoothing her skirt, and delivered the closing line she had polished longest of all: "Check the page against the vault impression yourself. Break the seal on the original if you must — though I'd ask you not to; some fusions don't survive curiosity. But check it. I'm not asking for faith, Inquisitor. I'm asking for verification. Faith is for people who can afford it."

At the door, his voice stopped her.

"Archivist Quill."

She turned. The firelight made Halven Marr look almost kind, which was the most frightening thing she had seen all week.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I believe you. Spite is the one confession no one fakes." A pause. "Your guildmaster should have paid more attention to you. People who feel unseen make the finest instruments."

"Good night, Inquisitor," Sera said, and walked out with her spine straight and her stomach full of ice, and did not let herself shake until she was three corridors away, in the dark of the map gallery, with her forehead pressed against the cold glass of a case full of charts of places that no longer existed.

*Instruments cut both ways,* she thought. *Remember that. Remember it every single day.*

* * *

She worked until the fourth bell of morning.

The map gallery held the guild's cartographic heart: surveys, trade routes, the great linen chart of the Weeping Wood drawn ninety years ago by a surveyor who had gone in with twelve assistants and come out alone and mute. Sera pulled it down and spread it across the reading table, weighting the corners with brass astrolabes, and studied the country south and east of the city until her eyes ached.

The third ford of the Ashen River. Her invention. But inventions had to live somewhere, and she had chosen the fords deliberately — far enough from the Wood's deep interior to be useless to Halven, close enough to the truth to survive scrutiny. The trouble was that a false route needed maintenance. Soldiers would report back. Scouts would find nothing at the third ford and grow suspicious, and suspicion was a debt that came due with interest.

So she began to build the maintenance into the lie. Marginal notes in the surveyor's hand — *ford impassable in spring melt, crossing better at the old weir upstream* — details that would send searchers wandering in widening, fruitless circles. A forged letter from a treeline village elder, dated two days ago, mentioning smoke seen near the fords. She wrote it in a hand she invented on the spot, a farmer's cramped scrawl, and aged it, and sealed it with a counterfeit of a village stamp she found in the dead-letter drawer, and when it was done she sat looking at it and understood that she had just become the kind of person who did this. Fluently. At the fourth bell of the morning. For love, or whatever this was — this thing that had no name she dared write in any ledger.

*Loyalty expressed as betrayal.* She thought of Coral saying once, absently, over a cataloguing error, *the guild's whole business is making impossible things ordinary.* Well. Here was hers.

She was blotting the forged letter when she heard the footsteps — soft, deliberate, stopping at the gallery door. Vess stood there in her nightclothes and boots, silver hair unbound, holding a lamp that threw her shadow huge across the maps.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Then Vess's gaze traveled over the table: the great chart, the marginalia, the letter, the ink still wet at its edge. Sera watched her guildmaster read the scene the way she read everything — completely, instantly, and with a grief that predated whatever it was grieving.

"You're feeding him," Vess said quietly.

"I'm buying time," Sera said. "There's a difference."

"Is there." Vess came forward and set the lamp down, and her hand, Sera noticed, was not entirely steady. "How long?"

"Four days. Five, if the rain holds."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had been decided behind them — something large, and terrible, and final. "Then listen to me, Sera Quill, because I only have the courage to say this once." She gripped the table's edge. "Halven will break your bargain. Men like him always do — the writ is worthless, the moment you stop being useful you're evidence. And when he breaks it, he'll need a map." Her voice dropped. "There is one map that matters. One. Of the paths to the heart-tree. It has been in this guild for a hundred years, and I swore an oath the night Coral was born that no one would ever see it."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the corridor. Outside, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the treeline, came a sound Sera had been pretending for three days not to hear — the Wood, weeping, or laughing, or calling.

"Where is it?" Sera asked.

Vess looked at her for a long moment, and then reached into the collar of her nightgown and drew out a key on a cord, black with age, that Sera had never seen in twenty years of serving this woman.

"Under the ninth stair," Vess said. "Where the undying lamp was lit. Take it before he does. And Sera — " her voice cracked, finally, all the way through — "when you find him, tell him his mother is sorry it took her this long to be brave."

* * *

Sera stood alone in the map gallery with the black key burning cold in her palm, listening to the Wood call through the dark, and understood that the bargain had just changed shape. She had sold Halven Marr a road to nowhere. But somewhere in this guild, under nine turns of worn stone, lay the one road that went all the way — and now she knew where it slept, and so, if she failed, would a man who smelled spite and called it truth.

She blew out the lamp and started down toward the Ninth Stair, moving fast and silent, a traitor to two masters and faithful, at last, to only one.
