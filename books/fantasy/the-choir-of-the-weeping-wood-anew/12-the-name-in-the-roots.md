## 12. The Name in the Roots

Wren woke him before dawn by singing.

It was not a song he knew — the notes bent strangely, falling where they should have risen, like water running uphill — but his body answered it before his mind could object. His heartbeat slowed to match her rhythm. The sap in the walls of the hollow tree where he slept thickened and thinned with each phrase. When he opened his eyes, the whole chamber was breathing: bark expanding, moss brightening, the small amber lamps of fungus pulsing in time.

"You do that on purpose," he said, voice rough with sleep.

"I do everything on purpose." She stood at the entrance, silhouetted against a sky the color of unripe plums. "Today you learn your name."

"I have a name. Coral Brightwater. Guild of Impossible Things, third ledger-keeper, oath-bound swordspeaker of—" He stopped, because the old formula caught in his throat like a fishbone. *Oath-bound.* Three days in the Wood had made the words taste different. He had come here believing his order was a shield raised over the world. He had seen the rings now. He had pressed his palm to living wood and read what swordspeakers had done when the world was young and they were its masters.

"Penance," Wren said, as if she'd heard the thought. She always seemed to. "Yes. But penance is only half a truth, Coral. The other half is this: the ones who cursed us also left us a key. They hid it so well that for a hundred years we believed there was no key at all." She extended her hand. Her fingers were long and faintly green-veined, and when she smiled, the fungus-lamps brightened. "They hid it in their own blood. In yours."

* * *

She led him down through the roots.

The Weeping Wood did not have an underworld so much as an under-*self* — a second forest grown downward, all root and amber and slow light, where the trees' tears collected in pools that never rippled no matter how the ground trembled above. Wren walked ahead of him along a root as wide as a guildhall corridor, and everywhere the wood had been wounded, sap welled up in glassy beads and fell, unhurried, into the dark.

"Say nothing yet," she warned. "Not one syllable of what I show you. The Wood listens, and it has been listening for a very long time. It is patient, but it is not gentle, and if you speak before you understand, it will take the words and run with them."

"Run where?"

"To war." She said it simply, the way one says *to market*. "That is what names are for, Coral. A true name is not a word. It is a handle. Whoever grips it can lift the whole thing — the Wood, the spirits bound inside it, every root and grief and hunger. Lift it to free it, or lift it to swing it. The name does not care. Names never care. That is why we gave ours away."

They descended until the darkness turned amber, then gold, then a deep honeyed glow with no source he could find. And there, in the widest chamber of the root-world, stood the archive.

He had thought the rings above were the Wood's record. He had been wrong. Those were history. This was something older — a lattice of roots grown into shapes that hurt his eyes to follow, spirals within spirals, and suspended among them, dozens of translucent pods, each holding a single drop of sap that glowed like a held breath.

"What are they?"

"Spirits," Wren said. "The ones who could not flee when the burning came. The swordspeakers sealed them in their own tears and hung them here like lanterns." Her voice did not waver, but the nearest pod dimmed slightly, as if flinching from her words. "A century ago, my grandmother's grandmother counted four hundred and eleven. Now there are three hundred and eighty-eight. They are not dead. They are *waiting*, which is worse. Waiting is the cruelest verb there is."

Coral stepped closer. In the nearest pod, the drop of sap shifted — and for half a heartbeat he saw a face in it, or the idea of a face, features suggested rather than shown, turning toward him with an attention that felt like a hand laid flat against his chest.

"They're looking at me."

"They've always been looking at you. Every swordspeaker who ever walked these woods, they watched, hoping. Hating. Both at once." Wren came to stand beside him, close enough that he could smell rain on her skin. "And then you were born, and they stopped watching everyone else."

"Why me?"

"Because of what your blood carries." She drew a thin bone knife from her belt — ceremonial, he realized, older than any blade in the Guild's collection, its edge worn smooth by centuries of careful use. "Your order did not only enslave us. To bind a spirit, a swordspeaker must speak its name, and to learn a name, they took it into themselves. Every binding was a theft, and every theft leaves a mark. Your bloodline carries the stolen names of this Wood — all of them, compressed, generation upon generation, passed down like a debt no one told you you'd inherited." She turned the knife so it caught the golden light. "Compressed things want to expand. And somewhere in that compression, folded so small it cannot be seen, is the name of the Wood itself. Its true name. The one it had before anyone taught it shame."

"And if I speak it?"

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Above them, far away, a tree wept.

"Then everything changes," she said. "That is the honest answer, though I know you want a better one. If your heart is open when you speak — if you hold love larger than your fear — the bindings dissolve. All of them. My people wake, the curse ends, the Wood belongs to itself again." She paused. "But if your heart is closed — if what fills you is rage, or grief, or the desire to make someone pay — then the name does not free the spirits. It arms them. It gives them back their strength and points them like a spear, and Coral, there is an army at the treeline, and the Wood remembers what was done to it, and it would *love* an excuse."

* * *

He practiced first on something small.

Wren chose it for him: a fallen branch, dead for years, lying in a grove where the light came down green and forgiving. "Deadwood holds no spirit," she said. "If you lose yourself, you will only lose yourself a little. Speak to it. Not *at* it — the way your guild taught you to command blades. Speak *to* it, the way you'd greet someone across a table."

He knelt. The branch lay pale and split before him, ordinary as kindling. He reached for the place inside himself where swordspeech lived — that warm, disciplined channel behind the breastbone, the one that had obeyed him since he was nine years old — and found it changed. It was not a channel anymore. It was a door, and the door was standing open, and something on the other side was breathing.

"Let it rise," Wren murmured. "Don't push. Don't shape. Just let it rise and see what it wants to be."

The warmth moved up his throat. It gathered behind his teeth, and it was *heavy* — heavier than any oath he had ever spoken, heavy the way a ledger is heavy, full of entries he had never written. And then a sound came out of him that was not a word in any language he knew, two syllables braided together, resonant as a struck bell, and the dead branch—

The dead branch *woke*.

Not stirred. Not twitched. *Woke.* Bark crawled over the split like skin closing over a wound. A bud swelled at the broken end, unfurled, became a leaf so green it seemed lit from within. Roots burst from the cut base and plunged into the soil, and the whole limb arched upward off the ground like a sleeper stretching after a hundred years, and Coral felt all of it — felt the leaf opening as if it were his own lung filling, felt the root finding water as if it were his own thirst slaked — and he understood, with a vertigo that nearly toppled him, that he had not commanded anything.

He had *introduced* himself.

"Enough." Wren's hand closed on his shoulder, and the connection snapped. He fell forward onto his hands, gasping, the grove tilting around him. His nose was bleeding freely; the blood dripped onto the new leaf and the leaf drank it without comment. "Breathe. Small breaths. You went too deep."

"It wasn't dead," he managed. "Nothing in here is dead. Even the dead things aren't dead."

"No. Nothing here is dead. That is precisely the danger." She crouched beside him, wiping his chin with a sleeve that smelled of cedar smoke. "When you spoke just now, every spirit in the root-archive turned toward you. All three hundred and eighty-eight. I felt them turn. Do you understand what that means? For a hundred years they have slept facing their own sorrow. Today they faced *you*. They know the key has arrived. And they are listening, Coral, for how you will use it."

* * *

He should have stopped there. He knows that now, in the way one knows things afterward, when the knowing is useless.

But the army came to the treeline that afternoon — they heard it from the high groves, the distant crack and roar of Halven Marr's siege-engines being assembled, the smell of pitch and iron rolling over the canopy like weather — and something in Coral went cold and hard, and cold-and-hard was exactly the wrong material out of which to build a heart.

"Again," he said.

Wren studied him. "You're angry."

"I'm *ready*."

"Those are not the same thing, and the name knows the difference better than you do."

"Then let it judge me." He knelt again in the same grove, though the light had gone copper with evening, and reached for the door. It swung wide at his touch — too wide, eagerly, as if it had been waiting — and this time he did not let the sound rise gently. He pulled it up. He put the army in it, the burning groves, the screaming trees, the pods hanging in the dark like prisoners, and he spoke the two braided syllables with all of that behind them.

The grove exploded into growth.

Not gently. Violets surged up around his knees and kept surging, climbing his shins, their petals opening and opening and opening as if they could not stop. The deadwood branch became a sapling became a young tree in the space of ten heartbeats, its trunk creaking, its crown thrashing, and beyond it the whole grove answered — grasses whipping, fungi blazing from amber to a furious red he had never seen, the very soil buckling — and through it all ran a current of *hunger*, vast and ancient and not entirely the Wood's, because some of it was coming up through him, out of the place where his anger lived, and the Wood was only reflecting it back, magnified, delighted, *eager*—

"CORAL."

Wren hit him bodily, both hands flat against his chest, and spoke a word of her own — short, sharp, falling like an axe — and the world snapped shut.

He came back to himself on his back in the ruined grove, staring up at a young tree that had not existed ten minutes ago, its red-blazing crown slowly cooling to green. His hands were shaking. His mouth tasted of iron and honey. And beneath his ribs, where the door stood, something was still pushing — patiently, hungrily — wanting another word, a longer word, wanting to finish the sentence he had started.

"That," Wren said, kneeling over him, her face pale as birchbark, "is the weapon. Do you see it now? You didn't free that grove. You *armed* it. You gave it your anger and it grew armor." She sat back on her heels, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "One more word in that voice and you would not have stopped at the grove. You would have gone down to the archive. You would have woken all three hundred and eighty-eight with war in your mouth, and they would have loved you for it, Coral. They would have followed you out of those roots and burned Halven Marr's army to ash and then kept going, because that is what armed grief does. It doesn't know where to stop. It never has. That is how the world burned the first time."

He lay there, chest heaving, and looked at his hands. Ordinary hands. Ledger-keeper's hands, ink-stained at the nails. They had just done that.

"The temptation," he said quietly. "You warned me about it, and I thought you meant I'd be tempted to refuse. To walk away. But it isn't that at all, is it? It wanted to say yes. *I* wanted to say yes." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "Do you know what I felt, right at the end? Powerful. For the first time in my life, actually powerful — not a clerk with a sword's vocabulary, not the last of a dying order keeping books about bottled thunder. I felt like the answer instead of the question. And I wanted more of it."

"I know." Wren lay down beside him in the churned earth, shoulder to shoulder, and stared up at the cooling tree. "I have watched a hundred swordspeakers practice at this spot, over the centuries. Every one of them said some version of what you just said. The good ones said it with horror. The bad ones said it with pride." She turned her head to look at him. "Which are you?"

He didn't answer immediately. Below them, far below, three hundred and eighty-eight drops of sap hung in the dark, waiting, and he could feel their attention like sunlight through a magnifying lens — focused, patient, hopeful in a way that frightened him more than hatred would have.

"I don't know yet," he said finally. "And I think that might be the most dangerous answer there is."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she reached over and took his bleeding hand, and turned it palm-up, and pressed her thumb to the center of it, where the blood welled brightest.

"Then we don't practice again," she said, "until you know. Because tomorrow, Coral, you may not get to choose whether you speak. Halven's fires are at the treeline, and the outer groves are already screaming, and when the Wood breaks — and it *will* break, it is breaking now — the name will rise in your throat whether you call it or not." Her grip tightened. "So tonight you learn the hardest lesson of all. Not how to speak the name. How to hold it in your mouth like water, and decide, in the last instant, what kind of vessel you are."

Far off, at the edge of hearing, the first outer grove caught fire, and even at this distance, even through miles of intervening forest, Coral heard the trees scream — and felt, beneath his ribs, the door strain against its hinges, and something on the other side begin, very softly, to sing along.
