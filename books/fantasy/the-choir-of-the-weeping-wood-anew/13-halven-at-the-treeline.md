## 13. Halven at the Treeline

The Weeping Wood announced itself long before it appeared.

Halven Marr had been told it would smell of rot and old grief, and the report was half right. From three miles out, riding at the head of a column that stretched back along the salt road like a black thread pulled through grey country, he caught it on the wind: something sweet and wrong, like honey poured over a wound. The men shifted in their saddles. Horses tossed their heads. Somewhere behind him, a soldier began humming to steady his nerves and was quickly silenced by his fellows, as though even music might be overheard.

Halven did not hum. Halven did not fidget. He sat his horse the way he did everything — precisely, patiently, with the stillness of a man who had learned early that the world gave its secrets to those who waited without blinking.

He had waited eleven years for this road.

* * *

They made camp that first night within sight of nothing but stubble fields and a sky the color of pewter, and Halven took his supper alone in the command tent, as was his habit. Before him lay the map — not the guild's pretty cartography with its flourished borders and decorative trees, but his own: a working document annotated in his small, exact hand. Every village evacuated or bought. Every magistrate turned. Every record seized from every archive between the capital and this place, stacked and read and burned or kept according to their usefulness.

And at the center of it all, drawn in ink so dark it looked like a hole cut in the vellum: the Wood.

The reports called the trees weeping because they bled sap — pale, resinous tears that ran down their bark in slow channels and hardened into beads like frozen grief. Pilgrims used to collect them, before pilgrimages became inconvenient. Alchemists paid well for the resin. Children's stories said the trees cried for something lost a thousand years ago, and mothers hushed them, because it was bad luck to name what the forest mourned.

Halven believed none of it. He believed in ledgers. In inventories. In the doctrine that everything which existed could be counted, and everything counted could be owned, and everything owned could be controlled. The usurper's court understood this; it was why they had raised him from a minor clerk's son in a minor provincial town to Inquisitor of the Ninth Seal, keeper of the stolen guild-stamp that opened every door in the realm. Magic was simply an unfiled account. His life's work was the audit.

Which was why the last swordspeaker mattered. Not the man himself — men were interchangeable — but what he represented: one line item left open on a ledger a century old. An oath-bound order supposedly extinguished, yet the old records spoke of swordspeech as if it were a fire that could be banked rather than killed. And fires, Halven knew, kept.

Somewhere ahead of him, past the treeline, that ember was breathing.

He blew out his lamp and slept without dreams, which he considered among his finer accomplishments.

* * *

They reached the Wood's edge on the third morning, under a sky gone the color of skimmed milk.

Halven reined in where the salt road ended and the ground turned soft and black with leaf-rot, and looked up at the trees for the first time.

They were enormous. That was the first thing — not beauty, not strangeness, but sheer scale, trunks wide as watchtowers rising into a canopy so dense that noon arrived there as a kind of rumor. The second thing was the sap. It ran down the pale bark in long, glistening tracks, catching the light, and in the still air he could hear it — faintly, absurdly — dripping. A sound like a slow clock counting down in a language no one had bothered to translate.

The third thing was the silence. No birds. No insects. The forest stood there holding its breath, and Halven felt, for one unwelcome moment, the sensation of standing outside a door behind which someone very large had stopped moving to listen.

He dismounted and ordered the line formed. Four hundred picked soldiers, two siege-wagons carrying alchemical fire in lead-lined casks, and a company of sappers with axes and tar. Not enough to level the whole Wood — nothing was ever enough for that — but enough to take the outer groves, establish a perimeter, and begin the work of pushing inward, grove by grove, until the heart-tree stood inside a clearing of ash and could be taken like any other fortified position.

"Begin at the eastern stand," he told Captain Oryse, a broad, competent woman whose face never changed regardless of what she was ordered to do. "Fire the perimeter first. I want a break in the canopy wide enough to see through by nightfall."

"And the resin, sir? The men say it burns strange."

"The men say many things." He handed her the torch himself, a courtesy he extended to no one else, and watched her carry it forward.

The first tree caught with a sound he would remember for the rest of his life, though he would spend years refusing to remember it.

Not a roar. A note. A single sustained tone, low and resonant, as though the trunk were a string and the flame a bow drawn across it. The fire spread along the sap-channels the way light spreads along a fuse, and each tree it touched added its own voice to the first — a chord building, deepening, until the eastern edge of the Weeping Wood was burning in harmony.

Then the screaming started.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the smoke and the standing trees beyond it, a high keening that was almost — almost — only wind through branches. Almost. The men heard it too. Halven saw them stop in their work, torches guttering, faces turning toward the deep wood as if expecting something to come out of it. One young soldier dropped his cask of tar and ran, and Oryse let him go, which was unlike her, which meant she had heard it too.

For miles, Halven thought. The reports will say the screams carried for miles. And they would be wrong, because miles was a measure of distance, and this sound did not travel so much as arrive — present all at once in every ear within reach of it, the way a name arrives when someone speaks it in another room.

"Continue," he said.

They continued. By dusk the outer groves were a wall of orange light against the milk-grey sky, and the column had established its perimeter along a scorched seam of ground, and the work was proceeding exactly as planned. Halven stood at the treeline with his hands clasped behind his back and watched the oldest forest in the world burn, and felt — he searched himself carefully, honestly, the way he audited everything — satisfaction.

It lasted until the Wood answered back.

* * *

It began, as far as he could determine afterward, with the sap.

A sergeant came to him near midnight, helmet under his arm, sweat standing on his forehead despite the cold. "Inquisitor. The eastern fire's gone out."

"Fires do that."

"Not like this, sir. It didn't die down. It went out. All at once. Like—" the man groped for the word and visibly disliked the one he found— "like it was snuffed. And the ground where it burned is wet. The whole clearing. Wet with sap, sir. It ran uphill."

Halven walked the perimeter himself. He trusted reports less than his own eyes, and his own eyes less than his own suspicion, and his suspicion had been whispering to him since the first tree sang.

The burned clearing lay under a skin of pale resin, still warm, gleaming in the moonlight like the floor of some vast chapel. Where the charred stumps stood, new sap welled from the split wood — not trickling but welling, generous, obscene, as though the trees were bleeding on purpose. As though the blood were a message.

And in the middle of the clearing, where nothing had stood that morning, a single sapling rose knee-high out of the resin. Green. Unburned. Its leaves trembled in a wind that did not touch anything else.

Halven circled it twice. He did not touch it. He sent for a shovel instead, and had the thing dug out root and all, and ordered it sealed in an iron box, and noted the time in his field book in his small exact hand. Evidence. Everything was evidence. Everything was countable.

But walking back along the perimeter, past soldiers who would not meet his eye, past the standing darkness of the deeper Wood where the great trunks rose like pillars in a hall built for something much larger than men, Halven Marr admitted to himself — privately, in the locked room of his own skull where he kept the accounts no one else was permitted to read — that the feeling had returned.

The feeling of being watched.

Not by scouts. He knew how scouts watched; it had texture, direction, fear in it. This was different. This was the attention of something that did not need eyes, something that had been watching him since the salt road, perhaps since the capital, perhaps since the day eleven years ago when a clerk's son had first pulled a certain sealed file and begun, page by patient page, to assemble the theory of the last swordspeaker. As though the Wood had known his name longer than he had known its purpose. As though he were not the hunter approaching a quarry but a figure entering a story already written, in which his role — his exact words, his exact errors — had been set down a century ago in a book with a fused page.

He despised prophecy. It was the one form of accounting he refused to honor: numbers that claimed to know their own sum before the columns were entered. Arrogance dressed as fate. And yet.

At the treeline he stopped. Ahead of him the dark trees stood rank upon rank, weeping their slow bright tears, and somewhere among them — he was certain now, certain the way he was certain of arithmetic — the swordspeaker was moving deeper, toward the heart, toward whatever the fused page promised. Running. Frightened. Alone.

Good. Frightened things made mistakes. Alone things could be cornered.

But the Wood was not alone with him. That was the error in the entry, the discrepancy Halven could not reconcile. The Wood was awake around the man, and it was grieving, and it was angry, and tonight it had put out his fire and grown a tree out of its own ashes and screamed loud enough to be heard in villages that would by morning be telling priests about it.

Cold triumph curdles slowly, Halven thought. Like milk. You drink it anyway, because thirst is thirst.

"Inquisitor?" Oryse had come up beside him, silent as ever. "Orders for dawn?"

He looked out at the burning edge of the world's oldest forest, at the wall of smoke rising straight and tall into the starless sky, and beyond it the darkness where the heart-tree waited, and he felt again that vast, patient attention settle over him like a hand.

"Push the line inward," he said. "Groves first. Then the streams. I want the heart-tree inside our perimeter within the week." He paused. "And double the watch at night. If anything moves in the trees — anything — I am to be woken."

Oryse nodded and turned to go.

"Captain." She stopped. He did not look at her; he kept his eyes on the Wood, on the darkness between the trunks where the moonlight pooled like spilled resin. "When the trees scream tomorrow — and they will — the men are not to listen. Tell them that. Tell them exactly that. *You are not to listen.*"

"And if they ask why, sir?"

Halven Marr smiled, briefly, the way a blade flashes.

"Because listening," he said, "is how it gets in."

Far off in the deep of the Weeping Wood, so far it should have been inaudible and was not, something answered — one clear note, low and resonant, like a string being tuned. Like a voice trying out a word it had not spoken in a hundred years.

Halven stood very still in the dark, and wrote the time in his book, and did not sleep.
