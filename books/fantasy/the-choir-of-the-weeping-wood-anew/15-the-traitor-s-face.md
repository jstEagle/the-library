## 15. The Traitor's Face

The tent smelled of tallow and scorched iron, and Sera Quill stood in the middle of it with her hands folded like a woman delivering a market report.

Halven Marr sat behind a field desk that had been carried, she was certain, all the way from the capital on the shoulders of some unlucky corporal. It was black lacquer inlaid with silver filigree — the usurper's taste, all shine over rot — and it looked obscene here at the treeline of the Weeping Wood, where the air itself tasted of sap and smoke. He did not look up when she entered. He let her stand. That was the first lesson of Halven Marr: he made you wait, and he watched how you waited.

She had been feeding him for eleven days.

Eleven days of small betrayals, each one calibrated like a dose of poison — enough to keep him interested, never enough to kill what mattered. She had given him the guild's cipher rotations (three weeks out of date). She had given him the names of two archivists who had fled the city (both long gone past any border he controlled). She had given him the location of the undercroft's secondary vault (emptied the night before Vess sealed it). Every secret she sold him was a coin shaved thin, a forgery so good it took her whole craft to make it, and every night she went back to her narrow cot in the auditors' camp and lay awake listening to the Wood weep beyond the pickets, and told herself: *this is loyalty. This is what loyalty looks like when it has no clean hands left.*

"Archivist Quill." He looked up at last, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him because it was warm. "You've brought me something."

"The supply manifests you asked for." She set the leather folder on his desk. Inside were manifests she had spent four hours doctoring, numbers that would lead his quartermasters to three empty storehouses and one that held, genuinely, grain — a real prize wrapped around a real lie, the only kind of gift he'd trust. "And the inventory of the prophecy holdings recovered from the guild."

"And?"

"And nothing. That's everything."

He turned the folder toward himself but didn't open it. Instead he laced his fingers together and regarded her over them, the way a man regards a clock he is deciding whether to buy or break. The lamplight caught the stolen seal on his breast — the Guild of Impossible Things' own mark, the key and the candle, hammered into a badge of office it had never been meant to bear. Seeing it on his chest made her teeth ache. It was like seeing a mother's ring on a thief's hand.

"Do you know," Halven said pleasantly, "what I admire most about you?"

"I couldn't say, Inquisitor."

"Punctuality. You have never once been late to one of our meetings. Not once in eleven days. Traitors are usually late." He said the word *traitors* without inflection, the way one says *rain* or *Tuesday*, and Sera's blood went cold and stayed cold, because she understood in that instant that the word had not been an accident. "Traitors are late because they are busy being loyal somewhere else. You are never late. Which means either you are the most devoted traitor I have ever employed—" he opened the folder now, slowly, "—or you are not a traitor at all."

Outside, distantly, a tree screamed. The soldiers had learned to ignore it. Sera had not; she doubted she ever would. It was a sound like a violin bow drawn across the world's longest nerve.

"Inquisitor—"

"Halven. Please. We're colleagues." He drew out a single sheet from the folder and laid it flat. Her own hand looked back up at her, her careful archivist's script, the letters she had shaped ten thousand times in the undercroft's lamplight. A map. *Her* map. "Now. Here is a curious thing about this document. It shows the approaches to the heart-grove through the western ravines. Very detailed. Very current. And very wrong."

Sera kept her face still. Eleven days of practice, and before that a lifetime of keeping her face still while guildmasters lied to her and ledgers lied to everyone. "Wrong how?"

"Wrong in the way that only someone who *knows* the truth could make it wrong." He tapped the ravine path once, twice. "You moved the ford downstream by half a mile. You shifted the standing stones. Small errors. Elegant errors. Errors designed to march my vanguard into the flooded cut where the Wood's roots drink deep and the ground swallows horses." He looked up at her, and the warmth drained out of his smile until only the shape of it remained, a skull wearing a pleasant expression. "I sent a scout through last night. One scout. Do you know what came back?"

"No."

"His boots." Halven rose. He was not a tall man, but he filled the tent the way smoke fills a room. "The rest of him the Wood kept. So. Let us stop performing for one another, Archivist Quill, because I find I've lost my appetite for theater."

Her mind was moving fast and cold, the way it moved over a ledger that refused to balance — running columns, testing sums, looking for the error that would save the total. *He knows about the falsified map. Does he know about the others? How many? All? If all, then Coral has had eleven free days bought with nothing, and if Coral has had eleven days—*

"You gave me good forgeries," Halven continued, circling the desk, and she realized with a lurch that he had known for days, perhaps from the beginning, and had let her run because a running quarry shows you where its den is. "Good ones. Better than good. I want you to understand that I respect the work. But here is what your forgeries taught me, Sera Quill — may I call you Sera? — every lie you told me pointed away from the same place. The ravines. The western dark. The deep grove. You can forge a road, but you cannot forge the direction a person looks when they're lying. You have looked west every day for eleven days."

*Oh,* she thought, with a strange floating clarity. *Oh, you fool. You arrogant, meticulous fool.*

Because he had read her correctly and drawn exactly the wrong conclusion — or rather, the conclusion she had spent eleven days teaching him to draw. The false map, the western glances, the whole architecture of her deception had been built as a wall around the true approach, which lay north, through the burned groves, along the old processional road where the trees had died a century ago and left their ghosts standing. No one guarded the dead wood. Everyone knew the heart of the Weeping Wood must lie among the living.

Except that Halven, standing there with her forged map in his hand, believed the west was the truth she was hiding.

And then he said, softly: "So tomorrow my army goes west."

*No.*

The word arrived in her like ice water. Because she had built the lie to be broken — had built it expecting him to see through it eventually and waste a week thrashing the ravines — but not yet, not tomorrow, not while Coral was still on the north road, not while Wren Ashvale was still leading him through the living dark toward the heart-tree. If Halven marched west tomorrow, he would find nothing, yes — but he would find nothing *quickly*, and a man like Halven, denied once, would burn the entire forest simply to be sure. He would stop being patient. Patience was the only weapon she had left.

Unless.

Unless she gave him the north road herself.

The thought formed whole and terrible, the way the worst thoughts do — fully armored, already logical. *Give him the north. Give him the true road. March him straight into the heart of the Wood where Coral will be standing, where the prophecy says he must stand, where the name waits in the roots.* It was madness. It was handing the wolf the lamb's address. But the alternative was the wolf burning down the forest to find the lamb anyway, and at least this way—

At least this way Coral would know they were coming. At least this way the battle would happen at the heart-tree, where whatever power slept in the roots was strongest, instead of scattered across a hundred burning groves. At least this way she chose the ground.

*You are rationalizing,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Vess. *You are dressing a betrayal in armor and calling it strategy.*

*Yes,* she answered the voice. *Yes, I am. Watch me.*

"Inquisitor," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and hated how steady, "you're right. I've been lying to you since the first night. And you're right about why." She let her shoulders drop a fraction — just a fraction, the surrender of a woman whose game was up, not a woman mid-game. "Coral Brightwater is alive. He's in the Wood. And I have been trying to keep you away from both."

Halven went very still. Stillness, on him, was more frightening than motion.

"Go on."

"He's the last swordspeaker. You've suspected it — the guild records you stole say the line wasn't ended, only hidden, and you've always known Vess raised a foundling with no history older than his seventh year. He's in the deep grove. And the spirits are taking him to the heart-tree, because his blood carries the Wood's true name." Each sentence cost her something physical; she could feel it leaving her like heat off a wound. "If you want him, you'll need to move fast, and you'll need to move north, through the dead groves along the processional road. The living Wood won't let you cross anywhere else. It's already killed your scouts in the west. It knows you're coming."

"Why," Halven said quietly, "are you telling me this?"

"Because you'll find out anyway. Because you have my map and my tells and my eleven days of lies, and a man like you doesn't leave loose threads." She met his eyes and poured sincerity into the meeting like water into a cup, and despised herself with a purity she had never achieved in prayer. "And because if this ends with the Wood burned and Coral dead and me alive, I would rather it end faster. I'm tired, Inquisitor. I've been tired for eleven days."

It was, she thought later, the best performance of her life, and it worked precisely because most of it was true. Halven studied her for a long moment, and then he laughed — a short, genuine bark of laughter that was somehow worse than anything else in the tent.

"There it is," he said. "There's the honest face. Do you know, I think I might actually have liked you, in another life." He rolled up her forged map and tossed it into the brazier, where it curled and flared. Then he unrolled a second map — his own, military, dense with annotations in red — and weighted its corners. "North road. Processional route. Through the ash groves." His finger traced it, and Sera watched the finger move and felt each mile of it like a stone dropped into her chest. "We march at dawn. Two thousand men, fire-carts, and the new ordinance from the capital — alchemical, burns wet wood." He glanced up. "You'll ride with me. In case I need an archivist who knows the guild's seals."

*Of course,* she thought. *Of course you won't let me out of your sight. Of course.*

"Yes, Inquisitor," she said.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and the Weeping Wood watched them go.

Sera rode in the column's belly, between the fire-carts, close enough to smell the alchemical reek of the ordinance — a smell like vinegar and burnt hair that made her eyes stream and her throat close. Around her, two thousand men marched north into the ash groves, and the dead trees stood over the road like a congregation of gray witnesses, and no one sang, and no one spoke above a murmur, though no order enforcing silence had been given. The Wood imposed its own hush even in death.

She had one asset, and it was small, and she had spent the whole night preparing it.

Archivists of the Guild of Impossible Things were trained in palimpsest work — the reading and making of texts written beneath texts. For eleven days she had passed Halven intelligence written in ordinary ink, knowing he would have it copied and verified by his own scribes. But last night, after giving him the north road, she had asked permission to copy his campaign map for the guild's records — *"for continuity, Inquisitor, in case I'm killed"* — and he had allowed it, amused, indulgent, certain of her now. And while she copied, her other hand, the one below the desk's sightline, had worked a needle-thin stylus into the wax tablet she carried, writing a second message beneath the first where no eye but a trained one would ever find it.

The tablet was in her saddlebag now. The message was simple, addressed to the only person who might pass through the ash groves ahead of the army, the only ally she had left inside the Wood:

*Vess. Or whoever finds this. He comes north at dawn, two thousand strong, fire-carts, alchemical burn. He knows about Coral. He knows about the name. The heart-tree is the only ground where you can meet him. I am sorry. I am sorry. I am bringing him to you myself because it was the only road left. Tell Coral — tell him—*

She had stopped there, needle hovering, and finally written: *Tell him the ninth stair remembers.*

It was nothing. It was a nonsense phrase from their first year in the archives, a joke about the stair that creaked outside the restricted stacks, the one they'd both learned to skip. It meant: *it's me. It was always me. Even the lies were mine, and mine were yours.*

Now the army marched, and the tablet rode against her hip, and the question was delivery. There would be no chance. Halven kept her in the center of the column; the pickets doubled at night; the ash groves were empty of everything but ghosts. Unless—

Unless the Wood was not as empty as it looked.

It happened at the third hour, in a stretch of road where the dead trees grew thickest, their branches interlocking overhead into a roof of gray bone. The column halted — no command given, just a halt, the way a body halts when it hears something in the dark. Sera lifted her head. Ahead, at the column's van, men were shouting, and then the shouting stopped in a way that was worse than continuing.

A horse came back down the road alone. Its rider was gone. And tied to its saddlebow, wrapped in oilcloth, was a bundle that the quartermaster unwrapped with shaking hands: a dozen arrows, fletched in green and silver, and a branch — a living branch, budding, impossibly green amid all the ash — driven through the center of a soldier's helmet like a nail through a crown.

On the oilcloth, in characters that seemed to writhe when looked at directly, was written a single word in the old tongue of the Wood. Sera read it upside down from her saddle and her heart slammed against her ribs.

It said: *LISTENING.*

They know, she thought. The Wood knows the army is coming. Someone is scouting the column — someone fast, someone of the awakened spirits or allied with them — and that someone had just thrown away a perfect chance at terror to send a message instead. *Listening.* Not attacking. Listening.

Which meant the Wood wanted information too. Which meant, if she could get word to that scout—

The opportunity came within the hour, because opportunities come to those who have spent their lives noticing misfilings. At the noon halt, Halven summoned her to the van to consult on the road ahead — the processional forked at an ancient waystone, and his maps disagreed with themselves, and he wanted the guild's memory of the original route. She rode forward through the stopped column, and as she passed the baggage train she saw it: a mule team left standing unattended for the space of a minute, its driver gone forward to gawk at the waystone like everyone else.

Sera had practiced this drop a hundred times in her cell at night, her fingers moving in the dark. She slipped the wax tablet from her saddlebag, palmed it, and let it fall into the gap between the second mule's pack-frame and the third's, wedged in canvas shadow, invisible unless you knew to look. On its face, visible to anyone who checked her belongings — because Halven would check, of course he would check — was a bland inventory note in her own hand.

Then she rode on to Halven and gave him the guild's memory of the road, and it was accurate, every mile of it, because from here to the heart-tree she could afford no more lies. The lie was done. The trap was baited with truth now, and the truth was the cruelest thing she had ever carried.

* * *

They reached the edge of the living Wood at dusk.

The change came without warning — one stride the trees were ash, the next they were alive, and not merely alive but *aware*. Sera felt it through her horse's hide before she felt it in her own skin: a pressure, a regard, the sensation of walking into a room where every chair is occupied. The column stopped. Two thousand men stood at the treeline of the Weeping Wood and looked into a darkness full of slow-moving light, and the light was sap, rising in the trunks like lantern-oil, and the trees were weeping, great amber tears that fell and struck the ground and did not soak in but *waited*.

"Torches," Halven said. "Fire-carts forward. We make two miles before full dark."

And then the Wood answered.

Not with violence. With voice. From every trunk along the treeline, from the deep places and the high branches, came a sound like a choir tuning itself — whispers braided into words, words in no language the soldiers knew, except that every one of them understood it anyway, the way one understands a mother's tone. And what the Wood said, in a thousand overlapping voices, was a name.

*Coral.*

Two thousand men heard it. Sera saw them hear it — saw a veteran drop his torch, saw a boy of sixteen begin to cry without knowing why. The name rolled through the ash groves and back again, and the dead trees seemed to lean toward the sound like the faithful toward a bell.

Halven Marr stood unmoved in the front rank, and Sera, watching him, saw the exact moment his certainty cracked — not fear, nothing so honest as fear, but the first hairline fracture of a man discovering the world contains a larger ledger than his own. He had come to burn a forest. He had arrived, instead, at a coronation.

"Fire-carts forward," he repeated, and his voice was steady, and only Sera, who had studied liars all her life, heard the seam in it.

That night, in the officers' tent where she was kept under guard, Sera sat awake and listened to the Wood whisper the name over and over, and she pressed her palm flat against the earth floor as if she could push a message through it. *Hold on. Hold on. I'm coming, and I'm sorry, and I'm bringing the whole ugly world with me, and you have to be ready, you have to reach the heart-tree, you have to—*

Beyond the pickets, far off in the living dark, a horn sounded. Once. Long. High.

It was not a soldier's horn. It was not the Wood's whispering. It was a signal — human-made, guild-made, a call she knew from a hundred drills in the undercroft yard. Three notes descending, then silence.

The guild's signal for *the vault is breached.*

Someone was coming out of the Wood. Someone was coming toward the army, tonight, through the ash groves — and every instinct Sera owned, the archivist's and the liar's and the lover's, rose up screaming the same answer.

Coral Brightwater was coming to meet them.
