## 16. Armies of Sap and Ash

The Wood woke screaming.

Coral felt it before he heard it — a tremor in the ground beneath his knees, a shudder running up through the roots and into the bones of the world, as if some vast sleeping animal had been struck in its flank. He was still kneeling at the ring-archives where Wren had shown him the burned century, his palms pressed flat against living bark that now pulsed like a throat swallowing.

"They've reached the treeline," Wren said. She was already on her feet, and there was something in her face he had never seen there before: not fear, but a terrible readiness, the look of someone who has rehearsed a grief so many times it has worn smooth. "The outer groves. The ash-cedars first. They always burn the ash-cedars first."

Through the canopy far to the south, the dawn had gone the wrong color. Not gold, not grey — orange, a low sullen orange like coals seen through a shutter, and beneath it rose a sound Coral had no ledger-entry for. It was not the crackle of ordinary fire. It was a chorus. The trees were screaming, and the screams carried for miles, thin and high and heartbreakingly patient, the way a child cries when it believes no one is coming.

"Can they—" Coral began, and stopped, because his voice had failed him.

"Feel it? Yes." Wren pulled him up by the wrist. Her grip was iron wrapped in silk; her hands, he noticed for the first time, were scarred in old green lines, like sap that had bled and healed and bled again. "Every tree that burns, every spirit bound inside it burns with it. That is what your order did to us, Coral Brightwater. You sewed us into the wood and then you taught the world the wood was only timber." She turned south, toward the orange light, and her jaw set. "And now the world has come to finish the accounting."

* * *

They ran.

The Weeping Wood in full motion was nothing like the Wood at rest. Paths he had walked two days past had rearranged themselves; roots reared from the soil like the backs of sea-things surfacing, then sank again to let them pass or rose to bar their way, testing them, reading them. The trees wept as they ran beneath them — great slow beads of amber sap welling from every wound in the bark and falling with a sound like small bells, like rain on glass, like the ticking of an enormous clock counting down. The whispering he had heard since his first night here was no longer fragments. It was a name, repeated over and over through ten thousand mouths, and though he could not yet hold it in his mind, he knew — with the certainty of blood recognizing blood — that it was *his*.

Wren ran ahead of him, sure-footed as water finding its level, and shouted directions over her shoulder in a language that was not language: clicks and hums and long vowels that the trees answered. Once she stopped, laid both palms against a birch whose white skin had split down its length, and spoke softly to it for three heartbeats. When she stepped away, the birch's wound had sealed itself into a shape like a doorway.

"Shortcut," she said. "It will hold for maybe a hundred breaths. After that it closes whether we're through or not."

"Where are we going?"

"The grove of the wardens. Where the spirits wake first, and where the fighting will be worst." She looked back at him, and in the amber half-light her eyes were the exact color of the falling sap. "You don't have to come. Whatever happens next, no one — nothing — will think less of you for staying out of it. You didn't ask for any of this."

"I asked for none of it," Coral said, "and I was given all of it. That's what an inheritance is."

Something moved across her face — grief, or pride, or the place where those two things share a border — and then she went through the birch-doorway and he followed, and the world folded around them like a page being turned.

* * *

The grove of the wardens was already burning when they arrived.

Not all of it. A wedge of it — the southern arc, where the trees stood closest together and the undergrowth was thickest — had been fired by something more deliberate than stray flame. Fire-arrows, Coral thought, or Greek-fire pots hurled from the treeline, because this burning had edges, had intention, had been drawn on the forest like a line drawn on a map. And through the smoke came the soldiers.

Halven Marr's men advanced in ranks of thirty, shields up, torches forward, moving with the grim mechanical patience of men who had been promised the trees would not fight back. Behind them, siege engines creaked — great arms loaded with clay jars that burst into clinging flame when they struck. Ahead of them, the outermost spirits were rising.

Coral had seen the spirits asleep, in the deep groves: shapes of bark and moss and drifting pollen, dream-slow, barely more than suggestions. These were not asleep. These came out of their trees like knives out of sheaths.

The first one he saw clearly was small — no taller than a child, made of woven shadow and the pale wood of a lightning-struck pine, with eyes like two drops of resin catching firelight. It walked out of the smoke directly into the path of the front rank, and it did not run, and it did not hide, and it raised one twig-thin hand in a gesture that needed no translation: *stop.*

The rank did not stop. The rank had orders. Coral watched a soldier — a boy, younger than Sera, his helmet too big for him — drive a spear through the small spirit's chest, and he watched the spirit look down at the shaft of ash-wood protruding from it with an expression that was not pain. It was recognition. Ash to ash. The spear had been made from one of them, once, and the spirit touched the shaft almost tenderly before it came apart into embers and drifting seed.

Then the grove answered.

It was not a battle so much as a change of weather. Roots erupted under the shield-wall and threw men skyward like dolls. Branches that had been overhead a moment ago were suddenly *among* them, sweeping in wide scything arcs, and the weeping sap fell everywhere, hissing where it struck torches, sealing wounds, blinding eyes. The soldiers' formation broke into knots, and the knots broke into individuals, and the individuals discovered that the forest had stopped being terrain and become an enemy with ten thousand hands.

And still they burned things. Even dying, even screaming, they put their torches to the bark, because a man with a torch and an order is the most dangerous animal alive.

Coral stood at the grove's edge with Wren's hand on his arm, and he did the thing that had been trained into him across twenty years of ledgers and oaths: he assessed. He counted. He tried to find the neutral position, the auditor's vantage, the high shelf from which everything could be recorded without being touched.

There was no such shelf. There never had been. He understood that now, watching a warden-spirit — one of Wren's people, a tall figure of river-stone and lichen who had bowed to him yesterday with grave courtesy — take three spears meant for a sapling-spirit behind her, and go down, and keep going down, into the torn earth, while the soldiers cheered as though they had taken a wall.

"Neutrality," Coral said aloud, and the word tasted like ash, "was always just a slower way of choosing."

He reached for his swordspeech.

It came the way it always came — the old litany rising in his throat, the oath-shapes assembling themselves out of breath and will. But it came *changed*. Since the fused page had opened, since his blood had touched prophecy, the words felt less like commands and more like questions, and something vast was leaning in to hear the answers. He spoke the binding-oath anyway, the oldest one, the one every swordspeaker learned first: *Blade, be still.* He aimed it not at a sword but at the nearest knot of soldiers, at the torches in their hands.

For one glorious heartbeat, forty flames guttered flat, as if a giant had cupped them in a palm.

Then the power *twisted*, the way a hound twists against a leash, and Coral felt it straining toward something else entirely — toward the true name coiled in his marrow, toward the word that would not still forty torches but unmake the whole idea of fire, or crown it, or turn it loose. His vision doubled. He tasted copper and amber. Somewhere very far away, Wren was shouting his name, and somewhere much closer than that, the Wood itself was listening to see what he would become.

He let the oath go. The torches roared back to life. He staggered, gasping, and Wren caught him.

"Don't," she said fiercely, gripping his face between her hands. "Not here, not scattered, not in anger. If you speak the name with rage in your mouth, it won't free us, Coral. It will arm us. Every spirit in this grove will become a weapon wearing a memory, and we will burn the world exactly as thoroughly as your order burned us, and we will call it justice right up until the last field goes black." Her thumbs pressed hard against his cheekbones. "The name needs a clean heart. Do you understand me? It needs—"

The arrow took her through the side.

Coral saw it happen the way one sees lightning: complete, instantaneous, already past. One moment Wren was holding his face; the next she was turning — turning *between* him and the treeline, her body moving with the unhesitating economy of someone who had made this choice long ago and was only waiting for the chance to keep it — and the black-fletched shaft punched through her below the ribs and out again, trailing a rope of luminous sap that glowed like a struck match in the smoky air.

She made no sound. That was the worst of it. She looked down at herself with mild surprise, the way one looks at a spilled ledger, and then her knees unhinged and Coral caught her, and they went down together into the smoking loam.

"Wren. *Wren.*"

"It's nothing," she said, which was the first lie he had ever heard her tell, and they both knew it, and she smiled anyway. Sap welled between his fingers where he pressed the wound, warm and sweet-smelling, and each drop that fell from her body struck the ground and became, instantly, a tiny green shoot — life pouring out of her faster than the earth could catch it.

"You're healing," he said desperately. "You're a spirit, you can heal, tell me how to help you heal—"

"We don't heal from iron." Her voice was fraying at the edges. "We heal from *tending*. From being tended. And there's no time, Coral, listen to me, there's no time for tending." She seized his collar and dragged him down until his forehead nearly touched hers, and her resin-bright eyes held his with the full weight of everything she was. "The heart-tree. You have to reach the heart-tree. Halven's engineers are cutting the processional road even now — I can feel their axes in my teeth — and when they breach the inner grove, the choice stops being yours. They'll force the waking. They'll make us into the war whether you speak or not."

"I'm not leaving you."

"Yes," she said gently, "you are. That's what it costs. That's what it has always cost, everyone, always — the ones who carry names leave the ones who tend wounds." Her hand slid from his collar to his chest, over his heart, and pressed there. "Listen. In the rings, in the archive, you read how the first swordspeaker stood at the heart-tree and spoke our name with hatred, and bound us. The name doesn't care about good and evil, Coral. It cares about the *shape of the speaker's heart*. Hatred binds. Grief binds. Fear binds." Her fingers curled into the fabric over his heart. "Only one thing unbinds. Only one thing has ever unbound anything."

"And if I can't find it?" His voice cracked like young ice. "If I get there and my heart is just — this. Smoke and spears and you bleeding out in my arms?"

"Then you'll bind us again," Wren said, "and I will spend whatever years I have left teaching you to live with it. Because that's what love is, in the end. Not the feeling. The *tending*." She pushed him away — pushed him with the last of her strength, a real push, staggering him back a step — and turned her face toward the burning grove, toward her people falling among the invaders, and her voice went steady as a root. "Go. The birch-road is still open. Run, swordspeaker. Run and be the last of your order and the first of whatever comes after."

Behind her, the battle surged. A soldier broke from the smoke — the boy with the too-big helmet, the one who had speared the small pine-spirit, his face now smeared with ash and terror — and leveled his spear at Wren where she lay.

Coral did not think. Thinking was for auditors, for men on high shelves, for the person he had been four days ago. He drew the guild-blade from his back — the bound blade, the oath-blade, the sword that had answered his voice since he was twelve years old — and he spoke to it, one word, the simplest word in the whole litany:

"*Fly.*"

The blade left his hand like a bird leaving a cage. It crossed thirty feet of burning air and struck the spear from the boy's grip, and then the flat of it cracked against the boy's breastplate and knocked him down — disarmed him, dropped him, *spared* him — and Coral stood over Wren with his empty hand still extended and his heart hammering a single word against his ribs: *choice, choice, choice.*

The boy stared up at him from the ground. Around them, the grove burned and screamed and fought, and the sap fell like slow amber rain, and somewhere in the smoke a siege engine groaned as it swung another jar of fire toward the inner trees.

"There's a road," Coral heard himself say to the boy, and his voice came out strange and calm, the calm of a man who has finally finished an audit and found the number. "South, past the split birch. Your officers will tell you the trees are mindless. They aren't. They'll let you leave. Go home."

The boy scrambled backward, got his feet under him, and ran. Coral did not watch him go. He knelt, and gathered Wren against the root of a great oak whose bark had split in long weeping seams, and arranged her hands over her wound the way she had shown him, and she looked up at him with eyes already dimming at the edges and said, "The heart-tree. Now. Before the axes."

"I'll send help," he said. "Sera will come, Vess will—"

"Coral." She caught his sleeve. "Whatever you find at the heart-tree — whatever the last page says you'll lose — pay it. Promise me. Don't haggle with the price. Men like Halven haggle. Men like your order haggled, and that's how a penance becomes an empire." Her smile was fading now, going soft at the borders like a watermark. "Pay it whole."

He kissed her forehead, tasting sap and smoke, and then he ran — through the birch-doorway as it began to close, shouldering through the narrowing gap while the wood scraped his shoulders like a mother reluctant to let a child cross a street — out onto the processional road, alone, unarmed except for a blade that no longer fully obeyed him and a name he did not yet know how to say.

Ahead, through the trees, he could hear the axes. Behind him, the grove of the wardens burned, and Wren lay tending herself with nothing but her own stubborn love, and the last page of the fused prophecy waited at the heart of everything, legible now in his blood, saying what it had said for a century:

*the speaker will lose what he loves most.*

Coral Brightwater ran toward the price.

* * *

He found the heart-tree at dusk, and he found it besieged.

Halven's engineers had done their work well. The processional road — an ancient avenue of paired stones that the Wood itself had never dared close — was littered with the wreckage of the day: broken engines, abandoned shields, the bodies of soldiers and spirits tangled together like the aftermath of some terrible wedding. And at the end of it, rising out of a clearing where the light fell differently than light ought to fall, stood the heart-tree.

It was not the largest tree in the Wood. It was the *first* one — the trunk from which, if the ring-archives told true, all the Weeping Wood had grown, before the curse bent it low and taught it to weep. Its bark was silver-black and smooth as closed eyelids, and carved into it, worn nearly to invisibility by a thousand years of rain, was the original binding: the first swordspeaker's oath, cut deep, still faintly bleeding after all this time. A thread of red ran down the furrows of the carving like a signature that refused to dry.

Around its base, Halven's remaining companies had formed a hollow square, torches outward, axes biting into the root-flares wherever they could reach. And above them, on a platform of lashed timbers that had been built today, built *fast*, stood a figure in grey-and-gold that Coral knew even at this distance: Inquisitor Halven Marr, holding a book.

Not the fused prophecy. A ledger. Coral's ledger — the Guild of Impossible Things' own record, stolen from the vault, open in Halven's hands like a weapon sighted along its spine.

"Last swordspeaker!" Halven's voice carried across the clearing, amplified by some capital-made artifice, smooth and reasonable and utterly cold. "I know you're in the trees. Your guildmaster confessed everything before she walked into this forest to die. Come down. Bring the name. Or I begin cutting, root by root, and we discover together whether the Wood bleeds fast enough to matter."

The axes bit again. The heart-tree shuddered, and the red thread in the carving brightened, and Coral felt the true name stir in his marrow like a sleeper turning over.

He had come to pay a price. Halven, it seemed, intended to make him bid for it.

Coral stepped out of the treeline, into the firelight, and began to walk down the road of his ancestors.
