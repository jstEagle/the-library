## 20. What Was Taken

Wren Ashvale had lived three hundred years without sleeping, and so she had never once dreamed — until the night the name was spoken.

She did not know that was what it was, at first: the soft dark behind her eyes, the strange weightlessness of a mind unmoored from its body. For three centuries she had been bound to the Weeping Wood as a warden is bound to a warding-post, her awareness nailed to the groves like a nail to a doorframe. She had felt every axe. She had felt every fire. She had felt Coral Brightwater walk beneath the boughs for the first time and had known him before he knew himself, the way a river knows the shape of the stone it has been carving in the dark for a thousand years.

Now, in this new and unprecedented darkness, she dreamed of a garden that did not exist. There were no weeping trees in it. Nothing bled. A woman with Wren's face knelt in the loam planting something small and green, and when she looked up, her eyes were full of a grief so clean it could almost be mistaken for joy.

Then the dream broke like an egg against a bowl's edge, and Wren woke — truly woke, for the first time in three hundred years — to a Wood that was silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The difference rang through her like a struck bell. For three centuries the forest had whispered, had wept its amber tears down wounded bark, had murmured fragments of stolen childhoods into the ears of anyone who walked too deep. That whispering had been the sound of chains: spirits half-awake inside their trees, dreaming other people's memories because they had none of their own, speaking only in the voices of the dead because their own voices had been taken as collateral for a curse older than any kingdom.

The silence now was different. It was the silence of a room after a door has finally been opened. It was the silence of things listening to their own heartbeats for the first time.

Wren stood beneath the heart-tree, and the heart-tree stood over her, and where its trunk had been scarred by the original speaking — the great gouge where the first swordspeaker had driven his oath into living wood like a knife into a throat — the bark had begun, impossibly, gently, to close.

*It worked,* she thought, and then, because thought was not enough, she said it aloud, in her own voice, which she had not used in three hundred years except in whispers to trees: "It worked."

Her voice cracked on the second word. She was free, and the first thing freedom gave her was the ability to weep properly, with her whole body, and she used it.

* * *

They came out of the trees slowly, the way animals come out after a storm — one at a time, then in twos and threes, then in dozens. Wren watched them from the roots of the heart-tree and tried to find words for what she was seeing, and failed, and decided that failing was itself a kind of worship.

The spirit of the alder grove had been an alder for four hundred years. Now she stepped out of her tree the way a hand steps out of a glove, leaving the wood standing behind her, empty and alive and merely a tree — and she was a woman made of pale light and river-mud, blinking, staggering, laughing. The oak-spirits came next, vast and slow, unfolding out of trunks thick as siege towers, and their first act of freedom was to kneel. Not to Wren. Not to Coral. They knelt to the ground itself, pressing their newly-made hands into the soil, and Wren understood that they were checking. Making sure it was real. Making sure the earth still answered them, that they could feel it again through their own skin instead of through roots.

One of them — the birch-spirit, thin and trembling, who had been a child when the swordspeakers came, who had spent four centuries dreaming the memories of a girl who died in the burning century — looked up at Wren with eyes like sap and asked, in a voice rusted with disuse:

"Is it over?"

"It's over," Wren said.

"Who paid?"

And Wren could not answer that one yet. She turned instead toward the clearing at the Wood's southern edge, where the guild's people had made camp among the blackened stumps of the outer groves, and began to walk.

* * *

Coral Brightwater lay on a bedroll in the guild camp, and he was breathing, and his color was good, and Guildmaster Vess had not left his side since dawn. Wren stopped at the edge of the clearing and watched them through the thinning smoke — the old woman with her silver braids undone and her guildmaster's chain buried somewhere in the mud, holding her son's hand as though the holding itself were keeping him tethered to the world.

He looked younger asleep. He had looked young awake, too, but there had always been something behind his eyes — the ledger-keeper's vigilance, the swordspeaker's coiled readiness, the particular loneliness of a man who catalogs other people's wonders because he suspects he has none of his own. All of that was smoothed away now. His face was just a face. A handsome one, Wren thought, with an ache that had edges; she had loved that face across two lifetimes and it had never once looked this peaceful in her presence.

Vess sensed her — of course she did; whatever else Vess had done or forged or hidden, she had the Wood's own blood in her line, diluted but present — and lifted her head.

"You're the warden," Vess said. Not a question. Her voice was raw. "The one from the rings. From the records."

"I'm Wren Ashvale."

"He keeps saying—" Vess stopped. Swallowed. Started again, and Wren watched her rebuild herself brick by brick, the way she must have rebuilt herself a hundred times across a long life built on necessary lies. "When he wakes. He won't—"

"No," Wren said. "He won't."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet and furious and grateful all at once, three things that should not have fit in the same face. "I wrote part of the history he trusted. I forged ledgers to keep him safe. I told myself every lie was a kindness." Her jaw tightened. "And the price of the truth was paid by someone who never even got to choose whether to learn it."

"He chose," Wren said quietly. "That's the thing no one understands about the speaking, Guildmaster. The name doesn't take anything you're hiding behind. It takes what you love most. He knew. He stood under the heart-tree with my hands on his shoulders and I told him exactly what it would cost, and he said—" Her voice failed her, briefly. She let it. Three hundred years of practice at being silent had taught her that some pauses are not weakness but architecture. "He said, *then it will know what I value.*"

Vess bowed her head over her son's hand and said nothing at all, which was, Wren thought, the first honest thing the woman had done in years.

* * *

Coral woke at dusk.

Wren was not there when it happened. She had gone back to the heart-tree, because there was work — the freed spirits were frightened in the way of all newborn things, and the outer groves were ash, and Halven's army had melted back toward the capital like frost off glass, leaderless, their inquisitor carried out on a litter with his certainty burned out of him along with his vanguard. There were treaties to consider, boundaries to draw, a century of enslaved memory to help the spirits sort into theirs and not-theirs. Work was a mercy. Work kept her walking south instead of north, toward the camp, toward the bedroll, toward the moment she both dreaded and had already rehearsed forty times in her head.

So she was not there when he opened his eyes and saw Vess and said, in a voice hoarse as a crow's:

"I'm sorry — do I know you? Where am I? Why does my hand smell like sap?"

She was not there when Vess told him his name, and watched him take it like a stranger's coat. She was not there when he asked how long he'd been ill, and Vess, after a long silence, said, "About a year," and he laughed, kindly, confusedly, and said, "A year? Mother, I was in the undercroft last week cataloguing bottled thunder."

Mother. He said it easily. Naturally. As though it had always been true — and perhaps, Wren thought, in the ledger of things, it had always been true and simply unrecorded, one more entry filed in the wrong book.

She was there, though, an hour later, when he walked to the treeline.

She felt him coming before she saw him — not through the Wood anymore; that bond was severed, cut clean with everything else, and she was learning that freedom had teeth — but through her own senses, her own ancient warden's instinct, which apparently survived the loss of everything else. She was standing among the birches doing nothing in particular, which is to say grieving in the way she preferred: upright, motionless, facing away from the thing that hurt.

"Coral!" Sera Quill's voice, from the camp behind him — sharp, urgent, afraid. "Coral, wait—"

But he had already seen Wren. And Wren turned, because she had promised herself she would turn, because running would have been a cruelty to them both.

He stopped ten paces away. The evening light came through the birches in long gold blades and lay across his face, and he looked at her with the open, courteous puzzlement of a stranger meeting a stranger.

"I'm sorry," he said. "You're — you were in the camp. Are you one of ours? You look—" He hesitated, and something moved behind his eyes, some fish turning in deep water, some echo rising and failing to break the surface. "You look like someone I should know."

"Yes," Wren said.

"I'm Coral. Coral Brightwater." He offered his hand, absurdly formal, a ledger-keeper's handshake, and the gesture nearly undid her — because she had held those hands while they shook with power, had laced her fingers through his at the heart-tree, had taught him the shape of the name with her mouth against his ear, and here he was offering it to her like a merchant closing a trade.

"Wren," she said, and took his hand, and held it one heartbeat longer than a stranger would. "Wren Ashvale."

Something flickered across his face. Grief without content. A wound that remembered being a wound but had lost the story of the blade. He frowned faintly, touched his own chest as though checking for blood, and found none.

"That's strange," he said softly. "Your name feels like — like a page I've read. Like I know the shape of the letters without the words." He laughed, embarrassed, gentle. "Forgive me. Everyone says I've been ill. I keep having these — shadows. Feelings with nothing attached." His eyes went past her, up into the birches, and widened slightly. "This forest. Have I been here before? It feels like—" He searched. "Like coming home to a house I've never lived in."

"You've been here," Wren said. "You've been here more truly than anyone in a thousand years. And you saved it. Every tree you see — they're free because of you."

He stared at her. Behind him, at the treeline, Sera had arrived at last, breathless, mud to her knees, and stopped as if hitting glass. But Coral didn't turn. He was looking at Wren with an intensity that had nothing of memory in it and everything of something older than memory — the way a compass needle looks north without knowing why.

"Why does that make me want to cry?" he asked. Simply. Without shame. "I don't understand any of this. I don't remember saving anything. I don't remember you. But when you say it, it lands somewhere—" He pressed his fist lightly below his sternum. "Here. Like a bell struck in another room."

"Then it landed true," Wren said. Her voice held. She would make certain of that if it cost her the last of her three centuries. "That's enough. That's more than enough."

"Coral!" Sera called again, closer now, and this time he turned, and Wren watched the whole geometry of the future arrange itself in a single glance: him turning toward the woman running through the birches, the woman stopping short, the two of them looking at each other with the stunned recognition of people who have met in a dream neither can recall. Sera's face was doing something complicated — joy and devastation holding each other up — and Coral's face was doing something simpler: lighting.

"Do I know you?" he said to Sera, and smiled, and it was a real smile, warm and unguarded, the smile of a man with no ghosts.

"Not yet," Sera said. Her voice shook. She steadied it. "But I'm going to fix that. I remember everything, Coral. Every single day of it. And I'm going to tell you all of it, however long it takes."

* * *

Wren left that night.

She did not decide to so much as discover that she already had — that somewhere between the heart-tree and the treeline, between *it worked* and *you look like someone I should know*, the choice had made itself in the place where choices are actually made, deeper than deliberation. She packed nothing. She owned nothing. Three hundred years a warden teaches you that everything you carry is either borrowed or a burden.

The freed spirits gathered at the Wood's western edge to see her off, and the birch-child — the one who had been a girl once, before the burning century — pressed something into her hands: a cutting, no longer than a forearm, taken from the heart-tree's newest growth, wrapped in moss. Green shoots at the joints. Alive.

"For the city," the birch-spirit said. "For wherever he ends up. So the Wood can watch over him, even from far away. Even if he never knows."

Wren closed her fingers around it and could not speak for a moment. Then: "He keeps ledgers," she managed. "He'll plant it properly. Measure it. Write down its height every season." She smiled, and the smile hurt, and she let it hurt, because that was the whole point — she was free now, free to grieve at full volume, free to feel the whole weight of what she had lost instead of carrying it the way the chained carry things: silently, forever, in the dark. "He'll give it a name. He names everything. It's how he loves things."

"Will you go to him?" the alder-woman asked. "Tell him? Make him remember?"

"No."

The word came out steady, and Wren marveled at it, the way you marvel at a bridge that holds.

"If I stay beside him telling him our story every day, he'll spend his life mourning a ghost he can't see — loving a stranger's grief out of politeness. That isn't remembering. That's haunting." She looked back once, at the dark mass of the Wood behind her, at the fires of the guild camp glowing beyond it, at one small figure sitting up late by a lantern with a ledger already open on his knees, writing, because writing was how he stitched himself to the world. "He gets to be new. That was the gift. I won't spend it for him."

"And you?" the birch-child asked. "What do you get?"

Wren considered the question honestly, the way she considered everything now, at full price.

"Grief," she said. "Real grief, mine, chosen, at my own pace, in my own direction. Do you know how rare that is? The enslaved don't get to mourn. Mourning needs a self." She lifted the cutting a little. "I get to carry this somewhere green and put it in the earth and visit it. I get to be the one who remembers — freely, not by curse. And someday, maybe, I get to be a stranger who walks past a guildhouse window and sees an impossible tree growing through the floor, and smiles at it, and keeps walking."

She turned west, toward the mountains, toward the places on no map, and took her first step as a free woman.

Behind her, faint on the night air, she heard the guild camp: Sera's voice, low and patient and relentless, beginning at the beginning. *"It started with a ledger,"* Sera was saying. *"Everything with him starts with a ledger. There was this book in the vault, you see, with today's date written in it a hundred years ago..."*

Wren walked, and listened as long as the wind allowed, and then listened to the wind instead.

And far behind her, in a camp full of strangers who loved him, Coral Brightwater bent over an open page and wrote, in his meticulous, careful hand, the first entry of a ledger he did not know was the oldest one he owned:

*Item: one feeling, unaccounted for. Source unknown. Description: the sense of having given something enormous away, gladly.*

*Disposition:* he paused, pen hovering, then wrote — *keep.*
