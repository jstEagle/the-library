## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The trouble with a hundred years of dust, Astrid Ishikawa had learned, was that it did not respect a person's good intentions.

She had come down into the archive that morning with a plan. A real plan, written out in her tidy hand on the back of an inventory slip: *Catalogue the west alcove. Rebind anything with a cracked spine. Do not get distracted.*

She had been distracted for approximately eleven minutes.

The Emberwright archive lived beneath the guildhall like a secret the building kept even from itself—a low stone room where the lamps burned low and the air tasted of old paper and older smoke. The guild's whole history was supposed to be up there in the hall: the great ledger of bindings, the dragon-fire oaths, the wall of masters' portraits all scowling down with the confidence of men who had never once doubted themselves. But the archive was where history actually lived, in its shirtsleeves, uncombed. And nobody had combed it in decades.

Astrid sat cross-legged on the floor with her sleeves pushed up and ink already smudged along one forearm, working through a crate of ledgers that smelled of mildew and, faintly, of resin—the sweet pine smell that clung to everything that came from the Weeping Wood proper. She'd made it through a tax dispute from sixty years ago (the guild had apparently once been very passionate about the price of tallow) and a recipe book for fireproofing wax that someone had annotated, in furious pencil, *DOES NOT WORK*.

That was when she found the prophecy ledger.

It wasn't labeled as such. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, and its spine had been shelved spine-inward, which was either an accident or somebody's idea of hiding something and doing a lazy job of it. She pulled it out mostly because pulling things out was her nature, and opened it across her knees.

The first pages were ordinary enough—weather records, harvest tallies, notes on the valley's boundary stones. Then, about a third of the way in, the handwriting changed. It became smaller, more careful, the letters of someone writing slowly because they believed every word mattered.

*What is written in the wood will come to pass,* the new hand began. *The wood does not forget, though we may wish it would.*

Astrid felt the small pleasant chill she always got around old strange books, the collector's thrill. She reached for her catalogue pen.

Then she saw the date.

Not today's date. Tonight's date—written a century ago, in ink gone brown as dried blood, followed by a line of text so plain it seemed almost apologetic:

*On this night the last flame falters, and the debt comes due. The one called Astrid Ishikawa will find this page.*

She read it twice. Three times. Her pen hovered over the page and left no mark, because her hand had stopped cooperating.

There were perhaps two hundred people in the valley. Ishikawa was not a common name here—it had arrived with her grandmother, along with a lacquered box nobody was allowed to open and a stubbornness the whole family had inherited like eye color. There had never been another Astrid. Her mother had told her once, laughing, that they'd searched three parishes' worth of records before giving up and letting the name stand alone.

*The one called Astrid Ishikawa will find this page.*

"Well," Astrid said aloud, to the empty archive, because saying things aloud made them less enormous. "Well, that's—huh."

Her voice came back at her flat and small, swallowed by stone.

She should have taken it to Halvard immediately. That was procedure; that was sense. Instead she sat there on the cold floor with the ledger open on her knees and read backward through the century, hunting for context, and what she found made the chill deepen from pleasant to otherwise. The prophecies came in clusters, decades apart, always in the same careful hand. Most were small things. A flood year, correctly guessed. A bad winter. A birth.

And then, scattered through them, entries like these:

*The wood remembers what we paid. Do not let them count the chandeliers.*

*A soul is not spent, only moved. This is not comfort. This is arithmetic.*

*If the valley holds, thank the one beneath. If the valley fails, blame me.*

Blame me. No signature. Just those two words, sitting at the bottom of a page like a man stepping off a ledge.

Astrid became aware, gradually, that her heart was going too fast for someone sitting still. She closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something might be sleeping, and carried it upstairs.

* * *

Halvard Emberwright—guildmaster in name if not in blood, since the Emberwright line had thinned to exactly him—was in his study doing what he did most evenings: failing to enjoy his tea. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-bearded, with hands that had once been famous for their steadiness. Binding dragon-fire to a human soul required a hand that didn't tremble, the histories said. Halvard's hands hadn't trembled in forty years of service.

He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw her—a quick warming, like a banked fire catching. He'd taken her on at nine years old, an orphan with soot on her chin and an unsuitable number of questions, and he had never once made her feel like a charity case. He'd made her feel like kindling. *You're not here to be kept, girl,* he'd said. *You're here to catch.*

"Tell me you've finished the west alcove," he said, "and I'll die a happy man."

"I've found something better than finished." She set the ledger on his desk, open to the page. "I found this."

She watched him read it. She watched it happen in order: the recognition, arriving before comprehension; then the comprehension; then the long moment where a man decides what his face is going to do about it.

His face decided on *mild interest*, which was the least convincing performance Astrid had ever seen, and she had once watched Tomas attempt to lie about eating an entire festival pie.

"Huh," Halvard said. "Old Maren's book. She fancied herself a seer. Half the valley thought so too, which tells you more about the valley than about Maren."

"Maren," Astrid said. "Is that who wrote it?"

"That's the story." He reached for the ledger.

And his hands shook.

Not much. Not the dramatic trembling of a guilty man in a ballad. Just a fine, fast shiver, like a candle flame in a draft, there and mastered. But Astrid had spent ten years watching those hands—steady on the binding irons, steady on her shoulder the day her mother died, steady pouring tea he never drank. She knew their vocabulary. This was a word she'd never heard them say.

He took the ledger and closed it, gently, as if it were sleeping. Or wounded.

"Superstition," he said. "Maren wrote dates and names the way a miller grinds grain—enough of it, and some of it's bound to be flour. Put it back in the alcove. Catalogue it under curiosities."

"Halvard." She almost never used his name without the title. Using it now felt like setting something down on the desk between them. "It has my name in it. My exact name. Written a hundred years ago."

"Names repeat."

"Not mine."

"Astrid." He said her name the way he said it when she was nine and asking why the valley had no magic, why the trees wept resin instead of sap, why the guild's great oaths were performed but never explained. Gently. Deflectingly. Like a hand laid over a candle to put it out. "You've been down in that archive breathing mold for three days. It makes the mind wander. Go get some air. Take Tomas; he's been moping by the forge all afternoon looking for an audience."

"I don't need an audience. I need you to tell me what *do not let them count the chandeliers* means."

Something went through his face then—fast, buried, real. Not confusion. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a sentence he'd hoped never to hear spoken aloud again.

"It means," he said, "that Maren had a poet's streak and a brewer's habits, and neither one makes a prophecy." He stood, and the chair scraped, and the room felt suddenly smaller with him in it standing. "I mean it, girl. Back in the box. Some pages are old because nobody needed them."

But he wouldn't look at the ledger while he said it. He looked at her, and past her, and at the window where the mountain stood against the evening sky—the mountain where, everyone knew, nothing lived, nothing burned, nothing waited. Serevain's hollow, they called the dark seam near its peak, though nobody could say who Serevain had been or why the name survived when everything else about it hadn't.

The last light was going amber on the snow. Somewhere below, the forge clanged—Tomas, hammering out his mood—and the valley smelled of pine and bread and woodsmoke, exactly as it smelled every evening, exactly as it had smelled, as far as Astrid knew, forever.

Safe. Unchanging. Hers.

Except that a dead woman's handwriting had just put a crack through all of it, thin as a hair in glass, and cracks like that don't close. They wait.

"Halvard," she said quietly. "Your hands are shaking."

He looked down at them as if they belonged to a stranger. He curled them closed, slowly, and when he raised his eyes to hers there was something in them she had never seen in forty years of his steadiness—not guilt exactly, and not fear exactly, but the place where the two meet and shake hands.

"Go home, Astrid," he said. "Lock your door tonight. And tomorrow—" he hesitated, and the hesitation lasted a heartbeat too long, "—tomorrow, come see me before you go anywhere near the tree line. Promise me that."

"Why the tree line? I never go near the tree line."

"I know," said Halvard Emberwright, and picked up his untouched tea with shaking hands. "That's what worries me."

Outside his window, behind him, unnoticed, the sun went down on the mountain—and for a moment, just a moment, the dark seam of Serevain's hollow glowed. Faintly. Like a lamp running out of oil.

Like a flame guttering.
