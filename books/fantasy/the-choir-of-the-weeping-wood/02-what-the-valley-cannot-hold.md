## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The ledger had said *tonight*, and so Astrid did what any sensible person raised on candle-wax and caution would do: she told no one, packed a satchel with bread, a striker, and her grandmother's good knife, and went to test the edge of the world.

Not the whole world. Just the valley's.

The Weeping Wood had rules the way an old house has creaks—you learned them young or you learned them bruised. Rule one: nothing strange crossed the tree line. No charms, no hexes, no whispered wishes coming true. The guild had spent three centuries being very proud of this fact, the way a man is proud of a roof that doesn't leak, without ever quite asking why he'd built his house in a rainstorm in the first place.

Astrid walked up through the terraces as the sun went long and gold behind the western ridge, past the hop fields where old Marta was scolding her goats with genuine artistry, past the shrine where the Emberwrights' names were carved into slate—hundreds of them, then dozens, then the sad little cluster of nine that remained. Her boots knew the path so well she could have walked it asleep, which was fortunate, because part of her very much wanted to be asleep, back in her narrow bed above the archive, with last night having been a fever dream brought on by bad cheese and older paper.

But the date had been inked a hundred years before her birth. Cheese did not do that.

The tree line began where the hop fields gave out—a ragged wall of birch and black pine, their trunks streaked with resin that ran down the bark like slow amber tears. That was the wood's name, of course. Nobody remembered who'd named it. Nobody remembered much of anything before Halvard's time, which Astrid had always filed away under *the way of old places* and never under *suspicious*.

She stopped ten paces from the first tree and dug into her satchel for the thing she'd taken from the archive: a charm-wick, one of the guild's oldest tools, a braided cord that was supposed to kindle with dragon-fire at a word. It hadn't lit in living memory. Inside the valley, nothing of the sort could light. That was rather the point of the valley.

She stood at the boundary and spoke the old word—*vessa*, kindle—and felt foolish immediately, a nineteen-year-old muttering at trees while Marta's goats watched her with the flat judgment only goats can manage.

Nothing happened.

She took three steps closer to the tree line. *Vessa.*

Still nothing. But something else happened instead: a feeling, faint as a draft under a door, like warmth on the back of her neck when the fire is in the next room. She stepped again. The feeling sharpened. She could not have described what it was, only that the air ahead of her was *fuller* than the air behind, the way water is fuller than air, and everything she'd ever been had been shaped by breathing the thin stuff.

One more step. Her toe touched the shadow of the first birch.

The charm-wick caught.

Not with flame—with something before flame, a glow like a coal remembering being a star, and the light ran up the braid and into her palm and Astrid made a sound she would deny until her dying day, something between a laugh and a yelp, and stumbled backward out of the tree's shadow.

The wick guttered and died. The fullness drained away like bathwater. She stood in the ordinary air of the valley, heart slamming, hand still warm, and understood two things with terrible clarity.

First: magic was real, and it lived right there, a stone's throw from the hop fields, held off by nothing she could see.

Second: she had just carried fire in her bare hand, and it had not burned her.

She looked down at her palm. Unmarked. Warm. She closed her fingers around the memory of it the way you'd close your hand around a coal to smuggle it somewhere dark.

Above the tree line, the mountain called Greyfall went up and up into the evening sky, and there—she'd seen it all her life and never once *looked*—a smolder near the summit. A dull red pulse behind a shelf of rock, steady as a banked hearth. The villagers called it the Old Man's Pipe and blamed geology. The guild's oldest maps marked it with a single word: *Serevain.*

Astrid had assumed Serevain was a place. Standing there with a dead charm-wick and a warm hand, she suddenly, absurdly, knew better. Places didn't smolder in rhythm. Rhythms meant breath.

"Oh," she said, to no one, to the goats, to the whole safe small world. "Oh, that's a *dragon*."

* * *

Halvard was in the great hall when she got back, doing what he always did after supper: sitting by the hearth with the guild's account book open on his knees, reading entries he already knew by heart, because the alternative was admitting there was nothing left to count. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, with a beard he trimmed himself and therefore nobody else respected.

"You're late," he said, without looking up. "And you've been to the tree line. You smell of resin."

"You can't smell resin on someone."

"I can smell it on *you*. Sit down, girl, you're hovering like a moth."

She sat. She put the dead charm-wick on the table between them, and watched his face do something complicated—the way a frozen river looks right before the ice gives.

"Where did you get that?" he asked quietly.

"The archive. Third vault. It lit, Halvard." She leaned forward. "At the boundary. One step into the shadow and it lit like it had been waiting a century for permission. And I looked at the mountain, and I know what's up there, and I want you to tell me I'm wrong."

For a long moment the only sound was the hearth ticking through its cooling coals. Then Halvard closed the account book, which he never did, and set both hands flat on its cover, and Astrid saw that they were shaking. Not trembling. Shaking, the way hands shake when they're holding themselves back from something.

"You were always going to find the edge of things," he said. "You were the baby we found at the ward-stone, and I should have known. Children raised on stories either stop believing them or start checking. You checked."

"Tell me about the debt."

His head came up sharply. "Who said—"

"The ledger. Last night. The prophecy ledger with tonight's date in it and my name beside it. You looked at your own hands like they belonged to a stranger, and you said 'it can't be,' and then you lied to me at breakfast about the state of the roof. So." She folded her arms. "Debt. Now. Or I go back to the tree line and keep walking, and we both know I will."

He laughed then, short and hollow, with no humor anywhere in it. "There it is. There's the Emberwright in you. Your grandmother bargained exactly the same way. Frightened half to death and negotiating terms before the fear even lands." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he spoke again, his voice had gone low and rough, like a man reading aloud in church something he'd only ever prayed silently.

"There are things beneath Greyfall, Astrid. Old things. Courtly things, with beautiful manners and appetites you don't want described over supper. Long ago—before the ward, before the valley was safe—they came up the mountain and they took. Souls, mostly. Fire, when they could get it. And the guild, in those days, was strong enough to fight them and proud enough to try, and we lost. Badly. The wood drank our dead; you've seen how the resin runs red-black on the north trunks. That's not sap."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "The ward. The valley. Magic can't cross the tree line—"

"Because it was *paid* not to." He couldn't look at her. "A bargain was struck, a hiding bought, a line drawn that their kind may not cross. And every bargain of that court has a price and a term, and the term—" His hands, flat on the book, pressed down as if holding something shut. "The term comes due soon. Very soon. Tonight's date in that ledger isn't a prophecy, girl. It's an invoice."

"What was the price?"

"That is not yours to carry."

"Halvard."

"*That is not yours to carry.*" It came out harder than he'd ever spoken to her, and then immediately softer, which was worse: "Please. Of everyone in this valley, you are the one thing I still believe I did right. Let me keep one thing."

The hearth ticked. Outside, somewhere down the terraces, Marta's goats complained about the dark, and the sound was so ordinary it hurt.

Astrid stood, picked up the dead charm-wick, and tucked it back into her satchel with careful, deliberate movements, because her hands wanted very badly to shake and she wasn't going to let them.

"Then I'll ask differently," she said. "I'm not asking what you paid. I'm asking what happens when the bill comes due. Because I stood at that tree line tonight and felt the other side of it, Halvard, and it was *hungry*. Whatever's keeping us hidden—it's not a wall. It's a leash. And leashes run both directions."

He looked up at her then, and she watched ninety years of careful silence come apart in his face like wet paper.

"Astrid," he said, and his voice cracked clean through, "there is something in the third vault, behind the false shelf, wrapped in oilcloth. If I don't tell you myself by tomorrow's dusk—if I lose my nerve, which is possible, I am an old coward with excellent posture—then you open it, and you'll understand why the mountain smolders, and why I never let you children play on the north slopes."

"And if I open it tonight?"

"Then God help us both," said Halvard, "because you'll know why I can't let you leave the valley—and why, when the time comes, I won't be able to stop you."

He rose, kissed the top of her head like she was six years old, and walked slowly up the stairs to bed, one hand trailing along the wall the whole way, as though the floor might be listening too.

Astrid sat alone by the dead hearth for a long time. Then she took the striker from her satchel, lit a single candle, and looked toward the dark doorway of the archive corridor, where the third vault waited behind its false shelf with whatever truth Halvard had been carrying like a stone in his chest for longer than she'd been alive.

She blew out the candle.

Some doors, she thought, you should walk toward with your eyes open.
