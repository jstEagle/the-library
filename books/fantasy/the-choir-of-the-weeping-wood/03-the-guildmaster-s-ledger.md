## 3. The Guildmaster's Ledger

Halvard Emberwright had exactly three habits that never varied: he polished the guild's brass door-knocker every morning whether it needed it or not, he took his tea so bitter it could have stripped rust, and he lied about the state of the archive roof.

"The leak is cosmetic," he'd said for eleven years, while Astrid balanced buckets beneath a stain shaped vaguely like a weeping face. "Cosmetic," he repeated now, standing in the doorway of the archive with his arms crossed, watching her haul the last of the water-damaged ledgers into the light.

"Halvard," she said, "the mold has opinions. It's forming a committee."

"Then let it govern. You've been down here since dawn. Come eat something before you turn into one of these books—dry, brittle, and full of other people's arguments."

She smiled at him because it was impossible not to. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a door that had been leaned on by generations, and he had raised her from the age of six with a firmness that was mostly love wearing a stern coat. But she didn't come up. Not yet.

Because under the water-stained ledgers, wedged behind a false panel she'd only found when the shelf bracket snapped and nearly brained her, there was a book that did not belong to any catalog she'd ever made.

It was small. Bound in grey leather gone soft as moth-wing. No title on the spine. And pressed into the cover, so faint she'd thought at first it was just wear, was the guild's sigil: a flame inside an open hand.

The Emberwrights hadn't used that sigil in a hundred years. Not since the Binding, whatever the Binding actually was—the histories in this archive were suspiciously vague about their own founding, which Astrid had always chalked up to bad record-keeping and good intentions.

"Astrid." Halvard's voice had changed. Still gentle. But underneath the gentleness was something taut, like wire drawn to its breaking weight. "Put that down."

She looked at him. He looked at the book.

"You knew it was here," she said.

"I knew where *not* to look." He came into the archive slowly, the way a man walks toward a dog he suspects is injured and liable to bite. "Eleven years I've kept you from that shelf. Eleven years, and a bracket chooses tonight to fail."

"Tonight," she echoed. The word sat between them like a coal. Last night she had shown him the prophecy ledger—*Astrid Ishikawa descends, the flame gutters*—inked in a dead archivist's hand a century before she was born. He had dismissed it as coincidence. His hands had shaken so badly he'd knocked over his tea, and Halvard never spilled anything; spilling was untidy, and Halvard believed untidiness was a moral failing.

He lowered himself onto the work stool with a grunt, older suddenly, as though the stool were a mountain and he a weary pilgrim. "Read it, then. You'll read it whether I say yes or no. You have your grandmother's chin when you've decided something."

"My grandmother's chin?"

"Stubborn. Pointed. Built for butting." He closed his eyes. "Read."

* * *

The journal began without preamble, in a hand she almost didn't recognize—Halvard's, but younger, sharper, written by a man in his thirties who still believed sentences could fix things.

*I have done a terrible thing,* the first entry said, *and I intend to do it properly.*

*Astrid is four years old today. She ate cake frosting off her fingers and fell asleep in my chair, and I sat there holding her and thinking: this child will die old in this valley, safe, never knowing what I paid. That is the whole of my ambition now. Small ambitions are the only honest ones left to me.*

*The court beneath the mountain does not want coin. Coin is nothing to them. They want what burns longest. Veyra asked me for the guild entire—all our souls, strung together like festival lights—and I told her she could have mine instead, and she laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound I have ever heard, because it was kind.*

*"One soul," she said, "for a valley. You Emberwrights always did undervalue yourselves."*

*The ward holds because I hold. When my time comes due, the valley's hiding fails unless another bargain stands in my place. I have bought them—what? Thirty years? Forty? Long enough for Astrid to grow gray and happy and never once wonder why the trees at the tree line stand like soldiers.*

Astrid's hands had stopped moving. She became aware, distantly, that her breath was doing something wrong—too shallow, too fast—and that Halvard was watching her with the expression of a man watching weather he cannot stop.

She turned pages. The entries grew sparser, then stopped for years, then resumed in a hand gone shakier.

*Astrid is nineteen soon. She asks questions about the Binding. Good questions. I give her bad answers and hate myself efficiently, which is a skill, like whittling.*

*The debt comes due within the year. I can feel it the way you feel a storm in a healed bone. I have looked for another way for thirty years. There isn't one. There was never one. I knew that when I signed.*

*What I did not know was how much I would love her. That was the flaw in the plan. Love makes the arithmetic obscene.*

The journal ended there. Below the last line, someone had written something else—not Halvard's hand, older, in ink gone brown:

*The ledger balances or it does not. The wood remembers. — E.W., first of the name*

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The bucket beneath the famous leak went *drip*, patient as a metronome.

"You sold your soul," Astrid said finally. Her voice came out flat and strange, like a coin dropped on stone. "To keep us hidden. To keep *me* hidden. And it comes due—" She couldn't say it. Her mouth refused.

"Tonight," Halvard said. "Or near enough. The court doesn't send invitations; they simply collect. One morning the valley wakes and the tree line is just trees, and everything with wings and teeth remembers we're here." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I meant to tell you when you were thirty. Then forty. There's never a good age to learn your father-figure is collateral."

"You're not my—" The correction died. He was. Of course he was. That was the whole unbearable point.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she asked, and hated how young she sounded. "All those years. All those lessons about honesty and craft and *keeping the embers*—"

"Because telling you wouldn't save me, and it would break you early." He met her eyes, and there was no apology in his face, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "Astrid. Listen to me. This is the part that matters. Do not go down there. Do not go to the mountain, do not go to the court, do not try to bargain. Veyra does not lose bargains. People who meet her come back changed, or they don't come back, and either way the guild loses the one person I spent my soul keeping safe."

"So I'm supposed to just let you be collected. Like a debt. Like a *chattel*."

"Like a guildmaster," he said, with terrible mildness. "It's the job. Somebody has to be the wall."

"And I'm supposed to be what? The person the wall protects? Forever?" She stood, and the journal fell shut in her lap, and she heard herself say things in a voice she barely recognized: "You taught me the fire-keeping rites since I could walk. You told me the Emberwrights *bind* things—that's the whole guild, binding, holding, carrying. And now the one thing worth binding is walking into the dark to be eaten, and I'm supposed to sit here and polish the door-knocker?"

"It's a very nice door-knocker."

"*Halvard.*"

He laughed—a short, cracked sound—and then he wasn't laughing, and for the first time in eleven years she saw him look afraid. Not of dying. She understood that with a lurch. He was afraid of her.

"You have your grandmother's chin," he said quietly, "and your mother's way of looking at a locked door like it owes you money. If I tell you where the mountain path starts, you'll walk it. So I am going to tell you nothing, and lock this archive, and tomorrow we will burn this journal, and you will hate me for a decade, and you will be alive to do it." He rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "That's my final answer as your guildmaster."

"And as the man who raised me?"

He paused at the door. The light through the broken roof caught the grey in his beard, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and every year of the bargain besides.

"As the man who raised you," he said, "I'd tell you the truth. Which is why I can't be that man anymore. Forgive me, little ember. I truly did try to buy you a whole life."

The door closed. The key turned.

Astrid sat alone among the drowned books, the journal heavy in her lap, and felt the shape of her world rearrange itself around a new and awful fact: the safest place she had ever known was a cage, and the bars had been forged out of love, and the lock turned from the outside.

*Drip*, said the bucket.

She looked at the journal. At the sigil on the cover. At the false panel, gaping open like a held breath.

There were other records. There was always another record—she'd spent half her life teaching herself that archives lie by omission, and omissions leave edges, and edges can be followed. Somewhere in this room, or in the resin of the weeping wood itself, was the rest of it: what the court wanted, what the ward ran on, what the first Emberwright had meant by *the wood remembers*.

And beyond the tree line, past the boundary where magic died, the last dragon smoldered on the mountain like a lamp running out of oil.

Halvard had locked the archive door.

He had forgotten, bless his tidy heart, that Astrid Ishikawa had been crawling out of windows since she was seven, and that she already knew exactly where the mountain path began—because he'd told her himself, three winters ago, pointing at the peak over his bitter tea: *"That's the one place in the world you must never go."*

She blew out the lamp and started climbing through the window.
