## 4. Crossing the Tree Line

I did not sleep. I don't suppose Halvard did either, though he'd have sooner eaten his own ledger than admit it.

By the time the sky over the eastern ridge went from slate to pewter to a thin, apologetic silver, I had packed what a sensible person packs for an errand that might kill her: bread, cheese, a flask of cider, the prophecy ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and Halvard's journal, which I had stolen back off his desk while he pretended to doze in his chair. He was not pretending well. His breathing had the careful rhythm of a man listening.

At the door I paused. "Guildmaster," I said, because nineteen years of habit does not break in one night, "if I'm not back by sundown—"

"You'll be back by sundown," he said, without opening his eyes. "You're my apprentice, Astrid Ishikawa, and apprentices are constitutionally incapable of running away. Too much unfinished paperwork."

It was almost funny. It would have been funny, if his hands hadn't been shaking so badly he'd needed both of them to pour his tea the night before, and if we hadn't both known exactly where I was going.

"I love you too," I said, and left before either of us had to survive the silence after it.

* * *

The tree line was a twenty-minute walk from the guildhall if you took the goat path, forty if you took the honest road, and I took the goat path, because whatever I was about to do, I intended to do it like a person sneaking out rather than a person making history. There is a difference. Sneaking out, at least, you can still talk yourself out of.

The Weeping Wood gets its name from the resin that beads along the trunks of the white-barked trees, slow golden tears that harden into amber beads no bigger than a thumbnail. Children collect them. Old women string them into necklaces. I had spent my whole life thinking of them as pretty and nothing more, the way you think of rain, or bread, or anything else that has always simply been there.

The ward itself was invisible. That's the thing nobody tells you about the edge of your whole world: there is no wall, no gate, no sign reading BEYOND THIS POINT, DRAGONS AND OTHER INCONVENIENCES. There is just a place where the grass grows slightly differently, where the birdsong changes key, where the air stops feeling like the inside of a held breath.

I stood at that place for a long time with my toes in the dew and my heart going like a blacksmith's hammer.

"Right," I told the trees. "Nothing personal."

Then I stepped across.

* * *

I want to describe what happened next, and I am going to fail, because our language was built inside the valley, and the valley has no words for this.

Magic did not enter me. That is the closest phrase and it is wrong. Magic *recognized* me. It came up through the soles of my boots and down through the crown of my head and met somewhere behind my sternum, and it said — not in words, but in the way a fire says *warm*, in the way a mother says *home* — *oh. There you are.*

I fell to my knees in the wet grass and laughed until I cried and cried until I laughed, and I could not have told you which was happening at any given moment. Every color was louder. The dawn light came through the leaves in ropes of gold I could nearly hold. Somewhere overhead a lark was singing, and I could hear not just the song but the *shape* of its wanting, the small fierce bright thing of it, and I thought: *this is what they kept from us. This is what Halvard sealed away. Not danger. This.*

And then, underneath the joy, the terror arrived, right on schedule, the way it always does when you realize the floor you've stood on all your life was only ever one room in a much larger house.

Because magic was flooding into me, and I was made of valley-stuff, of ember-tending and ledger-keeping and bread and cider, and I could feel it looking for something in me to burn. My fingertips glowed faintly, like coals under ash. When I pressed my palm flat against the ground, ferns uncurled toward me, quick as breath.

"Stop," I whispered. "Stop, stop—" And it stopped. It gathered itself around me like a dog that has knocked over a table and knows it, trembling with eagerness, waiting to be told again how to be gentle.

That frightened me more than the rest of it. Things that obey you are things you can hurt.

* * *

I walked. I don't know for how long. Time outside the ward moves strangely, or perhaps I moved strangely through it; the sun seemed to change its mind twice about where it should be. But eventually I came to a grove where the white-barked trees grew close and old, their trunks wider than doorways, and every one of them was weeping.

Not the ordinary beading. Rivers of it. Resin ran down the bark in slow golden sheets, catching the light, and the air smelled of honey and smoke and something older, something like the inside of a sealed room.

I touched one of the tears.

The world folded.

I was standing in this same grove, but the trees were young and thin, and there were people here — dozens of them, gaunt, ragged, their clothes scorched, and at their center a dragon the size of a chapel, wings folded, head bowed low, and it was *weeping too*, great molten tears that the people caught in clay pots with shaking hands. A woman with iron-gray braids — I knew her face from the guildhall portraits, though the portraits never showed her looking this exhausted, this *ashamed* — lifted a pot of dragon-tears and spoke words I felt rather than heard: *we did not flee the fire. We took it. Gods forgive us, we took it, and now we must hide what we've become—*

The vision snapped shut like a book.

I sat down hard in the moss. My hands were shaking. Resin clung to my fingers, still warm.

"That's not true," I said aloud, to no one. "We fled. The founding story says—we fled the burning age, we sought sanctuary, the Emberwrights were *refugees*—"

But I had seen the scorch marks on those refugees' own hands. I had seen the way the dragon bowed, not in welcome but in grief. Refugees do not carry away their benefactor's tears in pots.

I touched another tear, further down the trunk, older amber, and the grove folded again—

—Halvard, young, dark-haired, kneeling before a figure I somehow knew without seeing was of the mountain court, saying *yes, take it, take my soul, seal the valley, buy them time, I'll pay when it comes due, only let them have their time—*

—I pulled my hand back so fast I scraped my knuckles on the bark.

So. The journal hadn't lied. The ledger hadn't lied. Only everything else had. The founding, the sanctuary, the whole warm story we told children at hearth-side while the embers settled — a story laid over a theft the way you lay a clean cloth over a wound.

I sat in the weeping grove for a long while, watching the golden tears fall, and I found, to my surprise, that I was not angry. Or not only angry. Underneath the anger was something worse and better: the vertigo of a world grown suddenly, enormously larger than my loyalties. The guild was real. Halvard's sacrifice was real. The people I loved were real. But they were all of them standing on a crime, the way a house stands on its foundations, and you cannot pull out the foundation without asking whether you ever wanted the house at all — or whether you only wanted it because it was the only roof you'd ever known.

A bead of resin fell onto my knee. Without thinking, I cupped it, and it showed me one last thing, brief as a blink:

*A girl with ink-stained fingers, standing at this very tree line at dawn, laughing and crying at once.* Me. An hour ago. The wood had already begun keeping me.

"Oh no," I said, and wiped my eyes, and laughed despite myself, a wet, wobbly sound. "You don't get to keep me. I haven't even decided who I am yet."

The trees, naturally, said nothing. Trees are terrible conversationalists. But the resin kept falling, patient as snowfall, recording everything, and I understood suddenly why the valley's oldest law was *never speak ill within the groves*. The wood remembered. The wood remembered *everything*, and someday someone would come read it all.

Perhaps that someone was supposed to be me.

I got up. I brushed off my skirts. I ate half the bread and drank some cider, because heroism on an empty stomach is just recklessness with better lighting, and then I looked up at the mountain.

Serevain smoldered on the high shoulder of it like a lamp about to gutter — I could see her now, truly see her, a dim red pulse beneath the rock, slower each time it came around. Dying. Due tonight, if the ledger was honest, and ledgers are always honest; that's the whole point of them.

"All right," I told the mountain, in the tone I used for stubborn ovens and stuck archive drawers. "Let's go meet the landlord."

And I started to climb.
