## 5. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The hollow of Serevain was not a cave so much as a throat.

Astrid understood this within the first hundred steps, when the walls began to narrow and warm and glisten, and the air took on a taste she had no name for—something like copper, something like rain on hot stone, something like the smell of a struck match held just a little too close to your fingers. The dragon's hollow breathed. She could feel it breathing around her, slow as a sleeping thing's breath, drawing her inward with each exhale the way a river draws a leaf toward its middle.

She had told herself she would turn back at every landmark. At the first bend. At the pool of black water that reflected nothing. At the place where the stone gave way to something smoother, veined with light like frozen lightning. Each time she stood at the turning point and looked back up the long dark throat toward the gray coin of daylight, and each time she thought of Halvard's hands shaking over the ledger, and of his soul—if that was what it was—hanging somewhere below her in the dark, dimming like a lamp forgotten in an attic.

So she kept walking. It was, she reflected, the only skill the Weeping Wood had ever truly taught her: how to keep walking when everything sensible said stop.

* * *

The descent changed her in small ways she noticed only afterward.

Her shadow stopped behaving. In the wood, magic died at the tree line; here, three days' walk beneath the mountain, it soaked into everything, and Astrid's shadow had begun to lag—a half-second behind her heels, leaning the wrong way against light that came from no direction in particular. When she stopped to stare at it, it stared back with the particular insolence of a cat pretending not to have heard you call its name.

"You're being ridiculous," she told it.

It flicked, once, like a dog shaking off water, and settled.

"Fine," she said. "But if you start waving at things, I'm leaving you here."

There was no one to hear her argue with her own shadow, which was perhaps why she did it so freely. Three days alone in a dragon's throat will make a person chatty. She had named the echo that followed her footsteps (Bertram, because it seemed like an echo's name), apologized aloud to a stalactite she'd nearly walked into, and composed no fewer than four speeches for Veyra, the regent of whom Halvard's journal had written in a hand gone suddenly small and careful, the way people write about things that frighten them: *She is beautiful the way a frost pattern is beautiful. Do not look too long. Do not want anything while she is watching.*

Astrid had read that line eleven times by lantern-light in the guild archive, and she had done what any sensible person does with good advice from a dead man who sold his soul: she'd memorized it and planned to ignore it completely.

* * *

On the fourth day—or what she believed was the fourth day; time down here moved like honey off a cold spoon—the throat opened.

She felt it before she saw it: warmth on her face like standing near a bread oven, and a sound, faint and layered, that took her a long moment to identify. It was singing. Or something adjacent to singing—many voices, very far away, woven together into a single sustained note that never quite resolved. Choral music, she thought, except that choirs breathe. This sound did not breathe. It simply was, continuous as the hum inside a seashell.

Then the passage turned one last time, and Astrid stepped out onto a ledge above the court beneath the mountain, and forgot, for a full minute, how to do anything at all, including breathe.

* * *

Imagine a cathedral built inside a geode.

The cavern was vast beyond reason—its ceiling lost in a haze of glow, its floor a lake of black glass so still it might have been a second sky. From the walls grew crystals the size of ships, faceted and translucent, lit from within by a slow pulse of rose-gold light, and strung between them, hanging in the air at heights both terrible and graceful, were the chandeliers.

They were not made of glass.

Astrid knew this immediately, the way you know a face in a crowd is someone you loved. Each chandelier was a knot of light shaped like a person—not a ghost, not a reflection, but a person rendered entirely in radiance, arms flung wide or folded close, suspended in a lattice of silver chains that ran up into the dark. There were hundreds. Thousands. Each one burned with its own color, its own rhythm, and where their lights mingled they cast colors that had no names in any language Astrid spoke—colors that made her chest ache in a way she suspected was not entirely her own feeling.

The singing was them. All of them. Every soul in the cavern sang the same endless note, and the note was what fed the light in the crystals, and the light was what let the court see itself, and the seeing was—

Astrid pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed.

*Stop*, she told herself. *Look for him.*

She scanned the hanging lights, forcing herself to see them as what they were: people. A farmer-shaped brightness with dirt still worked into the memory of its hands. A child, small and fierce, burning white-gold near the ceiling. An old woman whose light pulsed in the rhythm of a lullaby Astrid almost recognized and then deliberately refused to recognize, because recognizing it would mean admitting she could hear them, and hearing them meant this was real, and real meant—

There. Third tier from the left, hanging lower than most, as though even in captivity he couldn't stop trying to be useful.

Halvard.

His light was gray. Not dim, exactly—dim things still flickered—but grayed, like ash stirred into a fire, like a man seen through frosted glass. His shape hung with its head bowed, and where the other souls' light reached outward, hungry and bright, his drew inward, conserving itself the way he had conserved everything his whole life: carefully, quietly, for someone else's sake later.

"Halvard," Astrid whispered, and her voice fell into the black glass lake and vanished without a ripple.

The gray light did not stir. But the singing—she could have sworn, afterward, that the singing bent, just slightly, around the place where she stood, the way water bends around a stone.

* * *

"You're early."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which should have been frightening but was instead, horribly, pleasant—warm as tea, smooth as a stone worn kind by centuries of water. Astrid turned. Descending the great crystal stair that spiraled up the cavern wall was the regent Veyra, and Astrid discovered, in that moment, that Halvard had undersold her.

He had warned her not to look too long. He had not warned her that looking would feel like coming home.

Veyra was tall in the way that mountains are tall—not a height but a fact. Her skin held the same rose-gold light as the crystals, as though she were lit from within and had simply decided, out of courtesy, to take human form so as not to alarm anyone. Her hair moved without wind. Her eyes were the exact color of the black glass lake, which is to say they reflected whoever looked into them, and Astrid caught herself, twice, admiring her own reflection there before catching what it meant and looking away.

"Do not look too long," she murmured, quoting the journal under her breath. "Do not want anything while she is watching."

Veyra smiled as though Astrid had paid her a compliment. "Oh, I *like* you. You've been reading his little diary." She drifted the last few steps rather than walking, which Astrid chose not to think about. "Did he warn you about me? He always did enjoy warnings. Gave them the way other men give gifts—frequently, and with an air of tremendous self-sacrifice."

"I've come for his soul."

"Yes." Veyra tilted her head, and the light moved across her face like a slow tide. "I know. You announced it to the entire cavern. Very dramatic. The souls appreciated it—they get so few visitors, and half of those are tax collectors." She gestured, and the nearest chandelier—a young man's light, green as new leaves—flared briefly brighter, and its note in the great song climbed a half-step, and Astrid felt the climb in her own throat, a rising she hadn't chosen.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?" Veyra spread her hands, all innocence, all grace. "Sing? They sing whether I ask or not. That's rather the point of them." She began to walk along the ledge, and Astrid found herself falling into step beside her, which she realized too late was exactly what Veyra had intended. "Walk with me. Negotiations go better with movement. Standing negotiations curdle."

Below them the court spread out in its terrible beauty. And it *was* beautiful—that was the trap of it, the rot at the center of the sweetness. Every crystal, every chain, every color had been paid for. Astrid could see the price tags now, hanging in the dark: thousands of persons, burning so that a court could have light.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

"Souls?" Veyra considered. "Four thousand and some. Fewer than there used to be. Your valley's ward eats well—it runs on the same fuel, you know. Did Halvard mention that? He mentioned so little. Such a reserved man. It's why I liked him." She paused at a balustrade of woven silver and looked down at her collection with an expression Astrid could only describe as fond. "You're wondering if I'm a monster. Everyone wonders. It's the first question and it's so boring. I prefer the second question."

"Which is?"

"Which is: *what will you give me?*" Veyra turned, and her eyes did the reflecting thing again, and this time Astrid made herself hold the gaze, made herself see her own face floating there—tired, travel-stained, nineteen years old and three days deep under a mountain—and made herself not flinch. "Because here is my offer, little Emberwright, and I'll make it only once, so attend. Halvard's soul comes due tonight. The debt is called. I can release it to you—hand it over, whole and gray and dimming, yours to carry home. In exchange, you leave me a soul in its place. Any soul. Yours, if you're feeling noble. Someone else's, if you're feeling practical. The court requires balance. One light goes out, another must be hung."

"And if I refuse?"

Veyra's smile widened by a degree that should not have been possible. "Then the debt collects itself. Halvard gutters out at midnight, the ward over your valley fails within the season, and magic comes home to the Weeping Wood like a tide returning—and everything your guild spent two hundred years protecting drowns in it. Including, I should mention, the last dragon, who is rather attached to your valley staying hidden. Serevain has opinions. Dragons generally do."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, each ring wider than the last. Astrid gripped the silver balustrade and felt it hum faintly under her palms, alive, listening.

"There has to be another way," she said. "Something that isn't a soul."

"Oh, undoubtedly." Veyra leaned close, and her breath smelled of snowmelt and something older, sweeter, wrong. "But you haven't earned the other ways yet. Earn them, and we'll talk. For now—" she straightened, and gestured upward, and high above them Halvard's gray light swayed gently on its chains, "—the offer stands until midnight. One soul for one soul. Take your time deciding. Though not too much time; midnight is famously punctual."

And then, before Astrid could speak again, Veyra added—lightly, almost kindly, which was somehow the worst thing yet:

"One more thing, since you'll wonder otherwise. Look closely at his light. Really look. Souls down here can't lie, dear girl. It's the one mercy of the arrangement." She nodded toward the gray flame. "Ask him what he traded the valley's safety *for*. Ask him what else was in the bargain. And then decide how much of what you owe him is loyalty—and how much is pity for a man who's been lying to you since before you could walk."

Astrid looked up at the dimming light that had raised her, taught her, lied to her, and loved her, probably, in whatever way he could afford.

Midnight was coming. And for the first time, she wasn't sure which side of it she wanted to stand on.
