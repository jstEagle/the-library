## 6. A Soul for a Soul

The chandeliers had names.

Astrid understood that now, standing beneath them in the regent's hall, her neck craned back until it ached. Veyra had said it casually, the way Halvard might mention a neighbor's new roof: *That one was a shepherd, two hundred years ago. That one sang. We keep the singers near the windows; they like the light.*

Each soul hung in its glass globe like a lamp of gray fire, and each one flickered when Veyra spoke, as if listening. As if *hoping*.

"You're staring," Veyra said. She poured something amber into two cups and offered one across the table of black glass. "Most visitors stare at me. It's flattering, but predictable."

"I'm staring at people."

"You're staring at light sources." The regent's smile was kind in the way a knife is clean. "But I admire the instinct. Compassion is so rare down here. It spoils quickly."

Astrid didn't take the cup. Her hands were shaking and she wanted them free to do something else—grip the table, run, anything but hold a drink she'd been too polite to refuse. Above her, through miles of stone and the hollow where Serevain smoldered like a banked hearth, the sun would be setting on the last day of the ledger's promise. Midnight was coming, and Halvard's soul hung three globes from the window, dimming by the hour, and she had come all this way with nothing but a guildmaster's guilt and her own stubborn heart.

"Name your price," Astrid said. "For his."

Veyra set down the untouched second cup. For a moment—and this was the thing Astrid would remember later, the thing that kept her up on the climb out—the regent looked genuinely pleased. Not triumphant. *Pleased*, the way a gardener is pleased when the seedling finally comes up.

"Straight to trade. Good. Haggling bores us both." She drifted along the row of globes, trailing one pale finger beneath them without touching. They brightened faintly after her, like dogs after their mistress. "The honest answer is that souls aren't fungible, dear. A shepherd won't burn like a singer. Your guildmaster's flame has a particular... seasoning. Seventy years of guilt, forty of tenderness he never admitted to, and a truly remarkable capacity for self-deception. Do you know what we call that vintage?"

"No."

"*Devotion*," Veyra said, and the word came out warm as bread. "He sold himself to protect you all. Every hour he spent lying to you made the lie brighter. Guilt is fuel, child. Love is fuel. He gave us a soul that burns like a festival night." She turned. "I don't want an equivalent. I want better."

The hall was quiet except for the soft crackle of a hundred small fires. Somewhere above, water dripped through Serevain's hollow, each drop landing with the patience of centuries.

"Me," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected, which felt like a betrayal of how terrified she was. "You want me."

"I want what you'll become." Veyra crossed back to her, close enough that Astrid could see the regent's eyes held no color at all, just depth, like wells. "Nineteen years raised outside magic. A soul that magic has never touched, never shaped, never *fed on*. Do you understand how rare you are? Everyone else who walks into my court arrives already half-spent. You arrive whole." She reached out and, without asking, tucked a strand of hair behind Astrid's ear—a gesture so grandmotherly it made Astrid's skin crawl worse than any threat could have. "One soul, freely given, for one soul freely released. His light goes home tonight. Yours comes off the shelf in, oh, sixty years? Seventy? You'd live your whole life first. Marry. Grow old. And then, instead of whatever thin guttering end waits for you up there, you'd shine here forever. In beautiful glass. Where everyone can see you."

It was a good offer. That was the horror of it. Astrid stood there doing arithmetic she hated: seventy years against eternity, a whole life traded for one old man's freedom, and somewhere under the fear a treacherous little voice whispering that it was *fair*, that Halvard had spent his whole life paying for her people and here at last was a bill she could cover herself.

"Say it properly," Astrid managed. "The terms. All of them."

"Freely given, freely taken. No compulsion. The oldest law beneath the mountain." Veyra spread her hands. "Ask me anything. Bargain hard. I respect it."

So Astrid asked. She asked about the release—would Halvard return to his body, which she now understood sat empty in the valley, breathing out of habit? Yes. Would he remember? Everything. Would he be *himself*?

And here Veyra paused, just a fraction, and the hundred globes flickered all together, like a crowd holding its breath.

"He will be everything he was," the regent said carefully, "minus what the burning consumes. Souls are fuel, dear. I told you. Some of him has already gone to light and heat. What returns will be... the remainder."

"How much remainder?"

"A generous portion."

"That's not a number."

"It's not a number I'm required to give." Veyra's smile returned, softer now, almost apologetic, and Astrid felt the floor of the bargain tilt beneath her. "You're learning. Most don't ask until it's too late. Shall we say midnight? You have until midnight to decide, and I do encourage you to take every minute—"

"No," Astrid said.

Veyra tilted her head.

"I mean no, I don't need until midnight. I need you to answer one question first, and I need you to answer it *truly*, because you said souls down here can't lie, and I'm hoping that rule applies to regents." Astrid's heart was slamming against her ribs, but she made herself look up—not at Veyra, at the globes. At the shepherd. At the singer near the window. At the ones that flickered when she spoke. "When my guildmaster's soul goes free. When it leaves this hall. What happens to it?"

"What a strange question."

"It's the only question."

Veyra was silent for three full seconds. Astrid counted them by the drip of water far above.

"The soul departs," Veyra said at last, "and returns to its body, and lives out its natural span, diminished but intact—" and as she said it, every globe in the hall flared white, every single one, a hundred small fires crying out at once like a struck bell, and the sound went through Astrid's chest and came out the other side as understanding.

They couldn't lie either. But they could *testify*.

"You can't release them," Astrid whispered. "Any of them. Ever. That's not the arrangement. That's not the *price*. The court eats them. Slowly. Forever. There is no 'released.' There's only 'not yet burned.'"

Something moved behind Veyra's colorless eyes—annoyance, maybe, or appetite, or both wearing the same face. "The globes are old," she said lightly. "Old glass sings at odd moments."

"They sang *no*."

"They sang *careful*, child. There's a difference." The warmth drained out of the regent's voice, and what remained was older than the hall, older than the mountain, patient as pressure. "Here is truth, since you've developed a taste for it. Yes. Nothing leaves. Nothing has ever left. The bargain I offer is real in every word I speak and false in every word I don't. Your guildmaster knew that when he signed. He signed anyway, because the alternative was watching your valley die, and he decided his soul was worth the spending. Noble men make excellent candles." She leaned in, and her breath smelled of cold iron. "So. The question is not whether I keep my word. The question is whether you love him enough to feed him to me slowly, or whether you'd rather watch him gutter out honestly. Choose. Midnight is coming either way."

Astrid stood very still.

She thought about running. She thought about offering more, less, anything, some third door that surely existed because there was always a third door—wasn't there? Wasn't that what stories promised? But the globes hung silent around her now, a hundred spent souls pretending to be lamps, and she understood suddenly that she was looking at her own future in every one of them: bright, obedient, *kept*.

Despair came first. It arrived whole, like a wave, and she let it pass through her because fighting it would only give it somewhere to stand.

And then, underneath the despair, something else. Small and furious and familiar—the same thing that had made a nineteen-year-old archivist argue with a century-old ledger. If there was no third door, then she would make one. If the court fed on souls, then the court could be *starved*. Fire was fuel, Veyra had said. Fine. Astrid had grown up tending embers. She knew what happened to a fire when you took away its air.

She just needed something that burned hotter than souls.

"I'll decide by midnight," Astrid said, and heard her own voice go quiet and level, a stoker's voice, a keeper-of-embers voice. "But I'm done bargaining in this room. Show me the way up. I want to visit the dragon before I sign anything."

Veyra's smile froze by a degree. "Serevain is dying. There's nothing left to trade there."

"Then there's nothing to lose," Astrid said, "and I've noticed that's when I think best."

* * *

Tomas found the note pinned to the archive door with his own belt-knife, which meant Astrid had wanted him angry enough to read it fast.

*Tomas — Gone below. Don't follow. Watch the tree line at dawn tomorrow; if I'm not back, tell them the truth, all of it, even the parts about Halvard. Especially those. — A.*

He read it four times. Then he read the pages scattered across her desk—the ledger with the impossible date, Halvard's journal open to the entry that had made Astrid's handwriting shake, the sketch she'd drawn of the mountain with a route marked in charcoal that stopped, insultingly, at the tree line.

"Don't follow," Tomas said aloud, to the empty archive. "Right."

The trouble with growing up with someone was that you learned exactly which of their instructions were load-bearing. *Don't touch the kiln* had been real. *Don't tell Marta her singing was bad* had been real. *Don't follow*—from Astrid Ishikawa, who had once walked into a collapsing ember-shed to save a cat that turned out to be imaginary—was a request for company, badly worded.

He packed in twenty minutes: rope, bread, the good lantern, Halvard's journal because Astrid had marked three pages and he intended to find out why, and the whetstone, because the knife was coming home dull if the mountain had anything to say about it.

At the tree line he stopped, the way you stop at the edge of cold water. The ward hummed there, invisible, the valley's whole soft lie pressed flat against the evening air. Beyond it the wood began—the true wood, the weeping wood—and beyond that, the mountain, where a dying dragon glowed like the last coal in a grate.

"Watch the tree line at dawn," he muttered. "If I'm not back, tell them the truth."

He stepped through.

Magic hit him like the river in spring. It wasn't gentle—it got into his teeth, his blood, the backs of his eyes, and the world *opened*, colors deepening, sounds arriving from too far away, the trees leaning toward him with what he swore was curiosity. He staggered, laughed, gagged, laughed again. Somewhere overhead a bird sang a note that went on too long and harmonized with itself.

"Okay," he told the wood, gripping a trunk that was warm and faintly trembling, like a flank of a living thing. "Okay. You're real. Noted."

The trunk wept a bead of resin onto his hand, and the resin showed him a flash of something—fire, wings, a circle of hooded figures taking rather than asking—and Tomas stood very still in the darkening wood, holding a memory that wasn't his, feeling the ground shift under everything he'd ever believed.

Above him, the mountain glowed. Below him, somewhere in the dark, Astrid was walking into a court that ate souls.

"Shouldn't have followed," he told himself, and kept walking.
