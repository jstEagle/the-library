## 7. The Founding Lie

Tomas had always been the one who stayed.

It was a joke between him and Astrid, mostly. She was the one who climbed the archive ladders and argued with Halvard about cataloguing systems; he was the one who fixed the ladder afterward and made sure there was bread in the oven when the arguing got loud. "You're the roots," she'd told him once, when they were fourteen and she'd just been caught reading the sealed ledgers for the third time. "I'm the part that keeps trying to fly off."

He'd liked being the roots. Roots didn't need prophecies. Roots needed patience, good soil, and the ability to hold on while everything above them did something dramatic.

So when Astrid vanished through the tree line at dawn—leaving behind a note that said *Don't follow me* in handwriting that shook so badly the words nearly fell apart—Tomas read it twice, folded it neatly, put it in his pocket, and began packing.

"Don't follow me" from Astrid Ishikawa, he reasoned, had always meant *follow me, but be sensible about it.* She simply lacked the imagination to see how those two instructions could coexist. He did not lack that imagination. He packed bread, a knife, the flint she always forgot, and—for reasons he couldn't fully explain—the guild's oldest ledger, the one Halvard kept locked and which Astrid had left open on her desk like an apology.

The lock had been broken. That was new. That was very new.

* * *

The tree line was worse than he'd imagined and stranger than he'd feared.

He'd grown up on stories of what happened to magic at the valley's edge: how it pooled against the ward like rain against glass, how anything touched by it withered or burned or simply stopped. The Emberwrights' whole history lived inside that story. *We fled the burning world,* the children's lessons said. *We built a place where fire could sleep.*

What the stories hadn't mentioned was the sound.

Tomas heard it before he saw the boundary—a low, continuous hum, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. And when he stepped out of the trees into the last meadow before the wood proper, he saw where the sound came from: the resin.

The weeping wood bled. Everyone knew that. Amber tears slid down the great trunks in slow golden ropes, and the guild harvested them for sealant and lamp-fuel and, if you were a child with more courage than sense, chewing. What nobody had told him—what perhaps nobody knew—was that the resin wept *downhill*. Away from the valley. Toward the mountain.

And every drop of it glowed faintly as it went, like embers being carried somewhere on a long errand.

"That's not right," Tomas said aloud, because saying things aloud helped him think, and because there was no one there to tell him to hush. "That's not right at all."

Ward-water wasn't supposed to move. Ward-water was supposed to sit at the tree line like a wall and be boring. This wasn't a wall. It was a *drain*.

He crouched at the base of the nearest tree and looked properly, the way Astrid looked at things—close, unkindly, without letting the story do the looking for her. The trunk was scarred with old channels, grooves cut deliberately into the bark, generations ago by hands that had wanted the resin to flow in exactly this direction. Cut grooves. In a wild wood. In a wood the guild had supposedly never managed, only mourned.

Someone had plumbed this forest like a cistern.

Tomas sat down hard in the moss and ate half his bread without tasting it.

* * *

By midday he'd found the first memory.

He hadn't gone looking for it. He'd leaned against a trunk to rest, and the resin—warm, sticky, faintly singing—had stuck to his palm, and then the world had tilted sideways and he'd been standing in a courtyard he didn't recognize, watching a woman he didn't recognize argue with a dragon.

Not a vision. Not quite. More like reading a letter written in light. The resin held what the wood had witnessed, and the wood had witnessed a great deal, and apparently all you needed to read it was a bare hand and terrible luck.

The woman wore the grey-and-ember sash of the first Emberwrights—the founding generation, the ones whose portraits hung in the great hall with their faces worn smooth by reverent dust. Her name, when another figure addressed her, was Maren. Maren Ashvael. First Guildmother. The one whose statue stood by the well with a lantern raised, welcoming refugees out of the burning world.

In the memory, Maren was not welcoming anyone anywhere.

"You promised us passage," she was saying to the dragon, and her voice had a crack in it that no portrait had ever shown. "Passage, not plunder."

The dragon was vast and old and tired in a way that made Serevain's smolder on the mountain look almost lively. Its scales were the color of cooling iron. When it spoke, the whole memory shuddered.

"I promised you *shelter*, little thief. I did not promise you my heart's blood to line your walls with."

"We have children starving in the passes." Maren's jaw was set. "The war took everything. You hoard fire while people freeze."

"And so you will take it instead. From my sleeping kin. While I am bound here by your clever little oath." The dragon's eye, huge as a barn door, fixed on her. "Know this, Maren Ashvael. Fire taken is not fire given. It remembers. It will want back what you stole, and it will collect through whatever door you build to keep it."

Maren turned away. And Tomas, watching from inside a century-old tear of amber, watched her give the order anyway.

They came at night with cold-iron chains and oaths of silence. They took the sleeping dragons' flame the way you'd take honey from a hive you'd smoked into stillness—not one dragon woke, because the first Emberwrights had spent a year learning how to make sure nothing would wake. And they built the valley's ward out of what they carried home, weaving stolen fire into the tree line, and they called it sanctuary.

They called it *refuge*.

Tomas came back to himself on his knees in the moss, his hand bleeding slightly where he'd gripped the bark, and the word *sanctuary* sat in his mouth like a stone.

* * *

He should have gone home. He knew that even as he climbed higher instead, following the resin channels up-slope, following Astrid's trail—her boot prints in the soft ground, unmistakable, going exactly where he'd told himself not to go. Every sensible root in his body said *turn around, bring this to Halvard, let the guild decide together.*

But the guild couldn't decide together anymore. That was the thing the memory had broken. He'd spent nineteen years loving a story, and the story was a confession written in someone else's blood, and Halvard had known—Halvard must have known—and Halvard had shaken hands over tonight's date in a ledger and said *old ink, girl, nothing more* with trembling fingers.

Loyalty, it turned out, could be a locked room. He'd been proud of holding the key.

Near dusk he found the second memory, and this one was worse, because it was recent enough that he recognized the voices.

Halvard. Young Halvard, forty years younger, standing in this same wood with a man Tomas didn't know—a courtier, by the dress, all silver thread and too many buttons—and the conversation was quiet, businesslike, obscene.

"The ward runs thin," young Halvard said. "The stolen fire gutters. You know what happens if it fails."

"The mountain knows," the courtier agreed pleasantly. "Which is why the court offers renewal. A sealing, fresh and strong, for the usual consideration."

"Name it."

"A soul. Freely offered. Yours, I think—we find guildmaster souls particularly nourishing. All that duty. All that *devotion*." The courtier smiled the way frost smiles on a window. "And one small clause, for the court's amusement. The souls already woven into your pretty wall stay woven. We keep what we keep. Your founders' theft becomes our pantry, and your valley's safety becomes our leash, and everyone goes on calling it a miracle."

Young Halvard was silent a long time. Long enough that the memory's edges flickered.

"They're alive in there," he said finally. "The souls in the ward. My grandmother's oath-brothers. The ones who wove it."

"Alive," the courtier agreed, "the way a candle is alive. Do sign here, guildmaster. The ink is traditional."

And Halvard signed.

Tomas sat with his back against the weeping tree until full dark, and the wood wept its glowing tears down the cut channels toward the mountain, and he understood at last what he was looking at. Not a ward. A *pipeline*. The valley's safety wasn't a miracle or even a crime committed once, long ago—it was a crime renewed every generation, paid for in souls, feeding the court beneath the mountain drop by drop, century by century. The Emberwrights hadn't fled the burning world.

They'd robbed it, moved in, and set up a subscription.

He laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and he pressed his sleeve to his mouth until it stopped. Above him, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, Serevain's glow pulsed once—weak, guttering, a lamp running dry—and beneath his feet the resin flowed steadily downward, carrying its stolen light to pay a debt his guild had been pretending wasn't a debt since before his grandfather's grandfather.

Astrid had gone up there. Into that. Alone, with a note that shook.

Tomas got up. His knees ached. His whole chest ached, in the specific way of a man whose foundations have been inspected and found hollow—but here was the strange thing, the thing he'd think about later with something close to wonder: under the ache, under the disillusionment, something else had lit. Because if the loyalty was built on a lie, then it had never actually been tested. And if it had never been tested, then it wasn't gone. It was just waiting for something true to attach itself to.

Maybe the truth was ugly. Maybe belonging could be rebuilt on uglier, better bones. He was willing to find out. He was, it turned out, exactly the kind of person who would climb a haunted mountain to find out, which would have surprised everyone who'd ever called him the roots.

He checked Astrid's boot prints one last time, now climbing steeply toward the smoking hollow where the last dragon smoldered, and followed them up into the dark.

Halfway up, the ground began to tremble—softly at first, then less softly—and far below, down in the glass-lit dark beneath the mountain, something that had hung quietly for decades gave a single, brilliant flare, like a chandelier remembering it was once a soul.

Tomas ran.
