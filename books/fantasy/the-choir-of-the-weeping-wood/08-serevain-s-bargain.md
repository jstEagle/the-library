## 8. Serevain's Bargain

The mountain path did not want her climbing it.

Astrid felt that plainly, the way she'd once felt a kettle about to boil over—something in the stone itself leaning against her, patient and enormous, like a hand pressing down on her shoulders without any malice at all. Just gravity with opinions.

She climbed anyway. Her boots, which Halvard had resoled for her every autumn since she was nine, found holds in the black glass of the mountainside, and the higher she went, the warmer the stone grew, until she could feel it through her soles like a hearth floor. Above her, wreathed in cloud, the dragon smoldered.

*Smoldered* was the only honest word for it. From the valley she had always imagined Serevain as a bonfire on the peak, a beacon. Up close, the truth was smaller and sadder: a dull red glow in a hollow of the rock, breathing. In. Out. In. Out. Each breath a little fainter than the last, like a man falling asleep in a chair he'd sworn he wasn't tired in.

"You are the one from the ledger," said a voice like a banked fire settling.

Astrid's heart performed a maneuver she had not authorized. "You can see me?"

"I can see warmth," said the dragon. "You are the only warm thing on this mountain that isn't stone."

The hollow opened before her, and Astrid stepped into it, and for a long moment she simply stood there and let herself be small. Serevain was vast—vaster than the guildhall, vaster than the valley's whole eastern ridge, she thought, though it was hard to measure a creature who seemed to be made partly of shadow and partly of the ember-glow leaking through the cracks of her own hide. Her scales were the color of cooling iron. Her eyes were old lamps, amber going gray at the edges.

"Hello," Astrid said, because her mother—her memory of a mother, the one warm thread she had left of before—had taught her that you greet enormous things politely. "I'm Astrid Ishikawa. I'm told you've been waiting for me for a hundred years."

"A hundred years, four months, and eleven days," said Serevain. "I counted. There is very little else to do when one is dying."

* * *

Astrid sat down on a shelf of warm stone, because her legs had informed her they were finished with the day, and the dragon told her the story the way old people tell stories—sideways, with digressions, with long pauses in which the only sound was the wind worrying at the mouth of the hollow.

She told her about the Emberwrights' founding, and it was not the founding Astrid had been raised on. Not a guild of refugees fleeing magic, sanctifying a valley where it could not follow. A guild of thieves. A guild that had stolen fire from a sleeping dragon and bound it into souls, and when the souls began to burn out, had stolen more, and when the mountain court came to collect, had bought their hiding place with a guildmaster's soul and the last of a dragon's freedom.

"I was not imprisoned by them," Serevain said. "I made a bargain. My flame for their silence. They would tend me while I dimmed, and I would be the anchor of their little ward, and when the flame guttered, the debt would come due." Her breath rattled like a draft through a cold chimney. "I have dimmed for a hundred years, Astrid Ishikawa. I am nearly finished."

"Then the valley—"

"Falls. The ward unravels. The court beneath walks out into the world with its chandeliers, and everything your guildmaster bought with his soul is spent." The great head turned, and one amber eye regarded her with something that might, in a smaller creature, have been kindness. "Unless."

"Unless," Astrid repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "The prophecy. My name in the ledger. Tonight's date."

"The prophecy was mine," said Serevain. "I wrote it a century ago, in the only ink I had left, which was fire, into the only paper I trusted, which was the resin of the weeping wood. Your guildmasters have been reading their own doom for a hundred years and calling it scripture." A sound came out of her that took Astrid a moment to recognize as laughter—dry, crackling, like a green log finally catching. "They thought it foretold a savior. It only foretold a *carrier*."

"Carrier of what?"

"Of me," said Serevain simply. "Of the last of me. Listen, child, and understand, because I will only have the strength to say this once. Fire cannot live in a vessel that has magic in it. Magic and dragon-fire are cousins who hate each other; put them in one soul and the soul tears like wet paper. Everyone born inside your valley's ward was born steeped in its residue. Every Emberwright, every farmer, every child. Their souls are already dyed through." The amber eye brightened, just slightly, with something like hope, which was more terrible than any threat. "But you—you crossed the tree line four days ago, and the magic poured into you, and you did not tear. Do you know why?"

Astrid shook her head. Her hands had gone cold despite the warm stone.

"Because you were raised in a valley where magic *cannot enter*. Nineteen years of nothing. A blank page, Astrid Ishikawa. The only one in the world. The magic that flooded you at the boundary sat on the surface of you like rain on glass, and it has already run off, and what remains underneath is clean." The dragon's voice dropped, and the hollow grew very quiet. "You can carry my fire. No one else alive can. That is the whole of the prophecy. That is why your name is in a hundred-year-old ledger beside tonight's date. Not because you are chosen. Because you are *empty*."

Astrid thought she should feel insulted. What she felt instead was a strange, ringing clarity—the same feeling she'd had at the tree line, when the world had gotten suddenly, vertiginously larger than her loyalties.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I just walk back down and let it all fall?"

"Then it falls," said Serevain, without judgment, which somehow made it worse. "The court comes out. The valley ends. And I finally sleep. I confess the last part does not displease me. I have been dying for a very long time, child, and dying slowly is not living. It is only dying, drawn out like bad bread."

* * *

Astrid was quiet for a while. The wind came and went. Somewhere far below, she imagined she could hear the valley—the smithy's evening hammer, Tomas arguing with someone about fence posts, the particular creak of the guildhall door that everyone complained about and no one fixed. Nineteen years of small sounds. She had never once thought of them as precious. That was the thing about a safe world, she was learning: it doesn't tell you it's safe. It just lets you be bored in it.

"Say the offer," she said at last. "All of it. Halvard always said a bargain with an incomplete price is the only kind that ruins you."

Serevain's breath went out long and slow, and the glow of her dimmed, and brightened, and dimmed again.

"The offer is this," the dragon said. "I give you my flame. All of it, the last of it, poured into you, where it will live as long as you live. With it you can go down beneath the mountain and break Veyra's court, free the souls she hangs in her dark, reclaim your guildmaster, and carry an honest fire home to your valley—one that asks no stolen soul to feed it. The guild endures. The ward holds, rebuilt on something given freely instead of something taken. Everything your people believe about themselves can remain true, or can become true, which is nearly as good."

"And the price."

"The price," said Serevain, "is that fire does not share a vessel with anything. It will burn away everything in you that is not itself. Your magic is already gone—it ran off you like rain. But your *memories*, Astrid Ishikawa. Your memories of the valley are soaked in it, in the ward's residue, in nineteen years of the place that magic cannot touch. The fire will consume them. All of them." The amber eyes held hers, and did not look away, and did not soften. "You will carry the flame home to people you will not know, to a valley you will not remember, having paid a price you will not remember paying. You will read your own name in the ledger like a stranger's. You may even read this very night, someday, in someone else's telling, and feel nothing."

The words went into Astrid like a splinter going in deep—no pain at first, just the awful knowledge of it.

"That's not a price," she said. Her voice came out strange. "That's everything. That's my mother's voice. That's the guildhall door that creaks. That's—" She stopped, because the list was endless, and every item on it was small, and the smallness was the whole point. "You're asking me to save a home by forgetting it."

"I am asking nothing," said Serevain. "That is the difference between me and the court below. Veyra will offer you thrones and half-truths and call it generosity. I offer you the whole truth and wait." The great head lowered until the ember-glow of her breath washed warm over Astrid's face. "You may refuse. I will not think less of you. I have watched your guild for a hundred years, child—watched them lie to themselves to stay kind to each other, watched them call a cage a sanctuary because the cage was comfortable. You are the first of them to climb this mountain. Whatever you choose, you are already more than they were."

And that—*that*—was the cruelest thing anyone had said to her in days, because it was a gift, and gifts could not be refused without cost either.

Astrid stood. Her legs had recovered, or decided that trembling was a luxury. She looked out past the dragon's shoulder, down the black glass of the mountain, to where the valley must be—invisible from here, hidden even from the creature who had anchored its hiding for a century. Somewhere down there, Halvard's soul hung in a chandelier, dimming. Somewhere down there, Tomas was probably doing something reckless on her behalf, because that was what Tomas did instead of crying.

She thought about her mother's voice. She could not remember when she had last heard it, and the not-remembering was a small rehearsal of the enormous not-remembering to come, and she found, to her surprise, that it did not stop her. It only made her hold the thought of it, the *idea* of it, the way you cup a match in wind.

"If I do this," she said, "will it hurt?"

"Yes," said Serevain.

"Will I scream?"

"Probably."

"Good," said Astrid Ishikawa, nineteen years old, raised in a valley where nothing ever happened, and discovered that she meant it. "Then I'll know it took."

She stepped forward into the ember-light, and held out her hands.

"Wait," said the dragon, and there was something in the old voice that Astrid would spend the rest of her life—whatever remained of it—trying to name. Gratitude, perhaps. Or fear. "Before I pour it into you, you should know one thing more. The fire will keep one memory. Fire is not cruel; it is only hungry, and even hunger spares the thing that feeds it. It will keep the memory of *this*—of the choice, of the mountain, of the moment you said yes. Everything else goes. But you will always know what you paid, even if you never know what for."

Astrid thought about that. A single coal in all that ash.

"Then let's begin," she said, "while I still remember why."

And Serevain, last dragon of the weeping wood, opened her mouth, and the light that came out of her was not red at all.
