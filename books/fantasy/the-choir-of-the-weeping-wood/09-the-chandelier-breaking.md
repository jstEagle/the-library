## 9. The Chandelier Breaking

**Astrid**

The fire sat in my chest like a second heart.

Serevain had warned me it would hurt. She had not warned me it would *sing*. Every step down the mountain's throat, the flame hummed against my ribs, a note too low for ears and too high for bones to ignore. My hands glowed at the edges, translucent as lantern paper. I could see the veins of my wrists like rivers of amber, and I thought—giddily, absurdly—that I finally understood what Tomas meant when he said I talked too much even when I was scared. The fire was talking too. It never stopped talking.

The court opened before me the way a wound opens: all at once, and wet with light.

I had seen it once from the outside, glass-lit and lovely, Veyra's chandeliers hanging in their hundreds through a cavern tall as a cathedral. Now I walked into it carrying the one thing this place had been starving for since before my grandmother was born. The souls in their crystal cages turned toward me like flowers toward a window. Halvard's hung lowest, nearest the floor, dim as a coal banked under ash. Nineteen years of guildmaster, and they'd hung him where the dust collected.

Veyra rose from her throne of black glass, and her beauty hit me like weather. Silver hair unbound, falling past her waist; a gown that moved like smoke deciding to be silk. Her voice was honey poured over broken glass.

"You brought it," she said. "Clever little ember-keeper. Do you know how long we have waited for fire to walk in here on its own legs?"

"I know you can't take it," I said. "Serevain told me. It only burns if I let go."

"Everything burns if you let go." She drifted closer, and the cold came off her like the cold off a lake at night. "But you misunderstand your position, Astrid Ishikawa. You think you've come to bargain. You haven't. You've come home."

* * *

**Tomas**

Above, the world was ending politely.

I had followed her trail for two days—the resin-marks she left on the trees, each one a small pressed memory, each one telling me more than I wanted to know. By the time I reached Serevain's hollow, my hands were shaking so badly I dropped my pack twice, and the dragon watched me with one eye the size of a wagon wheel and said, with what I swear was amusement, "You're the friend."

"I'm the friend," I agreed.

"She talks about you. Constantly. Even asleep."

"That tracks."

Serevain gave me the truth the way she gave everything: plainly, and with interest accruing. Astrid carried the last flame. The court below would try to keep it. If the flame was freed—if the chandeliers broke—the mountain would convulse as a century of stolen power came undone, and the way out would close like a fist.

"Somebody has to hold it open," the dragon said. "From inside. Where the stone listens."

"And if nobody does?"

"Then she dies in the dark with a dead dragon's fire in her chest, and honestly, Tomas, I expected better of the guild's bookkeeping."

So here I was, standing at the mouth of the descent while the mountain began, very quietly, to shiver. Not earthquake-shivering. *Waking* shivering. The walls sweated glass. Somewhere far below, I felt—I don't know how else to say it—a note being held. A long, stubborn, Astrid-shaped note.

"Hold the door," I told the empty air, because it helped to say it out loud. "Hold the stupid door."

I put both palms against the rock and pushed my whole self into the asking. No magic. I didn't have any. Just stubbornness, which in the Weeping Wood we'd always treated as a renewable resource.

* * *

**Astrid**

"You could have it all," Veyra said, and the terrible thing was that she made it sound reasonable. That was her gift, I think. Not the beauty. The *reasonableness*. "Halvard's seat is empty by midnight whether you act or not. A soul owed is a soul owed. But a throne—" she spread her hands, and the chandeliers brightened around her like an audience leaning in, "—a throne is yours by conquest. You walked in carrying fire. Fire is kingship here. Take his place. Keep the valley sealed. Keep your people safe. Isn't that what he bought? You'd only be renewing the subscription."

She smiled. "And you'd remember everything. Your memories stay yours, up here on the throne. All of them. Halvard's face. The archive dust. The boy who follows you around leaving resin-notes like a lovesick pigeon."

That landed. I won't pretend it didn't. I thought of the ledger with tonight's date inked a century ago, of Halvard's shaking hands, of Tomas's terrible jokes in the archive stacks, and Veyra watched me think it and purred.

"There she is," she said softly. "Every soul has a price. Yours is just *belonging*."

"No," I said.

Her smile flickered. "No?"

"No. Because belonging isn't a price, it's a practice. And because you just told me the truth by accident: Halvard's seat is empty by midnight either way. He doesn't get freed if I sit down. Nobody gets freed if I sit down. You just want the fire fed a new keeper so the chandeliers burn another century." I looked up at them—all those little lights, all those people, dimming one by one like a city forgetting itself. "You don't want a regent. You want a warden."

"I want *survival*," Veyra said, and the honey went out of her voice all at once, and what was underneath was old and hungry and terribly tired. "Do you know what we were before the guild stole our fire? Before your precious Emberwrights bound us beneath this rock? We were *guests*. We were worshipped. And then your founders needed a ward for their coward's valley, and they took—"

"I know what they took," I said. "I read the resin. I know about the founding lie. I climbed out of it two days ago."

Something moved behind her face—not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone meeting the one person in the world who cannot be lied to anymore.

"Then you know," she said, quieter, "that if the chandeliers break, the ward breaks. Your valley opens. Everything your guildmaster sold himself to prevent—"

"Yes," I said. "They'll have to live with the truth. It's what the rest of us do."

I let go.

* * *

**Tomas**

Later—and I will be telling this story badly for the rest of my life, so allow me to tell it badly now—later, the mountain screamed.

Not the rock. The *court*. I heard it through forty fathoms of stone, a sound like a hundred years of held breath releasing, and the passage around me lit gold from below, and the walls stopped sweating glass and started weeping something warmer. My palms burned against the rock. I couldn't tell if I was holding the way open or if the way was holding *me* open, propped like a doorstop, and there was a long moment where I genuinely considered the possibility that I would spend eternity as architecture.

"Worth it," I told the rock. "Probably. Ask me again in a minute."

The light below went from gold to white. Then the sound came up the shaft—not screaming anymore but *singing*, dozens of voices, then hundreds, souls coming loose after a century in their cages, and I pressed harder against the stone and laughed, actually laughed, because it was the most beautiful horrible sound I had ever heard and it was getting louder and the mountain was shaking apart around it and somewhere in all of that was Astrid, walking up toward me through the ruin of a kingdom, glowing like a struck match.

* * *

**Astrid**

Breaking a chandelier, it turns out, is easy. They want to break. A century of caged light holds its breath waiting for permission.

I raised my hands and the fire left me in a single great exhale, and it did not burn me, because I did not let go of myself—only of the holding-back. The flame went through the cavern like dawn through fog, touching each crystal cage, and the cages cracked, and the souls came out.

Oh, they came out.

They rose off their hooks like sparks off a bonfire, and every one of them was a person, is the thing nobody tells you. Not lights. *People.* A woman with flour still ghosting her hands. A boy chasing something only he could see. An old man who paused beside me on his way up and touched my burning hair, gently, the way you'd touch a grandchild, and said, "Thank you, little keeper," in a language I somehow understood anyway.

Halvard came down instead of up.

His soul was thin, guttered, worn to a whisper—he'd been down here longer than anyone, paying interest on a debt of love. He gathered himself in front of me, barely brighter than a candle-end, and I felt him trying to speak and failing, and trying again.

"Don't," I said. My eyes were doing something embarrassing. "Don't apologize. Don't explain. I read the journal. I know why."

He managed it anyway, of course he did, because he was Halvard and halting speeches were his native tongue. *Forgive the guild,* he said. *Not me. The guild. It was built on a theft and kept alive with lies, and you are the only one who ever—*

"You're free," I said. "Go be free. Argue with me on the other side if there is one."

He laughed—that dry, surprised laugh I knew from a hundred archive evenings—and rose, and the last thing he did was turn once in the air, the way he always turned back at the doorway of any room he left, checking on me. Checking on me. Even as a soul. Even now.

Behind me, Veyra stood in the wreckage of her court, and she was not fighting. That frightened me more than fighting would have. She watched her chandeliers empty out one by one, and her beauty stayed perfectly intact, and that was the horror of it: nothing about her dimmed.

"You'll open the valley," she said. "And in fifty years, some frightened guild will seal another valley, and steal another fire, and someone will come down here and make my bargain all over again. You haven't ended anything, ember-keeper. You've just taken your turn."

"Maybe," I said. "But I'll be honest about it. That's the whole difference. That's the entire job."

I turned my back on the throne of black glass and ran for the shaft, and the mountain convulsed around me, and above—impossibly, wonderfully—I saw two hands pressed flat against the falling dark, holding it open, and heard a voice I knew shouting down:

"ASTRID! WALK FASTER! I AM NOT LOAD-BEARING!"

"TOMAS!"

"LESS TALKING! MORE CLIMBING! OH WAIT, THAT'S RICH COMING FROM ME—"

I climbed. The shaft groaned and sloughed glass behind me. Below, fainter and fainter, I heard Veyra begin, very calmly, to sing—and the mountain answered her, folding itself closed like a hand, and I threw myself through the narrowing gap between Tomas's straining arms as the stone kissed shut behind us with a sound like a book closing.

We lay on the mountainside in the dark, gasping, glowing—me gold, him gray with dust—and after a while he said, wheezing:

"So. Did you trash the place?"

"Tomas."

"Because from the noise, I'm guessing you trashed the place."

I started laughing and couldn't stop, and the fire in my chest laughed with me, humming that low sweet note, and far below us the mountain settled into silence, empty at last of everyone except the dark.

Then the wind shifted, and it came from the valley—from home—and it smelled wrong. Not smoke. Not storm. It smelled like the tree line *moving*.

Tomas sat up. "Astrid. What does it mean when the wood stops weeping?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't know—but the fire did, and it had begun, very softly, to sing a different song. One that sounded, God help me, like a door opening.
