## 10. Stranger in the Weeping Wood

The door in the mountain opened onto morning.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean there was a door—stone swinging aside like a held breath released—and beyond it a slope of wet grass and a valley full of light, and I stood at the threshold of it with fire singing in my chest and absolutely no idea where I was.

That is not entirely true. I knew *of* it. The fire knew it. When I looked down at the Weeping Wood—the trees silvered with resin, the smoke rising from chimneys I did not recognize but somehow recognized anyway—something in my chest leaned toward it the way a plant leans toward a window. But leaning is not remembering. Leaning is just the body's opinion. My mind was a room with all the furniture gone, and every window painted over, and one small coal glowing in the middle of the floor.

I knew three things:

I knew my name was Astrid Ishikawa, because the fire said so, patiently, the way you'd remind a child.

I knew I carried something that had to be delivered.

And I knew the young man lying in the grass beside me—who had, apparently, held a collapsing mountain open with nothing but stubbornness and a rope—was someone I would have trusted with my life, if I could remember having one.

"Tomas," he'd said, when I asked. "You're Astrid. You're home. And yes, before you ask again: you're sure."

"I'm not sure," I said. "That's rather the problem."

He sat up slowly, wincing, and looked at me for a long moment. There was something terrible in his face—not grief exactly, but grief's older sibling, the kind that has already done its crying and now just has to get through the day.

"You don't know me," he said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway.

"No."

"And you don't know—" He gestured at the valley, the wood, the smoke. "Any of it."

"No." I hesitated. "But I know I'm supposed to be here. Does that count?"

"It counts," he said, after a while. "It'll have to."

* * *

We came down through the wood together, and the trees wept over us.

I didn't understand that at first. I thought the resin dripping from the branches was ordinary sap, some quirk of the species. But Tomas kept stopping, pressing his palm to the trunks, and once he made a sound low in his throat like a man reading a letter from the dead.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Memories," he said. "The wood records them. It always has—it's why we call it the Weeping Wood. Every drop is a moment somebody lived here." He wiped his hand on his coat and didn't look at me. "There's one here from last spring. A girl stealing honey cakes from the guild kitchen and blaming a raccoon."

"That seems like a strange thing for a tree to keep."

"She was very proud of the raccoon story." His voice cracked on *she*, and he turned away fast, pretending great interest in a completely unremarkable birch.

I stood in the dripping silence and understood, with the coal in my chest pulsing slow and sorry, that the girl was me. That the wood remembered me better than I remembered myself. That somewhere in these silvered trunks was an entire life—nineteen years of it—and I was walking past it like a stranger past a lit window.

"Do you want them back?" Tomas asked quietly. "The memories? Veyra's court is broken. Serevain said—" He stopped. "Did Serevain tell you what the fire would cost?"

"The fire told me," I said. "Afterward. It's very honest once it's too late to matter." I touched my sternum, where the warmth sat. "It says the memories aren't destroyed. They're *in* the flame. Burned into it. Apparently dragon-fire keeps everything; it just doesn't give anything back for free."

"So you could—"

"No." I said it more firmly than I felt. "Serevain gave me this flame to carry, not to spend. And the guild needs it. Whatever the guild is." I looked at him. "Which brings us to the interesting question, doesn't it? Because everyone down in that valley keeps looking at me like I'm a ghost, and nobody will tell me what I'm a ghost *of*."

* * *

The guildhall smelled of woodsmoke and old paper, and my hands knew the way through it.

That was the worst part, in those first days. Not the blankness—blankness is just empty, and empty is bearable—but the way my body kept betraying knowledge my mind didn't have. My feet turned left toward the archive without being told. My fingers found the third loose stair by habit. I reached up to hook a lantern on a peg that wasn't there anymore, and stood with my arm raised like a fool until Tomas gently took it down.

"You hung it there yourself," he said. "Two winters ago. You said the old peg was 'an insult to carpentry.'"

"Did I fix it?"

"You fixed it *and* wrote a letter of complaint to the entire guild about it."

Despite everything, something in my chest warmed. Not memory. Just the shape where memory used to sit, the way an amputated limb still itches. I was learning to live in the shapes.

The people came to see me, in ones and twos. An old woman who pressed my hands and called me *little ember* and wept without explaining. A boy of maybe ten who informed me, gravely, that I owed him a rematch at stones and that he had been practicing. A broad-shouldered man with flour on his sleeves who hugged me hard enough to crack ribs and then apologized to *Tomas*, of all people, as though Tomas were the one who'd need consoling.

Each time, they watched my face for recognition. Each time, I gave them honesty instead, which is a colder meal but a cleaner one. And each time, they nodded and stayed anyway, sitting near me in the hall as though proximity were a kind of remembering.

On the fourth evening, I found the ledger.

* * *

It was where my hands insisted it would be—in the archive, third shelf, behind the binding manuals—and it fell open to a page marked with a ribbon, because of course it did. Of course I had marked it. The ink was a century old and brown as tea, and beside a date that had come and gone while I was underground, in a careful antique hand, was written:

*Astrid Ishikawa goes beneath. Astrid Ishikawa returns carrying fire. The valley opens.*

Below it, in different ink—younger, angrier, mine—someone had scrawled: *Who wrote this? Who am I to this book?*

I read my own name the way you read a stranger's name carved in a bench. *Astrid.* It fit my mouth. It didn't fit my history. Somewhere between the mountain and this shelf, I had become a person with a name and no story, holding a prophecy about herself like a letter addressed to the previous tenant.

Tomas found me there. He was good at finding me; I suspected I'd been easy to find my whole life, whatever whole life meant now.

"That's the page," he said. "Where it started."

"Tell me," I said. "All of it. Halvard. The soul. The founding lie. What the ward ran on. What I did down there. What it cost." I closed the ledger. "Everyone keeps deciding what I can handle. I'm done being handled. If I'm going to belong here again—if I ever did—I want to belong to the truth of it, not to a kind version."

He pulled up a stool. He took his time. And then, in the lamplight, with the freed souls' rest settling over the valley like snow, he told me everything.

He told me about Halvard, who sold his soul beneath the mountain to buy the valley time, and who came home dimmer every year and never once complained. He told me how Halvard had died—or finished, or been freed, the words still mattered to Tomas even if they were only words to me—and how the last thing Halvard's soul had done, rising out of the shattered chandelier, was turn back toward the surface. Toward home. Toward the guild he'd lied to and loved in the same breath.

He told me the founding lie: that the Emberwrights hadn't fled magic, they'd stolen it—that the ward around the valley had run on caged fire and bound souls, and that the loyalty holding the guild together had been built on a crime nobody living had committed and everybody living had inherited. He told me how the elders had taken the news. Badly, then quietly, then—slowly, the way ice gives up a river—honestly. The new ward, the one humming now at the tree line, ran on the flame I carried, freely given and freely fed. No cages. No chandeliers. Just fire, tended.

And he told me about us. About honey cakes and raccoons. About the rematch at stones I apparently owed a ten-year-old and was apparently losing. About a night on the archive roof when I'd said—his voice went careful here—*"If the world ever gets bigger than the valley, I hope I'm brave enough to go see it."*

"Was I?" I asked. "Brave enough?"

"You were brave enough," he said. "That's the whole problem. That's why you're sitting there with an empty head and a chest full of dragon."

I laughed, and heard in my own laugh a stranger's laugh, and decided—right then, with the ledger shut and the truth laid out between us like a shared meal—to keep her anyway. To let her be me. Memory, I was beginning to suspect, was not the only thing that made a self. There was also choice, repeated daily. There was showing up. There was telling the truth when a kinder lie was available, which was, I gathered, something the girl in the ledger had believed in enough to die for. Or nearly.

* * *

On the seventh morning, I climbed to the tree line alone.

The new ward shimmered there, faint as heat above a road, and beyond it the world went on being enormous. Far below, in the valley, smoke rose from honest chimneys. Somewhere in the wood, the trees were weeping new resin over new memories—over the night the chandeliers broke, over Halvard's soul going home, over a boy named Tomas holding a door open with his bare hands and refusing, absolutely refusing, to let it close.

I put my hand against the ward and the fire in my chest hummed its low sweet note, and for a moment—just a moment—I felt something flicker at the edge of me. Not a memory. Smaller than that. The ghost of a feeling: the specific warmth of coming home to a place you've never left, the smell of a kitchen, a voice saying *little ember*—

Gone. Like a fish surfacing and diving.

But I had seen it. And the fire had seen me see it, and it sang a little differently after that. Not the song of a door opening. Something quieter. The song of a hearth being built.

Behind me, boots on the path. Tomas, carrying two cups of something steaming and a look on his face that said he'd been rehearsing a speech and was going to deliver it whether the world wanted it or not.

"Astrid," he said. "There's something you should know. About what happens next."

I took the cup. I smiled at my own name in his mouth, at the stranger I was choosing to become, and waited for the truth.

Because that was the thing the fire had taught me, the one lesson worth the price of everything:

Truth outlasts memory. Truth is what you build the hearth from. And belonging—

Belonging, it turns out, can be rebuilt. All it costs is everything, paid forward, one honest day at a time.

"So," I said. "Tell me what happens next."

And far off, at the top of the mountain, where a great winged shape had begun—very faintly, very impossibly—to glow again, something answered before Tomas could.
