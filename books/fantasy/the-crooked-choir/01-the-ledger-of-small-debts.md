## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only works after midnight, and I am its high priest.

The back rooms of the Guild of Impossible Things smell of tallow, old vellum, and the faint copper tang of transactions that should never have happened. I sit at the long counting table with a pot of ink going thick in the cold, and I balance the books. That is my official title, if the guild can be said to have titles: Keeper of Accounts. Unofficially, I am the person who knows exactly how much a shadow weighs when it has been traded twice, and what happens to a lullaby when it is bottled for longer than a season. (It sours. Not all at once — first it goes flat, like bread left out, and then one night you open the bottle and what comes out is not a lullaby at all but something that hums your own name back at you, slightly wrong.)

Tonight's ledger is the small debts. I prefer them to the large ones. A large debt might be a kingdom's weather sold south, or a dead man's second life pawned against a living man's first. Small debts are human-scale: a widow's grief traded for her husband's shadow, which she keeps in a jar on the mantel and talks to on Sundays. A boy who sold his fear of the dark and now cannot sleep without a candle lit in every room, because the dark, having been removed, no longer recognizes him as its own.

I write: *One fear of darkness, minor grade, purchased from T. Bellamy, age nine. Condition: worn but serviceable. Stored, shelf fourteen.*

And then, because I am thorough and because thoroughness is the shape my cowardice takes, I add: *Note — subject's mother inquired after buyback terms. Declined per policy.*

Per policy. The guild loves that phrase the way a spider loves silk. Everything is per policy. The policy was written four hundred years ago by people who are now either dust or something worse, and it says that what is bought is bought, and what is stored is stored, and no one who walks through our unremarkable green door on Candlewick Lane leaves with more than they brought unless they can pay.

I dip my pen. My hand does not shake. It hasn't shaken in eleven years, not since the night I learned that shaking hands get noticed, and noticed things get named, and named things get *filed*.

Because here is the thing about me, the thing I keep folded up small behind my sternum where even the ledgers can't see it: I have the gift. The true gift. I can map what isn't there.

Not metaphorically. Anyone can draw a map of a place they've walked. What I do is different. Give me an hour and a blank page and I can chart the room where your lost years went. I can find the door in the back of your mind you nailed shut at seven years old and tell you what's behind it. When I close my eyes over an unmapped space — a stairwell that doesn't exist, a country that was never founded, the gap between one heartbeat and the next — I see it. Clearly. In lines and distances and bearings, as though someone had already surveyed it and simply neglected to tell anyone.

The guild would call this valuable. The guild would call this *priceless*, which is a word that means *we will take it from you*. And so for eleven years I have done my sums and kept my head down and drawn nothing but honest maps of honest places, and once a month I burn my false attempts in the tallow-stove so that even my failures leave no evidence.

Invisibility, I have learned, is not free. It is a loan. Every day I go unnoticed, the interest accrues. Someday the note comes due. I feel it the way you feel weather coming in an old scar — a dull pressure behind the eyes whenever Vess looks at me a moment too long, whenever a new apprentice asks too casually what I did before the guild.

Tonight the pressure is worse than usual. I tell myself it's the cold.

* * *

The door opens at half past the smallest hour, which means Odile, because Odile treats locks as suggestions and hours as rumors.

She comes in the way she always does, sideways, as though the doorway might be a trap, shaking rain off a cloak that has been rained on so many times it has given up being waterproof and settled into a kind of philosophical dampness. She is small and quick and has the face of someone perpetually mid-burglary, and she owes me a debt she has never once acknowledged out loud, which between us is a form of politeness.

"You're still here," she says. "Of course you're still here. You live here. Do you actually sleep, or do you just close your eyes and itemize?"

"I nap in the margins," I say. "What did you bring me?"

She grins and produces, from somewhere inside the cloak, a parcel wrapped in oilcloth. She sets it on the ledger with the delicacy of a bomb-disposal expert. "Shelf forty-one wanted auditing."

I unwrap it. Inside is a jar, and in the jar is a shadow — someone's shadow, traded away years ago, gone strange from storage. It moves wrong. Shadows in jars move like shadows, slow and liquid, following the light. This one moves like it's *listening*.

"Whose?" I ask.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters for the ledger."

"Then put down 'anonymous donor' and save us both the poetry." She hops up to sit on the counting table, which Vess forbids, knocking my ink pot so I have to catch it, which is also forbidden, probably. "Vess wants the forty-first shelf cleared by week's end. Says we're crowded."

"Crowded," I repeat. "We're storing souls and songs in a building the size of a rich man's stable, and the master's solution is decluttering."

"The master's solutions are always decluttering. It's why the master is the master." She watches me work for a while, chin on her fist. Odile watching someone work is like a cat watching a bird through glass — patient, affectionate, and fundamentally predatory. "You know what's funny? You've been here eleven years and I still don't know anything about you. Where you learned your letters. Who taught you bookkeeping. Whether Isandro Okonkwo is even your real name."

My pen pauses. One heartbeat. Two. Then it moves again, smooth as water. "It's on the guild rolls," I say. "That makes it real enough."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I agree pleasantly. "It isn't."

She laughs — she always laughs when I deflect, which is either a compliment or a warning, and with Odile those are frequently the same thing. She slides off the table, steals a candied plum from the dish I keep for exactly this purpose, and heads for the door. At the threshold she stops, and something in her posture changes, some thief's instinct pinging off a silence in the house.

"Isandro," she says, without turning around. "The front hall's wrong tonight. Can't say how. Doors are where I left them, floor's level, but it's like the house is holding its breath." She glances back at me, eyes catching the lamplight. "You'd know, wouldn't you? If a place was wrong. You've got a nose for rooms."

"I've got a nose for ledgers," I say. "Goodnight, Odile."

She goes. I sit very still for a long time, listening to the guild hold its breath.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding: it works. That's the horror of it. Year after year, it works, and each success teaches you that you could have hidden better, hidden deeper, and so the hiding becomes the whole architecture of your life, load-bearing walls made of nothing.

I finish the small debts by the third bell. I lock the ledgers in the iron cabinet — three turns, per policy — and I take out the one thing in these rooms that is mine alone: a slim notebook, unmarked, that I keep behind a loose brick where the guild's own thieves have never thought to look, because who robs a bookkeeper?

In it are my sins. Maps of places that don't exist. The stairwell under the harbor that appears only during ebb tide. The country called Elsewhere that certain children fall into and come back from speaking backwards. The door I found once, aged nineteen, in the wall of my own rented room, which opened onto a corridor lined with every version of myself I had decided not to be.

I don't look at that page often.

Tonight I don't look at any of them. Tonight I sit with the notebook closed under my hand and I listen to the guild breathe — or not breathe — above me, and I do the accounting I do every night, the real ledger, the one with no ink:

*One gift, concealed. Eleven years' interest, compounding.*
*One name, possibly not mine, held in trust by people who trade in trust.*
*One debt, accruing.*

The candle gutters. Somewhere overhead, a floorboard settles with a sound like a swallowed word.

And then, from the direction of Vess's study, I hear it: the scrape of the iron cabinet opening. Three turns, reversed. Footsteps. The soft, deliberate whisper of pages being taken — not read, *taken*, a sheaf of them, quickly, the way you remove things when there is no time left to be careful.

I know the sound of pages leaving a ledger the way a mother knows a cry in the night.

I sit in the dark with my hand still on my hidden notebook, and I understand, with the terrible clarity of the very tired, that whatever is coming has already begun — and that somewhere in this house, in some cabinet, on some shelf, there may be a page with my name on it.

I never wrote one.

But I have balanced enough accounts to know: things get entered in ledgers whether you write them yourself or not.
