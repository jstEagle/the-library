## 2. A Proclamation in Red Wax

The proclamation arrived the way bad news always arrives at the Guild of Impossible Things: late, overpriced, and smelling faintly of someone else's ambition.

I was in the back rooms, as I am most mornings, balancing the accounts. That morning's ledger was a modest one—a widow in Saltrow who'd traded her late husband's shadow for three more years of his laughter echoing in the house, a fiddler who'd sold the song he couldn't stop hearing in his sleep, a child's imaginary friend kept in a jar on the third shelf until the family could afford to let it go properly. Small debts. Small mercies. The kind of bookkeeping that lets you sleep at night if you don't look too closely at what you're counting.

I was entering the widow's interest—shadows accrue it too, though theirs is measured in length rather than coin—when Odile came through the door without knocking, which she never does. Odile knocks. Odile has picked locks since she was six years old and still knocks, because she says a thief who announces herself is a thief with nothing worth stealing.

She had something worth stealing in her hand.

"Town criers," she said, tossing a folded broadsheet onto my ledger, directly across the widow's shadow entry, as if to smother it. "Six of them, on every corner from the Tannery Gate to the Palace Steps. They're paying criers double rates. You know what that means."

"It means the crown wants everyone to hear it."

"It means the crown wants everyone to *remember* hearing it." She leaned against my desk, arms crossed, and watched me with the particular patience of someone waiting for you to do the thing they already know you'll do.

So I did it. I unfolded the broadsheet.

The paper was good stock—heavy, expensive, the kind used for marriage contracts and death warrants—and the seal at the bottom was red wax the size of my palm, pressed with the mountain-and-crown sigil of House Vael. But it wasn't the seal that made my hands go cold. It was the ink. Proclamations are printed. This had been *written*, every copy, by hand, in the same careful script, which meant scribes had sat in some cold room for days copying out the same words hundreds of times, which meant someone important had decided these words mattered enough to be worth the tedium.

*By order of Her Majesty's Court Beneath the Mountain,* it began, which was itself strange—the crown rarely acknowledged the under-court existed, the way you don't acknowledge the mold in your walls—and then:

*...whereas there exist persons gifted with the making of maps of realms not found upon this earth, being an abomination before law and nature both; and whereas such persons have trafficked in that which cannot be owned; and whereas the safety of the realm requires their discovery: let it be known that any soul possessing or concealing the gift of impossible cartography shall be taken into custody of the court, there to be examined, and their works confiscated and burned.*

*Reward offered for information leading to such discovery. Reward doubled for information concerning guild members.*

*Reward tripled for the location of any guild ledger.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because my eyes kept sliding off the middle paragraph like water off oiled cloth, refusing to hold the words *gift of impossible cartography,* as if the page itself were trying to spare me.

"They know," Odile said quietly. "Somebody told them."

"Somebody always tells them eventually." I set the broadsheet down and smoothed it flat with both hands, a pointless gesture, tidying paper when what I wanted was to tidy the world. "The gift's been whispered about for years. Half the nobility keeps a hedge-cartographer in the cellar for mapping their debts onto other people's estates. The crown just never cared before."

"The crown didn't care before because the under-court didn't ask them to care." Odile tapped the red wax seal with one finger. "That's a joint seal. Mountain and throne. Whatever's happening down there, it's spooked them enough up here that they're cooperating. And Isandro—" she paused, and I braced myself, because Odile only uses your full name when she's about to be either kind or cruel, and she's rarely kind first, "—that second reward. For guild members. That's us. That's *you.*"

"I'm a bookkeeper."

"You're a bookkeeper who once drew me a map to a room that doesn't exist so I could steal a key from inside it." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "You think I've forgotten? You think anyone who's seen you work has forgotten? There are maybe four people in this building who don't suspect what you can do, and two of them are dead and one of them is the cook."

"Then the cook is a security risk."

"Isandro."

"I'm a bookkeeper, Odile." I said it the way you say a prayer you've stopped believing in—out of habit, for the shape of it, because the alternative is standing in an open field during a storm. "I balance accounts. I file contracts. I have never in eleven years drawn a single map inside these walls, and if you value either of us, you'll forget the room that doesn't exist."

She held my gaze for a long moment. Then she pushed off from the desk and headed for the door, pausing with her hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the room that doesn't exist saved my life. So if it comes to choosing, I know which side of the ledger I'm on."

She left. I sat with the proclamation and the widow's shadow and the fiddler's song, and I thought about interest accruing, and how invisibility is a debt like any other—you pay nothing for years, and then all at once, all of it comes due.

* * *

Vess burned half the ledgers that night.

I know because I was awake to hear it—not the burning itself, which makes no sound, but the absence of sound around it. The guildhouse at night has a particular hum: the bottled lullabies on the third shelf murmuring to each other in their glass, the traded shadows shifting in their cedar chests like sleeping animals, the great iron safe in the archive room ticking as its wards renewed themselves hourly. Past midnight, the humming stopped. All of it, all at once, like an orchestra cut mid-phrase.

I went down the back stairs with a candle I didn't light, because I've spent eleven years learning to move through that building unseen, and found the archive doors open and the air thick with smoke and the smell of scorched vellum.

Vess stood in the middle of it, feeding pages into a brazier one at a time. Not hurling them—she doesn't hurl anything, our guildmaster, she's a small precise woman with white braid and hands like a surgeon's—but placing each page in with the care of someone laying flowers on a grave. She'd been at it a while. The floor around her was drifted with ash, and half the shelving stood empty, and the empty spaces were the worst part, because I knew those shelves. I'd inventoried them. I knew exactly which contracts had lived where.

"The Saltrow district records," I said. My voice came out flat. "The harbor ledgers. The lullaby registry. Thirty years of—"

"Gone." She didn't look up. "Sit down, Okonkwo."

There was only one chair left unburned, which I suspected was not an accident. I didn't sit.

"Do you know how long it takes to burn a ledger properly?" she went on, placing another page in the brazier, watching the corners catch. "You can't just put it to the flame. Ink remembers. If the binding contract isn't dissolved first—if the debt isn't formally released—the fire just frees the thing instead of destroying it. Every shadow in those chests would have walked home tonight. Every lullaby would have gone back to its child, grown now, forty years old, hearing their own infant sleep-song in the walls." She finally looked at me, and her face in the brazier-light was older than it had been that morning, which I hadn't thought possible. "So I dissolved them first. Three hundred contracts, released. Do you understand what that costs? The guild runs on held debts. We just gave away half our substance to keep the crown from finding it."

"To keep the crown from finding *it,*" I said. "Or to keep the crown from finding *us.*"

"Those are the same thing."

"They're not, though, are they?" I heard my own voice getting quieter, which is what it does instead of louder. "The proclamation asks for ledgers. It asks for cartographers. It doesn't ask for bookkeepers. You could have burned the trade records and kept the membership rolls. You could have kept—"

"I kept what protects people." Now she set down the page she was holding, and turned to face me fully, and I saw that under the precision she was afraid. Our guildmaster, who once stared down a debt-collector from the drowned courts without blinking, was afraid. "Isandro. Listen to me. When the witch-hunters come—and they will come, within the fortnight—they will want names. Names are the only currency that matters to them. The guild survives by having none to give. So we are going to become very boring. We are going to be accountants and clerks and nobody special. No gifts. No maps. No impossible things for the duration. And above all—" she stepped closer, and took my chin in her surgeon's hand, tilting my face up to hers, an intimacy I had never permitted her and she had never attempted, "—above all, no one speaks of what anyone can do. Not to colleagues. Not to lovers. Not in confession. Not even to yourself, in the privacy of your own skull, because there are things that listen to skulls. Am I understood?"

"Yes," I said.

"Say it back."

"No gifts. No maps. No names."

She released my chin and nodded, satisfied, and turned back to her brazier, and I should have left. Eleven years of survival instinct said leave, say nothing, go back to bed and be boring tomorrow.

But I looked past her, at the shelves, and the wrongness snagged in me like a nail in a coat.

"Where are the old ledgers?" I asked.

Vess went very still. Stillness, in Vess, is louder than shouting.

"The founding ledgers," I said. "The pre-guild records. The ones in mother-of-pearl binding, the ones nobody's allowed to touch. They weren't on the burn list—I checked the inventory yesterday, before all this. They were here."

"They've been moved."

"Moved where?"

"To a place the crown will never find them." She placed another page in the brazier, and the flame flared green for a moment—contract ink, dissolving—and lit her face from below like something rising out of a grave. "That's all you need to know."

And here is the thing I have thought about every night since: she said it kindly. That's what I can't get past. She said *that's all you need to know* in the same voice she used when I was nine years old and newly arrived, feverish and orphaned and full of questions, and she tucked the blankets in and said *the guild will keep you safe, that's all you need to know.* Protection, again. Protection as a wall built around me, brick by brick, higher every year, until I couldn't see out and no one could see in.

Betrayal wears protection's clothes so well you sometimes can't tell them apart until you're already locked in.

I went back upstairs. I lay in my narrow bed listening to the silence where the lullabies used to hum, and I told myself the story I've told myself for eleven years: *she loves us, she's keeping us safe, the less you know the safer you are.*

Then I got up, lit my candle, and did the one thing a good bookkeeper does when the master starts burning books.

I checked my own accounts.

Every member of the guild has a file. It's regulation—entry date, dues, marks of service, the standard biography. Mine was filed where it's been filed since I was nine years old, in the cabinet behind the duty roster, third drawer, between a retired fence named Corvus and a woman who trades exclusively in borrowed courage.

The drawer was empty.

Not cleared—empty. No dust ring where a folder should sit. No gap in the sequence. The folders on either side had been shifted together, snug, deliberate, the way you close a book so no one can tell where you stopped reading. Someone hadn't just removed my file. Someone had removed the fact that there had ever been a file.

I stood in the dark archive with my candle guttering, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. The founding ledgers, moved. My file, erased. A proclamation demanding cartographers, sealed in wax from the court beneath the mountain. A guildmaster who'd tucked me in at nine years old telling me *that's all you need to know.*

Eleven years of invisibility. Eleven years of hiding my gift so well that even I sometimes pretended I didn't have it. And somewhere in those eleven years, apparently, someone had decided that the safest place for Isandro Okonkwo was nowhere at all—not hidden, but *unwritten.*

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the last of Vess's fire was dying in its brazier, and I thought about the dragon-fire the old members whisper about when they think no one's listening—the flame under the guild, the one that guards our names, the one that's been guttering for years.

If my name wasn't in the files anymore, I wondered, whose keeping was it in?

And why did the answer feel like a debt I'd been paying interest on my whole life?
