## 4. The Cartographer's Oath

Vess summoned me at the hour when even the candles give up pretending.

I knew it was serious because she sent a boy with no name of his own—one of the foundlings the guild keeps for exactly this purpose, messengers who can be disavowed in under a breath if anyone asks who sent them. He tapped twice on my door, handed me a slip of paper that read only *NOW*, and vanished down the stairwell like water finding a drain.

Guildmasters do not say *now*. Guildmasters say *at your convenience* and *when you've balanced the quarter's accounts* and *after you've slept, Isandro, you look terrible*. Vess had never once summoned me directly in eleven years of employment, which meant either I was about to be promoted, exiled, or killed, and my money—such as it was, paid mostly in trade credit against bottled lullabies—was on exile.

The guildhall above was quiet in the way of a house where something has recently died. Half the shelves in the archive stood empty, their contents ash by now, if Vess had kept her word about the burning. She'd kept her word about everything else for thirty years, so I assumed arson was also proceeding on schedule.

Her study sat at the top of the crooked tower, behind a door that only opened for people whose names were in the guild's protection. I put my palm to the wood and felt the familiar tug—the dragon-fire beneath the hall reading me, checking me against its list, finding me present and accounted for. The door swung inward.

Vess sat behind a desk built from the keel of a ship that had, according to guild legend, sailed off the edge of a map on purpose. She is not an old woman, exactly. She is more accurately a woman who has been old since before I was born and simply stopped bothering to update the look. Her hair is iron-grey and cropped short as a penitent's, and her eyes are the pale grey of paper left too long in weather.

"You didn't burn my pages," I said.

Because that was why I'd come, obviously. Three days since the proclamation in red wax went up on every post in the city—*by order of the crown, all persons possessing the gift of mapping the unmapped shall surrender themselves for examination*—and three days since I'd gone looking for my own entry in the ledger of protected names and found the binding page gone. Not burned. Burned pages leave ghosts: scorch marks, stubs of spine, a smell like burnt sugar and regret. My page had been *removed*. Cut free with a blade sharp enough to leave no ragged edge, and there were perhaps four people in the guild who owned such a blade, and one of them was sitting in front of me.

"Sit down, Isandro."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." She folded her hands. "You didn't burn your pages because your pages aren't yours to burn."

The room got very small around those words, the way rooms do when they're holding their breath along with you.

"Explain," I said, and was proud of how steady it came out.

She rose, crossed to the far wall, and pressed her hand flat against a section of paneling that I had walked past eleven hundred times without once suspecting it was anything but paneling. It swung open on a narrow stair going down, breathing out air that smelled of hot metal and old feathers.

"You've worked for this guild eleven years," she said. "Balanced our books. Kept our secrets. Never asked what keeps the roof over your head dry and your name out of the crown's mouth." A pause. "Time you saw what you've been protecting."

"And if I'd rather not?"

"Then you can walk out that door and take your chances with the witch-hunters." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "But you already know your page is missing. You've known for three days. You've been sleeping in the archive, Isandro—I can smell the lamp oil on you. You're not leaving until you understand why."

She had me there, and she knew she had me there, and I hated her a little for the neatness of it. I followed her down the stairs.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was round, and old, and wrong in the way that very old things are wrong—proportions slightly off, as though the room had been built by someone working from memory, or from a map drawn by someone who'd only ever heard the place described.

And in the center, in a basin of black stone wide as a millwheel, burned the last dragon-fire.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I have. This was not fire the way a candle is fire. It was fire the way the sea is wet—a whole condition of being, compressed into a space the size of a hearth. It gave no heat I could feel at ten paces, and yet the stone around it had gone glassy-smooth centuries ago. Its light was the color of the moment just before dawn, and it moved like something breathing, slow and vast and patient, and every few breaths it flickered.

Flickered. Like a candle in a draft. Like a thing running low.

"The founding bargain," Vess said, standing beside the basin with her hands clasped behind her back like a schoolmistress at a particularly grim lesson. "Two hundred years ago, the first guildmaster made a deal with the dragon who lived under this mountain. The dragon was dying anyway—dragons always are, eventually; eternity turns out to be a rumor—and it agreed to spend its last fire on us. In exchange, we keep certain accounts for the court below."

"What court?"

"The court beneath the mountain. The one your maps don't show, because you've never been able to make yourself draw it." She glanced at me sideways. "Yes, I know about your gift. I've known since you were nine years old and drew the cellar of this building on the back of a laundry list, including the parts that don't exist."

My stomach performed a maneuver I usually associate with bad shellfish. Eleven years of careful nothing. Eleven years of being the bookkeeper, the gray man, the one who counts other people's shadows and never once lets his own hand wander toward a map. And she'd known since I was a child.

"Why keep me at all, then? If the gift is worth a cell in the crown's dungeons?"

"Because the gift isn't what the crown thinks it is, and neither are you." She turned back to the fire. "The bargain works like this. Every soul entered into the guild's ledger is written into the dragon-fire's keeping. While the fire burns, your name cannot be spoken against you. Contracts cannot bind you. Debts cannot find you. The crown can proclaim whatever hunt it likes; its hunters literally cannot hold our names in their minds long enough to act on them. That's why half the city's most inconvenient people joined the guild, and why the other half think we're a lending library for love potions."

"And when the fire dies?"

The flicker chose that moment to answer for her. The light guttered, dimmed to almost nothing, and the room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—it was the cold of a held note ending, of a door closing somewhere far away. Then the fire breathed back, weaker than before.

"When the fire dies," Vess said quietly, "every soul recorded in the ledgers reverts to the mountain court. Every contract the guild ever honored becomes enforceable again. Every name we shielded becomes speakable, sellable, claimable." She looked at me. "Every debt comes due at once, Isandro. All of them. Two hundred years of them."

I did the arithmetic I am constitutionally incapable of not doing. Fourteen thousand entries in the master ledger, give or take the ones Vess had burned. Souls traded, shadows pawned, songs sold, names hidden. Fourteen thousand people walking around above us believing themselves safe, believing the guild's mark on their wrist meant something permanent.

"How long?" I asked.

"A year. Perhaps two. Perhaps less—the fire doesn't decline evenly. It spends faster when it's working harder, and lately it's been working very hard indeed." She paused. "Since the proclamation, actually. As though something below knows the crown is hunting, and is hungry for it."

"That's cheerful."

"I don't traffic in cheer. I traffic in time, and I'm nearly out."

I circled the basin slowly, keeping a respectful distance, the way you'd circle a sleeping animal or a rich relative. Up close the fire resolved into shapes—not flames exactly, but suggestions of them, wings and coils and a long throat full of light. And threaded through it, faint as watermark, I could see writing. Lines of script flowing through the flame like veins through marble.

"The ledger," I said. "It's written in the fire itself."

"Every entry ever made. The paper copies are just receipts." Vess's voice had gone strange—flatter, older. "Which brings us to your pages."

"My pages."

"Your mother brought you here thirty-one years ago. You were an infant. She couldn't feed you and she couldn't hide you—your gift showed early, even then. Babies with the mapping gift cry in directions that don't exist. It draws attention." She said it clinically, but her hands had tightened behind her back. "So she came to the guild and she made a bargain. She entered your name in the ledger of protected souls. The fire took you into its keeping. No one could sell you, claim you, or speak your true name against you. Not the crown. Not the court below. No one."

"And the price?"

Vess was silent for long enough that the fire flickered twice.

"The price," she said finally, "is not mine to tell. That entry is sealed, and I keep seals better than I keep promises, whatever you may currently believe. What I will tell you is this: your page wasn't stolen, Isandro. It was called. Something below has begun calling in the oldest debts first, and yours is among the oldest. When the fire gutters out—or before, if the court grows impatient—you revert. Your soul reverts. To them."

"To the Mountain Queen."

"You know that name too. Of course you do. You know everything; you just write it down nowhere." She almost smiled. "It's why I hired you, in the end. A bookkeeper who can't be made to forget is worth ten archivists."

"Loyalty," I said. "That's what this is. You're telling me this because you want my loyalty."

"I'm telling you this because in a year, maybe less, everyone in this building is going to learn what you just learned, all at once, and this guild will tear itself apart. I need people who understood the situation while they still had time to think clearly." She stepped closer to the basin, firelight crawling up her face. "There may be a way to renew the bargain. There may be a way to save the fourteen thousand. But it requires someone who can map what isn't there, and right now, in this entire city, that is exactly one person, and she is standing in front of me trying very hard to look like a bookkeeper."

The word *she* landed strangely. Vess had never once gendered me correctly in eleven years, and I'd stopped correcting her around year two, and hearing it now—deliberately, precisely, as a gift or a hook—I understood that she had decided, tonight, to stop letting me be invisible. Which was either respect or the most elegant trap I'd ever stood in.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you run. Take Odile, take the trade-credit, disappear onto a map nobody's drawn. You might live five years. Ten, if you're clever, and you are." She shrugged. "But fourteen thousand people burn with you, and you'll know it every day of those ten years. I know you, Isandro Okonkwo. You balance accounts. You don't walk away from a debt just because it's ugly."

"That's not loyalty, that's accounting."

"In this guild, child, they were always the same discipline."

I looked at the fire. At the script threading through it, at the faint lines that might have been my own name, burning quietly in the throat of a dying god. Somewhere below us, a queen sat in a palace of mirrors with a choir of purchased souls, and one verse of that choir, apparently, belonged to me.

Loyalty. Self-preservation. The scales in my chest tipped back and forth like a shopkeeper's balance with a thumb secretly pressing one pan.

"One question," I said. "When my page was cut from the ledger—who held the knife?"

Vess met my eyes without flinching, which was itself an answer, though not the one I wanted.

"I did," she said. "On the court's instruction, six days before the proclamation. They demanded proof of good faith." A beat. "I bought this guild three months with your name, Isandro. I'd do it again. That's the oath I took, and the oath you're being asked to take now: the many before the one. Always."

The fire flickered. The room breathed cold.

"So," I said, and my voice sounded far away to me, calm as a ledger line. "You're asking me to swear loyalty to an institution that just sold me. Again. To buy time."

"I'm asking you to help me find another way before the time runs out. The selling is done. The question is what we do with what's left."

What's left. Fourteen thousand souls, one guttering god, one mother's sealed bargain, and me—the invisible man with a gift worth killing for, freshly informed that he had been currency since infancy.

I should have run. Any sensible person would have run. I thought about it honestly, standing there in the cold breathing dark, weighing the door against the basin, my skin against my conscience.

But Vess was right about one thing, and it was the worst thing she could have been right about: I balance accounts. And somewhere in the black stone and the dying light, an account had been opened in my name before I could walk, and I had never once seen the terms.

"I'll take the oath," I said. "But I want access to the sealed entries. All of them. Starting with mine."

Something moved behind Vess's eyes—relief, or fear, or the particular expression of a woman realizing she has just handed a cartographer the map to her own crimes.

"We'll discuss access," she said.

"No," I said, watching the fire gutter and breathe, gutter and breathe, counting its heartbeats like coins. "We won't. That's the price. Consider it my first entry in the new ledger."

Above us, muffled through two hundred years of stone, the city slept on, unaware that its safety was measured now in the flickers of a dying flame—and that the woman who'd sworn to protect it had already started spending its children to buy it time.

Vess swore the oath words. I repeated them. The fire accepted us both, and flickered, and I began, quietly, to plan.
