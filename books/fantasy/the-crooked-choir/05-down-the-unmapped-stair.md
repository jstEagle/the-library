## 5. Down the Unmapped Stair

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the guild's map room after midnight. It isn't peace. It's the silence of a held breath, of a house pretending to be empty while everyone inside listens for footsteps. I had spent thirty years cultivating that silence around myself like a hedge of thorns, and now I was about to walk straight through it with my gift held out in both hands like a lantern.

Odile found me there before I'd even unrolled the first blank sheet.

"I heard you leave your rooms," she said from the doorway, arms crossed, one hip against the frame. She has a way of standing as though every wall owes her money. "You left your lamp burning. You never leave your lamp burning."

"Maybe I've become careless in my old age."

"You're forty-one." She came in and shut the door behind her, which was either courtesy or conspiracy. With Odile it was usually both. "And you're not careless. You alphabetize your grief. So tell me why the guild's most boring accountant is sneaking into the map room at the hour when even the ghosts file for sleep."

I could have lied to her. I am good at lying; it's the family trade, apparently, though I hadn't known that until three nights ago, when I found my mother's handwriting in a ledger fragment and learned that I myself was an entry — *one soul, sold, price: one hidden child* — and that Vess had burned the pages that proved it. But Odile owed me a debt, and more importantly, I owed her the truth, because if this went wrong, she deserved to know what she was dying for.

So I told her. All of it. The missing pages. The entry in my mother's hand. The bargain Vess had confessed on his knees among the ashes of half our records: dragon-fire protecting every name in the guild's keeping, guttering low, and when it died, everything recorded reverting to the court beneath the mountain. Every soul. Every shadow. Every song.

Every *me*.

When I finished, Odile was quiet for a long moment, which for her was roughly equivalent to a monologue.

"So," she said finally. "The guildmaster sold you out decades before he sold you out this week. That's almost impressive. Consistency."

"He didn't sell me. He just... declined to mention it. For thirty years. While taking my labor."

"A distinction without much difference." She pulled a stool over and sat, elbows on her knees, studying me the way she studied locks — looking for the mechanism. "And your plan?"

"That's where it gets complicated."

"It was already complicated, Isandro. You're planning to rob a queen."

"My plan," I said, "is to draw us a door."

* * *

Here is what the crown doesn't understand about cartographers of unmapped realms, and what the guild spent centuries making sure they never would: we don't find what isn't there. We *agree* with it. The unmapped places exist the way a note exists before it's sung — potential, folded tight, waiting for someone with enough stubbornness to unfold them. My gift isn't sight. It's consent. I look at a blank space and ask it, politely, what it would like to become, and if the space is lonely enough, it tells me.

It is also, per the guild charter, section nine, paragraph three, forbidden. *No member shall render the unreal navigable.* Because a map of what isn't there is a weapon, or a door, or an invitation, depending on who's holding it. The guild preferred its impossible things bottled, labeled, and traded at markup.

I spread a sheet of vellum on the table and set my inkwell beside it, and my hands were shaking, and I let them shake. There's no shame in trembling before something true.

"What do you need?" Odile asked.

"Quiet. And if I start bleeding from the nose, don't make a fuss. It's undignified."

I closed my eyes and reached down through the floor of the city — through cellar and cistern and the packed bones of older cities stacked beneath like sediment — and I listened. The under-city is full of half-made places: staircases that were dreamed but never built, corridors abandoned mid-thought by architects who died or despaired. Most people walk past them their whole lives and feel only a draft, a shiver, the sense of a door where there is none. I felt the doors themselves. Dozens of them. Hundreds. The whole city was honeycombed with the unbuilt, and all of it leaning toward the mountain the way iron leans toward a lodestone.

Because the mountain wasn't finished either. The court beneath it had been digging for centuries, hungry, patient, and its tunnels reached up under our feet like roots under a garden. Somewhere below, the unbuilt places of the city touched the built places of the mountain, and at that seam, a passage existed that no one had ever walked because no one had ever agreed it should.

I opened my eyes and drew.

The ink moved strangely — I'll say that. It didn't flow so much as *remember*, pooling in shapes I hadn't chosen, lines curving where my hand went straight. A staircase appeared on the vellum, descending in a long spiral, and then a gallery, and then a stretch of nothing marked only with the words *hold my hand here*, written in my own handwriting, which I did not remember writing.

"Well," said Odile, reading upside down. "That's ominous."

"The map knows things I don't yet. That's normal."

"Is it? Is that normal, Isandro?"

"For me. Which is why it's forbidden." I blew on the ink. It dried instantly, eagerly, like it wanted to be done being drawn and start being real. "The guild calls this rendering the unreal navigable. The mountain court will call it trespass. The crown will call it treason. I'm calling it Tuesday."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference. Thirty years of hiding, Odile. Thirty years of balancing other people's impossible accounts while sitting on the one thing I was actually made for. Tomorrow — tonight, rather — I use it. Openly. In front of a witness." I looked at her. "That's you. Congratulations. You're the witness."

"And the accomplice."

"Also you. The debt you owe me—"

"—is paid by whatever this is," she finished. "Yes. I did the arithmetic before I followed you here. Though I confess I assumed we'd be stealing something sensible. Silver. Secrets. A moderately famous painting."

"We're stealing me back."

She stood, and for once she didn't have anything wry to say, and I understood that this was her version of a blessing.

* * *

We went down through the guild's oldest cellar, the one where Vess kept the surviving ledgers under triple lock, and past it, to a wall that every guild member knew was load-bearing and no guild member had ever thought to question. I put my palm flat against the stone and the map against the stone beside it, and I said the thing I had never said aloud in my life:

*Be a door.*

The stone considered this. Stone is slow; you have to give it time. Then, with a sound like a sigh held for three hundred years, it unfolded.

Not swung open. *Unfolded* — hinges appearing where geometry said there couldn't be any, the wall peeling back into a spiral stair that dropped away into dark, each step worn smooth as though generations of feet had used it, though no foot had ever touched it. The air that rose from below smelled of cold iron and old singing.

Odile whistled softly. "How long has this been here?"

"Since about ten minutes ago. And since forever. Both are true. Don't think about it too hard; it bites."

"Everything worth doing bites." She lit a shuttered lantern, checked her knives — four of them, plus the ones I wasn't supposed to know about — and looked at me. "Rules of engagement. If I say run, we run. If I say hide, we hide. If I say leave me, you leave me."

"No."

"Isandro—"

"You owe me a debt, not my obedience, and I'm not leaving anyone underground. Not again." I hadn't meant to say *again*. It slipped out with the truth of things that slip, and Odile's eyes narrowed, filing it away. Everyone in the guild had losses; mine had always been the quietest.

"Fine," she said. "Then new rule. Nobody lies to anybody else tonight. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Liar," she said pleasantly, and stepped onto the first stair, and I followed her down.

* * *

The stair went deeper than the city had any right to be deep. We passed cellars that belonged to houses that had burned a century ago, their contents still arranged, bread still on tables, preserved by the same logic that preserved the stair itself: things that are only half-real don't rot. We passed a corridor lined with doors that opened onto weather — rain, snow, a summer afternoon in a field somewhere — and Odile reached toward one and I caught her wrist.

"Don't. Those aren't places. They're *arguments* the mountain is having with itself. Step through one and you're a footnote."

"You're very calm for a man walking into a fairy court to steal his own soul."

"I'm terrified," I said. "I've simply been terrified so long it's stopped feeling like fear and started feeling like weather. You learn to work in it."

She glanced sideways at me. "That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard, and I once robbed a widow who narrated her own crimes."

"Then let's fix it. Ask me something you've always wondered. Talk keeps the dark honest."

She considered. "Why accounting? You've got — *that*." She gestured at the stair, the walls, the whole improbable architecture of my defiance. "You could have been anything. Why spend thirty years balancing ledgers of bottled lullabies?"

Because ledgers don't look at you. Because numbers keep secrets beautifully. Because a gift that makes doors out of nothing is dangerous, and the safest place to hide a dangerous thing is inside a boring man. But what I said was:

"Because somebody had to keep track of what the guild took. Somebody had to write down the price of every shadow and every song, so that someday, someone could add the column and see what it cost." I turned the lantern low as the stair began to curve through living rock. "I thought I was keeping the guild honest. Turns out I was building the case against it. Entry by entry. Thirty years of evidence, and I didn't know I was the plaintiff."

"And now?"

"Now I'm the thief." I smiled, and felt it land crooked on my face, unfamiliar. "Funny how the guild trained me for exactly this job and never meant to."

* * *

The seam came sooner than the map promised, or perhaps the map had redrawn itself while we walked; when I checked the vellum, the spiral had become a straight descent, and beneath the words *hold my hand here* new writing had appeared: *almost. don't sing along.*

"Don't sing along," I read aloud.

"Why would we sing along?" Odile asked, and then we both heard it, rising through the rock like water through chalk: voices. Many voices, braided, beautiful, and wrong — wrong the way a mirror is wrong, every note perfect and slightly delayed from itself, a choir singing in a place where sound shouldn't travel. The crooked choir. The sold souls. And underneath the harmony, faint as a watermark, a voice I knew.

My own.

I had never heard myself sing. I don't sing; singing is a thing you do when you believe you'll still be yourself tomorrow. But I knew it the way you know your face in glass — recognition arriving before permission. Somewhere below, my soul was holding a note, and it sounded tired.

Odile's hand found my arm. "Isandro. Your nose."

I wiped it. Blood, bright on my fingers. The gift straining against distance, against rock, against the sheer weight of a queen's claim. I sat down on the step, breathed, and redrew the last stretch of the map freehand — no listening now, no asking the dark what it wanted to be. This part I decided. A short passage, a gate, a border. I drew the gate as a crack, thin as a knife's edge, because I didn't want to give the mountain anything generous.

The wall ahead of us split along a line that hadn't existed a moment before, and cold air poured through, smelling of mirrors and deep water.

Beyond it: lamplight. Marble. The distant, patient harmonies.

Odile shuttered the lantern until only a thread of light remained. "Last chance for sensible theft," she murmured. "There's a jeweler on Copperlane whose back door I could open with a compliment."

"Odile."

"Yes?"

"Thank you for coming. Whatever happens down there — thank you."

She bumped her shoulder against mine, brief and hard, which from her was an embrace, an oath, and a threat all at once. "Don't get sentimental. It slows the getaway." Then she slipped through the crack in the world, quick as a pick in a lock, and I folded the map, tucked it against my heart where maps belong, and followed.

The gate sealed behind us with a sound like a book closing.

And from somewhere ahead, down a corridor hung with mirrors that showed us walking toward ourselves, the crooked choir changed key — and began, sweetly, deliberately, to sing my name.
