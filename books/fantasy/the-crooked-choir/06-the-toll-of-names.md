## 6. The Toll of Names

There are three rules to thieving that I learned before I learned my letters, and the third one is: never pay a debt you can walk away from.

I was breaking all three by breakfast.

The first rule — know what you're stealing before you steal it — I'd bent as far as it would go. What we were stealing was a soul, technically mine-adjacent, belonging to a cartographer who had spent the entire descent down the Unmapped Stair looking like someone reading their own obituary and taking notes. The second rule — never steal anything with a name attached — well. Souls are mostly name, when you get down to it. And the third rule I've already told you about.

"Odile." Isandro's voice came back along the stair, hushed in that way people think is quiet but is actually just tense. "You're counting your steps again."

"I'm not counting my steps."

"You're mouthing numbers."

"I'm praying," I said. "It looks similar. Fewer saints involved."

They almost smiled. That was the thing about Isandro Okonkwo that nobody at the guild ever noticed, because nobody ever looked: they were funny. Dry as old vellum and twice as likely to crumble if you handled them wrong, but funny. You don't spend years being invisible without developing either humor or hatred, and Isandro had chosen the cheaper option.

We had been walking down for six hours. Not stairs exactly — the Unmapped Stair was more of a suggestion of descent, a passage that existed because Isandro had drawn it into being somewhere around hour two, ink still drying on the air as we stepped through it. I'd watched them do it. Watched them hold out one hand like they were steadying themselves against a wall that wasn't there yet, and then the wall *was* there, and then there were steps, worn stone and moss and the smell of deep places, unspooling ahead of us out of nothing.

I owed them a debt, which is why I was here. The debt was this: three years ago I stole something from the guild's vaults that belonged to them — a bottled lullaby, one of the small tradeable griefs, and I sold it to buy my way out of a contract I'd signed while drunk and desperate. I never told them whose lullaby it was or why I needed the coin so badly. They never asked. They just said, when the crown's proclamation went up in red wax and half the guild started sharpening its knives against its own members: *"You owe me. Come underground with me and we'll call it even."*

Cheapest rescue I ever performed. Most expensive, too, depending on how tonight went.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the under-court: it isn't beneath the city the way a cellar is beneath a house. It's beneath the city the way a debt is beneath a friendship. You can live your whole life on top of it, cheerful and solvent-seeming, and never once feel the weight — until the day the weight decides it's tired of waiting.

Isandro felt it first. Of course they did. They stopped on the stair with one hand pressed flat against the living stone and said, "We're close. It knows we're coming."

"It knows?"

"The mountain keeps accounts." They said it lightly, but their hand stayed pressed to the rock a moment too long, the way you keep your hand on a door you're not sure you want closed. "Everything that passes gets entered. Everything entered gets owned, eventually. That's the whole architecture of the place. The palace is just where they keep the paperwork."

"Wonderful. A bureaucracy with teeth."

"A bureaucracy is only teeth with better handwriting."

I laughed, and the laugh echoed wrong — came back to us a half-beat late, like the stair was considering whether to return it. Isandro didn't laugh at all. They were looking at the space ahead of us, where the steps ran out into darkness, and I watched them do the thing I'd seen them do once before through a cracked door in the guild's back rooms: the small, involuntary movement of their fingers, sketching. Mapping what wasn't there yet.

A doorway drew itself out of the dark. Arched, ancient-looking, though I'd have sworn on my mother's grave — which is empty, long story — that nothing had been there a moment before.

"You could have led with this," I said. "The whole gift. The drawing-doors-out-of-night thing. Do you know how many locks I've picked in my life? With *pins*? Like an animal?"

"If I'd led with it, I'd have been dead by twelve." Isandro stepped through the arch they'd made, and their voice drifted back, stripped of all its dryness. "The crown hunts mappers now, Odile. They hunted us before there was a proclamation. The guild just finally wrote it down."

That shut me up. There's a particular silence that follows a sentence like that, and I walked through it, and through the door, and into the borderlands of the under-court.

* * *

The border wasn't a wall. It was a threshold of light — or rather, of the absence of any light that had ever been outside. Above ground, dark is just light that's been turned off. Down here, the dark was load-bearing. It held up the ceiling of the world, and the ceiling was carved, though carving isn't the right word, because the shapes up there moved. Slowly. Like the mountain was dreaming and we were walking through the dream's furniture.

And between us and whatever came next stood the gatekeeper.

I want to describe him properly, because later — much later, when I tell this story in taverns for drinks I no longer have to steal — people always ask about him, and I always undersell him, because the truth sounds like madness. He was tall the way a column is tall: functional, impersonal, grown rather than built. His face was smooth as an unwritten page. No eyes that I could find, and I am very good at finding eyes; it's a professional habit. Where his mouth should have been there was a seam, thin and patient.

He did not move when we approached. He did not need to. The seam opened.

*"Toll,"* he said, and the word came out like something that had been said ten thousand times and would be said ten thousand more, and every single time it would be true.

Isandro stepped forward. "What's the toll?"

*"A name."*

"We don't have—"

*"A true name,"* the gatekeeper said, with the mild inexorability of a scale settling. *"Not the one you wear. The one you are. Names are the only coin the court accepts. Everything else is counterfeit."*

Beside me, Isandro went very still. I knew why. I'd pieced together enough on the stair — the missing ledger pages, the entry in a dead woman's hand, the word *sold* written in a child's price column. Isandro Okonkwo's true name wasn't theirs anymore. It sat in a vault beneath this mountain, filed and bound and singing, probably, in some crooked celestial register. Offering it here would be like handing a thief your house key while standing inside the house.

Which left me.

Now. Let me tell you about names, since we're on the subject, and since I have been avoiding the subject for the length of this descent and can avoid it no longer, because the gatekeeper's blank face was turning toward me with the slow certainty of weather.

My name is Odile. That's the one I wear. It's real enough — I earned it, or stole it, which in my profession amounts to the same thing. But it's not the first name I had, and it's not the name that matters, and it is absolutely not the name the gatekeeper wanted.

The name that matters belonged to my brother.

His name was Tam. Tam Ashgrove — yes, Ashgrove, I kept the surname when I shed everything else, because you have to keep *something* or the thieves' guilds won't believe you've stolen anything worth having. Tam was older than me by four years and wiser than me by forty, which is the standard ratio for brothers. He taught me locks. He taught me which guards could be bribed and which could only be outrun. He taught me that the second rule of thieving is never steal anything with a name attached, and then he broke his own rule, because he loved someone with a name attached to a debt, and debts under this city travel downward the way water does.

Tam died paying someone else's toll. That's the short version. The long version takes a bottle and a locked door and I don't do the long version anymore. The relevant part is this: when he died, his name didn't die with him. Names don't work that way down here. A name goes where it's sent, and Tam's name was sent nowhere, and so it has been sitting inside me for six years like a coin sewn into a coat lining — useless above ground, weighing nothing and everything, carried because putting it down felt like the one theft I couldn't commit.

The gatekeeper's faceless head inclined toward me. Patient. The mountain dreaming overhead shifted its weight.

*"Yours?"*

"No," I said.

*"Then whose?"*

And here is the moment I want to lie to you about, later, in the taverns. Here is the moment I will dress up in better words, give myself a speech, make it sound like strategy. Because what actually happened was not strategy. What actually happened was that I heard Isandro breathing beside me — quick and shallow, a person standing at the edge of losing the last thing they had — and I thought: *not this one. I've already cost this one a lullaby. I'm not costing them a soul.*

And I reached into the lining of myself, into that six-year seam, and took out my brother's name.

"Tam," I said. "Tam Ashgrove. That's the toll. That's a true name — truer than anything else I'm carrying. Take it."

The gatekeeper went still in a new way. Even mountains, I think, know grief when it's offered over a counter.

*"This name is not yours to pay,"* he said. Which was, in its terrible bureaucratic way, the kindest thing anyone had said to me in six years. Someone, somewhere, in all the machinery of the under-court, had noticed that a dead man's name was not currency. Had noticed it was a person.

"It's the only thing I have that's real," I said. "And I'm told everything else is counterfeit. So."

*"If you pay with it, it is spent. It does not return. Whatever he was to you—"*

"He was my brother," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked down the middle like cheap ice. "Whatever he was to me, he's been dead six years, and his name has been rattling around in my chest keeping me awake, and I am *tired*, gatekeeper. I am so tired of carrying the only honest thing I ever owned. Take it. Spend it. Let it buy something. He'd have wanted — "

I stopped. Because he wouldn't have. Tam would have taken my face in his hands and said *Odile, you absolute disaster, never spend the real things,* and he'd have been right, and I was going to do it anyway, because grief is the only currency that appreciates, and I was rich beyond measure and bankrupt in every other denomination.

The seam that served him for a mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

*"Paid,"* he said.

And it left me.

I don't know how else to describe it. It was like setting down a pack you'd forgotten you were wearing — except the pack was made of mornings, of a boy teaching a girl to pick her first lock, of *watch the tension in the pins, little owl, listen for the give* — and the absence where it had been was enormous and cold and, God help me, light. I staggered. Isandro caught my elbow. Their grip was careful, the grip of someone who handles fragile things professionally.

"Odile." Quietly. "You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did." I straightened. My eyes were wet and I let them be, because the gatekeeper had no eyes to see them with, and Isandro — Isandro had earned the sight of them. "That's the trick nobody tells you about grief. Everyone says you should put it down, set it free, move on. Nobody tells you it's also *collateral*. Six years I kept his name safe because keeping it was the last job he ever gave me. Well. Job's done. Spent it on something worthy."

"What's it bought?"

I looked past the gatekeeper, past the threshold, into the deep of the under-court, where far below — so far it might have been imagination, except neither of us believed in coincidence anymore — something was singing. Many voices. Crooked, off-key, beautiful in the way broken things are beautiful, like a choir rehearsing in a hall with no ceiling.

"Passage," I said. "And maybe a way in."

The gatekeeper stepped aside. Columns, I remembered, only move when the building requires it. As we passed him, his blank face dipped toward me — toward me specifically, not Isandro, though Isandro was the one with the famous gift — and the seam parted one last time.

*"The name sings well,"* he said. *"It will be heard."*

I didn't ask what that meant. Some doors you don't open until you're standing in front of them with no other exit.

* * *

We descended into the proper dark after that, and the stair Isandro drew for us grew stranger — walls that sweated candlelight, ceilings hung with keys that turned slowly in locks made of air. Isandro mapped as we went, fingers moving, mouth moving, mouthing numbers or prayers or coordinates, I honestly couldn't tell anymore, and I found I liked not knowing. There are worse things than following someone who draws the road out of nothing. Being the one who has to draw it, for instance.

"Odile." Isandro's voice, low. We'd stopped on a landing where the dark pooled like spilled ink. "What you gave back there. I need you to understand what it may have cost."

"It cost a dead man a name. He wasn't using it."

"That's not — " They exhaled. In the candle-sweat light their face looked younger and older simultaneously, which I'm told is what happens when cartographers stop hiding. "Names paid into the court don't vanish. They're *filed*. And filed things in the under-court get used. Sung. Entered into the choir."

The distant singing swelled, just slightly, as if it had heard itself mentioned.

"So he's in there," I said. "Maybe. Possibly. A piece of him."

"Maybe. Possibly." Isandro's hand found my shoulder — the same careful grip. "I'm sorry. I mapped us the safest road I could find, and the safest road still ran through a tollbooth."

"Isandro." I said their name the way you set something down gently. "You spent twenty years pretending you weren't a mapmaker so the guild wouldn't sell you. I spent six years pretending I wasn't grieving so the city wouldn't notice. We're done pretending, both of us, starting now. Whatever's down there — whoever's down there — we look at it. That was the deal. Debt paid, remember? Except I find I'm not done. Call it interest."

Below us, faint and rising, the crooked choir changed verses. And among the many voices — threaded through them, unmistakable, impossible — I heard a voice I knew. Not Tam's. Something else. A voice I'd heard through cracked doors and across ledger desks for years, dry and careful and always pitched to be forgotten.

Isandro heard it too. I watched them hear it. Watched them go white as fresh paper in the false candlelight, one hand flying to their own throat as if to check that the voice hadn't already left.

"That's mine," they whispered. "Odile. That's *my voice*. It's already down here."

The stair beneath our feet shivered — the mountain, dreaming, turning over — and somewhere below, the choir began to sing a song I realized, with a thief's cold clarity, was a map.
