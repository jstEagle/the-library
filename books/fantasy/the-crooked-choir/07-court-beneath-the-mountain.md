## 7. Court Beneath the Mountain

There is a particular quality to walking through a passage that should not exist. It's like being a rumor about yourself. Every step confirms something the world insists is impossible, and by the tenth step you stop apologizing for it.

I had mapped us down. That was the only way to say it. I had sat in the dark of Odile's borrowed cellar with a stub of charcoal and a sheet of vellum, closed my eyes, and drawn the way beneath the city — not from memory, not from any guild archive, but from the strange second sight I'd spent thirty years pretending was indigestion. The lines had come out of my hand the way water comes out of a cracked jug: messily, inevitably, and all over everything.

Odile had watched me do it with an expression I'd never seen on her before. Not awe. Awe is cheap; thieves are professionally immune to it. What she wore was closer to recognition — the look of someone watching a dog stand up and ask politely to be let out of the house.

"You've been able to do this *how long*?" she'd asked.

"Since I was six," I said. "I thought everyone could. Then I thought no one could, and saying otherwise got people sold, so." I'd shrugged, which was a lie of a shrug, because nothing about it was casual. "It's been a busy week of finding out."

Now we walked the line I'd drawn, and the line held, which frightened me more than if it hadn't. A map that works is a confession. Somewhere under four districts and a river and the accumulated weight of the city's respectable lies, the mountain court opened before us like a mouth deciding whether to swallow.

* * *

The stair ended at a threshold made of salt and old bargains. I know that sounds like poetry. It isn't. It was literally a line of grey crystals set into black stone, and when Odile reached across it her hand came back looking slightly wrong — younger at the knuckles, older at the fingertips, as though the border couldn't decide what age of her to admit.

"Don't touch anything," I said, mostly to myself.

"I never touch anything," Odile said. "I borrow it briefly and against its will."

The gatekeeper — because of course there was one, there is always one, the universe employs more door wardens than it does honest carpenters — did not demand our names here. That toll had apparently been paid at the outer gate, where Odile had given up her dead brother's name with a steadiness that still made my chest hurt to think about. This one merely looked at us. He was tall in the way furniture is tall: functional, unlovely, immovable. His face was polished stone, and in its shine I caught our reflections, and something about the reflection was off in a way I couldn't name yet.

I would name it soon enough. I would have given a great deal not to.

"Cartographer," he said. My title, not my name. The word went through me like a needle finding the gap between ribs. "She has been expecting a mapmaker for some time."

"She can keep expecting," I said. "We're just passing through."

"No one passes through," he said, without malice, the way you'd say *water is wet*. "You may pass *to*."

Odile muttered something under her breath that I chose, for morale purposes, not to hear. We passed to.

* * *

I had imagined the under-court as a cave. Caves are what mountains offer when they're being honest. What we got instead was a palace, and what a palace — light spilled upward from sources I refused to examine too closely, catching on walls that were not walls but mirrors, floor to vaulted ceiling, thousands of them, none of them quite flat. Each one showed the corridor accurately except for one detail apiece. In one mirror my coat was red. In another, Odile smiled. In a third, I was alone.

"That last one's just accurate," Odile said quietly. "That's how you've looked for thirty years."

"Thank you for the emotional support. It's why I brought you."

"It's why you brought me? I thought it was the debt."

"The debt is the official reason. There's paperwork."

The sound reached us before the sight did, which is the way of music and of disasters. Singing. Dozens of voices, maybe hundreds, threading together in a harmony that kept almost resolving and then deliberately, cruelly, refusing to — a chord that leaned toward beauty and swerved away at the last instant, over and over, like a drunk trying to walk a chalk line. It should have been ugly. It was nearly unbearable instead, which is worse, because unbearable things get inside you and stay.

"The crooked choir," I said. Vess had named it once, years ago, in the tone other people use for weather. I'd assumed it was a guild fable, the kind of story told to apprentices to keep them from asking about the third cellar. Fables, I have learned, are just histories that someone paid to bury.

We followed the sound down a corridor of mirrors that showed us versions of ourselves I liked progressively less, until the corridor opened into the great hall of the Mountain Queen's court.

* * *

The hall was a bowl of black stone, and the choir stood in tiers along its curved wall, and every singer was translucent.

Not ghostly — I want to be precise, because precision was the only weapon I had left at that point. Ghosts are absence wearing the shape of presence. These were presences wearing the shape of almost-presence. You could see the risers through their torsos. Their faces were vivid and specific and heartbreakingly ordinary: a woman with flour still dusting her forearms, a boy of perhaps twelve holding his own hands like they were borrowed, an old man whose mouth moved around the notes with the practiced ease of someone who had sung his whole life and simply never expected the singing to be taken as payment.

They sang, and each note left them fainter. I watched it happen. A verse would end, and the flour-woman would be a shade less solid, the way a coal dims between bellows-breaths. And then the next verse would begin, and she would sing it anyway, and I understood with a lurch that she sang it *gladly*, or as near to gladly as compulsion can be dressed, because while the song took her memories it also held her together. Stop singing, stop existing. Keep singing, stop remembering. Those were the options on offer. I have seen bad ledgers before. I had never seen one written directly into throats.

"They're paying themselves out," Odile whispered. "Note by note."

"Every song is a withdrawal," I said. My voice came out flat, which is what my voice does when the alternative is screaming. "No deposits. No interest. The worst account I've ever audited."

At the hall's center, on a dais of mirrored glass, sat the Mountain Queen.

I will describe her badly, because describing her well felt like doing her work for her. She was beautiful the way an audit is beautiful: everything balanced, nothing warm. Her gown was the grey of ledger paper. Her crown was a circlet of small keys. When she turned her head, the thousand mirrors turned with her, so that wherever you looked, she was already looking back, and I realized the mirrors weren't decoration. They were witnesses. Everything in this place was witnessed by itself, endlessly, so that no transaction could ever be claimed to have happened otherwise than as recorded.

A queen who had made reality into a notarized document. I hated her instantly and professionally.

And then the choir changed songs, and I stopped hating anything, because I stopped being able to think at all.

* * *

The new melody began low, in the men's voices, a descending line like stairs going down into a cellar. And above it, entering on the fourth bar, came a voice I knew.

I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Not because it's distinctive — plenty of people write a similar hand — but because you've lived inside it. You know where it hesitates. You know which words it leans on.

The voice came from a singer in the third tier, slight, dark-haired, translucent as the rest. And it was my voice. Not similar. Mine. The exact instrument I'd been given at birth, singing a part I had never learned, in a hall beneath the world, while I stood in the audience of my own throat.

The horror of recognition is a specific horror. It isn't fear. Fear is for tigers and tax collectors. This is older and colder: the vertigo of seeing yourself from outside and realizing the outside version has been living its own life without consulting you. I gripped the rail. The rail was warm, which somehow made it worse — even the architecture here had a pulse.

"That's—" Odile started.

"I know," I said.

"Isandro, that's—"

"*I know.*"

The voice sang on, and I listened to myself the way you'd listen to a stranger read your private letters aloud in a market square. The song was in no language I recognized, but songs don't need permission to mean things. This one meant *loss*. It meant loss the way a flood means wet. Each repetition of the refrain shaved something off the singer, and I found myself flinching at each shave, some animal part of me insisting that whatever was being pared away from that translucent figure was being pared from me, and maybe it was. Maybe that was the trick of it. Maybe the soul doesn't care which copy sings.

Or maybe — and this thought arrived like ice water — maybe it wasn't a copy. Maybe souls don't duplicate. Maybe what I was hearing was the original, the true article, the thing my mother had signed away in handwriting I wouldn't discover for another few chapters of my life, and everything I'd ever called *myself* was the echo, the working draft, the cartographer's sketch of a soul that hung framed in a queen's hall.

Which one of us was the map, and which the territory?

The flour-woman beside my voice faltered on a high note. Just a half-beat. But the Queen's head turned — all thousand reflections of her head turned — and the faltering stopped, and the woman sang on, and two verses later I noticed she no longer had flour on her arms. Some memory of a bakery, gone. Paid out. Spent on a chord.

Nobody in the audience reacted. That was the detail that finally made my stomach turn over properly. The court — courtiers of smoked glass and bone, functionaries with faces like canceled stamps — sat and listened to human beings erase themselves, and they *applauded between movements*, gently, the way you applaud at a recital where your neighbor's child is performing.

"They don't know," Odile breathed. "Or they don't care."

"Worse. They know exactly what it is. That's the product." I heard my own voice go cold and clerical, the guild voice, the ledger voice, and I despised it and used it anyway because it was the only thing keeping me upright. "This is what we brokered, Odile. Upstairs. All those years. Bottled lullabies, traded shadows, 'small debts.' We were the front office. This is the back room."

"You didn't know."

"I balanced the accounts." My knuckles had gone white on the rail. "You can balance an account without knowing what's in it, but you can't balance it *innocently*. Not forever. Interest accrues. I said that once, didn't I. To myself. In the dark. I thought I was being dramatic."

Below, the Queen raised one hand, and the choir — my voice among it — fell silent mid-phrase. The silence rang worse than the song. A hundred translucent faces turned toward the dais, waiting, docile, emptying.

Then, in the hush, my voice spoke. Alone. Out of the third tier, out of my own mouth's twin:

"There you are."

* * *

The hall did not react. The courtiers did not turn. Whatever protocol governed this place, a soul addressing its owner was evidently either forbidden or routine, and I genuinely could not tell which, and both possibilities were appalling.

I descended the steps. Odile hissed something about plans and protocols and the debt she owed me not covering funerals, but she followed, because Odile follows, it's her single most reliable vice.

Up close, the singers were worse. Translucence at a distance reads as magic. At arm's length it reads as illness. I could see the risers through the flour-woman's chest, see the faint fossil-shadows of things inside her that might have been organs or might have been memories, rendered in the same fading ink. And I could see the one with my face — my face, thinner, worn smoother by whatever years pass underground, but mine, unmistakably, the way a signature is unmistakable even when the hand that wrote it is tired.

"What are you?" I asked, and my voice cracked on the second word, ruining the interrogation entirely.

"I'm what you haven't missed yet," it said, with my dryness, my cadence, the little downward inflection I use when I'm making a joke to avoid making a point. "You sing very poorly, by the way. Up there. All those years of humming over the ledgers. I heard every note. I want you to know the acoustics in your skull are terrible."

"This isn't funny."

"No," it agreed, and the joke drained out of my own face and left something naked behind. "It isn't. It's the least funny thing there is. Every verse, I lose a room of the house. Last month I lost the smell of rain on a hot street. Yesterday I lost whoever taught me to whistle. I know I had a teacher. I can feel the shape of the hole." Its eyes — my eyes — fixed on mine. "Do you know who sold me? Sold *us*?"

The question landed in the middle of everything I didn't know yet, in that tender unwritten country ahead of me where a scribe was dying and a truth was waiting and my mother's handwriting sat on a page like a knife laid handle-outward. I knew nothing. I said, "No. But I'm going to find out."

"You say that like a man balancing a ledger," it said. "Entries in, entries out. It doesn't balance, Isandro. Nothing down here balances. That's why the choir is crooked. We're all the rounding errors."

From the dais, the Queen's voice arrived without traveling — it was simply *in* the hall, the way a stamp is in wax. "My cartographer," she said, and the endearment was a lien. "Come. Let us discuss what you are owed."

Odile's hand found my sleeve. Below us, a hundred fading souls resumed their song, and my own voice rose with theirs, and the terrible crooked harmony wound up again toward a beauty it refused to reach.

I had come to steal back a soul. Standing there, I understood for the first time that I didn't know which one was mine — the one singing, or the one listening — and that until I found out, every step deeper was a wager with no stake I could name.

The Queen beckoned. The mirrors all leaned in.

I went down.
