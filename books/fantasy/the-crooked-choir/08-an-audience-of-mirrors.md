## 8. An Audience of Mirrors

The Queen's herald had a voice like a struck bell, and he used it the way other men used knives—precisely, and only when something was about to end.

"Isandro Okonkwo," he said, and my name rang down the mirrored corridor ahead of us like a coin dropped into a well. "The Mountain Queen will see you now."

I had been in the under-court for less than a day, and already it knew my name better than I did. That was the thing about this place. Above ground, I had spent forty years making myself small, folding my gift flat and tucking it into ledgers where no one would look. Here, everything I hid was laid out on display, polished, and hung at eye level.

Odile walked beside me, her hands loose at her sides in the way she pretended was casual and I knew was readiness. She'd paid her brother's name at the gate. It was still ringing in her, I could tell—the way grief rings in a person after they've spent it, hollowed out and echoing.

"You don't have to do this," she murmured as we walked.

"I know." I didn't look at her. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The throne room was not a room so much as an argument that mirrors had won.

They lined every wall, floor to vaulted ceiling, hundreds of them, none quite true. Some showed you older. Some showed you the child you'd been. One, I was almost certain, showed the person you might have become if you'd never learned to be afraid—and I caught myself glancing at it twice before I made myself stop. The ceiling was black stone shot through with veins of faint gold light, pulsing slow, like the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping overhead. Which, given that we were inside a mountain, was probably exactly what it was.

And between the mirrors, on low stone daises arranged in a crooked crescent, sat the choir.

I had heard them from the outer corridors—a sound like rain falling upward, like a hymn remembered wrong. Up close it was worse and more beautiful both. Dozens of souls, or what remained of them, singing in rounds that never resolved. Their faces were calm in the way of people who have stopped expecting anything. Some were old. Some had died young and gone on aging anyway, because time does strange bookkeeping underground. And one of them—

One of them had my voice.

I had found it last night, standing in the corridor outside this room, listening to the choir rehearse. A single thread of song rising above the others, and it was mine. Not similar. Mine. The particular grain of it, the way I flattened certain vowels, the little hitch I got when I sang anything in a minor key because my mother used to sing those songs and I could never finish them.

She was singing one now. The woman with my voice. Middle-aged, gray coming in at the temples, hands folded in her lap like a scribe between entries. When she reached the hitch in the melody—the hitch, *my* hitch—she faltered, recovered, kept going.

I stood there in the Queen's audience chamber and watched a stranger wear my voice like a borrowed coat, and I understood, finally, what kind of place this was. Not a court. A ledger. Every soul here was an entry, and the Queen was the ink.

"The cartographer."

Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which is what happens when a room is all mirrors. I turned toward the dais at the crescent's center and found her: a tall figure seated on a throne of fused looking-glass, wearing a gown that seemed cut from the same material, so that it was hard to say where she ended and her seat began. Her face was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful—you could admire it, but you wouldn't want to fall through.

She didn't rise. Queens who rise want something; queens who stay seated already own it.

"You heard your voice among my choir," she said. "Don't trouble yourself to deny it. I watched you flinch."

"It's a good voice," I said. Wryness is armor; I've worn it so long I sometimes forget it comes off. "Whoever's using it should pay rent."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Amusement, possibly. Or the memory of it.

"Honesty," she said. "How refreshing. My court lies so beautifully, so constantly, that truth has become a novelty act." She gestured, one long hand unfolding. "Sit, cartographer. This is not an interrogation. Interrogations are for people I intend to break. You I intend to *use*, and use deserves courtesy."

A chair slid across the mirror floor to meet me—I didn't see what moved it, and decided not to ask—and I sat, because refusing a chair in a room full of mirrors felt like a statement I wasn't ready to make. Odile stayed standing behind me. I felt her there like a hand between my shoulder blades.

"Do you know why your guild survives?" the Queen asked.

"Dragon-fire," I said. "Old bargain. Names protected while it burns."

"Mmm. And do you know what the fire buys them? Nothing. It buys them *time*. Time is the only currency that always devalues." She leaned forward, and the mirrors around her leaned too, a hundred reflections tilting in unison, which is a sight I will be describing inaccurately to people for the rest of my life. "Your guildmaster knows the fire gutters. She has known for years. She burns ledgers and calls it protection, but burning a ledger doesn't free a soul, cartographer. It just makes the debt harder to audit."

"I've read the ledgers," I said. "I kept them."

"Then you know they're honest in the way prisons are honest. Everything recorded, nothing forgiven." She studied me, and being studied by her felt like being appraised by an appraiser—she was checking for flaws, provenance, whether I'd been repaired. "You balanced their accounts for decades. Shadows traded by weight. Lullabies bottled and sold to insomniac nobles. Songs that shouldn't exist, catalogued and shelved. And every night you went home and drew maps of places that don't exist, and hid them under your floorboards, and told no one."

My spine went cold. "How—"

"How do you think your mother found the mountain court, Isandro Okonkwo?" The Queen let my name sit in the air between us, unhurried. "She followed a map. Yours. The ones you drew as a child, before you learned shame. She traced your hand across the unmapped dark and she came here, and she made a trade, and the trade was *you*. Or rather—" she tilted her head, "—the trade was supposed to be you. But that's a story for another audience."

Everything in me wanted to demand the rest of that story now. Everything wiser in me knew that wanting things in front of this Queen was how she harvested you. I folded my hands and waited, the way I'd waited through forty years of guild meetings where everyone spoke around the truth.

"You hate that word," she observed. "*Wait.*"

"I'm a cartographer," I said. "Waiting is just drawing slowly."

"And that is precisely why I summoned you instead of simply taking you." She rose, then—finally—and the mirrors rose with her, and for one vertiginous moment the whole room was a single standing figure multiplied past counting. "Your gift is wasted on the guild. They use you to file receipts. I am offering you the largest blank space any mapmaker has ever been offered."

She descended the dais steps, and the choir's song shifted beneath her, some instinctive deference written into music that had forgotten its own words.

"The realms beyond the mountain," she said. "The drowned kingdoms. The cities that fold themselves away when armies come. The countries that exist only on Tuesdays. Every realm your guild pretends doesn't exist because existing would mean obligations." She stopped three paces from me, close enough that I could see my own reflection repeated in the facets of her gown—dozens of small Isandros, all watching me decide. "Map them for me. All of them. Name their rivers, chart their passes, mark where their walls stand and where they've rotted. No one has ever valued what you can do. Your guild hid you. Your mother sold you. Your crown hunts you. I am the first power in your long, careful, invisible life to look at your gift and say: *this is worth a kingdom.*"

And here is the thing I will not pretend, even now, even in whatever account of this eventually gets written: for three full seconds, I wanted it.

Not the conquest. I want to be clear that the conquest part of the offer arrived in my heart pre-rejected, the way spoiled food arrives pre-smelling. But the rest of it—the *being seen* of it—went through me like brandy. Forty years of hiding. Forty years of drawing worlds under floorboards and burning the best ones so no one would find them. Forty years of colleagues who knew me as "the quiet one who does the books," and a crown that wanted me dead for the same reason the Queen wanted me crowned: because mapping what isn't there is power, and power terrifies the small and tempts the large.

Someone, finally, was saying out loud what I had always known alone in the dark: that what I could do mattered. That it was worth naming. Worth wages. Worth a throne room.

Three seconds. Maybe four. Long enough for the Queen to see it happen—she saw everything, that was rather her condition—and smile the way winter smiles at the first green shoot, not cruelly, just with confidence.

"There it is," she said softly. "There's the hunger. Don't be ashamed of it. Hunger is just proof you were paying attention."

"I'm not ashamed," I said, and found, to my surprise, that it was mostly true. "I'm *accounting*. There's a difference. In my experience, offers that arrive pre-valued usually have the valuation hidden somewhere unpleasant. What's the price?"

"No price. A salary."

"No one gives a salary without a ledger entry."

"Then consider it an inheritance," she said, and the word landed in my chest like a dropped anvil, because she knew—of course she knew—what inheritance meant to someone whose entire existence had begun as a transaction. "You would want for nothing. You would be *named*. Court Cartographer. Your gift spoken aloud in open session, admired, funded, feared. And in exchange, all I ask is the work itself. Maps. Only maps."

"Maps of places so you can take them."

"I am a queen," she said, with the mild patience of someone explaining weather. "Taking things is the job. You would not be conquering anyone. You would be *drawing*. There is a difference, and I suspect a man of ledgers can appreciate it. The knife and the inventory are different professions, however often they share a warehouse."

"That's a very comfortable distinction."

"I can afford comfort. Can you?" She turned, gowned in mirrors, and swept one hand toward the choir. "Look at them. Look at what happens to gifts that go unwitnessed. They get taken anyway—by whoever is strongest—but *crooked*. Broken, bent, sung out of tune, spent on someone else's glory. Your guild let the crown hunt its mappers rather than defend them. Your mother gave you away rather than raise you visible. Everyone who ever 'protected' you did it by erasing you. I am offering the opposite, cartographer. I am offering you *erased never again.*"

The choir swelled behind her, and my own voice rode the top of it, singing a song I half-remembered from a kitchen I couldn't picture anymore, and I felt the trap close around the offer's beauty: she was right. About all of it. That was the horror of her. Vess hid me out of fear dressed as care. My mother sold me—or so the ledger said—out of desperation dressed as love. And this Queen, this frozen-lake queen with her choir of the erased, was the only one offering to let me be *whole* in public.

It would have been so easy to say yes. To draw her the Tuesday countries, the folded cities, the drowned kings' roads. To tell myself the maps were just maps, that ink isn't complicit, that a knife and an inventory really are different trades. To spend the rest of my life admired, named, warm, fed, and quietly, comfortably damned.

Behind me, Odile shifted her weight. I didn't hear her say anything. I didn't need to. She'd paid a dead man's name to walk through that gate, and people who spend grief like currency develop a nose for bad exchange rates.

"Let me consider it properly," I said.

The Queen's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Cartographers deliberate. I admire that. How long?"

"A day."

"A day." She considered me, and I had the distinct feeling of being weighed against something I couldn't see—some future version of myself, perhaps, the one in the mirror that showed who I'd be if I'd never learned fear. "Very well. One day. You will be housed in the east gallery, comfortably, and guarded gently. At the end of it, you will give me your answer, and either way, you will give me something else first."

"Which is?"

"A page." She smiled, and the smile had edges. "One page from your own hand. A map of this court, drawn as only you can draw it—not the corridors, cartographer. The *bindings*. Where each soul in my choir is tied, and how, and to what. Call it... an audition piece. If you will serve me, I must know you can chart what matters."

"And if I refuse the page?"

"Then you have answered the larger question early, and we proceed to the less pleasant portion of the evening." She resumed her throne without hurry, settling back into the mirrors until she was more reflection than woman again. "You may go. Think carefully, Isandro Okonkwo. You are the first person in a very long time to be offered a future in this room rather than a fate. Try to appreciate the novelty."

The herald's bell-voice rang us out. As we crossed the mirror floor, I passed close to the choir's crescent, and the woman with my voice looked up—just once, just briefly, mid-verse—and our eyes met.

She didn't stop singing. But something in her face changed, the way a page changes when a reader finally opens it. Recognition, maybe. Or warning. Or a question she'd been holding in her throat for thirty years:

*Do you know who I am?*

I didn't. Not yet. But I intended to find out, and I suspected—walking out of that glittering argument of a room with Odile's steady presence at my back—that finding out was going to cost me exactly what the Queen's offer was designed to buy: the answer to who I was willing to be.

In the east gallery, behind a door that locked politely from the outside, Odile finally spoke.

"She offered you everything," she said.

"She offered me a salary," I said. "There's a difference, and I intend to itemize it."

Odile huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "For a second in there—for a second you wanted it. I saw it."

"For a second I wanted to be *seen*," I corrected. "The rest was packaging." I looked at the mirror across the cell-like comfort of the guest chamber, the one that showed me slightly older, slightly braver, slightly less tired. "But she asked for a page, Odile. A map of the bindings. And if I draw it honestly..."

"Then you'll know exactly where your soul is tied," Odile said. "And hers." She nodded toward the corridor, toward the choir, toward the woman with my voice. "Whatever you decide to do with a day, I'd spend it deciding who you're loyal to. Because that Queen doesn't collect answers, Isandro. She collects people who give them."

I sat down at the desk they'd provided—smooth stone, a lamp that burned without oil, paper of unreasonable quality—and I took out my pens, and I began, very slowly, to draw.

Not the map she'd asked for. Not yet.

First, the kitchen. The one I couldn't picture. I drew it from the hitch in a stranger's song, from thirty years of half-memories, from the shape of a lullaby in a minor key that I had never once been able to finish.

And as the lines went down—as the room assembled itself out of ink and grief and guesswork—the lamp flickered, and the mirror across from me fogged, and in the fog, faintly, in handwriting I recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded market—

Someone wrote back.
