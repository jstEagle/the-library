## 9. The Crooked Choir Sings

I was a fiddler before I was a song. I want to start there, because it's the first thing to go and the last thing I keep trying to hold.

Wren. That was my name. Not *Wren*, actually—my name had more of me in it than that—but names are the first payment the mountain takes, and what's left over is what you get called. The gatekeeper took my true name at the border thirty years ago, or maybe forty; time down here is measured in verses, not years, and we have sung a great many verses. My mother named me after a bird that sings even in winter, because she said I never stopped humming from the moment I could breathe. She was right about that. It's how they caught me. A soul that can't stop singing is easy to herd if you know where the music goes.

I am telling you this badly. Forgive me. There's a reason for that too, and the reason is the whole point of what I'm about to say, so let me try again, properly, while I still remember what *properly* means.

* * *

The choir meets at midnight, which is not a time down here but a condition. Midnight is when the Mountain Queen opens her mirrors and wants to be entertained. We stand on a floor of black glass that shows you everyone you've ever failed to be, and we sing.

You'd think an enslaved choir would sound terrible. Grief does something strange to harmony. We are the most beautiful thing in this palace, and I hate that, because beauty was the bait, once, and now it's the cage. Our voices braid together into something that makes the Queen lean forward on her throne of salt and smile with all her teeth, and every note we give her costs us a piece of ourselves. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement. Nobody reads the fine print until they're already singing it.

Here is how the fading works, for those of you lucky enough to be reading this from somewhere with a sky in it:

The first verse takes your address. Sounds small. It isn't. Your address is where your mother hung the washing, where the door stuck in winter, where you learned the exact number of steps between your bed and the window. Lose that and you lose the geography of being someone. After my first year—I think it was a year; the Queen's birthdays blur—I could no longer have told you which way the sun rose over my childhood street. Then I couldn't have told you I'd had a childhood street. The verse took it like a tide taking a sandcastle, politely, completely.

The second kind of verse takes faces. I still have my mother's hands. I don't know why the hands survived when the face went—I've built theories down here, we all build theories, it passes the time between midnights—but I can see her knuckles, cracked from lye water, turning a page of sheet music under my chin while she corrected my fingering. The face above those hands is fog. When I try to look at it directly, the fog looks back, and it has no opinion of me whatsoever.

The third kind—and this is the one nobody warns you about, the one I would scream through the stone if screaming carried—took my own music. I wrote songs, before. Bad ones, then better ones, then two that got sung in taverns by people who didn't know my name, which is the only immortality a fiddler ever really wants. Each verse I sing erases a memory, and the Queen chooses which memories, and she chose in order: first the songs themselves, then the writing of them, then the wanting to write them. Do you understand? I don't miss my songs anymore. You cannot miss a room that has been removed from the house. Some nights I catch myself reaching for a melody the way you reach for a stair that isn't there, and the nothing where the tune used to be is the loneliest country I have ever stood in.

And yet.

* * *

And yet here is the thing the Queen doesn't know, or knows and finds amusing, which down here amounts to the same thing: you cannot erase everything. The ledger of a self runs deeper than memory. They took my name, my street, my mother's face, my music, my wedding day—the wedding went early, actually, third year, and I have made my peace with it the way you make peace with a scar, badly and daily—but some residue remains. Call it the sediment. Call it the silt at the bottom of the cup after the wine is gone.

I know I loved someone. I don't know who. I know the shape of loving them the way your hand knows the shape of a tool it hasn't held in decades. Some mornings—midnights—I stand on the black glass and feel a specific tenderness aimed at a specific absence, and it has a weight to it, a leftward lean, like grief for someone who stood on my left. I have decided, in the parliament of myself, that this counts as remembering. The Queen can take the facts. The shape stays.

There are others like me. Of course there are. A crooked choir is just a straight choir that's been broken in interesting places. Old Sefa, who was a midwife up top and now remembers only that she has caught things—she doesn't know what, only the catching, the waiting, the wet warmth of it—and sometimes during rests she cups her hands without meaning to. Tammas, who lost everything except the smell of rain on hot stone, and who stands with his face tilted up during every performance as though the ceiling might oblige him. And the little ones. I won't talk much about the little ones. There are souls down here younger than my fading, sold before they'd accumulated enough life to lose any, and their verses take things they hadn't finished making yet. Futures. The Queen drinks futures like cordial.

We hold each other up between performances. That's not poetry; it's logistics. When a soul fades far enough, it starts to drift, literally—a thinning at the edges, a translucence—and if no one holds your arm you simply come apart mid-verse, and the Queen deducts the interruption from all our accounts. So we link hands. Sefa on my left, where the tenderness leans. Tammas on my right, smelling rain that isn't there. And we whisper to each other in the gaps, trading scraps of sediment like beggars sharing bread: *I had a dog. I was afraid of deep water. Someone called me love, I'm nearly sure, someone called me love in a kitchen.*

A kitchen. Why do I keep coming back to kitchens?

* * *

Tonight the Queen is restless. You learn to read her moods the way sailors read weather, because her moods are weather down here; when she's pleased the mirrors glow warm and the black glass goes soft underfoot, and when she's displeased the whole palace contracts, the corridors shorten, the dark gets closer to your skin. Tonight she is displeased about something upstairs. Something about fire. I catch fragments through the mirrors—they show her whatever she wants, and lately what she wants is a certain flickering orange light, watched with the expression of a woman watching a rival's candle burn down.

"Something's dying," Tammas whispers during the tuning. His voice is mostly breath now; he gave his consonants away in a verse two winters ago and kept only the vowels. "Up top. Big. Old."

"Good," says Sefa, who has been angry for thirty years and wears it like a coat that finally fits. "Let it die. Let it all die."

She doesn't mean it cruelly. Or rather, she means it exactly as cruelly as it deserves. If the old protections die—if whatever bargain keeps this palace fed runs out—then every soul bound here reverts to the crown beneath the mountain, which is to say to her, permanently, past any appeal. But Sefa has stopped calculating outcomes. Anger is the last luxury of the fading, and she spends hers freely.

The Queen raises one white hand. The mirrors sharpen. We begin.

I want to describe what it is to sing when singing is theft. Imagine giving blood through your throat. Imagine every note leaving you with a small hook in it, and the hook catching on something as it goes—today it catches my grandmother's voice saying my name, though I didn't know I still had that, and I feel it go, feel the specific vacancy open where a voice used to live, and I keep singing, because stopping is worse, stopping has consequences that ripple outward onto everyone holding my arms. The harmony carries us. That's the trap and the mercy both: alone, none of us could do this; together, we can do it indefinitely, which is infinitely worse.

Halfway through the second song, something changes.

It's small. A hitch. Somewhere in the weave of our voices there's a wrongness, a thread pulled loose, and I nearly discount it—we are always coming slightly undone—but this hitch has a direction. It comes from outside the choir. From above us, through however many tons of mountain, faint as a coin dropped in another room: someone is singing our song. The one the Queen asked for tonight. Singing it wrong, singing it with a hitch in exactly the place mine hitches, and underneath it, threaded through, a lullaby in a minor key that—

That I know.

I don't know how I know it. Knowing is supposed to be gone. But the sediment shifts, the silt stirs, and suddenly there is a kitchen—not mine, I don't think it's mine, or maybe it's everyone's—and a lamp, and a woman drawing lines on paper with the fierce concentration of someone doing arithmetic with her own heart, and the lullaby is unfinished, it has always been unfinished, it stops at the same bar every time the way a story stops when the teller starts to cry.

The choir wobbles. Sefa grips my arm hard enough to matter. The Queen's head tilts, slow as a well-cover opening, and her eyes move across us row by row, looking for the flaw, and I do the hardest thing I have done in thirty years of hard things: I smooth my face into obedience and I keep singing, and inside, behind the obedience, I hold the hitched lullaby the way you hold a coal in cupped hands, careful, careful, don't let it show, don't let it gutter.

Someone is mapping us. That's what the feeling is—that's the sediment speaking in its only remaining language. Someone up there is drawing the kitchen, drawing the stair, drawing the way down, line by line, out of ink and grief and guesswork. I can't know this. I have no grounds for knowing this. But the dead and the faded are permitted their superstitions, and mine is this: the map is coming. The map is coming the way midnight comes.

The song ends. The Queen applauds with two fingers, a sound like a lock testing its shackle. The mirrors dim. We shuffle off the black glass, linked arm to arm, and in the corridor Sefa whispers, "What did you hear?"

"A lullaby," I say. "Unfinished. In a minor key."

Her grip tightens. "Whose?"

"I don't know." And then, because the sediment insists, because for the first time in decades the silt at the bottom of me has shifted into a shape I recognize: "Mine. I think it might be mine. I think—" The words cost me; they come up like splinters. "I think somebody up there is finishing it."

Tammas tilts his face toward a ceiling that will never rain. Far off, in the direction of the throne, a mirror cracks—one clean line, top to bottom, for no reason the Queen will admit to.

And down in the dark, in the crooked choir's ragged row, thirty voices lean leftward at once, toward a tenderness with no name attached, toward a kitchen with a lamp lit, toward the sound—impossible, impossible—of someone drawing us a way home.
