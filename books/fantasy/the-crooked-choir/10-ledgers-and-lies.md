## 10. Ledgers and Lies

I have burned ledgers before. It is not the sort of thing one gets good at, exactly, but there is a technique to it, as there is to everything. You feed the fire slowly. You do not look at what you are feeding it. And you tell yourself a story while you work — that this is mercy, that this is protection, that ash is kinder than chains.

The story wears thin around the third hour.

By dawn the guild's back room smelled of scorched vellum and my own cowardice, which is a smell I have come to know intimately over forty years of stewardship. Half our records were gone. The shadow-contracts of three generations, the bottled lullabies' provenance, the debts owed to us and by us — all of it curling into grey moths on the hearth. What remained was what I judged safe. What remained was what I could use.

I want to be honest with someone, even if only in the privacy of my own skull, since honesty has become such a scarce commodity in these halls. So here it is: I did not burn those ledgers to protect the guild.

I burned them to protect the *idea* of the guild. These are different things. A guild is people — scribes and thieves and cartographers of impossible places, frightened now, looking to me for shelter. An idea is older than any of them and will outlive them all, and an idea can survive anything except exposure. The crown had declared its hunt. Red wax on every doorpost in the city, the royal seal pressed fat and glistening like a wound. *All who map what is not there will answer to the mountain.* Someone had to make sure that when the answering began, the Guild of Impossible Things would still exist to be answered for.

An institution, I have learned, is just a debt that refuses to die. I have spent my life servicing it. I am very tired. None of this matters to the debt.

* * *

The witch-hunter arrived at noon, which I thought showed a certain theatrical instinct. They always come at noon or midnight; I believe it is taught at whatever grim academy produces them. Hers was a woman called Serane Vail, and she came alone, without soldiers, which frightened me more than soldiers would have. Soldiers mean a superior expects resistance. A hunter alone means she is confident, and confidence in her profession is earned the way surgeons earn theirs.

She stood in my office without being asked to sit, her gloved hands folded, and looked at the shelves where the surviving ledgers lived behind their wards. She did not ask to see them. That was her first move, and I recognized it because it was mine: establish that you already know everything, and let your opposite number spend the conversation discovering how much.

"Guildmaster Vess," she said. Her voice was pleasant. That was worse. "You've been master here for thirty-one years."

"Thirty-two in autumn."

"You've seen hunts before. The Purge of the Salt Kings. The Ashwinter trials." She recited them the way a priest recites saints. "You survived both."

"The guild survived both," I corrected. "I merely declined to drown with it."

Something flickered across her face — not approval, exactly. Recognition. The look of one professional acknowledging another's craft. "Then you'll understand why I've come alone. I'm not here to arrest anyone today."

"No?"

"I'm here to discuss inventory."

I poured wine. Two cups, though I did not offer hers until she'd watched me drink from mine — old etiquette between poisoners, and we were both fluent. She accepted the cup and did not drink it either. We held our untouched wine like duellists holding blades neither intended to use yet.

"Inventory," I said.

"You keep accounts of things that shouldn't exist. Traded shadows. Bottled songs. Souls." She said the last word flatly, without the shiver most people give it, and I understood she had heard it so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. "The crown wants those accounts. Every name. Every binding. Every soul currently held under contract by this guild or by... others."

"Others," I said carefully.

Her eyes came up to mine, and they were the pale grey of paper left too long in sun. "We know about the mountain, Vess. We've known for years. Why do you think the hunt was declared *now*, of all centuries? The dragon-fire is guttering. Our diviners read it like everyone else reads weather. When it dies, every name that fire protects reverts to the court beneath — and the crown intends to be standing there with a writ when it happens." She smiled, and it was a terrible thing, patient and small. "The Queen below thinks she'll inherit. The crown above intends to seize. And your little guild sits in the middle holding the paperwork."

So. They knew. All of it — the founding bargain, the fire, the reversion clause written in blood and dragon-tongue before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I had spent two days burning ledgers and telling myself the secret was still a secret, and here was this woman drinking nothing in my office, informing me that the secret had been currency in the capital for longer than I had been alive.

"What is it you want from me?" I asked. There was no point in anything else.

"Time," she said simply. "The crown moves slowly. Writs, warrants, tribunals. But if I report that the guild is cooperating — that you're providing records, names, locations — the process takes months instead of days. Your members go unharvested while the lawyers argue jurisdiction." She set down her untouched cup. "In exchange, I need three things. First, your copies of the mountain contracts. Second, the registry of gifted members — the mappers, the finders, anyone whose talent touches the unmapped."

"And third?"

She let the silence stretch, savoring it, or perhaps letting me arrive at it myself, which is a cruelty I respect.

"The one who found the stair," she said. "Your cartographer. Okonkwo."

* * *

Here is what I told myself, sitting in my chair with the wine going bitter in my mouth: that Isandro was already lost. That the moment the proclamation went up in red wax, their fate was sealed regardless of what I did. That trading them was not treachery but triage — the arithmetic every guildmaster learns, the same arithmetic that let me burn half our history in a single night. One life against dozens. One name against the whole crooked architecture of the guild, its wards and protections, its people asleep upstairs trusting me.

And underneath that arithmetic, quiet as a second heartbeat, another truth I did not say aloud even to myself: that Isandro had been a liability since the day their mother brought them through our doors, and I had known it, and I had kept them anyway — no. Not kept. *Used.* Kept the way one keeps a coin one suspects is cursed: close, useful, never quite trusted. For thirty years I had watched them hide their gift from colleagues who loved them, watched them flinch at maps, watched them balance other people's impossible debts while their own accrued in the dark. And I had let them. Because a hidden gift is a controlled gift, and a controlled gift is a safe one, right up until the day control fails.

Control had failed. Isandro had gone below — Odile's empty bunk told me that much, and the seam of unmapped air behind the cellar wall that my own wards reported breached at moonset. They had gone down the stair toward the mountain court, alone except for a thief with a grudge-shaped debt, and every hour they were down there, the guild's protection thinned like watered wine. If the crown learned a guild member had opened a road to the under-court, there would be no tribunal. There would be fire.

So when Serane Vail asked for Isandro's location, I did not lie. That is the part I will have to live with, more than the burning, more than the rest. Lying would have been simple. Lying would have cost me nothing but my soul, and I sold pieces of that decades ago, standard terms.

"They've gone below," I said. "Through the unmapped stair. Toward the court."

Vail did not smile this time. She took out a slim black notebook and wrote, unhurried, and the scratch of her pen was the loudest sound in the room. "When?"

"Two nights past."

"And you didn't stop them."

"I didn't know until after."

That was a lie, or near enough to one that the difference felt like a hair drawn across the throat. I had known something. I had seen Isandro's face at the last guild supper, the particular stillness of someone who has decided. I had chosen not to see it, because seeing it would have obligated me to act, and acting would have meant choosing between the child the guild raised and the guild itself, and I am an old man who has made his choice so many times the choosing barely registers anymore.

Vail closed her notebook. "Cooperation noted. The writs will slow." She moved toward the door, then paused, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning. "One more thing, Guildmaster. The entry in your surviving ledgers — the soul sold for 'one hidden child.' The crown's archivists flagged it years ago. We assumed it was a clerical fiction." Now she turned, and her pale eyes had something almost like pity in them, which I could have done without. "It isn't, is it. The child grew up. The child is downstairs balancing your books."

"Get out of my office," I said, with all the authority of a man whose office had just stopped belonging to him.

She went. Her footsteps on the stairs were light, almost cheerful.

* * *

I sat for a long time afterward. The wine had gone to vinegar in the cups; I drank mine anyway, because wasting it seemed like one sin too many stacked on the day.

I told myself the story again — the one about protection, about triage, about the idea that must outlive the people. I told it the way I fed the fire last night: slowly, not looking directly at what I was feeding it. But stories, like fires, reach a point where they cannot be unwritten, and somewhere in the tasting of that sour wine I finally saw what I had fed to the flame.

Not ledgers. Not secrets.

A person. I had handed Serane Vail a person the way one hands over a debt instrument, and I had dressed it in the language of survival, and the worst of it — the part that curdled even my well-practiced self-forgiveness — was that some cold clerk-organ in my chest had registered the transaction as *sound*. Good terms. Favorable rates. The guild endures.

The guild endures. That has been my prayer for thirty-two years, and I suddenly understood it was also my epitaph, and that the two had become indistinguishable, and that I could not remember anymore whether I had ever served the guild or merely served myself wearing the guild's robes.

Upstairs, the last dragon-fire guttered in its iron lamp, and the shadows of the warding sigils trembled on the walls like handwriting in a dying language. Below the city, a cartographer I had sold was walking toward the mountain with a thief and a stolen page, mapping a road that had never existed, doing the one thing the crown feared and the Queen coveted and I had spent a lifetime forbidding.

Being honest. Being visible. Being *known*.

I rose, and took up my pen, and began — God forgive me, or the fire, or whoever keeps the true ledger of men like me — I began drafting the letter that would finish the bargain. Full registry. Every gifted name. Everything Vail had asked for and more, delivered before week's end, sealing the deal, buying the guild its months of borrowed time.

My hand was steady. That was the horror of it. Forty years of bookkeeping, and my hand did not shake once.

Outside, the bells rang the hour, and far beneath them, faint as a memory of music, I thought I heard singing — a choir, crooked and rising, growing louder with every verse.

And I understood, with the clarity that comes only after the ink is dry, that I had not bought the guild time at all.

I had simply told the fire which of us to take first.
