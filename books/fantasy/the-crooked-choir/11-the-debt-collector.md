## 11. The Debt Collector

There are three things every guild thief learns before she's allowed to touch a lock, and Odile Marchand had learned all of them before her twelfth birthday. One: never steal what you can't carry. Two: never steal from anyone who knows your name. Three: never, under any circumstances, owe the guildmaster more than the guildmaster owes you.

I had broken the third rule so thoroughly, so elegantly, that it had become something of an art form. Vess owned me the way a river owns a boat — not cruelly, but completely, and with the constant implicit threat of the rocks downstream.

Which is why, when the knock came at my door in the grey hour before dawn, I already knew who it was. Nobody else knocked like that. Everybody else in this city knocked like they were asking permission. The witch-hunter knocked like the door had already been opened, and the only question was whether I'd be standing on the right side of it.

I counted to four. It seemed polite. Then I opened the door.

He was exactly what the rumors promised and somehow less. The rumors said Seraphin Vale wore the crown's grey like a second skin, that his eyes were the color of a ledger's closing line, that he carried a set of silver scales that weighed sins instead of coin. All true, as far as it went. What the rumors left out was the smallness of him. He was a narrow man, a precise man, the kind of man who'd been designed by a committee of accountants and had come out the other side as a weapon.

"Odile Marchand," he said. Not a question. He had a voice like dry paper being folded.

"Nobody's called me that in years," I said. "Around here I'm just 'that woman who definitely didn't take the mayor's silver candlesticks.'"

"May I come in?"

"You may not." I leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and gave him the smile I used on customs officers. "But you can tell me why the crown's debt collector is visiting a humble fence at an hour when even honest people are asleep. Which is to say: you can lie to me, and I'll enjoy watching you do it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The memory of where a smile would go, on a better-constructed face.

"You owe the Guild of Impossible Things a debt of service," he said. "Eleven years of it, by my arithmetic. Debts of service are transferable. The crown has purchased a great many of the guild's obligations this week. Yours among them."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach, slow and quiet, like a stone sinking in deep water. I kept the smile on. It's amazing how long a smile can last when you're using it as a barricade.

"Vess sold my contract."

"Vess sold *many* contracts." He produced a slip of grey paper from his coat — not a warrant, I noticed, not quite. A ledger page. My name on it in Vess's cramped, upright hand, and beneath it, in ink that was still faintly wet-looking, though the page had to be days old: *transferred, crown's service, consideration rendered.* "The guild is cooperating fully with the hunt. In exchange for certain... considerations."

Considerations. I thought of Isandro, somewhere below the city with their forbidden gift and their missing pages, and I thought of the dragon-fire, guttering. I thought of how Vess had burned half the ledgers and called it protection.

Protection for whom, I wondered. Protection for how many. Protection at what price, and paid into whose pocket.

"Here's what happens now," Vale said, in the tone of a man describing weather. "You will accompany me to the guildhouse. You will answer questions. You will tell me where the cartographer has gone."

"And if I don't?"

"Then your debt of service converts to a debt of penalty." He said it the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "Penalty debts are paid in kind. Your kind is thieving. The crown has a use for thieving hands, Odile Marchand, but it does not require the rest of you to remain attached."

* * *

He didn't bind my hands. That was the first insult. He simply walked me through the waking streets with his hand resting on my elbow like we were courting, and the grey paper folded in his pocket like it was nothing, like my whole life wasn't folded in there with it.

The city was doing that thing cities do at dawn, when the night people and the day people trade places without looking at each other. Bakers and drunkards. Fishwives and thieves. I catalogued exits out of habit — the alley behind the chandler's, the culvert under Tanner's Row, the gap in the market wall that only I and three dead men knew about — and catalogued, just as habitually, why none of them would work. Vale walked like a man who had already read the map of the city and found it accurate. His eyes did the thing accountants' eyes do: they didn't look at things, they *tallied* them.

"You're wondering why I haven't bound you," he said.

"The thought had crossed my mind, along with several thoughts involving rooftops."

"You won't run." He didn't look at me when he said it. He was looking at my left forearm, where the guild mark sat under my sleeve — the crooked quill, inked in when I was nine years old, in ink mixed with dragon-ash so it would hold. "The mark binds. While you wear the guild's protection, your debt is the guild's debt, and the guild has transferred it to the crown. If you run, you run *owing*, and everything you love becomes collateral. That's how the old contracts work. I assume Vess explained them when she inked you."

Vess had explained nothing when she inked me. Vess had explained that I was an orphan with quick hands and no family, and that the guild was a family now, and that family meant the mark. I had been nine. I had thought the crooked quill was beautiful.

"The mark protects," I said. "That's the whole point. That's the founding bargain. Dragon-fire and the guild's name, and no crown can touch a marked member."

"Dragon-fire," Vale said, and there it was — the first crack in the dry-paper voice, something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than cruelty. Gentle was what you used on things you'd already won. "You do keep up with guild affairs, don't you. Then you know it's guttering. You know that when it dies, every name in every ledger reverts to the mountain court. Including yours. Including your brother's."

The street tilted. Just slightly. Just enough that I had to put a hand against a wall, and Vale waited, patient as interest accruing.

My brother. Théo. Dead eleven years, and this narrow grey man said his name like he owned it, and the worst part — the part that made my hands shake — was that he was right. Théo's name was in the guild's ledgers. Théo had owed the guild when he died, and the debt had passed to me, and his name had gone into the books the way dead men's names do, as collateral, as memory, as *property*. I had never thought about what happened to a dead man's name when the fire that protected it went out.

I thought about it now.

"You're lying," I said. "You can't know that."

"I read ledgers," Vale said. "It's what I do. The guild keeps very good ledgers. That's the tragedy of the Guild of Impossible Things, Odile Marchand — they write everything down. Every soul, every shadow, every song. They think the writing is the protection." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were the color of a closing line. "Writing is just a list of what can be taken."

* * *

They put me in a room at the guildhouse — my guildhouse, the place where I'd learned to pick locks and lie and love exactly one person at a time, never two, because two was how you got caught — and the room had a table and a chair and a lamp, and Vale sat across the table from me with the grey paper flat between us like a card he'd already won with.

"Tell me where the cartographer went," he said.

"And if I knew?"

"Do you?"

And here is the thing about me, the thing Vess never understood and Vale never would: I am a very good liar, but I am a *terrible* liar about the people I love. Isandro had stood in the rain outside my window two nights ago and asked me for a debt of a different kind — not the guild's debt, theirs, the old kind, the kind you pay because someone once pulled you out of a river and never mentioned it again. And I had gone down the unmapped stair with them into the dark under the city, and every step had felt like the first honest thing I'd done in eleven years.

So I looked at Seraphin Vale, and I lied.

"Isandro Ashgrove?" I shrugged. "The ledger-keeper. They barely leave the back rooms. If they've run, I couldn't say where. Cartographers." I smiled. "They get lost."

He watched me for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, in the guildhouse, I could hear the muffled sounds of the night shift — a door, footsteps, someone laughing at something that probably wasn't funny. The guild, going about its business, cooperating fully.

"You're lying," Vale said, without heat. "That's fine. Lying is a debt too. It accrues." He turned the grey paper around and pushed it toward me with one finger. "But here's what I actually came to offer, before the penalty terms. The crown will buy your debt outright. Clean conversion. Your service, discharged. Your brother's name, released from the ledgers — returned to you, to do with as you please. Bury it properly, if you like. Some people find that meaningful."

The room got very small and very quiet.

"And in exchange," I said, when I could make my voice work.

"In exchange, you tell me where the cartographer went. And you wear this." From his coat he took a small thing — a badge, grey wax, the crown's seal pressed into it, still soft enough that it hadn't finished setting. "The crown's mark. It replaces the guild's. It's a better mark. The guild's protection is dying; you can feel it, can't you? The ink going warm at night. The dreams. The crown's mark doesn't gutter. The crown's mark holds."

And the horrible thing — the thing I will be ashamed of until I die and possibly after — is that for one full breath, I wanted it. Théo's name, free. Théo's name out of the ledgers and out of the mountain's reach, buried properly, a grave instead of a line item. Eleven years I had carried that debt. Eleven years I had told myself I was paying it for the guild, out of loyalty, out of the mark, out of the crooked quill that had made a family out of an orphan. And here was a grey little man offering to tear the page out, and all it would cost was the one person who had ever asked me for help instead of ordering it.

One breath. I'm telling you this because it's true, and because if I leave it out, the rest is just a story about a brave woman, and I was never brave. I was bought at nine years old and I stayed bought, and for one breath in a lamplit room I nearly stayed bought a second time.

Then I thought of Isandro's face in the rain. Not asking. *Trusting.* Which is worse. Trust is the one debt you can't steal your way out of.

"No," I said.

Vale tilted his head. "No?"

"No." I rolled up my sleeve.

The guild mark looked the way it always had — the crooked quill, the ink gone slightly blue-black with age, the faint shimmer of dragon-ash that only showed in certain light. Except that in the lamplight, that night, the shimmer looked thin. Guttering. Like a candle seen through a window in a house where everyone's asleep.

"Odile," Vale said, and now there was something new in the dry-paper voice, something I hadn't heard before, and it took me a moment to name it because I'd never heard it from a man like him. Caution. "Think about what you're—"

"I've thought about nothing else since I was nine years old," I said. "You want to know what the mark is? It's a leash with a family painted on it. And the family just sold me to you." I looked up at him. "You said the debt converts. Fine. Let's convert it."

I reached for the lamp.

Here's what they don't tell you about burning off a guild mark: it works, but it works the way amputation works. The mark isn't ink, not really — it's a contract, and a contract lives in the blood as much as the skin, and when you burn it you're not burning a picture. You're burning a name off a page, and the page is you. The flame went blue where it touched the quill — dragon-fire blue, the last gift of a dying beast — and the pain was not pain exactly. It was *removal*. It was eleven years of service and protection and ownership being pulled out through the skin of my arm, and I made a sound I will deny to my grave, and I held the lamp steady, because if I dropped it I'd have to do it twice.

Vale didn't stop me. I want that recorded. The crown's debt collector sat across the table with his hands folded and watched a woman burn her own arm, and did nothing, because a debt that destroys itself is a debt closed, and closing debts is the only thing he loves.

When it was done, the mark was a scar — a crooked quill rendered in shiny, ruined skin, a ghost of itself. And the guild's protection, the dragon-ash, the founding bargain, all of it: gone. I could feel the absence the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled. The night air touched my arm and it was just air. Nothing between me and the crown. Nothing between me and the mountain. Nothing between me and anything at all.

"There," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to. "Debt discharged. In kind."

Vale looked at the scar for a long moment. Then he took the grey paper back, and with a small knife from his coat — a precise little knife, a committee-designed knife — he cut the page in half, and the halves in half, and set the pieces on the table like he was dealing out a losing hand.

"You understand what you've done," he said. "No guild. No crown's mark. No protection at all. The hunt doesn't stop at the guild's door anymore, for you. It starts there."

"I understand I'm not for sale," I said. "Took me eleven years and an arm. Bargain rates, really."

He stood. At the door he paused, and didn't turn around, and said, in the dry-paper voice, almost gently: "Your brother's name is still in the ledgers, Odile Marchand. Still bound. When the fire goes out — and it will go out — the mountain collects. I want you to know that I take no pleasure in it."

"You don't take pleasure in anything," I said. "That's your whole tragedy, and nobody's going to write a song about it."

And then he was gone, and I was alone in the lamplit room with four pieces of grey paper and a scar that throbbed like a second heartbeat, and I did the only sensible thing left.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out. Exile. Eleven years of service and I'd bought my freedom with fire and all it had cost me was everything, and Théo's name was still sitting in a ledger somewhere, waiting for the mountain like a coin in a dead man's pocket.

Well.

There was one person who knew where those ledgers were. One person who'd gone down into the dark two nights ago with a stolen page and a stolen plan and had trusted me — trusted *me* — to hold the door.

I wrapped my arm in a bandage, took my coat, my picks, and the knife I'd stolen from Vale's own table while he was cutting the paper — because rule one, never steal what you can't carry, and rule two, never steal from anyone who knows your name, and Seraphin Vale had made the fatal error of doing both.

Then I went to find the unmapped stair, and the cartographer waiting at the bottom of it, and I went to pay the only debt I'd ever taken on willingly.

Down in the dark, under the mountain, someone was holding my brother's name. And I had decided, somewhere between the lamp and the door, that I was done letting other people keep what was mine.
