## 12. Mother's Handwriting

The scribe's name was Pellam Hoss, and for thirty-one years he had copied the guild's ledgers in a hand so small and so perfect that three generations of cartographers had assumed he was not a man but a very patient machine. He lived above the ink room, in a space the size of a coffin stood on end, surrounded by jars of gall and ground lapis and one dried eel that nobody could explain. He had never once spoken to me at length. He had never once spoken to anyone at length.

So when Odile pressed his door open with her shoulder and said, "He asked for you," my first thought was that the world had finally tipped over entirely, and I might as well enjoy the view on the way down.

"He asked for *me*?"

"By name." She held the door. "Your full name. Which, given recent events, I found either very brave or very stupid of him."

"Both can be true."

Inside, the room smelled of iron-gall and camphor and the particular sweetness of a body quietly failing. Pellam lay on a cot that had been built for a smaller man, propped against a rolled map of a coastline that no longer existed — the guild kept those, maps of drowned places, as if geography were something you could grieve. His hands were folded on his chest like two closed books. They were spotted brown, trembling faintly, and even now they looked like they wanted to be holding a pen.

"Cartographer Okonkwo," he rasped.

"Just Isandro is fine."

"No." A ghost of a smile. "Names matter tonight. Cartographer Okonkwo it is. Sit. My chair. Mind the third leg, it lies."

I sat in the lying chair. Odile stayed by the door, arms crossed, doing her best impression of someone who wasn't listening to every word.

"You're dying," I said, because I have never been praised for my bedside manner.

"I am being *collected*," Pellam corrected. "There's a difference. Dying is what happens to people. Collection is what happens to entries." He coughed, wet and long, and when he finished he looked ten years older and somehow more amused. "I was entered into the ledgers when I was nineteen. Sold my shadow to pay an apprenticeship debt. Standard terms. The guild holds it, the fire protects me, everyone pretends this isn't slavery with better calligraphy."

"And now the fire's guttering."

"And now the fire's guttering, and the mountain court sends its collectors up the unmapped stairs like bailiffs at quarter-day." He turned his head toward me with effort. "You went down there. Odile told me. You went down and came back up with your soul still in your chest, which is more than most manage, and you came back asking questions about your mother's page."

I hadn't told anyone but Odile about the fragment — the scrap of ledger I'd pulled from Vess's burn pile, the entry in handwriting I'd know anywhere because I'd spent my childhood watching it make grocery lists and love notes and maps of places that didn't exist yet: *one soul, sold; price received: one hidden child, kept.*

"I'm asking," I said.

"Then ask properly."

I leaned forward. The candle between us guttered — everything in this city was guttering lately, fires and names and old men — and threw our shadows huge against the wall, where they looked like two figures from a cautionary woodcut.

"Pellam Hoss. Who wrote my mother's entry?"

His eyes were bright and terrible. "I did."

* * *

I want to tell you I took it calmly. That the cartographer in me simply noted the datum and filed it. But the truth is that something in my chest went off like a dropped lantern, and for a moment the whole room was fire.

"You *wrote* it. You watched her sign it. You—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "You stood there while she sold—"

"While she sold herself." He said it flatly, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "Yes. And I have waited thirty years for someone to come and be angry at me about it, and here you are, right on schedule, and I would get up and pour you wine if my legs still answered correspondence."

"Sold herself." The words didn't fit together. I tried them again, different order. "She sold *herself*. Not me."

"Not you. Never you. Read the entry again, cartographer. Properly, this time, with the eye you use on maps instead of the eye you use on wounds." He lifted one trembling hand and pointed at me. "*One soul, sold.* Whose? The entry doesn't say Isandro Okonkwo. It says a name you've never heard, because it was hers before she married, before she buried it the way women bury names that courts have learned to hunt. *Price received: one hidden child, kept.* Kept by whom? By the guild. By Vess. Your mother walked down into this building pregnant and terrified, with the crown's first witch-hunters already asking after cartographers in her village, and she made a trade. Her soul into the choir. Your freedom out of it. She bought you a life with the only coin she had left."

The room tilted. I gripped the lying chair's arm and the lying chair, true to form, wobbled.

"That's not what Vess said."

"What did Vess say?"

"That my mother sold me. To hide me. That the guild took custody of my name for my own protection."

Pellam laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Odile moved toward him and he waved her off with a hand like a dying bird.

"Vess says many things," he managed. "Vess has been saying things since before you were born. Here is what actually happened, and I will give it to you in ledger-form, because it's the only language I trust anymore:

"*Item:* One woman, name of Sefa Okonkwo, comes to the guild by night, autumn of the year the crown first outlawed the mapping gift. She carries a child and a deed she has drawn herself — a good deed, clean lines, honest bearings. Better work than half my masters ever managed.

"*Item:* She offers her soul to the under-court's choir. The choir wants voices; the court wants leverage over the guild; the guild wants the crown's hunters pointed elsewhere. Three appetites, one transaction. Everyone eats.

"*Item:* In exchange, the guild registers the child as guild-protected, name sealed under dragon-fire, unclaimable. The mother's condition — written into the margin, in her hand, witnessed by myself and one other — is that the protection is *permanent*. Not revocable. Not voidable. Permanent, or no sale.

"*Item:* The other witness dies within the year. Conveniently.

"*Item:* Some eleven years later, the guildmaster Vess, finding the permanent clause inconvenient to certain private arrangements of his own, voids it. Quietly. With my pen, which I lent him for a copying job, and which he used to strike the word 'permanent' and substitute 'at the guildmaster's discretion.' He did it at my desk. He did it while I was at supper. I found the alteration the next morning and said nothing, because I was a coward with excellent handwriting."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

"Why," I said, and my voice came out small and strange, "are you telling me this now?"

"Because I am being collected tomorrow, cartographer, and dead men's confessions are worth nothing, and living men's are worth everything, and I have been a coward for thirty years and would like to stop." He drew a breath that whistled. "Also because you came back from under the mountain alive, which means you're the first person in decades with both the standing and the spine to do something about it."

"Do something about *what*? The voiding?"

"The voiding. The choir. All of it." His eyes fixed on mine. "Do you understand what your mother's bargain means? Every soul in that choir was taken. Bought, tricked, gambled away, inherited by creditors. But yours — yours was the *price*, not the property. The court was paid. Paid in full, in advance, with the finest voice it ever bought. Whatever claim the Mountain Queen holds over you, it isn't a debt. It's theft. Even by the court's own crooked arithmetic, even by the guild's, even by the crown's — she has no paper on you. Vess voided the only thing standing between you and that fact."

* * *

Odile spoke for the first time from the doorway. "Why would Vess void it? What arrangement needed Isandro claimable?"

Pellam's smile was thin as a knife's edge. "Ask yourself who profits when a cartographer of impossible realms can be sold to the highest bidder. Ask yourself why the guild's protections have been failing for years while the fire still burns. Ask yourself what a guildmaster gets for keeping one specific name *off* the protected rolls." He paused. "Or don't ask. You already know. You've known since you found your pages missing from the burn pile. You just haven't let yourself say it."

I hadn't. I'd dressed it up as bureaucracy, as cowardice, as the guild's usual institutional rot. But Pellam was right. I had known. Vess hadn't failed to protect me. Vess had *un*-protected me — carefully, deliberately, years ago — and then spent every day since wearing the face of a man guarding a dangerous secret, when all along the secret was that he'd already sold it.

"She's still down there," I said. "My mother. In the choir. Thirty years."

"Thirty-one in the spring." Pellam's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "And fading. They fade, the choir souls. Each verse takes a memory. By now she may not remember your face. She may not remember she had a son. She may only remember the shape of the bargain itself — the last thing she chose." He looked at his own folded hands. "That's how they keep them singing. Hope curdles into habit. Habit into song."

Something broke loose in me then, and I want to describe it accurately, because it wasn't grief, or not only. It was rage — rage with love tangled all through it like two colors of thread spun into one cord, impossible to separate without destroying both. She hadn't abandoned me. She hadn't sold me. She had gone into that mirrored dark *on purpose*, trading everything she was for everything I might become, and then the man I called guildmaster, the man whose roof I slept under, had torn up the receipt and left us both in the dark.

"Where's the original?" I asked. "Her copy of the deed. The margin clause. Anything in her hand."

"Gone. Vess burned the originals the night of the proclamation — you saw the pile." Pellam's mouth twisted. "But cartographers should know better than anyone: burning a map doesn't move the mountain. I *copied* everything. Thirty years of copying, cartographer. Every entry, every margin, every alteration — including the alterations. Especially the alterations. I kept a second ledger. The true one. The one with the corrections shown, red against black, fraud laid bare in fair and honest script."

My heart stopped, restarted, and began running. "Where?"

"Under the floor of the ink room. Under the third jar from the left, the one with the chipped lid, beneath a false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth." He recited it like a shipping address, like a man who had rehearsed this delivery for decades. "It's yours. It was always meant to be yours — well. Meant for whoever finally came asking with anger in their mouth and her eyes in their face. You'll do."

"Why not take it to the guild council? Why me?"

"Because the council is Vess's, and you know it, and I'm tired of pretending otherwise." He coughed again, shorter this time, as if the truth had used up most of what was left of him. "Besides. There's a line in her margin clause I never showed anyone. Her condition. The permanence clause — the words Vess struck out. Do you want to hear what your mother actually wrote, in her own hand, thirty-one years ago?"

"Yes," I said, and it came out like a prayer.

Pellam closed his eyes, and from memory — a scribe's memory, a machine's memory, thirty-one years of patience — he recited:

"*Let this protection stand until my child stands somewhere safe and says their own name aloud, freely, to witnesses who mean them no harm. Then, and only then, let the account be settled, and let whatever remains of me go home.*"

Silence. The candle burned. Somewhere above us, the city went on not knowing.

"She wrote you an exit," Pellam whispered. "Not just protection. An exit. She always believed you'd find your way to it. She said—" his breath caught, "—she said, *my child draws maps of places that don't exist yet. One day they'll draw the way out.*"

I put my face in my hands. I am not ashamed of it. Well. I am slightly ashamed of it. But some things crack you along seams you didn't know the makers had left, and that was one.

When I looked up, Pellam was watching me with an expression I'd never seen on him — hope, badly disguised as professional interest.

"The collection comes at dawn," he said. "Bailiffs of the under-court, polite as priests. When they take me, they'll take my shadow, and my shadow knows where the second ledger sleeps. So you have until dawn, cartographer. Go now. Third jar. Chipped lid."

I stood. My legs felt borrowed. At the door I stopped, because there was one question left, the smallest one, the one that had been sitting in my chest since the burn pile.

"Pellam. Her handwriting. On the entry. Did she hesitate? When she signed?"

He was quiet a long moment.

"No," he said softly. "She wrote her name the way your mother did everything — like she was drawing a road on a map, and daring the world to follow it."

* * *

We went down to the ink room together, Odile and I, past the sleeping guild, past the wards that suddenly felt less like walls and more like stage scenery. The room smelled of camphor and copper and thirty years of Pellam's patience. Third jar from the left. Chipped lid. I knelt on cold stone and pried up the false bottom with the knife I'd stolen from Vale's table, because rule three, which I was only now learning: never steal anything you aren't prepared to have change your life.

The oilcloth bundle was heavy. Ledger-heavy. Truth-heavy.

I unwrapped it there on the floor, and lamplight fell across pages and pages of that tiny perfect hand — black entries, red corrections, fraud exposed in fair and honest script — and near the front, the first page, the founding entry of my entire existence, in a different hand entirely. Loops and roads and quiet defiance.

Sefa Okonkwo. My mother's name, written in her own hand, daring the world to follow.

"Isandro," Odile said quietly behind me. "Dawn's coming."

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest like something breathing.

Above us, the last dragon-fire flickered. Below us, a choir sang with my mother's voice in it. And somewhere between the two, a guildmaster was about to learn what happens when you strike out the word *permanent* in front of the wrong cartographer.

"Get your coat," I told Odile. "We're going back down."
