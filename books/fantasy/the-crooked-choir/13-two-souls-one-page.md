## 13. Two Souls, One Page

The archive beneath the under-court smelled of cold iron and old promises. I had been in a hundred archives before this one—guild vaults with their oiled shelves and their careful humidity, the private collections of collectors who collected things no sane person should own—but none of them had ever made my skin crawl quite like this.

Because this one was organized by grief.

The ledgers here were shelved not by date or alphabet but by category of loss: *Sold Willingly*, *Sold Unwillingly*, *Sold by Kin*, *Sold by Debt*, and then, on a shelf all its own behind a grate of blackened silver, *Irredeemable*. I ran my fingers along the spines as Odile kept watch at the stair, and I tried not to think about how many of those bindings had been written in hands that trembled. How many mothers' hands. How many fathers'.

"Find it yet?" Odile's whisper came from the dark, sharp as a pick slipping into a lock.

"I'm looking."

"You've been looking for an hour."

"I've been looking for an hour," I agreed, "because there are eleven thousand volumes down here and the catalog is written in a cipher that changes every third word." I held a page closer to the witchlight stub we'd smuggled past the gatekeeper. "Whoever built this system wanted it readable only to people who already knew what they were looking for."

"That's not paranoia," Odile said. "That's policy."

She wasn't wrong. The guild had taught me that much, if nothing else true: institutions do not keep secrets despite their structure. They keep secrets *with* it.

I turned another page. The cipher resolved slowly, the way a face resolves out of fog—familiar shapes rearranging themselves into meaning. *Binding, soul, singular.* *Binding, name, heritable.* *Binding, bloodline, compound—*

I stopped.

*Compound.*

My heart did something unhelpful in my chest, some arrhythmic stutter between hope and terror. Compound meant more than one. Compound meant linked. Compound meant—

"Odile," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which I counted as a small professional victory. "What shelf would a compound binding live on?"

There was a pause. When she answered, she'd moved closer, close enough that I could see the edge of her face in the light, the scar across her brow catching orange. "Behind the grate," she said. "The Irredeemables."

* * *

The grate was locked, because of course it was. Everything worth having in this world was locked, and everything truly worth having was locked twice.

Odile had picks; she always had picks; I suspected she'd been born holding them and had cried when they were taken away to be cleaned. She worked the lock while I kept the light low and listened to the distant sound of the choir rehearsing somewhere above us—a sound like a hundred voices trying to remember a song they'd once known by heart, each of them a half-step off in a different direction.

It should have been beautiful. That was the horror of it. It *was* beautiful, the way a cathedral is beautiful, the way a glacier is beautiful—the way anything is beautiful that is vast and cold and utterly indifferent to you.

"There," Odile breathed, and the grate swung open on hinges that had been greased recently enough to suggest someone came down here often. "After you, cartographer."

"After me," I said, "is exactly where I want everyone."

The Irredeemable shelf held perhaps forty volumes. They were bound differently from the others—not in leather but in something pale and smooth that I refused, firmly, to identify. Each spine bore a single sigil stamped in wax that had gone the color of dried blood: the Mountain Queen's mark, a mountain split by a seam of light.

I pulled the first volume and opened it at random.

A name looked back at me. Not written—*rendered*. The letters seemed to breathe, faintly, the way letters do when they are not merely describing a soul but *containing* one. Below the name, terms. Below the terms, a signature. Below the signature, a seal.

I closed it gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping person, and set it back.

"Not that one," I whispered.

"No?"

"No." My mouth had gone dry. "That one's still occupied."

We worked down the shelf together after that, faster now, Odile reading seals while I read names. Most of the volumes were singletons—one soul, one page, one transaction recorded in the flat language of commerce. *Received: one soul, adult, sound. Consideration: forgiveness of debt, three crowns, seven silver.* As though a soul were a horse. As though a soul were a *coat*.

And then, near the end of the shelf, wedged slightly out of alignment as though someone had once tried to hide it and thought better of it, I found a volume whose spine bore two sigils instead of one.

Two.

I didn't open it right away. I stood there with my hand on the cover, and I let myself feel the thing I'd been refusing to feel since the gate, since the stair, since the night in Vess's study when I'd learned my own name had been sold before I could walk. I let it move through me like weather.

Because I already knew what was inside. Some part of me—the part that mapped what wasn't there, the part I'd spent thirty years hiding like a deformity—had known since I saw the double sigil. Bindings were contracts, and contracts were maps of obligation, and I could read the shape of this one through the cover the way you can read a letter's contents through cheap paper.

"Isandro." Odile's voice, gentle for once. "Open it or put it back. We don't have until dawn."

I opened it.

* * *

The page was thick as vellum and old as sin. At the top, in handwriting I knew better than my own—because it was the hand that had taught me mine—two names.

Mine. And hers.

Not side by side. *Linked*. A line drawn between them like a road on a map, except the road ran downward, into the mountain, into the court, into the choir where my mother's voice had been singing crooked harmonies for thirty years while I balanced accounts upstairs and called myself an orphan.

Below the names, the terms. I read them twice. Three times. My eyes kept snagging on the same clause, the way a cartographer's eye snags on a river that flows uphill.

*Soul the first (the child): consideration paid, one hidden life preserved. Soul the second (the mother): consideration paid, the above.*

One transaction. One price. Two souls—and only one of them had been sold.

"Do you understand what this says?" I asked. My voice sounded strange to me, very far away.

Odile read it over my shoulder. She was quiet for a long moment. "It says your mother bought your freedom with herself," she said finally. "And the court took you anyway."

"The court didn't take me." The words came out flat. "Vess voided the exchange. Kept the payment. Kept *her*. And filed me away in the guild like a misfiled document, protected by dragon-fire, growing up thinking—" I stopped. Started again. "Thinking I was the debt. Thinking I was the thing that had been traded."

Odile said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been a lie, and Odile lied beautifully but never kindly.

I looked at the page again, and this time I looked at it as a cartographer looks—as the thing I was, the thing I'd spent my whole life apologizing for. And I saw what I should have seen immediately.

"This page isn't just a record," I said. "It's the binding itself. The contract isn't stored in the court's memory or the Queen's will—it's *here*. Ink and vellum. Physical. Portable."

"You're saying we steal it."

"I'm saying the entire architecture of my life rests on one piece of paper in a locked archive behind a grate that a thief picked in ninety seconds." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Thirty years of hiding. Thirty years of Vess's silence and the crown's hunters and the guild's little protections—all of it anchored to a page that fits in my coat pocket."

"So take it."

"I can't." I turned the page toward her, to the bottom, where the Queen's seal sat stamped in wax gone dark as garnet. "Look at the seal. It's not decoration, Odile. It's a lock. The binding holds as long as the seal is intact—and the seal is keyed to her. If I tear this page out, if I so much as crease it wrong without breaking the seal properly, the contract doesn't void. It *accelerates*. Both souls claimed instantly. Both of us in the choir by morning."

"And the proper way to break it?"

"The proper way," I said, "requires her seal. The living one. Pressed to the page in revocation, by her hand or her authority." I closed the ledger with exaggerated care, handling it like the bomb it was. "Which means before we run one step toward the surface, we need to steal the Mountain Queen's personal seal."

Odile stared at me. Then she started laughing—quiet, hopeless, delighted laughter, the laugh of a woman who has just been told the job is worse than she'd priced it for and has decided to take it anyway.

"A seal," she said. "From a queen. In her own court. Where every mirror watches and every gatekeeper knows our faces and I paid my dead brother's name just to get through the door."

"Yes."

"And you're smiling."

I was. I noticed it with something like surprise—the first real smile in weeks, maybe years, pulling at the corners of my mouth like a muscle remembering its purpose. Because underneath the terror, underneath the rage at Vess and the grief for my mother and the vertigo of being a transaction, something in me had gone very still and very clear.

For thirty years I had hidden my gift. For thirty years I had treated it as a wound. But standing in that archive, holding the paper my whole life was written on, I understood at last what the gift was actually *for*. Maps were never about places. Maps were about power—who held it, who walked where, who got to say *this is the way*. The Queen had built an empire of bindings because she understood that. And I had spent my life drawing maps no one could use because I was afraid of exactly this moment.

No longer.

"The choir performs at midnight," I said. "Every soul in the court attends—including, I'd wager, every guard worth the name, because the Queen likes an audience and punishes absence. That's our window. While she watches her stolen voices sing, we find her seal."

"And if we're caught?"

"Then we're caught." I slid the ledger back onto the shelf, precisely where it had been, spine aligned, dust undisturbed—I was a thief of information, not a vandal. "But we won't be. Because I know these corridors now. I've been mapping them since we came down the stair, every turn, every mirror, every blind spot. The court thinks it owns the underground." I tapped my temple. "It doesn't own the map."

Odile studied me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly. "There you are," she said softly. "Been wondering where you went, all these years of ledgers."

"She's been here the whole time," I said. "Just unmapped."

We doused the witchlight and climbed back toward the upper galleries, toward the mirrored halls and the singing, toward the seal. Behind us the archive settled into its silence, forty irredeemable souls breathing their slow paper breaths in the dark.

Soon, I promised them silently. All of you. Not just mine.

Above us, faint through the stone, the choir began to sing.

Midnight was coming. And for the first time in my life, I was walking toward it instead of hiding from it.
