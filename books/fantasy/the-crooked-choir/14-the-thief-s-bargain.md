## 14. The Thief's Bargain

There are three rules to going back underground, and I learned all of them the hard way the first time I was dragged down there in chains.

One: never take the same road twice. Two: never trust a door that opens too easily. Three: never, under any circumstances, tell yourself you're doing this for free.

I broke rule three before my boots touched the first stair.

Not because Isandro had asked me to come back—I hadn't given them the chance to ask. I'd left them in the drowned chapel beneath the tannery district, sitting cross-legged on a slab of stone like a saint in a bad fresco, their fingers still ink-stained from drawing a map of a place that had no business existing. They'd looked at me when I said I'd be back by moonrise, and they'd had the decency not to say thank you, which I appreciated. Thank yous are heavy things. You carry them whether you want to or not.

I told myself it was the debt. That was the story I'd sold Isandro, and debts are clean. Debts have numbers. What I actually owed them wasn't a number—it was the fact that when the witch-hunter's men came through the Lamplighters' Quarter three nights ago, Isandro had stood between me and a door that was about to be kicked in, calm as a clerk, and said, "She's with me," as if that meant anything, as if being with anyone had ever protected anybody in this city since the proclamation went up in red wax.

It had worked, though. That was the maddening part. The hunters had looked at Isandro Okonkwo—small, ink-blotched, unremarkable—and something in the guild's old protections had flickered around them like a candle deciding to be brave, and the men had moved on. For me. A thief whose guild mark I'd burned off my own wrist two days later rather than let it lead anyone to me.

So no, this wasn't for free. This was for a debt with no ledger entry, which is the worst kind, because you can't balance it out. You just keep paying until someone tells you to stop.

* * *

The road down was one I'd mapped myself, back when mapping other people's escape routes was my whole trade and I'd been very good at it. It started behind a shuttered dye-house where the vats had gone sour years ago and the whole street smelled like a bruise. There was a drainage arch that didn't appear on any surveyor's chart because I had paid two clerks and threatened a third to make sure it didn't. Through the arch, down a stair cut into living rock by hands that predated the city, past a niche where somebody centuries ago had left an offering of river stones stacked into a little tower that nobody had knocked over since—which either meant deep piety or deep fear, and down here those were usually the same thing.

The dark got older as I went. That's the thing about the under-city people don't understand until they feel it: the dark isn't empty. It's *tenured*. It has seniority over every torch you bring.

I counted turns the way Isandro had taught me—not by steps, but by the way the air changed. Cold, then colder, then a strange mineral warmth where the mountain's blood ran close under the stone. At the fourth change, I stopped and did what we'd agreed.

I whistled.

Three notes, descending, then one held long—the opening phrase of a lullaby from a bottled song I'd once stolen out of the guild's own vaults and never turned in. Isandro had known it instantly when I'd hummed it to them. "That's a Wren song," they'd said, and gone pale in a way that told me more about the choir than they probably intended.

The answer came back up the passage a full minute later: the same three notes, ascending. Inverted. Everything down here was inverted; I should have taken it as an omen.

"You're late," said a voice from the shadow to my left, which was exactly where he'd said he wouldn't be, because stewards of the Mountain Queen are trained in nothing so thoroughly as lying about their positions.

"I'm early," I said. "You're just standing somewhere stupid."

Master Corvane—no, not master anymore, they'd stripped the title when they stripped his office, though he still wore the chain of keys under his coat like a widow wears a wedding ring—stepped out of the alcove. He was thinner than the last time I'd seen him, which had been across a bargaining table in the court of mirrors, back when I was selling and he was buying on the Queen's behalf and neither of us had yet done the thing that got us ruined. His hair had gone from iron-grey to ash-white in what couldn't have been more than a season. Down here, seasons are measured in how much of yourself the mirrors take.

"You brought it?" he asked.

I patted the satchel at my hip. "You brought yours?"

He drew out a ring of keys, black iron, each one shaped differently—one like a bird's foot, one like a half-moon, one that was just a bar with teeth on both sides. He held them up so the light of my lamp caught them, then put them away.

"Seal room, choir gallery, the Queen's private stair," he said. "And the door behind the throne that even she pretends isn't there."

"And the seal itself?"

"Under her hand at midnight performances. She rests her palm on it while the choir sings. Superstition." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She thinks the souls sing sweeter if she's touching the instrument of their binding. Maybe they do. I never asked them."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't have."

He flinched. Good. I wanted him flinching. A man who flinches remembers why.

* * *

We walked together through the service passages, the ones built for people whose job was to be unseen, and I'll tell you what surprised me most about Corvane: he talked. Not the polished court patter I remembered—he'd been famous for it, a man who could sell a war in six sentences—but low, ragged talk, the kind that leaks out of people who've been alone too long with what they've done.

"You think I want out for myself," he said, ducking under a lintel scored with claw-marks that predated the city's founding. "Everyone assumes that. Self-preservation. It's the only motive the court understands, so it's the only one anyone believes."

"What's the real one, then?"

He was quiet for eleven paces. I counted. Old habit.

"There's a soul in the choir," he said finally. "A girl named Wren. She was a singer above ground—a real one, taverns and festivals, nothing grand. She was sold by a landlord who owed the court a debt. I processed her intake. My hands on the page, my seal in the wax." He stopped walking. In the lamplight his face was the color of the stone. "Do you know what happens to a soul down here, thief? Each verse takes a memory. First it's small things—the name of a childhood dog, the taste of a particular bread. Then it's faces. Then it's the reason you were angry, and then the anger itself, and then—" He made a gesture, opening his hand like a book falling open to blank pages. "Wren forgot her brother last month. She asked me who kept writing letters to her, because the letters arrive and she doesn't know the name signed at the bottom anymore. And she smiled when she asked. That's the worst part. She smiled, because forgetting hurts less than remembering, and the Queen made sure of that."

"The brother," I said. My voice came out flat. "Dark hair? Came to the border gate asking after her, maybe four years back?"

Corvane stared at me. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I paid his name as toll to get in here once," I said. "A dead man's name. The gatekeeper took it and wore it like a coat." I started walking again, faster than I meant to. "Your Wren's brother is dead, Corvane. Has been for years. Drowned saving another kid, if the story I heard was true. And some part of me thought—if I gave the gate his name, maybe it would find its way to her. Stupid. Sentimental. Thief's arithmetic."

We walked in silence after that, but it was a different silence. Not the tenured dark. Something shared. Two people carrying the same weight on opposite shoulders walk steadier than one person carrying it alone—that's not wisdom, that's physics.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, we went over three times in a dry cistern off the choir gallery's service corridor, our voices dropped to the register you use in houses where the walls might be listening, because down here they always might.

Midnight performance. The choir sings in the Gallery of Mirrors, and the Queen sits enthroned with her palm on the Great Seal, drinking the sound like wine. Corvane, despite his disgrace, still carried keys enough to be assigned the gallery's upkeep—disgrace in the under-court means you're demoted to work, not dismissed, because dismissal would mean freedom and the Queen doesn't grant that. He would unlock the maintenance hatch behind the mirror-wall. I would go through the space between the glass and the stone—it was barely a body's width, and I've been through worse—and come out behind the throne dais while every eye in the court, living and otherwise, was fixed on the singing.

Then the hard part. The seal sat in a cradle of dragon-iron beside the Queen's hand. Dragon-iron, because everything important down here was bound in the stuff—the last leavings of the fire that was guttering out above us, and I tried not to think about that, about Isandro's clock running down while I crouched in a cistern rehearsing a burglary. Dragon-iron can't be lifted silently. It hums. It knows when it's moved.

"So we don't lift it silently," I said. "We lift it loudly, but at the right moment. When the choir hits the verse where the whole court weeps—there's always one, Corvane, I've heard it from outside the gates. Everyone's eyes shut. Even hers. Especially hers. Grief makes people honest, and honest people close their eyes."

"And if she doesn't close her eyes?"

"Then I'm fast, or I'm dead, and either way you'll have time to be elsewhere."

He didn't argue, which I respected. A man who argues about contingency plans is a man planning to blame you later.

But then he said the thing that changed the shape of the night. He said it quietly, looking at his keys instead of at me.

"When you have it—the seal. Don't run for the border. Run for the choir gallery floor. Put it down among them. Let the souls see it."

"Why?"

"Because binding works both ways, thief. That's the secret the Queen built her whole court on and hopes no one reads closely. The seal binds them—but it also binds *her*. Whatever claim runs through that iron runs both directions. If it's ever broken, or redirected, or—" he hesitated, choosing the word with visible care, "—*rewritten*, it won't just free them. It will reach her. And there is nothing in this world or under it she fears more than her own contract coming due."

I thought of Isandro then. Of ink-stained fingers and maps drawn of places that shouldn't exist, of a gift the crown hunted and the guild hid and the Queen wanted to buy. *Rewritten.* The word fit them the way a key fits a lock you haven't found yet.

"Someone I know is good at rewriting," I said.

"Then pray they're fast," Corvane said. "Because the fire above us is dying, and when it dies, the Queen doesn't need the seal to claim anything anymore. The seal is for *now*. After the fire, she holds every soul in that ledger by right of default." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reading his own obituary and taking notes. "Midnight tomorrow. After that, there is no bargain to make. There's only the fire going out, and everything it was holding back."

* * *

I climbed back toward the surface alone, through the cold and the tenured dark, past the little tower of river stones that nobody had knocked over in five hundred years.

At the third turn I stopped, because I'd heard something. Faint, rising up through the rock from far below—the choir, practicing, or grieving, or erasing themselves verse by verse for an audience of one. And threaded through it, thin as wire, a single voice I recognized, singing a lullaby I'd once stolen and never returned.

Wren. Singing a song about a brother whose name she no longer knew.

I pressed my palm against the cold stone and made the kind of promise you don't make out loud, because spoken promises can be used against you down here, and then I climbed.

Above, the sky over the city was the wrong color. I noticed it the moment I came out of the dye-house arch—dawn still hours off, but the horizon glowing anyway, a sickly amber, like the last coals of something enormous.

Like a fire, guttering.

Faster than it should be. Faster than anyone had calculated.

I ran.
