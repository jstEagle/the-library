## 15. Guttersong

The fire was dying, and I could hear it.

Not with my ears—though the sound was there too, a thin whine like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, coming up through the floorboards of the guild's back rooms. I heard it in my gift, in that second sight I'd spent thirty years pretending I didn't have: the dragon-fire beneath the guildhall had always been a shape on my inner map, a small gold knot of heat and old promises, steady as a heartbeat for as long as I'd been keeping ledgers. Steady as a heartbeat for longer than that, if you believed Vess, who claimed it had burned since the founding bargain, since some nameless cartographer had traded a map of the mountain's veins for a flame that would hold every guild member's name safe from the court below.

Now the knot was flickering. On my inner map it guttered like a candle in a doorway, flaring, shrinking, flaring again, and each time it shrank I felt something scrape past me in the dark—a sensation like a page turning somewhere I couldn't see, like a hand running down a ledger column looking for a particular entry.

I knew whose entry it was looking for. Mine.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Odile said from the doorway. She had a sack over one shoulder and soot on her cheekbone, which meant she'd been out in the city, which meant she'd seen it too. "The staring-at-nothing thing. The thing where your eyes go all faraway and your lips move like you're arguing with someone invisible."

"I'm mapping."

"You're brooding with extra steps." She dropped the sack on my desk, on top of the ledger fragments I'd spent three sleepless nights piecing together—the surviving pages, the ones Vess hadn't burned, the ones with my mother's handwriting crawling across them like a confession written in a language only I could read. "It's bad out there, Isandro. It's worse than bad. They took the Ferrier twins at dawn. Just walked into their shop on Candlemaker's Row and took them, no warrant read aloud, no questions asked. And the twins aren't even *gifted*, not properly—they just draw maps of places they've dreamed. But they're in the registry, because everyone's in the registry, because we were all so very clever about writing everything down."

"The registry is protection," I said. It came out flat and automatic, the guild catechism, the first thing every apprentice learned. *What is recorded cannot be erased. What is named under the fire cannot be taken.*

"Then why are they being taken?" Odile sat on the edge of my desk, which she knew I hated, and looked at me with those thief's eyes that saw through locks and lies with equal ease. "Vess keeps saying the fire will hold. Vess keeps saying the hunt will blow over, that crowns forget, that witch-hunters get reassigned. But you've seen it, haven't you? You've seen what I've seen."

I hadn't seen what she'd seen. I'd seen worse. I'd seen the gold knot gutter down to almost nothing while I slept, and woken with my heart hammering and my hands cold, certain that my name had already left my body like a tooth pulled in my absence.

"It's flickering," I admitted. Saying it aloud made it more real, which is the trouble with truth—it behaves like ink, spreading once it touches anything porous. "The dragon-fire. It's not holding. It's dying, Odile, and when it goes, every soul bound to the court reverts to the Mountain Queen. Every name in every ledger we didn't burn. Including mine."

"Including yours," she repeated softly. "And your mother's."

"My mother's soul has been singing in that crooked choir for thirty years. Mine's been *promised* to her for just as long, sitting in the court's coffers like a debt waiting to be collected. When the fire dies—" I stopped. My voice had gone strange. "When the fire dies, the Queen won't need to hunt anyone. She'll just reach out her hand and close it."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Outside, somewhere above us, a bell began ringing—not the hour, not an alarm, but the ragged urgent peal of a church tower where someone had climbed the stairs and was pulling the rope with panic in their arms. Then another bell joined it. Then a third.

"That's the signal," Odile said. "That's the guild's own bell. Vess called the members in."

"She never calls the members in."

"No," Odile agreed. "She doesn't."

* * *

The guildhall had never been full in my lifetime. That was rather the point of the Guild of Impossible Things—we were a scattered lot, cartographers and shadow-brokers and dealers in bottled lullabies, people who worked alone in back rooms and kept our gifts folded up small as pocket squares. We met perhaps twice a year, at solstice, to renew our marks and drink terrible wine and pretend we were colleagues rather than conspirators.

But the hall was full now. Full and frightened. Maybe sixty of us, maybe seventy, standing among the long tables where the ledgers used to sit before Vess burned half of them, and the air smelled of smoke that wasn't from any hearth. People were pressed together in knots—old Sera Halloway who dealt in forgotten birthdays, clutching her mark like a talisman; the Ferrier twins' cousin, white-faced, asking anyone who'd listen if anyone had heard word from the cells beneath the courthouse; young Pell, barely apprenticed, who mapped the insides of locked rooms and had been crying quietly since I came in.

And at the head of the hall, on the raised dais where the master's chair stood empty, Vess.

Guildmaster Vess was seventy if she was a day, built like a door, with a face that had spent decades learning not to react to anything. She'd taught me bookkeeping. She'd taught me how to record a soul-sale without flinching, how to write *one hidden child* in a ledger column as though it were a quantity of cloth. She'd told me once, when I was fifteen and reckless and had nearly let my gift show in front of a client, that invisibility was the guild's greatest art, and that the finest practitioners were never seen at all.

She looked at me across that crowded hall, and I watched her face do the thing it never did: react.

"Members of the guild," she said, and her voice carried the way it always did, level and dry as August. "You know why you're here. The crown's hunt has entered the city. The witch-hunter Corvane has warrants for forty-one names, and he knows where twenty-nine of them sleep. The Ferrier twins are in custody. Three others were taken in the night. The protections of this guild are failing."

A murmur went through the hall like wind through wheat. Someone said, loudly, "The fire holds. The fire has always held."

"The fire," Vess said, "is guttering."

Silence then. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your eardrums. I felt every eye in the room slide toward the floor, toward the vaults beneath us where the last dragon-fire burned in its iron cradle, fed on nothing, sustained by nothing but a bargain older than anyone present.

"How long?" That was Sera Halloway, her voice cracking on the second word.

"Days," Vess said. "Perhaps hours. Perhaps less."

"And the ledgers?" someone else called. "You burned them, guildmaster. You burned half of them without a vote. Did you burn the bindings too?"

I watched Vess's jaw tighten. I thought of the missing pages—my pages—and the entry in my mother's hand, and the way Vess had ordered silence instead of answers, and I felt the old dread turn over in my chest and show its teeth.

"The bindings cannot be burned," Vess said. "They were never ours to burn. They belong to the court beneath the mountain, by the terms of the founding bargain. What I burned were copies. Records. Things that could be used against us." Her gaze swept the hall. "What protects you is the fire. While it burns, your names are hidden. When it dies—"

"When it dies, we're hers," said a voice from the back, bitter and clear. "Every soul in her coffers. Isn't that the bargain? Didn't we all sign?"

"We signed," Vess said, "because the alternative was being hunted by the court instead of protected from it. Because a hundred years ago the Queen's collectors took gifted children off the streets, and the founders chose servitude of paper over slavery of flesh. It wasn't a good bargain. It was the only one on offer." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that I couldn't read, and I have spent my life reading faces the way other people read street signs. "I am telling you now what I should have told you weeks ago. The fire is dying. The court will come to collect. And I am negotiating."

"*Negotiating,*" someone echoed.

"With whom?" I said.

It came out louder than I intended. The hall turned toward me. Vess's eyes found mine and held them, and for a moment—one long, terrible moment—I saw her weigh something, the way she weighed entries in a ledger, debits against credits, and I understood with sick certainty that I was in one of her columns.

"With the crown," she said finally. "With Corvane. With anyone who can buy time until the fire can be renewed. That is what guildmasters do, Isandro. They make ugly bargains so that others don't have to."

"And what are you offering him?" I asked. "To buy time. What's the price?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere beneath our feet, faint as a dying man's pulse, the dragon-fire whined.

"Go home," Vess said. "All of you. Bar your doors. Burn anything with your handwriting on it. Wait."

*Wait.* The word followed us out of the hall like a debt collector.

* * *

Odile walked me back through streets that had gone wrong overnight. I don't know how else to describe it—I've mapped this city in my head for thirty years, every alley and stair and rooftop, and suddenly the map was inaccurate, because fear redraws streets faster than any surveyor. Doors that had always stood open were barred. Windows that had always glowed were dark. On the corner of Bellwether Lane, someone had chalked a crude eye on the wall with a slash through it—the old symbol, the one that meant *a seer lives here, beware*—except now the slash meant the opposite. Now it meant *here is one to be taken.*

"They're marking houses," Odile murmured. "Corvane's people. Chalk marks. See how the tail of the eye curls left? That's his warrant-men's sign. I've seen it in three districts tonight."

We passed the Ferriers' shop. The door was sealed with red wax—the crown's wax, the same wax from the proclamation, stamped with the mountain sigil that everyone pretended was merely decorative heraldry and everyone knew was not. Through the window I could see the workshop exactly as the twins had left it: half-finished maps pinned to the walls, dreams of coastlines that didn't exist rendered in careful watercolor. A warrant-man's boot had crushed one, a single heel-print dead center in a dreamt ocean.

"They weren't dangerous," Odile said. "They drew places nobody could go. Who does that threaten?"

"Everyone," I said, "if the person in power believes that a map is a claim. Which it is. That's the whole secret of my trade, Odile. To map a thing is to say *this exists, and here is how to reach it, and here is what it costs.* The crown isn't afraid of the twins. The crown is afraid of what the twins prove—that reality has soft edges. That the world can be redrawn."

I stopped walking. I stood in the middle of Bellwether Lane like an idiot, in the open, in the marked dark, because a thought had arrived whole, the way the true ones do, and it had knocked the breath out of me.

"What," Odile said.

"The bindings can't be burned because they belong to the court. That's what Vess said. But bindings are just agreements written down, and agreements are just maps of obligation—who owes what, to whom, along which lines. And I'm a cartographer." My heart was going hard. "Not a broker, not a scribe. A cartographer. I map what isn't there, Odile. I've spent my whole life hiding it because the guild taught me that the gift was a liability. But what if it's the opposite? What if it's the one tool that can touch a binding?"

"You're talking about rewriting the contract."

"I'm talking about redrawing the map underneath it."

She stared at me. Above us, the bells had gone quiet, and in that quiet I heard it again—the whine from below, thinner than before. Fainter. The gold knot on my inner map shrank, and I gasped, actually gasped, because for half a heartbeat my name simply *wasn't there*. Not hidden. Absent. Like a room with the furniture carried out.

"Isandro." Odile caught my arm. "Isandro, look at me. Your eyes just went completely wrong. What happened?"

"The fire flickered." I steadied myself against a wall. The chalk eye on the brick seemed to watch me. "For a moment I couldn't feel my own name. It's starting, Odile. The collection. It's starting early, or the fire is dying faster than Vess knows, or—" I stopped. Or Vess knew exactly how fast it was dying, and had known for weeks, and had spent those weeks negotiating with Corvane over a price list that had my name on it.

Or Vess had sold my pages thirty years ago and had been managing the interest ever since.

"We can't wait," I said. "Waiting is what got the twins taken. Waiting is what got half the ledgers burned. Every hour we stand still, the debt gets bigger. I have to go down. Tonight. Now. Before the fire goes out and takes my name with it."

"Down." Odile said it the way you'd say *overboard*. "To the court. Through the unmapped stair."

"There is no unmapped stair yet. There's going to be. I'm going to draw it." I looked at her, and I hated what I was about to do, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was doing it later with less time. "I owe you the truth, since you're about to risk your neck on it. My mother sold a soul to the court thirty years ago. The ledger says it was mine. I think—I think the truth is worse and better than that, and I think the answer is down there, in the choir, in whatever's left of her. And whether I find my mother or just my own stolen self, I have to go before that fire dies. Because when it dies, the Queen collects everything at once, and there will be nothing left to steal back."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Down the lane, a patrol lantern swung into view, and she pulled us both into a doorway with the practiced ease of her trade, and we stood chest to chest in the dark while the light crawled past, and I felt her heart going as fast as mine.

"You know what my brother used to say?" she whispered, when the lantern had gone. "He used to say that debts don't get smaller with time. They just get heavier. You carried mine for me once, Isandro, when carrying it nearly broke you. So here's my answer: yes. Tonight. Now. Show me where the stair goes, and I'll walk it with you."

Above us, distantly, the guild bell began to ring again—but wrong this time, arrhythmic, panicked, the pattern that meant *members taken, members taken, members taken.*

And beneath us, so faint I might have imagined it, the dragon-fire gave a sound I had never heard it give in thirty years of listening.

It cracked. Like ice. Like a bone.

Like a page being torn out.

Odile looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said what we both understood: whatever time we'd thought we had, we had less.

"Draw fast," she said.
