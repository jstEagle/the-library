## 16. Theft of a Seal

The steward's name was Pellam Hoss, and he had the particular smell of a man who had spent thirty years standing behind a throne: lamp oil, dust, and the faint sourness of swallowed opinions.

He met us in a disused cistern two levels below the choir gallery, holding a shuttered lantern like it might bite him. I'd expected a courtier—silk and simpering. What I got was a stooped man in a grey coat with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had counted other people's treasures so long he'd forgotten he owned nothing himself.

"You're the thief," he said. Not a question. He looked me over the way you'd inspect a lockpick found in your own desk drawer: with professional resentment.

"I'm the debt," I said. "Isandro Okonkwo holds the paper on me. They've called it in."

"Cartographers." He spat the word like a seed. "My grandmother sold maps at the fish market. Honest ones. You people draw doors that aren't there and then act surprised when something walks through them."

"And yet here you are," I said, "about to help one walk through yours."

That shut him up for a moment, which was the most I could hope for. Isandro was above us somewhere—or below us, depending on how you counted, since everything under the mountain was a question of which direction the lies pointed—waiting for word that we'd secured the Queen's seal. Without it, the ledger page binding their soul and their mother's to this place might as well be carved into bedrock. With it, we could lift it out of the record like a splinter.

Pellam set the lantern down and unrolled a diagram of the palace drawn from memory, his hand steady even as his voice wasn't. "The seal lives in the Signet Room, behind the choir gallery. The Queen attends the midnight performance every night without fail—she calls it *maintenance*. While she watches, the room is guarded by two of the Hollow Men." He paused. "You know what those are?"

"I've seen them from a distance. They don't blink."

"They don't breathe either. They're what's left of guards who failed her once. She keeps them working as a courtesy to the next failure." He tapped the diagram. "But during the third verse of the midnight song, the whole court kneels. Guards included. It's not devotion—it's compulsion. The music bends everyone who owes her anything. Which is everyone."

"Not you?"

"I owe her my life," Pellam said flatly. "She took it forty years ago and never gave it back. Debts to the dead don't bind the same way." Something moved behind his face, quick and buried. "I'll go in while the court kneels. You watch the gallery door. If anyone comes early—if the song falters—you signal, and we run, and if we can't run, you leave me."

"That's a generous offer."

"It's not an offer. It's arithmetic. The seal is worth more than I am. Even I know that, and I'm the one being spent." He rolled up the diagram. "One more thing. If I'm taken—" he looked at me directly for the first time, and I saw how tired he was, tired down to the marrow, the kind of tired that has made its peace with itself— "she will ask me questions. I want you to know now that I intend to lie. But I'm an old man who has never been tortured before, and I'd rather you didn't count on my courage. Count on your legs instead."

I should have argued. A better person would have argued. Instead I heard myself say, "Understood," because he was right, and because some part of me—the part that had survived the guild by learning exactly what things cost—had already done the arithmetic and agreed with him.

Gods forgive accountants. We're all of us just keeping books for someone.

* * *

Midnight under the mountain doesn't arrive so much as accumulate. There's no sky, no bells, only a slow thickening of the dark until the lamps along the corridors burn lower, as if the mountain itself were drowsing. Pellam and I moved through it in single file, him leading, me memorizing every turn—not because I needed the route, but because mapping is what I do when I'm afraid. Odile the cartographer's apprentice, drawing exits she hoped she'd never need.

The choir gallery was built like an amphitheatre turned inside out, all the seats facing a mirrored stage where the sold souls stood in their crooked crescent. I'd heard them once before, from far off, and thought it beautiful. Up close, beauty curdled. Each voice was lovely the way a wound is red—technically true, and missing the entire point. And beneath the harmonies, if you listened wrong, you could hear the seams: verses that repeated, words worn smooth as river stones, sung by throats that had been singing them for decades.

Isandro's voice was in there somewhere. That thought sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

Below us, the Mountain Queen settled into her seat of black glass. She was smaller than I'd imagined, and stiller—a woman shaped like a held breath. When she raised one hand, the first verse began, and the entire court knelt as one, a thousand bodies folding down with a sound like wind through wheat.

Pellam squeezed my shoulder once and slipped away toward the Signet Room.

I took my position by the gallery door and did what thieves do best, which is stand very still and look like furniture. The second verse rose. In the kneeling crowd I saw faces lit by mirror-light, and I noticed—I couldn't help noticing—that some of them were crying as they knelt. Tears running freely down devoted cheeks. Compulsion, Pellam had said. But I wondered how much of worship is just compulsion that's had time to settle in and call itself love.

The third verse began.

And the song faltered.

It was small—a half-beat where a half-beat shouldn't be, one voice in the crescent stumbling over a word it must have sung ten thousand times. Wren, I'd learn later. A soul near the end of their erasure, reaching for a memory that wasn't there anymore and finding the verse built on it gone soft as rot. The Queen's head tilted. One of her attendants leaned toward her. Somewhere in the palace, a mechanism older than any of us began to consider whether tonight required attention.

*Come on, Pellam,* I thought. *Old man, move.*

The gallery door behind me clicked.

Not opened—clicked. The sound of a lock being tested from the other side, or a key turning halfway, or a guard deciding to check on a room no one ever checked on because the song always held. I pressed into the shadow of the doorframe and put my hand on my knife and did the arithmetic again, fast and ugly: if that door opened, I could not let whoever came through it reach the Signet Room corridor. Pellam had been clear. The seal was worth more than both of us.

The click resolved into nothing. A servant, maybe. A rat in boots. The song steadied, Wren's voice finding its place again or being dragged back into it, and the moment passed the way dangerous moments do—leaving you shaking at your own relief, disgusted by how cheap your life suddenly felt to you.

Then Pellam was beside me, and his fist opened, and there it was: the Queen's seal, a disc of black glass banded in dragonbone, heavy as a sin and cold as one too. His hands were bleeding. He'd cut them getting it out of whatever cradle they kept it in, and he hadn't made a sound.

"Go," he whispered.

We went.

* * *

We almost made it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives all the others: we almost made it, and the fault was mine, and I will carry it the way Pellam carried the seal—with both hands, all the way down.

There's a junction beneath the west galleries where three corridors meet, and I mapped our route through it on the way in. On the way out, I chose the left passage. It was the faster one. It was also, it turned out, the one the Hollow Men used, because guards patrol fastest routes too, and I had been so busy memorizing exits that I forgot to memorize habits. Cartography is not just drawing what's there. It's drawing what *does* there. Isandro would have known that. Isandro had tried to teach me that, back in the guild's back rooms, over candle stubs and stolen wine, and I'd laughed and said habits don't leave footprints.

They leave footprints. Two of them, seven feet tall, breathing nothing, blinking never, standing at the junction like the answer to a question I should have asked.

Pellam didn't hesitate. That's the thing I keep returning to, the thing that sits on my chest at night. He didn't hesitate at all. He shoved the seal into my hands, turned, and walked straight at the Hollow Men with his bleeding fists raised and his voice cracked wide open, shouting—shouting!—in a palace where silence was law, giving every ear in the dark a direction that wasn't mine.

"*Run, girl!*"

I ran. Of course I ran. He'd priced it out for me in the cistern, and I'd agreed, and agreeing is a debt like any other. I ran with the seal against my sternum and his shout chasing me through three turnings, and I told myself it was what he wanted, and it *was* what he wanted, and none of that helped even a little.

They caught him at the second turning. I know because I heard it—the wet, unhurried sounds of the Hollow Men doing what they do—and because his shouting stopped mid-word, which is somehow worse than a scream. Screams fade. A stopped word just hangs there forever, unfinished, waiting for you to come back and complete it.

I didn't go back. I ran until the corridors went soft and unfamiliar, until I found the crack in the wall that led to the disused cistern, and I sealed myself in the dark with a stolen seal and a debt I'd promised to deliver, and I shook, and I hated myself efficiently, thoroughly, in the guild manner—itemized, categorized, each failure entered neatly in its column.

Then I stopped shaking, because somewhere above me, muffled through forty feet of stone, I heard the beginning of a sound I knew from the witch-hunter's manuals I'd once stolen and read out of morbid curiosity: the specific quiet of a room prepared for questioning.

Pellam had told me he intended to lie.

I lay back against the cold stone and stared into the dark and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one I'd been avoiding since the cistern. An old man, never tortured, thirty years of swallowed opinions finally being asked to come back up. How long would his courage hold? Minutes? Less? And what name would they want from him? Not the Queen's enemies in general—they had those by the sackful. They'd want the specific one. The thief. The accomplice. The girl who ran.

They'd want *Odile*.

My name. My actual, true, guild-registered name, the one stitched into my protection mark, the one that meant the guild's old bargains still, technically, covered me. Give it up under torture and it wouldn't just reach the Queen's ears. Names travel. Names are currency, and currency circulates, and the witch-hunter upstairs had been buying everything the guild would sell.

I had perhaps an hour before Pellam's courage ran out and my name started spending itself across two kingdoms.

An hour to get the seal to Isandro. An hour to decide whether to run for the surface instead—to burn my mark like I'd burned it once before in a doorway, sever the protection, become nobody, unfindable, free. Nobody can be ransomed. Nobody can be traded. I'd done it before and it had cost me everything except my life, and I remembered, lying in that cistern, exactly how little that had felt like afterward.

The seal was warm now against my chest. My body heat, nothing more. But I pretended, for a few seconds, that it was the dragon-fire itself, guttering, calling time on all of us.

"Stay alive, old man," I whispered to the stone, knowing he probably couldn't hear me, knowing that if he could, he'd tell me to stop wasting breath on sentiment and start wasting it on distance.

I got up. I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, once, hard, the way you close a ledger at the end of a bad day. Then I stepped out of the cistern into the dark and began to map my way toward the choir gallery, toward Isandro, toward the page that held two souls and one chance—while somewhere above me, in a room I refused to picture, an honest man was about to buy my survival with the only coin he had left.

His name for mine.

And mine, it turned out, was already in the market.
