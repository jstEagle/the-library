## 17. The Queen's Offer, Refused

The Mountain Queen did not send guards for me. She sent a mirror.

It arrived at my cell—*guest chamber*, the steward had insisted, and I will grant that the bed was generous and the wine was not poisoned, which in a place like this passes for hospitality—carried by two servants who never once looked at it. That was the first wrong thing. You do not carry a mirror without looking into it unless you have been taught, thoroughly and recently, what happens to those who do.

The mirror was taller than I am, framed in black wood carved with mountains that weren't the mountains above us. These were older. Hungrier. The glass didn't reflect the room. It reflected a corridor of pale stone lit by candles that burned without flickering, and at the end of that corridor, a throne.

And on the throne, a woman who raised one finger.

I want to be clear about what happened next, because later accounts would get it wrong: I did not step through the mirror. The mirror stepped around me. One moment I was standing in a guest chamber beneath a mountain, and the next I was standing at the end of a long approach, and the Queen was watching me with the patient expression of someone who has done this ten thousand times and been refused perhaps twice.

"Cartographer," she said. Her voice was the sound a mountain makes when it settles. Not loud. Inevitable. "You've been walking my halls for three days, and you still believe you're hiding."

"I don't hide," I said. "I *map*. There's a difference. Hiding is passive."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Amusement, or the memory of amusement, worn smooth like everything else down here from too much use.

"You found the choir."

"I found the choir."

"And your voice among them." She said it as fact, not question. Of course she knew. She'd known before I crossed her border; she'd known, I suspect, since the night my mother signed a page in a guild back room and wept over the ink. "Tell me what you heard."

I could have lied. I considered it, briefly, the way one considers jumping off a bridge—one acknowledges the option and then notes that it leads somewhere bad. But there was something in the way she asked. Not a trap. Curiosity. Genuine, terrible curiosity, the kind that has kept her alive for centuries while everyone around her became furniture.

"I heard myself," I said. "Singing a song I've never learned. And underneath it—" My throat tightened. I made myself finish. "Underneath it, a voice I know better than my own. My mother's."

The Queen inclined her head. Around us, the mirrored court bowed with her—a hundred reflections of a hundred courtiers, all moving in perfect unison, all of them slightly out of sync with the people they were copies of. That's the thing about this place I understood only later: the mirrors don't show you what's there. They show you what was promised. Every reflection in this court was a soul that had been sold and was still, in some sense, waiting for delivery.

"Your mother," the Queen said, "had excellent penmanship."

And that—that was the moment I stopped being afraid.

Not because the fear went away. Fear doesn't go away; it just gets outranked. But something in the casual cruelty of it—the *penmanship*, as if my mother were a clerk and not a woman who traded herself into an eternity of singing so her child could walk free—something in that snapped a wire in me that had been holding taut for thirty years.

"You've read the ledger," I said.

"I wrote half of it."

"No," I said. "You didn't. You bought half of it. Writing requires knowing what the words mean."

The court went very still. Mirrors have a particular quality of stillness—they can't help reflecting it back at you, doubled and tripled until the silence itself seems multiplied. Somewhere far off, faint as breath, the choir sang its crooked verses, and I felt each note like a hook behind my ribs.

The Queen rose from her throne. Up close she was less a monarch than a collection of debts wearing a crown—there were seams in her, places where the light didn't quite reach, and I realized with a cartographer's certainty that she was *mapped onto*, not mapped. Someone had drawn her, ages ago, out of bargains and appetite, and she had been holding the shape ever since by making others hold it for her.

"You are angry," she observed.

"I'm a professional," I said. "Anger comes later, filed under miscellaneous."

That earned me a real smile, brief and sharp as a paper cut. "Good. Anger makes people stupid, and I am about to make you an offer that requires intelligence." She descended the last steps and stood before me, close enough that I could see my own reflection scattered across her eyes—dozens of tiny Isandros, all looking back at me with expressions I hadn't decided to wear yet. "The fire above is dying. You know this. When it gutters out, every name the guild protected reverts to me. Every hidden gift, every unmapped talent, every soul that ever signed a page to stay invisible—they become mine, and I will finally have use for them."

"Use," I repeated. "You mean the choir."

"The choir is a fraction. A demonstration. What I intend—" she gestured, and the mirrors around us shifted, showing cities I recognized and cities I didn't, coastlines that bent wrong, rivers flowing uphill through country no one had surveyed, "—is everything. Realms beyond your maps, cartographer. Places that exist only because no one has drawn them yet, which means they cannot be governed, taxed, claimed, or saved. Chaos, wearing the costume of freedom. I have waited four hundred years for a tool that could see them."

She turned to face me fully, and the whole court held its breath—or would have, if mirrors breathed.

"Map for me," the Queen said. "Not as a slave. Not as a sold thing. As my Cartographer Royal. Your name spoken in my court with honor. Your mother's soul released from the choir—today, before the fire dies, whole and singing free. Every comfort, every protection, every resource of the under-mountain laid open to you." She paused, and here her voice softened into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "No one has ever valued what you can do, Isandro Okonkwo. The guild hid you. Your mother spent you like coin. The crown hunts you. I am the first power in your entire life to look at your gift and say: *yes, exactly this, more of it.*"

And here is the part I'm not proud of: for three full seconds, I wanted it.

Three seconds. That's how long the offer took to sink its teeth in. Because she was right, and that was the poison of it. The guild had hidden me. My mother had—no. I stopped there. I stopped on that thought the way you stop on a stair when the next step isn't where your foot expects it.

Because Odile had told me, two nights ago in the dark below the stair, what the dying scribe confessed. *She didn't sell you. She sold herself. The price was her soul for your freedom, and Vess voided the bargain.* My mother hadn't spent me like coin. My mother had spent *herself*, and men in ink-stained chairs had quietly rewritten the receipt.

The Queen was offering me truth wrapped around a lie, and the lie was load-bearing.

"You're waiting for my answer," she said.

"I'm waiting for you to finish," I said. "Offers like yours always have a second half. The map the realms, the honored name—and then what? I draw you the roads into the unmapped places, and then the people living at their edges start appearing in ledgers. Sold. Sung. Filed." I looked past her, at the mirrors, at all those waiting reflections. "You don't want a cartographer. You want a foreman."

The softness left her face. What remained was older and more honest, and I respected it more. "The alternative," she said, "is that you refuse, and I take what I was always owed. The fire dies within days. Your guild's protections die with it. And every bound soul in my ledger—including yours, including hers—comes home to me regardless. Refusal changes nothing except how comfortable your last week is."

"There it is," I said. "The second half."

"Was I wrong to make the offer?"

"No," I said, and meant it. "You were wrong about one thing only. Small thing. Technicality, really."

Her eyes narrowed. Behind her, the court leaned in—not literally, mirrors can't lean, but the light did, the whole reflected world tilting toward us like an ear cupped to hear.

"When my mother came to your border," I said, "she didn't sell me. She sold *herself*. One soul, freely given, in exchange for her child's freedom. That was the bargain. That was the price paid in full." I took the folded scrap of ledger fragment from inside my coat—the one I'd pieced together from Vess's burnings, my mother's handwriting marching across it like soldiers—and I held it up between us. "But a bargain requires both parties to keep their side. The guild voided theirs. Which means the consideration failed, which means—" I let myself smile, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's shoes. "Which means the contract was fraud from the foundation. And your Majesty, of all people, knows what fraud does to a claim."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere in the deep of the mountain, the dragon-fire guttered—I felt it, a shiver through the stone, the whole under-court flinching like a body whose heart skipped.

"You would argue *law* to me," the Queen said slowly, "in my own court."

"I would argue law anywhere," I said. "It's the family trade. Ink, signatures, small print. My mother taught me penmanship after all—just not the kind you meant." I stepped closer, close enough that our reflections merged in her eyes, and I said the rest loudly enough for the whole mirrored court to hear, because some things should be said on the record:

"Those souls were never yours to buy. Not mine, not my mother's, not Wren's, not any of the hundred voices in that choir. A soul given in love cannot be transferred by fraud. A soul taken by trickery has no owner but itself. You have spent four hundred years collecting receipts for things that were stolen, and calling the theft purchase." My voice didn't shake. I checked. It didn't shake. "I am Isandro Okonkwo, cartographer, and I am telling you: the map ends here. Your claim is a forgery, and I am the one person alive who can prove it line by line."

For a moment—just a moment—she looked afraid.

Then the fear closed over like water, and the Queen smiled, and it was the worst thing I had seen in a court full of terrible things, because it was patient.

"Prove it," she said softly. "You'll need the original page to prove anything, little cartographer. The binding ledger. Both souls, one entry, under my seal." She turned back toward her throne, trailing candlelight. "And my seal does not leave my hand."

Behind her, in the deepest mirror, I saw a flicker of movement—Odile, somewhere in the servants' corridors, moving fast, with a disgraced steward beside her and larceny written all over both their faces.

"Everything leaves your hand eventually," I said. "That's what hands are for."

The Queen paused on the third step. She didn't turn around.

"The choir sings at midnight," she said. "Attend if you like. It's the last performance before the fire dies, and I do so love an audience for the finale."

* * *

They let me walk back unguarded, which told me everything. Either she considered me already caught, or she wanted me to run so the running could be documented. With monarchs, these are usually the same plan.

In the guest chamber, the mirror had gone dark, showing nothing but my own face—which, after a day in that court, felt like an act of mercy. I sat on the generous bed with the unpoisoned wine and laid out what I had: a forged claim, a dying fire, a mother's handwriting, and roughly forty hours.

Forty hours to steal a seal from the hand of a queen.

I thought about my mother's voice in the choir, singing verses that erased her. I thought about Wren, who couldn't remember rain. I thought about the word the scribe had died whispering—not *sold*, not *sorry*, but *stay*, though I wouldn't learn until later why that word mattered.

And I thought about the fact that I had just, for the first time in my life, said my own name out loud in a room full of powers, and the room had blinked first.

"Not bad," I told my reflection, "for someone who used to balance other people's accounts."

My reflection didn't answer. But somewhere below, faint through miles of stone, the choir began to warm up—and beneath their crooked harmony, I could swear I heard footsteps in the servants' corridors, moving toward the throne room, quick and quiet and absolutely certain.

Midnight was coming. So was Odile.

All we needed now was a miracle with a handle on it, and I knew exactly where the Queen kept hers.
