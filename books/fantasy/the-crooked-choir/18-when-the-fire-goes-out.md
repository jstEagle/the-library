## 18. When the Fire Goes Out

The last dragon-fire did not go out the way fires are supposed to. It didn't gutter, or shrink, or fade politely into embers. It simply stopped—mid-flicker, mid-breath, like a singer cut off on a held note—and the silence that followed was worse than any sound I have ever heard.

I felt it in my teeth first. Then in the ledger page pressed against my chest, the one with two names in my mother's handwriting, which had been warm as a living thing since Odile stole the Queen's seal and now went cold as riverbed stone.

"Oh," I said, to no one, in the small voice of a person watching a door close that they were standing in.

Then the mountain began to sing back.

* * *

You have to understand what a claim sounds like when it comes from something old. The Mountain Queen's voice didn't travel through the air; it traveled through *ownership*. It arrived in the marrow of every bound soul in the under-court at once, and because I had spent three days holding a page that bound mine, I heard it from the inside.

*MINE,* said the mountain, in the tone of a landlady who has finally read the lease. *ALL OF IT. ALL OF THEM.*

The mirrored palace shuddered. In the great hall below, the crooked choir—the stolen souls, Wren among them, singing their fading verses for the Queen's amusement—staggered mid-phrase. I could see them from the gallery where Odile and I crouched behind a pillar of black glass: dozens of figures in grey, their mouths opening, their eyes going wide and then strangely empty, like windows in a house whose owner has died.

The claim was pulling them. Not their bodies—their *selves*, out through the mouth, up through the throat, into the Queen's mirrors, where she would keep them the way misers keep coins: never spent, never seen, never free.

"Isandro." Odile's hand closed on my wrist. Her knuckles were still split from the steward's cell, her guild mark burned off her own arm by her own hand, and she looked at me with the flat calm of a thief assessing a lock she doesn't like the look of. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a gift," I said. "And a page. And approximately no plan."

"That's the worst sentence I've ever heard."

"It gets worse. The gift is maps."

She stared at me. "Maps."

"Maps of things that aren't there," I said. "Which is about to be very useful, because in about thirty seconds, everything those souls are is going to stop being there."

Below us, the Queen rose from her throne of stacked mirrors. She was taller than I remembered, or perhaps the room had decided to flatter her. Her gown moved like spilled ink. And in her hand she held a thing I recognized with a lurch of pure professional horror: a ledger. Not mine. Hers. The master record, the original binding, the book in which every sold soul in the under-court was written down in a hand older than my mother's mother.

She opened it. The pages turned themselves.

And the choir began to rise.

Not walk. *Rise.* Their feet left the floor an inch, then a handspan, souls streaming out of them in threads of pale light, winding upward toward the Queen's open mouth like smoke deciding to become a person again—but the wrong person, only one person, all of them funneling into her, and I understood with terrible clarity that she wasn't collecting them. She was *eating*. Decades of hoarded song, swallowed whole before the protections died and someone like me came along to make trouble.

Wren was third from the left. I had spoken to her once, briefly, in the echoing dark behind the choir stalls—a girl who'd been sold for a debt she hadn't incurred, who remembered her brother's face but not her brother's name, and who had asked me, in a voice worn down to thread, whether the sun was still yellow up there. I had promised her nothing, because I am not in the habit of promising things I can't map.

I looked at the page against my chest. Two names. My mother's soul and mine, bound together in faded iron-gall ink, held fast by a seal that Odile had bled for.

And I thought: *a binding is just a map.*

A map of ownership. A map drawn by someone who wanted a thing to stay put. Lines and borders and claims, ink on vellum, saying *this belongs here, this belongs to me, this cannot leave.*

I have spent my whole life drawing maps of places that don't exist. Nobody ever told me the second half of the gift—the part Vess spent decades teaching me to hide, the part my mother paid a soul to conceal. Maps don't just record what is. A true cartographer's map *insists*. Draw a road well enough, believe it hard enough, and the world grudgingly makes room for it.

The Queen was redrawing her ledger right now, mid-claim, amending the bindings to pull everything in at once. She was, in the most literal sense possible, *mapping*.

And I had been doing this longer than she had.

"Odile," I said. "How much do you trust me?"

"Not at all," she said. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to need you to hold this page flat and not ask questions."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about using your gift openly for the first time after a lifetime of hiding it: it feels like unclenching a jaw you forgot was set.

I stepped out from behind the pillar. The Queen saw me immediately—of course she did; the palace was hers, the light bent toward her—and her smile was the smile of a creditor finding a debtor at last.

"The cartographer," she said. Around her, the threads of soul-light thickened, the choir rising higher, their songs thinning to whistles, to whispers, to almost-nothing. "Come to watch? Or come to bargain? My offer stands. Map my conquests, and your name comes off the page. You above all should understand, little scribe. Everything is a ledger. Everything is a claim. I merely hold more of them than anyone."

"You hold them wrong," I said.

Her smile flickered. Just at the corner. "Excuse me?"

"A claim is a line," I said, walking forward, my heart hammering so hard I could feel it in my ears, my throat, my teeth. "A line says *here is the edge of what's yours*. But lines have to be drawn *accurately*, Your Majesty. That's the whole discipline. And your ledger—" I pulled the page from my coat, and Odile, bless her thieving heart, stepped up beside me and held it flat between her bleeding hands without being asked twice. "Your ledger contains a fraud."

The hall went quiet, except for the rising song.

"My mother's entry," I said. "Read it. *One hidden child, price paid in full.* Except it wasn't paid by her, was it? She sold herself for my freedom, and your steward's records show Vess voided the exchange—kept the payment, returned the goods. You took a soul under false pretenses. And a binding built on fraud—" I lifted the seal Odile had stolen, its weight suddenly enormous, suddenly exactly right. "A binding built on fraud isn't a claim. It's a *mistake*. And mistakes get corrected."

"You dare," the Queen said, "to audit *me*?"

"Ma'am," I said, "auditing is the family business."

And I drew.

Not with pen. With will. With the gift I had hidden since childhood, the sense that lived in my fingers and my sleep and the margins of every honest map I'd ever faked—I reached out and *saw* the bindings, all of them, hundreds of lines running from the Queen's ledger into the chests of the rising choir, taut as guy-wires, humming with the force of her claim. And I saw the flaw in each one, the same flaw, stamped through the whole lattice like a watermark: *fraud, fraud, fraud*, the original sin of the first false sale repeated down the decades, every binding downstream of a lie.

Every line can be redrawn.

I put my hand on the page—my mother's handwriting, my own name, the seal—and I pushed.

It was like pushing a river. The claim fought me; the Queen's will came down on mine like a mountain, because she was a mountain, she had always been the mountain, patient and vast and certain that everything loose eventually rolls downhill to her. The threads of soul-light strained. Wren's voice cracked on a note. Somewhere in the lattice, my own name lit up like a struck match, and the claim seized it, and *pulled*—

—and I let it.

For one horrible, endless heartbeat I let the claim take my name, let it run down the binding toward me, because a claim has to arrive somewhere, has to *land*, has to have coordinates. And I gave it coordinates. I fed the Queen's own power into the map I was redrawing, used her claim as the ink, her hunger as the compass needle, and I turned every line—

—from the souls

—to the source.

To the ledger in her hands.

"*No,*" said the Mountain Queen, in a very different voice.

The bindings snapped taut, reversed, and *rewrote themselves*. Hundreds of lines of ownership, decades of sold and stolen selves, all of them re-aimed in one screaming instant at the single object every one of them had been written *into*: the master ledger itself. The claim hit the book like a wave hitting a seawall that has quietly become a door.

The Queen screamed. The choir fell—gently, all at once, like puppets whose strings had been cut by someone kind—and lay gasping on the marble, whole, present, *theirselves* again. Wren rolled onto her side and I saw her mouth move, shaping a name, her brother's name, remembered, returned.

And the ledger burned.

Not with fire. With *claim*. Every soul it had ever bound now claimed the only thing the reversed lines touched: the book, the binding, the institution of the binding. The vellum blackened. The Queen's fingers, still gripping it, began to smoke, and she could not let go—of course she couldn't; she was the greatest holder of claims in the world, and the claim was now *hers to hold*, and holding was the one thing she could never refuse.

"You cannot," she said, backing away, trailing ash, her mirrors cracking one by one around the hall like ice on a pond. "The book is *mine*—"

"It was," I agreed. "Past tense. Check the grammar. Cartographers are very precise about tense."

The last page curled. The last line of the last binding glowed like a coal and went out.

The mountain stopped singing.

In the ringing dark, Odile exhaled beside me, long and shaky, and said, "So when you said 'no plan'—"

"I improvised," I said. My legs chose that moment to inform me they were done, and I sat down rather abruptly on the cold marble next to a choir member who was crying and laughing in equal measure. "It's a cartography term."

The Queen was still standing, barely, in a ring of shattered glass, holding a book that was now only ash and leather and nothing, staring at her empty hands. She looked, for the first time, small. Old. Like something that had been sitting still so long it had mistaken itself for geography.

But mountains do not die in an afternoon. She raised her head, and her eyes found mine across the ruined hall, and what was in them was not gratitude and was not defeat. It was arithmetic.

"This isn't over, cartographer," she said. "A claim reversed is still a claim. And the first soul those lines touched—" her ash-grey lips peeled back from her teeth, "—was written in your mother's hand."

Cold walked up my spine. Because she was right. The reversal had run backward along every binding—to the source, to the origin, to the oldest entry, the first fraud, the page that started everything.

The page in my mother's handwriting.

The page that was, even now, glowing softly in Odile's hands, warm again, alive again, *pulling*—

And somewhere far above us, faint through miles of stone, I heard a voice I knew, calling a name that was mine.
