## 19. Ash and Ink

### Isandro

Here is what nobody tells you about burning a book that holds souls: it weighs more than it should.

The ledger page trembled in my hands — the page with two names in my mother's handwriting, mine and hers, bound to the court beneath the mountain by ink and fraud and thirty years of institutional cowardice. The Queen's seal was still warm where Odile had pressed it into the wax, and around us the mirrored palace rang with a sound I will hear until I die: the crooked choir, singing their own names back to themselves.

"Isandro," my mother's voice said — not from the choir stands, not from any throat, but from everywhere at once, the way a voice lives in a room after the singer has left it. "Now."

I struck the flint.

I want to tell you I hesitated. That some part of me understood the gravity, weighed the consequences, performed the solemn arithmetic of a cartographer who has spent his whole life measuring things. I didn't. My hands shook so badly the first spark missed entirely, and the second caught on the third try, and when the flame took the page's edge I laughed — actually laughed, high and unhinged, because of course. Of course the thing that unmade the Mountain Queen was fire and paperwork. Every empire is just an archive waiting for a match.

The page burned green first. Then gold. Then a color I have no name for, though I am, professionally, a man of names.

And the choir stopped singing.

Not the way they'd stopped before — cut off, silenced, swallowed mid-verse. They stopped the way a held breath stops. Three hundred souls in the mirrored gallery went still, and then, one by one, they began to *remember*.

* * *

### Wren

I was a cooper's daughter before I was a verse.

That's the first thing that came back, and it came back like water through a cracked dam — not gently, not all at once, but in a rush that knocked me off the choir riser and onto my knees on marble gone suddenly warm. *Cooper's daughter.* Father's name was Aldous. He made barrels that never leaked, and he smelled of oak shavings and river water, and there had been a dog — there had been a dog, a brown one, what was its name —

*Bramble.*

I said it out loud and my voice cracked down the middle, because it was my voice, my whole voice, not the thin silver thread the Queen pulled songs from. Around me the others were doing the same thing. A woman clutching her own wrists and saying a word over and over that might have been a child's name. An old man laughing and weeping in the same breath, reciting what sounded like a recipe for plum preserves. A boy — no, not a boy, a man grown small by centuries of being sung at — standing very straight and very quiet, touching his own face like he was checking whether it still fit.

We were remembering ourselves faster than we could hold it. Each verse the Queen had wrung from us had taken a memory, and now the memories were flooding home to find the doors they'd been stolen through rotted away. Some of us would not survive our own return. I understood that even as I wept for Bramble the dog and the smell of oak shavings. You cannot pour thirty years of self back into a vessel that forgot how to be full.

But oh — oh — to be *full*. Even leaking. Even breaking.

The mirrors along the gallery walls had begun to scream.

* * *

### Isandro

Because here is the arithmetic the Queen never did, or did and refused to read: she had written herself into the contract too.

Every soul she bought, every binding she sealed, every verse she extracted — it all ran through her, the way a river runs through a mill. She was the mechanism. And when the page burned, the claim didn't simply vanish. It reversed. It flowed backward up the channel it had been poured down, and the Queen found herself holding three hundred souls' worth of debt with no debtors left to pay it.

She stood at the center of her mirrored hall as the glass began to fail — not shattering, worse than shattering, *unmaking*, each mirror forgetting what it had ever reflected. In each failing pane I saw her differently: a woman, a mountain, a hunger wearing a crown, and once — only once, in the last mirror, the one directly behind her throne — something small and cold and terribly lonely, huddled at the bottom of a dark place, waiting for someone to come.

Maybe that was true. Maybe that was the oldest reflection of all. It didn't matter. Loneliness is not a license.

"You would unmake me," she said, and her voice was already losing its architecture, syllables sliding loose like stones from a cliff face. "You would trade a kingdom for a *name*."

"I'd trade your kingdom for three hundred names," I said. "Yours I'm just declining to save."

"You are your mother's mistake."

"No," I said, and the burning page was nearly ash now, drifting upward against all sense, sparks rising like the choir's voices had risen. "I'm her inheritance. There's a difference. Look it up — oh, wait."

Wry to the end. She hated that. I could see her hating it, in the last mirror, in the last instant, and then the Queen of the Court Beneath the Mountain crumbled the way a snowfield crumbles in thaw — quietly, structurally, all at once — and her palace of mirrors came down around her like rain made of yesterday.

Odile grabbed my collar and hauled me behind the fallen throne as the ceiling came apart. "Sentimental *and* suicidal," she shouted over the roar. "You're going to be insufferable if we live."

"If," I agreed.

* * *

### Odile

I have been in collapsing buildings before. It's a professional hazard. There's a technique: don't look up, don't stop moving, and never — *never* — waste breath on last words.

So naturally Isandro stopped moving.

"The choir," they said. "We can't leave them under this."

"We can't leave them under this *dead*," I said, but I was already turning, because the truth is I'd been turning since the moment the first mirror screamed. Debt is debt. I owed Isandro Okonkwo a rescue from the night they mapped me out of the Guild's cells, and I pay what I owe, and somewhere in the last few weeks — I'll deny this — paying it had turned into something with less arithmetic in it.

We dug them out together. Souls don't weigh anything, it turns out, once you stop owning them. They're light as paper. We carried them up through the ruin in armfuls, dozens of trips, while the mountain groaned around us like an old man changing position, and every soul we set down in the open air of the great cavern — the real sky overhead, actual stone, actual dark — gasped like a diver surfacing.

Wren organized them. Of course she did. She'd been a cooper's daughter and a choir-soul and apparently both jobs teach you logistics. She stood in the cavern mouth calling names — *their own names, given freely, the way names should travel* — and sorting the strong from the fading, the ones who could walk from the ones we'd have to carry, and by the time the last of the palace fell in on itself we had three hundred people sitting in the dark looking at the first sky most of them had seen in decades.

Some of them cried. Some sang — freely now, crooked and off-key and theirs. One old fellow asked if anyone had plum preserves, and eleven people answered yes at once, and I had to walk away for a moment and pretend to check the tunnels because my eyes were doing something unprofessional.

Then Isandro went very still, the way they do when they're reading a map nobody else can see.

"She's still here," they said. "One of them. She's — Odile, she waited."

* * *

### Isandro

My mother stood at the edge of the freed, slightly apart, the way she must have stood for thirty years — near the choir but not of it, near freedom but not touching it, watching.

She looked like the ledger entry. That's the only way I can describe it. She looked like handwriting: familiar, precise, a little hurried at the ends of lines, beautiful in a way that suggested the beauty cost effort. Her soul had been sold to buy my safety, and Vess had voided the bargain and kept the payment, and she had spent three decades in the dark anyway, singing verses that erased her, because the alternative was letting the sale go to waste.

"Mama," I said. I hadn't called her that since I was six. It came out sounding eight years old, and I let it.

"Look at you." Her voice was hers, wholly hers, the first time in thirty years. She reached up and touched my cheek the way she used to when I'd come in muddy from drawing maps in the garden dirt. "All this time I thought I was buying you a hiding place. Turns out I was buying you a *purpose*. Terrible value for money."

"It was a terrible contract. I've read it."

"I know. I heard." She smiled, and it was my smile, the one I make at ledgers. "You always did talk back to authority figures. I wondered where you got it. It wasn't your father."

Behind her, the others were beginning to move — toward the unmapped stair, toward the long climb home, drawn by whatever compass freed souls carry. Wren was counting them through. But my mother didn't move, and I understood, with the particular cruelty of a cartographer who can always read the terrain ahead, that she wasn't going to.

Thirty years of erasure doesn't all come back. Some vessels don't refill. She was standing in the last of herself the way you stand in the last of the daylight, spending it carefully.

"Come with us," I said. "There's a stair. I drew it myself. It goes all the way up."

"I know. I watched you draw it." Her hand found mine. Her grip was already getting lighter, or my memory of grip was, and I couldn't tell which and didn't want to. "Isandro. Listen to me. The guild will want to make you into Vess. Or into a martyr. Refuse both. Make them into something new instead — you're good at that. Making things new. You redrew a queen out of existence with a match and a grudge."

"That was mostly the match."

"Take the credit anyway. You've earned a little theft." She laughed, and the laugh was the lullaby she used to hum, the one the guild bottled and sold a hundred copies of, except this one was free and unrepeatable and mine. "And find the dog. When you get above. Ask at the cooper's on Tanner's Row. Bramble's descendants are probably terrible, but ask anyway."

Her edges had gone soft, luminous, the way ink goes when the candle gets close. The freed souls were passing through her now, or she through them, and none of them saw her but Wren, who paused, and bowed — one chorister to another — and moved on.

"Stay," my mother said.

And for one wild heartbeat I thought she meant it as a plea — stay here, stay with me, stay in the dark where I can keep you — and my whole body lurched toward her.

But she pressed her palm flat against my chest, over the heart, the way you press a map flat so it will lie right, and she smiled, and she said it again, and this time I heard it correctly:

"*Stay.* Stay exactly as you are. Stay loud. Stay impossible. Stay *mine*."

And then she was light, and then she was warmth, and then she was the particular quality of silence that means someone has finished speaking and meant every word, and I stood alone in a cavern full of the freed, holding nothing, holding everything, crying in a manner I intend to deny under oath.

* * *

### Odile

I gave him — them, Isandro, fine — an hour. It was generous. It was the longest hour of my life, standing guard at the cavern mouth while three hundred ghosts-in-recovery asked me questions I didn't have answers to (*is the war over? which king? what year?*) and Isandro sat alone in the dark doing whatever cartographers do instead of grieving.

When I finally went to fetch them, they were already standing, already wiping their face with the efficiency of someone closing a ledger for the night. Their eyes were wrecked. Their spine was straight.

"Well?" they said.

"Well *what*?"

"Are you coming or not? Someone has to carry the map. And someone has to steal us provisions for the climb, because I refuse to eat cave moss, Odile, I have standards."

I looked at them — this ridiculous, impossible person who had walked into hell with a flint and a grievance and walked out with three hundred souls and a dead queen's palace on their boots — and I thought about the guild mark burned off my arm, and the guild that sold them, and the long gray road between here and making any of it right.

"Yeah," I said. "I'm coming. Someone has to keep you from mapping anything else into existence. You're a menace."

They grinned. It was a terrible grin, wet-eyed and crooked, and it was the truest thing I'd seen in years.

Above us, faint through the mountain's bones, came a sound neither of us expected: bells. City bells, ringing wrong — fast, urgent, the alarm-pattern. And beneath them, thinner but unmistakable, carried on air that shouldn't reach this deep:

Voices. Many voices. Calling a name.

Calling *Okonkwo*.

Isandro and I looked at each other in the dark.

"They're either hunting you," I said, "or they've already started burning the rest of the ledgers without you."

Isandro picked up the satchel of ash and ink that was all that remained of everything, and shouldered it, and turned toward the stair.

"Then we'd better hurry," they said. "It's my ledger. If anyone's going to burn it, it's going to be me — and I'm going to read it aloud first."

We climbed.
