## 20. The Guild Reckoned

The city had changed while I was under the mountain. Or perhaps I had changed, and the city was simply the first honest thing I'd seen in weeks.

I climbed the guild stairs on legs that ached like borrowed things. Odile walked beside me, her sleeve still scorched where her guild mark had burned away, and every sentinel we passed looked at that bare patch of skin the way you look at a wound you're afraid to touch. They let us through anyway. Word travels fast underground, and faster still when it comes up trailing ash.

The great hall smelled of smoke and old paper. Half the shelves stood empty—Vess's midnight bonfire from weeks ago—and the other half sagged with ledgers that had survived only because they'd been hidden by clerks who disobeyed orders. I would learn their names later. That matters to me now, so I'll write them down eventually: Pellam, who kept the bottled lullabies; Sister Quill-and-Thimble, whose real name nobody knew but everybody trusted; young Corvo, who had been hiding ledger pages inside his coat for a month like a boy smuggling sweets.

They were all gathered in the hall when we came in. Maybe forty souls left of what had once been hundreds. The dragon-fire was dead—I felt its absence the way you feel a missing tooth—and without it, every one of them should have been claimable by the court beneath the mountain. Every one of them should have been mine to save or fail.

But the ledger that bound them had burned with the Queen. Ash and ink, as my mother might have said if she'd stayed long enough to say anything at all.

Vess stood at the head of the hall, behind the master's lectern, as though the intervening catastrophe had been a scheduling inconvenience. She looked older. Not frail—Vess would sooner die than be frail—but worn, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. Her eyes found me across the room, and I watched her do the arithmetic: what I knew, what I could prove, what it would cost her.

"Isandro," she said. "You're alive."

"You sold me," I said. "So yes. Barely."

A murmur went through the hall. Vess didn't flinch. I'll give her that much; she never flinched. It was one of the things I'd loved about her, back when love and loyalty were the same word to me.

"The witch-hunter is dead," she said. "The crown's hunt collapsed when the mountain stopped answering. Whatever you think happened down there—"

"I know exactly what happened down there." I walked forward, slow, letting them all see me. "And I know what happened up here, while I was gone. Because Odile told me. Because the steward told me. Because your own handwriting told me, Vess, and unlike you, ink doesn't know how to lie."

I set the stolen page on the lectern. My mother's contract—the original, pried from the Queen's archive before the mirrors fell. Beside it, I laid Vess's letter to the witch-hunter. *One cartographer, location enclosed, in exchange for the guild's exemption.* Her seal sat at the bottom like a black beetle.

"Read it," I said to the hall. "Read all of it."

Nobody moved. Then Sister Quill-and-Thimble stepped forward, took the letter in both hands, and read it aloud in a voice like a bell cracking. When she reached the part where Vess offered my name as final payment, the silence afterward had weight. You could have hung a soul from it.

"She bought us time," someone said from the back. Old Hesk, I thought. "She kept half the guild alive—"

"She kept herself alive," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and she wrote it in a letter and sealed it with wax. Ask yourself why the fire died and she was still standing. Ask yourself why the protections failed for everyone except the master's chair."

"That's not—" Hesk started.

"Isn't it?" Odile said, and her voice was quiet in the way that made everyone lean in. "I burned my own mark off rather than be worth more than my friends. What did she burn?"

* * *

It took three hours. I won't pretend it was dignified. Grief and betrayal don't argue cleanly; they circle, they bite, they drag up thirty years of small favors and quiet rescues until the whole hall smelled of old smoke again. Vess defended herself the way she'd done everything—competently, coldly, with an accountant's precision. Every sin was a subtraction that balanced a survival. Every sacrifice she'd made was itemized. She never once said she was sorry. I don't think the word existed in her ledger.

What broke her wasn't my evidence. It was Pellam, the lullaby-keeper, standing up with shaking hands and saying, "You taught me that every debt gets called eventually, Master. Even yours."

She resigned before sunset. Not gracefully—she tore the master's pin from her collar and threw it on the floor, which I suppose was graceful, for Vess. Then she walked out of the hall and out of the guild and out of my life, and I felt the loss of her like a tooth coming loose, painful and strange and leaving a gap my tongue kept returning to.

That left the chair empty. And forty pairs of eyes turning toward me.

* * *

"No," I said.

They hadn't even asked yet. That's how obvious it was. The one who'd gone down and come back up, the one who'd broken the Queen, the one with the gift and the grievance and the freshly minted legend. Of course they wanted me in the chair. Legends make such tidy masters.

"Someone has to hold the ledgers," Sister Quill-and-Thimble said.

"Then hold them. All of you. That's rather my point." I climbed onto the dais—not to sit, but so they could see the lectern, and the ledgers stacked beside it, and me standing between the two like a border drawn in ink. "For two hundred years this guild has kept secrets the way other institutions keep gold. We told ourselves it was protection. A name hidden is a name safe. A soul recorded is a soul guarded. And some of that was true. Some of it saved lives—mine included, once, or so I believed."

I picked up the oldest surviving ledger. Its spine crumbled a little under my fingers, shedding brown flakes like autumn.

"But a secret kept *for* someone becomes a secret kept *about* them. And then it becomes a secret kept *from* them. My mother sold herself to buy my freedom, and Vess voided the sale, and I spent thirty years not knowing I was owned because the people who loved me decided I was safer ignorant. Ignorant, and invisible, and accruing interest." I let that sit. "How many of you are in these books? How many of you know what page?"

Silence. But it was a different silence now—uncomfortable, self-searching. Good. Discomfort is the tax honesty pays upfront.

"So here is what I refuse, and here is what I demand. I refuse the chair. I refuse to be the next person who decides alone what happens to your names. And I demand that every surviving ledger be opened. Copied. Made public record—to members, to the families of the sold, to anyone whose name appears in our columns. No more ownership disguised as guardianship. This becomes a registry of protection. You will be able to walk into this hall, open a book, and read your own entry with your own eyes. And if you find a debt against your name that you never agreed to, you will have the right—the *right*, written into charter, witnessed and sealed—to strike it out."

"That's madness," Hesk said. "Half those contracts depend on secrecy. The courts, the collectors—"

"The courts can read them too, then," I said. "Let the crown see exactly what we've been trading in. Let the witch-hunters' successors choke on the evidence of what fear makes people sell. Sunlight is a terrible thing to owe money in, Hesk. That's rather the point."

"And you?" Sister Quill-and-Thimble asked softly. "Your own page. Your mother's. Will you strike them out yourself?"

I had thought about this, on the long climb up. I'd expected the question.

"No," I said. "Those pages stay. Open, public, readable by anyone—including me. Because I want to remember what a hidden thing costs. I want every clerk who learns this trade to read my mother's handwriting and understand that behind every entry is a hand that trembled. We don't erase the debts. We stop pretending they were ever ours to carry alone."

* * *

We argued until dawn. Of course we did. Guilds are built of argument the way cathedrals are built of stone. But somewhere past midnight, Corvo—the boy with the smuggled pages—stood up and said, "My mother's in book nine. I've never been allowed to look." And after that, the vote was a formality.

By morning light, we had a charter amendment scrawled in six hands and sealed with whatever wax we could find—brown candle stubs, mostly, which felt appropriate. The Registry of Protection, we called it, because none of us were poets and honesty rarely comes with a good title. Pellam volunteered to oversee the copying. Sister Quill-and-Thimble took custody of the seals. Odile, to my lasting surprise, asked for the debt-collection records.

"Why those?" I asked her.

"Because somebody owes me," she said, "and I intend to forgive them properly, in writing, with witnesses. If we're going to be honest, we may as well be thorough."

The ledgers came out of their hiding places one by one. Corvo surrendered his coatful of pages with the solemnity of a knight returning a relic. Clerks who had defied Vess were named aloud and thanked, which several of them found more embarrassing than any punishment. And through it all, I stood at the edge of the dais and did not sit in the chair, and the chair sat empty behind me like a question no one wanted to answer twice.

Near midday, a runner arrived from the lower city, breathless and gray-faced. He handed me a note, and I read it twice because the first reading refused to make sense.

*They're coming up out of the mountain,* it said. *The freed ones. Dozens of them. Singing. And they're asking for the cartographer.*

Odile appeared at my shoulder, reading over my arm. "Well," she said. "You did tell them the registry was open to anyone whose name appears in the books."

"They weren't in the books," I said slowly. "They were *under* them."

Outside, faint but unmistakable, drifting up from the direction of the gates—a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat. The crooked choir, singing. But straighter now. Finding its pitch.

Coming home.
