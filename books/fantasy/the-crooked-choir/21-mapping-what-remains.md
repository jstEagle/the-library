## 21. Mapping What Remains

They came up the guild steps in ones and twos at first, then in a flood, blinking against a dawn none of them had seen in years—some in decades. The crooked choir, unbound, still sang as they walked, but the song was changing shape with every step. It was less a performance now than a conversation, each voice remembering its own melody, arguing gently with the others about the key.

I stood in the doorway of the guildhall with Odile beside me and watched them arrive like weather.

"They're looking at you," she murmured.

"They're looking for doors," I corrected. "I'm just standing near one."

But that wasn't entirely true, and I knew it. There was a particular quality to the way a freed soul looks at the person who cut their chains—not gratitude, exactly, not worship, nothing so simple or so comfortable. It was closer to the way a drowning sailor looks at a chart of the coastline. *Tell me where I am. Tell me how to get home.*

And that, at least, was a question I could answer. It was, if I was honest with myself, the only question I had ever been built to answer.

The first one through the door was Wren.

I hadn't expected to recognize her outside the choir—underground she had been mostly voice, mostly grief given grammar—but there she was, small and gray-faced, wearing a shift that might once have been fine and was now mostly memory. She stopped three paces from me and looked up, and her eyes did something complicated.

"You're shorter than your voice," she said.

"People say that."

"Is it true you can draw a road that doesn't exist yet?"

"It's true I can draw a road that hasn't decided to exist yet," I said. "Whether it follows through is between the road and whatever gods roads answer to."

Wren considered this. Then she held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer something fragile. "I was a dyer," she said. "Before. In Sallowmere, on the Vell. My mother's shop had blue shutters. I can't remember her face anymore—the Queen's mirrors took that verse first, they always take faces first—but I remember the shutters. I remember the exact blue." Her hand trembled slightly. "Can you find a place from a color?"

I thought of every map I'd ever been punished for making. Every impossible coastline, every stair into nowhere, every country that existed only because my pen insisted.

"I can find a place from less than that," I said, and took her hand.

* * *

We commandeered the old counting room by noon.

It seemed fitting. That room had been the heart of the guild's trade for two hundred years—the place where shadows were weighed, where lullabies changed hands, where my own name had once been entered into a ledger by a woman who loved me enough to sell herself and not enough, or too much, to tell me. The long oak tables where brokers had haggled over bottled sleep now stood bare, scrubbed clean, waiting.

Odile kicked the door open with her boot and dropped an armload of fresh paper onto the nearest table like a general seizing high ground.

"Right," she said. "Rules first, before we drown in sentiment. One: nobody maps anything for payment. Two: nobody maps anything for *favor*. Three—" she pointed at me, "—you eat something today, Isandro, I swear on every god I've ever robbed, or I will force-feed you myself."

"I ate yesterday."

"Yesterday doesn't count. Yesterday you were saving the world. Today you're a person again, and persons require soup."

She was right, which was irritating, and she knew it, which was more irritating still. But there was work to do, and work has a way of holding hunger at arm's length, so we compromised: she put a bowl within my reach and threatened it with consequences, and I mapped.

Oh, how I mapped.

It was nothing like drawing for the guild. For years my gift had lived in the dark, folded small, passed off as clerical neatness—a cartographer of unmapped realms pretending to be a bookkeeper of impossible ones. Now I spread paper across the counting tables and let the pen do what it had always wanted to do, and the ink moved under my hand like something waking from a long sleep, hungry and grateful.

Wren sat across from me and described Sallowmere until she ran out of words, and then she kept going anyway, giving me fragments: the smell of the dye vats, the sound of the river in flood season, the way the blue of the shutters looked at dusk versus noon. And my pen listened. That is the only word for it. The lines came out of me half-remembered and half-invented, and somewhere in the space between those two things, the road found itself.

"There," I said finally, turning the page toward her. A town on a river, drawn in the loose confident strokes of a place that exists whether or not anyone has surveyed it. A shop with blue shutters. I hadn't planned the shutters. They arrived on their own.

Wren made a sound I will never forget—half laugh, half sob, the noise a person makes when a wound and its healing happen in the same instant. She pressed both hands flat against the map as though checking it for fever.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's—it's *home*, it's actually—" She looked up at me, wet-eyed and fierce. "How far?"

"Three days' walk, if the road behaves. Four if it sulks. Roads sulk sometimes; it's in their nature."

"And the others? The ones still down in the hall?"

"One at a time," I said. "Everyone gets their own page. Nobody's journey gets crowded."

* * *

By evening the counting room had transformed into something the guild had never contained in all its centuries: a workshop of departures.

Freed souls sat at the tables in clusters, trading memories like currency—which, I suppose, after what they'd endured, memories were. An old man named Corvus remembered a lighthouse. A pair of sisters who had been sold together, souls entered on facing pages, remembered each other's childhoods in pieces and were patiently stitching them back together aloud. A young man who would only give his name as Tam kept describing a bridge he couldn't name in a city he couldn't place, and getting frustrated, and starting over.

Odile worked the room like the thief she was, but thieving in a new direction—collecting details, coaxing out the small specific things that make a place real. Not "a village in the north" but "the village where the well froze so deep they rang it like a bell." Not "my mother's house" but "the house with the crooked chimney where the swallows nested every summer and my father pretended to hate them."

"The specifics are the anchors," she told me, low, when we paused to watch the lamplighters work the hall. "You taught me that without meaning to. Back when I owed you that debt—you never asked why I paid attention to how you worked. Details are what a thief needs to get *in* anywhere. Turns out they're also what a lost person needs to get *back*."

"You could have led with that observation decades ago."

"I could have led with a lot of things." She bumped my shoulder with hers. "So could you."

Outside the windows, the guild was changing shape around us. Word had spread—word travels fast among people who spent years listening underground—and others were coming. Not freed souls now, but the hunted. The mappers, the shadow-speakers, the song-keepers, everyone the crown's proclamation had marked, arriving at our gates with everything they owned in bundles on their backs. Vess's protections had died with the dragon-fire, but something sturdier was being born in its place: not a bargain struck beneath a mountain, not names hidden in ledgers, but simply *doors left open*.

An old woman with a cartographer's satchel asked me at dusk whether the registry was really open to anyone.

"It's open to anyone who wants their name written honestly," I said. "Which is a smaller number than you'd think, but growing."

* * *

It was past midnight when I drew the last map of the day, and it was the hardest one.

Not because the destination was unclear. Because it wasn't mine to draw alone.

My mother's soul had gone free with all the others—one word left behind, *stay*, and then ash and light and a silence I am still learning to live inside. There was no homeward path for her now. Whatever shore she'd reached, no chart of mine would ever touch it.

But there was a different kind of mapping to do for her. So I took a clean page, and instead of roads I wrote what I remembered: her handwriting, slanted and sure. The way she hummed when she worked. The weight of her hand on my head, brief and warm, the last time I saw her—I know now that she knew it was the last time, and I know now what it cost her to make it look like an ordinary goodbye.

Then, beneath it, in my own hand:

*Isandro Okonkwo. Cartographer. Child of Amara Okonkwo, who paid everything and signed nothing. Entered here freely, witnessed by no one, needing no one's permission.*

I did not burn the page. I did not file it. I set it on the corner of the table where the morning light would find it first, and I went to bed, and for the first time in longer than I can calculate, I slept without dreaming of ledgers.

* * *

Morning brought Odile, two bowls of soup, and news.

"You're popular," she said, setting the bowls down and sliding one toward me with her foot, because her hands were full of paper. "There's a queue at the gates. Freed souls, hunted folk, and—" her mouth twitched, "—three separate delegations from cities that want to hire you. Officially. With money. One of them used the phrase 'court cartographer.'"

"Absolutely not."

"I told them you'd say that. I may have said it with some flourish." She dropped the papers on the table—petitions, requests, letters sealed in wax that wasn't red, thank all the patient gods. "But Isandro. Look at this one."

It was a letter, unsigned, delivered sometime in the night. Inside, a single line in a hand I almost recognized:

*There are other courts beneath other mountains. Some of them heard what happened here. Some of them are afraid. And at least one of them has begun collecting names again.*

Odile watched my face. "Well?"

I looked out the window. Below, the queue stretched down the hill—souls and strays and singers, all of them waiting for a page, a road, a chance. Wren was already at the tables, helping a frightened boy describe his village, her voice steady, her blue-shuttered map pinned above her like a promise kept.

Every one of them had come because someone had finally told the truth in a place built on lies. And truth, I was learning, does not end things. It begins them.

"Well," I said, reaching for my pen. "I suppose we'd better start mapping."
