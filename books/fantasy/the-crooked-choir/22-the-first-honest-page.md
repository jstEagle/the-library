## 22. The First Honest Page

The guild hall still smelled of smoke and lye, but underneath it — faintly, stubbornly — it smelled of bread. Odile had commandeered the old counting room's hearth for baking, on the theory that a building where people had been sold should learn to smell like something being made instead. I had stopped arguing with her theories around the time she burned her own guild mark off her arm. You don't argue with someone who has already priced their own skin.

I sat at the long table where Vess used to conduct her quiet cruelties with a fountain pen and a pleasant voice, and in front of me lay a book.

It was not an impressive book. That was the point. The old ledgers had been bound in dragon-hide and clasp-locked in iron, as though the souls inside them might make a run for it if left unattended. This one was sewn from plain linen thread into paper Odile had bought, legitimately, with coin she'd earned, from a stationer who thought we were a choral society. Which, in fairness, we were. Just not the kind anyone had ever auditioned for willingly.

The first page was blank. It had been blank for eleven days.

"You're going to have to write something eventually," Odile said, setting a loaf down beside me like a challenge. "The paper's starting to think you don't love it."

"The paper is right to be suspicious," I said. "Every other page I've ever written has been a lie of one flavor or another. Omission. Euphemism. *One hidden child.*" The words still tasted like copper when I said them. My mother's handwriting, looping and careful, pricing me like a bolt of cloth. Even now, knowing what the scribe had confessed — that she hadn't sold me at all, that she'd sold herself and Vess had quietly voided the exchange and kept both entries anyway, because a ledger with two souls on one page is worth more than a ledger with one — even knowing all that, the handwriting haunted me. Love, written in the grammar of commerce. She'd had no other language. The guild had never taught her one.

Odile pulled out a chair and sat across from me, arms folded, flour on her jaw. "Say the thing you keep circling."

"I'm trying to decide what a name is *for*," I said. "The old ledgers used names as handles. You write a name down and suddenly it can be picked up, carried, sold, claimed. The mountain court ran on it. Write it small enough and tight enough and a person becomes portable." I turned the blank page toward her. "So if I write mine here, I need it to mean the opposite. I need ink that gives instead of takes."

"And your gift?"

"My gift," I said, "has opinions."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about mapping what isn't there: the realms that don't exist are not empty. They are full of everything people refused to say out loud. Every debt unspoken, every name swallowed, every *I am* held behind the teeth until it curdled — all of it pools somewhere, and a cartographer of unmapped things learns to read those pools the way sailors read weather. For years I had used the gift the way the guild taught: quietly, privately, to find the passages between here and elsewhere, the stairways down, the doors that only open for the lost. I had treated my own interior the same way. There were whole provinces inside me I had never surveyed. I'd drawn the borders around them and marked them *here be dragons* and moved along.

The fire going out had changed the geography. When the last dragon-fire guttered and died — and I was there for it, underground, mid-theft, watching the great bronze basin exhale its final ribbon of gold — every protection the guild had ever woven came undone at once. And in that single terrible unguarded moment, I felt every name in every ledger the way you feel a floor give way. So I did the only thing a cartographer can do when the map is killing people: I redrew it. I took the Queen's claim — that vast grasping line of ownership snaking through ten thousand names — and I bent it back on itself, redirected it onto the ledger that held it, so that the contract devoured its own binding. Ash and ink. The choir freed. The Queen crumbling into her own mirrors, bound by the letter of a bargain she'd spent centuries cheating others with.

And then, in the settling ash, my mother's soul had passed by me on its way to wherever souls go when they are finally done being owned. She had looked at me — or the shape of looking, which is what souls do — and said one word.

*Stay.*

Not *goodbye*. Not *run*. Not even my name, which she had never once been permitted to say aloud after she signed that page. Just *stay*, in her voice, in the voice I realized I'd been hearing under every lullaby the guild ever bottled and sold. Stay. An instruction. A wish. A mother's whole remaining vocabulary, compressed to a single imperative because the choir had sung away everything else.

I intended to obey it. But staying, I was learning, is not the same as standing still.

* * *

The survivors of the crooked choir began arriving at the guild hall in the second week, because word travels strangely underground and freed souls travel stranger still. They came up through the unmapped stair two and three at a time, blinking in daylight they hadn't seen in decades or centuries, clutching each other's sleeves like the air might be another kind of cage.

Wren came first. Of course Wren came first; Wren had always been the choir's front voice, the one whose verses cost the most. Each song had eaten a memory — that was the mechanism, the horrible elegant engine of the place — and Wren had sung more verses than anyone, trading childhoods and faces and the taste of particular rain for the privilege of continuing to exist. When I met them in the under-court they had known their own name only because the Queen insisted her property be labeled.

Now they stood in the guild hall doorway, squinting, and said, "It's louder up here than I remembered."

"That's the living," Odile said. "You get used to it. Mostly."

Wren drifted through the halls those first days like a person reading a room they'd once lived in. They would stop at doorframes and frown, hunting for a memory that wasn't there anymore, and then — this was the part that undid me — they would simply build a new one on the spot. Touch the wall deliberately. Say aloud, "This is where I stand on a Tuesday now." Inventory-taking as resurrection.

It was Wren who gave me the idea for the ledger's form, though they didn't know it. I found them in the old scriptorium one evening, turning the pages of a salvaged fragment of the original ledgers — one of the few that survived Vess's burning, rescued more as evidence than anything else. Wren read the cramped columns of names and prices with an expression I couldn't parse, and then they looked up at me and asked, "Did anyone ever write down what these people *wanted*?"

I opened my mouth to say no, and discovered I couldn't speak, so I shook my head.

"Then that's your first column," Wren said, and went back to reading, casual as anything, having just handed me the whole architecture of the rest of my life.

Because that was it. That was the thing the old ledgers had never once recorded, in three hundred years of meticulous bookkeeping. Not what a soul was worth. What a soul wanted. Where it wished to go. What it had loved. Who should be told, if telling became possible. A registry of protection, I'd called it, when I stood before the surviving guild members and refused Vess's chair. But protection needs content. Protection needs to know what it's protecting *toward*, not just what it's protecting against.

So I designed the new page. Name, freely given. Witnessed by. Homeward path, if known. And at the bottom, in a space wide enough for honesty: *what they want.* No prices. No columns for transfer or claim or lien. No iron clasps. A book you could burn without destroying anyone, because the point was never to hold the names — the point was to remember that they'd been spoken.

There was just the matter of the first entry.

* * *

"You could write a practice one," Odile suggested, on day twelve of the blank page. "Use mine. Warm up."

"Your name isn't mine to write."

"No," she agreed, and stole a corner of my bread, "but it's mine to offer, and I'm offering. Consider it debt repayment. You did save my life in a tunnel, and I've been carrying that around like a stone in my shoe."

"Your debt was paid at the gate," I reminded her. "You gave your brother's name to get us in. Grief as currency. I watched you do it and I've never forgiven the world for requiring it."

She was quiet a moment. Odile quiet is a weather event; you notice. "His name was Tam," she said finally. "Nobody's said it out loud since the gatekeeper ate it. It feels — lighter, saying it here. Like it came home wrong and this is the right address anyway." She shrugged, elaborate and fake-casual. "Put him in your book if there's room. Not as a debt. As a *want*. He always wanted to see the sea. Somebody ought to record that somebody wanted something."

I wrote it. *Tam. Brother of Odile. Wanted the sea.* Three lines, and my hands shook doing it, and when I finished, the page didn't feel like a ledger at all. It felt like a grave marker that pointed forward instead of back.

But the first entry — the true first entry, the one the whole book was waiting for — stayed unwritten. Because writing my own name meant deciding, finally and on the record, what my name actually was.

* * *

Here is the trouble. Isandro Okonkwo was the name the guild gave me, or rather the name my mother gave me and the guild recorded, and both halves of that sentence are complicated. Okonkwo was her name, carried like a candle through countries that kept trying to blow it out. Isandro was — I think — a hope. A name meaning something like *defender of the people*, chosen by a woman who could see exactly what kind of world her child would need defending from. She wrote it herself, in that looping hand, on documents I have spent this past year reading like scripture and autopsy reports simultaneously.

And beneath all of it, unrecorded anywhere, was the name my gift knew me by. The name the unmapped places whispered when I crossed their borders. Cartographers of impossible things don't get to keep their names private; the territories learn them. The stair beneath the city had known it. The pool of unsaid things had known it. The dragon-fire, in its last moment, had looked at me with ancient recognition and I had understood that it had always known who I was, better than any ledger, better than I did.

The name I was deciding whether to write was not the one on my mother's page. It was the one underneath — the true one, the given one, the one that existed before any transaction touched it. Inheritance, reclaimed. Not the inheritance of a sold soul, which was fraud from the first stroke of the pen. The older inheritance. The one that comes free or not at all.

* * *

On the fourteenth evening, the choir's survivors gathered in the hall — nine of them now, plus Wren, plus a handful of guild folk who had stayed after the reckoning, the ones who'd chosen accountability over comfort when I laid Vess's bargain bare before them. Vess herself was gone; the crown's witch-hunters had taken her in the end, trading on secrets she'd sold twice, and I had found I couldn't muster grief about it, only a tired sort of pity. Institutions teach people that survival means selling. She'd just never learned there was another curriculum.

They arranged themselves without being asked, the way choirs do, in a loose crescent around the table. Nobody had planned a ceremony. Wren brought candles anyway, because Wren believed occasions deserved light, and after a lifetime of singing in the dark I wasn't going to argue.

"This is a witnessing," I said, and my voice did something unsteady on the second word, and nobody pretended not to notice, which is its own kindness. "In the old ledgers, names were taken. Written down by other people, for purposes the named never agreed to, at prices set by strangers. This book works differently. Nothing goes in it unless the person speaks it themselves. Nothing in it can be sold, transferred, claimed, or inherited against anyone's will. If the book burns, the names aren't lost — they were only ever borrowed by the page." I looked around the crescent of faces, some scarred by decades of singing, some still learning the shapes of their own forgotten wants. "I'm told a registry needs a first entry. And I'm told the registrar goes first."

Odile slid the pen across the table. Wren struck the first candle.

I put the pen to the page and my gift rose in me like a tide — not the dread-tide of the old days, the accruing-interest feeling of invisibility compounding, but the other thing, the true thing, the cartographer's joy of setting the first line on empty vellum and feeling a country assemble itself around it. I let it guide my hand. I mapped myself.

*I, Isandro Okonkwo,* I wrote, *called also by the name the unmapped places know, which I will say aloud now for the first time since I was a child —*

And I said it. Out loud. In a room full of witnesses. The syllables felt strange in my mouth the way a key feels strange in a lock you've only ever picked, and then they turned, and something deep in the architecture of me swung open. The candles leaned, all together, though there was no draft. Somewhere far below the city, I felt the old stair shift its stones, adjusting its route to accommodate a person who had finally stopped hiding from themselves.

*— do enter my own name into this book freely,* I continued, *not as property, not as payment, not as proof, but as inheritance: received from my mother, who gave me everything she had left, and returned to myself, who am the only rightful holder. Witnessed by the freed choir and by those who choose to remain. Let this page bind no one. Let it warm whoever reads it, the way a hearth warms a passerby who asks nothing.*

Then, in the wide space at the bottom, the column Wren had given me:

*What I want:* and I paused, pen hovering, aware that fourteen days of blankness had really been fourteen days of not knowing how to finish this sentence. Then I wrote it, plainly, without flourish:

*To stay. To draw maps that lead people home. To hear the choir sing because they want to.*

* * *

Wren sang first. Not a verse that cost anything — those songs were dead, their engine ash — but a song of their own choosing, assembled from the fragments that remained and the new memories they'd built on top of the absences. Their voice cracked in the middle and they kept going, and the others joined, and it was crooked, genuinely crooked, notes landing where they pleased, rhythms disagreeing cheerfully with each other. It was nothing like the polished horror the Queen had demanded. It was everything that horror had been strangling.

I sat with my book open on my knee and listened, and added a second entry while they sang — Odile's, spoken aloud in her flat thief's cadence, witnessed, wanting *a door that locks from the inside for once*. Then a third, a fourth. The freed souls lined up to speak their names into the linen-threaded pages, some whispering, some declaiming, one woman laughing halfway through her own name because she'd forgotten how it tasted. Each entry ended with a want. *Rain on a specific roof. To finish a letter begun forty years ago. My sister's face, or failing that, a good guess.* The book filled slowly, the way honest things do.

Later, much later, when the candles had burned to stubs and the choir had gone to whatever beds the reborn guild could offer them, Odile found me still at the table, rereading the first page.

"Well?" she said. "How does it feel, being a first entry?"

"Like a border that finally got surveyed," I said. "Turns out the territory was livable all along. I just never drew the roads."

She nodded as if this were a normal sentence, which, in our line of work, it was. Then she tapped the page, below my entry, where the linen showed blank lines waiting. "Room for more?"

"Whole volumes," I said. "There are other courts under other mountains, you know. Other ledgers. Other choirs nobody's heard yet." I looked at the candle stub, at the bread crumbs, at the woman who'd walked into the dark with me because a debt said so and stayed because a choice did. "Somebody should map the way to them."

"Somebody," Odile agreed, reaching for the pen, "with excellent handwriting and a documented history of stealing things back."

Outside, dawn was considering the rooftops. Inside, I turned to a fresh page, and for the first time in my life, the blankness didn't look like a debt accruing.

It looked like an invitation.
