## 2. A Pilgrim's Last Request

I want it on the record that I did not invite her.

My camp that night was a textbook example of good practice: tucked into a granite hollow off the smuggler road, fire small enough to hide under a copper kettle, bedroll laid out with the precision of a man who has slept badly in four hundred different places and intends to sleep well in at least one of them. I had eaten. I had inked the day's survey by firelight — the pass from yesterday still refusing to appear on paper no matter how many times I drew its walls, like a word you've known your whole life suddenly spelled wrong. And I was contemplating the particular silence of high Thornfell in autumn, which is not an absence of sound but a presence of it: wind worrying the pines, scree ticking as the mountain settles its accounts.

Then a stick snapped on the slope above me, and a voice said, "O Great Mother of the Wellspring, Oren Who Opens Every Door, hear your servant Maren, who is lost."

And the mountain answered.

Not with words. With weather, if you can call it that. The wind stopped — all of it, everywhere, mid-gust, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in. The fire leaned toward her instead of away. And along the ridge above my camp, every stone that could possibly roll rolled downhill, in order, largest to smallest, like a staircase assembling itself out of politeness.

The pilgrim came down those stones without stumbling once. She was maybe fifty, gray-faced, cheeks hollowed to knife-edges, wearing the travel-rags of someone who had been walking much longer than any sensible person walks. She looked at me sitting frozen by my kettle-fire and said, with tremendous dignity, "Is there tea?"

* * *

Here is what I knew about the old gods up until that evening, and I want to be precise about this because my professional reputation rests on knowing things: nothing. Less than nothing. I knew the names the way you know nursery rhymes — Oren of the Doorways, the Mother of the Wellspring, the Ninefold Hearth — words grandmothers used to frighten children away from cliff edges. The Church of the Unified Flame had spent three generations explaining, patiently and with increasingly creative violence, that there was exactly one divine power in the world and it lived in the capital and wore a crown, metaphorically speaking. The old gods were myths. Dead myths. Myths that had never even bothered to be alive in the first place, according to the Flame's scholars, who had written several very long books about it.

I had mapped Thornfell for eleven years. Eleven years of passes and smuggler roads and shrines so old they'd grown into the rockface, and I had catalogued every one of those shrines as *pre-Flame cult sites, function unknown*, because that is what you do when you are a professional and professionals do not write *gods live here* in the margins of documents that might be read by crown surveyors.

So when the wind stopped for a starving woman's prayer, my first thought — I am ashamed to report — was *that's going to complicate the legend key.*

"Tea," I said. "Yes. Sit down before you fall down."

She sat down exactly as she'd descended the ridge: with a strange, borrowed grace that didn't match the state of her body. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and something colder underneath, like snowmelt in a cave. Her hands shook around the cup I gave her. Her eyes didn't shake at all.

"You're the mapmaker," she said.

"I'm a surveyor."

"You're Kestrel Ishikawa." She said it the way people say the names of towns they've been walking toward for months. "You draw the roads that move. They told me at the last village — the ferryman's wife — that if anyone could find a way through Thornfell to the capital before midwinter, it was you. That you find roads that don't exist yet."

"The ferryman's wife," I said, "is a romantic who owes me money."

"Will you take a message to King Aldric?"

I should have laughed. A starving pilgrim, half-dead on a mountainside, asking a smuggler's cartographer to carry her mail to the most guarded man in the realm. Instead I heard myself say, "Why me?"

"Because the mountain led me to you." She turned the teacup in her ruined hands. "Because you walk where the land remembers. Because—" and here her voice did something complicated, grief and apology braided together— "because you were always going to be the one, and I'm sorry for it."

"Sorry for what?"

"For what it costs you." She drank the tea in three swallows, scalding herself and not caring, and then she took off the outer layer of her rags and unwrapped the bundle beneath: oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, a letter.

I have handled a lot of letters in my line of work. Smugglers' manifests, coded orders, blackmail, love notes between feuding houses. I know paper. This paper was heavy cream stock, watermarked with a crown, sealed with black wax stamped by a signet I recognized from my survey copies of court records — the personal seal of Aldric IV, King of the Amber Throne, Breaker of the Northern Rebellions, etcetera, etcetera, a man whose face was on every coin I'd ever earned and whose death, six years ago, had been announced by every bell in every town I'd ever mapped.

The bells had rung for weeks. There had been a period of official mourning. Then, very quietly, the announcements had been walked back — *the king's illness was grave but he lives*, the criers said, and everyone had shrugged and accepted it, because kings coming back from the brink is the sort of thing kingdoms prefer to believe.

The letter was dated nine days before the bells.

"I don't understand," I said, which was true, and also the understatement of my career to date.

"No," she agreed. "But you will. Not tonight. Tonight you only need to carry it." She pressed it into my hands. Her fingers were cold as the stones on the ridge. "Deliver it into the king's own hand, Kestrel Ishikawa. Not his ministers'. Not his guards'. His hand. Swear it."

"Why does it matter? He's—"

"Alive," she said, and smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was full of pity. "Yes. He is alive. That is rather the whole of the problem."

* * *

She lasted until an hour past midnight.

I did what I could, which was nothing, and we both knew it while I did it. Starvation doesn't reverse for tea and hardtack; her body had simply been waiting for permission to stop, and delivering the letter had been the permission. She talked, in the end, more than she prayed — fragments, mostly, that I assembled later and wished I hadn't. She'd come from the west, from somewhere past the maps I sold to honest clients. She'd been walking since spring. There had been others walking with her, once; she said their names like a litany and stopped after the fourth.

"They're answering again," she told me, staring into the fire. "After all these years of silence. Did you feel it? The bindings are fraying. Something is leaking through, and it isn't only us praying anymore. It's them reaching back." Her eyes found mine. "You felt the pass close behind you yesterday."

I went very still. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because the mountain told me," she said, as if this were a normal sentence, as if I hadn't spent the previous day telling myself the rockfall had been coincidence, the missing trail a mapping error, my own memory unreliable. "It likes you, the mountain. It's been waiting a long time for you to come home." She coughed, wet and deep. "Don't trust anything that answers too easily. The true gods make you knock. Whatever else is loose in the world right now — whatever wears the pretty shapes — it opens doors unasked. Remember that. Doors unasked."

"Maren—"

"Hush. I'm spending my last hour lecturing a stranger about doors; let me have it." She lay back against my pack, and the strange grace had gone out of her entirely now, leaving just a tired dying woman on a cold mountainside, and somehow that was worse. "You'll swear the oath?"

"What oath?"

"That it reaches his hand. His actual hand."

I looked at the letter. Black wax, royal signet, nine days before a dead man's funeral bells. Everything in me — eleven years of professional caution, of keeping my name off manifests and my head below ridgelines — everything said *drop it in the next ravine and forget her.*

What I said was: "I swear it. Into the king's own hand or not at all."

Something in her face unclenched. "Good," she whispered. "That's the first door opening, and you opened it yourself. That matters. That will matter." Her breath rattled. "They marked you, mapmaker. Long ago. Before you could walk. I'm sorry — I was told not to tell you, and I've never been good at obeying—"

"Marked me how? Marked me *what?*"

But her eyes had gone past me, up through the gap in the granite hollow to the stars, and she smiled again — the real one this time, small and relieved — and said, "Oh. There you are. You came," to absolutely no one, and then she wasn't talking to me anymore, or to anyone.

The wind started again all at once, everywhere, like the mountain exhaling.

* * *

I buried her at first light, which is to say I stacked stones over her until my arms gave out, because Thornfell's soil is a rumor. I marked the cairn on my survey with a symbol that isn't in any legend key: a circle with a door drawn in it. Then I sat on a boulder, took out the letter, and held it for a long time without opening it.

I want to explain why I didn't open it, since I did open it, two nights later, alone in a cave with rain hammering the entrance and the wax already broken under my thumb before my conscience finished clearing its throat. The reason is simple: I told myself I couldn't deliver it blind. A courier needs to know what he's carrying — whether it's treason, whether it's a trap, whether some dying fanatic has strapped me to a plot against the throne that would end with my head on the Amber Gate. Professional due diligence. That's the story I told myself, and like most stories I tell myself, it collapsed under the first honest question, which is: *Kestrel, would you have opened it if she'd asked you not to?*

Yes. Obviously yes. I broke the seal because I have never in my life left a locked box locked, and because part of me — the part that watched a mountain build a staircase for a starving woman — needed to know whether the paper itself was wrong. Whether the date was a forgery. Whether there was some explanation, some boring mortal explanation, for a letter written in a dead king's hand nine days before his kingdom rang its bells for him.

There wasn't.

I know Aldric's hand the way I know any hand that signs things — survey grants, border charters, the land patents smugglers forge and I quietly correct. The letter was his. Not similar. *His.* The impatient downstroke on the A. The way he crossed his t's like a man slashing at something. And it began:

*Aldric IV, King of the Amber Throne, to whoever holds this when I no longer hold myself: I write this on the ninth day before my death, which I can feel approaching the way you feel weather in a joint. If you are reading this, then either I failed, or I almost succeeded, and I no longer know which of those to pray for.*

I read it four times by candlelight while the rain came down, and each time the same sentence snagged me like a nail in a boot sole:

*I do not know what wears the crown yet, or what it wants, but I know it is learning to be me faster than I am learning to resist, and by the time you read this, I suspect the difference will no longer show.*

The rest was instructions, warnings, names — a minister's name underlined twice, a chapel, a phrase in Old Thornic I couldn't read — and one line near the end that I must have read ten times, sitting in that cold cave with a dead pilgrim's cairn two valleys east and the whole comfortable architecture of my eleven careful years quietly collapsing around me:

*If you cannot reach the princess, reach the mapmaker. The gods will know where to find them. The gods have always known.*

I put the letter away. I banked the fire. I lay down in my bedroll and stared at the cave ceiling and conducted a brief, furious internal audit of my life, itemizing everything that had ever happened to me and asking each event, one by one: *coincidence, or door?*

The ferryman's wife sending me this route. The pass appearing where no pass should be. The mountain building a staircase. A dying woman walking out of the unmapped west to die precisely within reach of the one courier in Thornfell who could plausibly get a letter past the Amber Gate.

Doors unasked, she'd said. Don't trust anything that answers too easily.

Well. Someone had answered *me*, years ago, apparently, and I'd never even thought to knock.

By dawn I had made the only decision available to a professional whose landscape has begun lying to him: I packed my kit, checked my ink, and turned south, toward the capital, toward a king who was dead and a princess I'd never met and a delivery that had stopped being a favor to a ghost somewhere around the second reading of that letter.

The mountain, I noticed, had already cleared the road ahead of me.

I chose not to think about that. It was, I reflected grimly, getting harder and harder to find things I could afford not to think about — and I suspected, with the letter warm against my ribs, that I hadn't seen the bottom of that particular inventory yet.
