## 4. Princess Sable, Under House Arrest

There are forty-one ways out of the Amber Court that I know of, and my father sealed thirty-nine of them the morning after my birthday.

He left the servants' stair and the coal chute, which tells you either that he doesn't know about them or that he wants me to have options he can plausibly deny. My father has never in his life done anything he couldn't plausibly deny. It's practically his heraldry: *A King Rampant, Denying Everything, on a Field of Reasonable Explanations.*

So when the guards appeared at my door with his written order — *for Her Highness's safety, until such time as the present unrest resolves* — I did not scream or weep or demand an audience. I read the order twice, noted that the seal was correct but the handwriting was wrong — too even, like a child copying letters from a board — and thanked the captain with a smile I'd practiced in front of mirrors for twenty years.

Then I started counting.

* * *

Let me be fair to myself here: I did not begin with the theory that my father was dead and something else was wearing him. That is not where a reasonable person starts. A reasonable person starts with *Father is ill.* Then *Father is being controlled by his council,* which, honestly, would not have been unprecedented; Chancellor Vess had been steering him like a barge since before Mother died. Then maybe *Father is enchanted,* because this is Thornfell's capital and enchantment is the sort of thing you file under weather.

The dead-and-wearing-him theory came later, and it came, as most terrible theories do, from small things.

It came from breakfast.

My father used to eat like a man defending a border. He took his eggs hot, his bread torn rather than cut, and he had a habit — endearing to everyone but the kitchen staff — of stealing preserves off my plate when he thought I wasn't looking. He said it kept me humble. I said it kept him fed. It was, in retrospect, one of perhaps four uncomplicated joys either of us possessed, and we guarded it like a treaty.

Three weeks after my confinement began, I was granted a supervised breakfast with him. Supervised — two guards inside the door, Vess's secretary pretending to read dispatches in the corner. And my father sat across from me, and the eggs went untouched, and the bread was cut into neat geometric soldiers, and when I pushed the apricot preserve toward him — an old signal between us, meaning *I know something's wrong, tell me true* — he looked at it for four full seconds.

Four seconds. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of screaming; it's cheaper and the results last longer.

Then he smiled, said "Thank you, Sable," in a voice pitched exactly right, and ate one spoonful with the deliberate precision of a man completing a choreographed step. He did not take a second. He did not steal from my plate. The preserve sat there between us like a dead bird on a windowsill, and I understood, in the way you understand a floor has rotted through only after your foot goes through it, that whatever appetite had once lived behind my father's face had been replaced by instructions.

"You're not eating," I said.

"I ate earlier," he said.

"You never eat earlier."

"Sable." Gently. A hand laid over mine, and I want to record, for the historical record, that I did not flinch, though every nerve in my arm filed a formal complaint. "You've been under strain. The unrest in the city, the restrictions — I know they feel like punishment. They're protection. When things settle—"

"When things settle," I repeated. "When do things settle?"

"Soon." He patted my hand twice, precisely, like stamping a document, and withdrew. "I promise you."

My father does not make promises lightly. My father makes promises the way other men make threats — rarely, and meaning every syllable. So when he said *soon* and I heard nothing underneath it, no weight, no cost, no father, I knew I was having breakfast with a forgery so good that only love could detect it.

That was the eggs. The blinking came later, and it was worse.

* * *

Audiences were my one sanctioned public appearance — an hour each week, seated beside the throne, a decorative princess arranged next to a functioning king. I used to dread them. Now I studied them like a scholar with a heresy she can't stop reading.

Here is what I learned about my father over six audiences:

He never blinked.

Not rarely. Not less than usual. Never. I watched for it the way you watch a candle flame, waiting for the flicker that proves air is moving. Nothing. His eyes stayed open through petitions, through a farmer weeping about a burned granary, through a full hour of Chancellor Vess's voice, which would blink the eyes of a statue. At the end of each audience, when the herald called the closing, my father would close his eyes once — a single slow shuttering, both lids together, held for exactly three seconds — and open them again.

Like a lamp being turned down and up. Like something checking its own equipment.

And there was more, because once you see the seam in a mask you start seeing all of them. He smelled wrong. That sounds mad — I wrote it in my journal and then crossed it out and then wrote it again, because it was true and I am many things but not a coward about my own observations. My father smelled of cedar oil and horse and, in winter, of the pepper salve he rubbed into his hands. The thing on the throne smelled of cold stone. Deep cold, cellar cold, the cold of places that have never been warm and don't intend to start. The guards didn't notice. The court didn't notice. Distance and incense and the general human willingness not to smell anything unusual about a king covered it completely.

But I had been brought up pressed against his side on long rides. I knew his smell the way I knew my own name. And the man on the throne smelled like the crypt under the east chapel.

I want you to appreciate how long I resisted that particular comparison.

* * *

The third piece arrived in the fourth week, and it arrived because of a spider.

House arrest, properly managed, is a school of observation. You learn the rhythms of your jailers, the creak-signature of each stair, which guard hums and which prays. I learned that Captain Dree — the one who hummed — had a daughter with a bad lung, and that sympathy could buy me ten extra minutes in the library. I learned that the secretary who attended my breakfasts, Master Quillon, was left-handed and frightened, and that frightened people will tell you enormous amounts if you ask them questions they can answer without feeling like traitors.

"Does the king sleep poorly these days?" I asked him, idly, over the dispatches he pretended I couldn't read upside down. "He seemed tired at audience."

Quillon's pen stopped. Just for a beat. "His Majesty keeps... irregular hours, Highness."

"Irregular how?"

"He — that is — the night attendants say—" He caught himself, visibly re-rigging his face into loyalty. "Forgive me. It isn't my place."

"Of course not," I agreed warmly, and made a note to destroy whatever remained of his career later, gently, if it turned out he deserved it.

But I had the thread, and I pulled it the way you pull any thread in a palace: through servants, who see everything and are paid to forget it. What the night attendants said, filtered through a laundress, through a page, through Wren — no, not Wren yet, that came later — through a scullery girl who owed my old nurse a favor, was this:

The king did not sleep.

He sat. In his chambers, in the dark, in the chair by the window, upright, hands on knees. All night. Every night. The attendants had been instructed that His Majesty preferred to rest in stillness and was not to be disturbed, and they had obeyed, because he was the king, and because — this was the part the scullery girl whispered, the part that made the laundress cross herself — sometimes, deep in the night, they heard him talking.

Not to anyone. Just talking. In a voice that was almost his voice. Saying numbers.

Counting.

I sat with that for a long time. Something in the dark, wearing my father, counting. I thought of the single slow blink at the end of each audience, the lamp turned down and up. I thought of the preserve going uneaten. I thought of the handwriting on my confinement order, too even, letters copied from a board.

And then I stopped thinking, because thinking had gotten me as far as it could, and I did the thing I should have done in week one.

I went to look at him. Properly. Close enough to touch.

* * *

Getting out of my rooms required no heroics; it required laundry. The service corridor behind the linen stores connects to the royal wing — corridor nine of my forty-one, unsealed because sealing it would have meant admitting someone remembered it existed. I wore a maid's smock over my own clothes, carried a basket of sheets I had genuinely folded, because if you're going to commit treason you may as well do the work properly, and walked the way servants walk: slightly hurried, entirely invisible.

The king's sitting room door stood open. Of course it did. Doors are for people.

He was at the window, standing, looking out at the winter garden. Up close, without the throne and the distance and the incense, the wrongness stopped being a collection of details and became simply the truth, the way a map stops being lines and becomes a country.

His skin. That was the worst of it, worse than the smell, worse than the eyes. Skin moves. It shifts over muscle when a man turns his head; it creases; it lives. My father's skin had been brown and wind-roughened from years he'd spent, before the crown, riding the border roads he loved more than the court. The skin on the face at the window was smooth in the wrong places and creased in others, like a very good painting of skin hung over a shape that didn't quite need it. And it was cold. I knew it was cold before I touched him, knew it the way you know rain is coming, and then I touched him anyway — reached out and laid my fingers on the back of his hand, the hand that had taught me to hold a sword and a pen and, once, memorably, a hawk.

Stone. Cold stone, faintly moving, breathing when it remembered to.

"Father," I said.

He turned. The turn was perfect. That was the horror of it — everything was perfect now, every gesture calibrated, a king performed by something that had studied kingship the way scholars study dead languages: fluently, correctly, and without a soul in the translation.

"Sable." Warmth in the voice, exactly measured. "You shouldn't be here. For your safety."

"Yes," I said. "Everyone keeps saying that. Father, look at me."

He looked at me. And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, the thing that turned my dread into a blade I intend to use: for one moment — one, no longer than the blink he never gave us — something moved behind those eyes. Not the smooth performance. Something older and smaller and drowning. The look of a man pressing his face against the inside of glass.

His lips parted. No sound came. But they shaped something, carefully, deliberately, the way a man mouths words through a window to someone he cannot call to.

Two syllables. I read them the way I'd been reading lips across council chambers since I was twelve, because my father taught me that skill too, back when he was still allowed to be my father.

*Sab-el.*

My name. Or — no. I've turned it over a hundred times since, in the dark, in the laundry-smelling safety of my gilded cage, and I know what I saw. Not *Sable.*

*Daughter.*

He said *daughter*, the word he used when he meant *I love you* and was too much a king to say it, and then the glass came down over his eyes again, and the thing wearing him smiled its perfectly calibrated smile and said, "You should go back to your rooms, Highness. It's nearly time for your supper."

* * *

I walked back through corridor nine with the empty basket and my heart hammering out a war rhythm, and somewhere around the linen stores the hammering settled into something colder and far more useful.

Resolve.

Because here is what I know now, and I set it down plainly, the way Father taught me to set down orders: my father is in there. Buried, throttled, pressed behind glass — but in there, alive enough to spend his one stolen moment on a word for me. Which means he is not dead, whatever else is true, and means also that he is a prisoner in the one cell in this kingdom no one will ever think to search.

And something else is wearing the crown. Something that doesn't eat, doesn't sleep, doesn't blink, smells of the crypt, counts in the dark, and has begun — I checked the court calendar today, casually, while the chamberlain preened — something that has scheduled a grand coronation rite for midwinter. A *renewal* of vows, the heralds call it. A sacred binding of king and crown and realm.

Binding.

I don't know yet what that word fully means. But I know what it means to be confined for your safety while someone else arranges your future, and I know the difference between a ceremony and a cage, and midwinter is eleven weeks away.

Eleven weeks to get out of these rooms. Eleven weeks to find allies among the servants, the smugglers, anyone the crown doesn't watch. Eleven weeks to learn what walks in my father's skin and how to kill it — or better, how to free the man trapped underneath.

I sat at my window that night, watching the light die over the Amber Court, and I did not cry, though I gave myself permission to and waited a decent interval in case grief wanted the appointment.

Instead I took out my journal, turned past the crossed-out madness, and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in my neatest hand, the way you write something you intend to act on:

*The king is a prisoner. I am his daughter. This is a rescue.*

Below it, I began a list. Item one: find out what, exactly, is counting in the dark.
