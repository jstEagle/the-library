## 6. What the Gods Want

I have died four times, and none of them took.

That is not a boast. It is a diagnosis. A god of thresholds does not die so much as get closed — shut like a door in a house whose owner has forgotten the house exists. The first three times were during the Binding Wars, when the masons of the new faith came with their chisels and their certainty and carved my name off every doorway from the coast to the Thornfells. The fourth time was quieter. No armies. Just a village burning behind me and a hundred small prayers going out like snuffed candles, and I stepped through the last open threshold in Thornfell and found nothing on the other side but me.

So understand that when I say the fire Kestrel built that night was the warmest thing I had felt in sixty years, I am not being sentimental. I am being precise. Warmth is a threshold of a kind — between cold and not-cold — and I had spent six decades on the wrong side of it.

Kestrel built the fire badly, for the record. Too much green wood, stacked like they were drafting it rather than lighting it. But their hands knew what they were doing even when their mind was elsewhere, which is the mark of someone who learned a craft before they learned to doubt themselves. They fed the flames in silence while I sat against a boulder that had once been a boundary-marker — I could feel the old intention still in it, faint as a fingerprint on glass — and finally Kestrel said:

"All right. Talk."

* * *

"You've been circling questions since the pass," I said. "Since the road closed behind you like a wound knitting itself shut. Ask them properly."

"I've been mapping Thornfell for eleven years." Kestrel's voice had the flatness people use when they are holding something very heavy very carefully. "Eleven years. And today I walked through a pass that doesn't exist, on a route I've surveyed twice, and when I turned around it was gone. Not hidden. Gone. The rock face was smooth. Like it had never been cut at all." They poked the fire with a stick, aggressively. "So you're going to explain what I walked through, or I'm going to decide you're a fever dream and walk back down the mountain alone."

"You'd die," I said. "The crown soldiers are still combing the lower switchbacks, and they have your description now. Also, I'm not a fever dream. Fever dreams are better dressed."

"Talk, Oren."

So I talked.

"What do you know about the old gods?"

"They're myths." Kestrel said it quickly, the way people repeat lessons rather than beliefs. "Stories the mountain villages tell. Grandmother-tales. The Pilgrim's Hundred. The Shepherd of Doors. The Lady Under the Hill." They paused. "Shepherd of Doors. That's you, isn't it. That's why the doorway trick."

"It's one of mine, yes. Though I never cared much for 'shepherd.' It implies I chose the flock. Mostly the doors chose themselves and I just kept them honest." I held up a hand before they could interrupt. "Not myths. Memories. Your grandmother-tales are the sediment left behind when something enormous was dragged out of the world. Every story about a god who asked for bread and repaid it tenfold, every tale of a well that spoke, every superstition about knocking on wood — those aren't inventions. They're fossils. You've been walking over a graveyard your whole life, cartographer, and drawing beautiful maps of the headstones without ever reading them."

Kestrel went very still. It was the stillness I would come to know well — the stillness of a person re-examining everything they thought they'd measured.

"The binding," they said slowly. "You mentioned it on the road. Before you shoved me through a door in the air."

"Sixty-three years ago, give or take a season — time gets slippery where I live — the kingdoms of the lowlands finished a war. Not a war of armies, though there were those. A war of doctrine. The new faith — the One-Above, the Crown Celestial, whatever name the priests were using that decade — decided the world could only be governed if it could only be *addressed*. One heaven. One voice. One chain of authority running from the throne down through every soul, clean as a ledger line." I watched the fire. "And we were mess in the ledger. A hundred gods, two hundred, each with our little jurisdictions and our little bargains and our little temples in the roots of things. You cannot tax a god of thresholds. You cannot conscript the Lady Under the Hill. We were irregularity itself, and the new order wanted a world with no irregularities in it."

"So they killed you."

"They couldn't kill us. That was the problem. Divinity doesn't die; it only goes somewhere else. So instead they did something cleverer and crueller. They bound us out." I drew a circle in the ash at my feet, then crossed it with a line. "There is the world. Everything inside the line. The binding pushed us outside it — not up, not down, not into some other country you could march an army to. Outside. Beyond the edge of the map, if you'll forgive the phrase. They built stones for it, great anchor-stones sunk into the deep places, and the stones sang a single note forever, and the note was *no*. As long as the stones held their chord, the door between the world and the rest of reality stayed shut, and we stayed on the far side of it, pressing our hands against the glass."

"And you got out anyway."

"A crack, nothing more. Sixty years of frost and flood and forgetfulness. Stones crack. Priests die and their successors mumble the liturgies without understanding them. And lately—" I hesitated, because this was the part I had been turning over for months, the part that frightened me more than exile ever had. "Lately the bindings haven't just frayed. They've been *unraveling*. Fast. In the last two years I have felt more of the old chord go silent than in the previous fifty. Something on the other side isn't waiting patiently anymore. Something is pulling threads."

Kestrel stared into the fire. When they spoke again, their voice had changed — quieter, almost reluctant. "The scars. On my maps."

I looked up.

"There are places in Thornfell I've never been able to survey properly. Everyone who maps these mountains runs into them eventually. Dead zones. Compasses spin, distances won't add up, trails loop back on themselves. I always assumed it was ore veins, magnetic anomalies, bad geology." Their hand drifted toward their satchel — the maps rolled inside it, I realized, the life's work of a careful and honest mind. "They're not anomalies, are they."

"No."

"They're the binding-stones."

"Some of them, yes. And the roads around them are the seams where the world was stitched shut. You've been charting the surgery, Kestrel. Every dead zone on your maps is a place where the world was sewn closed over a wound, and every crooked trail, every route that refuses to behave, is scar tissue pulling at the stitches." I let that sit. "You told me once — on the road, when you thought I wasn't listening — that you became a cartographer because the mountains kept lying to you and you wanted to catch them at it. Well. Congratulations. You caught them."

Kestrel didn't laugh. They wrapped their arms around their knees and stared at the fire as though it were a document requiring translation.

* * *

"There's more," I said eventually. "The part you're not going to like."

"I've had a long day," Kestrel said. "A pass ate itself behind me, soldiers tried to arrest me for carrying a dead woman's letter, and a man made of doorways pulled me through solid rock. I don't think there's a part I'm going to like."

"Fair." I fed a stick to the fire. "When the bindings frayed, everyone assumed — those few of us who noticed anything at all — that the old gods would start seeping back through. That's what I waited for. Sixty years pressed against the glass, and when the crack finally came, I came through it like a man diving through a closing gate." My mouth twisted. "But I wasn't first."

Kestrel's head came up.

"Something else slipped through ahead of me. Something older than the binding. Older than the new faith. Older, I think, than most of us." I had not said this aloud before, and saying it made it heavier, the way naming a thing always does. "We are gods of *places*, Kestrel. Of doors and hills and hearths and rivers. We need somewhere to be. Whatever came through first doesn't. It needs no hill, no river, no threshold. It needs only an object — one object — that enough people look at and obey. A point of focus. A thing that gathers gazes the way a lens gathers light."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a night bird called and got no answer.

"And in a kingdom," Kestrel said, very quietly, "what object do enough people look at and obey?"

"Now you're asking the right questions."

"The crown." Kestrel unfolded, stood, paced two steps, stopped. "King Aldric. The letter — the pilgrim's letter — Aldric wrote it himself, years ago, before he supposedly died. I read it. His hand, his seal, his grief for his daughter. And yet the king lives. The king holds audiences. The king announced his own coronation rite — a *second* coronation, which no herald can explain, which the priests call a renewal of vows." They turned to me, and their eyes in the firelight were fierce and frightened in equal measure. "Something is wearing him."

"Veyra." The name came out of me like a stone from a sling. I hadn't meant to say it yet. "That is the name I heard whispered on the far side, in the dark, by things older and stranger than me. Veyra. Once a god of order — not tyranny, understand, order. Boundaries, hierarchies, the clean rule. There was a time when she was honored alongside the rest of us. Granaries stood because of her. Laws held. Roads ran straight." I shook my head. "But order starves when it isn't worshipped, and starvation changes the appetite. She was bound out with the rest of us, and she spent her sixty years in the dark doing what none of us did: she stopped pressing her hands against the glass and started looking for a keyhole."

"And the crown is the keyhole."

"The crown is the *lock*, the *door*, and the *house* all at once. Think about what it is, Kestrel. Every subject swears to it. Every law issues from it. Every coin bears its shape. It is the single most-looked-at object in the realm, the point where all the gazes of a nation converge — and a nation's gaze, concentrated, is the closest thing mortals have to worship. She didn't break in. She was *invited*, a thousand times a day, by every bowed head in the kingdom. By the time anyone thought to wonder whether the king blinked often enough, she had been drinking from that well for years."

Kestrel had gone pale. "The audiences. The reports travelers bring down-mountain. The king who hasn't aged, hasn't stumbled, hasn't changed his mind about anything in five years—"

"Order," I said. "Perfect, seamless, unblinking order. She thinks she is saving them. That's the worst of it. She looks at the realm and sees chaos — smugglers' roads, squabbling lords, prayers scattered among a hundred forgotten shrines — and she believes, with the whole sincere weight of divinity behind it, that one will, one law, one voice, is mercy. She is not a wolf in the fold, Kestrel. She is a shepherd who has decided the sheep will be safer as wool."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The fire settled. Kestrel sat back down slowly, the way people sit when their legs have stopped consulting them.

"The coronation rite," they said. "The second one. If she's already wearing him—"

"Then the rite is the final stitch. A possession held through borrowed worship leaks; it frays; the vessel fights it, even a vessel as worn as poor Aldric must be. But a true coronation — crown, altar, anointed, sworn before the assembled realm — that would fuse her to the throne permanently. And the throne sits at the heart of the ley-lines, the old roads of power the binding-stones were sunk along. Fuse Veyra to the throne and she is not merely wearing a king. She is wearing the kingdom. Every oath, every coin, every bowed head, forever, with no seam left for any of us to slip through." I spread my hands. "After that, the bindings don't matter. She'll simply be the only god left with a door."

Kestrel was quiet for a long time. Then, in a voice stripped of all its professional flatness, they asked the question I had been dreading.

"Why me?"

Ah. There it was.

"Why did the doorway open for me? Why did the pass exist for me and close behind me? Why did a dying pilgrim crawl across half of Thornfell to put a letter in *my* hands?" They leaned forward, firelight jumping in their eyes. "You knew my name before I gave it. Don't think I missed that. So tell me plainly, Oren, because I am done being lied to by mountains: what do the gods want with me?"

And here I must confess something, since this account will likely be the only one anyone bothers to keep: I lied. Not wholly. I am a god of thresholds, and thresholds are honest about being passages — but I told Kestrel that I didn't know, that the old ones worked in mysteries, that perhaps the pilgrim had simply chosen the nearest traveler.

It was mostly true. What I withheld was the shape of the truth: that the cracks in the binding had opened widest in Thornfell, that the last god-touched village in the mountains had burned there in the wars, and that the old ones on the far side had spent sixty years preparing for the day the glass broke — preparing not an army, but an *answer*. A vessel. A body born inside the world, outside the binding's reach, shaped and marked and set walking the very roads where the seals were weakest.

I did not tell them that. Partly because I only half-knew it myself, and a god should not traffic in half-knowledge. And partly — I will be honest, since no one else in this story will be — because Kestrel Ishikawa had shared their fire with a ragged stranger, and I did not want to see what that generosity would become when they learned it might not have been theirs.

"The letter," I said instead. "The pilgrim's letter for King Aldric. May I see it?"

Kestrel hesitated, then drew it from their coat — oilcloth-wrapped, sealed with wax stamped by a signet ring I recognized the way you recognize a scar on your own hand. They passed it over, and I held it to the firelight, and I felt what I had felt when I first saw it on the mountain road: the weight of it. Not paper-weight. Prayer-weight. Someone had poured a dying wish into this envelope, and wishes leave residue, and the residue on this one was old, and royal, and *grieving*.

"This letter," I said, "was written by a man who is either dead or imprisoned inside his own body. Either way, it is addressed to a king who no longer exists — except that he does, except that something wears him, except that his daughter is locked in her own rooms in the Amber Court for noticing." I handed it back. "Deliver it, and you deliver it into the mouth of the thing that ate him. Refuse, and a dying woman's last prayer dies with you. Those are your options, cartographer. I apologize. The gods have never been good at offering clean choices. It's why they bound us out."

Kestrel took the letter back and held it the way they held their maps — carefully, precisely, as though it might tear along lines only they could see.

"The coronation," they said. "When?"

"Midwinter. Weeks, no more."

Kestrel looked at the fire for a long moment. Then they reached into their satchel, drew out a map — one of the dead-zone charts, all its crooked seams and refusing trails — and smoothed it flat on a stone between us.

"Then show me," they said, "exactly where every binding-stone in these mountains is. Because if I'm going to walk into a palace and hand a letter to a hollow king, I want to arrive knowing more about the shape of this prison than the thing that built it."

I looked at that map — at eleven years of stubborn, careful, honest work charting the scars of a war the world had been forbidden to remember — and I felt, for the first time in sixty years, something adjacent to hope.

"All right," I said. "But you'd best sleep first. Tomorrow we climb to a village that isn't on any of your maps."

Kestrel frowned. "Every village in Thornfell is on my maps."

"Not this one," I said, and fed the fire another stick, and did not meet their eyes.
