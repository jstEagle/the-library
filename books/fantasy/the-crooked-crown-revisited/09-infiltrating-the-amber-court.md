## 9. Infiltrating the Amber Court

The capital of Aldermere was called the Amber Court because, at sunset, its thousand windows caught the light and burned like honey held up to a flame. Every ballad mentioned it. Every pilgrim's account mentioned it. I had drawn it myself, once, from secondhand reports — a careful little engraving of towers and walls with a note in my own hand: *approach from the south is well-patrolled; approach from the north is impossible.*

I want it on record that the note said *impossible*, not *inadvisable*. Oren had simply never read my notes.

"There," he said, crouched on a ridge above the northern ravine, pointing at what looked to me like a sheer cliff face wearing a scarf of dead ivy. "There's a door."

"That's a wall."

"It's a door that's been pretending to be a wall for two hundred years. Doors get tired, Kestrel. They forget themselves." He rubbed his hands together — he was always rubbing his hands together, as if warming them over a fire only he could see, which, knowing him, was probably literal. "All I have to do is remind it."

We had been traveling together for eleven days by then, long enough that I'd catalogued his habits the way I catalogued everything: Oren slept sitting up, facing exits. He flinched at hinges. He talked to doorways the way other men talked to horses, low and coaxing, and — this was the part I tried not to think about too hard — the doorways sometimes seemed to listen.

"Remind it how?" I asked, though I already regretted asking.

"With courtesy." He started down the slope before I could point out that courtesy had never once gotten me through a locked anything.

The smuggler routes had gotten us this far. That much of my trade still worked. The Thornfell paths I'd spent nine years mapping — the ones that shifted when the binding-scarred land flexed, the ones no crown surveyor could hold onto — those had carried us east like a river carrying a leaf, and I had felt, for the first time since the pilgrim died in my camp with her letter pressed into my hands, something like competence. Something like purpose. I knew these roads. The roads knew me back, which was either comforting or ominous depending on the hour.

But roads end at cities, and cities are made entirely of doors, and doors were his jurisdiction now, not mine.

At the base of the cliff, Oren pressed his palm flat against stone and murmured something in a language that sounded like a hinge remembering oil. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the ivy shivered, the rock sighed — there is no better word for it, the rock *sighed*, deep and mineral and put-upon — and a seam opened where no seam had been, tall as a man and narrow as an apology.

"After you," Oren said.

"You're not coming?"

"I'll follow. Slowly. If we go through together, the door notices us twice." He glanced at me, and in the grey light his eyes did their unsettling thing, the thing where they seemed to look at me and also through me and also at some third thing standing just behind me that I couldn't see. "Kestrel. Inside the wall, there will be a stair. At the bottom of the stair, there will be a guard post that hasn't been used since the old king's reign. Do not touch the iron brazier. It remembers being lit, and it would like to be lit again, and you do not want to be the one who indulges it."

"Noted," I said. "Any other furniture with opinions?"

"All furniture has opinions. Most of it keeps them to itself. Go."

I went.

The passage inside the wall was exactly as wide as my shoulders and no wider, which told me it had been built for smugglers or priests, two professions with similar relationships to secrecy. The stair went down farther than the wall was tall, which should have been impossible, and I made myself stop counting steps around step ninety, because counting was how I held panic at arm's length and I needed both hands free for the dark.

At the bottom: the guard post, just as he'd said. Dust like grey felt on every surface. An iron brazier squatting in the corner with an air of wounded dignity I chose to ignore. And past it, a door — a real one, oak-banded, with a lock of a make I recognized from a hundred sketches: crown-work, old pattern, the kind the palace locksmiths had used before they started putting wards in everything.

This part was mine. This part I could do.

I knelt, drew out my picks — yes, a cartographer carries picks; you try mapping smuggler roads without learning locks — and set to work. Old lock, old habits. It took me less time than it takes to tell it. The tumblers gave way with a sound like a small polite cough, and the door swung inward onto torchlight, warm air, and the smell of beeswax and woodsmoke and, underneath it all, cold stone.

The smell of cold stone. Sable's letters — the ones she'd sent years ago to her father's mountain estates, copies of which circulated among the border gentry — had described the Amber Court as smelling of beeswax and woodsmoke. None of them had mentioned the cold underneath. But then, none of them had been written recently.

I stepped through into the under-halls of the palace of Aldermere, god of thresholds at my back, a dead king's letter against my ribs, and thought: *nine years of maps, and the most important room in the kingdom was behind a door any decent lockpick could open.*

Maybe that was the whole problem with the realm. Nobody important ever checked the obvious doors.

* * *

They say a caged bird sings. What they don't tell you is that a caged princess learns to count footsteps.

Four guards on the morning rotation outside my wing. Two on the afternoon. Six after dark, because Father — because *the thing wearing my father* — had grown more cautious as midwinter approached, though cautious about what, precisely, I couldn't imagine. I was nineteen, unarmed, and confined to six rooms and a garden with a wall too high to be worth climbing. The kingdom's great threat, me, in my wool stockings.

I counted footsteps because counting was the only rebellion available, and because patterns are a kind of map, and maps are how you escape places.

That evening, the pattern broke.

It was a small break, the kind anyone else would have missed. The afternoon pair — Hesk and the young one, Tam, who still couldn't meet my eye — changed their circuit. Instead of walking the corridor's full length, they stopped at the far end, near the service stair, and stayed there. Talking. Low. Tam kept glancing toward the stairwell door.

Someone was in the service stair.

Now, a sensible princess would have reported this. A sensible princess would have knocked on her own door and said, *Guards, there may be intruders in the walls, please investigate,* and earned herself another week of confinement and the guards' gratitude.

I had stopped being sensible somewhere around the fourth month, when I'd confirmed what I'd only dared suspect before: that my father did not blink. Not during audiences, not during meals, not during the long terrible evenings when he sat across from me and asked about my studies in a voice pitched perfectly between warmth and nothing at all. He smelled of cold stone instead of skin. When he thought I wasn't looking, his head turned in small precise increments, like a weathervane finding wind. Whatever wore the crown of Aldermere, it was not my father, and every day it grew more practiced at being him, and every day I understood a little more clearly that when the coronation rite came at midwinter — the rite the courtiers spoke of with such shining faces — whatever remained of Aldric Ishikawa would be gone entirely, and I would be the daughter of a hollow thing, forever.

So when the pattern broke, I did what any daughter of a puppet-king would do.

I went out the window.

Not climbed — *walked*. There is a difference, and the difference is four months of bribing a laundress with embroidered handkerchiefs to leave the garden gate key in the sundial, and three weeks of studying which ivy on the outer wall bore fruiting bodies of a lichen that only grows where mortar has crumbled, and one humiliating afternoon of falling into a rosebush. I had mapped my own prison the way I mapped everything, obsessively, secretly, because mapping was the one inheritance from my father that the thing upstairs couldn't take from him.

The servants' corridor behind the laundry was empty at the change of the watch. I moved through it in soft-soled shoes, hugging the wall where the floorboards didn't sing, following the draft of cold air that meant the service stair — and stopped dead at the bottom landing, because the door to the under-halls stood open, and someone was inside the key-room.

I heard them before I saw them: a faint metallic whisper, the particular music of a lock being persuaded. Then a voice, low, muttering — not a prayer, exactly. More like a cartographer cursing a badly drawn legend.

"*Third ring, second hook, behind the ledger of tolls*," the voice recited. "*Come on. Wren's information had better be worth what it cost us.*"

Wren. The name meant nothing to me. The voice meant something — it was roughened by mountain air and road dust, and it was saying *tolls* and *ledger* with the accent of the Thornfell passes, the border country my father had loved and neglected in equal measure.

I should have called out. I should have run. I did neither. I stepped into the doorway of the key-room, planted my feet the way the fencing master had taught me before Father decided princesses didn't need fencing masters anymore, and said:

"Step away from the keys."

* * *

In fairness to Princess Sable, she nearly had me.

She stepped out of the dark with a candlestick raised like a mace and her chin set at an angle I recognized immediately, because I'd seen it on every border lord's heir who ever sneered at my trade: the angle of someone who has been told all her life that she matters, and has recently begun to suspect that nobody else believes it.

For one absurd heartbeat we stared at each other — the fugitive cartographer with a stolen court key in each fist, and the escaped prisoner in soft-soled shoes holding a candlestick — and I watched the same realization move across her face that was moving across mine.

"You're not a guard," she said.

"You're not supposed to be out of your rooms," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it sounded like I'd read her file.

Her eyes narrowed. "You know about my rooms."

"I know about the guards' rotation. Four, two, six. I've been watching this palace for two days." I lifted the keys slightly, so they caught the candlelight. "And I know that whoever wears your father's face doesn't sleep."

The candlestick lowered an inch. Only an inch. She was smarter than the angle of her chin suggested. "Who are you?"

"Kestrel Ishikawa. Cartographer." I hesitated, then decided the truth was a weapon I might as well draw. "Born in Thornfell. And you're Sable. Which makes us —"

"Don't," she said sharply. "Don't say cousins. Everyone says cousins. Half the realm claims some connection to the crown when it suits them."

"Fine. Not cousins." I set the keys down on the table between us, deliberately, palms open. Behind me, I felt rather than heard Oren fill the doorway — he moved quietly for a man whose entire existence was entrances and exits. "I'm here because a dying woman asked me to deliver a letter to King Aldric. I read it. It was written in the king's own hand, three years ago, sealed with instructions that it be opened only if he stopped being himself. So either I'm a fool, or you already know what's wrong with your father, and I'm betting on the second, because you just snuck out of a guarded wing with a candlestick and excellent footwork, and fools don't learn footwork."

Silence. Somewhere above us, the palace ticked and settled, a thousand rooms digesting their secrets.

"The letter," she said at last. Her voice had changed — stripped of the court polish, down to something younger and rawer. "What did it say?"

"That if you're reading this, Veyra has won the first round. That the bindings on the old gods were fraying, and something slipped through ahead of them, and it went for the throne because the throne is the strongest anchor left in the world. That he could feel himself —" I stopped. Some sentences shouldn't be delivered standing up in a key-room. "He wrote that he was sorry. That he'd tried to hold on. That his daughter would know what to do, because she'd always seen more than he told her she could."

Sable's hand went to her mouth. Not sobbing — she was far too angry to sob — but pressing, holding something in, the way you press a wound. When she spoke again, her voice was steady in the way of things balanced on an edge.

"Four months," she said. "Four months I've known, and everyone looks at me like I'm mad, and the physicians come with their tonics, and *he* comes, smiling with my father's mouth, asking about my studies." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath it all, desperately tired. "You watched the guards for two days. You picked a lock older than my grandfather. Who taught you the rotations?"

"Nobody. I watched. It's what I do — I watch, and I write down what moves, and eventually the movement tells me where the roads are." I shrugged. "Even in palaces. Especially in palaces. Palaces are just roads that learned to stand still."

Something in her expression cracked — not breaking, but thawing. She looked at me the way I'd looked at the pass that appeared on none of my maps: wary, hungry, unwilling to hope and hoping anyway.

"And him?" She nodded past me, at Oren.

Oren bowed, which for him involved a strange folding motion, like a door closing courteously. "Oren," he said. "God of thresholds. Formerly. Currently more of a consultant."

"A god," Sable repeated flatly.

"A faded one. Don't kneel, it embarrasses us both." He straightened. "Your highness, I knew your father. Before. He once held a door open for me in a rainstorm in Duncarrow, which sounds like nothing, but kindness to strangers at doorways is the oldest prayer there is, and I remembered it. I have come a very long way to repay it."

I watched her weigh him — the ragged coat, the road-grime, the eyes that looked slightly past everything — and I watched her decide. It took perhaps three seconds. Later, I would learn that this was her way: fast decisions, held onto with a grip that could crack stone.

"My father is alive," she said. Not a question. A foundation stone, laid down.

"Some of him," Oren said gently. "Enough to matter. Enough to fight for."

"Then we have until midwinter." She set the candlestick down at last, and squared her shoulders, and I saw the princess and the girl and the soldier all arrive in the same body at the same moment. "The rite is scheduled for the solstice. I've read the ceremonial drafts — they won't show me the true ones, but the choreography alone tells me it's a binding, not a crowning. We have five weeks."

"Five weeks," I agreed. "Which means we need the rest of those keys, the layout of the under-croft, and a way to reach the king without going through forty guards and whatever Veyra's senses can reach."

"Wren can get the layout," Sable said.

I blinked. "Wren? Your Wren or our Wren?"

She frowned. "A pickpocket. Fifteen, maybe. Ears like a bat, morals like weather. Why do you —"

"Oh no," I said, with the deep weary certainty of a man watching two separate maps turn out to depict the same disaster. "Oh, *no*. Tallis said she sold the route to a spymaster. Tallis said she'd been asking about the palace kitchens."

"She's been *here*?" Sable and I said it together, in unison, with identical horror, and for one strange moment we were perfectly aligned, two people discovering they shared a problem shaped exactly like a teenage thief.

Oren laughed — a dry, rusted-hinge sound. "Doors," he said, "open in the strangest places."

Above us, faint through floors and stone, a bell began to ring the change of watch, and somewhere in the palace a thing wearing a dead king's face turned its head, very slightly, toward the sound of its daughter's absence.

We had five weeks. It was time to go find Wren.
