## 10. An Audience with the Hollow King

My father used to say that ruling was mostly a matter of knowing which doors to leave open and which to lock. He said it the way he said everything, as though it were a joke only he had been told yet, and he'd laugh at his own punchline before he finished it.

He hasn't laughed in three years.

I learned early, in this gilded cage they call protective custody, that the guards report everything. So I stopped trying to escape through doors — I started leaving them open instead. A princess who reads too long by candlelight is suspicious. A princess who leaves her window unshuttered on a cold night is merely careless. Carelessness, I have discovered, is the most useful costume a prisoner can wear.

The candle trick took two weeks to perfect. I let the wax pool against the sill until it sealed the latch loose, then complained loudly to my maid about drafts until she mentioned it to the chamberlain, who mentioned it to no one, because chamberlains are paid to forget things. By the time the first hard frost came, my window opened onto the servants' stair like an old friend.

Which is how I came to be standing in the antechamber of the Amber Court at an hour when honest people are asleep, watching a smuggler pick the lock of my father's private study with a bent hairpin and a god's blessing.

"You're doing it wrong," I whispered.

Kestrel Ishikawa — for of course I'd worked out who they were; a stranger in patched leathers asking after court keys draws attention even among thieves — glanced up. In the dark their face was all sharp angles and stubbornness. "Doing what wrong?"

"The lock. It's a Merrow tumbler. You have to lift the third pin twice." I crouched beside them, took the hairpin, and demonstrated. The lock gave a small, satisfied click. "My father taught me. He said a ruler should know how every door in her house opens."

"Did he now," Kestrel said, in a tone that suggested they knew something about my father I didn't. They'd been wearing that tone since we collided in the corridor ten minutes ago — me in stolen servant's grey, them smelling of mountain cold and panic, both of us frozen mid-step, each waiting for the other to scream.

Neither of us had screamed. That, more than anything, was why I was here.

"And you?" I handed back the hairpin. "What does a cartographer want with the king's private correspondence?"

"I don't want his correspondence. I want his keys. There's a room under the east wing—" They stopped themselves, jaw tightening. "You wouldn't believe me if I told you."

"Try me. I've spent three years living with something I can't explain. My tolerance for unbelievable has grown considerably."

They looked at me then — really looked, the way surveyors look at terrain, measuring whether I'd hold their weight. Whatever they saw, they didn't share it. "Later," they said. "Keys first."

I should have pressed. I was raised to press; pressing is half of politics. But there was a hunger in their voice when they said *later*, a hunger I recognized, because I heard it in my own voice every time I spoke about my father. So I let it go, and we went into the study together, and that was the night I decided to trust a smuggler, a decision my father would have called either very wise or very foolish, depending entirely on which of him you asked.

* * *

Here is what everyone in the palace knows and no one says aloud: the king does not blink.

Watch him during audiences. Watch him through the long petitions and the grain disputes and the ambassadors' tedious speeches. His eyes stay open, wet and fixed, like a man staring into a well. The courtiers have learned not to meet his gaze. The physicians call it "the king's melancholy affliction" and prescribe rest. The priests call it piety. The servants call it nothing at all, because the last servant who called it anything was dismissed so thoroughly that her family received letters in her handwriting for a year afterward.

I noticed it first at supper, three years ago. He held a spoon over his bowl for eleven minutes. I counted. Eleven minutes, and the soup went cold, and his eyes never moved from the middle distance, and when Mother touched his hand he set the spoon down with terrible precision, exactly where it had been, and smiled.

It was a good smile. That was the worst part. It was *his* smile, the crooked one, the one that meant he was about to say something outrageous. But the eyes above it were empty as winter windows, and when he leaned close and kissed my forehead, he smelled of cold stone. Not sweat, not wine, not the cedar oil he'd worn my whole childhood. Cold stone, deep underground, the smell of a crypt.

I was fourteen. I told myself I imagined it. I told myself that for two years, right up until the night I found him standing in the nursery corridor at three in the morning, facing the wall, motionless, whispering numbers under his breath. When I said "Father?" he turned with a smoothness no grieving insomniac possesses and said, "Go back to bed, Sable. All is well."

All is well. He'd never once said that phrase in his life. My father believed that saying a thing made it true, and he distrusted true things on principle.

Two weeks later I was confined to the royal apartments "for my safety." Assassins, they said. Dangers abroad. The regency council would handle matters of state; Father needed rest. And my father — the thing wearing my father — signed the order himself, in his own hand, with a signature so perfect it made me sick. Whoever wore him had studied him well. They had everything but the blinking.

So when Kestrel Ishikawa appeared in my corridor smelling of mountains and fear, carrying questions about keys and rooms beneath the east wing, I did not scream. I did the arithmetic instead: strangers do not sneak into palaces to ask about the king unless someone outside knows something. And anyone who knows something might know *everything*.

I intended, that first night, to use them. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. I intended to pump the smuggler for information, trade it to whatever faction seemed useful, and buy my way out of confinement. I was my father's daughter. I thought in trades.

Then I watched them look at my father across a candlelit table, and the arithmetic stopped working.

* * *

Getting the audience approved was almost insultingly easy. That should have warned me.

I petitioned the Lord Chamberlain for a private meeting with His Majesty — daughter's privilege, filial devotion, all the soft words that make hard men comfortable. I requested it for the hour after evening prayers, when the court empties and the guards change shift and everyone with ambition is at dinner performing loyalty. I asked that my companion be permitted to attend: a traveling mapmaker, I said, presenting the crown with new surveys of the Thornfell passes, a gift for the king's renowned interest in geography.

My father had no renowned interest in geography. My father thought maps were "pictures of arguments waiting to happen." But the Chamberlain did not know that, or did not care, and the request slid through the machinery of the court like a coin through a gambler's fingers — too smoothly, as I say. Later I would understand why. Later I would understand that *it* wanted Kestrel close, wanted to look at them, the way a spider wants to look at the thing trembling in its web. But that night I walked back to my apartments believing I had outmaneuvered the palace, and I let myself feel clever about it for nearly an entire hour.

Kestrel was less pleased. "She let us in too easily," they said, pacing my sitting room, boots leaving faint mountain dirt on carpets older than the kingdom. "Nobody grants an audience that fast. Not to a smuggler in borrowed clothes."

"Perhaps the Chamberlain is simply incompetent."

"Perhaps. Or perhaps the king already knows we're coming." They stopped pacing. "Sable. What do you actually know about your father's condition?"

And there it was — the question I'd been circling for three years, asked plainly by a stranger with ink-stained fingers. I could have lied. I could have given the official version: grief, illness, the king's decline. Instead I heard myself say, "I think something is wearing him. Like a coat. And I think the coat smiles better than he ever did."

Kestrel sat down slowly. "Then I'll tell you what I know. But you're not going to like it, and afterward you're still going to walk into that room tomorrow, and I need you to promise me you'll keep your face still no matter what happens."

"My face is the one part of me they've never managed to confine," I said. "Promise kept in advance."

* * *

The Amber Court at dusk is beautiful the way a preserved specimen is beautiful. The light comes through amber glass on the eastern wall and turns everything the color of honey held up to a flame — the pillars, the banners, the faces of the petitioners. As a child I thought it was the loveliest room in the world. Now I understood it differently: the amber glass filtered everything. Nothing entered that room unchanged. Whatever stood on the throne dais was seen through a warm, forgiving haze, and the court had mistaken the filter for mercy.

My father sat on the throne with his hands folded, and the last light pooled around him like something poured.

"Sable." My name in his voice. Three years since I'd heard it, except through doors, except at distance, except in the flat ceremonial cadence of proclamations. Up close, in the quiet, it undid me a little. It was his voice. Deeper than memory, rougher, the voice that had sung off-key on purpose to make Mother laugh. "You've grown."

"You say that," I managed, "as though you weren't there for it."

Something crossed his face — a flicker, a stutter, like a lamp guttering in wind. Then the smoothness returned. "Time moves quickly, when one is... occupied."

"Occupied," I repeated. "Yes. I imagine the crown keeps you busy."

Beside me, Kestrel bowed with the precise awkwardness of someone who had rehearsed courtly gestures in front of a mirror and hated every second. "Your Majesty. Kestrel Ishikawa, cartographer. I've brought surveys of the Thornfell high passes. Routes that appear on no chart in your archive."

"Routes that appear on no chart," the king repeated. His head tilted — slowly, mechanically, a doll's tilt. Those wet, unblinking eyes settled on Kestrel, and I felt the temperature of the room change, felt the amber light thicken like syrup. "How interesting. And how did you find roads that do not wish to be found?"

"They found me, Your Majesty."

Silence. Somewhere in the walls, the old clock in the west tower struck the quarter hour, and I realized my father had not breathed through any of it. Not once. His chest was still. His hands were folded. His eyes were open, and open, and open.

"Leave us," he said to the guards.

"Your Majesty," the captain began, "the council's instruction—"

"*Leave us.*"

They left. Of course they left. You do not refuse the king's voice, even when the king is wrong, even when the king is a locked room with something moving inside it. The doors closed, and the four of us — father, daughter, stranger, and whatever else had come in with the stranger, though I didn't know its name yet — were alone in the honeyed light.

Kestrel stepped forward and laid the maps on the table before the throne. I watched my father's eyes track them — not reading, *hunting*. Watching Kestrel's hands the way a snake watches warmth.

"These routes," Kestrel said quietly, "converge on Thornfell. On a village that burned thirty years ago. Your Majesty may remember it."

"I remember everything," the king said. And then, horribly, gently: "That is rather the problem."

My heart stumbled. Because that was not the crown speaking. That cadence — dry, self-mocking, the joke-told-only-to-himself — that was *him*. That was bedtime-story voice, chess-game voice, the voice of a man who had taught me locks and maps and which doors to leave open.

"Father?" I said.

His head turned toward me. Slowly. With effort, I realized — with visible, grinding effort, like a man hauling a door open against a strong wind. And his eyes changed.

I cannot describe it better than that. The wet blankness *drained*, the way fog lifts off a valley, and underneath was my father. Tired. Frightened. Thirty years older than the man who'd signed my confinement order, because some part of him had been awake inside his own body for three years, watching hands that weren't his sign things, watching a smile that wasn't his charm the court, screaming into a silence no one could hear.

His lips moved. No sound came. Whatever wore him kept the throat locked tight as a vault.

But I had grown up reading my father across crowded rooms, decoding his eyebrows during tedious ambassadorial dinners, learning the language of his mouth when discretion demanded silence. I watched his lips shape the word, once, twice, three times, with the desperate patience of a drowning man signaling.

*Daughter.*

Not *help*. Not *run*. Not even *Sable*, my name, the word he'd chosen for me before I was born.

*Daughter.*

As if, after three years in his own private hell, the first thing he needed me to know — the only thing — was that he knew me. That somewhere behind the locked door, he had never once confused the thing wearing his body with the girl he'd raised. That every time it smiled at me with his mouth, some buried part of him had been shouting *that is not me, that is not me, look again, look again.*

I had looked again. Three years too late, but I had finally looked.

My face stayed still. I had promised. I kept my spine straight and my hands folded and my expression composed into the mild, dutiful blankness of a princess at an audience, while inside me something broke and reformed around the breaking, the way bone knits.

Kestrel saw it too. I felt them go rigid beside me. But they played their part, murmuring something about survey lines and ley-roads, buying seconds, and my father's eyes flicked between us — between the smuggler and the daughter — and his lips moved again, faster now, urgent, spelling out words I couldn't catch, and tears were running down his face without his expression changing at all, which was somehow the most unbearable thing, tears falling from those resurrected eyes while the mouth below stayed perfectly, politely neutral—

The doors opened.

The change was instantaneous, total, obscene. The tears stopped. The fog rolled back over his eyes like a tide reclaiming a shore, and the King of Aldenmere unfolded from the throne with fluid grace and said, warmly, in a voice with no one home:

"Thank you for the maps, Cartographer. We shall examine them with great interest." A pause. The amber light caught the crown on his brow and made it glow like a coal. "Do give my regards to your associate. Oren sends his love, after all."

Kestrel made a sound I'd never heard a human being make — half gasp, half snarl.

"And Sable." The king smiled his crooked smile, the good one, the stolen one. "You will be pleased to know the council has reconsidered your confinement. You'll dine with the court again tomorrow. It's time you were seen more. Midwinter approaches, after all, and the realm loves its princess."

It released me. It was *releasing* me, and every instinct said this was not mercy but strategy, a piece moved on a board, and I curtsied anyway, because my legs were shaking and the curtsy hid it.

We walked out through the amber light. Behind us, on the throne, my father sat with his hands folded and did not blink, and I carried his single silent word out of the room like contraband, like a coal cupped in bare hands, certain only of this: he was alive in there. He had waited three years to tell me so.

And whatever had let us in so easily — whatever had arranged this audience like a host arranging flowers — had gotten exactly what it wanted.

Midwinter. Two weeks away. I just didn't yet know what was coming at midwinter, or why the thing in my father's eyes had looked at Kestrel Ishikawa like a spider looking at a fly it had personally invited into the web.

But I was going to find out. My father had taught me which doors to leave open.

I intended to open every last one of them.
