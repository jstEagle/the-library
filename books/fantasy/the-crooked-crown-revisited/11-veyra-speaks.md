## 11. Veyra Speaks

Let me tell you the first thing they never understand, the historians and the priests and the clever little cartographers of the world: I did not steal this kingdom. I inherited it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

I was a god once. Properly speaking, I still am — divinity does not wear out like a coat, though it can be folded away into spaces too small for it, which is what they did to me, which is what they did to all of us. But before the folding there was a country that loved me, and my name was spoken at every hearth from the Amber Court to the salt marshes, and my domain was order. Not tyranny — order. The distinction matters to me even now, especially now, when everyone who remembers my name at all uses it as a curse.

Order is the thing you only notice when it fails. Nobody prays to the wall that holds. Nobody lights a candle for the bridge that stands. For four hundred years I kept the roads straight and the laws legible and the seasons arriving on schedule like guests with good manners, and in return the people of this realm forgot me so thoroughly that when the binding wars came, they helped.

That is the part that stings, if a god can be said to sting. It wasn't the mages with their chalk circles. It was the farmers. It was the merchants. It was a woman in a village not thirty miles from where Kestrel Ishikawa sleeps tonight — but I am getting ahead of myself — a woman who set out bread for Oren's doorways and milk for the river-gods and nothing, nothing at all, for me, because I had done my work so well that she believed the world simply *was* orderly, the way stone is hard, the way water runs downhill. She thanked the rain and never thought to thank the arrangement that let the rain fall on fields instead of on her roof.

I do not hate her. I want to be clear about that, because everyone assumes hatred, and hatred would be simpler. What I felt watching her was something colder and more patient. I looked at her small, careless, blessed life and I thought: *you are a house with no one home.* You live inside a machine you cannot see, tended by hands you will not acknowledge, and you call your ignorance peace.

So yes. When the bindings frayed — and they were always going to fray; chalk fades, faith thins, and gods are patient in the way that mountains are patient — when I finally slipped through the crack in the world, I did not come as a plague. I came as a correction.

* * *

You should understand what it is like to be bound. Not imprisoned — prisons have walls, and walls have outsides, and an outside means somewhere else to be. A binding is smaller than that. They folded me into the crown itself, into the gold and the old king's oath and the ceremony of crowning, and for centuries I existed as a kind of pressure, a weight pressing down on every head that wore me. Kings complained of headaches. Chroniclers called it "the burden of rule" and wrote poems about it. Poems! I was right there, pressed against their skulls, listening to them misgovern, and they wrote *poems* about their headaches.

Aldric was different. That is the tragedy I will admit to, since I am apparently confessing things tonight. Aldric was the best of them — conscientious, tired, genuinely devoted to the realm in the way of a man who loves a garden he will never sit in. He wore me for thirty years and apologized to me nightly, in his prayers, in the small hours. *Forgive me,* he'd think, rubbing his temples. *I know you're in there. I know this isn't fair.*

Do you see? He knew. He knew and he wore me anyway, because the alternative was abdication, and Aldric could no more abdicate than a keel can jump ship. So we developed an arrangement, he and I. He governed; I whispered. When his judgment failed him — and it failed him more often near the end, as age does to all flesh — I lent him mine. A suggestion here. A certainty there. The sense, arriving fully formed at three in the morning, that the northern lords were planning something and should be watched.

He noticed, of course. He was not stupid. But by then the whispering had been going on for decades, and a man cannot untangle which thoughts are his own, and eventually he stopped trying. And then one winter night, coughing blood into a silk handkerchief behind the altar screen, dying at sixty-one with his work unfinished, he made the mistake of praying to me directly.

*If you can help them finish it,* he thought, *take whatever you need.*

I want you to appreciate the theological elegance of that moment. A king, offering his body to a god of order, in exchange for the realm's stability. That is not possession. That is a contract. I have the paperwork — metaphorically. His own words, witnessed by his own god, sealed with his own death.

The court physicians called it a miraculous recovery. The court called it a second spring. His daughter called it suspicious, because Sable has her mother's eyes and her mother's talent for looking directly at things everyone else has agreed not to see.

* * *

Ah, Sable.

She is the flaw in my design, and I say that with something adjacent to pride. I have ruled through Aldric's body for six years now, and I will tell you honestly: ruling is harder than being worshipped. Worship is passive; it asks nothing of you but presence. Governance is a thousand small decisions a day, each one disappointing someone, and I make them all, and I make them well, and the granaries are full and the border raids have stopped and the coin no longer clips, and still the court murmurs, because the court can smell that something is wrong the way dogs smell weather.

What's wrong, of course, is that Aldric does not blink during audiences. I have tried to remember blinking. It seems such a small thing — eyelids, moisture, a half-second of darkness twice a minute — but the body keeps its own ledgers, and six years of unblinking attention has left its mark on the courtiers' unease. Also I run cold. Flesh wants warmth the way faith wants fuel, and I spend what heat I have on the important performances: speeches, coronations, the occasional wave from a balcony. I cannot spare it for smelling of skin. Cold stone is what I have. Let the gossips make of it what they will.

Sable noticed everything. The cold. The stillness. The way her father stopped humming her mother's songs. She was twenty-two and sharp as a drawn blade, and she started asking questions in that quiet, dangerous way of hers, and so I did the mathematically necessary thing: I confined her. House arrest, we called it. *For her safety.* The phrase has a long and distinguished history of meaning *because you frighten me*, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, and we have been conducting our cold war through locked doors ever since.

Here is what troubles my sleep — if a god wearing a dead man could be said to sleep. Here is what sits in the back of the throne room like an unpaid debt: I need her. Not captured. Not destroyed. *Crowned after me.* My rite at midwinter will fuse me permanently to the throne, to the ley-lines beneath it, to the whole nervous system of the realm — and then I will need an heir the people love, an heir with warm hands and her father's jaw, to stand beside me while I do the actual work. Sable thinks I keep her prisoner because she knows too much. In truth I keep her close because she is the last piece, and pieces must not be lost.

She will come to understand. She is her father's daughter; she understands duty, even if she hates the word. And when midwinter comes and the rite completes and the realm stops tearing itself apart at the seams — when the smugglers' roads close and the feuds cool and every crooked thing is drawn straight — she will look out over a kingdom at peace and she will hate me, and she will be grateful, and she will learn, as I learned, that those two things can share a heart without either one winning.

* * *

Now. The vessel.

I felt Kestrel Ishikawa the moment the bindings began to fray — felt them the way you feel a draft under a door. The old gods made that child for a purpose, you know. This is not my secret to tell in full, and besides, they don't know it themselves yet, which is the most delicious part. But mark me: a cartographer who maps roads that shift, who walks passes that close behind them like healing wounds, who was born in the last god-touched village the year it burned — that is not a person, that is a *doorway with legs*. The old gods poured everything they had left into making a vessel they could pour themselves back through. It was their masterstroke. Their one hope of returning.

And they are walking their vessel straight toward my capital.

I could stop them. Do not mistake me — I could stop them tomorrow. One word to my spymaster, whose loyalty I purchased years ago and whose name I will not sully by using it here, and Kestrel Ishikawa dies in a ditch with their beautiful maps scattered in the snow. I have considered it. I consider it nightly, the way one considers putting down a sick animal: necessary, regrettable, and deferred because some stubborn part of you wants to see how it ends.

Here is why I defer. Here is my confession, since we are being honest, and honesty is the one luxury I permit myself in the dark hours when Aldric's body aches with a dead man's rheumatism:

I am starving.

There. Said. Six years inside this body, six years of governing, and the terrible truth is that governance feeds me less than worship ever did. Order maintained is invisible; order *imposed* makes people pray — but they pray in fear, and fear is thin gruel, fear is broth made from boiling the bones of a meal already eaten. I need to be *loved* again. I need hearth-altars and harvest hymns and children taught my name alongside their letters. And the old gods, if they return — if they pour themselves through their cartographer-vessel and spread across the land like spilled ink — they will take the worship with them. All of it. Every candle, every prayer, every crumb of belief this realm has left. And I will go back into the dark, back into the fold, back into the space too small for me, and this time there will be no fraying, because vessels do not fray. Vessels hold.

Unless.

Unless the vessel chooses differently. Unless someone gets to Kestrel Ishikawa first and offers them a better bargain than martyrdom. The old gods made Kestrel a door. But doors, my dear anxious reader, open in both directions — and nobody, in all their long scheming, thought to ask the door what *it* wanted.

I have been asking. Quietly. Through dreams, mostly, slipped in along the ley-lines while the vessel sleeps in smugglers' safehouses. I show Kestrel a kingdom at peace. Straight roads. Full granaries. No burning villages, no binding wars, no gods at all — just people, unmolested by the divine, living lives so orderly they can forget the divine entirely, which is, if you think about it, the kindest gift a god can give. Kestrel wakes from these dreams unsettled. I can feel the unsettlement from here, a small warm itch at the edge of my awareness, like a fire lit in the next room.

They think the dreams are their own doubts. That is the beauty of it. You never have to conquer a mind that is already arguing with itself.

* * *

Tonight I walked the palace corridors alone — Aldric's body needs exercise or it stiffens, another indignity of flesh — and I stood at the window of the observatory looking down at the capital in the snow. Ten thousand rooftops. Ten thousand hearths. Somewhere down there, Wren — yes, I know about Wren, I know about the safehouse, I know about the pickpocket who sold their names to my spymaster and then wept about it, mortals are so wonderfully predictable in their loyalties — somewhere down there my enemies are huddled around a fire planning my downfall, and I found, examining myself honestly, that I wished them well. Warmth to them. Full bellies. Let them come to midwinter rested and hopeful. Hope makes the disappointment cleaner.

Below me, the city glowed. And I thought: *mine. All of it, mine, and none of them know my name, and every single one of them is safer for it.* Is that not mercy? To protect people so completely they never have to think of you? Mothers do it. Walls do it. I do it. And when midwinter comes and the rite binds me to the ley-lines forever, I will do it for every road and river and roof from Thornfell to the sea, and the age of squabbling gods and crooked borders and prayers that go unanswered will end, and the realm will be one thing, whole, at peace, under one will —

Under one will. Yes. I hear how it sounds. I have always heard how it sounds. But ask yourself, whoever you are, reading these thoughts like a thief reading letters in a drawer: when the flood comes, do you want five voices telling you where the high ground is, or one?

One. It is always one. Unity is not tyranny. Unity is the flood receding.

In the morning I will review the coronation preparations. The rite is scheduled; the documents are signed; the ley-lines beneath the palace wait under their binding-stone like horses under harness. Everything proceeds. Everything is on time, because of course it is — punctuality is the smallest of my sacraments.

But tonight, one last thing happened, and I record it here because even gods should keep accounts. As I turned from the window, I felt — faintly, from far away, from the direction of the binding-stone deep beneath my feet — a stirring. Something probing the old locks. Something with blood that fits the keyhole.

The vessel has found the stone.

Well then. Let them look. Let them put their clever fingers on the mechanism their makers built. By the time they understand what they've found, midwinter will be nine days gone, and I will be permanent, and the door they were meant to be will be nailed shut from the other side.

Nine days. I have waited four hundred years.

I can wait nine more.
