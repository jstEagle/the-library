## 12. The Coronation Rite

My father used to say that a kingdom is just a story everyone agrees to keep telling. He said it the way he said most wise things — absently, over breakfast, with jam on his thumb — and I spent my childhood rolling my eyes at it. Now I live in a palace where the story has been taken over by an unauthorized narrator, and I find myself wishing I'd paid closer attention to the rest of that particular lecture.

Here is what I know about being under house arrest in your own home: the walls are familiar, the tea is terrible, and the guards assigned to keep you safe are almost pathetically easy to charm if you've spent nineteen years learning exactly how charming you can be before someone reports it.

I had been confined for six weeks when I decided I was done waiting for rescue. Not because I'd found allies — I hadn't, unless you counted my maid Brissa, who was loyal, terrified, and armed with nothing more dangerous than a hairpin and a talent for eavesdropping. No, I was done waiting because of the smell.

Cold stone. That was the thing nobody else seemed to notice. My father — the thing wearing my father — smelled of cold stone and old copper, like a crypt that had learned to walk. When he embraced me at Midsummer's Eve (he had insisted on embracing me; the court had sighed collectively at the tenderness of it), I had pressed my face against his shoulder and breathed in the grave.

The real Aldric Vane smelled of cedar oil and horse and, on bad days, of the brandy he swore he'd given up. He blinked. He hummed off-key when he thought no one was listening. He had a scar on his left palm from a falcon's talons when he was twelve, and he picked at it when he lied.

The thing in his body did none of these things. It sat through four-hour audiences without blinking until its eyes went glassy as river stones. It signed decrees in a hand that was *almost* his — the letters correct, the rhythm wrong, like music played by someone who had studied the score but never heard the song.

And now it was planning something. I could tell because the palace had begun to hum.

Not literally. Well — possibly literally. There were nights when I put my palm flat against the floor of my chambers and felt something beneath the stone, a vibration like a held breath, like the whole city sitting on top of a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. The servants blamed the winter winds. The priests blamed the gods' departure, which was rich, since the gods' departure had been official doctrine for three generations and nobody had ever blamed them for anything before.

I blamed my father's crown. And I intended to prove it.

* * *

Brissa brought me the documents on a Tuesday, folded inside a bolt of grey linen ordered for my winter gowns.

"From the chancery clerk's office," she whispered, not looking at me while she brushed out my hair, which was our code for *this conversation does not exist*. "Tomas owed me a favor. He says these came down from the King's own desk with orders to copy them for the rite-masters. He says—" her voice dropped further, "—he says the copies are supposed to be burned after. Every scribe who worked on them sworn to silence."

"And Tomas gave them to you anyway."

"Tomas wants to marry me." She met my eyes in the mirror, and hers were wet and furious. "He says if something's wrong with the King, someone ought to know. He says the rite-masters have been drinking like fish and praying like drowning men, and neither one is helping."

"Give Tomas my blessing," I said, "and tell him to stop drinking long enough to count how many rite-masters there are and where they sleep."

Then I unfolded the papers, and the world tilted sideways and did not right itself for a very long time.

* * *

The first document was a summons list. Names, titles, arrival dates. The Archpriest of the Unbroken Circle. The Master of Ley-Works, an office I had assumed was ceremonial, the way Master of the King's Swans is ceremonial. Delegates from every duchy, every free city, every guild that mattered. All summoned to the capital by midwinter.

For a coronation.

That was the word used. *Coronation.* Which was strange, because my father had been crowned twenty-two years ago. Kings do not get coronated twice. Once is the whole point of crowns; twice suggests the first one didn't take, and there are entire civil wars founded on less.

The second document explained why this coronation was different.

It was written in the rite-masters' cipher — the old liturgical script they use for ceremonies too sacred or too ugly for common eyes, all vertical strokes and closed loops, meant to look like decoration to anyone untrained. Fortunately for the realm, my father had believed a princess should learn everything a prince learns, plus the things princes were considered too important to bother with. I had spent two bored winters as a girl deciphering liturgical script with a tutor who smelled of onions and disappointment. I had hated every hour.

I blessed those hours now. I blessed the onion-smelling man by name.

The document was titled, in that looping grave-script: *Rite of Perpetual Union Between the Sovereign Will and the Land's Deep Veins.*

Perpetual. Union. Deep veins.

I read it three times. By the third reading my hands had stopped shaking, mostly because I'd gripped the edge of the dressing table hard enough to hurt.

Here is what the rite would do, as far as I could translate: on midwinter night, at the turning of the year, the King — the crown, the thing in the crown, whatever wore him — would be bound to the throne of Amber Court permanently. Not symbolically. The throne was not furniture. I had always known that much; every child raised in this palace grows up knowing the throne sits over the meeting-place of the ley-lines, the deep roads of power that run under the realm like roots under a tree. The kings of old had been crowned above them so the land would recognize its keeper.

This rite would make the recognition permanent. Irreversible. The sovereign will and the land's veins fused into one continuous substance, the way wax poured into wax cannot be separated again. Whatever sat on the throne at midwinter midnight would not merely rule the realm.

It would *be* the realm. Every road, every river, every field that drew strength from the lines below — all of it flowing up through the throne into one will. One will, spread across a nation like frost across a window.

And the will in question belonged to whatever had been smelling of cold stone and signing my father's name.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself here. My first instinct was not courage. My first instinct was to be sick behind the dressing table, and I did that, and then I drank the water Brissa brought without being asked, because she had been reading over my shoulder and had already moved to the next step of caring for me. That is the kind of person Brissa is. If the realm had any sense it would crown *her.*

"My lady," she said quietly, "the date."

"I saw the date."

"Midwinter is—"

"Nineteen days away." Nineteen days. Three weeks minus two. Less time than it takes to plan a decent wedding, and considerably more was being planned here. "Nineteen days, and then whatever this is becomes permanent, and we all get to find out what it feels like to be weather."

Brissa's mouth twisted. "That's not funny, my lady."

"No," I agreed. "It's really not. I've simply discovered that if I don't laugh at the apocalypse, it gets to win on points."

She wrung the brush in her hands. "What do we do?"

Which was the question, wasn't it. Nineteen days. A princess locked in her wing with two loyal servants and a hairpin. An enemy that wore the king, commanded the guard, owned the chancery, and had somehow persuaded the entire religious establishment to assist in its wedding to the land itself.

I sat down at my writing desk, because sitting makes thinking easier and standing makes panicking easier, and I made a list. Lists are how the Vanes fight. My father used to say that too — *when the world goes mad, write down what's still true* — and then he'd make lists of the harvest accounts while bandits burned the western farms, which perhaps explains some things about my upbringing.

Things still true:

One. The rite requires the rite-masters, and rite-masters can be delayed, deceived, discredited, or dead. I did not love the last option, but I wrote it down anyway, because a list that flatters your conscience is a useless list.

Two. The rite requires the throne, and thrones cannot be moved — but the documents mentioned preparations. Consecrations. Something about the *sealing of the underways*, which meant there were underways, which meant there were ways *under*, which meant the palace had doors even I didn't know about. Doors could be found. Doors could be opened. Doors could, in principle, be stood in front of by a princess with a hairpin and poor impulse control.

Three. The rite required a king. And the king, whatever else he was now, still had moments. I had seen them — small ones, terrible ones. The flicker behind the glass-stone eyes. Twice in six weeks I had watched his hand twitch toward mine across a table and then stop, forcibly, like a dog jerked back on a chain. Whatever puppeted him was strong, but it was holding something down to hold him, and held things strain.

Four — and I wrote this one slowly, because writing it made it real —

Four. Someone had tried to warn us. Six weeks ago, before my confinement, a pilgrim had died at the palace gates asking for the King, carrying a letter, and the guards had laughed about it at supper where the kitchen girls could hear. *Mad mountain woman, wanted to deliver a message to His Majesty personally, wouldn't give it to anyone else.* They'd turned her away. She'd presumably died somewhere in the lower city with her letter undelivered, and I had thought of it since only as one more small cruelty in a season full of them.

But now I wondered. Now, reading about ley-lines and perpetual unions, I wondered what a mad mountain woman had known that the rite-masters were burning their copies to hide.

There had been a cartographer with her, the kitchen girls said. A mapmaker from Thornfell, odd-looking, quiet, who'd carried the pilgrim the last miles. The guards had questioned the cartographer and let them go, because what harm could a mapmaker do.

I put down my pen.

Somewhere out there was a person who had been the last one to speak with a dying messenger bound for my father. Somewhere out there was a stranger who might know what the letter said, or who sent it, or why the mountains had started producing prophets. It was not much. It was a thread thinner than the ones Brissa pulled from my hems.

But I had nineteen days, a hairpin, a maid with a married admirer in the chancery, and a lifetime of training in exactly one art: getting places I was not permitted to be.

* * *

That night I did not sleep. Instead I sat by my window with the stolen documents hidden in the false bottom of my jewelry box — my mother's jewelry box, which had a false bottom because my mother had been the sort of woman who believed in false bottoms, and I missed her with an ache that had teeth.

Below my window, the palace lay under snow, beautiful and poisoned. Torches marked the guard rotations. Beyond the walls, the city glittered, unaware that in nineteen days its streets and rivers and graves might become extensions of a single alien appetite. And beyond the city, faint as memory, the Thornfell mountains stood black against the stars — the direction the pilgrim had come from, the direction the cartographer had gone.

I pressed my palm against the cold sill and felt it again: the hum under the world. Stronger tonight. Closer to the surface. As if the land itself were lying awake, listening to the plans being made above it, waiting to find out what it was about to become.

"Hold on," I told it, or my father, or both. "Nineteen days. I'm coming."

I went to my wardrobe and began, methodically, to pack for an escape I had no route for yet.

Because that was the other thing my father taught me, though he never said it aloud: you start moving before you know the way. The way has a way of meeting you halfway.

In the morning, I would need Brissa to find out where the rite-masters slept.

Tonight, I needed to learn how to pick a lock with a hairpin, and I had nineteen days to practice.
