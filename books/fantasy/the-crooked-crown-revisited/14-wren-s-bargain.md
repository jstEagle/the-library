## 14. Wren's Bargain

Here is a thing nobody tells you about being a pickpocket: it's the easiest job in the world until the day you have something worth stealing. Then every pocket you've ever picked starts feeling like a debt, and every coin you touch feels like evidence.

I'd been three days without stealing anything. Three days of sitting in a smuggler safehouse that smelled like tallow and old onions, watching Kestrel redraw maps by candlelight and Oren sit so still by the door he might've been furniture, and Sable — a *princess*, an actual princess with actual calluses from climbing out her own window — mending socks like she'd done it all her life.

Three days of eating their food. Wearing their charity. And the money I'd lifted off that court clerk back in the capital was gone, gone into bread and lamp oil and a new boot for Kestrel whose old one had given up its sole somewhere on the mountain road.

We were going to run out. Not food, exactly — Oren could apparently make a doorway open onto a root cellar if you asked him nicely and he was feeling strong enough, which lately he wasn't; the god was fading like ink left in rain. But we'd run out of everything else. Rope. Medicine. The little vial of willowbark tea that kept Kestrel's hands steady enough to draw, because apparently vessels-to-be got headaches like the world was ending, which, fair.

And there was a man I knew who had money. A man who paid for things.

His name was Corvus Dain, and he was the spymaster's left hand, or maybe his right, depending on which hand did the dirty work that week. I'd sold him information twice before, back when information meant *which merchant caravans were carrying silver* and not *where the rightful heir to the throne was sleeping*. Back when selling it didn't make my stomach feel like I'd swallowed a lockpick sideways.

He'd found me on Tuesday. Or rather, his boy had found me — a kid no older than me, standing at the mouth of Tanner's Row with a look on his face that said *I know who you are and I know where you sleep*, which in my trade is basically a formal letter of introduction.

"He wants to talk," the boy said. "The Blue Door. Tomorrow. Come alone."

"Tell him I'm dead," I said.

"He said you'd say that. He said to tell you he pays double for ghosts."

Which is how I ended up, on Wednesday afternoon, telling myself I was only going to *listen*, walking through the rain toward the Blue Door with my hood up and my heart going like a rabbit in a snare.

* * *

The Blue Door wasn't blue anymore. It hadn't been blue in years — some landlord had painted it a sickly green — but everyone still called it the Blue Door because names stick to places like burrs to wool, and because saying *the Green Door* would mean admitting the world changes, and nobody in this city wanted to admit that. Especially not lately, what with the king possibly being a coat that something else was wearing.

Corvus Dain sat in the back booth like he owned the building, the street, and the concept of sitting. He was a soft-looking man, round-faced, with kind eyes and hands that were wrong somehow — too still, too deliberate, like they'd been taught patience by someone very frightening. He had a pot of tea poured for two.

"Wren." He smiled. "You look terrible."

"You look rich," I said, sliding into the booth. "Somebody's been paying you for good news."

"Somebody's been paying me for any news at all." He pushed the teacup across. "The city's nervous. Midwinter's coming, the coronation rite has every noble house sharpening knives, and His Majesty's spymaster wants to know why four people vanished off the face of the earth two weeks ago. A cartographer. A disgraced priest, or so we thought. A princess who climbed out of a window like a common burglar." He sipped his tea. "And a small thief nobody important ever bothered to name."

"I'm nobody," I said. "You've got the wrong ghost."

"Mm." He set down the cup. "Wren. Let's not do the dance. You're cold, your friends are colder, and the safehouse on Candlemaker's Lane leaks heat out the chimney like a signal fire. I could find you tomorrow without lifting a finger. I'm offering you the chance to be the one who tells me, instead of the one I tell."

My stomach did the thing. The lockpick-sideways thing.

"What do you want to know?" I heard myself say, and hated how easy it sounded. How *hungry*. Because here's the truth about hungry: it doesn't care what you feed it. I'd been hungry since I was six years old, since the workhouse, since the winter my mother didn't come back, and hunger doesn't get fixed by two weeks of borrowed family. Hunger just waits. Hunger sits in the Blue Door and pours you tea.

"The cartographer," Corvus said gently, like he was asking after my health. "Where are they keeping the maps? What routes have they marked? And the god — how much is left in him? Days? Hours?"

"And if I tell you?"

"Twenty crowns now. Twenty when it's confirmed. Enough to eat like a person until spring. Enough to leave the city, if you want. New name, new start." He leaned forward, kind eyes kinder. "You're a survivor, Wren. I can see it. Survivors don't drown with a sinking ship out of sentiment."

"They're not sinking," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "They're drowning. There's a difference, but it won't matter to you."

I want to tell you I fought it. I want to tell you I stood up and walked out and spat in his tea. What I actually did was drink the tea, because it was hot and real and mine, and then I talked. Not everything. I told him the safehouse was temporary, that they were planning to move before midwinter, that Oren was weak — weaker than anyone knew, maybe hours of doorway-work left in him, maybe less. I told him Kestrel kept the route-maps drawn on their body half the time, inked along their forearms, because paper burns and skin remembers.

I did not tell him about the binding-stone beneath the palace. I didn't even know about it yet — that came later, that came with Oren's grey face and his terrible quiet voice saying *her blood opened it once* — but even without knowing, I felt the shape of the betrayal. I felt it settle into my chest like swallowed river water.

Corvus counted twenty crowns onto the table, one by one, letting each coin click against the wood like a small verdict.

"Pleasure doing business," he said. "Come find me if anything changes. It always changes."

I walked out into the rain and made it exactly one street before I stopped and was sick into a gutter, twenty crowns heavy in my pocket, each one weighing approximately as much as a soul.

* * *

Here's the arithmetic I did walking home, because I am a professional and professionals count:

Twenty crowns buys rope, medicine, willowbark, boots, dried meat for a month. Twenty more buys passage out of the city for four — five, counting me — on a ship that doesn't ask questions. Run now, tonight, before Corvus moves. Beat him to it. Take the money and the family and go somewhere the crown's long arms don't reach.

Except.

Except Oren couldn't run. Fading gods don't run; they flicker. Except Sable wouldn't run, not while whatever wore her father planned to nail itself to the throne forever at midwinter. Except Kestrel — Kestrel who'd carried a dying woman's letter across a mountain range for no reason except that someone asked, Kestrel who'd looked at a birth-record that turned their whole life into somebody else's plan and then *chose anyway* — Kestrel would walk into that coronation if I dragged them away, and they'd walk alone, and they'd die there.

And except, underneath all of it, small and shameful: they'd fed me. They'd let me sleep by the fire. Sable had shown me how to mend a sock and laughed at my terrible stitching and called it *character*. Kestrel had started teaching me map-reading, patient as rain, saying *you've got a good eye for edges, Wren, better than mine at that age*. Nobody had ever said I had a good anything. I'd spent my whole life being quick fingers and a fast exit, and these idiots had looked at me and seen an apprentice. A person.

Corvus had seen it too. That was the worst part. He'd known exactly what to buy, because he knew the price of belonging, having presumably sold his own years ago.

By the time I reached Candlemaker's Lane I'd settled it. Confess. Tonight. Before Corvus moved, before the information curdled into blood. Give them the chance to run, or to change the plan, or to throw me out into the rain where I belonged. Any of those was better than sitting by the fire warm and bought.

What I hadn't counted on was how hard it is to confess to people who trust you. Every hour I delayed, they handed me another reason. Kestrel passed me the bread first. Sable asked my opinion — my *opinion* — on whether the eastern stair or the cistern route was safer for getting into the palace, and actually listened to the answer. Oren, who barely spoke anymore, looked up when I came in and said, "You're shivering," and moved aside so I could have the warm spot near the hearth, and a god gave up warmth for me, a god who had maybe a spoonful of divinity left in his whole fading body.

I almost didn't do it. I want to be honest about that. I sat there through supper with twenty crowns burning a hole in my jacket and thought, *they'll never know, you can fix this, you can warn them without confessing, just say you overheard soldiers talking—*

But that was Corvus's voice. That was the smooth arithmetic of a man who sells pieces of himself until nothing's left but the hands. And I decided, sitting there chewing bread that tasted like ash, that I would rather be thrown out honest than kept bought.

So when the fire burned low and Kestrel rolled up their maps and Sable banked the coals, I said, "I did something bad," and my voice came out smaller than I'd planned, and every head in the room turned.

* * *

I told it all. Standing up, because sitting felt like hiding. The Blue Door, the tea, the twenty crowns — I dumped them out on the table between us, and they rang against the wood the same way they'd rung in the tavern, small verdicts, and I watched Kestrel look at them and then look at me, and their face did something complicated that I couldn't read, which terrified me worse than anger would have.

"I told them the safehouse was temporary," I said. "I told them Oren was nearly spent. I told them Kestrel keeps the routes inked on their—" My voice cracked and I hated it and kept going anyway, because stopping was the coward's door and I'd already walked through enough doors this week. "They'll come. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe tonight. I don't know how fast he moves, but he knows, and it's my fault, and you should throw me out, and I'd understand, and—"

"Wren." Sable's voice. Quiet. Princess-quiet, the kind that had commanded rooms full of kneeling courtiers. "Breathe."

"I sold you," I said. "I sold all of you. For money. For *food money*, like some—"

"For supplies," Kestrel said.

I blinked. "What?"

Kestrel picked up one of the crowns. Turned it over. Set it back down. "You sold us for supplies. Rope and medicine and boots. Which is what I sent you out to get, if you remember, because we needed them and none of the rest of us can pass for a street kid." They rubbed their eyes. "You went about it in the single worst way available to you, and I'd like to have strong feelings about that, but honestly? Half the smugglers I've mapped for would've done the same. The other half would've taken forty crowns."

"That's not—" My throat closed. "That's not the point. The point is I did it and then I sat here and ate your bread and didn't say anything for six hours."

"Yes," Sable said. "That part was bad."

"It was really bad," I said.

"It was," she agreed, and there was something in her face that wasn't forgiveness yet, wasn't quite, but was the ground forgiveness grows in. "And you told us anyway. When you didn't have to. When lying would've been easier and safer and warmer." She looked around the room, at Kestrel, at Oren. "I grew up in a palace full of people who never confessed to anything. They just arranged for the truth to die quietly somewhere. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

I didn't. Not yet. My brain was still stuck on *you should throw me out* and the fact that nobody was throwing me anywhere.

"She's telling you the confession counts," Oren said.

Everyone looked at him, because Oren spoke maybe ten words a day and we'd all learned to treat them like coins dropped in a well — listen for the splash, guess at the depth.

"A threshold is a choice," he said. His voice was thin as old paper, but it filled the room the way it always did, like the room had been built around it. "Every door. In or out. You chose in. Twice, tonight — once badly, once well. The second one is the one I keep." He closed his eyes again, spent, and that was the end of the sermon, and it was somehow the most anyone had ever said to me about myself in my entire life.

Kestrel swept the coins back into a pile and pushed them across the table to me.

"Take it," they said. "Buy the rope, the medicine, the willowbark. Buy yourself a hot meal you didn't steal. And then—" they tapped the table, "—you're going to help me spend it twice as carefully, because we move tonight, and this time the spymaster's boy follows *you*, and you lead him somewhere that isn't anywhere near us. Understood?"

"That's it?" I said. "No — I don't know — trial? Banishment? A dramatic vow?"

"Oh, there's a dramatic vow," Sable said, and for the first time all night she smiled, tired and sharp. "You swear that next time you're scared and hungry and someone offers you tea, you come home and tell us *before* you drink it. That's the whole oath. It's very binding. We're very formal people."

I laughed. It came out wet and ugly, halfway to crying, and I didn't fight it, because fighting it seemed suddenly like the dishonest thing. "You're all insane," I managed. "You know that, right? A princess, a god, and a mapmaker, and your security plan is *trust the thief*."

"Our security plan," Kestrel said, rolling their maps with ink-stained fingers, "is that we're four people nobody expects to matter, moving through a city that thinks it already knows how the story ends." They slung their pack over one shoulder and looked at me, and their eyes held that thing I'd been seeing in them for days now — the look of someone who'd found out they were made for a purpose and had decided, calmly and completely, to refuse it. "Get your coat, Wren. We've got a binding-stone to find under the palace, and I'm told my blood opens it, which means we need to be very sure we're the ones holding the key when it turns."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Somewhere across the city, bells were ringing the late watch, and under the bells — faint, wrong, like a note played on a string that shouldn't exist — I heard something else. A hum. Deep and patient, rising up through the cobblestones, through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my feet.

Oren stopped in the doorway — of course Oren stopped in the doorway, Oren was constitutionally incapable of passing one — and went very pale, and very still.

"It knows we're coming," he said. "It's started singing."
