## 16. Betrayal at the Stone

I have spent my life learning to read ground. Contour lines, scree fields, the way water carves a valley when it thinks no one is watching. I thought I knew what danger looked like on a landscape: a narrow defile, a blind bend, a ridge line that offered no cover.

I was wrong. Danger, it turns out, looks like exactly the thing you came for.

The binding-stone sat beneath the palace the way a heart sits beneath ribs — hidden, essential, and utterly taken for granted by everything living around it. Oren had led us down through a smuggler's tunnel older than the capital itself, a passage so old it predated the smugglers who used it, predating perhaps the concept of smuggling. Wren went first with her stolen lantern, moving through the dark like she'd been born in it, which I suspect she nearly had. Sable followed with her hood up and her jaw set in the particular way I was beginning to understand meant *I am about to do something my father — the real one — would have forbidden.*

And I carried the vial of my own blood like it was a lit fuse.

Because that's what it was. That's what I was. The blood of a god-touched vessel, drawn that morning from a cut on my palm that had already stopped bleeding before I finished wrapping it. My body, apparently, healed fast. One of many small gifts I'd never asked for and never noticed until someone told me they were signs.

"Here," Oren said, and his voice did something strange in the stone chamber — echoed once, then again, then a third time, each echo fainter and *older*, as though the room remembered voices from before ours. "Mind where you step. The floor remembers."

"The floor *remembers*," Sable repeated flatly.

"All thresholds remember. This one has been holding a wound shut for six generations." He gestured, and my lantern-light caught it: a pillar of grey stone rising from the chamber floor, taller than two men, carved over every surface with script that hurt to look at directly — not because it was bright but because my eyes kept trying to read it as something else. A map, maybe. A contract. Both.

The stone was cracked. Not broken — *cracked*, a single seam running from crown to base, thin as a hair, weeping a light that wasn't quite light. The fraying Oren had described. The hole Veyra had slipped through.

"That's where she came out," Wren whispered. She didn't sound scared. She sounded like a pickpocket appraising a lock. "That little crack?"

"That little crack," Oren said, "is all it took." He turned to me, and in the lantern light his face looked less like a man's and more like a doorway someone had hung curtains over. "Kestrel. Your blood opened this binding once — your grandmother's blood did, at any rate, and yours carries the same mark. It can close it. Or open it wider. The gods bound here can be freed, or the seal can be broken past mending. There is no third option at this stone. Do you understand? Whatever you choose here, you choose completely."

I understood. I understood more than I wanted to. I crouched at the base of the pillar, unstoppered the vial, and felt the stone *notice* me.

There's no other word for it. The hair on my arms rose. The crack in the pillar brightened. Somewhere far above us — through six floors of palace, through the Amber Court where a hollow king sat blinking his unblinking eyes — I felt something turn its attention downward, slow and vast, like a whale passing under a rowboat.

"Hurry," Sable said. She'd gone pale. "She knows we're here. I don't know how I know that, but I know it."

"She doesn't know yet," Oren said. But he moved closer to the stair, and I noticed for the first time how thin he'd gotten since the village. Since he'd told me what I was. Fading, he'd called himself. A god of thresholds with fewer thresholds left to tend, running low on whatever it was that made him more than a ragged man with good timing.

I pressed my bleeding palm to the stone.

The world *rang*.

Not sound — something underneath sound, a frequency that lived in my teeth and the marrow of my wrists. The script on the pillar began to move, rearranging itself, and I realized with a cartographer's horror that I could read it now, that it was reading *me*, comparing what I was against what I'd been made to be, checking a signature against a ledger six generations old.

*Match,* it seemed to say. *Match. Match.*

"Kestrel." Oren's voice, very quiet. "What do you see?"

"It's counting me," I said. "Like I'm — like I'm a key being tested in a lock—"

And that was when Wren said, from the top of the stair, in a voice gone small and terrible:

"There are torches coming down."

*

They came in perfect order, which should have been my first warning days ago. Crown guards don't move like that through tunnels — they move in clumps, cursing, bumping their pikes on low ceilings. These came down the stair in files of four, silent, torches spaced at even intervals, as if the darkness had been scheduled.

We were trapped in a round chamber with one stair, one god, one princess, one thief, and one bleeding cartographer kneeling at the most important object in the realm.

"How many?" Sable demanded, already pulling her hood back because there was no point hiding anymore.

"Thirty," Wren said. "Maybe forty. And there's—" She swallowed. "There's a man behind them. Fine coat. Rings."

The spymaster. Master Corvane, Wren had named him, the night she'd come to our safehouse shaking and unable to look at any of us, confessing what she'd sold and for how little. We'd forgiven her. I'd told her — meant it — that family absorbs; it doesn't expel.

I thought about that forgiveness now, kneeling in my own blood, and found to my surprise that I didn't regret it. Even now. Especially now. Whatever happened in the next hundred heartbeats, I refused to let it make a liar of the only warm thing we'd built.

"Oren," Sable said. "Tell me you have a way out."

"I have a way through," Oren said. "It isn't the same thing."

He stepped between us and the stair, and he straightened, and for a moment I saw him as he must have been before the exile: not tall, exactly, but *present*, the way a doorway is present, the way an open gate changes everything on either side of it. The torchlight bent toward him. So did the darkness.

"Kestrel," he said, without turning around. "Take your hand off the stone."

"But the binding—"

"Will still be there tomorrow. You will not. Take your hand off the stone and take them to the far wall. There's a door there."

"There's no door there. I've been looking at that wall since we arrived, it's solid—"

"Look again," Oren said gently. "You're a maker of maps, Kestrel. Make one."

So I did. I snatched the charcoal stub from my pocket and drew on the stone wall — freehand, fast, a door. An arch, a lintel, hinges, a handle. My hand shook. My blood-smeared fingers left red prints on the drawing, and the wall drank them in like rain on drought-cracked earth.

The door swung inward onto blackness that smelled of rain and pine and somewhere very far away, the sea.

"Go," Oren said. "All of you. Now."

"You're coming with us," Sable said. It wasn't a question, which was brave of her, because it obviously was one.

"I'm the hinge, Highness. Hinges stay." He rolled his shoulders, and light came off him — actual light, gold-white, pouring out of his skin like heat off a forge, and I understood with a lurch that he was spending himself. Burning down his reserves, the last hoarded divinity of a disgraced god, the way a man burns his own house for warmth in a killing winter. "Wren. Take Kestrel's arm. Sable — go first, keep hold of the frame, doors are honest if you hold them properly."

"Oren—" I started.

"Don't thank me yet," he said. "Thank me when you've redrawn the world. That's an order from a god, so mind you follow it."

The first guards reached the bottom of the stair.

What happened next, I saw mostly sideways, dragging Wren toward the door while she fought me — fought *me*, small fierce furious Wren, screaming that we weren't leaving him — and so I only caught pieces. Oren raising one hand and the stairway *folding*, the steps crumpling like paper, forty guards suddenly climbing a slope that used to be stairs. Oren turning, spreading his arms, and every threshold in the chamber waking at once — the arch of the stair, the seams between stones, the crack in the binding-pillar itself — all of them flaring open like eyes.

He was laughing. I'll remember that. He was laughing like a man at a festival, spending the last of his fortune on the final round, and the light was pouring off him in sheets now, and the guards were shouting and shielding their faces, and the spymaster's fine voice cut through it all, calm as a clerk:

"Take the god alive. The vessel too. Kill the others if they struggle."

*The vessel.* Not Kestrel. Not even *the cartographer*. I heard my name for what it had always been in official documents: a function.

Sable hauled Wren through the door. I got one foot over the threshold, and I looked back — because of course I did, I'm a mapper, looking back is the whole profession — and I saw Oren turn his head and find me in the chaos with absolute precision, the way he always did, the way a doorway knows exactly who means to pass through it.

"Draw well," he said.

Then he closed his fists, and every threshold in the chamber slammed shut except mine, and the last thing I saw before the door swallowed us was the spymaster's men swarming over a god who had chosen to stop burning — chosen it, folded his light away, let them take him — because a captured god might bargain, but a dead one couldn't, and he was buying us the one coin we still had: time.

*

The other side of the door was a hillside above a river, night air, pine trees, rain just starting. Sable had kept hold of the frame the way he'd told her, and it saved her life, because the door began to die the moment we were through — the charcoal lines fading, the wood going grey, then translucent, then nothing, until we stood in front of empty hillside and Wren was pounding her fists against bare rock and screaming words I won't repeat and some I will.

"He let them take him! He *let* them—"

"Wren." Sable caught her by the shoulders. "Wren. Look at me. He bought us the rite. Midwinter. We have weeks, and he made sure we'd live to use them."

"Weeks to do *what*?" Wren's voice cracked clean through. "Without him we don't know the ways anymore! Without him we can't even get back *in*!"

I knelt down in the wet grass. My palm had stopped bleeding again, fast as ever, the vessel's convenient little gift. I pressed it flat against the earth and felt nothing — no hum, no attention, no gods. Just dirt. Just an ordinary hillside in an ordinary country that believed its king was alive.

"We get back in," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "We get back in, and we finish what he started, and then we get him out. All three things. In that order or none."

Sable looked at me across the rain, this woman I'd known a matter of weeks, whose father wore a crown and a stranger's will, and something passed between us that needed no threshold-god to carry it.

"And how," she asked quietly, "do we get into the most guarded palace in the realm, three days after they took our god?"

I looked at my own hand. At the palm that healed too fast, the blood that matched a six-generation-old lock, the body built by dead gods for purposes I hadn't agreed to.

"They think I'm a vessel," I said. "Something to be collected. Something that gets *carried* through gates." I stood, and the rain ran cold down my neck, and I felt the plan assemble itself in me the way a map assembles — line by line, wrong turns corrected, until the route appears where there was only blank paper. "Let them collect me. Let them carry me right through the front door."

Wren stopped crying. Sable went very still.

"Kestrel," she said slowly, "that is either the bravest thing I've ever heard or the stupidest."

"With my family," I said, "it's traditionally both."

Far away, back toward the capital, lightning lit the clouds from within — or something that wasn't lightning, a gold-white flicker, brief as a closing eye. A god's last light, maybe, banked but not extinguished, held somewhere beneath the palace in the dark beside a cracked and waiting stone.

They had taken Oren. They had taken our way in, our teacher, our hinge.

But they had also, without knowing it, taken the bait — because the vessel they'd captured a god to protect was already walking toward them, and I had finally learned to draw my own doors.
