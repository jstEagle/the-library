## 18. Storming the Coronation

### Kestrel

Midwinter morning arrived the way bad news does: early, gray, and certain of itself.

I stood in the bell-tower shadow across from the Amber Court with a cup of vendor tea going cold in my hands, watching pilgrims funnel toward the cathedral doors like water finding a drain. Snow fell in fat, indifferent flakes. Somewhere under that roof, on an altar older than the dynasty that claimed it, sat a crown that ate kings.

"You're doing the face," Wren said beside me.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're pricing your own funeral."

"Accurate," I said. "I'm estimating costs."

She snorted, but her hands were busy at her belt, checking picks and wires for the fourth time, which told me she was scared too. Twelve years old and already fluent in fear's dialects. I'd have sent her away if I thought she'd go — if I thought any of us could afford to lose her particular genius for doors.

Oren had been taken two nights ago at the binding-stone, and his absence had a shape, like a missing tooth my tongue kept finding. What remained of his divinity had gone into getting us out, burning through him like a fuse. The last thing he'd said to me, half-dragged by guards into the dark, was: *The nave, cartographer. They'll walk her down the nave. Everything in that building is a threshold. Find me one I can use.*

Everything is a threshold. For a fading god of doorways, that wasn't poetry. It was a map key.

And maps were mine.

I'd spent three days redrawing the Amber Court from memory and Wren's stolen floor-plans, and what I'd found was this: cathedrals are built to move people. Processions are rivers, and rivers have banks, and every bank is a wall — except where the architects, being practical men, left gaps. Service corridors. Crypt stairs. The old processional way beneath the nave that hadn't been used since Aldric's own coronation, sealed behind a door everyone had forgotten because no map still printed it.

No official map.

I finished the tea, tipped the dregs into the snow, and felt the letter against my ribs where I'd carried it these many weeks — a dead king's handwriting, delivered at last to a living lie. Today I'd finish that errand or die of it.

"Wren," I said. "Doors."

She grinned, all teeth and terror. "Doors," she agreed.

* * *

### Sable

They dressed me in white, which was the first insult. White is a bride's color, or a mourner's, and they intended both — the realm wedded to Veyra's order, my father mourned without ever being buried.

"Arms out, Highness," said Mistress Corvel, and I obeyed, because obedience was the last weapon they'd left me and I had learned to sharpen it.

The seamstresses worked in silence. Through the fitting-room window I could see the cathedral square filling, the scaffolds hung with banners bearing the new sigil — not the stag of House Aldric but a compass rose with a single point, all directions collapsed into one. *One will,* Veyra liked to say. *One road. No one lost.* She said it the way a cage-maker describes a birdcage: as a kindness.

For weeks I had played the broken daughter. Wept where they could see. Asked childish questions about the rite so they'd explain it to me twice. And all the while I counted.

Guards on the nave doors: eight, changing at the hour bells. Priests of the Amberglass: thirty-one, none of them armed, all of them frightened — I'd watched their eyes when Veyra passed. The crown itself would be carried to the altar at the third bell by four bearers walking in step, because everything about Veyra was step, rhythm, order, and order can be tripped.

My father — the thing wearing my father — had come to me last night. It stood in my doorway wearing his body the way a thief wears a stolen coat, and it said, in his voice, "You seem calmer today, Sable. I'm glad. Today will be beautiful."

"I know what you are," I told it.

"Yes." No denial. That was almost a mercy. "And yet you'll stand where I put you, say what I've written, and be crowned heir beside me. Because the alternative is disorder, and disorder kills people, little princess. I have seen centuries of it."

It turned to go, and I said the thing I'd been saving, the knife I'd kept flat against my tongue for days:

"He mouths your name, you know. When you sleep. He doesn't sleep, exactly — but there's a place down inside him where he's still digging, and what comes out of his mouth is *daughter.* Not Veyra. Daughter."

The thing stopped. For one moment its shoulders did something I had never seen them do in five years of audiences: they sagged.

Then it straightened, and said, "Third bell," and was gone.

I let myself shake for exactly sixty seconds. Then I checked the seamstress's pins for looseness, because a princess who might need to run should always know which stitches will give.

### Wren

Here's what nobody tells you about palaces: they're full of holes.

Not metaphor holes. Actual holes. Dumbwaiters nobody uses, drainage chases from before the plumbing, priest-holes from the purges, and — best of all — the old processional tunnel under the nave, which some clerk had bricked up two hundred years ago and then, in the special way of clerks everywhere, forgotten to write down.

Except somebody had written it down. On a scrap of vellum in the palace archive, misfiled under *Bakery Accounts*, which is why you never trust a filing system, only a thief who's read the whole thing.

Getting in was the easy part. The kitchens on coronation day were a war zone of geese and shouting, and a small girl carrying a tray of candied things is furniture. Nobody looks at furniture. I walked past six guards, two priests, and a man who owed me money, and slipped through the door to the cellar stairs with my heart going like a woodpecker.

The bricked-up arch was where the vellum said. Old brick, soft mortar. I'd loosened three courses two nights ago, during the chaos after the ambush, when the whole guard was hunting Oren's trail instead of watching cellars. Now I pulled them free, quiet as teeth coming out, and cold air breathed through the gap — air that smelled of stone and candle smoke and something else underneath, something like lightning before it lands.

The ley-lines. Even I could feel it down here, and I wasn't anything special. The whole cathedral sat on a river of power, and today they meant to dam it with a crown.

I squeezed through. The tunnel beyond was tall enough to stoop in, running straight as a coffin toward the nave, and light came down through grates in the floor overhead — gold light, moving light, the light of ten thousand candles and a thousand robes.

Above me, faintly, the choir began.

I pressed my ear to a grate. Under the singing, footsteps in procession rhythm. Under the footsteps, a voice giving orders in a tone I knew from the spymaster's rooms — smooth, patient, absolutely certain.

Veyra. Walking my friend Kestrel's world like it was already hers.

"Not yet," I whispered, and got my picks out, and went looking for the next hole.

* * *

### Kestrel

The third bell rang as we came up out of the crypt stair into the ambulatory, and the sound went through my bones like a struck fence.

Wren had done the impossible quietly, the way she did everything — three locked doors between us and the gallery above the nave, each opened in under a minute, each left looking untouched. Now she peeled off toward the bell-ropes with a nod, off to do the last thing we'd planned: at the right moment, ring the changes wrong. Confuse the rhythm. Give Sable her opening.

Which left me alone in the shadows behind a pillar of amber glass, watching the procession form.

They came down the nave in perfect order. Priests first, censers swinging in metronome arcs. Then the bearers with the crown on a cushion of cloth-of-gold — and even at this distance I could see the thing was wrong, the way a drawing is wrong when the perspective lies. Light bent toward it. My cartographer's eye, the gift I'd spent my whole life pretending was just a knack, saw what it was actually seeing: the ley-lines of the city converging on that cushion like roads on a capital, all of them flowing toward a single point.

Toward Veyra.

And there she was, walking behind the crown in my king's body, robed in white and gold, and the congregation rose around her like wheat before weather. Her stride never varied. Her face — Aldric's face, hollowed now, polished — never blinked.

I looked for Sable and found her at the front, in white, hands folded, expression serene. Serene the way ice is serene. Our eyes didn't meet. They weren't supposed to.

The plan was simple, which meant it was doomed, which meant it was ours. Sable would stall the rite — a princess's prerogative to object, to demand the old words, to invoke precedents until the priests drowned in their own liturgy. Wren would break the bells' rhythm and open the crypt way. And I would walk down the nave in front of two thousand witnesses and deliver a dead man's letter, because you cannot stop a coronation, but perhaps you can interrupt a wedding.

The officiant raised his arms. "Let the realm be gathered—"

Sable stepped forward.

"Before the crowning," she said, and her voice carried the way trained voices carry, filling the vaults, "I claim the right of objection. Blood-right. Let it be heard."

A rustle went through the congregation like wind through grain. The officiant faltered — because the right was real, ancient, written into law by some long-dead king who'd wanted his heirs to have one honest weapon. Veyra's head turned, slow as a drawbridge.

"Sable," it said gently. "Not today."

"Today especially," said the princess, and produced, from her sleeve, a scroll I recognized — she'd stolen it from her own confinement, the old charter of the rite, and began reading its clauses aloud in a clear, relentless voice while the priests scrambled to check their copies and found, to their horror, that she was right about every word.

The rhythm broke. I felt it break — the procession's perfect meter stuttering, guards glancing at guards, two thousand people suddenly individuals again.

Now.

I stepped out of the shadow of the pillar and walked into the nave.

### Sable

I had read the charter for three hours a day for nine days, and I read it now as though my life depended on the punctuation, because it did. Clause by clause I tied the rite in knots — the requirement of witness by blood, the prohibition of crowning during an unresolved succession dispute (which I formally resolved to create), the archaic provision that any bearer of an undelivered royal message had right of approach to the throne.

That last one I'd found on the sixth day, and it had changed everything, because it meant Kestrel didn't have to fight their way down the nave.

They had a legal right to walk it.

I saw them come — hood back, hands empty and visible, the letter held high — and the congregation parted around them the way crowds part around madness, uncertain whether to be outraged or merely astonished. A guard moved to intercept. I lifted my voice over the charter: *"By the Provision of Approach, unobstructed passage is guaranteed to the bearer!"* And the guard hesitated, because soldiers fear lawyers more than knives, and in that hesitation Kestrel walked past him.

Ten rows away. Five. Close enough now that I could see the frost still melting in their hair, close enough to see their eyes flick to me once — *hold the line* — and to the figure on the throne.

To my father.

He was looking at Kestrel. But his mouth was moving, tiny movements, silent, and I had learned to read those lips across five years of audiences where he never blinked.

*Daughter.*

*Yes,* I mouthed back, and kept reading the charter, and my voice only broke a little.

* * *

### Wren

Up in the tower with the bells, I'd done my part — rung the changes crooked so the hour sounded wrong, so the whole ceremony below stumbled on its own count — and then I'd come down the rope-way faster than sense allowed, because I wanted to see it. Sue me.

Through the grate in the triforium gallery I watched Kestrel walk the nave, and my stomach was in my boots, because I knew what was coming. I'd sold information to the spymaster once, one time, one terrible time, and I knew what he'd sold onward: that the vessel was coming to the coronation. The guards at the altar weren't there for show.

Kestrel reached the steps. Held up the letter. Said something I couldn't hear — probably something dry, knowing them, something like *delivery for a dead man* —

And the altar guards drew steel.

* * *

### Kestrel

Four blades came out in the same heartbeat, and the congregation screamed, and somewhere above us the bells rang wrong-wrong-wrong, and I understood with terrible clarity that Veyra had known. Had let me come. Had wanted the vessel delivered to the altar, wrapped in my own cleverness.

"Kestrel Ishikawa," said the thing in the king's body, and the whole cathedral heard it, the voice rolling out warm and reasonable and utterly inhuman. "You brought me my key. How thoughtful."

The guards advanced. I backed up a step, two, the letter still in my hand because some stubborn part of me refused to drop a dead man's errand even now—

And the floor opened.

Not broke — *opened*, the stones swinging inward like a door deciding to be a door, and out of the dark beneath the nave came a man made mostly of scars and embers, ragged, half-gone, burning down to his last spark. Oren. Dragged here in chains as evidence, as bait — and spending whatever was left of himself on one act:

He put his hand flat on the threshold between me and the throne, and said, "This one is *mine*," and every door in the cathedral — every gate, every gate-shaped archway, every mind's idea of an entrance — slammed shut at once.

The guards froze mid-stride, caught in a doorway that had stopped being passable. The officiant's voice died. And in the ringing silence, Oren looked at me with eyes going out like coals, and smiled.

"Nave's yours, cartographer," he said. "Walk it."

Behind me, Sable's voice rose, reading on. Above, Wren hammered the bells into chaos. And ahead, at the top of the steps, the crown waited on its cushion, and the thing wearing my king rose from the throne to meet me.

I took the first step up.
