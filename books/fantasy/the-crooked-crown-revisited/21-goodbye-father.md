## 21. Goodbye, Father

The crown came off his head at the hour of the wolf, and my father came back with it.

I want to say I knew him instantly. That some daughter's instinct cut through everything — through Veyra's borrowed posture, through the years of audiences where he sat like a statue that had learned to breathe badly, through the cold-stone smell that had replaced the smell of cedar oil and ink that had been *him* since I was small enough to sit on his desk and ruin his charts. I want to say love is a bloodhound.

It wasn't. What told me was smaller than that. He blinked.

He blinked twice, three times, fast, like a man surfacing from deep water who has forgotten what air is for, and then he looked at me and said, in a voice rusted from disuse: "Sable. You've grown."

I had grown eleven years ago. I said so. My voice cracked on the second word, which rather ruined the effect.

* * *

They moved him to the queen mother's old apartments — mine, technically, now, though I'd slept there perhaps a dozen nights in my life before the house arrest, before all of this. The physicians wanted him in the infirmary. Kestrel overruled them with the particular authority of someone who has recently redrawn the borders of reality and found the experience clarifying. "He's not sick," they said, in that flat cartographer's way of theirs, as if reading off an elevation chart. "What was holding him up is gone. There's nothing to treat. There's just... time." They didn't say how much. They didn't have to. The physicians' faces said it for them, in the careful language of men who have delivered this particular verdict so often they can do it with their eyebrows.

Hours. We had hours.

I dismissed everyone. Then I sat down on the edge of his bed and discovered I had absolutely no idea what to say to my father, which was absurd, because I had spent six years rehearsing exactly this conversation in the dark of my confinement, composing speeches to the man inside the hollow king, arguments and accusations and confessions, whole epistolary novels addressed to a prisoner whose cell was his own body. Six years of material. And now the prisoner was sitting up in bed looking at me with eyes that were finally, finally *his* — brown, tired, creased at the corners in a way Veyra's perfect mask had never permitted — and every word I'd ever rehearsed evaporated.

"You're staring," he said.

"You're blinking," I said. "It's very distracting. I'd forgotten you did that."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, the ghost of the dry look he used to give my mother when she caught him sneaking honey into his tea against the physician's orders. "Six years," he said. "Is it six? It felt like being buried under a lake. Long enough that I stopped counting. Long enough that I—" He stopped. His hands, lying on the coverlet, curled slowly into fists, uncurled again. "I could hear you. Sometimes. When she let the walls thin. You talked to me. In the audience hall, when you thought no one was listening."

My face went hot. Because yes. Yes, I had. Every audience, standing at my appointed place below the dais, I had murmured commentary under my breath like the world's most treasonous theater critic. *Father would never say that. Father hated the Amber Guild. Father, whoever you are, you blink wrong.* I had assumed it was madness. A coping mechanism. The princess talking to an empty chair because the alternative was admitting the chair was occupied by something else.

"You heard that," I said. "All of it?"

"The part about my blinking was particularly cutting." And there it was — the smile, the real one, crooked on the left side where a childhood fall had left a scar through his lip. Veyra had worn his face for six years and never once reproduced that scar properly. I'd noticed. I'd told myself I was imagining it. Gods, the things we tell ourselves to stay sane in a mad house.

"I had a lot of time to workshop material," I said, and then the tears arrived without any warning whatsoever, which was humiliating, and he reached out and took my hand, and his grip was weak — so much weaker than I remembered, his hands had been able to crack walnuts, he'd done it at dinner once to make me laugh — but it was warm, and it was his, and I held on like the bed might tip us both into the lake he'd been drowning under.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry I couldn't fight her. I'm sorry you grew up in a house with a stranger wearing my face. I'm sorry about your mother—"

"Don't." I wiped my face with my free hand, gracelessly. "Don't spend your hours apologizing. I have a list, Father. A very long list. Things we need to discuss. The succession, and the guilds, and the eastern levees, and whether you actually meant to disinherit me in year three or whether that was her, and the matter of Master Corvin's pension, which you promised and then mysteriously forgot—"

"Sable."

"—and I made a list, I literally wrote it down during the arrest, I had nothing else to do, it's forty-one items—"

"*Sable.*" His voice cracked like a whip, the old command voice, and then immediately softened, and I understood that he hadn't used it to silence me but because he was laughing, or trying to, and it hurt him. "Forty-one items. Of course there's a list. You are your mother's daughter entirely." He pulled a breath that whistled slightly. "We'll get through as many as we can. But first — first I need to say the thing the crown never let me say. The thing I've been trying to say for six years through a mouth that wasn't mine."

I waited. The candles guttered. Somewhere below us, the palace was still in chaos — guards who had served Veyra being gently disarmed, courtiers weeping or scheming depending on their temperaments, Kestrel somewhere drawing lines on the world while Wren followed them around stealing things, ostensibly for practice. The realm was learning to share its edges. My father had hours. All of it pressed at the windows like weather.

"I loved you," he said. "Every day. Even from under the lake. Especially from under the lake. She showed me what the kingdom would be if I surrendered completely — order, peace, no more famine years, no more border wars — and the only thing that kept me fighting, even when fighting was only remembering my own name, was you. Was the thought of you growing up ruled by something that had never been a child, never been afraid, never burned its hand on a stove or grieved anything. Perfection, Sable, is just the absence of love. Remember that when you wear the crown. The crooked parts of you are the parts that are *yours*."

Well. That was item forty-two, and it undid me completely.

* * *

We got through eleven items before dawn. Not the important ones — I skipped those deliberately, the succession and the levees, the statecraft, because the statecraft would keep and this would not — but the small ones, the human ones, which turned out to be the ones that mattered.

Item nineteen: yes, he had known about my climbing the orchard wall since I was nine. He'd paid the gardener to look the other way. "You were so careful," he said, delighted. "You checked the wall for watchers every single time. You never checked the tree line." Item twenty-three: the dog. I had been told Bristle had run away. Bristle had bitten a visiting ambassador and Father had quietly rehomed him with a farmer in the vale rather than have him put down, and had lied to me about it for four years because I was seven and grieving and he couldn't bear it. I informed him that this was the worst thing anyone had ever done and also that I forgave him, and he laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and I held a cup of water to his lips and pretended my hands were steady.

Item thirty: my mother. This one I almost skipped. But he saw me hesitate over it, because he always could read me, even through a closed door, even through six years of stone.

"Ask it," he said quietly.

"Were you happy?" I asked. "After she died. Truly. Because I've wondered — I've wondered if losing her is what opened the door to her. To Veyra. If grief was the crack in the wall."

He was silent a long moment. Outside, the sky had begun its slow bruise toward gray; the shortest night of the year was spending itself.

"Grief is always a crack," he said finally. "That's what Oren's lot understand and we don't. Every loss is a doorway. She came in through mine, yes. But Sable — I would not board up that door if I could live the years again. Twelve years with your mother was worth whatever came through the broken place after. Love costs. That's not a flaw in the design. That *is* the design." He looked at our joined hands. "Kestrel understands that too, I think. Ask them sometime why they refused both the god and the crown. It wasn't courage. It was that they'd already chosen what they loved, and it was smaller and better than either."

"The map," I said.

"The map," he agreed. "And the people drawn on it."

* * *

Near the end, he asked me to open the window.

The physicians would have objected. Cold air, night damp, all their careful liturgy of the dying. But the dying man is the one who sets the terms, and besides, I had spent six years in rooms with sealed windows, and I understood viscerally why a man might want the world let back in.

So I opened the shutters, and the midwinter dark poured in, clean and enormous, full of stars and the smell of snow coming down off the Thornfells. Far to the east, past the city walls, there was a faint luminosity along the horizon that the maps no longer explained — the new country, Kestrel's country, the blank land filling up with old gods, glowing faintly like a page still wet with ink. My father looked at it a long time.

"It's beautiful," he said. "She would have hated it. Something she couldn't catalog." He smiled his crooked smile. "That's how you know it's good."

Then he said, "Tell me about you. Not the princess. Not the heir. I've had state reports read to me for six years — she liked hearing them, I think, the way a spider likes hearing about flies. Tell me the rest."

So I did. I told him about learning to pick the confinement locks and failing for two years until Wren taught me the trick of it. About the letter Kestrel carried, and how I'd cried over my own name written in his hand. About the safehouse meals, Wren's terrible card cheating, Oren teaching us threshold-songs that made doorways hum. About being afraid, genuinely afraid, in the nave on coronation day, and how Kestrel had walked toward the crown anyway, unarmed, carrying nothing but a rolled map, and how I had understood in that moment what kind of ruler I wanted to be — not the kind who holds, but the kind who draws.

He listened the way he had always listened, which was completely, as though I were the only weather in the world. And when I ran out of words, he said, "There. You see? You were never waiting to inherit a kingdom, Sable. You were already ruling. You just hadn't been given the paperwork."

"I'm going to be terrible at it," I said. "The paperwork especially."

"Terrible at it," he agreed warmly. "Thank the gods. Perfect kings are the ones who eat kingdoms. Be crooked. Be slow. Change your mind in public. Blink wrong, if you like." His eyes were closing. "Just keep loving things. It's the only law that matters. Everything else is — is footnotes—"

His breathing changed. I knew the change. Everyone knows the change, even if they've never heard it before; it lives in some older room of us than memory.

I climbed onto the bed beside him, the way I had when I was small and afraid of storms, and took his hand, and put my other palm flat on his chest, above the heart, feeling the slow labor of it. The window stayed open. Snow began to come down, fine and silent, catching the light of the far country on the horizon.

"Dawn's coming," I told him. "You always said dawn was the honest hour. No shadows left to hide in. You can go at dawn, Father. I'll be fine. I have a list. Forty remaining items. I'll work through them and report quarterly."

His fingers tightened on mine. Once. Twice. The old signal — *all's well, proceed* — that he'd used on campaign, that he'd used across banquet tables when some ambassador droned, that I suddenly realized he must have used, in the last six years, whenever Veyra's attention thinned and he could feel me near. *All's well. Proceed.*

"All's well," I whispered back. "Proceed."

And at dawn, gray-gold and snow-bright, with his daughter's hand in his and the window open to a world with new edges, King Aldric proceeded.

* * *

I did not cry at the end. I want that recorded somewhere, in case history gets ideas. I sat with him until the sun cleared the rooftops, and then I closed his eyes — gently, grateful, *grateful*, gods forgive me, that the blinking was finished — and I kissed his forehead, and I stood up, and I was dry-eyed and steady as a drawn line.

Then I opened the door, and Wren was sitting in the corridor outside, cross-legged, having clearly been there all night despite explicit orders, and she looked up at my face and burst into tears on my behalf, which was extremely convenient of her, and I held her while she sobbed, and sobbed a little into her hair myself, and after a while Oren appeared — gods, he filled a doorway differently now, restored, humming — and simply stood in it, keeping the threshold between the room where my father lay and the world where the rest of us had to go on living, and none of us crossed it for a long, kind while.

When I finally did step through, Kestrel was waiting at the end of the corridor with a roll of fresh vellum under one arm and snow melting on their shoulders.

"The council's assembled," they said. "They're asking for the heir."

"They can wait five minutes."

"They can," Kestrel agreed. "But there's something else. Word came from the east an hour ago. From the new country." They hesitated — Kestrel, who never hesitated over data. "Veyra sent a message. Through the ley-lines. One sentence." They handed me a slip of paper, and I read it, and the weight of the crown settled onto my unbowed head a full day before any rite would place it there.

*The borders hold,* it said. *For now. But I have drawn a question at your southern edge, little queen, and you should come see it before spring.*

I folded the paper. I straightened my shoulders. Somewhere behind me, in a quiet room, my father was beyond needing me, and somewhere ahead, at the edge of a map that was no longer finished, a god of order was waiting to see what kind of ruler the crooked parts had made.

"Get my cloak," I said. "And wake the cartographer. Apparently I have an item forty-two."
