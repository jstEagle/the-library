## 22. Edges Shared

The trouble with drawing a country out of nothing, I have learned, is that everyone has opinions about the coastline.

I sat on the high porch of the Mapwright's House in Amber Court — a title the building had acquired without anyone officially granting it, the way titles do — with my boots up on the rail and three separate delegations' worth of correspondence stacked beside me like siege works. The morning fog was burning off the new country's edge, which is to say it was burning off the place where the world politely agreed to stop being sure of itself.

Beyond that line of uncertainty lay Veyrath. Or the Godlands, if you were Sable's chancery clerks, who could not bear to name anything after a former antagonist no matter how reformed. Or the Wide Country, if you asked the gods themselves, who had turned out to be terrible at naming things, having never needed names before. Oren lobbied hard for *Orenland*. He was overruled by unanimous vote, including his own, once Wren explained to him how embarrassment worked.

On my drafting table inside lay the current master copy: two countries sharing an edge like sisters sharing a blanket, one drawn in ink and certainty, the other in a silver-gray wash that shifted when you weren't looking directly at it. The border itself was the interesting part. It wasn't a line. It was a negotiation — a band perhaps half a mile wide where the cartography got honest about its own limits, marked with a convention I'd invented and was quietly proud of: a dotted stroke I called the threshold rule, after a certain god who had insisted on the honor.

Somewhere below, Wren was teaching the apprentice cartographers to pick locks.

This was not part of the curriculum. It had simply become part of the curriculum, the way things do when Wren is involved.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last time I wrote anything in this journal I was running down a palace nave with divinity pouring off the walls like heat off a forge, and a person should record how they got from there to here, if only so future generations understand that history is mostly luck wearing a confident coat.

After Aldric died — after Sable held his hand through those last hours while dawn came up gray and forgiving over the capital — there had been a fortnight I still can't describe without my chest tightening. A kingdom with a dead king, a living queen-in-waiting, a crown with nothing inside it anymore, and a hole in the world where a god-country was supposed to go. The old gods had poured toward the blank space Kestrel Ishikawa had sketched into unmapped reality, yes, but blank space is not the same as finished space. Someone had to finish it. Guess who.

I spent that fortnight drawing until my fingers bled at the corners of the nails, which is a thing that happens to mapmakers who work too long and is exactly as charming as it sounds. Every river I placed, something older than rivers came along behind me and said *yes, but what if it also remembered being a glacier*, and I learned to draw water that had opinions. Every mountain range needed a past I hadn't witnessed, so I gave them one, and the past took root, and suddenly there were foothills where I'd only gestured. It turns out creation is less like painting and more like negotiating with a very large, very patient committee that genuinely wants you to succeed.

Veyra helped. That sentence still looks wrong to me, months later, but there it is.

She had kept her word — released Aldric the moment her dominion became real rather than borrowed — and then she had done something none of us expected. She had stayed at the border. Not as a warden. As a neighbor. She came to the first boundary conference wearing a form she'd built herself: tall, gray-eyed, dressed in the severe style of a woman who has run an empire and found it wanting. She opened negotiations by saying, "Your maps are sentimental and your borders are wishful thinking," and Sable, who had every reason to hate her, leaned forward and said, "Finally, someone reasonable," and I understood that those two were going to be either great friends or great disasters, possibly both.

They are currently both. It's working better than anyone predicted.

* * *

Sable was crowned at spring equinox, in a ceremony she personally rewrote to be half as long and twice as honest. The Crooked Crown itself — the actual object, the one Veyra had worn like a second skull — sits now in a glass case in the Amber Court's entry hall, and beneath it a plaque Wren may or may not have had a hand in: *HERE LIES AN ARGUMENT ABOUT POWER. THE ARGUMENT CONTINUES ELSEWHERE.*

Sable rules well, which surprised no one except Sable. She rules the way she escaped house arrest: thoroughly, angrily, and with an excellent memory for doors. Her father's ghost — the real kind, the grief-shaped kind, not the puppeting kind — walks with her, she told me once, but it walks beside her now instead of sitting on the throne. "He used to say a king's job is to know where the realm ends," she said, watching me ink the threshold rule for the hundredth time. "I think he meant it differently than he knew."

She comes to the Mapwright's House most weeks. Officially to review border accords. Unofficially to eat whatever Wren has stolen from the palace kitchens and argue with Veyra's envoys and sit in my draft room in silence for a while, doing paperwork, being family in the way people are family when nobody assigned it.

* * *

Oren got his doorways back.

Not all of them — you don't come back from burning most of your divinity in a prison break the way he did; even gods run on budgets — but enough. Enough that thresholds across both countries hum a little when he passes. He spends his days tending them: propping open the doors that should stay open, easing shut the ones that should close, standing in the liminal places where travelers hesitate and giving them, apparently, a small invisible push toward courage. He says the work suits him better than worship ever did. Worship, he told me over the terrible stew Wren makes, is a meal other people feed you. Tending is a meal you make yourself. Then he cried into the stew a little, and we pretended not to notice, because that's what family does.

His favorite project is the border itself. Every crossing point between the realm and the Wide Country is a doorway, technically, and he treats each one like a child. There's a ford on the Graywater where pilgrims from both sides leave offerings for each other's gods — bread for ours, ink for theirs, which tells you everything about the respective congregations — and Oren has taken to standing there at dusk just to watch the exchange. "Two countries," he said to me once, "that remember, every day, that the other one exists. Do you know how rare that is? Most borders are amnesia with fences."

I put that in the atlas preface. He doesn't know. He'll find out. He'll be insufferable.

* * *

Wren is apprenticed. Formally, this time, with papers and everything, to me — an arrangement the guild objected to until they met her, at which point they objected more quietly and then not at all, because Wren has a way of making objection feel like a bad investment.

She's fourteen now, or fifteen; she claims both depending on the situation. She draws a clean line, which is the whole secret of cartography and also of lockpicking, though she insists I stop saying that in front of the other apprentices. She has filled an entire notebook with what she calls *the honest margins* — notes on all the things maps leave out: smuggler paths, beggar routes, which cellars flood, which guards can be bribed with oranges. I've begun incorporating her marginalia into the official surveys. The atlas is better for it. So am I.

She still flinches sometimes at kindness offered too suddenly. We're all patient with that. Family absorbs; that's the job description. She taught us that word back in the safehouse, though she didn't know she was teaching it — she taught it the night she confessed to selling us to the spymaster and braced to be thrown out, and instead Sable handed her a bowl of soup and said, "You'll make restitution by being useful forever," and that was that. Contract sealed. No takebacks.

* * *

And me.

Kestrel Ishikawa, born in a village that burned, made as a vessel, marked before I could walk. For a while after the birth-record, I thought that origin was a verdict. That everything I loved — the roads, the blank spaces, the smell of fresh ink — had been installed in me like fittings in a ship built for someone else's voyage. That I was a door the gods had made and forgotten to hang.

Here's what I know now, sitting on this porch with the fog lifting off a country I drew out of nothing: origins are drafts. First ones. Nobody signs off on a first draft. What I am is what I did with it — the choosing, the refusing, the redrawing. I was made to hold a god. Instead I gave the gods a home and kept myself. That's not defiance anymore. It's just authorship.

I still map the Thornfell roads. Some habits are load-bearing. But the passes don't lie to me now; when a road appears that wasn't there yesterday, it's usually Oren testing a new threshold, and I send him a note telling him to file proper paperwork, and he sends back a note that is just a drawing of himself shrugging, and honestly, the correspondence alone has been worth everything this cost.

Yesterday a delegation arrived from the Wide Country's eastern reaches — gods I'd never named, whose territories I'd sketched in a single feverish night — requesting that their coastline be revised. They wanted more storm. Apparently I'd given them a temperate shore and they had ambitions. Their envoy was a tall figure made partly of weather, and she stood in my draft room dripping politely on the floorboards and said, "We have reviewed your work. It is generous. We would like to be more ourselves."

So I sat down and redrew their coast with bigger waves, and the moment the ink dried, somewhere far away, a sea learned to be wilder, and the envoy smiled with lightning and thanked me, and left footprints of rain that evaporated before the floor complained.

That's the job now. Not mapping what is. Negotiating what wants to become.

* * *

This evening Sable came by, unannounced, carrying a bottle of wine from her father's cellar and a document folder she kept trying to hide under her cloak, badly. Wren spotted it immediately, obviously, and said nothing, magnanimously, which for Wren is a form of sainthood.

"Read it later," Sable said, pressing the folder into my hands. "It's the new treaty language for the threshold rule. Veyra's people want the border widened. Ours wants it narrowed. Everyone's being diplomatic, which means everyone's lying beautifully." She poured the wine. "You'll find the compromise. You always find the middle of the map."

"The middle of the map is where the monsters live," I said.

"Yes," she said, and clinked her cup against mine. "We've established that's where we live too."

Wren fell asleep against Oren's shoulder around the second cup, mid-argument about whether the Graywater ford counted as one doorway or two. Oren let her sleep and kept arguing gently with himself on her behalf, taking both sides, losing gracefully. The fire settled. Out past the porch rail, the edge of the Wide Country shimmered its silver-gray shimmer, half a mile of honest uncertainty, holding.

I looked at my family — a queen, a god, a thief, all arranged around a dying fire in a house full of maps — and I felt the thing I feel now instead of the old unease. Not confidence, exactly. Confidence cracks, I learned that on a mountain pass that closed behind me like a wound. This is sturdier. This is the knowledge that whatever shifts tomorrow, whatever the landscape decides to lie about or tell the truth about, these edges are shared. Drawn together. Signed by everyone.

Tomorrow there will be letters. Delegations. A coastline that wants more storm, a border that wants widening, a god filing complaints about his own naming rights. Tomorrow the world will keep negotiating itself, and I get to hold the pen.

Tonight I'm going to finish this page, bank the fire, and stand for a moment in the doorway — Oren's favorite place, and lately mine — that threshold between the day that was and the one being drafted.

The door stays open. That's the rule now.

Everything else, we're still drawing.
