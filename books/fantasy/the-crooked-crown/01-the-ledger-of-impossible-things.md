## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The rain came down on Ashvale like a debt being collected, and Wendell Galloway sat in the back room of the Guild of Impossible Things doing what they did best: nothing that would be remembered.

The ledger lay open before them, its pages the color of old teeth. Each entry was written in ink mixed with a drop of the rememberer's own blood — guild law, older than the guild's current mistress, older probably than the building itself, which had a way of settling at night like an animal rearranging itself in sleep. *Item forty-one,* Wendell wrote, *one memory, minor: the taste of a specific apple, sold by a farmer's widow who could no longer bear to recall her orchard.* Below it: *Item forty-two, one memory, moderate: the face of a drowned brother, surrendered by a fisherman who wished to keep working the water.*

Routine work. Sad work. Necessary, if you believed the guild's charter, which Wendell did not, but believing was not a requirement for employment. Survival was. Wendell had learned that lesson young and paid for it in the only currency they'd ever had.

Outside, Ashvale huddled under the storm. The lamplighters had given up an hour ago, and the streets ran black and silver, gutters chuckling with runoff. The guild house stood at the crook of Candlemaker's Row where it bent toward the river, a narrow building three stories tall with a door that had once been painted red and was now the color of dried blood anyway, so no one had bothered to repaint it.

Wendell dipped the pen. Listened to the rain. Dipped the pen again.

That was when the knocking started.

Not at the front door — at the *tradesman's entrance*, the low door off the alley used by clients who didn't wish to be seen selling pieces of themselves. Three knocks, small ones, spaced like someone counting them out before making each.

Wendell set down the pen. The guild's night porter, a vast soft man named Odo who slept sitting up and claimed this constituted vigilance, snored gently in the corner. Wendell considered waking him, decided against it, and took the lamp instead.

The alley smelled of wet stone and rotting cabbage leaves. The lamp's flame bent sideways in the wind, throwing shadows that moved wrong, and for one irrational moment Wendell thought: *I know this. I have stood in an alley like this before, in the rain, waiting.* The thought arrived whole and unbidden, like a word remembered from a dream, and then it was gone, leaving only the shape of where it had been.

No one stood at the door.

But there was a child.

A girl, or a boy — it was hard to say, wrapped as she was in a blanket that might once have been fine wool, now gray with road dust. She couldn't have been more than six or seven. Her hair was dark and plastered to her forehead with rain. She did not shiver, which was the first wrong thing. Children shivered; it was what children were *for*, in a sense, small engines of complaint against the cold. This one stood perfectly still in the downpour and looked up at Wendell with eyes so pale a gray they seemed almost without color, like water over slate.

"Guild's closed," Wendell said. "Come back when there's daylight and something worth trading."

The child said nothing. Of course she said nothing. But she lifted one small hand — fingers blue with cold, nails bitten to nothing — and pressed it flat against the door, and then looked at Wendell with an expression of such terrible patience that Wendell felt the hair rise along their arms beneath their sleeves.

"You can't stand here."

She could, apparently. She intended to.

Wendell swore — quietly, because Mistress Corvane fined for profanity within earshot of the vaults, claiming words stuck to memories like burrs — and pulled the child inside.

* * *

The tradesman's hall was a long low room with a fire that never quite caught, a table scarred by two centuries of desperate elbows, and a smell of tallow and damp wool that no amount of rosemary had ever defeated. Wendell sat the child by the hearth, wrapped her in the good blanket from Odo's cot — Odo, still snoring, would not miss it until morning — and put a bowl of barley soup in her hands.

She held the bowl. She did not eat. She watched Wendell with those slate-water eyes, and the silence coming off her was not the shy silence of a frightened child. It was a *held* silence, the way a breath is held, the way a room holds still when something important is about to break.

"Eat," Wendell said. "Or don't. But you can't stay. Whoever left you will come back, and I'd rather not be holding stolen goods when they do."

At the word *stolen* something flickered across the child's face — not fear, not anger. Recognition. As if the word had been addressed to her in a language she understood all too well.

And then she spoke.

It was not Common. It was not the river-trade pidgin Wendell had picked up hauling crates on the docks during the famine years, nor the high-court Vellish that Mistress Corvane occasionally affected when she wanted to remind everyone she'd once been somebody. It was nothing Wendell had ever heard spoken aloud — and yet every syllable of it landed somewhere deep beneath Wendell's ribs with the weight of something *known*.

The sounds were old. That was the only way to describe it. They were round and heavy and deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall, vowels that opened wide as doors, consonants that fell like axes. The cadence rose and fell in long slow waves, and though the meaning was sealed to Wendell, the *shape* of it was not. The shape of it was a shape Wendell's body knew the way a body knows a staircase in the dark.

The bowl slipped from the child's hands. Wendell caught it without looking, which was fortunate, because Wendell was not looking. Wendell was standing very still in the middle of the room with cold walking up their spine, listening to a dead language come out of a living mouth, and thinking — with the particular horror of one's own mind turning traitor — *I have heard this before. I have heard this before and I know where, and I sold it.*

There was no reason to think that. There was no memory attached to the thought. That was precisely the problem. When Wendell reached for the recollection — a rainy alley? a dying man? a voice? — they found the smooth blank place where it should have been, the place Wendell knew better than their own heartbeat, the place every seller came to know: the socket where a memory had been drawn out clean, leaving not pain but *absence*, a tooth-shaped hole in the smile of the mind.

Everyone who sold to the guild had such places. Most people learned to walk around them the way you learn to walk around furniture in a dark house. Wendell had several. They had never, in eleven years of careful bookkeeping, had one *open up and speak to them*.

The child stopped talking. She looked at Wendell — at Wendell's face, specifically, at whatever was happening there — and tilted her head, and said one more phrase, shorter this time, softer, almost gentle.

And Wendell's hand, entirely without permission, rose to their own chest and pressed flat against it, exactly the way the child had pressed her hand to the door.

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the child, to the room, to the smooth blank place behind their ribs. "No, you don't."

* * *

Mistress Corvane was woken, as she insisted on being woken for anything above item fifty: badly, and with complaints. She came down the stairs in a quilted robe the color of a bruise, her iron-gray hair bound in a net, her eyes already sharp enough to draw blood.

"A child," she said.

"Left at the trade door. No note, no token, no payment."

"The guild does not take charity cases, Galloway."

"I'm aware."

"And yet you brought her inside, fed her, and woke me." Corvane circled the child slowly, the way she circled everything she intended to price. The child watched her go, unblinking, soup untouched. "Has she spoken?"

Wendell hesitated. Eleven years of instinct said: *say nothing, log nothing, notice nothing.* Instinct kept bookkeepers alive in this house. But the words were already climbing up through the hole in Wendell's memory, demanding to be accounted for.

"She spoke," Wendell said. "Not any tongue I know. Not any tongue I've *heard of*. And—" They stopped. Started again. "The cadence was familiar. From something I sold. Years ago. I couldn't tell you what."

Corvane went very still. In Wendell's experience, Corvane going still was considerably worse than Corvane shouting.

"Familiar," she repeated.

"Like hearing your own footsteps in an empty house."

Corvane crouched before the child, robes pooling, and studied her the way she studied ledgers — looking for the entry, the number, the price. "Child. What is your name?"

The child looked at her. Looked past her, at Wendell. And spoke again — that same rolling, stone-heavy speech, four words this time, five, her small voice steady as a bell that cannot be rung off its note.

"What did she say?" Corvane asked, without looking up.

"I don't know."

"Liar," Corvane said mildly. "You said the cadence was familiar. Something in you understands her, even if your head doesn't." She rose, joints cracking, and fixed Wendell with a look that had ended careers. "She goes in the east room tonight. Locked. Logged as *item pending*. And Galloway—"

"Mistress?"

"If she speaks again, you write down every sound she makes. Exactly as heard. If your sold memory wants to come home, we'll find out why." She turned toward the stairs, then paused. "And Galloway. Who leaves a child at a memory-broker's door in the dead of night, in the rain, with no payment and no note?"

"Someone who wanted her forgotten," Wendell said.

"Or someone who wanted her *remembered*," Corvane said, "by exactly the right person," and climbed away into the dark.

* * *

The east room was a cell with a bed, a brazier, and a window too narrow to pass a cat. Wendell carried the child up herself — she weighed almost nothing, another wrong thing — and set her down, and made to leave.

The small hand caught their sleeve.

The child looked up, and for the first time her stillness cracked, and underneath it was something so old and so weary that Wendell's breath caught. It was not the weariness of a child. It was the weariness of a thing that had been waiting a very long time to be found, and was afraid the finding had gone wrong.

She spoke again. Slowly this time. Deliberately. One phrase, over and over, touching her own chest, then Wendell's, then her own again, as if teaching, as if *reminding*.

And in the smooth blank place behind Wendell's ribs, something stirred and turned over, like a sleeper disturbed, and a single word surfaced into Wendell's mind without translation, without permission, glowing like a coal raked out of ash:

*Aldric.*

Wendell did not know what it meant. Wendell was certain they had known once, and certain they had sold it, and certain — with a certainty colder than the rain outside — that whoever had left this child at the door had known exactly whose hands they were leaving her in.

The child released their sleeve. Lay down. Closed her eyes, content, as though everything were now properly underway.

Wendell stood in the doorway a long moment, lamp trembling, heart hammering against the hollow where a memory used to live.

Then they went downstairs, took out a fresh page, and wrote, in ink mixed with their own blood:

*Item forty-three: one child, silent, speaking a dead tongue. Provenance unknown. Value: pending.*

Their hand shook on the final word. Outside, the rain went on falling on Ashvale, patient as a creditor, and somewhere in the vaults below, in the dark among ten thousand bottled years, something that belonged to Wendell was waiting to be asked for.
