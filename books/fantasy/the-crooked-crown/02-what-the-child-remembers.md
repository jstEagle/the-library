## 2. What the Child Remembers

Mistress Corvane arrived before dawn, as she always did when something in the guild had gone wrong enough to interest her.

Wendell heard her first — the tap of her cane on the flagstones of the entry hall, unhurried, deliberate, the rhythm of a woman who had never once needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around her instead. By the time Wendell reached the top of the stairs, she was already standing over the child, who had not moved from the spot where they'd been found. The blanket Wendell had draped over those small shoulders the night before lay pooled on the floor, rejected or simply forgotten.

"Bookkeeper." Corvane did not turn. "How long has it been speaking?"

"Since roughly the third bell, mistress. On and off."

"And no one understands a word of it."

"No one living," Wendell said carefully. "It's not any tongue in the archive. Not Old Casteric, not the river dialects, not High Vessarine. I checked."

"I know you checked. You checked at length, which is why I'm asking what you're *not* saying." Now she turned, and her eyes — pale as skimmed milk, and just as opaque — found Wendell's face and stayed there. Corvane had been a memory-thief herself once, before she'd risen high enough that other people did the stealing for her. She could read a face the way Wendell read a ledger. "You recognized something."

Wendell's mouth went dry. There was no point lying to Corvane; there never had been. Lying to her was like hiding coin from an appraiser. She would weigh you, find the false bottom, and charge you for the privilege.

"The cadence," Wendell said. "The stress patterns. I've heard them somewhere before. I don't remember where."

"You don't remember where," Corvane repeated, and something flickered behind the milk-pale eyes — not suspicion exactly, but the appetite that preceded it. "In your line of work, bookkeeper, *not remembering* is rarely an accident. It's usually a transaction." She looked back down at the child, who sat with their hands folded in their lap, watching the two adults with the terrible patience of the very young. "Containment, then. Room nine. Warded, windowless, comfortable enough that we're not monsters. Feed it, keep it warm, and write down every sound it makes. Every syllable, Galloway. If this is what I think it might be, the Guild of Impossible Things has just become the most important building in Ashvale — and I intend to know why before anyone else does."

She tapped her cane twice against the stone, a period at the end of a sentence, and climbed back up the stairs toward her offices, leaving Wendell alone with the child and the cold morning light leaking under the door.

The child looked up at Wendell and said something soft and liquid and old.

Wendell didn't understand it. But some part of them — some locked room deep in the cellar of their own head — leaned toward the sound like a plant toward sun.

* * *

Room nine had been built for containing things that were dangerous in ways the guild preferred not to name. The walls were lined with lead and salt-ash; the door bore three separate wards, each keyed to a different mistress's blood going back forty years. It was, Wendell had always thought, the closest thing the guild had to a conscience: a place where impossible things were kept from hurting anyone until someone decided what to do about them.

They brought the child bread and honeyed milk and a stool to sit on, and the child accepted all three with grave courtesy, the way a small ambassador might. Then Wendell sat across from them with a fresh ledger open on their knees and began, for the second time in one night, to do the only thing they knew how to do.

Record.

The child spoke in bursts. Sometimes a single word, held and turned over like a stone in the mouth. Sometimes long cascades, whole paragraphs poured out in that ringing, vowel-heavy language, the child's small hands moving as if shaping the sounds in the air. And Wendell wrote — phonetically at first, then, as patterns emerged, with growing speed and a growing sickness in the pit of their stomach.

Because the cadence wasn't just familiar. It was *specific*. The rising lilt at the end of declarative sentences. The way certain consonants softened when grief entered the voice. Wendell had catalogued ten thousand stolen memories in fifteen years at the guild — lovers' quarrels, dying breaths, the taste of a fruit no longer grown anywhere — and every one of them carried a cadence, a fingerprint of the self that made it. And this child's cadence matched, note for note, a fragment filed away in Wendell's own ledger.

Entry 4471. Provenance unknown. Purchased at auction from the estate of a dead man whose name Wendell had never learned. A fragment of speech in an unidentifiable language, catalogued as *curio, linguistic, value negligible.*

Value negligible. Wendell had written that themselves, years ago, with a steady hand and a bored heart.

They finished the morning's transcription, sealed the room, and walked back to their office on legs that felt borrowed. The ledger of stolen memories lived in the vault below, but Wendell kept a working copy of the index upstairs — titles, provenance, catalogue notes, nothing of the substance itself. They ran a finger down the entries until they found 4471, and then, because the index alone wouldn't quiet the screaming in their chest, they went down into the vault after all.

The vault required two keys and a spoken word. Wendell had both. That was the joke of the guild, the joke Wendell had stopped finding funny sometime around year six: the people who stole everyone else's pasts were trusted with the keys to all of them.

The memory came out of its case as a thin sliver of grey glass, cool as a dead man's finger. Memories always felt like that in storage — the warmth pressed out of them, the life paused rather than ended. Wendell had handled thousands. They had never once hesitated like this.

They pressed it to their temple, and the vault fell away.

* * *

A torchlit corridor. Stone worn smooth by centuries of feet. The smell of tallow smoke and, beneath it, something scorched — hair, cloth, flesh, the particular stink of a palace burning from the inside.

A man walking fast, robes hiked up, one hand pressed to his ribs where dark blood seeped between his fingers. He was old, white-bearded, his face half-collapsed with pain, but his eyes were clear — clearer than any dying man's should be. Behind him, through a shattered archway, Wendell saw a throne room, and on the throne a figure in gold, and the gold was *moving*, crawling, drinking —

And the mage — he must be a mage, the memory supplied the word with the calm certainty of witnessed fact — the mage stopped, turned, and spoke aloud in the ringing language, as if dictating to the air itself:

*"Hear me, whoever finds this. The crown is not a crown. It is a mouth. It ate him from the outside in — his name first, then his loves, then his face. What remains on that throne wears Aldric's body the way a coat hangs on a hook. The true king is scattered — broken into pieces of himself and hidden where the usurper would never think to look. In the tongues of children. In the mouths of the innocent, too young to know what they carry. Find the fragments. Gather them. And when the last piece speaks —"*

He coughed blood. Pressed on, faster now, stumbling.

*"— when the last piece speaks, the Unbinding can be wrought. But hear the cost, hear it and despair: the rite demands kindling. A speaker of the old tongue must give freely what the crown stole falsely. Memory for memory. Self for self. There is no smaller price. There is no other door."*

He reached a heavy door, shouldered it open onto night air and rain, and the memory shuddered, fraying at the edges the way dying things do. His last words were almost lost under the roar of fire behind him:

*"I have taught it to one who will carry it where I cannot go. Forgive me, Aldric. Forgive me for how little —"*

Grey glass. Cold vault. Wendell's own ragged breathing, loud in the silence.

* * *

They sat on the vault floor for a long time. The sliver of memory lay in their palm, spent, its surface clouded now — a memory viewed degraded, the guild called it, though Wendell knew better. A memory *given*, even secondhand, even accidentally. It never came back quite whole.

One who will carry it where I cannot go. A dying court mage, in a burning palace, teaching a forbidden language to someone in the last minutes of his life. Teaching it to whom? To a servant? A scribe? A child?

To someone who, years later, starving in the famine winters, had walked into the guild with everything they owned in a single bag and sold off their childhood by the pound to buy bread.

Wendell remembered selling it. That was the cruelty of the trade — you remembered the sale, the weight of the coins, the broker's sympathetic smile, even as the contents of your own head were carried away in labelled cases. You remembered losing what you could no longer remember having. They had sold the summer their mother taught them to swim. They had sold their father's laugh. They had sold a hundred small bright things, and the brokers had paid fair rates, and Wendell had eaten, and survived, and told themselves for fifteen years that what they'd lost was only the ordinary past that everyone loses anyway, just faster.

But somewhere in those transactions — Wendell was suddenly, horribly certain of it — had gone an afternoon with a dying man, a language older than kingdoms, and the only ritual in the world that could pull a true king out of the mouth of a false crown.

*I have taught it to one who will carry it where I cannot go.* And that one had carried it straight to the Guild of Impossible Things and traded it for a sack of flour and the illusion of safety.

Wendell got up off the vault floor. Their knees cracked. Their hands, when they looked at them, were perfectly steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside their skull.

Upstairs, the guild was waking. Clerks lighting lamps, apprentices hauling ledgers, the ordinary machinery of impossible commerce grinding into motion. Wendell walked through it unseen, as they always did, and stopped outside Mistress Corvane's office, where voices murmured behind the door — Corvane's, and another, lower, unfamiliar. A visitor. At dawn. Asking questions, from the few words that slipped through, about *the vessel* and *the old tongue* and whether the guild understood *what Her Majesty's agents would pay.*

Corvane would sell the child. Of course she would. She would sell the child, and the fragment of a king, and if she ever learned what Wendell had sitting in vault case eleven-shelf-four, she would sell that too, and smile while she priced it.

Wendell stood in the corridor with their heart hammering and understood, with total clarity, that the locked room in their own head had just been opened — and that everything they had sold to survive was about to come due.

They turned toward the stairs leading down to Room Nine, and began to walk faster.
