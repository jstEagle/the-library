## 3. The Price Already Paid

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Wendell finished the child's transcription, and still the words would not sit still on the page.

They sat instead in the back room of the guildhouse, where the ledgers lived — the true ones, the ones no clerk was meant to read after dark. Wendell had pulled the third volume from the shelf of sold things: *Memories Dispersed, Years of Famine*, and set it beside the child's paper. The lamplight made both look older than they were.

*The crown drinks what the head remembers.*

Wendell read the line again. It was in their own hand — younger, less careful, the letters leaning like a person walking against wind. They had written it eleven years ago, the night they'd catalogued a dying man's memories for storage. A court mage, the intake slip said. Name struck through. Cause of death: poison, suspected. Provenance: the palace, before the palace changed hands.

At the time it had been one more job. One more bundle of someone else's life pressed into glass and shelved in the dark.

Now the same phrase looked up at them from the child's babbling, transcribed phonetically in Wendell's own newer hand: *the crown drinks what the head remembers.* The old tongue's cadence wrapped around it like a vine around a fence post, but underneath, the meaning was the same. Word for word. Wendell had checked three times, then a fourth, because checking a fourth time was what you did when you were afraid to stop.

Somewhere in this ledger — somewhere in these pages of things Wendell had sold to buy bread — was the rest of it. The rite. The unbinding. The knowledge that could crack the Crooked Crown off a king's skull like ice off a branch.

And Wendell had carried it out of the palace in their own head, walked it through the famine gates, and traded it away for a winter's worth of flour.

* * *

The famine years smelled like this, Wendell remembered — or rather, like something just adjacent to remembering, the way you can feel a scar itch without recalling the wound. Ashvale in the fourth year of the blight. Bread lines that moved like funeral processions. Mothers selling hair, then teeth, then things that couldn't be regrown.

Wendell had been nineteen. Their mother had been dead two winters. Their father — a cooper, a good one, a man whose hands could coax a barrel into holding water without a single nail — had taken the cough that went around that year, and the cough had taken him back.

That left Wendell alone with a trade nobody wanted and a hunger that wouldn't negotiate.

The memory-thieves had come through the quarter in their grey coats, quiet as weather. Not thieves in the way the word suggested — nothing so dramatic as a knife and a whispered command. They bought. That was the obscenity of it. They stood in doorways with brass scales and velvet cases and offered coin for pieces of people, the way butchers might offer coin for pieces of cattle. First kiss. Dead father's voice. The smell of a childhood kitchen. Everyone knew the prices; everyone pretended not to.

A summer afternoon, whole and intact: four coppers.
Your mother's face, clear and moving: eight silver.
A full childhood, birth to twelve years: enough to live on until spring.

Wendell had stood in that doorway for a long time before going in. They remembered standing there. That was the cruelty of the thing — the guild never took the memory of the selling. You kept the transaction, crisp and bright, forever. You forgot everything except the moment you agreed to forget.

Inside, the broker had been kind. That was worse than cruelty. He'd explained gently how it worked: the extraction felt like a tide going out, slow and painless, and afterward there would be a smoothness where the used-to-be had been. He'd asked which years. Wendell had said all of them. Birth to twelve. Everything before the coughing started, because everything before the coughing started was the part worth money, and also — though Wendell hadn't let themselves think this clearly at the time — the part that hurt too much to keep.

Their father teaching them to raise a stave. Their mother humming off-key while she mended. The dog, whose name Wendell could no longer retrieve even now, only the shape of its absence. Snow. A river. A birthday with a honey cake. All of it, weighed on brass scales, stamped into a receipt, and gone.

Fourteen silver and thirty coppers. Enough flour to last until the thaw. Enough tallow. A coat from the pawnshop with someone else's initials in the collar.

Wendell had walked home lighter. That was the word for it, and they had hated the word even then. Lighter. As if the weight had been a burden and not a life.

* * *

In the present, the candle guttered, and Wendell realized they had been sitting motionless for the better part of an hour.

The third volume lay open to the intake record from the night the court mage died. Wendell had almost forgotten that night entirely — filed it under routine, the way you file a thousand routine things — but the ledger's margin held their own younger handwriting, a note to self, the kind clerks leave when a job feels strange:

*Subject spoke in fragments. Mixed tongues. Kept saying "the vessel will remember" — vessel? Asked him; he wept. Stored 41 units. Note: units 12–19 contain ritual material, language unknown. Flagged for the mistress. She sealed them. Sealed means sealed. Do not open.*

Do not open. Wendell had written that to their future self like a letter across a chasm, and their future self was reading it now by guttering light with a child's transcription spread beneath it, feeling the chasm close.

Forty-one units. Nineteen of them sealed behind the mistress's wax and iron. And among those nineteen, if the fragments matched the way Wendell's stomach insisted they matched, was the Unbinding Rite entire — every word of it, spoken by a dying man who had chosen a memory-thief as his confessor precisely because a thief knows how to carry what isn't theirs.

He had wept. Wendell remembered that much; it was in the margin, and something fainter beneath it, like a footprint in snow that hasn't quite melted. The mage had gripped their wrist. Had said — the note didn't say what, but the shape of the missing sentence ached — something urgent, something entrusted. *Keep it safe until the vessel comes.*

The vessel. The child at the door, speaking a language older than any kingdom, was the vessel. Had come. Was sleeping upstairs under guard, dreaming in a dead king's grammar.

And the key to all of it — the rite, the words, the whole architecture of Aldric's rescue — sat sealed in the guild vaults, in glass, in the dark, labeled in Wendell's own hand.

Sold. Not stolen from them. Sold. That distinction was doing its quiet work in Wendell's chest tonight, turning over and over like a stone in a stream. Because a theft you could rage against. A sale you had to live with. Somewhere in the famine winter, Wendell Galloway had held the salvation of a kingdom in their head alongside their mother's off-key humming, and had priced it all together, by weight, and spent it on flour.

* * *

There was one more thing in the ledger. Wendell turned the page and found the disbursement record, because the guild recorded everything, even grief, especially grief.

After the sale, the broker had asked a standard question, required by guild charter: *Any residual attachment to waive?* Meaning: any leftover feelings about the removed material that the client wished to have extracted as well, for an additional fee. Homesickness. Phantom longing. The ache where a memory used to be.

Wendell had waived it. Couldn't afford not to. Four coppers for the ache, and the ache was the worst part — worse than the loss itself, the brokers said. People who kept the ache stood outside bakeries crying at the smell of bread their mothers never baked. People who kept the ache jumped in rivers.

So Wendell had paid the four coppers, and the ache had gone into a little glass unit of its own, and been shelved, and forgotten.

Which meant that somewhere in the vaults, in the dark, next to the sealed rite, there was a small vial containing everything Wendell had ever felt about the childhood they no longer possessed. The love. The grief. The homesickness for a house that existed nowhere anymore except inside their own sold past.

They had sold the memory, and then sold the missing of it, and called themselves whole.

Tonight, holding the child's transcription against the ledger's margins, Wendell finally understood the shape of the trap they'd built around themselves without ever seeing it. To free the king, they would need the rite. To take the rite from the vaults, they would need to unseal what the mistress had sealed. And to use the rite — the child's fragments made it plain, the phrases repeating like a refrain — the rite demanded kindling. Memories, willingly surrendered, burned in the crown's own fire. The vessel was too small to burn; the vessel had to survive; the kindling had to come from elsewhere.

From somewhere. From someone.

Wendell closed the ledger. The candle died. In the dark, the arithmetic assembled itself with terrible patience, the way debts do.

The rite needed a fire of surrendered memory large enough to crack a crown forged to devour identity itself. There was exactly one hoard in the world of memories already surrendered, already paid for, already belonging to no one — forty-one units in the vaults, nineteen of them sealed, and every single one of them bearing the same name on the intake slip.

Galloway, W.

The dying mage's rite. The famine-child's past. The same shelf. The same hand on the intake slip. Eleven years ago, Wendell had sold the unbinding of a king to buy bread, and some cold, patient part of the world had been waiting ever since for them to come back and finish the transaction.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, the child murmured in its sleep — the old tongue, rising and falling, and one phrase recurring, the way a name recurs in a dream:

*The keeper returns. The keeper returns. Ask the keeper what he paid.*

Wendell sat alone in the dark, listening, and did not sleep.
