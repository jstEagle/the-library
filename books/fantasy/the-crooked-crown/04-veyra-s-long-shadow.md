## 4. Veyra's Long Shadow

Wendell did not sleep, and so was the first to hear the knocking.

It came an hour before dawn — not at the guild's front door, where the desperate came to sell themselves in pieces, but at the loading entrance on Cordwainer Lane, three slow blows with something heavy, then silence. Then three more. The rhythm of a man who had strength for exactly one knock at a time.

Wendell took down the ledger lamp and went, because it was their job, and because after a night of listening to a child murmur *the keeper returns* through the floorboards, any interruption felt like mercy.

The man in the doorway had been a soldier once. He was one still, in the way a burned house is still a house. His left arm hung wrong, bound in a field dressing gone brown and stiff, and his face was grey as old tallow. He wore no colors — he'd cut them off, by the look of the ragged stitching at his collar — but Wendell knew the cut of the coat anyway. Veyran regulars. The Ninth Marches.

"I'm looking," the man said, and stopped, and swallowed, "for the Guild of Impossible Things."

"You've found it."

"Good." He tried to step forward and discovered his legs had opinions of their own. Wendell caught him under the good arm, grunting at the weight, and together they got him over the threshold into the smell of ink and dust and old paper.

"Name?" Wendell asked, easing him down against the wall.

"Brannoc." A wet laugh. "Formerly Sergeant Brannoc, Ninth Marches, Veyran Army. Formerly." He said the word like he was spitting out a stone. "I've come to desert twice, apparently. Once from her army. Once from her story."

"What story?"

Brannoc looked up at them. His eyes were the worst part — not the wound, not the grey face, but the eyes, which had seen something and could not put it down.

"The story she tells her soldiers," he said. "That the war is for order. That the river cities begged for her banner." He shook his head slowly. "Galloway. Your name's on the door plate. I know your name."

"Everyone in Ashvale knows the guild. Not everyone knows the bookkeeper."

"Not from the door plate." Brannoc's hand crept inside his coat, and Wendell's own hand went to the iron pry-bar they kept by the loading door — but what the soldier drew out was paper. Folded, bloodstained, soft as cloth from handling. An intake slip. Guild letterhead. Guild seal.

Wendell took it and read it by lamplight, and the reading took four seconds, and those four seconds rearranged everything they understood about the last eleven years.

*Received into custody: one (1) vessel, minor, speaking the Old Tongue. Disposition: transfer to Casterhale vault pending valuation. Intake officer: Galloway, W.*

"That's not my slip," Wendell said. "I never—"

"No," Brannoc agreed. "But it has your name. And names are how she finds things." He leaned his head back against the shelving and closed his eyes. "She has a list, Galloway. Every name that ever touched this child. The midwife who delivered it. The ferryman who carried it across the Sorrel. The priest who hid it in a grain sack. All dead now, or running. And every clerk who ever logged it." He opened his eyes. "You're on the list. You've been on the list for eleven years. She just didn't need you until now."

Outside, the sky was going the color of a bruise. Somewhere above them, faintly, the child murmured in its sleep.

***

Brannoc had been at the taking of Marrowford, and before that at Sorrelbridge, and he told it the way soldiers tell things — flat, in order, without decoration, which made it worse.

"The river cities fell in nine days," he said. They were in the guild kitchen now, because Wendell had decided that if the man was going to die, he would not do it alone in a loading bay, and if he was going to live, he'd do it with broth in him. "Marrowford opened its gates when the Hollowed walked up to the walls. Just walked. No ladders, no rams. The garrison saw their faces and—" He turned his cup in his hands. "You've seen the Hollowed?"

"I know what they are."

"Then you know why Marrowford fell without a sword drawn. And Sorrelbridge fought, and it didn't matter. Three days. Then the queen-regent stood on the bridge itself and gave a speech about reunification, and her voice carried to the far bank without wind, and half the town knelt before she finished." He drank. "I knelt too. That's the part I can't get out. I knelt, and it felt like coming home, and I have hated myself every hour since I stopped feeling that way."

"Why did you stop?"

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — Mistress Corvane, awake early, or the child turning in its sleep.

"Because of the orders that came after," he said finally. "After Sorrelbridge. A circular, sealed with the privy signet, read aloud to every company. *All persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report a vessel of the Old Tongue will be attainted.* Not arrested. Attainted. Name struck, family struck, house pulled down, well salted." He laughed, short and terrible. "We weren't fighting cities anymore. We were hunting a word. A language. And every soldier who heard that circular understood, without anyone saying it, that the thing we hunted was small. Child-small. Because you don't salt wells for an army."

Wendell kept their hands very still around their cup. "Why does she want it? A child who speaks a dead language."

"Dead." Brannoc shook his head. "That's the other thing. That's the thing I deserted over, if you want the truth of it, more than the wells and the kneeling. In Marrowford, after the gates opened, they brought prisoners to the square for the oath. And there was an old woman among them, some schoolteacher, and when the Hollowed came for her she cursed them — in the old tongue. Full sentences. Fluent. And every Hollowed within earshot *stopped*. Stood still as posts. And then—" his voice caught, steadied, "—then they all turned their heads toward her at exactly the same moment, like weathervanes, and the queen-regent said, *there, you see, it is not dead,* and had the woman taken away. Not killed. Taken. And I understood that we hadn't conquered two cities. We'd been dragging rivers for something. Sifting. And the circular wasn't a warning to traitors. It was a net."

He set down the cup. His hands were shaking now, fine and constant, like plucked strings.

"She's not hunting the child to kill it, Galloway. She's hunting it to *use* it. Whatever the old tongue is — whatever it does to the Hollowed, whatever it did to make her stop needing to sleep, and she doesn't sleep, I've stood watch outside her tent, I know — the child is the last living piece of it. The last vessel. And she means to pour it into the Crown."

***

Mistress Corvane received the news standing, which Wendell had learned was how she received everything: fire, flood, tax inspectors, the end of the world. She stood at the window of her office with her hands folded behind her back, watching the grey light come up over Ashvale's rooftops, and let Brannoc talk himself empty.

When he finished, she was silent long enough that Wendell began composing the report in their head — *deserter, Ninth Marches, wounded, claims—*

"No," Corvane said.

"Mistress—"

"You're composing a report, Galloway. Don't. There won't be one." She turned. Her face was unreadable, which from Corvane meant it was fully readable to anyone who knew her: she was afraid. "If even half of what this man says is true, the guild cannot be seen to hold the child. Not an hour longer than necessary. And it cannot be seen *not* to hold it either, because Veyra's people will check our ledgers within the fortnight, and a missing entry is louder than a present one."

"So we falsify the ledger," Wendell said. It was, they reflected, the most natural sentence they had ever spoken.

"We falsify nothing. We *reclassify*." Corvane crossed to the shelf where the intake slips lived and drew one out blank. "The child becomes a debt. A minor obligation, transferred to a sister house. Which sister house?"

"Galloway," Brannoc said quietly, from his chair by the cold stove. "Ask your bookkeeper. The slip already bears the name. Names are how she finds things — but names are also how *we* move things. Give the child to the one name on her list who isn't dead yet, and every road she watches becomes a road that leads to you instead of to us."

Corvane looked at Wendell. Wendell looked back, and understood that this had been the shape of the conversation from the beginning; that Corvane had summoned them here not to decide but to witness the decision being handed over, so that it would be theirs.

"The shrine roads," Wendell said. Their voice sounded strange to them, level and far away. "Through the Ashvale fells. If the rite I sold eleven years ago is real — if the words in my ledger are real — then there's a place they're meant to be spoken. The First Tongue keeps shrines older than any kingdom. One of them stands in the mountains past Casterhale."

"And you would take the child there yourself." Not a question.

"With him." A nod at Brannoc. "He knows Veyra's march orders. Her patrol patterns. What the Hollowed can and can't do."

"They can do everything," Brannoc said. "They just do it slowly, unless she points them."

Corvane wrote a single line on the intake slip — *transferred, per standing arrangement* — and signed it, and slid it across the desk. "You understand what you're carrying, Galloway? Not the child. The *war*. If Aldric's fragment dies on a mountain road, or worse, reaches her hands, then everything after is just the length of time it takes the world to finish falling."

"I understand what I'm carrying," Wendell said. And underneath it, unspoken, the other thing they understood: *ask the keeper what he paid.* Eleven years ago they had paid bread-money for the ending of a king. Now the bill had come due, with interest, and the collector was marching up the river valley with a faceless army.

They left Corvane's office and went upstairs, where the child sat awake in the narrow bed, watching the door. When Wendell entered, it stopped murmuring. It looked at them with eyes too old for its face, and said — clearly, deliberately, in the old tongue, and Wendell found, with a chill like water down the spine, that they understood it without knowing how:

*"The roads are watched. Go while the lamps are still lit."*

Wendell turned to Brannoc in the doorway. "Pack light," they said. "We leave before the city wakes."

***

Far to the south, on the ruined bridge at Sorrelbridge, Queen-regent Veyra stood where she had stood when the city knelt, and listened.

She always listened. The Crown drank and drank and never told her anything, but the *tongue* — the scraps of it her scholars had flayed out of prisoners, the syllables wrung from dying mouths — the tongue whispered to her of keepers and ledgers and debts outstanding. It spoke of a name.

"Galloway," she said to the morning, tasting it.

Behind her, ranks of the Hollowed stood in the river wind, faces smooth as eggs, waiting to be pointed.

She pointed north.
