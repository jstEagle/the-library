## 5. The Road Out of Ashvale

The guildhouse had a back door that Wendell had never once used in eleven years of employment, and it was a measure of how badly the night had gone that they now stood before it with a child on one hip and a satchel of stolen memories against their spine.

The child weighed almost nothing. That was the worst part — the lightness, as though Veyra's hunters had already begun taking pieces and Wendell simply hadn't noticed which.

"Left at the wall," Brannoc murmured behind them. The deserter moved like a man who had spent his whole life apologizing for his own footsteps, which was, Wendell reflected, exactly the skill they needed tonight. "There's a gap where the flood took the stones two winters back. Dogs can't scent through the ash-chalk if you rub it on your boots."

"You've done this before."

"I've *been* this before." His voice was flat. "Running. It's most of what soldiers learn that matters."

Wendell eased the door open. Rain had come in from the coast an hour past, thin and needling, and it hissed against the cobbles of the alley like something whispering advice. Above the rooftops, the guildhouse bell tower stood black against the bruised sky. Somewhere inside it hung the Ledger of Impossible Things, and somewhere in that ledger was an entry Wendell had written three nights ago in a hand that shook: *One (1) child, provenance unknown, speaks the dead tongue. Contained by order of Mistress Corvane.*

Contained. As though the child were a jarred specimen. As though Wendell hadn't sat with them every night since, listening to syllables older than any kingdom spill out in the dark like water finding cracks in a floor.

The child had said a word tonight. Just one, in the common tongue, spoken into the silence after Wendell finished their supper ration and split it without thinking — half onto the child's tin plate, half onto their own. The word had been *again.* And Wendell had understood, with the cold clarity of a bookkeeper reading a final column, that Mistress Corvane would sell this child to Veyra within the week, or worse, hand them over to whatever passed for mercy in the Queen-regent's court.

So: theft. Wendell Galloway, who had stolen ten thousand memories and never once a living thing, was stealing a child out of their own guildhouse.

"Again," the child said now, softly, into Wendell's ear, and pointed down the alley toward the dark.

"Yes," Wendell whispered. "That way."

* * *

They went out through the flood-gap at first grey light, ash-chalk on their boots, the child wrapped in Brannoc's spare cloak so thoroughly that only two eyes showed. Ashvale's farmland began beyond the last tannery, and it was farmland no longer. It was a lesson written in charcoal.

Veyra's army had come through in high summer, and the fields still wore their burning. Stubble stood in rows like the teeth of something long dead. A windmill leaned against the sky at an angle that hurt to look at, its sails folded like broken wings. The road itself had been pulled up in places, the stones stacked into crude barricades and then knocked flat again by something heavy — siege engines going north, Brannoc said, toward Casterhale and the free cities beyond.

"They'll have taken Marrowford by now," he said as they walked, keeping his voice low even though there was nothing alive to hear it. "And Sennet's Ford. She moves fast when she smells something she wants."

"She wants the child."

"She wants *what's in* the child." Brannoc glanced at the small hooded shape between them. "You know what I heard, before I ran. In the officers' tents. They talk about the Hollowed like weather — how many she's made, how many she's spent. But they talk about the old tongue like it's scripture. Like the war can't be won without it."

"And why is that?"

Brannoc was quiet for three strides. Four. "Because her crown eats her," he said finally. "Because everything she is goes into it, and everything it takes from her goes into the legions, and there's less of her every year. She needs more to feed it. Something old. Something *whole.*"

The child stopped walking. Wendell felt the small hand close around two of their fingers — not pulling, just holding, the way a person holds a rope over a long drop.

"We rest at the burned barn," Wendell said. "Then we walk until dark."

* * *

The barn had been someone's pride once; now it was a ribcage of black beams open to the sky. They sheltered in the lee of its fallen door, and Wendell dug out the food they'd taken from the guild stores — hard cheese, a heel of bread gone stale but honest, dried apples in a twist of cloth. They fed the child first, then Brannoc, then themselves, in that order, and did not examine why.

The child ate with the terrible efficiency of someone who has known hunger, and then, done, looked up at Wendell and began to speak.

Not the common tongue. The old one — the cadence Wendell knew from a memory sold years ago, from fragments matched line by line against their own ledger in the small hours of too many nights. But this time Wendell listened differently. This time they listened like a bookkeeper, and the entries resolved:

*A king. A crown that drank him. Pieces scattered so the drinking would take longer. One piece hidden in a tongue nobody spoke anymore, put into a body too small to be worth hunting.*

The child finished and looked at Wendell with an expression no child should own — patient, ancient, apologetic.

"That's what you are," Wendell said quietly. "A piece of him. Of Aldric."

The child touched their own chest. Then, carefully, touched Wendell's.

"Don't," Wendell said, and their voice came out wrong. "Don't do that. I'm not part of any king. I'm a clerk. I file things. I steal things for people who pay, and I keep the change, and that's the whole of me."

Brannoc, cleaning his nails with a knife across the doorway, said nothing. But he looked at Wendell for a long moment with an expression Wendell chose not to catalogue.

They slept in shifts. When Wendell's turn came to watch, the child slept against their side with the boneless trust of the very young, and Wendell sat in the ash-smelling dark and thought about the vault beneath the guildhouse, and the shelf, and the small lead box marked with their own name in their own younger handwriting.

*GALLOWAY, W. — childhood, assorted. Sold under famine provisions.*

Somewhere in that box was a dying mage in a burned corridor, pressing a rite into a child's mind like a seal pressed into wax. Somewhere in that box was the answer to all of this, and Wendell had traded it for bread.

* * *

They saw the first Hollowed on the third day, and they knew it was Hollowed because it did not behave like a man.

It stood at the crest of a low hill perhaps half a mile off, at the edge of a field where the wheat had burned down to silver ghost-stalks, and it simply stood. Wind moved its cloak. Nothing else about it moved. From this distance its face was a pale oval, and Wendell's eyes, trained by years of cataloguing the impossible, supplied the detail they could not actually see: smoothness. No brows. No creases. A face like an egg before anything hatches from it.

"It's seen us," Brannoc breathed. He had gone very still, the knife already in his hand. "It doesn't need to follow close. It marks us and others come. We have maybe a day."

"Then we don't take a day." Wendell lifted the child onto their hip and turned off the road, west, toward the mountains that had been growing on the horizon like a slow tide. "The shrine's in the high passes. Nine days by the road. Less if we cut across the fells."

"Less if we die on the fells, you mean."

"Yes," Wendell said. "That's what less means."

Something happened to Brannoc's face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "All right, clerk," he said. "Lead on."

They walked. The land climbed by degrees, soft hills giving way to stony moor, the burned country falling away behind them like a bad memory being set down. The child rode on Wendell's hip and then, when the slope steepened, on Brannoc's shoulders, gripping his cropped hair with both fists and issuing directions in the old tongue that meant, as far as Wendell could parse, *faster* and *that rock is rude.*

By the second evening on the fells, Wendell caught themselves humming — some nonsense marching tune from nowhere, half-invented — and realized the child was humming it back, and that Brannoc was keeping the rhythm with his boot-heels, and that none of them had remarked on it, because remarking on it would have made it fragile, and fragile things on the fells broke.

That night, sheltered in the mouth of a shallow cave with a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, the child fell asleep between them, one small hand curled around Brannoc's strap and the other around Wendell's sleeve.

"Never had children," Brannoc said to the fire. "Never had much of anything. Marched where they pointed me and burned what they handed me the torch for." He turned his smoothed-something face — no, not smoothed, Wendell thought, *tired* — toward the sleeping child. "This is the first thing I've ever run *toward.*"

"Don't get attached," Wendell said, automatically, the guild maxim rising like bile. *Nothing in the ledger is yours. Everything in the ledger is inventory.*

"Too late," Brannoc said simply, and Wendell found they could not write the correction.

* * *

On the fifth morning, the Hollowed stopped pretending.

There were three of them now, spread across the valley below in a loose line, moving without hurry, without speech, without any signal Wendell could detect — three blank faces tilted upward like flowers tracking a sun that wasn't there. Behind them, far off on the plain, dust rose in a column. Not an army yet. The seed of one.

"They're herding us," Brannoc said. He'd carried the child since dawn, setting a pace that would have shamed a mountain goat. "West is blocked — there'll be more in the pass ahead. They want us tired, cornered, delivered breathing."

"The shrine's northeast. Through the scree fields."

"Nobody crosses the scree fields."

"Then we'll be the first entry in a new ledger." Wendell took the child from his shoulders and settled them against their chest, feeling the small heartbeat quick and stubborn against their own. Below, the nearest Hollowed had begun, at last, to walk. Straight up the slope. Directly at them. No wasted motion, no hesitation, a thing drawn on a string.

"Wendell," the child said.

Wendell froze. It was the second time the child had used their name in the common tongue, and it landed differently this time — heavier, deliberate, a key turning.

"What?"

The child raised one small arm and pointed northeast, at the scree, at the grey wilderness of sliding stone. Then they spoke six words of the old tongue, and Wendell — who had spent weeks matching those sounds against fragments in a stolen ledger, who had learned the grammar of a dead king piece by piece in the dark — understood them completely.

*The stones remember the way. Trust them.*

"Well," Wendell said, and their voice cracked and they let it. "We're trusting the rocks, then."

Behind them, unhurried as frost, the Hollowed came on.
