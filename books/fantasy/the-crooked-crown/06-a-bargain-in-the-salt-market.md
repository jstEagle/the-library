## 6. A Bargain in the Salt Market

Casterhale smelled of brine and burning sugar, and beneath both — faint as a lie told well — of memory. Wendell had always been able to smell it. Other people walked through the free city and noticed the fish stalls, the rope-makers, the gulls screaming over the harbor cranes. Wendell noticed the brokers' quarter: a low haze of something like ozone and old paper that clung to the streets where memories changed hands.

The child rode on Brannoc's shoulders, wrapped in a fishmonger's cloak two sizes too large, its face turned up at the awnings and banners with an expression Wendell refused to call wonder. Wonder was dangerous. Wonder got people followed.

"Keep your hood up," Wendell said for the fourth time.

The child looked down at them and said nothing, which was its habit, but its eyes said plenty. *You are afraid,* those grey eyes said. *Why are you afraid of a market?*

Because markets were where Wendell had done their worst arithmetic. Because this market in particular held a shop with a green door and a brass bell, and behind that door sat a broker named Ilex Vane who bought futures the way other men bought wine — by the cask, without asking too closely what was in it.

"You wait outside," Wendell told Brannoc. "Both of you."

"The Hollowed—"

"Won't walk into Casterhale in daylight. The free cities still have walls and the walls still have meaning." Mostly true. Enough true. "Watch the crowd. If anyone smooth-faced comes near the child, you run for the eastern gate and don't look back for me."

Brannoc's jaw worked. He'd argued less since the bridge at Marrow Ford — no, that hadn't happened yet; since the ford at Ashvale, then, where they'd waded hip-deep through ash-grey water while hunters combed the far bank. He was learning what Wendell already knew: that some orders were kindnesses wearing armor.

"And you?" he asked. "What do you run for?"

Wendell didn't answer. They pulled their collar up and went into the crowd alone.

* * *

The shop with the green door hadn't moved, though everything else about Casterhale seemed to have shifted in the years since Wendell last stood before it. The salt market had grown a second tier of walkways; the fishwives sang new songs; a temple to some river god had gone up where a tannery used to stink. But the green door stood exactly where memory — someone's memory, not Wendell's — insisted it should, and the brass bell above it gave the same thin, sweet note when Wendell pushed inside.

Ilex Vane had not aged so much as settled, like silt. He was broader than Wendell remembered, or perhaps Wendell had simply been smaller the last time they stood in this room, seventeen and hollow-eyed and holding a ledger full of other people's yesterdays, looking to sell one of their own. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves sat jars — hundreds of them, thousands — each stoppered with wax, each holding a curl of pale light that moved when you weren't watching it directly. Memories. Bottled, labeled, priced.

"Well," said Ilex, setting down his pen. "The Guild of Impossible Things sends me a bookkeeper after all these years. Or does the bookkeeper come on her own errand?"

*Their* own errand. Wendell let the misgendering pass; Ilex did it deliberately, the way a card sharp bends a corner of the deck. "On my own."

"I thought so. Guild business smells of ink and fear. This smells only of fear." He smiled without warmth. "Sit. You want a glimpse. They always do."

Wendell sat. The chair across from his desk was the same chair. That was somehow worse than if the room had changed entirely.

"I sold a memory here," Wendell said. "Famine year. I was—" They stopped. They didn't actually know how old they'd been. That was rather the point. "It was winter. I sold a bundle. Childhood, mostly. The broker who took it said it was good stock."

"Merrin Stave. Dead now. Her stock came to me in the estate sale." Ilex rose and drifted along the shelves, fingers trailing over labels. "Childhood bundles are common. Most people sell them young and spend the rest of their lives wondering why certain songs make them cry. Yours, though—" His finger stopped. "Yours I remember. Not because of what it was. Because of what was *in* it."

He brought down a jar the size of a fist. Inside, the pale light didn't drift lazily the way the others did. It *burned*, quick and restless, throwing shadows against the glass like a moth in a lantern.

"Do you know what most childhoods contain?" Ilex asked, turning the jar so the light caught his face. "Bread and scoldings. A dog, maybe. First snow. Perfectly ordinary stock, worth a few coins to the sentimental. But yours has a thread running through it that made Merrin whistle when she catalogued it. A dying man. A tower room. Words spoken in a language that predates the kingdoms." He set the jar on the desk between them, gently, the way one sets down a sleeping animal. "The Unbinding Rite, bookkeeper. You've been carrying it your whole life without knowing. And now you've come all this way to look at it. Shall I tell me why?"

Wendell's mouth was dry. "A child came to the guild. It speaks the old tongue."

"Ah." Ilex sat back down. For a long moment the only sound was the moth-light buzzing against its glass. "Then you know what it is. What it *is*." He tapped the jar once. "And you want to see what you sold. Not buy — you couldn't afford to buy, not yet, not from me — but see. A glimpse. Ten heartbeats of your own past, returned to your head long enough to be felt."

"What's the cost?"

"For a glimpse? Trivial." He waved a hand. "A taste. One small memory off the top of your present — last week, say, or the taste of yesterday's breakfast. You won't miss it. Nobody misses the small change." He slid the jar closer. "But understand what you're asking. Once you've seen it, you can't unsee it. Knowledge is a door that only locks from the outside, and you'll be standing on the wrong side."

Wendell thought of the child asleep against Brannoc's shoulder. Thought of the words it had murmured in its sleep — words Wendell had transcribed by candlelight and matched, line by trembling line, to fragments catalogued in the guild vaults under their own name. Thought of the hollow feeling they'd carried since childhood, the sense of a shape cut out of them, edges still sharp.

"Glimpse," Wendell said.

Ilex unstopped the jar. The light poured out like smoke with intent, crossed the desk, and slid between Wendell's eyes before they could flinch—

* * *

—sunlight through a kitchen window, thick as honey. Flour on the table because it was baking day, and flour on small hands, and flour on the nose of a woman laughing —

*Mama, look, I got it everywhere—*

—she's wiping the child's face with a damp cloth, scolding without heat, and the child is laughing so hard she has to hold its shoulders to keep it still, and there is a father's voice from the doorway saying something dry and warm, and the whole kitchen smells of bread and woodsmoke and *belonging*, and the child — Wendell, the child is Wendell, small and round-cheeked and utterly unafraid — runs to the door and is swung up into strong arms and spun until the kitchen whirls like a painted wheel—

*I love you, little thief,* the father says, *you steal my heart daily and give nothing back—*

—and everyone laughs, and the light is golden, and it goes on, it goes on and on, this ordinary impossible happiness, ten heartbeats of it, nine, eight—

* * *

Wendell came back to themselves with tears on their face and no memory of starting to cry.

Ilex was watching them with the expression of a man counting money he hadn't yet stolen. "There it is," he said softly. "That's the look. Every time. Grief with nowhere to sit."

"They loved me." Wendell's voice came out scraped raw. "I had — there was bread, there was a dog, there were *names* — my mother had a name, my father—" They pressed the heel of a hand against their eyes. "I sold that. I sold being loved. To eat. For a *winter*."

"You sold it to live, and living was the right bargain, whatever the ledgers say." Ilex leaned forward. "But here is the cruelty of your trade, bookkeeper, and mine: the past doesn't rot in the jar. It waits. And now you've tasted it, and now you'll spend every night for the rest of your life reaching for it the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. So." He drew the empty jar back toward himself. "Full restoration. Everything in that bundle — the rite, the names, the faces, the dog, all of it, returned whole and permanent. You'd walk out of here knowing who you were. Knowing the rite by heart instead of by rumor. Imagine what that's worth to a certain child."

The temptation landed like a blow. Wendell had prepared for tricks, for haggling, for the broker's smile — not for the simple, naked offer of *yourself, returned.*

"Price," Wendell managed.

"One year." Ilex said it lightly, terribly lightly. "One year of your future. Not your death — I'm not in that trade, whatever the rumors say. Just a year. Struck from your span. You'll never know which one. Could be a year at the end, in bed, surrounded by grandchildren. Could be next spring. The ledgers balance either way." He folded his hands. "You've spent your life selling pieces of yourself to survive, Wendell Galloway. Here at last is a chance to buy a piece back. All it costs is more of you."

The shop was very quiet. In the jars along the walls, a thousand small lights drifted and burned, and Wendell understood suddenly that every one of them had been somebody's desperate arithmetic, somebody's winter, somebody's *I had no choice.*

"No," Wendell said.

Ilex raised an eyebrow. "No?"

"No." Wendell stood. Their legs shook; they made them hold. "You want me to keep paying myself out forever — a memory here, a year there, until there's nothing left but a name and a trade. I've done that math my whole life and it never balances. The child needs the rite. Fine. I'll get it another way — from the guild vaults, where my memories sit in *their* jars, paid for already, bought with coin I earned in blood and tedium. I won't pay twice for the same self."

"A bold plan. The Guild of Impossible Things guards its vaults better than kings guard crowns." Ilex smiled. "And when you fail?"

"Then I fail as someone who kept what was left of me."

At the door, Wendell stopped. Without turning: "The mother. In the memory. Her name — I saw it, for ten heartbeats I knew it. Tell me and I'll go."

"Now that," said Ilex, "would be giving away stock."

Wendell nodded, unsurprised, and pushed open the green door into the noise and salt-smell of the market — and stopped dead on the threshold.

Brannoc was pushing through the crowd toward the shop at a dead run, the child clutched against his chest, his face grey as ash. Behind him, at the mouth of the lane, three figures stood perfectly still while the market flowed around them like water around stones. They had no expressions to read. Their faces were smooth as river-worn pebbles.

The Hollowed had found Casterhale after all.
