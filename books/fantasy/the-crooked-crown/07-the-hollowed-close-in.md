## 7. The Hollowed Close In

Brannoc had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one truth worth carrying: you could tell how a fight would go by the sound it made before it started.

The shrine road made no sound at all.

That was wrong. The road out of Casterhale climbed through pine country where everything should have been talking — jays quarreling in the branches, wind working through the needles, some small thing rustling in the bracken. Instead there was only their own noise: Wendell's boots on the gravel, the child's bare feet whispering beside them, and beneath it all a silence so complete it pressed against his ears like water.

He had heard that silence once before, at the fall of Vell's Crossing. The Hollowed did not march loudly. They did not need to. Fear went ahead of them and emptied the world.

"They know we're here," he said quietly.

Wendell didn't stop walking. "They've known since the salt market."

"Then say it plain. We're being herded." He nodded up the road, to where the pines thinned and the land dropped away into a gorge. A river ran along the bottom, gray-green and fast with snowmelt, and across it — the only crossing for twenty miles in either direction — stood the old toll bridge, its stone arches furred with moss, its parapets crumbled to waist height. Beyond it the road bent up into the mountains, toward the shrine, toward whatever Wendell believed was waiting there.

"You want to turn back?" Wendell said. There was something raw under the words. Brannoc had watched them for three days now — the memory-thief who talked about their own past like a man reading someone else's letters — and he had learned that Wendell's sharpness was just fear wearing armor.

"No," Brannoc said. "I want us to choose the ground instead of letting them choose it for us." He looked at the bridge again, measuring it the way he'd measured a hundred bridges and gates and breaches. Two arches wide. Parapet broken on the far side, solid enough on the near. One approach road, narrow, hemmed by rock on both flanks. "A bridge is a terrible place for them and a fine place for one man with a spear."

"One man," Wendell repeated.

"I was at Vell's Crossing when it fell," Brannoc said. It came out of him before he could decide whether to say it. Maybe because they were going to die soon, or maybe because the child was watching him with those old, old eyes and he found he couldn't lie in front of them. "I ran. Whole garrison ran. I told myself it was sense — dead men hold nothing. But I've been running eleven years, and every city Veyra took, I was already gone from it before her banners came over the hills." He rolled his shoulders, felt the old wound in his back pull. "I'm tired of arriving places after they're already lost. This time I'd like to be standing somewhere when it matters."

Wendell looked at him for a long moment. Then at the child, who had stopped walking and was staring at the bridge with an expression no child should own — recognition, or grief, or both.

"The rite needs me alive," Wendell said finally. "And it needs *them*." They touched the child's head, gently, the way you'd touch something you were afraid of breaking. "It doesn't need you."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They crossed at dusk, when the light went long and copper through the pines and the river below turned to hammered bronze. Wendell wanted to argue — Brannoc could hear them wanting to, could see the arguments queuing behind their eyes as they crouched by the near parapet, checking the stones, testing the footing. But the child took Wendell's hand and tugged, once, toward the far side, and whatever silent counsel passed between them, Wendell's shoulders dropped and the arguing stopped.

Brannoc busied himself with the work of soldiers. He broke up the paving stones on the near approach and dragged them into a low barricade across the road, waist-high, ugly, effective. He wedged his spear's butt into a crack between two blocks where the road narrowed. He counted his arrows — nine — and set them point-up in the earth where he could reach them without looking.

The child appeared at his elbow while he worked. It didn't speak — it almost never spoke; the common tongue seemed to sit in its mouth like a stone it hadn't learned to swallow — but it held out its hand, palm up, and in the palm lay a sprig of mountain ash, red-berried, snapped fresh from somewhere though there wasn't an ash tree within a mile.

"For luck?" Brannoc asked.

The child considered this. Then it reached up and tucked the sprig through the strap of his breastplate, over his heart, with the grave precision of someone pinning on a medal.

Brannoc had buried friends. He had burned friends. He had left friends unburied on walls and in ditches from one end of the river country to the other. None of it had ever undone him quite the way a silent child giving him a berry branch did.

"Well," he managed. "Thank you."

From down the valley, faint as breath, came the sound of many feet not making any sound at all.

* * *

They came with the last light, and they were worse than he remembered.

At Vell's Crossing he had seen them at distance, a gray tide flowing over the walls. Up close, in the dying day, the Hollowed were worse than soldiers — they were soldiers *emptied*. Their armor was Veyra's black and silver, but their faces were smooth as river-worn stone, features blurred away as if someone had rubbed them out with a wet thumb. No eyes to read. No mouths to shout with. They came up the road in perfect silence, thirty of them, then forty, filling the narrow way between the rocks like water finding its level, and the only sound in the whole world was their boots on the gravel.

Brannoc put an arrow into the first one at forty paces. It took the Hollowed through the smooth center of its face and it did not fall, did not stagger, did not break stride — but the ones behind it stumbled over it, and that bought him seconds, and seconds were what he'd come to buy.

"GO!" he roared back over his shoulder, and heard Wendell and the child's footsteps quicken on the far side of the bridge, fading up the mountain road.

Then they were on him.

The first hour was arrows. He fired until his fingers bled, and the Hollowed kept coming, climbing over their own fallen without haste, without fury, the way ants climb over other ants. The second hour was the spear. They reached the barricade and he met them there, in the gap between the rocks, where only two could come at him at once and a long spear in strong hands is worth ten swords. He killed them and killed them and killed them, and they did not scream, and that was the worst of it — the silence, the smooth faces, the way they fell like furniture toppling.

Somewhere in the third hour he took a blade across the thigh and stopped noticing his body much after that. He fought from somewhere above himself, watching a big gray-bearded man hold a bridge against the dark, and thought distantly: *so this is what it was for. Eleven years of running, and it comes down to one bridge, and it's enough. It's actually enough.*

They pulled him down eventually. Of course they did. But it cost them — the barricade was a mound of broken Hollowed by then, and the bridge behind him stood empty, and the night was deep, and up the mountain road two small figures had hours of darkness to hide in.

As they bore him down — no blows, just weight, dozens of smooth hands pressing him into the stones — Brannoc saw one of them pause at the edge of the bridge. It looked back the way he'd come from, up the dark mountain, and its blank face tilted, listening.

Listening for something. Or someone.

*Hold on,* Brannoc thought, to whoever might be listening, to the child, to the whole crooked world. *Hold on. Some things are still worth the price.*

Then the smooth hands closed over his face, and the world went white, and he knew no more.

* * *

Far above, in the black throat of the pass, Wendell stopped walking.

The child stopped too. Below them, miles down, the bridge was invisible, but the silence had changed shape — the particular silence of a thing finished.

"We can't help him," Wendell said. It wasn't clear whether they were telling the child or themselves. Their voice cracked in the middle. "If we go back, we all die, and the king stays buried, and Veyra wins, and none of it — none of it means anything—"

The child let go of their hand.

For a moment Wendell thought it was simply turning away, and grief rose in them like floodwater — but the child stepped closer instead, close enough that Wendell could see its face pale in the starlight, could see tears cutting clean lines down it. And the child looked up at them with those ancient eyes, eyes that had watched a king be unmade, and opened its mouth, and spoke.

Not the old tongue. Not the language older than kingdoms, the language of fragments and sealed names.

Common speech. Rough, halting, a voice used perhaps never before, each word placed like a stone across a stream:

"Wendell. Galloway."

Just the name. Nothing else. The child said it the way you'd say a prayer, or a promise — the way you say the one true thing you know when everything else is burning.

Wendell stood very still. Being remembered, they discovered, was a terrifying intimacy — worse than being known, because the child had no memories of its own except the scattered pieces of a dead king, and yet somehow, impossibly, it knew *them*, knew their name the way the rite would know it, the way the vaults knew it, the way the ledger of impossible things recorded everything that could not otherwise survive.

"I'm here," Wendell whispered. They knelt, and took the small cold hands in theirs. "I'm here, and I'm going to finish this. For him. For Brannoc. For everyone the crown has eaten." They swallowed. "Can you show me the rest? Can you show me what the rite needs?"

The child nodded slowly. Then it lifted one hand and pointed — not up the mountain, toward the shrine.

Back down. Back the way they had come. Toward Ashvale. Toward the guild.

Toward the vaults where Wendell's sold memories waited in the dark, patient as graves.

"That's not the shrine," Wendell said. "The mistress said—" They stopped. Understood. The shrine was where the rite was *performed*. But the kindling — the kindling had been sold years ago, catalogued, shelved, locked behind guild iron. "We have to go back."

The child squeezed their fingers. Once. Yes.

Behind them, far below, something vast began to move in the valley — torchlight kindling along the river road like a lit fuse, Veyra's army marching on the free cities, on Casterhale, on everything that still drew breath.

Ahead lay the guild, the vaults, the self Wendell had sold to survive.

Wendell Galloway picked up the child, turned their back on the shrine, and started down into the dark.
