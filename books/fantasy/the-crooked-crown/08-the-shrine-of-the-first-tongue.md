## 8. The Shrine of the First Tongue

The mountain did not want them there.

Wendell felt it in the way the path narrowed, in the way the pines leaned back from the trail like a crowd giving a plague-carter room to pass. Three days out of Casterhale, two days since the bridge, and Brannoc's absence walked beside them like a third traveler no one would name aloud.

The child rode on Wendell's hip, lighter than any child had a right to be, as though grief were slowly unmaking its weight along with everything else. It had not spoken since the bridge. Not in the old tongue. Not Wendell's name. It only watched the road behind them with eyes too old for its face, and pressed one small hand flat against Wendell's chest as if checking that something there still beat.

"We're close," Wendell said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind. "The broker's map said the tree line breaks at the scree field. After that—"

The child made a sound. Small. Almost nothing. But it turned its face toward the peak above them, and its hand lifted from Wendell's chest and pointed, sure as a compass needle finding north.

* * *

The shrine was not what Wendell expected, which was foolish, because nothing about this had been what they expected since the night a silent child was left on the guild steps wrapped in a blanket that smelled of ash.

There were no walls. No roof. No idol with jeweled eyes or altar stained by centuries of sacrifice. There was only a ring of standing stones on a shelf of bare granite above the clouds, each stone carved so deeply with the old tongue that the letters had become furrows, channels, wounds — and rainwater, when it came, would have run down those grooves like ink down a page. The stones were black with age except where lichen had taken hold, green-gold, patient. In the center of the ring stood a single tree, leafless, silver-barked, dead or sleeping, Wendell could not tell which.

The child slid from Wendell's hip the moment they crossed into the ring. It walked to the tree, laid both palms against the bark, and began to speak.

The sound filled the hollow of the mountain. Wendell had heard the old tongue from the child before — fragments, whispers, the half-remembered cadence of a memory sold — but never like this. Never at full voice. The words were not loud so much as *deep*, the way an earthquake is deep, felt in the teeth and the ribs and the wet dark of the lungs. The lichen on the stones brightened. The dead tree shivered without wind.

And Wendell understood it.

Not all of it. Not the way one understands a sentence. But the way one understands fire, or falling — the body understanding before the mind can object. The words meant *unmake*. The words meant *return*. The words meant *a king sealed in his own crown, screaming where no one could hear, for eleven years*.

Wendell sank down onto a fallen stone and put their head in their hands.

* * *

They found the rite itself carved into the inner face of the easternmost stone, low, near the ground, as if the carvers had meant it to be read kneeling. The old script wound around a central figure — a person standing in the ring, arms open, mouth open — and around that figure the letters spiraled outward like water going down a drain.

Wendell couldn't read it. That was the cruelty of it. They stood before the answer to everything and could not read a single word.

But the child could.

It came to stand beside them, small fingers tracing the grooves, and it translated — not into common speech, because Wendell suspected it barely knew any, but into meaning, pressing images directly against the inside of Wendell's skull the way it had done once before, on the guild steps, the first night. A king being poured into a crown like wine into a cup. A mage dying with a secret held between her teeth. And now this:

*A speaker must stand in the ring.*

*The speaker must speak the Unbinding in the First Tongue.*

*The kindling is memory. All of it. Freely given.*

*What is given cannot be kept. What burns does not return.*

Wendell read it three times. The child's translation did not change. Nothing softened. There was no clause for mercy, no footnote, no clever reading. The rite wanted a soul poured out like wine, and it wanted the pouring to be willing, because unwilling sacrifice curdled, apparently, the way bad blood curdles, and the crown would drink the curdled thing and grow stronger.

"Freely given," Wendell said aloud. Their voice sounded very small under the sky.

Then they looked at the child, and the arithmetic arrived, cold and complete, the way terrible arithmetic always arrives.

The child was the last living speaker of the First Tongue. The child was a fragment of Aldric himself — the piece of him that remembered how to be unbound, smuggled out of Veyra's citadel in whatever vessel her sorcerers had used, hidden in a shape too small and too innocent to be hunted until now. If the child stood in the ring and spoke the rite, the crown would shatter, the king would rise —

— and the child would burn. Every memory. Every word. Everything it was. Kindling. Ash on the mountain wind.

And worse, Wendell realized, stomach turning over slowly: if the child burned, then the fragment of Aldric inside it burned too. The king might rise from the crown, but he would rise missing the piece that knew how to be free. He would rise a man with a hole in him shaped exactly like this child, and holes like that did not stay empty. Holes like that got refilled. By crowns. By queens-regent. By whatever came next.

The rite demanded a speaker who would die speaking. The only speaker was the child. Therefore the child died, and freedom died with it, half-born.

Unless.

Wendell sat down hard on the granite. The stone was cold through their clothes. Below them, the world was a sea of cloud turning gold in the late light, beautiful and indifferent, and somewhere beyond that sea, Veyra's armies were moving, and somewhere behind them, on a bridge over a gray river, Brannoc was either alive or not, and Wendell had not gone back to check because the child had pulled at their sleeve and pointed up-mountain with absolute certainty, and there had been no time, there was never any time—

"Kindling," Wendell said. "Memory. Freely given."

The child looked at them. Waiting. It had been waiting its whole short existence, Wendell thought, for someone to do the arithmetic correctly.

"It doesn't have to be *your* memories," Wendell said slowly, testing each word like a man testing ice. "It says memory. It says freely given. It doesn't say whose."

The child tilted its head.

"I'm a memory-thief." Wendell laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have spent nine years stealing other people's pasts and locking them in ledgers. I have a vault full of them. Weddings. Funerals. First kisses. Last words. An entire guild archive of things people paid to forget." They stopped. Their hands had begun to shake, and they looked at them — these hands, these careful, quick, dishonest hands — and finished anyway.

"And I sold mine. Years ago. My childhood. My mother's kitchen. The famine winter. A dying court mage who taught me—" Wendell's breath caught, because saying it plainly, here, under the standing stones, made it real in a way nine years of not-thinking-about-it never had. "She taught me the Unbinding Rite. I carried it out of the palace in my head and I sold it to buy bread, and the guild has been sitting on it ever since, filed and catalogued and *mine*, in every way that matters, because I gave it freely. I signed for it. I took the coin."

The wind moved through the dead tree. Somewhere far below, thunder muttered, or artillery, or both.

"If the kindling has to be willingly surrendered," Wendell said, "then it doesn't have to be yours. It can be mine. It's already in a vault. It's already paid for. All I have to do is go back and take it — steal it, properly, one last time — and bring it here, and give it away."

The child reached out and touched Wendell's face. Its hand was warm. Its expression was not pity, exactly, and not gratitude. It was recognition — the look of one fragment meeting another, one unfinished thing looking at a second unfinished thing and understanding that wholeness, for both of them, would cost the same price.

"You'll forget," Wendell told it, and their voice broke on the second word. "When I burn it. You'll forget your language, maybe. Your name. Whatever you are of him. You'll be — new. Empty-ish. Alive." They swallowed. "I'm sorry. It's the best I can find. Believe me, I've read the fine print my whole life. This is the best."

The child nodded. Once. Slowly. The way kings nod.

* * *

They did not sleep that night. Wendell sat in the ring of stones with the child asleep against their shoulder, watching stars wheel over the black peaks, and made lists, because lists were the only prayer Wendell Galloway had ever known how to say.

The guild vaults lay four days' ride south, beneath the House of Impossible Things in Ashvale — if Ashvale still stood, if the guild mistress hadn't fled, if Veyra's hunters weren't already crawling through the archives looking for exactly the ledger Wendell needed. The Hollowed didn't tire. The Hollowed didn't eat. The Hollowed had smoothed faces and followed orders, and their queen wanted the child, and failing that she would want the thief who'd stolen it.

Four days there. Four days back. Nine days since the bridge. Veyra's army had been two river cities behind them then.

Behind them now, Wendell guessed, it would be less.

The child stirred and murmured something in the old tongue — soft, half-dreaming, a word that Wendell's bones understood even if their mind did not. It sounded like *hurry*. It sounded like *almost*. It sounded like a door beginning, after eleven years, to open.

Wendell looked south, toward the lowlands, toward the vaults, toward everything they had sold and everything they were about to spend.

"All right," they said quietly, to the child, to the mountain, to the dead tree, to whoever had built this place and left its instructions carved in stone for a bookkeeper and a ghost to find. "One last theft."

They rose before dawn, wrapped the child against the cold, and started down the mountain — south, toward the guild, toward the vault where their own life sat filed under someone else's catalogue number.

Behind them, on the horizon, the sky over the lowlands had begun to glow. Not sunrise. Sunrise came from the east.

This glow came from the south, and it flickered, and it spread.

Ashvale was burning.
