## 9. The Unbinding

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its vault beneath the counting house, behind a door that had no handle and no keyhole and no seam — a door that was not so much built as *remembered* into being. Wendell had walked past it for eleven years, carrying other people's yesterdays down those stairs in lead-lined boxes, and had never once thought of what lay on the far side.

Now they stood before it with a child's hand in theirs and Brannoc's blood still drying on their sleeve, and the door looked back at them the way doors do when they know why you've come.

"It opens for the ledger," the child said. The old tongue had left her over the weeks of the road, syllable by syllable, spent like coins; she had perhaps a dozen words of it remaining, and she guarded each one the way starving children guard bread. "The book remembers the way in."

Of course it did. Wendell had been such a fool about so many things, but never about this: the Ledger of Impossible Things did not record the guild's inventory. It *was* the guild's inventory. Every memory ever stolen, catalogued in ink that was not ink, bound in leather that had once been something that dreamed. And the vault answered to it, because the vault was only the ledger's body, the way the child had once been only a king's.

Wendell drew the ledger from their coat. It was heavier than it should have been. It had grown heavier every mile since Casterhale, as if it knew.

"I sold these," they said quietly. Not to the child. To themselves. A last accounting before the final entry. "My mother's kitchen. The smell of rain on the farm road. My own name said by someone who loved me. I sold them to eat, and I told myself they were only memories, that I could live without them." They looked at the blank door. "I was wrong about what they were."

The child squeezed their hand. She did it the way children do — without ceremony, without understanding, with a trust that was more terrible than any demand.

Wendell opened the ledger to the page bearing their own name — *Galloway, W., vendor*, eleven years of entries in their own careful hand — and pressed the open book flat against the stone.

The door exhaled.

It did not swing. It simply stopped being where it had been, and there was a stairway going down into a dark that was warm, that breathed, that smelled of woodsmoke and cut grass and bread — smells that hit Wendell like fists, because they were not the vault's smells at all. They were leaking out of the boxes. Eleven years of sealed yesterdays, and every one of them was *theirs*.

They went down together, into the dark that remembered.

* * *

The vault was not a room. It was a harvest.

Shelves rose out of sight in every direction, and on the shelves sat boxes of grey lead, thousands upon thousands of them, each one humming faintly with somebody's stolen afternoon. Wendell had carried a hundred of these up those stairs without ever wondering whose life cooled inside. Now the hum seemed deafening. Now every box sounded like a throat trying to speak.

The index burned itself into Wendell's mind the moment they crossed the threshold — the guild's oldest trick, the one they'd never been told about, the map of the place written directly into the thief's memory. *Aisle nine. Shelf forty-one. Galloway, W.*

Nine aisles. Forty-one shelves. Their hands shook so badly the box nearly slipped.

It was small. That was the cruelty of it. Eleven years of grief compressed into a container no bigger than a loaf of bread, and stamped along the lid in the guild's neat script: *CHILDHOOD, RURAL. FAMINE LOT 88. QUALITY: HIGH. NOTE — CONTAINS ANOMALOUS ACQUISITION. SEE APPENDIX.*

The dying mage. The court mage who had crawled into the famine camp with his robes burnt off and his king's name breaking apart in his mouth, and who had poured the Unbinding Rite into a farm child's head because he had nowhere else to put it. Wendell had carried that knowledge for years without knowing it was there — and then had sold it, sealed it, shelved it, and walked away whistling.

"Open it," the child whispered.

"Not here." Wendell tucked the box against their chest and felt it beat, or imagined it beat, faint and quick as a bird's heart. "The rite needs fire and sky and a speaker willing to burn. Not this tomb."

They were three steps toward the stair when the lamps above went dark.

Not all of them. Just the ones near the door — snuffed in a line, fast, like footsteps passing. And then came a sound Wendell knew from the road, from Ashvale, from the burning farms: a great many soldiers moving very quietly, which is the loudest sound in the world.

"They're here," the child said. Not afraid. Resigned, in the way of someone who has been hunted since before she could walk. "She followed my words. When I spoke at the gate. I'm sorry. I didn't know—"

"Hush." Wendell pulled her behind the shelf, into the smell of strangers' lives, and listened.

Boots on the stairs. Many boots. And over them, cutting through the vault's warm hum like frost through water, a voice that was smooth and cold and terribly reasonable — a voice Wendell had heard only in rumor and deserter's testimony, and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a knife.

"Search the shelves," said Queen-regent Veyra. "But gently. Break nothing that holds a memory. I have use for this place."

* * *

They came down in files of six, and they were wrong in a way that made Wendell's skin crawl even though Brannoc had warned them. The Hollowed did not look around the vault. They did not glance at the shelves or each other. They moved through the aisles like fingers through hair, smooth and purposeless-seeming until they found what they wanted, and their faces —

Their faces were the worst. Smoothed blank, as if someone had taken a thumb to wet clay. No brows worth naming. Mouths like closed drawers. Where eyes should have held light, they held only reflection.

Veyra came last.

She was smaller than the rumors made her, and that was somehow worse. A narrow woman in grey silk with a circlet of crooked iron above her brow — the Crown itself, worn openly now, its barbs catching the lamplight and bending it wrong. She walked the center aisle slowly, trailing one finger along the shelf edges, and where her finger passed, the boxes went quiet. Silenced. Drunk.

"So much of it," she murmured, almost tenderly. "So much of everyone." Her gaze drifted down the aisle and stopped.

Wendell felt it land like a hand on the back of the neck.

"You're the bookkeeper," Veyra said. Conversational. Pleasant, even. "Galloway. Your guild sold me routing schedules for my supply trains, did you know? Very thorough people, thieves. You catalogue everything." She smiled, and the Crooked Crown drank the smile and gave back nothing. "Including, I'm told, a certain anomalous acquisition. Lot eighty-eight. A farm child who met a dying man with a king's name in his mouth."

The Hollowed fanned out between Wendell and the stairs. There was no way past them. There had never been a way past them; Brannoc had known it on the bridge, and had held it anyway, and Wendell carried that knowledge now like a second heartbeat.

"You want the rite," Wendell said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "You can't take it. It doesn't work that way. It has to be given."

"Oh, I know how it works." Veyra drifted closer. Up close, Wendell could see that the Crown was not resting on her head. It was *rooted*. Fine black filaments ran from its barbs down into her scalp, into her temples, vanishing under skin gone faintly translucent at the hairline. "I've worn it for nineteen years. Do you imagine I don't understand what it eats? It ate my name first. Then my face. Then everything I was before, and I let it, because what it gave back was an army." She spread her hands. "And armies need feeding too, bookkeeper. Every soldier I Hollow, the Crown grows a new barb. Every city I burn, it grows another. It is very hungry, and I am very good at finding it food."

"And when it runs out?"

Something flickered across the smoothed blankness of her expression — not fear. Recognition. The look of someone hearing their own death described accurately.

"That is why I need the rite," she said softly. "Not to break it. To *bind it better*. The old king's mages built it sloppy. I intend to have it rebuilt, and the rite is the key to the original working." Her eyes went to the box under Wendell's arm. "Give it to me, and I'll let the child live. I give you my word."

"The word of a woman with no name," Wendell said.

Veyra's pleasantness fell away all at once, like a mask dropping, and what was underneath was so tired and so vast and so empty that Wendell almost pitied her. Almost.

"Take it from them," she said.

* * *

Later — in whatever life came after — Wendell would try to remember the fight and fail, because memory was their trade and they knew better than anyone that terror does not record; it erases. What remained were fragments:

The child's hand torn from theirs.

A lead box hurled, not at a soldier but at a lamp, bursting in a spray of someone's stolen wedding day, and a Hollowed soldier stumbling as a stranger's joy flooded through him and his blank face *flickered* — one instant of confusion, of grief, of a man remembering he had once been a man —

One instant was enough. Wendell was through the gap and running, and the vault's warm dark became the night sky, and the night sky became the shrine hill, and the shrine hill —

— was where they had always been going. They understood that now. Every road out of Ashvale had bent toward it. The rite required fire and sky and a speaker willing to burn, and the Shrine of the First Tongue had a fire-pit older than kingdoms and a sky full of indifferent stars, and Wendell stood at its edge with the box in their arms and Veyra's army pouring up the slope below like spilled ink running backward.

"Will it hurt?" the child asked.

"Yes," Wendell said. "All of it. That's how I'll know it's real."

They broke the seal.

* * *

Eleven years arrived at once.

Rain on the farm road. Mother's kitchen, flour to the elbows, singing off-key. Father's laugh, deep as a well. The dog, the dog, the dog's name — *Bramble, his name was Bramble* — summer lightning, winter fevers, a sister born blue and screaming and alive, alive, alive. And underneath it all, threaded through it like wire through clay: the dying mage in the famine tent, gripping a child's wrist with burnt fingers, saying *listen, listen, little one, remember this even if you forget me* — and the rite pouring in, word by word, the old tongue's grammar of unmaking, the true names of crowns and the prices of kings.

Wendell knelt at the fire-pit with their whole life in their arms and began to speak.

The old tongue came out of them fluently, horribly, in a voice that was half their own and half the dead mage's, and the words were not words so much as *openings*. The fire stood up. The stars leaned closer. On the slope below, the army faltered as if the ground had tilted beneath it.

Veyra reached the hilltop alone — her Hollowed unable to follow onto consecrated ground, or unwilling, or simply no longer needed — and she screamed at Wendell to stop, and her scream cracked halfway through into something younger, something frightened, something that might once have had a name.

The memories caught fire.

That was the rite's price and its engine: kindling. A life, freely surrendered, burned as fuel. Wendell fed it everything. Mother. Father. Bramble the dog. The sister's first cry. The smell of rain. Their own name, spoken with love, spoken at all — they fed it all, and it burned green and gold and impossibly bright, and with every memory that went up, the child blazed brighter beside them, filling, completing, becoming.

The last thing to burn was the smallest: a moment, four years old, sitting on a fence post at dusk, perfectly happy, knowing nothing, wanting nothing.

Wendell let it go.

The fire went white. The Crooked Crown shrieked — a sound like a kingdom dying in reverse — and its black filaments tore free of Veyra's skull in a single wrenching instant, and she collapsed, emptied, a dress full of dust and nineteen borrowed years, and before she finished falling she was nothing at all, not even dust, just a crooked circlet ringing on stone.

And the child stood up out of the light.

She was taller. Older. Her eyes were grey as river-ice and ancient as the first word ever spoken, and when King Aldric opened his mouth, the voice that came out was a man's voice, whole and unhollowed and shaking.

He looked at Wendell — at the stranger kneeling in ash where a life used to be, nameless now, memoryless now, holding nothing but the smoke of their own childhood — and the king wept.

"I remember you," Aldric said. "From her. From the fragment. You were kind to her when no one had been kind to her in a hundred years." He knelt, king to ash, and took Wendell's smoke-stained hands in his. "Tell me your name."

Wendell opened their mouth.

Nothing came. Nothing was there. The ledger of themselves was empty, every entry burned, every line struck through — and yet standing in that emptiness, hollowed clean as a bell, Wendell felt something they had not felt since the famine years.

Light. Unburdened. *Possible.*

"I'll tell you tomorrow," Wendell said. "I think I'd like to choose it first."

Below the shrine, dawn was coming up over an army that no longer had a queen — ten thousand Hollowed faces turning toward the sunrise, and on every smoothed-blank face, for the first time in years, something flickering. Something waking. Something beginning, very slowly, to remember.
