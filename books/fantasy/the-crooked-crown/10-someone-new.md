## 10. Someone New

The war ended the way a fever breaks — not with a shout but with a long, shuddering exhale that left everyone blinking in the sudden quiet, unsure what to do with their hands.

Wendell stood on the steps of the ruined guildhall in Ashvale and watched the last of Veyra's soldiers walk out of the city. They came in ones and twos at first, then in ragged columns, their blank faces slowly remembering how to be faces. Some of them wept. Most of them simply looked around, dazed, like men waking from a dream they could not name, touching their own cheeks as if to confirm the features were still theirs. The Hollowed had been Hollowed no longer since the moment the Crooked Crown shattered; whatever Veyra had siphoned from them had come flooding back, and now they stood in the mud of the market square holding identities they hadn't worn in years, trying them on like coats from another man's closet.

One of them stopped at the foot of the steps. A woman, broad-shouldered, her hair grown back in uneven patches where the smoothing had taken it. She looked up at Wendell with eyes that were still learning how to hold an expression.

"Did I know you?" she asked. Her voice was rough with disuse. "Before. Did I know you?"

"I don't think so," Wendell said.

She nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then she smiled — a crooked, unpracticed thing, like a door opening on rusted hinges. "Then perhaps I'll know you after." And she walked on into the city that had been her enemy's stronghold a week ago, carrying nothing but herself.

Wendell watched her go for a long time.

* * *

Aldric came to find them three days later.

The king arrived without fanfare, which was itself remarkable; Wendell had seen enough of kings-in-waiting to know that most of them traveled wrapped in ceremony the way wounds are wrapped in linen, to keep the world from pressing too hard against the tender parts. But Aldric walked up the guildhall steps alone, wearing plain wool and muddy boots, and he was younger than his portraits and older than his years, and when he saw Wendell standing in the doorway of the ledger room he stopped as though he'd struck glass.

"You're smaller than I remember," he said.

"Do you remember me?" Wendell asked carefully. "Truly? Or do you remember the child?"

Aldric considered this with the gravity of a man who had spent centuries being spoken *about* rather than *to*, and who intended never to let it happen again. "Both," he said finally. "The child carried my words, but the voice was its own. And you—" He stepped closer, and his gaze was the gray of old silver, patient and heavy. "You burned yourself to set me free. I felt every piece of you as it went into the fire. I have been king for a hundred years, thief, and I have never been given anything so large."

"It wasn't a gift," Wendell said. "It was a trade. You were worth more than I was."

"That," said Aldric, "is the arithmetic of a bookkeeper, and I am here to tell you it is wrong." He moved past Wendell into the ledger room, into the dimness where dust hung in the light from the high broken window, and he laid one hand flat on the great empty table where the Ledger of Impossible Things had lain open for two hundred years. The ledger itself was gone — burned, along with everything Wendell had ever stolen and stored, every memory in the guild's keeping, consumed as kindling for the Unbinding. The vaults beneath the floor stood open and hollow, echoing like pulled teeth.

"Everything in this room belonged to someone," Aldric said. "Every stolen year, every sold childhood, every secret pried loose and shelved. And it is all ash now, returned to no one. Except you." He turned. "You gave back more than anyone ever took. So ask me for anything. A title. Land. A place at my right hand. The first free city, if you want it. I will not offer twice."

Wendell had thought about this. Had thought about little else, in the days since the crown shattered and the sky over Ashvale cleared for the first time in living memory. They had stood in the ruins of their own life and inventoried what remained, the way they'd once inventoried other people's memories: here is a body, scarred and tired; here is a trade, half-remembered; here is a child asleep against my shoulder who speaks a dead language and calls me by name.

Here is nothing else. Here is room.

"I want work," Wendell said.

Aldric's eyebrows rose.

"Not a title. Not land. Work." Wendell heard their own voice come out steady, which surprised them, because inside they were anything but. "There are people coming out of Veyra's armies with holes where their lives used to be. There are families who sold memories during the famine years to buy bread, same as I did, and the brokers' vaults are still standing in Casterhale and Saltmarket and gods know where else. Someone has to find those memories. Someone has to carry them home." They paused. "I know how the trade works from the inside. Every trick, every price, every lock. I'm the only person alive who can undo it all."

For a long moment the king said nothing. The light through the broken window shifted; somewhere below, in the square, children were shouting, and the sound came up through the floorboards like water rising in a well.

"You would make yourself a restorer of stolen things," Aldric said at last. "In a kingdom that has just learned what theft costs."

"Yes."

"And what shall I call you? The ledgers need a name, and so does the seal I'll put on your commission." His mouth curved, dry. "Unless you'd prefer *the thief who unmade a queen*. It has a certain ring."

"Wendell Galloway died in your shrine," Wendell said quietly. "They burned with everything else. Whoever walks out of this room needs a new name." They had turned the question over for days, turning it like a stone in the pocket, looking for the shape of it. The answer had been there all along, small and obvious, waiting the way answers do. "Call me Keeper. Just that. It's not a name anyone gave me. It's a name I chose."

Aldric studied them for a long breath. Then he inclined his head — not the shallow nod of a monarch acknowledging a subject, but something deeper, older, the bow of one soldier to another across a field they'd both survived.

"Keeper," he said, tasting it. "It suits you better than you know. The work is yours. The guildhall is yours, if you want it — it has bad bones and worse history, but the foundation is honest." He moved toward the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The child. What will become of it?"

"They stay with me," Wendell said. No hesitation. None needed. "They're the last speaker of the First Tongue. Everything Aldric lost — everything *you* lost — lives in their head. Someone should help them grow up whole instead of harvesting them for pieces. I was going to be harvested once. I know how it goes."

Something moved across the king's face, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Then guard them well," he said. "And Keeper — when the work grows heavy, and it will, send for me. I owe a debt I cannot repay. I intend to spend a very long time trying."

His boots faded down the stairs. Wendell stood alone in the ledger room, in the dust and the slanting light, and listened to the sound of the city being born outside.

* * *

The fresh book arrived a week later, carried up from Casterhale by a carter who complained the entire way about the roads. It was nothing grand — good paper, leather covers, sewn binding, the kind of book meant to be used rather than admired. Wendell set it on the great table where the old ledger had ruled for two centuries, and stood over it for a long while without opening it.

The child sat cross-legged on the windowsill, watching. They spoke little of the common tongue yet, but they were learning fast, the way children learn everything — by falling down and getting up and falling down again. Their name, as near as anyone could render it, was Sef. In the old tongue it meant something like *the part that remains*, and Wendell thought that was exactly right.

"Are you going to write in it?" Sef asked. The words came out careful, each one placed like a stone in a stream.

"I don't know what to write yet."

Sef considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "You could write what happened."

"Everyone knows what happened."

"Not what happened." The child hopped down from the sill and came to stand beside Wendell, small hand resting on the edge of the table. "What it *was*. Brannoc holding the bridge. The broker's shop smelling of salt. You, before. You don't remember you-before, but I do. I carried some of it, in the beginning. Your mother singing. The taste of the bread she made. You should write those down, so they exist somewhere besides me. In case." The child did not say *in case of what*. They didn't need to. Children understood loss with a clarity adults spent lifetimes avoiding.

Wendell's throat tightened. "Maybe," they managed. "Maybe later."

They opened the book. The first page lay white and waiting, and Wendell found their hand did not shake, which seemed right somehow; the shaking had all been used up already, spent in a vault beneath this very floor while a crown shattered and a king came home.

They dipped the pen. And then, instead of an inventory, instead of a catalogue of impossible things, Wendell wrote:

*Entry the first. The Guild of Impossible Things is dissolved. In its place stands the House of Returned Things, and its business is this: that nothing which belongs to a person — no year, no name, no grief, no joy — shall be bought or sold or held against them ever again. We begin with four hundred and eleven accounts in the brokers' vaults of Casterhale. We begin tomorrow.*

*First account: a woman named Maren Ostreth, age thirty-four, who sold the memory of her daughter's face to buy medicine during the famine winter. The daughter is alive. She lives in Saltmarket. She has been looking for her mother for nine years.*

*We will bring them the face. We will bring them all of it back.*

Wendell set down the pen and read it over, and found that it was true, every word, and that truth required nothing more of them than to keep writing.

Outside, the light was going gold over the rooftops of Ashvale. Somewhere below, Sef was teaching the neighbor children words from the First Tongue, and their laughter came up through the window like something returning to a house long shut. Somewhere south, in a vault that smelled of salt and secrets, four hundred and eleven stolen lives waited in the dark for someone to come and carry them home.

Wendell closed the book gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and reached for the ink.
