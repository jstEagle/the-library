## 1. The Taster's Table

The poison was in the pheasant.

Jude knew it the way he knew rain coming—by a pressure behind his eyes, a faint metallic whisper at the back of his throat that had nothing to do with taste and everything to do with the small illegal magic coiled in his blood. He set down his fork with the unhurried grace of ten years' practice and dabbed his lips with linen while the hall roared on around him, oblivious, golden, doomed to eat nothing tonight but bread and suspicion.

"Master Delacroix." The under-steward hovered at his elbow, sweat shining at his hairline despite the autumn chill seeping through the great windows of Vell's feast-hall. "Well?"

"The bird is off," Jude said. "Not fatally. Spoiled in transport, I'd guess—the giblets turned early. It would sicken His Majesty for three days and make him distrust the Duke of Marren's gift forever after."

A lie wrapped in truth, which was the only kind of lie worth telling. There was no spoilage. There was arsenic-and-ashroot, a classic pairing from the southern guilds, subtle enough that no tongue untrained in charms would ever catch it. But *spoiled* was a word palaces understood. *Poison* was a word that started investigations, and investigations had a way of ending at tasters' doors.

The under-steward bowed and swept the platter away. Down the length of the high table, King Aldous the Third laughed at something his fool had muttered, a big wet laugh, the laugh of a man who believed himself beloved by every soul in his kingdom. Jude watched him and felt the familiar hollow open behind his ribs, the one shaped exactly like the question he never let himself finish: *and what am I to you, Majesty? A servant? A shield? A thing you'd replace by morning if I choked wrong?*

He already knew the answer. He'd known it since he was nineteen and desperate enough to sign the taster's oath, since the crown's physicians had pressed the first vial into his palm and explained, in the gentle voice one uses for children and the condemned, how the immunity worked.

*Drink this nightly. It will not prevent poison. It will simply ensure that whatever kills you kills you slowly enough for us to notice.*

That was the bargain. Not safety—only slowness. A decade of dinners purchased at the price of a body permanently steeped in charmwork, so saturated with illegal enchantment that if the Ember-wardens ever tested him properly, he would burn brighter than any witch they'd dragged to the pyres in the last twenty years.

Jude lifted his wine cup and, beneath its rim, out of habit older than thought, thumbed the wax seal off the little pellet hidden against his wrist. The nightly dose dissolved into the dark red without a ripple. He drank. The charm went down cold, then warm, then settled into his marrow like an old dog finding its place by the fire.

Ten years tomorrow. Ten years of tasting death in small doses so a king could sleep soundly, and every night sealing a forbidden charm into his own veins to survive the job. If that wasn't the definition of a crooked life, Jude didn't know what was.

He finished the cup. Around him the feast resumed, pheasantless, and nobody looked at the thin man in the grey livery who stood slightly apart from all the warmth and light, as he always did, as he always would.

* * *

His rooms were in the Taster's Wing, which was a grand name for four cells above the scullery where the smell of rendered fat never quite left the stone. Jude climbed the back stair two at a time, shed his livery coat, and allowed himself the small ritual that ended every day: he lit the stub of candle on his sill, cupped his hands around it, and whispered the warming-charm his grandmother had taught him before the wardens came for her.

It was nothing. That was the point. A breath of heat, a trick to take the chill off water or ease an ache in the hands—a charm so minor that even the most zealous inquisitor would call it superstition, not sorcery. Everyone in Vell knew someone who knew such tricks. The law drew its line in fire anyway, because lines drawn in anything softer could be argued with.

The flame leaned toward his palms like a cat seeking a scratch. He closed his fingers around the light and held it there, feeling the small pulse of it against his skin, and for a moment—just a moment—he let himself imagine a city where he could do this openly. Where the warmth in his hands was just warmth, and the charm in his wine was medicine instead of contraband, and the loneliness that had been his constant companion for a decade was a choice rather than a sentence.

Then he opened his hands, and the candle burned ordinary again, and the room was cold, and he was alone, which was also ordinary, which was also the whole shape of his life.

He was reaching for his boots when he saw the note.

It lay on the floor beside his bed, square and pale as a moth's wing. Jude went very still. His door was locked—he'd locked it; he always locked it—and the window overlooked a sheer drop to the scullery yard, and yet here was paper where no paper should be, placed precisely where his foot would find it, which meant whoever left it had known exactly where he'd stand.

He crouched. He didn't touch it. Ten years in a palace taught you that gifts from strangers were rarely gifts.

The note was written in a fine, cramped hand, the ink brownish-black, the kind made from oak galls and iron—the old recipe, the one scribes used before the crown standardized its ledgers. Four words and a date:

*Tonight matters. Come alone.*

And beneath them, in figures that seemed almost to smolder against the paper: **the 14th of Emberfall**.

Jude read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because his mind kept snagging on the date the way a boot snags on a loose nail.

The 14th of Emberfall was today.

No—that was impossible. The 14th of Emberfall was a date out of storybooks, out of the one book every child in Vell learned to fear along with their letters. *On the fourteenth of Emberfall, the Crooked Ember shall be rekindled, and the Covenant-Bearer shall stand between the realms.* His grandmother had sung it to him as a lullaby before she understood what singing it might cost her. Every schoolroom in the capital recited it once a year, on Emberfall's eve, as a warning: *this is what happens when men traffic with the hidden realm. This is why we burn them.*

The prophecy was a hundred years old. Its date had come and gone a century ago, unfulfilled, and the priests had spent generations explaining why: the covenant was broken, Ashmoor was sealed, and anyone who whispered otherwise was next for the pyre.

And now someone had slipped a note through a locked door into a locked room to tell a food-taster that tonight mattered.

Jude stood very slowly. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the scullery boys were scrubbing the feast-hall's mountains of dishes, and their clatter rose up through the floor like the heartbeat of the vast machine he served, the machine that would grind him fine the instant he became more useful as an example than as a shield.

*Come alone.*

He crossed to the window and looked down into the yard. Empty. Across the lane, the Saltmarket's late stalls were shuttering, lanterns swinging in the wind off the harbor, and beyond them the palace walls rose black against a sky the color of cooling ash. Somewhere out there, past the ash-choked borderlands that no map honestly charted, lay the realm the kingdom pretended did not exist.

He should burn the note. That was the sensible act, the survival act. Feed it to the candle, watch the date curl and vanish, go to bed, rise tomorrow, serve the king his breakfast, begin year eleven.

Jude Delacroix had survived ten years in a palace by being many things—careful, quiet, forgettable, useful—but he had never once managed to be a coward, and apparently he wasn't going to start tonight.

He folded the note into his boot, pulled his plainest cloak over his shoulders, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. The nightly charm hummed in his blood, steady as a second heart. His grandmother's voice surfaced from thirty years down: *Magic isn't the crime, love. Fear is. They only burn what frightens them.*

"Well," he said to the empty room, to the ten lonely years, to whatever waited in the dark outside. "Let's see what frightens me."

He blew out the candle and went down the back stair, alone, into the wind, toward a date that had died a century before he was born—and that had, somehow, been waiting for him all along.
