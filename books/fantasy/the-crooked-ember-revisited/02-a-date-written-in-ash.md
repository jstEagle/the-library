## 2. A Date Written in Ash

I didn't sleep. That was nothing new—ten years of tasting for a paranoid king had burned most of the sleep out of me—but this was different. This was the kind of not-sleeping where you lie on your back in a rented room above a chandler's shop and watch the candle gutter and think about a stranger's handwriting.

*The ninth of Emberfall. The taster. Come alone.*

The note had been tucked under my wine cup at the feast, folded so small it fit inside a walnut shell. No seal, no signature, no scent of charm about it—which meant whoever wrote it either had no magic or was very, very good at hiding it, and both options frightened me equally.

The ninth of Emberfall was tonight.

I told myself it was a trap. Of course it was a trap. I was the king's food-taster; I had enemies the way a butcher has flies. Someone wanted me lured into a dark room, and the date was bait dressed up as mystery. Any sensible man would burn the note and go back to counting spoons.

But here is what ten years of small illegal magics teaches you: you stop being sensible. You start being *curious*, which is worse. Curiosity is how tasters die—not from poison but from wondering, just once, *what if I didn't swallow the antidote first?*

So at the hour when even the watchmen yawned, I put on my plainest coat, left my silver chain of office coiled on the table like a shed skin, and went out into Vell.

* * *

The address on the note led to Candlewick Row, then off it, down an alley so narrow I had to walk sideways past a wall of stacked crates that smelled of tallow and rat. The apothecary at the end had been shuttered for years—I remembered it closing when I was new to the palace, some scandal about unlicensed simples—and its windows were painted over with a century of grime. The sign above the door read HALLORAN & SONS, REMEDIES AND RESTORATIVES, though the paint had flaked until it looked like the shop itself was dying of something.

The door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It swung inward at my touch with a groan that seemed rehearsed.

Inside: dust, dark, and the ghost-smell of dried herbs so thick I could taste them—bitterwort, feverfew, something older underneath, like rain on hot stone. Shelves climbed the walls into shadow, mostly empty, a few jars still standing sentinel with their contents long evaporated to stained glass. A counter ran along the right side, scarred by knife-marks from a hundred years of chopping root.

And behind the counter, lit by a single shuttered lantern, sat a woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that assessed me the way I assessed food: quickly, thoroughly, and with a professional expectation of finding poison.

"You came," she said.

"You doubted it?"

"I hoped." She stood. She was younger than her voice suggested—thirty, maybe thirty-five—with the kind of tired face that comes from reading too much by bad light. "Jude Delacroix. Crown taster, ten years' service. You dose yourself nightly with a numbing charm against arsenic and a warming one against nightshade, and once a quarter you take something stronger against the rare poisons, the ones with names in old books."

My hand moved toward my knife before my brain caught up. "Who are you?"

"Sera Vane. Formerly scribe of the third archive, formerly copyist to the Lord Chancellor, currently unemployed and legally forbidden to hold a quill." She said it like a recitation she'd grown fond of. "You're wondering how I know your charms."

"I'm wondering several things. That's only the first."

"Then sit, and I'll show you the rest." She lifted the lantern. "Mind the dust. It lies."

I did not sit. I should have—I was exhausted and the floor was right there—but instead I watched her carry the light to the back of the shop, to a door I'd have sworn was a cupboard, and unlock it with a key she wore on a cord around her neck. Beyond it, stairs descended.

"An apothecary with a cellar," I said. "How original."

"Halloran hid more than remedies." She glanced back at me. "I'll be honest with you, Delacroix, because you strike me as a man who's been lied to professionally for a decade and might appreciate the novelty. Everything I show you tonight puts your life in danger. Staying home would have been safer. Coming here and then walking away would be safer still—if you can walk away. So decide now."

Every instinct I owned, all ten years of them, said *turn around*. The king's taster does not follow strangers into cellars. The king's taster goes home, reports nothing, suspects everyone, and lives to be quietly poisoned another day.

I followed her down the stairs.

* * *

The cellar was bigger than the shop above it, which is the sort of thing that shouldn't surprise me anymore but always does. Stone walls, dry air, and shelves—more shelves, these full, crammed with books wrapped in oilcloth and boxes sealed with wax. In the center of the room, on a table draped in black cloth, lay a book.

It was large. That was the first thing. Large the way altars are large, built to a human scale but demanding reverence anyway. Its cover was leather gone dark as dried blood, and across it, stamped in metal that had once been gold, curled a design like a flame that couldn't make up its mind which way to burn.

"The Ember Codex," Sera said. "Written a hundred and six years ago, give or take. Before the Purges. Before magic became a hanging offense with extra steps."

"I know what the Codex is supposed to be," I said. "Everyone knows. It's the story they tell children to scare them straight. A book of prophecies that burned with the last covenant-keeper."

"It didn't burn. It was hidden. Halloran hid it—he was the last of the Order of the Quiet Flame in Vell—and his family kept it until the family ran out, and then it passed to people like me. People who could read old script and keep their mouths shut." She set her hand flat on the cover, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Three nights ago I found a passage I'd never seen. Not because it was hidden. Because I finally understood its dating convention—it uses the old Ashmoor calendar, offset from ours by eleven days. I'd been reading every date wrong since I learned my letters."

She opened the book.

I want to say I felt something—a hum, a warmth, the hair rising on my arms. I didn't. It looked like an old book: vellum gone ivory and brown, ink faded to the color of weak tea, marginalia in at least three hands. Beautiful, yes. The lettering had that pre-Purge precision, each word laid down like masonry. But beautiful isn't the same as true.

"Read it," Sera said, and turned the page around to face me.

I read it. And the cellar got very cold, or I did.

*"On the ninth day of Emberfall, in the hundred-and-sixth year of the Sundering, the Covenant-Bearer comes to the crooked flame. He will be a taster of the crown, a keeper of small charms, a man who has swallowed death a thousand times and lived. His name is written in ash: Jude Delacroix. Through his hand the pact shall be reforged, or through his silence it shall fail forever."*

"That's—" My voice cracked. I started over. "That's a trick. A good one. You found out about the note, you wrote this page, you—"

"Look at the ink. Look at the vellum. Touch it if you don't trust your eyes." She held the book steady. "I've handled forged documents, Delacroix. I made three of them before the Chancellor caught me making the fourth—that's the disgrace, if you're wondering. I know what a century of age looks like and I know what someone faking a century of age looks like. This page is old. Older than both of us. Older than the king's grandfather."

I touched it. The vellum was soft and cool, worn smooth at the edges the way paper gets only from years of turning. The ink had sunk into it, bloomed slightly at the strokes, feathered the way old ink feathers. Nobody fakes feathering. Fakers think of everything except the things that take time.

"Names change," I said, grasping. "Delacroix isn't rare. Taster isn't rare. The ninth of Emberfall happens every year—"

"Not with the old calendar offset. Not in a hundred and six years. And not—" she tapped the page, just below the passage, where smaller script continued, "—not this."

I read the smaller words and stopped breathing for a moment.

*"He will doubt. Doubt is the door. Let him knock."*

"Charming," I managed. "Your prophet had a sense of humor."

"She had accuracy. Which is worse." Sera closed the book partway, keeping one finger in it like a bookmark in a wound. "Here's what I believe, and you can call it madness. A hundred years ago, there was a pact between Vell and Ashmoor—the realm under the ash hills, the one your history books call a fairy story. The pact broke. Nobody says how. Since then, magic in this city has been dying—thinning, going wrong, needing more and more charm for less and less effect. Your little protective spells included. You've noticed, haven't you? Dosing twice as often as you did five years ago?"

I hadn't let myself notice. Now that she said it, I couldn't unnotice it. The warming charm that used to last a week lasting four days. The numbing charm needing a second swallow. I'd blamed my own tolerance, my own aging blood.

"What does that have to do with me?"

"Everything. If the prophecy is true, you're not a victim of the thinning. You're the cure. The Covenant-Bearer." She watched me with those appraising eyes. "Say it's nonsense. Say it aloud and mean it, and I'll pour you a drink and we'll never speak again."

"It's nonsense," I said.

Nothing happened, because of course nothing happened. Words are just words.

But she'd left me the opening, hadn't she? *Let him knock.* And I've never in my life been able to leave a door alone.

"Fine," I said. "Watch."

I took out the little lead token I carried everywhere—the cheapest charm I owned, a cantrip any street witch would sell for two coppers. A light-charm. Child's magic. You whisper the word, pinch the lead, and it glows faintly enough to find a dropped coin in the dark. I'd cast it ten thousand times. It had never done anything worth writing home about.

I whispered the word and pinched the token.

The cellar blazed.

Light poured off my fist like I was holding a piece of the sun—white-gold, roaring, throwing our shadows huge and trembling up the stone walls, lighting shelves I hadn't known were there, lighting Sera's face, which wore an expression I'd never seen on anyone who wasn't watching a miracle or an execution. The jars on the shelves caught the light and threw it back in a hundred fractured pieces. For three heartbeats the whole underground world was noon.

Then it guttered out, and the dark rushed back, and my ears rang with the silence after.

Neither of us spoke. The token in my palm was warm. Warm, and lighter somehow, as if it had given something away.

"That," Sera said finally, very quietly, "hasn't happened in a hundred years."

"It's a strong batch," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Bad storage. Ambient—"

"Delacroix." She said my name the way the codex had, and I flinched. "The prophecy said you would doubt. It didn't say you'd be stupid."

I looked down at my hand. At the dead little token cooling in it. Ten years I'd survived on small magics, rationing myself like a miser, telling myself the thinning was natural, was tolerable, was nobody's fault. And now a book older than my grandmother's grandmother said otherwise. Said the world had a hole in it and I was the patch.

I should have felt chosen. Special. The hero of something.

What I felt was dread—cold and total, rising from the soles of my feet like floodwater. Because I knew how the crown treated useful things. I knew what happened to men who became important. I'd watched it from the safest seat in the kingdom, the one beside the king's plate.

"If I'm what this book says," I heard myself ask, "then what am I supposed to do?"

Sera turned the page. Where the passage ended, the vellum was ragged—torn, deliberately, close to the spine, three clean wounds in a row where leaves had been ripped away.

"That," she said, "is the problem. Three pages are missing. And I think I know who took them—and why they'll kill to keep them lost."
