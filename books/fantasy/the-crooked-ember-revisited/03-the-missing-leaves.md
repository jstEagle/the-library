## 3. The Missing Leaves

Sera Vane had learned, in the years since the court cast her out, that the truth was a thing you fed slowly. Too much at once and people choked on it. She watched Jude Delacroix across the apothecary's scarred counter—watched him turn the Ember Codex's pages with the careful hands of a man who handled poison for a living—and she rationed.

He had read his own name in ink a century old. He had read tonight's date. And still he stood there with his jaw set like a man waiting for the punchline.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you're thinking."

"I'm thinking this is an elaborate way to rob me." He didn't look up. "Or recruit me. Or kill me. In this city those are usually the same business model."

"You're not wrong to be careful." Sera pulled the lamp closer. Its light caught the codex's cover—black leather gone grey at the edges, cracked like riverbed clay. "But look at the marginalia. Look at the hand that wrote your name."

He looked. She let him.

The script was small and slanted, the letters leaning into each other like conspirators. Ashmoor cursive, though no one in Vell would recognize it; the script of a realm that officially did not exist, written by scribes who had been burning officially for a hundred years.

"This is a forgery," Jude said finally. "A good one. But the ink—"

"Smell it."

He hesitated, then bent over the page. His nostrils flared. Sera saw the exact moment he caught it: woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, like apples left too long in a cellar. Ember-ink. It was made from ash and honey and things she'd never been permitted to know, and it never fully dried. A century later, it still breathed.

"Forgeries don't smell," she said quietly. "Forgeries don't *wait*."

Jude straightened. His hands had gone very still on the counter, the way they went still, she suspected, when he was swallowing fear. Ten years as the king's taster would teach a man that trick.

"Even if it's real," he said, "even if some scribe wrote my name before my grandmother was born—that doesn't make it true. Prophecy is a con artist's favorite tool. You tell someone what they're destined to do, and they do it, and then everyone marvels at the accuracy."

"That's exactly what Corvane wants you to think."

His head came up sharply. "I haven't mentioned Corvane."

"You didn't have to." Sera met his eyes. "You're the crown's taster. The spymaster owns every servant in that palace the way a farmer owns fields. If he hasn't noticed you yet, it's because you're not interesting enough. That will change."

* * *

She took him down into the cellar.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of tallow and dried yarrow, and at the bottom, behind a shelf of useless tonics, was a door that hadn't existed when the building was a legitimate shop. Sera spoke the old word—*ilven*, which meant both *open* and *remember*—and the door unmade itself into a rectangle of cold air.

Beyond it: a room the size of a coffin, lit by a single stub of candle, its walls papered with documents. Confession transcripts. Execution orders. A child's drawing of a woman with flame-colored hair, labeled *Mama, burned Tuesday*. Forty years of the Quiet Flame's patient, desperate bookkeeping.

"What is this?" Jude's voice had changed. Lower. Careful.

"The part of the story I couldn't tell you upstairs." Sera lifted a lantern and turned so he could see all of it at once—the sheer weight of it, the accumulated evidence of a century of fire. "This is why I was disgraced. This is why I'll probably die in a room like this one day. Because I keep records, Jude Delacroix, and records are the one magic the crown can't burn out of existence."

He moved along the wall slowly, reading. She watched him stop at the execution orders. Watched his shoulders change.

"How many?" he asked.

"In Vell? Four hundred and eleven since the covenant broke. That we know of. There are villages where the count simply stops, because there was no one left to write it down." She kept her voice level. It had taken her years to manage that. "You testified at some of those trials. I've read your depositions. Very precise. Very damning."

"I said what the court required me to say."

"Yes. And the court required it because Lord Corvane told them to require it." She let that sit. "Do you want to know what actually happened forty years ago? Not the version in the histories?"

* * *

She told him standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender.

Forty years ago, the pact between Vell and Ashmoor had been sealed at a stone beneath the palace foundations—the Crooked Ember, a fragment of the old realm's first fire, kept alive by mutual oath. Magic flowed legally between the realms. Healers crossed the border freely. Children born with the gift were trained, not tried.

And then the Ember went dark. Officially, it guttered on its own—a natural death, the histories said, the old faith simply expiring like an old man.

"But covenants don't die of age," Sera said. "They die of murder. Three pages were torn from the Ember Codex the same night the Ember failed. The rite of renewal. The names of the bound. And the confession-rite—the mechanism by which a broken covenant could be exposed and repaired. Without those pages, the Quiet Flame could prove nothing. Accuse nothing. We've spent forty years searching for them while our people burned."

"And you think I can find them." His laugh was short and bitter. "I taste soup for a living."

"I think the Codex named you Covenant-Bearer a century ago, and I think the charm you cast upstairs—" she had seen it through the floorboards, a flare of light bright enough to leak through cracks, "—burned like a bonfire when you touched this book. I think the prophecy doesn't care what you do for a living. It cares what you're willing to do now."

He turned away from her. Studied the child's drawing on the wall. When he spoke again, his voice was rougher.

"Why me? Why not one of your faithful? Someone who believes."

"Because belief isn't the requirement." Sera stepped beside him. "I've read the rite fragments we do have. The covenant answers to choice, not faith. It needed someone with no stake in either realm—someone who could walk into the palace and out of the borderlands without anyone marking him. Someone invisible." She paused. "Someone who has already learned to survive by doing small illegal things every single day, and hating himself a little more each time."

That landed. She saw it land, saw his jaw tighten around something he didn't say.

"And if I refuse?" he asked.

"Then I'll show you the door, ward the apothecary behind you, and go on losing people at the rate of one every few weeks until they come for me too." She shrugged, and made herself mean it. "I won't lie to you, taster. I need you. But the prophecy doesn't. It waited a century. It can wait for a better candidate."

* * *

It was the promise that broke him. She knew it would be—it was the only coin she had left worth spending, and she hated spending it, because hope was crueler than any threat.

"If the covenant is reforged," she said, "the old law dies with the new oath. Every convicted witch in Vell—every living prisoner rotting in the palace cells, every branded survivor hiding in the Saltmarket—their sentences void. Pardoned. Not by royal mercy, which is a lottery, but by treaty. By law older than the crown itself." She held his gaze. "There are thirty-one people in the dungeons right now awaiting trial. Some of them were named in testimony."

She didn't say *your testimony*. She didn't have to. She watched him do the arithmetic himself, watch it land in his face like a blow.

"My sister," he said, after a long silence. "They took her two years ago. Charm-craft. She got eight years in the cells instead of fire because a guard owed my mother a favor." His voice was flat, stripped of everything. "I send money when I can. It buys her blankets."

Sera said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't cruelty dressed as comfort.

Jude turned back to the codex, lying open on the counter above them, and stared at his own name for a long time. The candle guttered. Somewhere overhead, a cart rattled past on the Saltmarket cobbles, ordinary as bread.

"Three pages," he said at last. "Where do we start?"

Relief hit her so hard she nearly swayed. Sera had prepared three speeches for this moment—pleading, reasoning, shaming—and now none of them were needed, and she was absurdly grateful, and she buried it fast because hope was a thing you protected by not showing it.

"Not where," she said. "*Who.* The Order hid the pages separately, in the hands of different keepers, decades ago. Most of those keepers are dead. One of them might not be." She reached into her coat and drew out a scrap of vellum, charred at one corner, salvaged from a burned safehouse two winters past. On it, in a dying hand: *the salt-wife remembers*. "Her name was Maren Ostwick. Sold charms out of a stall in the Saltmarket until last month."

Something cold slid down Sera's spine even as she said it.

"Until last month?"

"She died," Sera said carefully. "A stall fire. The guards ruled it accidental."

Jude had gone very still again. "Charm-sellers don't die in stall fires," he said. "We're careful with fire. It's the first thing they teach us."

"I know," Sera said. "Which means either the trail is dead—" she folded the vellum away, "—or someone else is reading the same prophecy we are, and they've decided you should never reach the second page. We leave for the borderlands tomorrow. Before whoever killed Maren decides apothecaries burn just as easily as stalls."

Jude looked at her, and then at the walls of the dead, and Sera saw the last of his disbelief go out—not extinguished, but consumed, the way kindling becomes flame.

"All right," he said. "Tomorrow."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the lamp by the codex flickered once—bright, brief, glad—and went steady again.
