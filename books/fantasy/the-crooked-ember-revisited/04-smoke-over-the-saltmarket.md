## 4. Smoke Over the Saltmarket

The fire had been out for hours by the time Jude reached the Saltmarket, but the smell of it still hung over the stalls like a held breath—char and tallow and something sweeter underneath that he refused to name. He knew that sweetness. He'd smelled it on condemned prisoners. It was the smell of burned charmwork, of magic cooking out of the world.

He should have been on the road east already. Sera had said tomorrow, and tomorrow had come gray and cold, and then a boy had found him at the apothecary door with a scrap of paper and no memory of who'd paid him to carry it: *They got to Oskar first.*

Oskar Ferren. Charm-seller, third stall from the fishmongers' row, the man whose name Sera had pried out of Maren's ledger two nights ago—the last living link to the Order of the Quiet Flame, the man who might know where the second page slept.

Jude pushed through the crowd gathered at the edge of the market square. They stood in the particular silence Vell reserved for fires—not grief, exactly, not yet, but the hungry hush of people waiting to be told what to feel. The guards had already strung their rope. Already decided.

The stall itself was a black skeleton. Oskar's awning had collapsed inward, folding the little booth around its owner like a shroud, and beneath it lay what was left of him. Jude had seen bodies before. Ten years as a food-taster meant he'd seen men die at table, mid-laugh, poison blooming in their faces while he sat three chairs down counting his own heartbeats. But this was different. This was a man made into an argument—a warning written in flesh for anyone else who might remember the old faith.

A guard sergeant stood at the rope line, hands clasped behind his back, delivering the verdict to anyone who'd listen.

"Careless with his lamp, this one. Kept oil under the counter. Whole thing went up in a breath." He shook his head with practiced sorrow. "Tragic. But the market's full of fools."

Jude drifted closer, wearing the face he wore at court—the mild, slightly dull expression of a servant who was nobody. It was the most useful charm he owned, and it cost nothing but practice.

"Oil," he said quietly, to no one. "In a charm-seller's stall."

The sergeant's eyes flicked to him. "Something to say, friend?"

"Just thinking how fast it must have gone." Jude nodded toward the ruin. "Fast enough that he didn't even try to run."

"They say it was quick." The sergeant's voice flattened. "That's a mercy, isn't it?"

It wasn't a question. It was a door closing.

Jude looked past him at the stall again, and now he let himself actually see it, the way he'd been trained to see—ten years of tasting had taught him that death leaves signatures, that poison has a handwriting all its own. Fire did too.

The burn pattern was wrong. Oil fires spread outward, low and greedy, licking up from the floor. This burn had started *above* the counter, at head height, and driven downward and inward, a fist closing around the center of the stall. And the stone flags beneath the wreckage were scorched in a circle too neat, too symmetrical, for any accident. That was a contained burn. A shaped one. Someone had stood in front of Oskar Ferren and opened a flame in the air between them, and held it there while the man burned.

Jude had seen that kind of fire once before—from the far side of a scaffold, when he was nineteen and the crown still made witches' executions a public spectacle. The royal pyromancers used a controlled flame. Clean. Efficient. *Deniable*, if you were the one who paid for it.

"You're staring," the sergeant said.

"I'm grieving," Jude said. "I bought charms from him. Little ones. Against headaches." He let his shoulders slump into servility. "I didn't know it was dangerous."

"Nothing dangerous about a dead man." The sergeant turned away, dismissing him, and raised his voice for the crowd. "Accidental fire! Clear out, all of you. Nothing here but ash."

*Nothing here but ash.* Jude thought of the codex, of pages torn out and hidden, of a covenant broken a century ago and a trail being swept clean link by link—Maren the apothecary, and now Oskar the charm-seller. Someone was not merely hiding the path. Someone was erasing it backward, methodically, the way you'd erase your own footprints in snow. And they were moving faster than Sera and he could walk it.

He slipped away from the rope line and found her in the shadow of the fishmongers' arcade, hood up, watching the crowd with the flat attention of a woman memorizing faces.

"Well?" she said.

"It wasn't an accident. The burn pattern's wrong. Controlled flame, centered above the counter—he never had a chance to move." Jude kept his voice below the noise of the market. "And the guards ruled it accidental before the embers cooled. Either they're stupid or they're paid, and guards who lie that smoothly aren't cheap."

Sera was quiet a moment. "Corvane."

"Or someone who wants us to think Corvane." She pulled him deeper into the arcade, out of sightline of the square. "Listen to me. There's something I've been turning over since Maren died, and I need you to hear it without flinching."

"I taste poison for a living, Sera. Flinching is a luxury."

"Maren knew about the codex. Knew enough to point us to Oskar. Oskar knew enough to matter. Both of them are dead within a week of each other—and yet here we stand, the two people who actually have the codex, untouched." Her eyes were steady on his. "Why?"

Jude felt the answer arrive before he could refuse it, cold sliding down his spine like water finding a seam.

"Because we're being allowed," he said slowly. "Because whoever's doing this doesn't want me dead. They want me *moving*. Following the trail. Finding the pages." His mouth had gone dry. "They can't search for the Quiet Flame's caches themselves without revealing what they're looking for. But a prophecy-named Covenant-Bearer walking the old roads—that draws the faithful out of hiding. Every keeper, every sympathizer, every hidden page comes to *him*."

"And then," Sera finished softly, "whoever holds his leash takes everything he gathers."

The market noise seemed very far away. Jude looked back through the arcade pillars at the blackened stall, at the guards roping off a dead man's life, and understood the shape of the trap he'd been standing inside for days without seeing it. His immunity—his precious, illegal, carefully hoarded immunity, the small charms that had kept him alive through a decade of poisoned cups—wasn't protection. It was a feature. A tool built into him by people who had never asked his permission. The crown had spent ten years using his body as an instrument, dosing him, testing him, keeping him alive because a dead taster was useless. Now someone else had found a better use for the same instrument.

Useful. And disposable, the moment usefulness ended. Oskar Ferren had been disposable. Maren had been disposable. The only difference between them and Jude was that Jude hadn't finished being useful yet.

"Sera." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "When we find Mother Ilex. When we find the page. Who do we tell? Who do we trust?"

"No one," she said. "We tell no one, we show no one, and we stop following the trail like good little bloodhounds." She pulled the folded vellum from her coat—the notes she'd copied from the codex—and held it between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "From tonight, we don't follow the prophecy. We get ahead of it. We decide where we go and who knows it, and we change our road so often that whatever's hunting us loses the scent."

"And if the prophecy needs me somewhere specific? If the rite only works at the covenant-stone, on a certain night?"

"Then we arrive unannounced, unrehearsed, and unwelcomed by everyone except the Ember itself." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, sharp as a paper cut. "Faith was never supposed to be comfortable, Jude. That's how you know it's real."

Above them, smoke still rose from the Saltmarket in a thin gray thread, bending east on the wind—east, toward the borderlands, toward Ashmoor, as if the fire itself were pointing the way. Jude watched it go and made a decision he suspected he'd have cause to regret.

"We leave within the hour," he said. "Not tomorrow. Not by the east gate, either—I'll buy passage on a smuggler's barge down the canal, the way charm-runners travel. If Corvane's people are watching the roads, let them watch empty ones."

Sera raised an eyebrow. "You know smugglers?"

"I *am* a smuggler," Jude said. "I've been smuggling charms into my own bloodstream for ten years. I know every man on the canal who asks no questions, because I've paid most of them." He turned toward the water, then stopped. One more thing. He looked back at the ruined stall, at the place where Oskar Ferren had sold headache-charms and hope to a city that punished both.

"He didn't die for a lamp left burning," Jude said quietly. "Someone should say it. Even if it's just us."

Sera bowed her head. For a moment the two of them stood in the fish-smelling dark of the arcade, two outcasts saying a dead man's name against a city that wanted it forgotten.

Then a bell began to ring from the palace hill—one long stroke, then another, the cadence Jude knew too well. Not fire alarm. Not curfew.

*A summons.* The crown calling its servants home.

Jude went still. Tasters weren't summoned by bell. Tasters were summoned by note, by whisper, by the quiet machinery of the household. Bells were for matters of state.

Sera read his face. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Jude said, "that either the king has heard about the fire—or the king's spymaster wants everyone in Vell to believe he has." He pulled his collar up and stepped out of the arcade, toward the sound of the bell, toward the palace that had owned him for a decade. "Stay out of sight. I'll find you at the canal by moonrise."

"And if you're not there?"

Jude paused at the edge of the light.

"Then burn my notes," he said, "and finish it without me."
