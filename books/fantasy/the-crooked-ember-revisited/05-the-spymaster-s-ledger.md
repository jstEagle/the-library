## 5. The Spymaster's Ledger

The palace archive smelled of dust, tallow, and old lies.

Jude had walked past its iron-bound doors a hundred times in ten years of service, and in all that time he had never once seen them open. The archivists, such as they were, entered through a side passage off the servants' stair, and everyone below the rank of undersecretary pretended the room did not exist. That was the palace's whole architecture, Jude had learned: not walls, but pretenses. You could walk through most of them if you walked like you belonged.

Tonight he belonged. Tonight he carried a tray.

The king's private supper had been a small affair—oysters in aspic, a haunch of venison, wine from the Ashmoor border vineyards that hadn't been tended by human hands in a generation. Jude had tasted everything, as he always did, and slipped his charm into the second cup, as he always did, and felt the familiar cold thread of it settle under his tongue. The king had eaten well and gone to bed. Jude had cleared the tray himself, which was beneath his station, which was the point. No one questioned a man doing his own work.

The side passage was unguarded at the change of the watch. He had counted the gaps for three nights, standing in the dark of the stairwell with his ear to the stone, and the gap was eleven minutes long, and he needed eight.

He gave himself six.

The archive was a long vaulted room, shelves rising into shadow, candles burned down to stubs that someone had never bothered to replace. A ledger table sat in the center under a brass lamp, and beyond it, at the far end, a door of black oak with a lock that was newer than everything around it. Corvane's private collection. The spymaster kept his own keys and his own hours, and the servants said the room was cold even in summer, and the servants said a lot of things they shouldn't, which was why most of them didn't last.

Jude set the tray down and went to work.

He had a system, because Sera had drilled one into him across three feverish nights in the apothecary's back room. *Don't read everything. Read the shape of things. A man's guilt has a shape—the way his hand changes when he writes money he shouldn't, the way the ink darkens when he presses harder on the entries he hates.* Sera had been a court scribe for nine years before her disgrace. She knew the way powerful men's hands trembled.

*Start with the years after the covenant broke,* she'd said. *Everyone looks at the night itself. Corvane won't keep the night itself where anyone could find it. But a man who breaks a kingdom has to pay for the pieces. Follow the money.*

The archive's general ledgers were chained to the table—inventory, provisioning, the endless accounting of a palace that fed three hundred people and lied to all of them. Jude turned the pages fast, not reading, scanning. His fingers were steady. He noticed that about himself and was faintly surprised. Somewhere between the burned stall in the Saltmarket and the memory-rites at the border, the fear had changed its flavor. It was no longer the fear of a man who might be caught. It was the fear of a man who had already seen what happened to people who stopped.

*Follow the money.*

There. The year of the breaking—forty-one years past. A disbursement column that didn't match its own summary. Small sums, recurring, entered under a heading that read only *CONTINGENCIES—BY ORDER OF L.C.* And the summary total was always higher than the sum of the entries, by amounts that varied, by amounts that someone had clearly wanted lost in the noise.

Jude pulled a stub of charcoal and a scrap of paper from his sleeve and copied the figures. His hand did not shake. He copied the dates, too, and noticed the pattern Sera had promised he would find: every disbursement fell within a week of a burning. He knew the burnings. They were in the public record, the ones the crown had bothered to make public—witch-fires in the Saltmarket, in the Yards, once in the palace courtyard itself, a woman whose name Jude had eaten dinner over for years without knowing he'd swallowed her ash.

*CONTINGENCIES.* Payment for firewood, probably. Payment for the guards who looked the other way. Payment for the witnesses who lied.

The black oak door was locked, but locks were a language, and Jude had spent a decade learning the small illegal grammar of things. He knelt, breathed a thread of charm into the keyhole—a warming cantrip, the gentlest thing he knew, the kind that wouldn't even register as magic unless you were looking—and felt the tumblers soften like wax in sun. The charm flared brighter than it should have. It had been doing that since the apothecary. Since the codex had said his name.

*The Ember knows you're coming,* Sera had said, half laughing, half afraid. *Everything you do is a little louder now.*

The door opened without a sound.

Corvane's private room was small, and cold, and it did not smell of dust. It smelled of ink and iron. One desk, one lamp, one shelf—and on the shelf, a ledger bound in grey leather with no title at all. It sat alone, the way a weapon sits alone.

Jude told himself he had time. He had maybe ninety seconds before the watch completed its circuit, and he needed to be back on the servants' stair with his tray and his blank, useful face. Ninety seconds to read forty years of a spymaster's sins.

He opened the ledger.

The first page was a list of names. Not victims—*payees*. Men and women who had received coin from the office of the Spymaster of Vell, each name followed by a service rendered in Corvane's tidy, unhesitating hand. *Informant, Saltmarket. Informant, temple district. Arsonist (deniable). Arsonist (deniable). Scribe, records falsified.* Page after page. A kingdom's conscience, itemized.

Then, further in, the entries changed. The sums grew larger. The services grew shorter, and crueler, and were written in the same hand with the same steadiness, which was somehow the worst thing Jude had ever read: *Keeper of the western gate, silenced. Ashmoor envoy, turned back. Covenant-stone, warded against repair.* And then, on a page dated forty-one years and one month past, a single entry that took up more room than any other in the book, as if Corvane's hand had finally allowed itself weight:

*The pact is broken. The realms will forget each other, and in the forgetting, all power passes to the crown. Let them call it fate. Fate is the cheapest lie, for no one audits it.*

Jude stood very still in the cold room with the ledger open in his hands, and he understood, all at once and completely, that he was not looking at a crime. He was looking at a *policy*. Forty years of burnings and bribes and borders sealed in ash—not a spymaster's lapse but a spymaster's *design*, executed with the patience of a man planting an orchard he intended to harvest in his old age. The Quiet Flame hunted and scattered. The convicted witches rotted in the Yards. The Crooked Ember guttered unattended beneath the palace, and the man who had snuffed it collected a salary every quarter-day and dined with the king.

Jude's hands were shaking now. He let them. He copied the entry word for word, the dates, the payees, the page numbers, and he folded the paper into the lining of his sleeve where the charcoal couldn't smudge it, and he was reaching to close the ledger when the lamp behind him went out.

Not guttered. *Went out*, all at once, the way a candle goes out when a hand closes over it.

"I did wonder," said Lord Corvane, from the doorway, "when you would come."

Jude did not turn around. Turning around was a confession. He stood in the dark with his back to the spymaster of Vell and made his breathing slow and stupid, the breathing of a servant who has gotten lost.

"The tray, my lord," he said. "I took a wrong turning. The servants' stair—"

"Is behind you, yes, and has been for ten years, and you have never once taken a wrong turning in it. You are the most reliable man in this palace, Jude Delacroix. That is precisely why I hired you." A match scraped. Lamplight bloomed again, soft and gold and terrible. "And why I have been waiting for you to make yourself useful in a rather different way."

Jude turned.

Corvane was not what the rumors made him. The rumors said gaunt, said grey, said a man worn thin by other people's secrets. In truth he was broad and comfortable, silver at the temples, with the open, weathered face of a country gentleman—the face of a man you would trust with your horse, your daughter, your kingdom. He held a second lamp in one hand and the black oak door stood open behind him, and he was looking at Jude with an expression of mild, patient interest, the way a man looks at a clock he has wound and is now content to watch.

"You found the general ledgers," Corvane said. "The contingencies. Clever. Most people go straight for the oak door, and the oak door is a lesson, not a lock." He set his lamp on the desk, unhurried. "The grey ledger, of course, is the real one. You've had your look. I find it's better that way. A man who has seen the truth and walks away anyway—that man is *mine* forever. Much more useful than a man who doesn't know."

"I don't know what you think I've seen, my lord. I'm a taster. I read menus."

"Yes." Corvane smiled, and it was the warmest thing in the cold room. "You taste. You taste everything, don't you, Jude? Every cup, every dish, every danger meant for another man's throat. Ten years of swallowing poison on a king's behalf, dosed with your little charms, breaking the law in a hundred small ways before breakfast—and never once thanked, never once pardoned, never once *safe*. Do you know what I admire about you? Your loyalty. It's real. It has survived on nothing at all."

He stepped closer. He smelled of ink and iron, exactly like his room.

"So here is what happens now," Corvane said pleasantly. "Tomorrow morning, the king will announce a new office: Taster-General. Authority over all provisioning, all household security, a salary you could not spend, and—this is the part I imagine interests you—a full pardon. Signed. Sealed. For every charm you have ever cast, every law you have ever bent, every small illegal thing you have ever done to stay alive." He paused, letting the word *pardon* sit in the air between them like a coin on a table. "You would be the first man in the history of Vell legalized for his magic. A title. A future. Belonging, Jude, at last, in the only house that matters."

"And in return."

"You'll bring me the pages." Corvane said it the way he said everything—without hurry, without heat, as if the outcome were a formality the universe had already notarized. "The torn leaves of the Ember Codex. You know the ones. Your little scribe friend knows the ones. Deliver them to me, and the pardon is yours, and your friends in the Quiet Flame will simply never be found. Refuse—" he spread his broad, trustworthy hands, "—and I will have to conclude that the crown's most loyal servant has been a traitor all along. And the fire, Jude, will need someone. It always does. It's very hungry. It's been fed on the innocent for forty years, and I confess I have grown curious what it makes of the guilty."

The room was silent. Somewhere above them, the palace went on sleeping, three hundred souls warm in their beds because a man with a tray had tasted their supper.

Jude bowed. Low, smooth, the bow of a servant receiving an honor. "I am the king's taster, my lord," he said. "I go where I'm pointed."

"I know," said Corvane, smiling his country-gentleman smile. "That's rather the point. You have until the next quarter-day."

Jude carried his tray back up the servants' stair with a steady hand and a face like blank paper, and it was not until he reached the apothecary, and Sera saw his eyes, and Mother Ilex's charm went cold in his pocket like a warning—that he let himself say it aloud:

"He didn't search me. He didn't take the ledger's page from my sleeve. He *let me keep it*."

Sera went white. "Why would he—"

"Because a pardon needs a traitor to pardon," Jude said. "And because he wants me to run. He wants the Quiet Flame to gather around me. He wants every name in one room." He unfolded the charcoal copy of the grey ledger, and his voice, when it came again, was quiet and absolutely certain. "So we'll give him one. But it won't be the room he thinks."
