## 7. The Hollow Crown's Bargain

The summons came at dawn, folded inside the morning's tasting schedule like a knife wrapped in linen.

*Lord Corvane requests the taster's presence. Private chambers. Before the king wakes.*

Jude read it three times, then held it over his candle and watched it curl to ash. Ten years of service had taught him what a private summons from the spymaster meant. It meant someone was about to be promoted, or someone was about to disappear, and the difference between those two fates usually came down to how well a man could lie in the first thirty seconds.

He dressed carefully. Not his finest coat—that would look like ambition—but not his workaday wool either. Something forgettable. A man who looked like he belonged in any room and stood out in none.

Sera was still asleep on the pallet by the apothecary's cold hearth, her scribe's calluses curled against her cheek, ink stains ghosting her fingers even in sleep. Mother Ilex's memory-rite still sat heavy in Jude's chest—the weight of his own confession, the prisoners he'd condemned with a nod and a swallow, finally spoken aloud where something ancient could witness it. He'd thought that confession would lighten him. Instead it had made every choice since feel heavier, because now he knew exactly what he was capable of.

He crouched beside Sera and touched her shoulder.

She woke the way scribes woke—instantly, eyes sharp before her body caught up. "What time is it?"

"Early. Corvane wants me."

Her face went pale in the grey light. "Does he know?"

"If he knew everything, I'd already be in irons." Jude kept his voice low and steady, though his pulse disagreed. "This might be nothing. A test of loyalty. Spymasters collect loyalty the way misers collect coins—they count it nightly."

"Jude." She caught his wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "If he offers you anything—a title, gold, pardon—take it. Take whatever he gives you and smile while you do it."

"That sounds like advice for a man planning to betray someone."

"It's advice for a man planning to survive long enough to win." She pressed something into his palm: a sliver of dried rowan bark, thin as a wafer, marked with a sigil in silverpoint. "Chew it if you're taken. It'll mask your thoughts from scrying for an hour. And—" she hesitated, then pulled a scrap of paper from her sleeve, already written, already sealed with the Quiet Flame's crooked-ember mark, "—if you get the chance, if there's any way at all, get this to me. One line. That's all I need. One line saying you're alive and playing along, and the Order will know to hold position instead of scattering."

Jude looked at the note. *Hold position.* Meaning: don't run. Meaning: trust him.

"You realize," he said, "that if Corvane searches me, this kills us all."

"Then don't get searched." Sera almost smiled. "You've survived ten years of poisoners. Survive one bureaucrat."

He tucked the note into the lining of his boot, flat against the leather where a pat-down would miss it, and went out into the waking city.

* * *

Corvane's private chambers sat above the Hall of Whispers, and everything about them was designed to make visitors feel the height. The windows overlooked the palace courtyard, where the burn-stake stood as a permanent fixture, its base blackened by decades of use. The spymaster had chosen his view deliberately. Jude was certain of it.

Lord Aldous Corvane was smaller than his reputation. Grey, neat, precise, with the mild face of a librarian and hands that never stopped moving—adjusting a quill, squaring papers, aligning objects on his desk until the world matched some internal grid only he could see. He did not rise when Jude entered. He simply gestured to a chair positioned exactly opposite him, at a distance calculated for conversation without intimacy.

"Taster Delacroix." His voice was soft. Soft men had frightened Jude more than loud ones ever had. "Ten years of service. Do you know how rare that is? Most tasters last four. Five, if they're careful."

"I'm very careful, my lord."

"Yes." Corvane aligned a letter opener with the edge of his blotter. "You are. That's what interests me."

Jude sat. He let his hands rest loose in his lap, the posture of a man with nothing to hide. Under his boot sole, the note pressed against his ankle like a second heartbeat.

"The king trusts you," Corvane continued. "More than he trusts most of his council, which says less about your virtues than about his council. You taste for him nightly. You sample his charms—" and here the mild eyes lifted, and Jude felt the floor tilt beneath the words, "—his *little* charms. The ones that keep a body immune to things it shouldn't be immune to. Tell me, Delacroix. Where does a palace food-taster learn to dose himself with hedge-magic without burning for it?"

Every exit in the room sealed itself in Jude's mind. There were two doors, four guards in the corridor, and a window overlooking the stake. He chose the oldest trick in a taster's book: tell the truth until the lie is needed.

"I learned it the way everyone in the Saltmarket learns it," he said. "From a charm-seller who charged too much and taught too little. I've been careful for ten years, my lord. Careful enough that no one has cared."

"No one has *wanted* to care." Corvane rose and crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back. Below, a servant swept ash from the flagstones near the stake—ash that never fully left. "But circumstances change. There is a matter of state security, and your particular talents have become relevant to it."

Here it comes, Jude thought. He kept his breathing even, his face attentive, the face of a simple man trying to follow complicated words.

"A century ago," Corvane said, "the crown maintained certain... arrangements. Understandings with parties beyond our borders. Those arrangements ended. They should have stayed ended. But recently, documents have surfaced—old documents, forged or fantasized, I haven't determined which—that suggest these arrangements could be *renewed*. By a specific individual. At a specific time." He turned. "You can imagine my concern when the specific date in question happens to fall within the month."

"My lord, I don't—"

"You were seen." Corvane's voice didn't rise at all; that was somehow worse. "At the Saltmarket, speaking with a disgraced court scribe. In the borderlands, in the company of an exiled Ashmoor witch older than the treaty itself. My agents are thorough, Delacroix. They watch the people who watch the king's food."

The room narrowed. Jude became aware of his own heartbeat, of the note against his ankle, of the rowan bark in his inner pocket. *Chew it if you're taken.* Was he taken? Or was this still the space between?

"I was doing my job," Jude said quietly. "A taster hears things. Poison doesn't always come through the mouth, my lord. Sometimes it comes through rumor, through old books, through desperate people looking for miracles. I thought the crown would want to know who was asking about covenant-stones and prophecy. So I listened. I listened very carefully."

Silence. Corvane studied him the way a jeweler studies a stone, looking for the flaw, the fracture line, the place where pressure would split it clean.

"That," Corvane said at last, "is either the truth or the best lie I've heard this year. Fortunately for you, I don't need to know which. Because either way, your usefulness is identical."

He returned to his desk and drew out a document. Heavy paper. Wax seal unbroken. He laid it flat and turned it so Jude could read it upside down, which was its own kind of insult—or invitation.

"A full pardon," Corvane said. "Retroactive. Every charm you've ever swallowed, every law you've ever broken, erased as though it never happened. Two hundred crowns a year for life. A residence in the Keeper's Quarter. And a title, if you want one—Taster Royal has a ring to it, don't you think? Your mother would have been proud."

Jude's blood went cold at the name. His mother, dead twelve years, burned in everything but official record—officially she'd died of fever, officially, officially. Corvane knew. Corvane had always known.

"All I require," the spymaster continued, folding his hands, "is the codex pages. Three leaves, currently in the possession of the Order of the Quiet Flame. Deliver them to me, intact, and sign this, and you walk away whole. Wealthy, even. Respected."

"And if I can't find them?"

Corvane smiled. It transformed his face into something patient and terrible. "Then I find them myself, and I find the people hiding them, and the crown does what the crown does with witches. The scribe Vane. The keeper Ilex. Their little congregation of smugglers and hedge-priests. There's a stake in the courtyard, Delacroix. It doesn't care whose crimes it burns. Only yours would be pardoned."

There it was. The bargain laid bare—not wealth offered, but lives ransomed. Jude looked at the pardon, at the spymaster's mild waiting face, and felt something settle in him that wasn't fear. It was colder and steadier than fear. It was the clarity that comes when a man stops pretending he has good options and starts choosing among bad ones.

*Take whatever he gives you,* Sera had said. *Smile while you do it.*

"I'll need time," Jude said. "And I'll need their trust. If I go to them empty-handed, they'll scatter, and you'll get nothing but ashes—forgive the phrase. Give me six days. Let me work them the way I've worked everyone else for ten years. I'll bring you the pages myself."

Corvane considered. Somewhere below, the sweeper moved on, and the courtyard lay clean and black and ready.

"Five days," Corvane said. "And Delacroix—if you're lying to me, know that I will know. I always know. The last man who lied to me in this room is now a footnote in a ledger no one reads." He slid the pardon across the desk, unsigned. "Sign when you deliver. Not before. I want your motivation... fresh."

Jude stood, bowed the precise depth owed to a lord, and walked to the door with legs that felt borrowed.

"One more thing," Corvane said, without looking up from his papers. "Your little immunity charms. I'd stop taking them, if I were you. A man planning to serve the crown honestly has no need of illegal magic. And I'd hate for a search to turn up... contraband."

The door closed behind Jude with a sound like a verdict.

* * *

He made it three streets before his hands started shaking. He ducked into the shadow of a dye-house, pressed his back to the wet brick, and breathed until the shaking slowed. Then he took out Sera's note, read it once—*hold position*—and did the hardest thing he'd done in ten years of hard things.

He altered it.

He couldn't pass it along unchanged. *Hold position* was written for a world where Jude was free; in Corvane's world, holding position meant standing in a field the spymaster had already mapped. So he borrowed a pencil stub from a fishmonger's boy for a copper bit, and beneath the Quiet Flame's seal, in handwriting he disguised as his own disguised hand—layers within layers—he added a single line:

*Five days. He watches me. Move the third page tonight. Trust the plan, not the man.*

He sealed it again with wax softened over a match, pressing the crooked ember deep enough to hide the tampering, and spent the afternoon becoming invisible. A taster's greatest skill was never the palate—it was the passing-through, the being-seen-and-forgotten. He used it now, threading through the Saltmarket's morning crush, past the gutted stall where the charm-seller had burned, past the shrine-niche where someone had left fresh rowan sprigs despite the law, down to the tannery row where Sera kept her second lodging.

He didn't go in. Going in was how tails followed. Instead he bought a meat pie from the vendor who worked the corner—a Quiet Flame sympathizer with flour-white forearms—and slipped the note under the pie's bottom crust the way he'd once slipped charms into wine. Old skills. All of it old skills, repurposed.

"Heating instructions," Jude said. "For a friend. Tell her the oven runs hot."

The vendor's eyes flickered—understanding passing between them like a coin—and the pie vanished into a basket beneath three others.

By dusk, word would reach Sera. By midnight, the third page would be moving through smugglers' tunnels toward a hiding place even Corvane's thoroughness hadn't found. Jude had bought his friends five days and a head start, using the only currency he had left: Corvane's belief that greed could buy him.

He walked back toward the palace as the lamps were lit, past the courtyard, past the stake, its blackened base gleaming in the torchlight. Corvane thought he had purchased a traitor. Perhaps he had. Just not the one he imagined.

Above the Hall of Whispers, a lamp burned in the spymaster's window, and Jude felt its gaze on his back all the way to his narrow bed—where he lay awake counting hours, five days draining away like sand, knowing that somewhere in the dark, Corvane was counting too.

And knowing, with a certainty that settled into his bones like ash, that the spymaster would not wait out all five.
