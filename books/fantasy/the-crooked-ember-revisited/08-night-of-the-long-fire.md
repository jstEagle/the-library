## 8. Night of the Long Fire

Sera smelled the smoke before the bells.

She was in the Saltmarket safehouse, copying the second page's cipher into her memory for the fourth time that week, when the tallow candle beside her guttered sideways—not from any draft, but from a pressure change, the whole city inhaling at once. She had lived through two purges as a court scribe. She knew what an organized burning sounded like: not chaos, but order. Boots in rhythm. Doors kicked on a count.

The first bell rang. Then the second. Then every bell in Vell at once, which had no official meaning and only one unofficial one.

"They're hitting all of us tonight," said Tam, the youngest of the Quiet Flame's runners, appearing in the doorway with his coat half-on and his face gone gray. "Mother Ilex's house first—Ilex is taken. The chandlery on Rope Walk is burning *now*."

"Where's Jude?"

"Gone to meet the ferryman about passage. He doesn't know."

Of course he didn't know. Jude Delacroix spent ten years learning to survive by never knowing anything until the last possible moment, and now it might kill him. Sera swept the codex pages into the oilcloth satchel she'd sewn against her ribs and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading since Rope Walk: three pages, one already lost to Corvane's raiders years ago, one recovered at the border—and the third still hidden where only she and Mother Ilex knew.

And Ilex was taken.

"Tam. Listen." She caught his chin and made him look at her. "You run to the ferry stairs. If Jude comes back, you tell him the word *emberglass* and nothing else. Then you get out of the city if you can. Do you hear me? Out."

"You're coming too—"

"I'm going to the third page before they do." She was already moving, snuffing the candle, pulling the loose brick from behind the flour shelf where the escape ledger lived. "Go."

He went. She heard his footsteps fade into the drumming rain, and then she stood alone in a dark room that smelled of tallow and old paper, and let herself feel, for exactly three breaths, the full weight of what was coming.

Then she went out into the fire.

* * *

Vell burned in pieces that night, neighborhood by neighborhood, like a man being dismembered while the surgeons argued over terminology.

Sera moved through back lanes she'd memorized as a girl, past the chandlery collapsing in a roar of orange, past a guard patrol dragging a woman in a keeper's gray toward a wagon with barred doors. The woman wasn't screaming. That was the worst part. The convicted ones never screamed anymore; they'd learned it changed nothing.

The third page was hidden in the one place Corvane's men would search last, because searching it required admitting something about themselves. The reliquary of Saint Aldous-in-the-Drift—the royal chapel's annex, consecrated ground, crown property. Mother Ilex had hidden the page inside the hollow base of the saint's censer twenty years ago, with the cold joke that no witch-hunter would ever put his hands on holy things he didn't intend to steal.

But Corvane would. Corvane put his hands on everything.

Sera reached the chapel annex as the bells were dying down and found the door unguarded—which told her everything. They'd already been inside. She slipped through anyway, because hope is a discipline you practice even when the evidence is against you, and crossed the empty nave to the censer stand.

The hollow base had been forced open with a blade. Neatly. Professionally. The page was gone, and in its place, weighted so it wouldn't blow away, lay a single white card bearing the palace seal and four words in a hand she recognized from a decade of copying warrants:

*Come and be useful.*

Corvane hadn't just found the page. He'd left her an invitation, because he wanted the scribe too—wanted the woman who could read the cipher, who could assemble what the pages meant, who could be made to testify that Jude Delacroix was the traitor and Lord Corvane the loyal servant who uncovered him.

Sera stood in the dark nave holding the card, and made the mistake of hesitating.

The hand closed on her shoulder from behind the pillar, and the hood came down over her head, and the world became wool and rope and the smell of her own fear.

* * *

They took her to the dungeons beneath the palace, which she had visited once before in her old life, escorting documents for a treason trial. It was colder than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply been warmer then—warm with the stupid confidence of a woman who believed the law was a wall around decent people instead of a weapon pointed wherever power aimed it.

They put her in a cell with a chair, which told her she wasn't meant to rot. Chairs meant questions. Questions meant someone still needed something from her, and need was leverage, and leverage was the only currency she had left.

The hood came off. Lamplight. A table. And across it, Lord Corvane himself, spymaster of Vell, looking less like a villain than a tired administrator working late—a gray man with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers, which was somehow more frightening than any monster.

"Sera Vane," he said pleasantly. "Forged writs, harboring convicts, conspiracy against the crown. Enough rope to hang you twice over. And yet here I am, offering you a chair instead of a cell without one. Why do you suppose that is?"

"Because you need me to read the pages."

"I need you to *confirm* what they say," Corvane corrected gently. "There's a difference. Anyone can read a cipher, Mistress Vane. What I require is credibility. A disgraced court scribe, weeping before the assembly, confessing how the taster Jude Delacroix seduced her into treason. That has weight. That has *shape*. Whereas your corpse—" he spread his ink-stained hands— "has none."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow night you burn in the Ember Square alongside Mother Ilex, whose interrogation begins within the hour, and whose age, I'm sorry to say, will make the process brief but not gentle." He slid a fresh sheet of parchment and a pen across the table. "You have until the lamplighter makes his rounds. I'm told confession reads best in a steady hand."

They left her alone with the pen.

Sera looked at it for a long time. Then she picked it up, and wrote—but not what Corvane wanted. She wrote in the old Ashmoor shorthand, the script the Quiet Flame used for messages that might be intercepted, the script Corvane's scholars had never cracked because it wasn't a cipher at all but a language of omission, meaning carried in what was *left out*. She wrote a confession that read, to any loyalist eye, as complete surrender and denunciation of Jude Delacroix.

And threaded through it, invisible unless you knew to look, she wrote to Jude:

*They have the third page. They have Ilex. The rite must be done at the covenant-stone, and it must be done freely or not at all—that is the trap, Jude, that is the whole trap. He wants you to come. He needs your hand on the stone to legitimize his claim. Don't come to save me. Come to end him.*

She signed it with her true name, which in Ashmoor shorthand was itself a message: *I trust you with my life, so spend it well.*

Then she sat back and waited for the lamplighter, and discovered that terror, fully ripened, tastes almost like calm.

* * *

Jude came anyway. Of course he came anyway.

She learned this later, pieced together from Tam's breathless account and from the scorch marks that never quite left the Saltmarket cobbles. How Jude had returned to find the safehouse ash and Tam waiting with one word—*emberglass*—and understood in a single sickening instant that everything had moved, that the game he'd been carefully playing for weeks was over and the losing had begun.

How he had gone to the smugglers' dens under the harbor wall with his pockets turned out, offering the last of his protective charms—the ones that had kept him alive through ten years of poisoned cups—for passage into the palace. The smugglers had laughed at first. Nobody got into the palace dungeons; the palace dungeons got into you. But then Jude had laid the charms out on the wet stone one by one, each one glowing faintly with the harmless magics he'd brewed to survive, and the smugglers had stopped laughing, because even criminals could recognize a man selling his own skin.

"He gave them the warding charm first," Tam told her afterward, still shaking. "The one that turns blades. Then the breath charm, for poison gas. Then the sleepless charm. Brannan the ferryman said he'd never seen anyone trade away their luck piece by piece and keep smiling. Said it was like watching a man pay for his own funeral and tip the gravedigger."

The last thing Jude traded was the oldest charm he owned, the one his mother had stitched into his collar before she burned—a knot of thread that was supposed to bring the wearer home. He gave it to Brannan and said, "If I don't come back, give this to a woman called Sera Vane. Tell her it worked anyway. Tell her I got home."

Then he went up through the smugglers' tunnel—the old wine route, bricked shut officially, bribed open eternally—into the bowels of the palace where the woman he loved and the old keeper who had taught them both were caged, and where Lord Corvane waited with three stolen pages and a covenant-stone and a plan ten years in the making that required exactly one thing Jude possessed:

A bearer who chose freely.

Which was, of course, why Corvane had left the invitation. Which was why the tunnels had been left open. Which was why every door between Jude and the dungeons swung wide as if oiled that very morning.

The trap was not that Jude couldn't reach the stone.

The trap was that he could.

* * *

In her cell, Sera heard the change in the palace's breathing before any guard did—a running of boots in the wrong direction, a shouted question cut short, the particular silence of a building realizing something has gone wrong inside it.

Her door unlocked. Not opened—unlocked, quietly, from outside, and then footsteps retreating fast.

She stared at the door for three heartbeats. Every instinct honed by a decade of court intrigue screamed that it was another test, another velvet threat, another corridor shaped like a mouth.

But somewhere below her, faint through the stone, she heard singing—the old Ashmoor walking-song, off-key and terrified and unmistakable, the song Mother Ilex had taught them on the border road.

Jude was in the palace. And whatever happened next, it would happen at the covenant-stone, tonight, with the whole rotten architecture of Corvane's ambition balanced on a single choice.

Sera Vane stood up, tested the door, and walked out of her cell into the dark—toward the singing, toward the stone, toward the fire that was coming for them all.
