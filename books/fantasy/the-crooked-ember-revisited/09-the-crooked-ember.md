## 9. The Crooked Ember

They took the chains off in the antechamber beneath the palace, which told Jude everything he needed to know.

Corvane didn't want a prisoner down there. He wanted a willing hand. Chains would leave marks on the rite—Mother Ilex had said as much, three nights and a lifetime ago, her voice like ash settling: *The stone knows compulsion. It always has.*

So they unshackled him, and two guards walked on either side of him through corridors that smelled of wet granite and old smoke, and Jude counted his steps because counting kept him from thinking about Sera in a cell somewhere above him, or the third page folded inside Corvane's coat, or the fact that his last protective charm had been spent buying passage from a smuggler who'd taken his coin and his trust and given him exactly what he'd paid for: a way in, and no way out.

The stair descended farther than any stair in Vell should have. Torches guttered as they passed, leaning toward Jude like men craning to look at something rare.

*The Ember remembers you,* Ilex had said. *It's been waiting a hundred years for an honest hand.*

He hoped it hadn't been waiting for this particular night.

The chamber at the bottom was round, older than the palace above it, older than the dynasty that built the palace. The ceiling was raw stone, veined with channels that might once have carried light or water or something stranger. And at the center stood the covenant-stone—or what remained of it. A broken pillar, waist-high, its surface split by a jagged fissure that ran top to bottom like a wound that never closed. The Crooked Ember itself sat in the fissure's heart: a sliver of fire no larger than a thumbnail, burning without fuel, crooked as a bent finger, guttering the way a candle gutters when it has almost nothing left.

A hundred years ago it had been a pillar of flame visible from the Saltmarket. Jude knew that from the codex. He knew it the way he now knew too many things written by dead hands about a living night.

Corvane stood beside the stone, and he was not wearing armor. He was wearing court gray, immaculate, and he looked like a man presiding over a wedding rather than a desecration.

"Jude." Warm. Almost fond. "You came."

"You had my friends," Jude said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. Ten years of tasting poison taught you to keep your face still while your stomach screamed. "You didn't need to send an invitation."

"A formality." Corvane gestured, and a guard stepped forward with the third page, unfolded, held open before Jude like scripture. The old script glowed faintly in the Ember's light—the confession-rite, the words the bearer must speak to reforge what was broken. "You've read it, of course. You're the only man in Vell who has read all three pages and lived."

"Give me one reason it isn't a lie."

"Because you'll watch me hold the power when it's done." Corvane smiled, thin and real. "I've never lied to you, Jude. That's rather the point of you. You taste truth for a living."

*And you've been feeding me poison dressed as wine for ten years,* Jude thought. *Just never in the cup.*

"The rite requires my name spoken freely," Jude said. "Your scribes will have told you. Compulsion fouls it."

"Compulsion fouls it." Corvane nodded slowly. "Which is why there are no chains, no knives, no threats in this room." He paused, and the smile sharpened. "In this room. Your apothecary friend sits in my dungeon. Mother Ilex is being tracked through the borderlands as we speak. Every member of the Quiet Flame we've taken is alive, Jude—all of them—and they stay alive exactly as long as the Ember burns in *my* name tonight. Speak freely. I'm giving you every reason to."

There it was. Freedom with a knife behind every rib of it. Jude looked at the little crooked flame and thought: *this is how you kill a covenant. Not with swords. With bargains shaped like mercy.*

He stepped up to the stone.

The heat hit him first—not fire-heat but something older, pressure-heat, the warmth of a hand held near his face. Up close the Ember was dying. He could see it now, the way he could see poison in wine: the light stuttering, the color wrong, more gray than gold. A hundred years of neglect. A hundred years of a city burning its faithful and calling it justice.

"Begin," Corvane said.

Jude opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the guards along the wall—young men mostly, palace-issue, bored and uneasy, the kind of soldiers who followed orders because orders were easier than questions. He'd been those men once, in a way. Following orders. Tasting quietly. Dying quietly, one small illegal charm at a time.

Then he looked at Corvane, and stopped performing.

"You're right," Jude said. "The rite requires a bearer who chose freely. So let me choose."

He turned to face the room—to face the guards, not the spymaster—and lifted his voice the way he'd never dared lift it in ten years of service.

"I am Jude Delacroix, food-taster to the crown, and I bear witness."

Corvane's expression didn't change. But his weight shifted onto his back foot, and Jude had spent a decade reading men's bodies across banquet tables. He pressed on.

"I confess first my own crimes, as the rite demands. For ten years I testified against the accused. I named witches in courts where the verdict was written before the trial. My word sent eleven people to the pyre. Eleven. I told myself I had no choice. I dosed myself with charms each night so I could survive the work of condemning people for surviving the same way." His voice cracked and he let it. "That was mine. I own it. Witness it."

The torches flared. All of them, at once, and the nearest guard took a step backward.

"Jude." Corvane's voice was silk over a blade. "The rite. In my name. Now."

"In your name." Jude laughed, and it came out ugly. "Yes. Let's speak names, since the confession-rite demands them. Lord Aldous Corvane, Spymaster of Vell. I bear witness against him."

"Guards—"

"Twenty-three years ago," Jude said, louder, "this man took payment from the Ashmoor faction that wanted the covenant dead. It's in his ledger—I've read it, page by page, in your own archive, under your own seal. He bribed the covenant-keepers' guard. He opened the vault beneath this very palace. He shattered the stone himself and blamed the realm of Ashmoor for it, and then he spent twenty years burning the faithful to keep anyone from ever proving otherwise. The charm-seller in the Saltmarket. The archivist in the royal library. The keeper families deported to the borderlands to die of ash-lung. Every fire in this kingdom for two decades has been lit by *him*, and every verdict called it justice."

The chamber had gone very quiet. One of the younger guards was looking at Corvane now instead of at Jude, and that look was worth more than any army.

"He offered me pardon tonight," Jude went on, "in exchange for the pages and my silence. He offered it with my friends' lives as collateral. That's the freedom he gave me to choose with. So here is my choice."

He put his hand on the broken stone.

The Ember flared—gray, doubtful, flickering like a thing deciding whether to die. Corvane moved at last, lunging, shouting for the guards to seize him, and Jude spoke the final line of the confession-rite over the chaos, the line Sera had made him memorize until it lived behind his teeth:

"*What is witnessed cannot be unwitnessed. What is owned cannot be stolen. Let the fire judge the honest hand.*"

Corvane's fingers closed on Jude's collar—

—and the Ember went white.

Not gray. Not guttering. White as the heart of a forge, white as the first morning of the world, and the heat that rolled off it threw Corvane backward across the chamber floor like a doll. The fissure in the stone began to knit—not healing so much as *remembering*, the crack closing inch by inch as the flame climbed, thumb-sized, fist-sized, taller than a man, a pillar of crooked golden fire that bent and twisted like a dancer and burned without consuming anything at all.

The guards were on their knees. All of them. Jude hadn't told them to kneel. Something in the light had.

He stood alone at the stone with fire climbing past him into the dark, and it did not burn him. It warmed him, the way a hearth warms, the way belonging warms—a thing he had not felt in ten years and had nearly forgotten the name of.

"It chose," whispered the youngest guard, staring. "Saints preserve us, it *chose* him."

"No," Jude said. His voice was hoarse. He found he was smiling, and that his face was wet. "It just finally heard the truth. There's a difference."

Corvane stirred against the far wall, blood at his hairline, and reached for the knife at his belt with the reflexes of a man who had survived twenty years of secrets and intended to survive one more.

But the guards were already rising. And they were not looking at their spymaster anymore.

They were looking at the fire.

* * *

Above them, the whole palace woke.

Jude felt it through the stone before he heard it—bells, boots, the great doors of the throne hall booming open. The Ember's light had risen through the foundations like water finding cracks, and every soul in Vell who still remembered the old faith, and half who didn't, had seen the sky over the palace turn gold.

Guards escorted Corvane up the stair between them, his wrists bound with his own signature seal, and if his eyes found Jude's in passing—if they promised, silently, that this was not finished—Jude met the look and did not blink. Ten years of tasting had taught him that much: some poisons lose their power the moment you name them.

Sera was waiting at the top of the stair, cell-pale, irons freshly struck off, laughing and crying at once as she grabbed his arms. Behind her, dazed and blinking in the impossible golden light flooding the great hall, stood the freed prisoners of the Quiet Flame—two dozen of them, ash-marked and hollow-eyed and *alive*.

"You absolute fool," she said. "You beautiful, impossible fool. The rite needed a free choice and you made it in front of thirty armed men."

"It seemed," Jude said, "like the only witnesses who mattered."

Beyond the high windows, over the rooftops of Vell, the Crooked Ember burned tall and crooked and whole for the first time in a century—and in the Saltmarket below, in the borderlands beyond, in the hidden places where the old faith had never quite died, people were stepping out of doorways to look up.

Mother Ilex's voice came back to him across the miles, soft as falling ash: *The stone knows compulsion. But it knows honesty better.*

Jude stood in the gold light with Sera's grip tight on his arm, and for the first time in ten years, he wasn't tasting anything for anyone.

Tomorrow there would be trials—real ones. Tomorrow the king would demand explanations, and the realms of Ashmoor would send envoys through a border that was no longer choked, and someone would have to teach a frightened city what to do with legal magic.

But tonight, the outcasts were coming out of hiding, and they were looking for him.

And Jude Delacroix, for the first time in his life, walked toward them instead of away.
