## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The finger-bone arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in waxed linen and tied with the kind of knot that said *please be careful* in every language grief speaks.

Zephyrine Lockridge set it on her workbench beneath the lamp and did not touch it for a long moment. This was ritual, though she'd never have called it that. Ritual was for priests and guildmasters. She was a bone-carver, third rank, Guild of Impossible Things, Grayharbor chapter, and what she did was craft.

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and chalk dust and the faint sweet rot of the harbor coming through the shutters even in winter. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, the guildhouse went about its evening — boots on stairs, the clink of the mess hall, an enforcer laughing at something another enforcer had said. Zephyrine heard all of it the way she heard everything: clearly, completely, and from behind a wall.

She unwrapped the linen.

The phalanx was old. That was common — widows sent their fathers, mothers sent their sons, and by the time a family decided someone needed a keepsake carved, the bones had usually been waiting in a drawer or a locket or a shoebox under a bed for years. Age didn't matter to the work. What mattered was that it had been loved. A bone nobody had loved carved like soapstone, dull and dumb. A loved one sang.

This one hummed before her knife even touched it. A low note, patient, like a man waiting his turn at a counter.

"Good evening," Zephyrine said quietly. She always said it. The dead couldn't hear — she was fairly sure they couldn't hear — but it seemed rude to begin otherwise. "I'm going to make you into something your daughter can hold."

She turned the bone in the lamplight. Index finger, right hand. Callused ridge along the pad where a lifetime of rope or pen or plow had worn its groove. She'd know more once she started. She always knew more once she started. That was the gift, and the gift was also the problem, and she had spent eleven years making sure those two facts never met anyone's attention at the same time.

Officially, the Guild of Impossible Things employed bone-carvers to make mementos mori. Rings, pendants, small figures — a sailor's phalanx shaped into a tiny ship, a grandmother's knuckle into a songbird. The living paid well for them. The dead, as far as the guild's ledgers were concerned, contributed material and nothing else.

Unofficially — in the private ledger Zephyrine kept in a locked drawer beneath her bed, written in her own shorthand, forty-one entries long — the dead said things. Last words mostly. Sometimes warnings. Once, memorably, a recipe for plum brandy that she had made and drunk alone and never told anyone about, because telling anyone would have meant explaining how she got it.

Entry forty-two began tonight.

She set the knife to the bone.

The first stroke is always the loudest. It came up through her fingers like cold water rising in a well — not sound exactly, more like remembering on behalf of someone else. She breathed through it, the way she'd taught herself, slow and even, and let the man's life pool into the room.

*Harbor-man.* Rope calluses, then. Dockworker, fifty years of it. Salt in everything. A wife who died young — he'd carried that grief so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in his pocket. A daughter born late, treasured past all sense. He'd walked her to school every day until she was embarrassed by it, and then he'd kept doing it anyway, walking twenty paces behind with his cap in his hands.

Zephyrine carved. The bone gave up its shape willingly: a gull, wings half-spread, because dockworkers always wanted gulls and daughters always needed something that could fly away without leaving.

And underneath the ordinary warmth of him, the sediment of a long decent life, there was the last thing. There was always a last thing. The final hours left the deepest groove, and when her blade crossed it, the man's voice rose through her wrist and settled behind her teeth like a coin under the tongue.

*Count them,* he was saying, urgent, urgent, the urgency of a man who has seen something at night from a doorway and told no one because who would believe a dockworker. *Count them, girl, count them every month, because I counted and there's one too many.*

Zephyrine's knife stopped.

She sat very still in the lamplight, rain ticking the glass, the gull half-born in her hands.

*One too many,* the dead man said again, and this time she caught the shape of what he meant, the image folded inside the words: a winter sky over Grayharbor, clear and hard, and the moon up in it, and the man standing in his doorway counting — because his father had taught him to count, some old harbor superstition, *count the moons, boy, count them every turn* — and getting thirteen.

Thirteen.

There were twelve moons. Everyone knew there were twelve moons. Children learned their names before they learned to read: the Shepherd, the Fisher, the Pale Wife, and nine others wheeling overhead in their slow procession, each with its turn, each with its season. Twelve. It was arithmetic, practically. It was the ground underfoot.

But the dead man had counted thirteen, and the dead did not lie to her. That was the one mercy of her gift. Living people lied constantly, generously, for kindness and cruelty and boredom. The dead only ever said what they had truly carried.

*One too many,* he whispered, fading now as the carving took its final form, the last groove closing like a door. *Tell my girl to count.*

Then silence, the particular silence of a finished piece, and Zephyrine sat alone with a small bone gull and a warning she could do nothing with.

* * *

She almost filed it. Her hand actually moved toward the locked drawer — the motion worn smooth as the dockworker's callus, eleven years of practice — and then stopped.

Forty-one entries. Forty-one last words, confessions, warnings, recipes, apologies meant for other people, love meant for other people, all of it transcribed in her careful shorthand and buried under her bed like contraband. And in eleven years, she had delivered none of it. Not one message. Not one *your father said he forgave you*. Not one *he knew about the money; he was proud, not angry*.

Because delivering them would mean admitting she heard them. And admitting she heard them would mean the end of everything she had.

She understood the arithmetic perfectly. The guild did not employ women who heard the dead. The guild *managed* the boundary between living and dead — that was its oldest charter, older than Grayharbor itself — and management required that the boundary stay quiet. Bone-carvers shaped keepsakes. They did not converse. If Master Corvane ever suspected that his quiet third-rank carver was conducting a correspondence with every relic that crossed her bench, she would not be punished. She would be *studied*. And then she would be used. And then, when her usefulness outgrew her discretion, she would be closed like a ledger.

So she filed. For eleven years she had filed, and the filing had a shape she knew intimately: the loneliness of being fluent in a language no living person spoke. She could sit across a table from a widow and hear her father's whole heart pouring out of the little wrapped bundle between them, and say only, "A gull, I think. He would have liked a gull." She was the most crowded room in Grayharbor and no one had ever knocked.

But tonight her hand stayed on the desk.

*Count them,* the man had said. *There's one too many.*

She got up, went to the shutter, and opened it. Cold air, rain-smell, the harbor lights smeared gold down the hill. And above it all, through a tear in the clouds, the moon — the Fisher, fat and white and three nights from full, hanging over the water like a lamp over a table where something was being discussed.

Twelve moons. She counted anyway, the way you check a lock you've checked a thousand times, the way the dead man's father had taught him. Shepherd. Fisher. Pale Wife. She named them all, moving west, and reached twelve, and stood there feeling foolish in the cold.

And then the cloud tore wider, and high above the Fisher — so high and so thin she might have gone her whole life calling it a star, might have called it a star tonight if a dead dockworker hadn't put the counting in her head — hung a thirteenth light. Small. Silver-white. Wrong.

It was moving. Slowly, the way the moons move, which stars do not.

Zephyrine stood at the window a long time, rain coming in past her shoulders, the little bone gull warm in her hand as if the man were still holding on. Below her, the guildhouse murmured on — enforcers laughing, ledgers closing, Master Corvane somewhere in his tower rooms keeping whatever counsel he kept. Twelve moons, everyone said. The covenant held. Everything was in order.

She thought of her locked drawer. Forty-one entries of truth with nowhere to go.

She thought: *if I write this one down and file it under my bed, I am filing the sky.*

Down at the tideline, far below, something pale washed against the rocks — driftwood, probably. A barrel. A body.

Probably.

Zephyrine closed the shutter, put the gull in its box for the widow, and reached for her coat instead of her ledger.
