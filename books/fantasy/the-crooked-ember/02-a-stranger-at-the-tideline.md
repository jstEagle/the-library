## 2. A Stranger at the Tideline

The tide gave up its dead at dawn, and Zephyrine Lockridge had learned long ago not to look too closely at what it left behind.

She was walking the tideline because she could not sleep, which was itself a kind of confession. In the guildhouse at Grayharbor, sleep came to those with clean ledgers and quiet hands, and hers had been neither for three nights running. The widow's father's finger-bone still sat in her workbox downstairs, half-carved, humming with a dead man's last words about the moon. She had filed the warning away the way she filed everything—neatly, silently, in the place where inconvenient truths went to be forgotten. But inconvenient truths had a way of walking back out of their drawers.

So she walked. Grayharbor's beach was gray shingle and black sand, littered with kelp-ropes and crab shells and the occasional drowned gull, and at this hour it belonged to no one but her and the gulls themselves, who screamed at the horizon as if they knew something.

She saw him first as a piece of driftwood.

Then the driftwood coughed.

He lay where the waves had dumped him, half in the foam, dressed in courier's leathers gone dark with seawater. One arm was bent wrong beneath him. The other was pressed flat against his chest, guarding something, the way a man guards a wound or a child or a secret—and from the look of him, he had been guarding it a very long way.

Zephyrine should have walked back to the guildhouse. Should have fetched the harbor-watch, or better yet said nothing at all, because things that washed ashore carrying secrets were guild business, and guild business was the one thing N. M.—the one thing *she*—had spent eleven years avoiding.

Instead her feet carried her forward across the shingle.

Up close he was young. Younger than she'd guessed from a distance—mid-twenties, maybe, with salt crusted white in his eyebrows and lips gone the blue of deep ice. His eyes found her and fixed there with the terrible focus of the dying, that last reallocation of everything a body has left.

"You're—" He tried to sit up. Failed. Tried again, and this time got one elbow under himself, which seemed to cost him a year of his life. "You're a carver. I can see the marks on your hands."

Zephyrine looked down at her own fingers, at the fine pale scars every bone-carver wore like a signature, and thought: *so much for invisible.*

"Who sent you?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"Doesn't matter." A wave broke over his legs and he didn't even flinch, which told her how far past flinching he was. "Listen. There's no time. They're coming behind me—the guild's hounds, Corvane's enforcers. They'll be here by full light."

Her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the sea wind. "You're mistaken. The Guild of Impossible Things doesn't send enforcers after couriers."

"They do when the courier carries this."

His hand came off his chest, and in it was a roll of something pale and thin, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with brine. Even before he unwound a corner of it, even before she saw the faint silver shimmer of the surface beneath, some animal part of her knew what it was.

Moon-skin. Inked with a map.

No one inked maps on moon-skin. Moon-skin was covenant-stuff, older than the guild, older than Grayharbor, the membrane between what lived and what remembered. Touching it felt like touching the inside of her own skull.

"Take it," he said.

"No." The word was out of her mouth before she'd decided anything. "Whatever you're carrying, it's yours to carry. I keep ledgers. I carve keepsakes. I don't—"

"*Thirteen turns.*"

He said it quietly, and the words went through her like a blade through wet paper. Thirteen turns. The thirteenth moon. The same warning the dead man's finger-bone had given her three nights ago, spoken through her own carving knife into her own ears: *when the moon turns thirteen times, ask who holds the stitches.*

"I don't know what that means," she lied.

It was the second lie of her life. The first had been small—a childhood thing, a broken cup blamed on a brother. This one tasted different. This one tasted like a door opening.

"Yes you do," the courier said, and almost smiled. Salt and blood on his teeth. "That's why you're standing here instead of running. Take it. Hide it. And when you've read it—when you understand what's unraveling—remember that I died getting it to someone who'd *listen*."

"The guild listens," she said. "I'll bring you to the guildhouse. Master Corvane will—"

"Corvane." He said the name the way men say the names of storms. "Corvane is why I'm bleeding into your ocean."

Somewhere up the shingle, a gull screamed, and beneath its scream—faint, distant, but unmistakable—came the rhythmic crunch of boots on stone. Many boots. Moving fast.

The courier heard them too. Whatever was left of his strength, he spent it all at once: he seized her wrist with a grip that should have been impossible from a man that far gone, and pressed the map into her hands, folding her fingers over it.

"Thirteen turns," he whispered. "The moon comes round the thirteenth time, and the covenant breaks, and everything the dead are holding shut comes open. Unless someone reforges it. Unless someone pays." His eyes were going glassy now, but they held hers. "They'll ask you for your memories, carver. That's always the price. Don't pay it blind. Promise me you won't pay it blind."

"I promise," she said, and meant nothing by it, and understood even as she spoke that she would remember this promise for the rest of her life.

The boots were closer. She could hear voices now, low and clipped, giving orders.

"Hide it," the courier breathed. "Say you found me drowning. Say you saw nothing. Say—" A cough took him, wracking, and brought up blood pinked with foam. "Say whatever liars say. You'll find you're good at it. Everyone is, once they start."

Then his grip failed, and he fell back against the shingle, and his eyes went past her shoulder to the brightening east, to the place where the moon was setting fat and pale over the water.

One moon. She counted without meaning to. One moon hanging over Grayharbor, and twelve more turns until the thirteenth.

The boots crested the dune line.

There were four of them, enforcers in guild-gray, moving with the economical certainty of people paid to be certain. Their leader was a broad woman with a scarred jaw whom Zephyrine recognized distantly from guildhall corridors—Vess, or Veska, someone whose name existed only in other people's business. Her eyes swept the beach and landed on Zephyrine crouched over a dying man, and narrowed.

"Carver." Not a greeting. An identification. "Step away from him."

"He washed ashore," Zephyrine said. "I was walking. I heard him coughing."

"We know." Veska came down the shingle without hurry, boots crunching, and the other three spread out along the tideline with the smooth choreography of a net closing. "We've been tracking him since Kellwick. Where is it?"

"Where is what?"

The lie came so easily it frightened her more than the enforcers did. Three lies now, inside of two minutes. The courier had been right. It *was* easy. The map sat against her ribs inside her coat, thin as a second skin, warm as a held breath, and Veska looked straight at her and saw nothing but a bone-carver in a nightcoat, cold and harmless and half-asleep.

Veska crouched beside the courier. Put two fingers to his throat. Waited a moment longer than necessary, the way people do when they already know the answer and are performing the checking anyway.

"Dead," she said. She looked up, and her gaze was flat and patient and utterly without mercy. "Died of drowning, did he? Before he could tell you what he was carrying?"

"He never spoke," Zephyrine said. "He was past speaking. I don't think he even saw me."

Four pairs of eyes. Four faces arranged around her like the points of a compass, and at the center of the compass, herself, holding still, holding the biggest lie of her life against her heartbeat where they couldn't see it.

Something moved at the edge of her vision—nothing, a shimmer, a disturbance in the air like heat off summer stones—and she felt, absurdly, impossibly, a pressure against her awareness that she recognized, because she had spent her whole life listening to the dead and this was the first time the dead had ever seemed to be listening back.

*Not past speaking,* something said, in a voice like wind through reeds, amused and pained at once. *You lying little saint.*

She did not let her face change. Eleven years of practice, keeping her face still while dead men spoke through her knives. But inside, where the map burned against her ribs, something wild and terrified and *exhilarated* was laughing.

Because she had lied to the Guild of Impossible Things, to its face, to its teeth, and gotten away with it. Because she was invisible no longer—invisible to everyone except a dying courier and, apparently, his ghost. Because somewhere under the fear was a feeling she hadn't had since she was a girl: that her life, the small gray careful life she'd built ledger by ledger, had just been picked up and turned over like a stone, and underneath it was something enormous.

Veska stood. Brushed off her knees.

"The guild will want a report," she said. "You'll come to the guildhouse and give it. Formally. Under quill."

"Of course," Zephyrine said. "I have nothing to hide."

The fourth lie. It slid out smooth as oiled bone.

But as the enforcers turned to march her up the beach—as Veska fell into step beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched—Zephyrine felt the shimmer at the edge of her vision move with her. Keeping pace. Keeping watch.

And beneath the shingle, beneath the sand, beneath the whole sleeping town of Grayharbor, she felt—or imagined she felt, or *hoped* she felt—a faint, patient pulse, like a stitch straining at its seam.

Twelve turns, said the pulse. Twelve turns, carver. Start counting.

* * *

They walked her through the waking streets, and Zephyrine used every one of them to think.

The map rode against her ribs. The enforcers rode at her elbows. And between them, unseen, unheard by anyone but her, the courier's ghost kept pace—she was almost sure it was him; the voice had his cadence, his sardonic edge, the particular tiredness of someone who has run a very long way.

*Left at the chandler's,* the voice murmured as they reached the guildhouse steps. *They'll search your rooms, you know. First thing. So the question, carver, is whether you're clever enough to get it out of your coat before they get their hands in it.*

Zephyrine climbed the steps. The guildhouse doors stood open, gold light spilling out, and somewhere inside Master Corvane was surely awake, because Corvane was always awake, because Corvane missed nothing.

*Well?* said the ghost. *Any ideas?*

As a matter of fact—one. It was a terrible idea. It relied on a lie, a distraction, and the one room in Grayharbor no enforcer would ever think to search.

She squared her shoulders, and followed the light inside.
