## 3. What the Bones Remember

The bone-cellars beneath the guildhouse smelled of chalk and old salt, and Zephyrine had always found that comforting. Tonight it felt like hiding.

She'd taken the courier's map down three flights of stairs, past the drying racks where antler shanks hung like laundry, into the deepest vault where the Guild stored its unclaimed dead—the bones of paupers and strangers, waiting years for someone to pay for a keepsake. Nobody came down here. Nobody ever did. That was rather the point of her, Zephyrine thought bitterly. The woman nobody came for.

She lit a single tallow stub and spread the map on a workbench scarred by forty years of chisels.

Moon-skin was illegal in Grayharbor. Moon-skin was *impossible*—thin as onion paper, silvered on one side, warm to the touch like something recently alive. She'd carved bone all her life and never touched anything that felt this much like flesh.

"Thirteen turns," she murmured. "What does that even mean?"

"It means you're talking to yourself again."

Zephyrine didn't jump. She'd been hearing the dead since she was six; a voice alone couldn't startle her anymore. But she turned anyway, because this voice was new, and new voices were rare enough to be precious.

The courier stood at the edge of the lamplight, translucent as breath on glass, still wearing the waterlogged gray of his last coat. He looked annoyed about being dead, which struck her as reasonable.

"You're the one from the tideline," she said.

"And you're the one who lied to the enforcers." He drifted closer, peering at the map upside down. "Zephyrine Lockridge. Bone-carver. Second ledger-keeper to the Guild of Impossible Things. You have ink on your left ear."

She wiped at her ear. "How do you know my name?"

"I read your ledgers while I was dying." He said it flatly, without apology. "You keep very good books, by the way. Terrible secrets in them, but very good books."

Her stomach dropped. "Those are confidential—"

"They're *evidence*." He tapped the map with a finger that passed through it. "Wren. Courier, formerly of the Salt-Road Circuit. And before you ask—yes, I'm dead, no, I don't recommend it, and yes, I'm going to haunt you until you help me."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and it went badly, because neither of them trusted the other yet.

Wren knew things he shouldn't—names of covenant-marks, words in a language that made the tallow flame bend sideways when he spoke them. Zephyrine knew how to read the map's medium, because moon-skin wasn't just skin; it was *bone*, in its way, and bone spoke to her if she listened hard enough.

"Press your palm here," Wren said, pointing to the map's center. "Flat. Don't carve. Just listen."

"I know how to listen."

"Do you? Because you've spent your whole life pretending you can't."

That landed harder than she wanted it to. She pressed her palm flat against the moon-skin anyway, mostly to prove him wrong.

The map woke under her hand.

Not light—*memory*. The skin remembered being part of something whole, and it poured that wholeness into her: twelve points arranged in a ring, each one a stitch where the living realm had been sewn to the dead one, long ago, deliberately, by people who understood that the two worlds needed each other the way lungs need air. Twelve marks. Twelve anchors. And threading between them—

A thirteenth point. Unmarked. Moving.

"The thirteenth moon," Zephyrine whispered. "It's not a moon at all. It's a *seam*."

"Now she's catching up," Wren said, but gently now.

She traced the ring with her finger. Eleven marks glowed steady under her touch, small knots of warmth. The twelfth flickered. And the first—the mark nearest Grayharbor, out past the harbor bar where the drowned lights sometimes showed on fog nights—

The first mark was torn. She felt it the way you feel a missing tooth: an absence with edges. Something had cut the stitching there, cleanly, deliberately, and the realms were bleeding into each other through the wound.

"When did this happen?" she asked.

"Four months ago." Wren's voice had gone quiet. "I carried the proof to the capital. Corvane's enforcers caught me at the Salt Road ford. I got the map into the river instead of their hands, and then I got myself into the river after it." A pause. "The swimming was fine. The dying was the inconvenient part."

"Corvane." Her guildmaster's name sat strangely in her mouth. "You're saying Master Corvane tore the covenant."

"I'm saying someone with access to guild records, guild keys, and guild enforcers tore it. Draw your own conclusions. You're good at drawing things."

She pulled her hand back from the map. It dimmed, sulking. "Why me? Why bring this to a bone-carver?"

"Because the map only opens for someone who can hear bone. There are maybe four people alive who can do that. Two work for Corvane. One is a child in Vell." He looked at her, and dead eyes could be terribly direct. "And one has spent ten years filing away warnings from the dead instead of acting on them. Your ledgers, Zephyrine. Half the widows in Grayharbor have brought you last words about the moon, the seam, the tearing. You wrote them all down beautifully and told no one."

"I told the Guild."

"You told *him*." Wren drifted around the workbench, restless as weather. "Think. Every warning you filed, every strange death you recorded—who read those ledgers besides you?"

She wanted to argue. She opened her mouth to argue.

And remembered, instead, the widow's father from three nights ago, his finger-bone whispering *the moon is wrong, tell someone the moon is wrong*, and herself carving the warning into the record, sealing it, shelving it. Remembered Corvane reading her summaries every month, nodding, praising her discretion.

*Discretion.* He'd used that word often. She'd taken it as respect.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes," said Wren.

* * *

Dawn came down through the cellar grates in thin gray bars. Zephyrine hadn't slept. Wren didn't seem to need her to sleep; he hovered and prompted and occasionally argued with her about interpretation, and somewhere in the fourth hour of argument she realized she'd stopped flinching when he moved through her lamplight.

"So there are eleven marks left," she said, copying the ring onto ordinary paper, in her own cipher, because she trusted cipher more than she trusted safety. "One already torn. If the thirteenth moon completes its turn—"

"The seam opens all the way. The veil goes with it. Everything dead comes through, everything living gets—" He shrugged, a horrible casual gesture. "Unstitched. My employers called it the Unbinding. I called it the end of the world, but I was paid to be dramatic."

"Who employed you?"

"The Remembrancers. An order that predates the Guild. They kept the covenant before your masters decided they'd rather keep the magic." His form guttered, steadied. Dead men faded fastest when they talked too long about what mattered. "There are three keys, Zephyrine. Covenant-keys. They can mend the tear or end the covenant entirely, depending on the hand that holds them. The Remembrancers hid them generations ago, and they hid them well, because they knew the Guild would come looking."

"How do you know where they are?"

Another shrug, but this one cost him. "Because I spent two years finding out. Because I died carrying half the answer. And because—" he hesitated, and ghosts should not be able to hesitate so convincingly— "because the keys aren't hidden in places. They're hidden in *people*. Bloodlines. The Remembrancers bound them to families, thinking blood would keep them safe."

Zephyrine set down her pen. The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above, the guildhouse was waking—boots on floorboards, the kettle in the common room, Corvane's measured footsteps crossing toward his study.

"Wren," she said carefully. "Whose bloodline?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide whether to lie to her, and watched him decide not to.

"Lockridge," he said. "The keys are Lockridge bones. Your family, Zephyrine. Your mother's line. Which means Corvane doesn't need to find the keys anymore."

The tallow flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"He just needs to find *you*."

Above them, faint through stone and earth, a door closed. Then another. Boots on the cellar stairs—not hurrying, not sneaking. Walking the way a man walks when he owns the house and knows exactly which room he's going to.

Wren flickered out like a snuffed candle. Zephyrine pinched the tallow dark and stood alone in the black, holding a map of the end of the world, listening to footsteps come down toward her that she had known and trusted for ten years.

Master Corvane knocked politely on the cellar door.

"Zephyrine," he said warmly. "We need to talk about your gift."
