## 4. The Guildmaster's Kindness

Zephyrine had been back in the guildhouse for six hours before anyone noticed she'd ever left.

That was the thing about being useful and quiet: you could vanish into your own life like a coin dropped in deep water. She had slipped through the kitchen door before dawn, mud to her knees, the moon-skin map pressed flat against her ribs beneath her apron, and she had sat down at her workbench as if the night hadn't happened, as if a dying man hadn't put his whole ruined weight into her arms on the tideline. She picked up her burin. She set it down again. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have.

Wren stood by the window where the light came through thinnest, which was nowhere at all, really, because ghosts don't stand in light so much as near it, the way a cold room stands near a fire. He had not stopped talking since the shore.

"You should have taken the east road," he said. "I told you. The east road. You took the *causeway*, like a woman who wants to be seen."

"I wanted to come home."

"Home." He said the word like he was tasting something spoiled. "You hid a dead man's map under an apron full of flour and you call this home."

"It's flour from yesterday," Zephyrine said. "And keep your voice down."

"I'm a ghost. My voice is exactly as loud as you let it be, and you're letting it be loud because you want company in your panic."

She hated that he was right. She hated more that having him be right felt almost like friendship.

The workshop around her was familiar enough to ache: the racks of finished keepsakes, each one tagged with a name and a date; the little brass scale for weighing bone against silver; the smell of oil and dust and the faint sweet rot of old ivory. Eleven years she had worked here. Eleven years of carving other people's grief into shapes they could hold, and listening—always listening—to what the dead left behind in the material, and saying nothing about any of it. Not to the guildmasters. Not to anyone.

The map lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Thirteen turns. One mark already torn.

She was reaching for the burin again when the door opened without a knock, and Master Corvane came in.

* * *

He did not look like a man hunting a runaway. He looked like a man come to share a pot of tea. He wore his gray traveling coat rather than the guildmaster's black, and he carried two cups on a tray, actual porcelain, the good kind with the hairline gold rims that only came out for donors and dignitaries. He set the tray on the corner of her bench, next to the widow's finger-bone she still hadn't delivered, and poured with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been interrupted in his life.

"You missed supper," he said.

"I wasn't hungry."

"Mm." He handed her a cup. "You also missed breakfast, lunch, and, I'm told, the entire previous day, during which time your lodgings went unvisited and your bench went cold. You've been gone three days, Zephyrine."

Three days. She had thought it was one. The cellars under the harbor had no windows, and Wren didn't sleep, and neither, apparently, had she. She took the tea because refusing it would have been a confession.

"I needed air," she said. "The sea air. For my head."

"For your head." Corvane smiled. It was a good smile. It had won over guild councils and suspicious widows and, once, a magistrate who had come to shut them down. "Your head, which has always been perfectly sound, though everyone in this building has spent eleven years pretending otherwise."

The cup was warm in her hands. The room got very quiet, the way rooms do just before something breaks.

"I don't know what you mean, Master."

"No," he said gently. "But I do. Sit down, Zephyrine. Please. This is not an interrogation. If it were an interrogation, there would be enforcers here instead of tea, and you would already know it, because you've spent eleven years learning to read this house the way you read bone." He settled onto the stool across from her, folding his long frame down with the ease of a much younger man. "You read bone. That's what we're here to discuss."

Her mouth was dry. The tea didn't help. "Master—"

"The Aldermoor commission. Four years ago. A mother wanted her son's last words, and you carved the keepsake and told her the bone held nothing—that some dead simply go quiet." He sipped his tea. "Except the boy's last words were 'tell my mother the debt is paid,' and she wept when I repeated them to her, and asked how I'd known, and I said the guild has its methods. Which was true. The method was you. It has always been you."

Zephyrine set the cup down before she dropped it. Eleven years. Eleven years of careful silence, of filing away warnings and confessions and deathbed pleas in the locked ledger of her own skull, and he had known—the whole time—

"How long?"

"Since your first week. You carved a horse for a child whose sister had drowned. You came out of the workroom white as tallow and asked me whether keepsakes could be made to hold less. Hold *less*. As though the words were a burden you were carrying for them." His eyes were pale gray, patient, terribly kind. "Only someone who heard them would ask that."

The map burned against her ribs. She thought of Wren at the window, invisible, watching, and willed him—absurdly—to stay quiet.

"Why?" she managed. "Why say nothing? Why keep me carving trinkets for eleven years if you knew what I could—"

"Because you weren't ready." And here his voice changed, softened, became the voice he used for grieving families, the voice that made people feel that their worst day was safe in his hands. "Because a gift like yours, untrained, is a wound. You've been bleeding quietly into your work for a decade, child, and calling it discretion. I called it patience. There is a difference between hiding a talent and protecting it, and I chose protection. I have never once regretted it."

It would have been so easy to believe him. That was the terrible thing. Eleven years of loneliness sat up inside her like a dog hearing its name. *Someone knew. Someone saw. All this time you were not mute among the living—you were simply waiting.* She could feel the shape of the relief, warm as the tea, and she wanted it with a desperation that embarrassed her.

"And now?" she said. "Why tell me now?"

"Because you're ready now. Because three days ago a courier died on our tideline carrying something that belongs to this guild, and the men I sent to retrieve him found only gulls and a disturbed wrack-line—and because this morning, my dear, I watched you lie to me about sea air with your hand pressed flat against your ribs like a woman guarding a wound." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing soft in it at all. "You are ready, Zephyrine, because you have finally done something worth keeping secrets about."

The room tilted. She kept her face still—eleven years of practice—but inside her everything was falling.

"What was he carrying?" she asked. Too fast. Too interested. She heard herself do it and couldn't stop.

Corvane rose and crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, silhouetted against the gray harbor light. When he spoke again, the kindness was back, laid over something else like gilt over lead.

"A map," he said. "Inked on moon-skin. Very old. Very dangerous—not dangerous the way knives are dangerous, but the way truth is dangerous to people who have built their lives on comfortable lies." He turned. "The covenant-marks, Zephyrine. The stitches that bind the living realm to the dead. Do you know what happens when a stitch tears?"

She thought of the map in the cellars, the western mark inked over in fresh black. *Already torn.*

"I imagine things get through," she said carefully.

"Things always get through. That's the problem. Whispers. Hauntings. Grief that won't stay buried where it belongs." He came back toward her, and his voice dropped into warmth, into confidence, into the register of a man sharing a secret with an equal. "The Guild of Impossible Things was founded to manage that traffic. To keep the border tidy. But the founders misunderstood something fundamental, and I have spent thirty years correcting their error in small ways, quietly, patiently—the same way I protected you." He stopped beside her bench. "The dead are not souls to be shepherded, Zephyrine. They are echoes. Residue. And the covenant that gives them passage through the living world is not sacred—it is a tax. Levied on us. Paid by us. Every haunting, every whisper in a bone, every cold spot in every nursery—we pay for all of it, forever, out of the only currency the living truly have."

"Which is?"

"Attention. Memory. Peace." He touched the lid of her brass scale, lightly, almost tenderly. "You of all people know the cost. You've heard the dead your whole life and told no one. How many nights of sleep has that gift cost you? How much of yourself have you poured into strangers' grief? Imagine a world where the channel closes. Where the dead rest—truly rest, finally, completely—and the living are free." His gray eyes held hers. "I can teach you what your gift actually is. Not a wound. An instrument. With training, you wouldn't just hear the dead—you could quiet them. Shape them. And when the covenant comes undone, as it is already beginning to do, you could stand at the center of what comes after. The Voice of the new order. Mine, yes—but honored. Cherished. Never again hidden in a cellar carving finger-bones for widows."

There it was. Belonging, offered outright, wrapped in flattery and truth—he *had* protected her, or something like it; he *was* offering to end the loneliness that had been her whole adult life. She could feel herself leaning toward it the way a cold hand leans toward a stove. Eleven years. One sentence of recognition and she was ready to follow him anywhere, and some cold clear part of her—the part that had lain in the dark cellars decoding moon-skin while enforcers walked the streets above—watched herself lean and did not like what it saw.

"The courier," she said. "Did he suffer?"

Corvane blinked. Of all the responses he might have prepared for, that one plainly wasn't among them.

"He was a carrier of contraband," he said. "Whatever befell him on the water, the guild did not—"

"You sent men to the tideline within minutes of the tide going out," Zephyrine said. "Minutes. As if you knew when he'd arrive. As if you knew what he carried and who he'd stolen it from." She looked up at him, and the loneliness was still there, God help her, but underneath it something harder had surfaced, something that sounded like Wren's voice in the dark saying *you took the causeway, like a woman who wants to be seen.* "Who tore the western mark, Master?"

For the first time in eleven years, she saw Master Corvane decide something. The kindness stayed on his face, but it went still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"Rest," he said, rising, smoothing his coat. "Take the day. We'll speak again tomorrow, and I'll show you the first lesson, and you'll understand why I've waited so long." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The map will keep, Zephyrine. Whatever you're carrying under your apron—it will keep. Things written on moon-skin can only be read by the blood that's meant to read them. I couldn't decipher it if I wanted to." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "But I know who can. And so, now, does everyone I employ."

The door closed behind him.

Zephyrine sat very still until his footsteps faded down the stair. Then she pulled the map from beneath her apron and spread it on the bench among the bone-dust and the widow's finger-bone and the two cups of untouched tea, and Wren's voice came low out of the empty corner of the room, stripped of all its sarcasm at last:

"He gave us until tomorrow," the ghost said. "So we leave tonight. There's a grave in the Marrowfield cemetery we need to open—and I'd start running, girl, because the man just told every enforcer in Grayharbor exactly where you'll be standing at dawn."
