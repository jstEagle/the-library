## 5. Thirteenth Turn Rising

Being dead is mostly waiting.

Nobody tells you that part. The couriers' guild trains you for weather, for wolves, for the kind of border guards who like to ask questions with their fists. Nobody trains you for the long gray afterward, when your body is done and what's left of you drifts after the living like a dog that missed the boat.

I'd been following Zephyrine Lockridge for three days now, and she still couldn't hear me properly. She heard *through* things — bone, ivory, teeth, anything that had once been somebody — but a ghost is not a thing. A ghost is an absence wearing a shape. So I did what I could: I leaned on her hearing when she touched bone, pushed words into the static of the dead, and hoped they arrived in something like the right order.

"Left," I told her, as she stood at the mouth of the bone-cellars with the moon-skin map folded against her ribs. "The road out of Grayharbor forks at the tannery. Left goes to the cemetery on the hill."

She flinched. Good. That meant some of it got through.

"You're going to hate where we're going," I said, though she couldn't answer me yet. "That's fine. I hated it too."

* * *

She surfaced from the cellars at dusk with sawdust in her hair and the map hidden in the lining of her coat, and she walked through Grayharbor like a woman trying to be furniture. I knew the walk. Every courier learns it. You make yourself boring and the world looks past you.

It almost worked.

At the tannery fork, two figures in guild gray stood talking under the eaves, and one of them turned his head as she passed — just slightly, just enough. Enforcers don't look at people. They look at *gaps*, at the places where a person should be and isn't. Zephyrine had been a gap in their ledger for three days, and gaps itch.

*Faster,* I urged her, pouring myself into the cold spot behind her shoulder, the way ghosts do when they want to be felt. *Take the left road. Walk like you're late.*

She took the left road. She walked like she was late. And the enforcer who had half-turned went back to his conversation, because even a hunting dog can be fooled by a rabbit that refuses to run.

The hill above Grayharbor was all graves and gorse, terraced up its spine in rows that had stopped being tidy a hundred years ago. The city buried its poor up here, and its forgotten, and its inconvenient. Wind came off the water and combed through the grass with a sound like someone reading aloud too fast.

Zephyrine stood at the gate and didn't go in.

I understood why. I'd stood at plenty of gates in my life, and I knew the difference between a door you can't open and a door you won't. This was the second kind.

"She's here," I said, and this time I put everything into it — every scrap of whatever it is that lets a dead man rattle a teacup or fog a mirror. "Your mother. Row nine, past the leaning stone, the one shaped like a wave. You carved it yourself. You'll know it when you see it."

Her breath went out of her in a long white ribbon.

"I don't remember carving it," she said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud to nobody is how the living keep from going strange. "I was fourteen. I don't remember—"

But her feet were already moving, which is the thing about grief: it knows the way before you do.

* * *

Row nine. The leaning stone. Shaped like a wave.

I watched her find it the way you watch someone touch a stove they've been warned about — knowing, helpless, necessary. The marker wasn't grand. It was maybe knee-high, sea-weathered, carved from graywhale bone by hands that had been better at it than they knew. A wave curling over a name worn nearly smooth:

**MAREN LOCKRIDGE**

And beneath the name, so small you'd miss it unless you were looking, a line of script no fourteen-year-old should have been able to write. Covenant-script. The old stitching-language, the one that holds the seams between the living realm and the dead.

She'd cut it into her mother's grave-marker without knowing what it was. Without knowing what *she* was. That's the thing about the Lockridge gift — it doesn't wait for permission. It leaks.

"That's it," I said, and my voice must have carried, because she went very still. "Under the name. The first key isn't *in* the stone, Zephyrine. It *is* the stone. Part of it. Your family's bones are in the ground under there, and the covenant-key grew into them like a root. You have to take it."

"No," she said.

"Yes."

"You don't understand." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I made this. I was a child and I made this, and I didn't know why, I just — my hands wanted to. And now you're telling me I have to dig up my mother's grave and break it apart, and for *what*? A map from a dying man I never met?"

"A map," I said, "from a man who died getting it to you."

Silence. The wind read its fast book through the grass.

"I'm sorry," I said, softer. "I'm sorry about the way I said it. Being dead has made me blunt. But Corvane's enforcers found the cellars empty tonight, and empty cellars make men think, and thinking men come to cemeteries, because cemeteries are where secrets get buried. We have minutes, not hours. Please."

She knelt. She dug with her hands, then with a carver's spade from her kit, and I looked away, because some things even the dead shouldn't watch. When I looked back she had the marker tilted up out of the earth, and she was running her thumb along the covenant-script, and her lips were moving — reading it, or remembering it, or both.

"It's a hinge," she whispered. "It's a *hinge*. How did I — I was *fourteen*—"

"Your hands knew," I said. "They always knew. That's why Corvane kept you filing ledgers. Not despite the gift. Because of it."

She went white. She'd been circling that thought for three days; I could tell I'd finally landed it square.

Then the light came over the hill.

* * *

Torches. Six of them, moving fast through the gorse, and ahead of them the smell I remembered from the border wars — lamp-oil and pitch. Enforcers don't carry fire to see by. They carry it to erase.

"*Run,*" I said, and threw myself into the wind to make it a shout, and whether she heard me or simply smelled the pitch herself, Zephyrine ran.

She ran wrong, though. She ran with the marker in her arms.

"Leave it!" I cried. "Leave the stone, take the key!"

"The key IS the stone—" but she was already working as she ran, which is the most Zephyrine thing I ever saw: fingers finding the seam in the covenant-script while her boots tore through wet grass, reading the hinge even as the hill burned behind her. Because that's what they were doing. They weren't chasing her. They were burning the cemetery. Burning row nine, burning the leaning stones, burning every name the harbor had tried to forget — and if the key stayed in the ground, it would burn with them.

Grief weaponized. That was Corvane's whole grammar. He never threatened the living. He burned the past instead, because he'd learned that the living will trade anything to keep their dead.

The first torch hit the gorse below row five and the hill lit up orange, and the smoke came down the slope thick and sweet and terrible, and I — I need to say this plainly, because ghosts rarely say anything plainly — I felt it. Fire doesn't hurt us. Nothing hurts us. But those graves held people I'd carried messages to and from, names I'd spoken across the veil, and watching the flames walk up the rows was like watching someone burn a library of letters that would never be answered again.

Zephyrine reached the crest and stopped, one instant, looking back.

"Don't," I begged her.

"My mother's in there."

"Your mother is *with me*," I said, and the truth of it rang through me like a struck bell. "Do you think the veil is only torn in one place? Do you think the dead can't feel their own names burning? She knows. She's telling you to run. Can't you hear her?"

And Zephyrine Lockridge — fluent in the dead, mute among the living, a woman who had spent her whole life translating other people's last words — went quiet, and listened, and whatever she heard coming up through the smoke and the burning grass, it made her turn east and run.

The marker rode in her arms like a child. Under her thumbs, the covenant-script had begun to glow a faint drowned green, and where it glowed, the bone softened, and by the time she reached the old coast road her hands had drawn the key free: a sliver of graywhale bone the length of a finger, dense as a debt, humming against her skin.

Behind us, the cemetery burned to the ridgeline. Ahead, the road ran dark toward the ferry-crossing and the Market of Impossible Things, and between here and there lay ten miles of open ground with six enforcers and a guildmaster who had just learned exactly which grave mattered.

"He'll know now," Zephyrine panted. She wasn't talking to me. She was talking to the key. Maybe to her mother. Maybe to herself. "He'll know what I am. He'll know where I'm going."

"Yes," I said.

"And he'll be kind about it." Her voice went flat and hard, harder than I'd heard it. "That's the worst part. He'll be *kind*."

She was right, and we both knew it, and neither of us said anything else for a long time. We walked east through the dark with the fire dying behind us and the thirteenth moon rising ahead — thin as a paring, sharp as a knife, one turn closer to full.

In my experience, that's how the big moments always arrive. Not with thunder. With a small light getting slowly, steadily brighter, while you walk toward it carrying everything you couldn't bear to leave behind.
