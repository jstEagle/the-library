## 6. The Market of Impossible Things

The drowned do not keep their market where the living can stumble into it. You have to be invited, or desperate enough that the tide mistakes you for one of its own.

Zephyrine was both.

Wren had led her south along the coast for two days, following a road that kept forgetting it was a road—dissolving into salt marsh, reassembling as a fisherman's path, vanishing entirely beneath a stretch of shingle that crunched like old teeth underfoot. She carried the map against her sternum, wrapped in oilcloth, and the first covenant-key in her pocket, warm as a held hand. Her mother's grave-marker had weighed almost nothing once she'd pried the carved sliver free. It was the weight of what it meant that slowed her steps.

"The market's ahead," Wren said. He drifted at her left shoulder, translucent in the gray morning light, his courier's coat still buttoned to the throat out of some habit death hadn't broken. "You'll smell it before you see it. Brine and beeswax. The dead trade in wax—it holds an impression the way nothing else does."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I carve bone. I know what holds an impression."

"You know relics." His voice had an edge she'd been hearing more often since the cemetery, since the smoke. "This is different. This is a place where you *spend*."

They crested the last dune and there it was.

The Market of Impossible Things sat in a tidal bowl below the high-water line, which meant it only existed between tides—and even then, only if you believed in it hard enough to see it. Stalls of blackened driftwood stood in rows along the wet sand, canopied with sails that had clearly once belonged to ships that no longer needed them. Lanterns burned underwater-green. And the vendors—the vendors were the drowned, or something adjacent to them: women with kelp braided through their hair and barnacles ringing their wrists like bracelets, men whose skin had the soft gray give of things long soaked, children who flickered when they moved too fast.

Every one of them turned to look at her.

"They can tell I'm alive," Zephyrine murmured.

"They can tell worse than that." Wren positioned himself between her and the nearest stall, though a ghost between a living woman and a dead merchant was a wall made of fog. "Listen to me. Everything here has a price, and none of the prices are written down until you've already agreed to pay. Ask three times before you buy anything. And whatever you do—"

"I know. Don't give them my name."

"Your name's not the danger anymore, Zephyrine." Something crossed his face—the ghost of a flinch. "What they'll want is what you're carrying up here." He tapped his own temple, once, with two fingers that passed through without resistance. "Memories are currency here. Good ones. The drowned have forgotten everything they loved, and they pay *dearly* to borrow the feeling back."

* * *

The ferryman found her before she found him.

He was set apart from the stalls, moored at the edge of the tidal pool where the water went suddenly, unnaturally deep—a boat of black wood with no visible seams, and a man standing in it who might have been forty or four hundred. His face was pleasant the way still water is pleasant. Around his neck hung a chain of small glass vials, each holding a curl of something luminous.

"Passage north," he said, before she'd spoken. "Past the Guild's cordon at Saltmere. Past the burning at Grayharbor's edge. I know where you're going, bone-carver. Everyone under the water knows where you're going. The thirteenth moon turns in six nights, and the whole drowned world can feel the stitch pulling loose."

"Then you know I need speed," Zephyrine said. "And silence."

"Speed I have. Silence costs extra." He smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Come aboard. Negotiations go better on the water. The living negotiate better when they can hear the deep listening."

She should have asked three times. Wren would later remind her of this, loudly and at length. But the enforcers' fires were two days behind her and Corvane's reach was longer than fire, and so she stepped down into the boat, and the moment her boot touched the deck the water around them went still as poured glass.

"Name your price," she said.

"Ah." The ferryman ran a finger along his chain of vials. "There it is. The question behind the question. Passage to the second mark, safe and swift and unseen—that's the goods. The price—" He looked at her the way she imagined an appraiser looks at an uncut stone. "A memory. One. Of my choosing from what you offer freely. And I warn you, bone-carver, I will choose well. That's my trade. I choose well."

Behind her, Wren's form sharpened with alarm. "*Zephyrine.*"

"Choose from what?" she said, keeping her eyes on the ferryman.

"From what you hold up. Open your hands—no, not your hands. Here." He touched his own temple again, mirroring Wren's gesture, and Zephyrine understood that this gesture was older than either of them. "Show me the shelf."

And because she had spent her whole life handling other people's dead, because she knew better than anyone that refusal at the wrong moment costs more than consent, she opened herself the way she opened a ledger.

The memories rose like fish coming to feed.

Her father teaching her to hold a chisel. The guildhouse kitchen, bread and tallow-smoke. The widow's tears on her workbench. The night she'd first heard a dead man speak through a half-finished carving and had run into the rain rather than believe it. And beneath all of these, glowing warmer than the rest, the one she'd kept at the very bottom of herself for twenty years:

A boy named Tam, sixteen years old, freckled across the nose, who had kissed her on the breakwater at Grayharbor while the autumn gulls screamed, and whose hand had fit around hers exactly, and who had died the following spring of the marsh-fever before either of them had learned what they were becoming. She had never told anyone about him. She had never carved him. Some things you keep off the ledger entirely, just to prove you're more than your accounts.

The ferryman's eyes fixed on it like a gull on a shining thing.

"No," Wren said. His voice cracked across the water. "No—not that one. Take the chisel lesson. Take the bread. Take anything—*Zephyrine*, don't you dare—"

"Quiet," she said, and it wasn't clear whether she meant Wren or herself.

"That one," the ferryman said softly. "The boy on the breakwater. Oh, that's a fine vintage. First love, unspent, ungrieved properly—sealed tight all these years. Do you know how rare that is? Most people wear theirs smooth by handling. Yours is mint." He drew one empty vial from the chain and held it out. "That's the price. That, and we sail within the minute."

"Take the bread instead," Wren begged. "Take the *bread*, Zephyrine, take the—"

"It's mine to spend," she said.

And here was the terrible truth, the one she would carry the rest of the way to the second mark and beyond: she wasn't saying it out of bravery. She was saying it because she had done the arithmetic, the way she did every day, every ledger, every small debt—and the sum came out clean.

Tam was twenty years gone. Grief for Tam had long ago stopped being a wound and become a wall—one she leaned against whenever the loneliness got loud, telling herself *you loved someone once, you were capable of it.* But walls don't warm you. They only hold you in. And ahead of her lay a covenant unraveling, a master hunting her, two keys still to find, and a choice that would demand everything she was. She could not afford to carry a sealed room inside herself. Not this road. Not now.

"Some things are worth forgetting," she said quietly. "Not because they mattered less. Because they matter so much they'd stop me otherwise."

"That's not wisdom," Wren snarled, "that's butchery—"

"It's bookkeeping," she said, "and it's the only kind I'm good at."

She reached out and closed her hand over the vial.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. There was a sensation like a page turning, like a name being struck neatly from a list, and then the breakwater was just a place, and the gulls were just birds, and somewhere in her chest a room she hadn't known she was standing in simply—wasn't. She swayed. The ferryman caught her elbow with a hand cold and gentle as deep water, and pressed the filled vial into her palm for one moment—letting her see, one last time, a curl of golden light turning slowly inside the glass, shaped like nothing, meaning everything.

"For the voyage," he said, "you may look at it. After that, it rides with me."

* * *

The boat moved faster than any living vessel had a right to move, skimming north through a sea gone the color of hammered tin. Zephyrine sat in the bow with the key in her pocket and the map against her heart and a strange new lightness she hated.

Wren sat opposite her—or perched, ghosts perch—and had not spoken in an hour.

"You're angry," she finally said.

"I'm *furious*." His voice was low and shaking, which for a ghost was nearly thunder. "Do you understand what you did? You gave away the proof. The proof that you were a person before you were a tool. Before the guild got its hooks in you, before Corvane, before any of this—you were a girl on a breakwater who got *kissed*. And you sold it to buy a boat ride."

"A boat ride to the second key," she corrected. "To the covenant. To the thing keeping every soul you know from spilling into our world screaming."

"And if saving the world costs you every warm thing in it, what exactly are you saving?"

She had no answer ready. That frightened her more than the enforcers had.

The ferryman, at the tiller, said nothing, but his eyes flicked to the vial at his throat, and once—just once—he touched it, the way a man touches a coin he hasn't decided to spend.

Night came down violet and starless in the east, except for one point of light rising where no moon had any business being. The thirteenth moon, early, hungry, a sliver of silver like a paring cut across the sky.

"There," Zephyrine whispered.

The ferryman followed her gaze, and for the first time his pleasant face showed something underneath. Fear, maybe. Or recognition.

"Bone-carver," he said, hauling the tiller hard, swinging the black boat toward a headland where a ruined tower clawed at the sky, "you should know—the second mark isn't sleeping. Something's already dug it open. And whatever's standing guard down there—" he glanced at the vial, then at her, "—it isn't one of yours, and it isn't one of mine."

Ahead, on the shore, torchlight moved in patterns too deliberate to be fishermen.

Guild torches.

Corvane had found the second key first.
