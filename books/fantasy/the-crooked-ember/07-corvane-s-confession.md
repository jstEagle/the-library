## 7. Corvane's Confession

The second key was hidden in a lighthouse that had never been lit.

That was the joke of it, Zephyrine thought as she climbed the spiral stair—her family had always had a taste for irony. The Saltmere Light stood at the end of the drowned causeway below Grayharbor, abandoned for sixty years, its lamp room dark since the night the sea took the keeper. Her grandmother. Zephyrine knew the story the way she knew all her family's stories: secondhand, worn smooth by retelling, emptied of anything true.

The key would not be empty. She could feel it already, a low hum in her teeth like a struck tuning fork, growing louder with every step.

Wren drifted beside her, translucent in the gray light leaking through the arrow-slits. "I don't like it," she said, for the fourth time since the cemetery. "It's too easy. Corvane's enforcers burned a graveyard to flush us out and now they've just... stopped?"

"Maybe he's out of enforcers."

"Maybe he doesn't need them anymore." Wren's edges flickered—the ghostly equivalent of pacing. "Zeph. He taught you everything you know about bone-work. Who better to guess where you'd run?"

Zephyrine didn't answer, because Wren was right, and because admitting it aloud felt like handing someone a knife.

The lamp room opened around her like the inside of a bell. Salt-crusted glass on every side, the sea flat and pewter-colored beyond, and in the center of the floor, set into the iron plate where the lamp's pedestal once stood—a socket. A hollow shaped exactly like something carved from a human femur.

Her grandmother's femur, if the map's inked annotations were honest. Carved into a lamp-pedestal by her own mother, who had learned the trade from Zephyrine without ever knowing what she was passing down. Three generations of women, stitching the covenant together one small carving at a time, and none of them told.

Zephyrine knelt beside the socket. Her fingers found the seam of the iron plate. She was reaching for the hidden catch when the door at the bottom of the stair closed, softly, below her.

Not slammed. Closed. With care.

She knew that care. She had heard it every morning of her adult life—Corvane entering the guildhouse workroom before dawn, moving among the sleeping apprentices' bunks to bank the fire, closing doors so no dream would be disturbed.

"He's here," Wren whispered. "Zeph. *Zeph.* We should go."

"There's nowhere to go," Zephyrine said. "He chose this room. He's been waiting."

She rose and turned to face the stairwell, and there he was, filling it the way he filled every doorway—not large, precisely, but *present*, the way a wall is present. Master Aldous Corvane, guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, wearing his traveling grays instead of his ceremonial blacks, his white hair tied back, his hands empty.

Empty hands. That frightened her more than drawn steel would have.

"You're not going to take it from me," she said.

"No." He stepped into the lamp room, unhurried. "You're going to give it to me. There's a difference, and I think you already understand the difference. It's why you lied to my enforcers on the tideline. It's why you're still climbing stairs instead of running for the ferries. You want to understand first. You've always wanted to understand first."

* * *

He did not approach the socket. He walked to the eastern window instead, where the glass had gone opaque with salt, and pressed one finger against it, clearing a small circle. Through it: Grayharbor, distant and hazy, its rooftops steaming in the morning cold. The living city. His city, though he'd never claimed it that way.

"Do you know how old the covenant is?" he asked.

"Older than the guild."

"Older than writing. Older than the wheel, probably. Some terrified ancestor looked at death and couldn't bear the silence after it, so they made a bargain: the dead may persist, whisper through relics, linger at the veil—but they pay for their persistence. And we pay for our magic. Every working draws from both sides of the ledger. Every spell is a debt shared between the living and the dead." He turned from the window. "Do you know who collects those payments?"

"The Voice," Zephyrine said. "The covenant's anchor. But the last Voice died two hundred years ago, and the covenant has run unattended since—"

"And look what unattended means." His voice stayed gentle. That was the worst part; it never rose, never sharpened. "Look at your widow, Zephyrine. The one whose father's finger-bone you carved last month. What did the dead man say?"

She hesitated. "A warning. About the moon."

"A warning. A *fragment*. Two hundred years without a Voice, and the dead have decayed into fragments—echoes repeating themselves in circles, memories bleeding out of them like light through cracked glass. They aren't people anymore. They're residue. Parasites on the living, drawing our strength across a bridge nobody maintains, giving back nothing but riddles and grief." He came closer, one step. "You hear them every day. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me the ledger of whispers isn't mostly static."

Zephyrine wanted to say he was wrong. She thought of the widow's father, his words looping, degrading, the same warning wearing thinner each time she listened. She thought of a hundred relics, a hundred voices gone hoarse and circular.

But she also thought of Wren.

"I've heard a courier," she said carefully, "who died three days ago and remembers everything. Argues. Complains. Remembers her own name."

"Fresh ghosts are whole. For a while." Another step. "And then the decay takes them too, because the covenant is broken, Zephyrine, and here is the confession you've earned: *I broke it.*"

The words landed in the salt-still air and seemed to hang there.

"Not carelessly. Not cruelly. I spent thirty years studying the tear-points before I touched the first one. One mark, quietly severed, in a marsh no one visits. A controlled experiment. And do you know what happened? Nothing. No catastrophe. No wailing of shades. The magic simply... pooled. Stayed on our side of the ledger. For the first time in history, the living kept what the living earned." His eyes were bright, and she recognized the brightness—it was the same light she'd seen in her own reflection some mornings, after a carving went well. Conviction. "One mark torn bought us a decade of abundance. Imagine what a full severance buys. No more tithe. No more dead men's fingers in our pockets. A world where magic belongs to the people who are alive to use it."

"And the dead?"

"Gardeners pull weeds, Zephyrine. It doesn't make them monsters."

"They're not weeds." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "They're people who haven't finished."

"They're *finished*," he said, "and they won't admit it. That's all death is—an argument with an ending. And we have been forced to listen to the argument forever because some ancestor couldn't bear to close the book." He spread his empty hands. "I'm offering to close it. Cleanly. Kindly. And I'm offering you the place you were born for."

* * *

There it was. She had known it was coming since the stairwell, and still it went through her like cold water.

"The Voice," she said.

"The new Voice. The covenant is dying, but it can be replaced—rebuilt, if you like, on new terms. An anchor that binds the realms not through mutual debt but through a single will. Yours. You'd hold the door shut yourself. No more fragments, no more parasites, no more widows paying carvers to hear static. And no more hiding." He smiled, and it was the smile from the workroom, warm as banked coals. "Did you think I didn't know? All these years, hearing the dead through your carvings and filing it away in secret ledgers? Zephyrine. I built the Guild of Impossible Things as a shelter for gifts like yours. I have been waiting for you since the day you arrived, sixteen years old, flinching every time you touched a relic. I let you hide because hiding was what you needed. Now you don't need it anymore."

And gods help her, some traitor part of her leaned toward it.

Sixteen years of secrecy. Sixteen years of carving truth into bone by lamplight and lying about it by daylight, fluent in the dead and mute among the living. And here was the only man who had ever made the guildhouse feel like a home, saying: *you were never wrong to be what you are. You were only early.*

"You'd be worshipped," Corvane said quietly. "Or respected, if worship embarrasses you—I remember that it does. You'd speak for the living realm, and the dead would fade the way a fever fades. Gently. People would stop tearing themselves open over graves. Grief would become what it should have been all along: an ending, honored and complete." He gestured at the socket in the floor. "Take the key. Bring it to me. And when the thirteenth moon turns, stand at the final mark and seal the new covenant as its Voice. Your gift, finally given a purpose worthy of it."

Zephyrine looked down at the iron plate. Her hand was already resting on the hidden catch. She hadn't told it to.

"That's the temptation, isn't it," she said. "That it all makes sense."

"It makes sense because it's *true*."

"No." She lifted her hand from the catch. "It makes sense because you left out the cost. You always leave out the cost—that's what ledgers are for, and you've been keeping yours in the dark." She turned to face him fully. "What happens to Wren?"

Something flickered behind his eyes. "The courier?"

"She's standing right beside me. She died getting that map to the tideline. She drowned doing the right thing, and under your new covenant she doesn't fade *gently*, does she? She gets erased. Unmade. Every ghost, every echo, every voice—including the ones that are still whole, still arguing, still *themselves*. You're not pulling weeds. You're burning a library because some of the books are damaged."

"The books are *suffering*—"

"Some of them. So am I, some days. So is everyone. We don't unmake people for suffering, Master Corvane. We mend them." Her hand found the catch at last, deliberately now. "You taught me that. In this very guild. You said a bone-carver's job is not to shape the bone into what she wants. It's to find what the bone already wants to be, and free it."

For a long moment he said nothing. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone—not replaced by anger, which she could have fought, but by something worse: patience.

"I taught you that," he agreed, "when you were carving keepsakes. This is larger than keepsakes." He glanced at the socket, then back at her, and sighed like a man setting down a heavy load. "I had hoped you'd come willingly. I want you to know that. Whatever happens next, I want you to know that I hoped."

"What happens next?"

He stepped aside from the stairwell door—and in the doorway stood three enforcers in gray, and behind them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the causeway being sealed. Stone grinding against stone. No way out except up, and nothing above but sky.

"The key stays in the lighthouse either way," Corvane said. "The only question left is whether you're holding it when the moon turns—or whether you're somewhere safe, grieving, and I'm holding it for you. Take until moonrise. Consider what I've offered. And consider, Zephyrine—" his voice gentled one final time, unbearably, "—that I am the only person who has never asked you to hide."

He descended. The enforcers did not follow him out; they stayed, flanking the door, and the lamp room settled into a silence broken only by the sea and the hum rising from the socket beneath the iron plate.

Wren drifted to her side. "Well," the ghost said. "That was horrible."

"He meant it," Zephyrine whispered. "Every word. That's what frightens me. He wasn't lying."

"Liars are easy. True believers are the ones who burn things down." Wren's glow dimmed. "So. Moonrise. What's the plan, Voice-to-be?"

Zephyrine knelt and tripped the catch. The iron plate swung open, and there it lay in its bed of salt-black cloth: a femur carved end to end with script finer than spider silk, her grandmother's name worked into the shaft in her mother's hand, and beneath the name, in a third hand she recognized with a jolt as her own—younger, clumsier, from a visit she barely remembered—one word.

*Listen.*

She lifted the key, and the hum in her teeth became words, and the words were not her grandmother's.

They were a warning, fresh and urgent, in a voice she knew from the guildhouse workroom—from a hundred quiet mornings:

*"Moonrise is a lie. He moves tonight."*
