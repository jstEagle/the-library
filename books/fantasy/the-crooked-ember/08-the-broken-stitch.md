## 8. The Broken Stitch

The mark was not where the map said it would be.

Zephyrine stood on the shingle at the world's edge, where Grayharbor's last street gave up and became tide-flat and nothing, and she understood that the map had never been wrong—it had simply been drawn before the ground finished moving. The covenant-mark that should have sat beneath the old lighthouse now lay exposed in a bowl of torn earth, as if something beneath the soil had exhaled after centuries of holding its breath.

It looked like a scar. That was her first thought, professional and cold: *a scar in the shape of a knot*. The stone beneath was pale as uncarved bone, and across its surface ran channels and whorls, the same interlocking pattern she'd spent her life cutting into keepsakes without ever knowing what she was copying. Her family's pattern. Her hands' inheritance.

The second key hung at her throat on a cord of braided hair—her mother's knucklebone, warm against her sternum like a swallowed coal. It had warmed further with every mile. Now it was nearly hot enough to hurt.

"It knows where it is," Wren said.

She could see him plainly here, which frightened her more than anything else had. In the guildhouse cellars he'd been a shimmer, a suggestion, a voice with edges. Here, standing on the rim of the broken mark, he was solid as a living man: courier's leathers salt-stiffened, one boot missing, his drowned-pale face wearing an expression she was learning to read as his version of gentleness.

"Then we're late," Zephyrine said.

"We were always late." He crouched at the mark's edge but did not touch it. "The first stitch tore weeks ago. Maybe months. Everything since—the moon coming wrong, the dead getting loud, Corvane's enforcers burning their way across three counties—we've been running along the hem of something already unraveling."

Zephyrine knelt. Up close the mark smelled of iodine and old rain, and beneath that, faintly, of the bone-dust she'd breathed for twenty years in her cellar workshop. She set her palm flat against the stone.

And heard everything.

Not words this time. Not the tidy whispers she pulled from carved relics, one voice per bone, each in its little room. This was a crowd. A city of voices pressed against the far side of a door, and the door was thin, and thinning, and somewhere below her palm something turned toward her touch the way a sleeper turns toward light.

She snatched her hand back.

"Zeph." Wren's voice, careful. "What did you hear?"

"Everyone." Her mouth had gone dry. "Not one dead man in a finger-bone. Everyone."

* * *

*They don't know what they're standing on,* Wren thought—or said; there was no difference anymore, thought and speech being the same weather inside a ghost. *That's the mercy of it. She hears them the way you'd hear rain on a roof. I remember them.*

He remembered because he had carried them. Eleven years a courier between the realms' quiet places, running letters and relics and small mercies along the stitches that held the world together, and every stitch had a weight of souls leaning against it from the other side—not clawing, not screaming, just *leaning*, the way the poor lean against a rich man's wall for its warmth.

Corvane called them parasites. Wren had carried Corvane's own sealed orders to the tearing-crews and had read them, because a courier who doesn't read what he carries deserves what he delivers. *Thin the veil at the outmarks. Let pressure equalize.* As if the dead were air. As if hunger were a leak to be patched.

Now the pressure was equalizing all at once, everywhere, and Wren stood on the largest tear with the woman who could stop it—if she chose to pay.

He watched her take the third key from its wrapping. Her own knucklebone, taken from her own hand years ago by a surgeon who told her it was cancerous tissue and burned the rest. Carved by her own knife during the fever nights she didn't remember. It sat in her palm like a seed, like a debt, like the smallest most terrible thing in the world.

"You don't have to do it tonight," he said, though the sky said otherwise. The thirteenth moon was rising wrong again, swollen and off-center, dragging the tide out past all reason until the sea stood a mile away, bared and steaming, exposing things the water had kept polite about for generations: wrecked hulls, sunken bells, the foundations of a town no chart admitted to.

"The moon finishes its turn tomorrow," Zephyrine said. "There is no other night."

"There's always another way. We just haven't found it yet."

"Wren." She looked at him, and he saw she'd already done the arithmetic and hidden the answer behind her eyes. "Restore the covenant, and the price is my memory. All of it. Every name in my ledger, every voice I've ever heard, every—" her hand went briefly to her collarbone, where the second key hung— "every person I've loved. I become a clean instrument. A Voice with nothing left to say."

"I know."

"And if I refuse, the veil tears fully, and everything pours through, and the living realm—" She stopped. Below them, in the bowl of the mark, the pale stone had begun to sweat. A fine moisture beading up through the channels of the knot-pattern, catching moonlight.

"That's not water," Wren said quietly.

* * *

It came up like frost forming in reverse.

Zephyrine watched the first of them arrive and braced herself for horror and received instead something far worse: recognition.

They rose out of the stone the way breath fogs a cold window. Shapes first, then features, then faces—and the faces were ordinary. A fisherman with tarred hands and a net-scar across his cheek. A girl of perhaps eight wearing a smock two sizes too large, sleeves rolled and rolled. An old woman carrying a bundle of kindling that had rotted to nothing decades ago and still she carried it, because her hands remembered the work even if the wood had gone.

Not monsters. Not the ravening dead of guild sermons, the hungry shades Corvane had built his whole philosophy upon. These were the forgotten poor. The unmarked graves. The ones who died owing money, or owed nothing and were owed everything, and whose names had slipped out of every ledger because ledgers only hold what someone paid to have written down.

The fisherman saw her and his face broke open with hope so naked it was obscene.

"You can see us," he said. His voice came from very far away and very close, both at once. "Lady. Lady, you can *see* us."

More of them now, dozens, rising through the shingle and the torn earth, filling the bowl of the mark like floodwater filling a cellar. The child stopped in front of Zephyrine and held up her empty, rolled sleeves.

"I lost my shoes," the girl said, apologetic, as if this were the whole tragedy. "I've been looking such a long time. Have you seen my shoes?"

Zephyrine's training failed her completely. There was no filing this away, no neat entry in a ledger of small debts. She knelt—knee-deep now in the gathering dead—and heard herself say, "What was your name?"

"Maret," the girl said, and the saying of it seemed to make her more solid, the way wind makes a flag real. "Maret Coble. My mother called me Ret. Nobody's called me anything in—" she frowned, counting on fingers that had no number— "I don't know how long."

Around them the dead were pressing closer, and every one of them had a name, and every one of them gave it, tumbling over each other, a tide of syllables: *Halewick, Tamsen, the brothers Odd, Widow Ferris, a boy called Sparrow, a midwife named Grace who delivered half of Grayharbor and was buried in the potter's field with no stone—*

"Stop," Zephyrine whispered. "Stop, I can't—"

But she could. That was the curse of her. She heard them all, distinctly, each voice finding its shelf in her mind, and she understood suddenly, horribly, beautifully, why the covenant demanded memory as payment. Because a Voice between realms must hold every name. That was the work. That was the whole unbearable work.

"They're not attacking," Wren said beside her. He sounded undone. "Corvane told us they'd pour through hungry. They're just—they want to be *counted*."

An old man took Zephyrine's hand. His grip was cold river-water. "We felt the stitching go," he said. "We felt it loosen, year by year. And we thought—someone up there will mend it. Someone remembers how. But the menders stopped coming, and the letters stopped coming, and then the young courier—" he nodded at Wren— "he came through the wrong way, drowned, and he told us someone was tearing it on purpose."

The crowd's murmur changed key. Grief to something harder.

"He said a woman would come," the old man went on. "A carver. One who hears. He said she'd stand where we're standing and hold the last piece in her hand." His clouded eyes dropped to her open palm, to the knucklebone gleaming there. "Is that true, lady? Are you the one?"

Every face turned to her. Hundreds now. Thousands, maybe, still rising from deeper down, from the bared seabed, from the drowned town's foundations, from every potter's field and plague pit and unmarked grave the living realm had ever dug and walked away from.

Zephyrine looked at the key in her hand. Her own bone. The price of restoring the covenant was written into its grain: *give up your memory, give up your names, become a vessel with nothing in it.* And the price of refusing was written in the faces before her: *let them pour through forever, nameless, homeless, a flood with nowhere to go.*

Behind her, at the rim of the mark, boots on stone. She knew the tread. She had known it for fifteen years, had trusted it for fifteen years, and her stomach turned over with a grief that had nothing to do with the dead.

Master Corvane stood at the edge of the crowd of ghosts, and he was not afraid of them, and that was the most frightening thing she had ever seen.

"Well," he said gently. "Now you understand why it has to end."

Zephyrine closed her hand around the knucklebone. It burned.

"No," she said. "Now I understand what it was *for*."

The thirteenth moon reached the top of the sky, and the veil began, at last, to come apart.
