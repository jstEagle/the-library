## 1. The Taster's Oath

The honey cake arrived at dawn, as it always did, on a chipped blue plate that had survived three reigns and one very determined kitchen cat.

Paloma Brightwater looked at it the way a farmer looks at weather: with respect, with resignation, and with the quiet certainty that whatever came next was out of their hands.

"Poisoned?" they asked.

"Obviously," said Cook Maren, who had arms like bread loaves and a face that suggested she'd personally resented every morning since the Dowager's coronation. "Arsenic in the glaze. Master Ferren's orders. The King's cousin from the eastern marches arrives at midday, and His Majesty wants to know if his kitchens can be breached."

"And if I die?"

"Then we'll know the recipe needs work." Maren slid the plate across the scarred oak counter. "Eat."

This was, Paloma reflected — as they did every morning, and would likely do every morning until one of these mornings finally killed them — a strange way to earn a living. Most people in the Valley of Vell grew up to be farmers or fletchers or keepers of bees. A lucky few went into the wool trade, which in Vell meant you were rich by Tuesday. Paloma had grown up to swallow poison for a living, and had done so since they were fourteen years old, when a palace steward had looked at their slight frame, their unremarkable face, and their orphan's hunger, and said: *You'll do.*

They picked up the cake.

It smelled wonderful. That was the cruelty of the work — the food was always wonderful. The palace confectioners were artists, and arsenic apparently paired beautifully with wildflower honey and a whisper of lemon zest. Paloma took a bite. Then another. The cake crumbled sweet against their tongue, and somewhere beneath the honey, faint as a rumor, sat the bitter mineral ghost of the poison.

They chewed. They swallowed. They set down the fork.

"Time?"

Maren squinted at the water clock in the corner. "Two minutes."

Paloma nodded and folded their hands in their lap, which was what you did while your body decided whether today was the day. Their mouth tingled. It always tingled; nine years of measured doses had built a wall inside them, brick by brick, dose by dose, but the wall still let you know it was there. A warmth spread up from their stomach, not quite pain, not quite pleasure — the feeling of a body arguing with itself and winning.

At minute four, the tingle faded. At minute six, Paloma asked for a second slice, because breakfast was breakfast, and Maren laughed her gravel laugh and cut one.

"You're getting fat on poison," she said fondly.

"It's the only thing that sticks."

* * *

The Valley of Vell had no magic, and this was its great fortune.

Paloma had known this the way you know the color of your own eyes: completely, without ever really thinking about it. Magic lived everywhere else in the world — in the empire beyond the mountains, where mages wore their power like jewelry and nations rose and fell according to whose patron wizard woke up crankier. But not here. Here, spells curdled and died at the border like milk left in sun. No mage could scry into Vell. No imperial army could find it on any map, because there was no map. The valley simply *wasn't*, in the way that mattered to the wide hungry world, and so the wide hungry world passed over it, generation after generation, the way a wolf passes a stone that looks like nothing worth eating.

Safe, the elders called it. Boring, said the young people, who then grew old enough to say *safe* themselves.

Paloma had made peace with boring early. Boring meant the same faces at market. Boring meant the bell in the palace tower ringing the hours, and the river running brown-green through town, and the smell of woodsmoke in autumn that never changed because nothing was allowed to change, because change required an outside world paying attention, and no one outside was paying attention to anything at all.

It should have been suffocating. Mostly it was. But there were mornings — this morning, for instance, standing in the warm steam of the palace kitchens with arsenic dissolving pleasantly in their belly — when Paloma thought: *I know exactly where I am. I know exactly who I am. How many people in the world can say that?*

Probably not many. Probably fewer every year, though they couldn't have told you why the thought arrived dressed in such strange clothes.

"You're brooding," said Marisol, sliding onto the counter beside them with a heel of bread and the expression of someone about to cause trouble. She was the other royal taster, younger by two years, quicker by ten, with a knife scar on her jaw she claimed was from a duel and everyone else knew was from falling off a hay cart drunk. "You get that line between your eyebrows. Like a little stitch."

"I'm contemplating my mortality."

"You're thinking about the archivist."

Paloma opened their mouth to object and found, annoyingly, nothing to put in it.

Marisol grinned and tore her bread in half, offering a piece. "Sef's cataloguing the sealed rooms this week. Third floor of the west archive. I heard him tell the steward he'd need someone to carry boxes."

"The steward has porters."

"The steward has *old* porters." She took a bite, chewing with tremendous self-satisfaction. "You have arms. You have a whole day off after the tasting. And you have been making eyes at that man for eight months, which in Vell is practically a betrothal."

"I don't make eyes."

"You make eyes like a lighthouse makes light. Everyone in the kitchens has a betting pool. Hobb has two coppers on you never speaking to him at all."

"Hobb is a pessimist with savings."

"He's a realist with savings. There's a difference." Marisol hopped off the counter, brushed crumbs from her apron, and pointed the bread at Paloma like a small edible sword. "Third floor. West archive. Boxes. Go be brave about something that isn't poison for once."

* * *

The west archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and, underneath both, the particular dry sweetness of very old paper — a smell Paloma loved without understanding why, the way you love a song you've heard since before memory.

Sef was exactly where Marisol had promised, halfway up a ladder with an armful of ledgers, his spectacles pushed up into his dark hair, squinting at a shelf label as though it had insulted him personally. He was long-limbed and ink-stained and moved through the archive's silence like he belonged to it, like the books had grown him along with themselves.

He looked down. He saw Paloma. He dropped precisely one ledger, caught it against his knee with a fumble that was almost graceful, and said, "Oh. Hello."

"Hello," said Paloma, who had rehearsed eleven openings on the walk over and deployed none of them. "I'm told you need boxes carried."

"I— yes. Possibly. There are boxes." Sef descended the ladder with the ledger clutched to his chest like a shield. "Although I should say the boxes are mostly empty. I'm cataloguing the sealed collection. Prophecies, mostly. Old ones. The palace sealed the room a century ago and nobody's touched it since, which means everything in it is either priceless or rubbish, and historically it's been about half and half."

"Which are the boxes?"

"The rubbish goes in the boxes." He hesitated, then smiled — quick, crooked, gone again. "Would you like to see the priceless half? Nobody ever wants to see the priceless half. Marisol came by yesterday and asked if any of it was flammable."

"That does sound like her."

"She also asked if prophecy books could predict lottery numbers. I said no. She seemed genuinely disappointed in the entire discipline of prophecy."

Paloma laughed, and Sef laughed too, and for a moment the archive felt less like a tomb of dead paper and more like a room where something might begin.

He showed them anyway. He walked them down the rows of shelving with a lamp held high, talking with his whole body, hands sketching the shapes of things: almanacs that predicted harvests wrong for forty straight years, a book of dreams some forgotten queen had paid a fortune for, star charts of constellations that didn't exist — *couldn't* exist, Sef said, the mathematics were impossible — and shelf after shelf of prophecy volumes, their spines cracked, their gilt flaked away, their pages full of futures that had quietly failed to happen.

"This is my favorite," Sef said, stopping at a lectern near the back. On it lay a book bound in green leather gone gray with age. "Nobody knows who wrote it. The hand changes halfway through — different scribes, maybe a hundred years apart. And the prophecies are all nonsense except—" He stopped himself, ears going pink. "Sorry. You came to carry boxes. I'm doing the thing where I talk too much."

"I like the thing," Paloma said, and meant it more than they'd meant most things lately. "Show me the nonsense."

Sef's smile stayed a beat longer this time. He turned the pages carefully, gloved to the wrist, and read aloud in a low voice — fragments, mostly. *A king will dream of drowning and wake thirsty.* *A red winter, followed by a redder spring.* *The bell tower will crack on the feast of Saint Ovel and be repaired within the year.* All of it vague, all of it harmless, all of it the sort of thing any clever fraud could write and wait a century to be accidentally right about.

Except.

Paloma leaned closer. Near the bottom of the open page, the handwriting changed — newer, sharper, the ink barely faded at all. And the words there weren't vague at all.

*On the eve of the eastern cousin's visit,* the fresh ink read, *the door opens.*

Paloma frowned. "That's tonight. The King's cousin from the eastern marches — Maren mentioned it this morning. That's why I ate the poisoned cake."

"Yes," Sef said slowly. "Yes, it is."

"And this book is a hundred years old. Sealed. Untouched." Paloma heard their own voice go careful, the way it went careful when they tasted something and couldn't yet name the flavor. "How does a sealed century-old book know what's happening tonight?"

Sef didn't answer. He was staring past Paloma's shoulder at the page, at something below the writing, and his face had gone the color of the old paper around them.

Paloma looked down.

Beneath the words, sketched in fine confident lines, was a drawing of a door — not a palace door, not any door in Vell that Paloma knew, but a doorway grown rather than built, framed in twisting vines and blossoms, crooked as a question mark. And standing before it, one hand raised toward the vines, was a figure.

Small. Unremarkable. Dressed in a taster's plain gray apron.

With Paloma's exact face.

The lamp flame guttered, though there was no draft. Somewhere far below, the palace bell began to ring the noon hour, and the sound came up through the floors like water rising.

"That's me," Paloma said. Not a question. Their voice sounded very small in all that silence. "Sef. That's *me*."

Sef reached out and closed the book with hands that were not quite steady. When he spoke, his voice had the forced calm of a man reading a label in the dark.

"I think," he said, "we should carry the boxes now. Quickly."

* * *

Outside the archive window, the afternoon lay golden and ordinary over the Valley of Vell. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight unhurried lines. Farmers brought carts in from the fields. The river ran its brown-green course, and the mountains stood watch on every side, and nothing anywhere suggested that the safe, boring, mapless world had noticed them at all.

Paloma pressed a palm flat against the cool stone sill and felt, beneath the skin, the faint familiar tingle of the morning's arsenic — proof that they were alive, proof that they were immune, proof that they knew exactly how to survive anything anyone put on a plate.

They just wished someone had warned them that survival might require swallowing this instead.
