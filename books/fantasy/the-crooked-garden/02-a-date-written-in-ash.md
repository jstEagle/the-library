## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The sealed room at the top of the palace's east tower had a smell Sef had come to think of as *waiting*—dust and tallow and old paper, the scent of a thousand years of things nobody wanted to know yet.

He let himself in with the archivist's key, which was really three keys braided together on a cord of woven hair, because in Vell even locks were made of something a person had given up. The door sighed open. Candlelight from his lantern slid across shelves that ran floor to ceiling, every one of them crammed with books bound in leather, in bark, in salted fish-skin, in one memorable case in the pressed petals of a flower that no longer grew anywhere in the valley.

Protocol said he catalogued alone. Protocol said the prophecy room was opened once a season, dusted once a year, and read never. The Dowager Queen's own grandmother had ordered it so, after the last royal seer drank herself to death trying to interpret a verse about her granddaughter's wedding. Prophecy, the palace had decided, was like arsenic: best kept locked away where only professionals could handle it.

Sef was not, strictly speaking, a professional. He was twenty-three years old, underpaid, and the sort of person who alphabetized his spice rack by country of origin. But he was the only archivist left who could read Old Vellish script without moving his lips, and so the task fell to him: catalogue the sealed collection before the winter solstice, note any deterioration, touch nothing with bare hands.

He touched everything with bare hands. It was a small rebellion, the kind he could afford.

"Good morning, you beautiful disasters," he told the shelves, and began.

* * *

The morning went the way mornings always went in the tower: slowly, then all at once.

He worked through a shelf of almanacs whose predictions had all been wrong in instructively boring ways—hail in high summer, a king's second marriage, a flood that never came. He found a cookbook with prophetic marginalia, which was either a joke or a tragedy; the recipe for eel pie sat directly beneath a warning about "the pale rider on the eastern road," and Sef chose to believe someone had simply been hungry while doom-watchful. He catalogued forty-one items by noon, ate a cold pigeon pie on the windowsill with his boots against the wall, and watched the valley go gold below him—the terraced fields, the crooked bell tower, the smoke rising straight up from chimneys because there was no wind, there was never wind until the mountain decided otherwise.

Vell did not appear on maps. Sef had known this the way you know your own heartbeat: completely and without thinking about it. Travelers came through the passes sometimes, got confused by the roads, and left again, their eyes sliding off the valley like rain off waxed cloth. The empire beyond the mountains was a story told to frighten children. *They ate the world,* his mother used to say, carving bread. *We are the crumb under the table they forgot to sweep.*

He had never wondered, until this year, why the forgetting felt so much like being hidden.

By late afternoon he reached the third shelf of the far wall, the one behind the broken lectern, and pulled out a book that weighed wrong.

That was the only way to describe it. Most old books weighed like paper and glue and the slow accumulation of dust. This one weighed like a held breath. It was bound in gray leather gone soft as chamois, unmarked except for a single stamped image on the cover: a door, slightly askew, framed in what might have been vines or might have been veins.

The clasp was cold. The pages inside were warm.

Sef stood very still in the amber light and listened to his own pulse. Then, because he was who he was, he opened it.

* * *

The first hundred pages were ordinary prophecy, if anything about prophecy could be called ordinary: verses in Old Vellish, tight and musical, foretelling harvests and deaths and one remarkably specific incident involving a bear, a wedding, and a vat of pickled quinces. Dated entries. A century of them, marching forward page by page, each prediction written down before the thing it predicted could possibly have been known.

And then the dates stopped being history.

Sef noticed it the way you notice a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. A date. Then another. Then a run of them, close together, recent—and then, near the bottom of the right-hand page, in ink still faintly glossy, as though the words had dried an hour ago instead of a hundred years:

*Tonight.*

Not tonight in general. Tonight specifically. The full date, month and day and year, written in a dead woman's hand a hundred years before Sef was born: *On this night the door remembers itself, and the taster stands before it.*

His mouth went dry. He turned the page.

There was a sketch.

It was done in charcoal, quick and confident, the kind of drawing an artist does when they've seen the thing already and are merely copying it down. A figure stood before a doorway grown out of living hedge—vines twisted into an arch, leaves shading into feathers shading into something like hands. The door was crooked. Everything about it was crooked, beautifully, deliberately wrong, like a garden that had decided architecture was a suggestion.

And the figure—

Sef knew that face. He had spent eleven months learning that face across the width of a kitchen table, over stolen honey cakes and arguments about whether thyme belonged in everything (it did; Paloma was wrong). The sketch showed a young person in a taster's plain gray coat, one hand raised toward the vine-wrought door, head turned as if listening to something behind them. The artist had caught the exact tilt of Paloma Brightwater's chin when they were pretending not to be afraid.

Beneath the sketch, one more line, smaller, pressed hard enough into the paper to nearly tear it:

*They will have swallowed death a hundred times and lived. Only such a mouth can knock.*

The lantern guttered. Outside, the light had gone the color of bruised plums, and somewhere below in the palace kitchens, Sef knew, the evening meal was beginning its long assembly—sauces reducing, ovens breathing heat, and Paloma standing at the Queen's table with the first spoonful of everything lifted to their lips, calm as tide, swallowing whatever the day might kill them with.

Sef looked at the book. The book, in its patient, papery way, looked back.

"Against protocol," he said aloud, to no one, and tucked it under his arm.

* * *

He told himself he was going to log it first. Log it, seal it, report it to the Dowager's steward, follow every rule like a good little archivist, and let the machinery of the palace decide what a hundred-year-old book naming tonight meant.

He got as far as the second landing of the tower stairs before his feet made the decision for him, carrying him down and across the courtyard and through the service corridor that smelled of onions and lamp oil, toward the kitchens, toward Paloma, holding a forbidden book against his chest like a stolen loaf.

The royal kitchens at evening were a cathedral of noise. Copper pots rang. The head cook, Mistress Aldery, conducted her brigade with a wooden spoon and a vocabulary that would blister paint. And at the long scrubbed table nearest the hearth sat the tasters—Paloma among them, sleeves rolled, methodically working through a tray of small dishes with the focused serenity of people whose job description included dying politely.

Marisol was mid-rant about the new sous-chef. Hobb dozed upright, as he did between courses, his ancient face slack and peaceful. Bramble was palming bread rolls with the smoothness of long practice. And Paloma—Paloma looked up the moment Sef came through the door, the way they always did, some taster's instinct keyed to his footsteps, and smiled with gravy on their lip.

"You're late," they said. "You missed the fish course. Marisol says it was trying too hard."

"The fish course has no inner life," Marisol agreed, without pausing her rant. "It is a fish that dreamed of being a sauce and woke up as neither."

"Sef." Hobb cracked one eye. "You look like a man who's seen a ghost with excellent posture."

"I need Paloma," Sef said. His voice came out strange. Flat. Too careful. "Alone. For a moment."

The kitchen's noise seemed to bend around the sentence. Paloma set down their spoon. Whatever was in Sef's face, they read it fast—they were trained to detect poison in a mouthful, after all, and fear apparently tasted similar—and rose, wiping their hands on their coat.

They met in the scullery passage, where the light was one candle and the noise was a rumor. Sef held the book against his chest a moment longer, feeling its wrong warm weight, and then made himself open it to the marked page and turn it around.

"Read the date," he said.

Paloma read it. Their lips moved. Sef watched the recognition arrive in stages—the frown first, then the stillness, then the slow lift of their eyes to his.

"That's today," Paloma said. "That's—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "That's tonight."

"Keep reading."

Paloma read. Sef watched them read, watched the candlelight move on their face, watched the exact moment the words *the taster stands before it* landed. Then Paloma looked down at the sketch, and Sef saw their breath change—the small hitch, quickly mastered, of a person who has just seen their own face looking back at them from a hundred years ago.

"That's me." Not a question. Their finger hovered above the charcoal figure without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping animal. "That's my—that's my face. That's my *chin*, Sef, nobody draws my chin but you, and you've never drawn me looking like I'm about to walk into a hedge."

"It was drawn before either of us was born."

"Don't say that."

"It was drawn a hundred years ago, Paloma. The ink isn't even fully dry. I checked. I—I don't know how that's possible, but I checked, because checking things is the only prayer I know." He heard his own voice climbing and hauled it down. "There's more. Read the last line."

Paloma read it. *Only such a mouth can knock.* Their hand drifted, unconsciously, to their throat—to the throat that had swallowed arsenic and nightshade and a hundred small engineered deaths, one dish at a time, for six years, building immunity the way other people built savings.

"What door?" Paloma asked. It came out almost angry. "There's no door in Vell. There's nothing *in* Vell, that's the whole point of Vell. No magic, no prophecy, no empire, nothing for anyone to find. We're the crumb under the table. So what is this? What is this book doing saying my name?"

"It doesn't say your name."

"It drew my *chin*, Sef."

"I know." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "I know. I've been standing in that tower for an hour trying to make the book be about something else. A coincidence. An ancestor. A very prescient portraitist. And every explanation is worse than the last one, because if it's an ancestor, then someone in your family stood at a door made of vines, and there are no doors made of vines anywhere in this valley, I would know, I've catalogued every garden record in the archive—"

"Breathe."

"—and if it's *not* an ancestor, then it's you, and it's *tonight*, and I brought it here against protocol because I couldn't stand the thought of it sitting in the dark knowing something about you that I—" He stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere he hadn't authorized.

Paloma was quiet for a moment. In the kitchen beyond, Marisol's voice rose and fell; a pot lid clattered; life continued being ordinary at a volume designed to be reassuring.

"Show me the door again," Paloma said finally.

Sef turned the page back. Together, heads bent, they studied the crooked arch, the vines feathering into hands, the figure with the familiar chin reaching toward it. Up close, Sef noticed things he'd missed in the tower: tiny details rendered with obsessive care. A leaf shaped like a tongue. A stone at the threshold carved with a cup and a knife. And along the base of the door, so small it was almost subliminal, a line of Old Vellish script that he translated half-aloud, the way he translated everything, compulsively:

"*Payment at the gate. The toll is memory.*"

Paloma went very still.

"Memory," they repeated.

"A memory. One. Paid to—whoever keeps the door. That's what it says." Sef traced the letters without touching them. "Paloma, whoever wrote this knew. A hundred years ago, someone knew there was a door out of Vell, and knew it took a toll, and knew a taster would be standing in front of it on this exact night. Which means either this whole valley is built on top of something we were never told—"

"Or someone's been keeping it from us," Paloma finished.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Somewhere far above them, in the sealed room at the top of the east tower, Sef thought absurdly of all the other books on all the other shelves, a thousand years of locked-away knowledge sitting in the dark, patient as arsenic, waiting for a professional.

"We take it to the Dowager," Paloma said at last, and their voice had changed—flatter, older, the voice they used when reporting a poison to the Queen's steward. Decision, not discussion. "Tonight. Before the night's out. If this book knows what tonight is, then tonight is already happening whether we understand it or not, and I refuse to be the last person in Vell to find out what my own face is doing in it."

"Protocol says—"

"Protocol," Paloma said, with the ghost of their usual grin, though it didn't reach their eyes, "says the taster dies first. Let's go be early for once."

They picked up the book. And as Paloma's fingers closed around the gray leather cover, the pages stirred—just once, just slightly, like a sleeper turning over—as if the book, a hundred years patient, had finally felt the hand it had been waiting for.

Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them felt it.

Above the palace, past the mountain passes, past the borders of the map, the night was coming on—and somewhere in it, though neither Sef nor Paloma could have known it yet, something was already moving toward Vell, following the taste of a nation that had forgotten how to be found.
