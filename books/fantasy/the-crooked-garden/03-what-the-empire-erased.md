## 3. What the Empire Erased

The Dowager Queen did not send for me. She sent for the honey cake.

That was how it worked in the palace of Vell: you never went to power; power sent a page boy with a covered dish and an expression of profound apology. The dish was cold by the time I reached her apartments on the western stair, which meant she had ordered it an hour ago and let it sit, which meant she wanted me to know she was willing to wait. The Dowager Queen waited for nothing. Except, apparently, for me.

Her rooms smelled of dried lavender and older dust. She sat by the window that faced the salt fields, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, and she did not look at the cake when I set it down. She looked at my hands.

"Show me your arms," she said.

I'd been asked stranger things before breakfast. Tasters learn not to flinch; flinching is a kind of swallowing. I pushed back both sleeves and turned my forearms over, palms up, then down. A hundred small scars, pale as fish bellies. Needle marks from the immunity draughts. A burn from the night someone tried arsenic in the wine instead of the soup.

The Dowager Queen touched one scar with one finger, feather-light, tracing the line of it like a scribe following a word she almost remembered.

"Do you know what these are?" she asked.

"Proof I'm bad at dying," I said.

Something moved across her face—grief wearing the mask of amusement, or the other way around. "They're receipts," she said. "Every one. You've been paying since you were nine years old, child, and no one has ever told you what you were buying."

* * *

She told me to sit. Nobody tells a taster to sit. We stand behind chairs, at doorways, at elbows—we are furniture that swallows. But I sat, because there was something in her voice like the first crack in a jar of preserves: sweet things about to come out, or rot.

"You saw the book," she said. "Don't lie to me. Sef's face when he came to me this morning was a confession written in ink."

My stomach did something complicated. "He wasn't supposed to—"

"No," she agreed. "He wasn't. And neither was I supposed to tell you what I'm about to tell you. There have been eleven Dowagers before me, and every one of us took an oath to keep Vell's truth locked in our chests until we died of it." She folded her hands. "I am eighty-one years old. I have decided to die of something else."

Outside the window, the salt fields lay white and flat under the morning sun, going on forever the way they always did, the way they had gone on for all of my life—a horizon with nothing past it, because there was nothing past it. That was what I had believed. That was what everyone believed. The Valley of Vell ended at the salt, and beyond the salt was more salt, and beyond that, the world simply declined to continue.

"The empire has a name," the Dowager Queen said. "You've never heard it, because names are the first thing they take."

"I've heard of the empire," I said carefully. "Everyone has. It's in the old songs. It burned the—"

"It burned nothing," she said. "Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. Burning leaves grief, and grief can be inherited. No. The empire learned long ago that fire is crude work." She lifted her chin, and for a moment I saw the woman she must have been at twenty, the one who had outlived three kings and a war nobody remembered. "They unmake. They take a country off the maps. Then they take it out of the memories of everyone who ever traded with it, fought it, loved it. Then they take it out of its own memory, thread by thread, until the people inside forget they were ever anything but a valley with no outside. And then—" her voice caught on something, "—and then the country is still *there*. Walking around. Cooking supper. Raising children. Alive, and erased. A nation kept like a fly in amber, so the empire can come back for it whenever it grows hungry."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the kitchens were starting the noon bread, and I could smell it rising through the floorboards—the smell I had slept inside my whole life, warm and ordinary and safe.

"That's a story," I said. My voice sounded far away. "That's a grandmother's story. A scaring-children story."

"My grandmother told it to me as a scaring-children story," the Dowager Queen said. "And her grandmother told it to her. That is how the last true thing survives in Vell—as a story too foolish to be worth erasing." She reached beneath her chair and drew out a box, iron-banded, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. She broke the seal with her thumb like it was bread crust. "But stories have a way of becoming relevant."

Inside the box was a map.

Not a map of Vell. I knew every version of those—the ones in the schoolrooms where the valley sat alone in a field of blank parchment, the ones in the captains' quarters where the salt fields faded into nothing at the edges. This was different. This was old, older than the paper had any right to be, and it showed a coastline. Rivers. Mountains with names in a script I didn't recognize, and cities—I counted them twice, because my eyes refused the first time—eleven cities, each drawn with its walls and gates and little careful towers.

One of them sat exactly where the palace kitchens stood.

"This is Vell," the Dowager Queen said. "This is what Vell was. Two hundred thousand souls. A fleet on the river. Universities. Vineyards that emperors wrote poems about. We had a name for ourselves that wasn't *Vell*—Vell is just the word for *valley*, did you know that? They took our name first. Left us calling ourselves after our own geography, like a dog named after its kennel." Her finger moved across the map, and I watched it shake, just slightly, just enough. "And here—" the finger stopped at the eastern edge, where the mountains began, "—here is where the door was."

"Door," I said. The word came out flat. Behind it, though, my heart was doing something it hadn't done since the prophecy book opened under Sef's hands: hammering like a fist on a locked gate.

"A way out," she said. "Out of the erasure. Out of the amber. The mages who made us invisible could not make everything invisible, do you understand? Erasure is a seal, and every seal has a seam. Our ancestors found the seam. A garden, grown crooked on purpose, grown from the bones of the nations eaten before us—because their bones remembered being real, even when everything else forgot. The Garden opens for those willing to pay its toll. And the toll—"

"—is a memory," I said.

We looked at each other. Outside, a cart rattled through the market square, and someone laughed, and the whole ordinary world went on being ordinary, and I felt it crack down the middle like a dropped plate.

"The book," I said. "Sef's book. The date was tonight. My face was beside a door made of vines. If the Garden is real—if any of this is real—then why tonight? Why now? We've been hidden for a hundred years. What changed?"

The Dowager Queen closed the map with the gentleness of someone closing a coffin.

"Three days ago," she said, "a shepherd on the eastern ridge watched a bird cross the border. Not a bird. Something shaped like a bird, moving without wings beating, humming like a struck glass. It crossed the salt fields and it turned around at the river and it went home." She met my eyes. "Nothing crosses the border, child. Nothing ever has. The erasure keeps things out as surely as it keeps us in. Whatever the empire sent to look at us—it got *in*. Which means the seal is thinning. Which means somewhere in the imperial capital, a mage has begun to taste us again."

"Taste," I repeated. It was the wrong word and the only right one.

"Mages who eat countries don't devour them all at once," she said. "They sip. They test the vintage. Ilvane—yes, I know the name, my mother knew it, her mother knew it—Ilvane has eaten six nations in her lifetime, and she does not gulp. She waits until a seal weakens, and she sends her familiars in like a tongue into a cup, and when she knows the flavor of a place—when she knows it the way you know honey cake—she comes, and she eats, and every soul inside becomes a flavor she owns forever."

I thought of the scars on my arms. Receipts, she'd called them. I had spent my whole life learning to swallow poison in small doses so that the big dose couldn't kill me, and now the entire valley was a cup, and something vast and patient was lifting it toward her mouth.

"Why tell me?" I asked. My voice cracked on it. "Why me? I taste food. I'm not a soldier. I'm not a mage. I'm not—"

"You're the one in the book," she said simply. "You're the face beside the door. Prophecy doesn't choose the qualified, Paloma Brightwater. It chooses the necessary. And besides—" and here her voice softened into something that hurt worse than everything else she'd said, "—you were born here. You grew up here. You love this place with the uncomplicated love of someone who never knew it was stolen. The rest of us grieve a country we half-remember. You would be grieving one you finally understood. Sometimes that is the stronger fuel."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand. My legs did it without consulting me, the way legs do when the body understands something before the mind agrees to.

"I need to find Sef," I said.

"He's in the archive," she said. "Where he's been since dawn, cross-referencing the ledger collections against every record of the eastern marches. He's a thorough boy. Frightened, but thorough." She paused. "Take the map."

I took the map. It weighed nothing and everything.

At the door, she stopped me with my name—not *taster*, my actual name, which almost no one above stairs used—and when I turned, she was looking at me with an expression I will carry longer than I carried the map, longer maybe than I carried anything.

"When you were a baby," she said, "the royal cook used to bring you down to the kitchens in a basket and set you among the pots while she worked. Everyone said you'd grow up to be a cook. Instead they made you a taster, and everyone said what a waste." Her hands tightened on her shawl. "It wasn't a waste. Nothing in Vell has ever been wasted. Everything we have ever done—all the oaths, all the swallowed poison, all the small brave ordinary lives—we did it so that when the day came, someone would be ready to walk through a door. Go and find your archivist, child. Find your door. And whatever it costs—" her voice finally broke, quietly, like ice on a pond in spring, "—whatever it costs, don't pay it alone."

* * *

I crossed the palace grounds with the map against my chest and the world rearranging itself around me with every step.

The orchard was still the orchard. The well was still the well. Children were still playing knucklebones on the chapel steps, arguing over a throw with the total moral seriousness of the young. But now I saw it all through the new terrible knowledge, the way you see a familiar room differently after someone dies in it. These streets had been *named*, once. These people had been citizens of something with a fleet and universities and vineyards that emperors wrote poems about. They weren't a valley. They were a country wearing a valley like a blindfold, and the blindfold was fraying, and something on the other side of the salt could already taste the skin beneath it.

Grief hit me in the market square, of all places, between the cheese stall and the flower cart—an ambush, sudden and total. Grief for a homeland that had never been allowed to exist. For the ships on the river that were never built, or built and then forgotten. For the poets whose names had been scraped out of their own mouths. For two hundred thousand souls reduced to a word meaning *valley*, living and dying inside a seal, generation after generation, keeping the candles lit in a house the world insisted wasn't there.

I put my hand against the warm stone of the fountain and breathed until the grief became something I could carry instead of something carrying me. Taster's discipline. Swallow it. Hold it down. Let it become part of you instead of part of your death.

Then I went to find Sef, because the Dowager Queen was right about one thing above all others:

Whatever came next, I was not going to face it alone—and if the seal was already thinning, if Ilvane's bird had already crossed the border once, then we had until tonight, and not one hour more.
