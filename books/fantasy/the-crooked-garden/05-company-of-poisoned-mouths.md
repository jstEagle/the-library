## 5. Company of Poisoned Mouths

### Paloma

The tasters' hall smelled of beeswax and old fear.

Paloma stood at the head of the long table where the royal food-tasters of Vell took their meals—meals that were never, strictly speaking, theirs. Six places. Three occupied. The fourth chair belonged to a woman named Marisol who had not spoken to Paloma in two years, and the fifth to Hobb, who was asleep in his soup, which was how he preferred his soup: warm and unbothered.

The sixth chair had been empty since the war of succession, when a taster named Odile Brightwater—no relation, though everyone assumed otherwise—died with a smile on her face because the poison she swallowed had been sweetened with plum wine, and she'd always said if it had to be done, let it at least taste like something.

Paloma cleared their throat. "I need to talk about the prophecy book."

Marisol didn't look up from her plate. She was twenty-three, built like a door slammed shut, with a scar across her knuckles from the time she'd punched a visiting ambassador for lying about the vintage. She could taste lies the way other people tasted salt—a gift the Dowager Queen found useful and everyone else found unsettling. "No," she said.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"You're going to ask me to do something stupid, and you're going to use the word *together*, and I've eaten enough of your cooking to know when you're seasoning a lie." She finally raised her eyes. They were the brown of river water over stones. "So. No."

"The book has my face in it," Paloma said. "And a date. Tonight's date."

That got her. Her fork stopped halfway to her mouth, and for one unguarded second Paloma watched the bravado drain out of her like broth through a sieve. Then she set the fork down with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

"Show me."

* * *

### Marisol

Show me, he'd said, and then he had, and now Marisol was walking through the palace archives at an hour when honest people were asleep, following a boy who tasted poison for a living and an archivist who blushed every time their elbows touched.

She hated this. She hated that she was here.

The thing about tasting lies was that you also learned what truth felt like in the mouth—it had weight, like marrow; it sat on the tongue and refused to dissolve. And everything Paloma Brightwater said had that weight. Every ridiculous, doomed word. *A door made of vines. A garden outside the valley. An empire that eats countries.* Truth, all of it, which was somehow worse than a lie, because lies you could spit out.

Sef walked ahead with a lantern, cataloguing shelves by touch, and Marisol caught herself watching the way he moved—careful, reverent, like the books were sleeping animals—and made herself stop.

"So this mage," she said, because someone had to say it. "Ilvane. She eats forgotten nations."

"She's eaten six," Sef said quietly. "Vell would be the seventh."

"And we're going to find a magic door before she gets here. With what army?"

"With us," Paloma said.

Marisol laughed. It came out wrong—too loud in the dusty dark, too close to a sob. "There are three of us. Four if Hobb wakes up. We are people whose entire profession consists of dying first so that better people can live. That's not an army. That's an appetizer."

Paloma stopped walking and turned, and in the lantern light their face did the thing it always did—that infuriating, level look, the look of someone who had swallowed arsenic on a Tuesday and gone back for seconds.

"You've been tasting lies your whole life," they said. "Tell me honestly. Does it feel like a lie? That we're the ones who should go?"

Marisol opened her mouth to say yes, obviously, and found nothing there. No bitterness. No weight of falsehood. Just the strange clean taste of a thing being true whether she liked it or not.

"I hate you," she said, which was the closest she could get.

"I know," said Paloma. "Come on. We need Hobb."

* * *

### Hobb

Hobb had been awake the whole time, of course. He was always awake. Sleep had left him around the same year his conscience had, and neither had come back.

He heard them coming down the corridor—the archivist's soft steps, Marisol's boots striking the flagstones like accusations, and Paloma's tread, light and deliberate, the tread of someone who had learned early that the safest way through any room was to be underestimated.

He arranged himself in his chair by the cold hearth, hands folded, and put on the face he wore for the world: a mild old man, half-deaf, mostly harmless. It was a good face. He'd worn it for forty years, ever since the last time he'd told the truth about anything.

They came in. Paloma sat down across from him without asking, which he appreciated. Manners mattered less than nerve.

"They've told you, then," Hobb said. "About the Garden."

Marisol blinked. "How did you—"

"You came from the archives. You walk like people carrying bad news." He smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Ask me, boy. Whatever it is you came to ask. Ask it plainly."

Paloma leaned forward. "You were a taster before Vell. Before the borders closed. You served outside once—we all know the story, the empire's court, thirty years ago. You know what Ilvane is."

Ah. There it was.

"I know what she is," Hobb said. His voice stayed level. Forty years of practice. "Do you know why I left her table, child? Why I came back to this damp little valley and spent my life swallowing poisons for a queen no map remembers?"

"Because you got out," Marisol said. "Because you survived."

"No." He looked at his folded hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the whole horror of him. "Because I helped her find a nation's door. A little country called Serevan, east of the salt fields. Lovely people. Terrible wine. They had a Garden of their own, and a gatekeeper who trusted a tired old taster who claimed to be fleeing the empire." He raised his eyes. "I gave her the way in. She gave me immunity—all poisons, all venoms, forever. My body is a fortress, and it cost me eleven thousand lives to build it."

The silence had texture. Marisol had gone pale. Even Sef, in the doorway, had stopped breathing.

"So no," Hobb continued, "I won't help you find the Crooked Garden. I'll help you *reach* it, and guard it, and stand between it and that woman until my heart gives out—which, given what I am, will take some doing. But understand what you're taking with you. Understand that the man walking beside you is the reason she knows how doors like these open at all."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. Then: "Why tell us? You could have lied. You're good enough."

"Because you're going to need to trust me at the worst moment of your life," Hobb said, "and I wanted to watch your faces when you decided whether you could." He unfolded his hands at last. "Well? Decide."

It was Marisol who answered, surprising them all, herself most of all.

"We decide later," she said. "When it matters. Come on, old man. If we're doing this, we need supplies, and I know exactly the thief to steal them."

* * *

### Bramble

Bramble smelled them before they reached the kitchen—four bodies, one lantern, and trouble, which had a smell like wet iron and indecision. She finished plating the night's leftovers into travel portions without looking up. Old habits. In her previous life, portioning food quickly had been the difference between eating and being eaten.

The kitchen door opened. Paloma Brightwater stood in it, flanked by the lie-taster and the old ghost, with the archivist hovering behind like a moth afraid of its own flame.

"Bramble," Paloma said. "We need to leave the valley."

"Everyone does, eventually," she said, still slicing. "Most of them come back disappointed."

"There's a door. A real one. And something is coming through the border after us, and I need someone who knows how to move quiet, cook fast, and steal well."

Bramble set down her knife and finally looked at them. She was small and quick and had a face like a fox that had read your diary, and she had spent nine years in Vell building a new name out of flour and butter, layer by careful layer, over the old one. The old one had teeth marks in it.

"You know what I was," she said. "Before."

"A cook," Paloma said.

"A thief who cooks," she corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that a cook feeds people and a thief decides who gets fed." She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly. "Why me? You have the whole palace. Guards, grooms, a queen with a private navy on the underground lake."

"Because guards follow orders," Paloma said. "Grooms follow horses. I need people who follow each other. And—" they hesitated, and Bramble watched the hesitation with professional interest, because hesitation was where the true things lived, "—because you came through the salt fields once. When you arrived. You know what's out there at the edge of the valley. Nobody else alive does."

Bramble was silent. Outside, the night pressed against the kitchen windows, and somewhere far off, past the orchards and the terraced hills and the wards-that-weren't-wards, just absence and distance, the way it had always been, the way it was supposed to always be.

Except it wasn't supposed to be anything anymore. There was a book with a date in it. There was a mage who ate countries. There was a boy who swallowed poison daily and called it a career, standing in her kitchen asking her to die interestingly.

"If I say no?" she asked.

"Then I'll eat alone tomorrow," Paloma said simply. "And the day after. Until either the world ends or I stop asking people to come with me. One of those happens first."

Bramble looked at the four of them—the liar, the ghost, the moth, and the mouth—and felt the old familiar itch under her skin, the one that had gotten her exiled from two cities and one criminal syndicate. The itch that said: *these are your people. Run.*

She untied her apron.

"Give me ten minutes to pack knives," she said. "Real ones and the kind you eat with."

* * *

### Paloma

They gathered at midnight in the tasters' hall, because it seemed right to end where the oaths began.

Five of them around the long table: Paloma, Marisol, Hobb, Bramble, and Sef, who had technically not been invited but had shown up with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heartbeat, and nobody had the heart—or the authority—to send him back.

Bramble had brought food, because Bramble always brought food: bread, hard cheese, honey cake from the kitchens, unblessed and unpoisoned, which made it the rarest meal any of them had eaten in years. For the first time in their working lives, none of them had to test it first. They just ate. It felt almost indecent.

"To be clear," Marisol said, around a mouthful, "I still think this is stupid."

"Noted," said Paloma.

"And I want it recorded that the old man confessed to genocide and we just... kept walking."

"Also noted," said Paloma.

Hobb raised his cup—an old man's gesture, wry and tired. "To Serevan," he said quietly. "May this go differently."

"To Serevan," they echoed, not knowing what else to do, and drank.

Sef unwrapped the book and laid it open on the table between the bread and the cheese, and they all looked at the page again: the date, the vines, the small figure with Paloma's face standing at a crooked green door. Outside the window, the valley lay dark and safe and magicless, the only home any of them had ever known, holding its breath.

It was Bramble who broke the silence, in the flat voice of a woman reading a weather report.

"There's smoke on the northern ridge," she said. "Been there an hour. Too low for a chimney, too steady for a wildfire." She turned her fox-sharp eyes on them. "Somebody's camped at the edge of our map. Somebody who thinks the map ends where they're standing."

Paloma looked down at the page—at the date, tonight's date, written a hundred years ago in ash and certainty—and felt the taste of the evening change on their tongue, sweet curdling toward bitter, the way it always did just before the poison arrived.

"Well," they said, standing, and reaching for the dark coat Sef held out. "Then we'd best not keep the Garden waiting."
