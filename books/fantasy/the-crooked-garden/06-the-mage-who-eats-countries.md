## 6. The Mage Who Eats Countries

The border of Vell did not look like a border. It looked like nothing at all, which was precisely the point.

Ilvane stood at the edge of the salt flats with her boots in the dust of two worlds and felt the absence roll over her like the breath of an opened cellar. Most mages would have sensed nothing. Most mages had been trained to read presence—the hot signature of a ward, the sour tang of a curse, the bright animal noise of a living soul. But Ilvane had made her career on the other discipline. She read *lack*. She read the shape of what was missing, the way a sommelier reads a wine by the space it leaves on the tongue.

And Vell was a magnificent vintage.

"Note the finish," she murmured to her attendants, though none of them could taste what she tasted. They never could. That was rather the tragedy of her profession: she was the only palate in the empire qualified for her own table. "A century of careful erasure. No oxidation. Whoever sealed this valley knew their craft."

Behind her, the imperial survey camp sprawled across the flats—white tents, brass instruments, forty clerks who spent their days writing reports about a country that officially did not exist and therefore required no report. Ilvane found the bureaucracy delicious in its own way. The empire had erased Vell so thoroughly that its own records now insisted there was nothing to erase. She was, technically, on holiday.

She was not on holiday.

She lifted her hand, and the air came to her.

That was the crude way to describe it, and she despised crude descriptions, but language had never quite kept pace with her appetites. The air came to her the way steam rises from a kitchen vent—thin, fragrant, carrying the ghost of everything cooking within. She let it settle on her tongue and closed her eyes.

Salt first. The flats always gave salt; they were the empire's great shallow wound, the place where erased things drifted to die. Beneath the salt, though—

Bread. Yeast working in warm dark places. Woodsmoke from a hundred hearths, all of them burning wood that had grown inside the same small circle of hills. Honey—there it was, honey again, sweeter than the last time she'd sampled this vintage, which meant someone in the valley was keeping bees, which meant someone in the valley was planning futures. Bees were optimism. Bees were a bet that next year would come.

Ilvane smiled, and her attendants, who had learned not to watch her smile directly, studied their instruments very hard.

"A hundred years," she said, "and they're still keeping bees."

* * *

She had eaten six nations.

Not in the vulgar sense—she had never developed a taste for flesh, and the empire's more theatrical historians had done her reputation no favors with their illustrations of her standing ankle-deep in ash. The truth was quieter and far worse. She ate countries the way a censor eats books: page by page, name by name, until nothing remained but the binding. A nation was not its soil. A nation was its memory of itself—its recipes, its songs, its grudges, the particular way its grandmothers said a child's name when the child was in trouble. Remove those, and you could leave every stone standing and still have nothing. Keep them, and you could burn every stone and have something indestructible.

The empire had learned this lesson badly, over centuries of clumsy conquest, and had eventually done the sensible thing: it had hired someone to do the forgetting properly.

Ilvane had been young when they found her—young and hungry in ways she hadn't yet learned to name, a war orphan from a country whose name she had already lost, filed away in some imperial archive by clerks who were only doing their jobs. Perhaps that was where the hunger started. She had never bothered to investigate. Self-analysis was a kind of indigestion.

Her first nation had been Serevan, a fishing confederacy on the northern coast, small enough to swallow in a season. She remembered the work of it fondly: three hundred years of sea-songs unpicked thread by thread, the word for their god's true name dissolved into static, the maps redrawn so that the coastline itself seemed to have been a cartographer's error. When it was done, the Serevani people still walked around in their bodies, still fished, still married and quarreled and died—but they no longer knew they had ever been anything. They became imperial citizens with the smooth, unresisting ease of water finding level ground.

It had been, she admitted, the purest meal of her life. Every one since had been, in some small way, an attempt to taste that first course again.

Kellum had taken four years. The Ashen Steps had taken nine, because they had written everything down—their whole culture committed to paper, stubborn as a stain—and she had needed to unwrite a library. Ondu she remembered least; it had gone down easily, almost carelessly, and she suspected that was why she sometimes woke at night with the sensation of having left a meal unfinished. The Twins had been two nations braided into one and had required her to eat them separately, which was delicate work, like deboning a fish without breaking the skin.

And then there had been the sixth. The one whose door had opened for Hobb.

She thought of Hobb now, as she often did at borders. Her taster, once. The only servant she had ever kept longer than a season, because a good taster was rarer than a good mage and considerably harder to corrupt. He had eaten poison for her for eleven years, building his immunity dish by dish, and in exchange he had given her the thing she actually needed from him: his tongue. Not for poison. For truth. Marisol-types aside, tasters develop palates that can distinguish a lie the way they distinguish almond from apricot kernel, and Hobb's palate had been exquisite.

He had found her the sixth nation's door. She had promised him immunity—true immunity, the permanent kind, the kind she alone could grant—and he had believed her, because eleven years of shared meals builds a terrible intimacy, and because he had wanted to believe her more than he had wanted to be right.

She had granted it, too. That was the part her enemies never understood, the part that made her genuinely dangerous rather than merely cruel: Ilvane kept her bargains. To the letter. Hobb was immune to every poison in creation. She had never said a word about regret.

She wondered, idly, whether he was still alive. Tasters usually were; it was the one profession where survival was the entire job description. She wondered if he still cooked, and what he cooked now, and whether he ever set a table for six ghosts.

Probably. Men like Hobb always did. It was why they were so useful, and so easy.

* * *

"Magister-Censor." The survey captain approached with the particular hunched gait of a man delivering news he hoped would not be his fault. "The instrument readings from the eastern ridge. There's—well. There's something."

"There is indeed," Ilvane said pleasantly. "There is an entire country. Do try to keep up."

The captain swallowed. "The readings show a—a discontinuity. A region where our instruments return values consistent with empty land, but the land itself is—" He consulted his notes, though he clearly knew them by heart. "Occupied. Roads. Roofs. Smoke from chimneys. Our observers can see it plainly. The instruments insist it isn't there."

"Yes." Ilvane turned back to the flats. The wind off them carried that faint honeyed warmth, and beneath it, fainter, something new. Something that had not been in the vintage before.

She tasted it again, carefully, the way one re-tastes a wine that has changed in the glass.

Iron. Ink. And under both, unmistakable, impossible: *magic*. Foreign magic, imperial magic, the specific ozone bite of a familiar crossing a boundary that should have been impassable.

One of her survey familiars had crossed into Vell.

She had sent it herself, weeks ago, half as an experiment and half out of boredom—a small thing, a lens-winged seeker, cheap enough to lose. She had assumed the valley's null would digest it. Instead it had crossed. Which meant the seal was thinning, or had been thinned, or—

Or someone inside had opened a window.

Ilvane stood very still, and the attendants nearest her took an involuntary step back, because there was a quality to her stillness that even the newest clerk had learned to fear. It was the stillness of a diner who has just been told the chef has prepared something off-menu.

"Well," she said softly. "How interesting."

* * *

They made camp that night at the exact line where the instruments went blind, and Ilvane dined alone in her tent, as she always did at the start of a meal. The tent was hung with silk the color of old bone, and the table was set for one, and the food was exquisite and tasted of nothing to her, because nothing had tasted of anything for a very long time. This was the price of her discipline, the little joke the universe had played on its most accomplished eater: the more she consumed, the less she savored. Six nations sat in her like ballast, and their weight had flattened her tongue to leather.

Only at borders did she taste anything at all. Only here, at the rim of a living absence, did the world remember how to be flavorful.

She ate slowly. Through the tent's open flap she could see the valley's hills by starlight, black against blacker sky, and she let her senses drift toward them the way a starving man presses his face to a bakery window.

A hundred years of isolation. A hundred years of bees and bread and children learning songs that no one outside would ever hear. A hundred years of a people marinating in their own uninterrupted selves, growing richer and stranger and more *themselves* with every passing season, like a cask left to age in a cellar the empire forgot it owned.

Other censors would have called it a problem. An anomaly to be corrected, a loose thread, a threat to imperial coherence. Ilvane had never understood her colleagues' relationship to food. They saw a meal; she saw a life's work. You did not correct a vintage. You waited for it. You watched the seals thin and the windows crack open, and you did nothing, nothing at all, because haste was the enemy of flavor and the empire had all the time in the world.

But tonight the vintage had changed. Tonight there was iron and ink and a seeker's magic dissolving into valley air, and somewhere in that honeyed dark, someone had touched the seal from the inside.

Someone was looking for a door.

Ilvane set down her fork. She found, to her mild surprise, that her mouth was watering—the first time in years, the first time since the Ashen Steps, since the ninth year of unwriting their library when she had caught herself reading one of their novels late at night purely for pleasure and had burned it in shame and hunger in equal measure.

A door meant a toll. A toll meant a payment. And a payment meant memory leaving a body, and memory leaving a body was the richest sauce in existence. If the people of Vell began paying their way toward an exit—if they began spending themselves, course by course, to escape her—

Then by the time she reached the table, the meal would already be seasoned.

She rose and stepped out of the tent into the salt wind. Her attendants scrambled after her. Above the eastern hills, the stars stood in their ancient indifference, and behind them, invisible, a valley full of bees kept its patient bet about next year.

"Captain," she said. "Send word to the capital. I'll want the full apparatus. Ward-breakers, archivists, the memory-engines—all of it."

"All of it, Magister-Censor? For one small—"

"For the seventh," Ilvane said.

The captain blinked. "The seventh what?"

She smiled at him, and this time he did look away.

"The seventh nation," she said, "is nearly ripe."

She turned back to the hills, and the wind came off the flats carrying honey and bread and woodsmoke and, threaded through it all now, thin as a hair, the scent of a door beginning to think about opening—and Ilvane breathed it in, deep, the way one breathes in the smell of a kitchen before a feast, and began, at last, to feel hungry.
