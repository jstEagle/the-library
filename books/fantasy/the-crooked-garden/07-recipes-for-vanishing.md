## 7. Recipes for Vanishing

The archives of Vell smelled the way Marisol imagined a tomb would smell if the tomb had been trying very hard to be a library: dust, tallow, old glue, and beneath it all something sweeter and more troubling, like honey left too long in a copper pot.

She hated it immediately. She hated the way her voice didn't echo—sound just died in here, swallowed by shelf after shelf of ledgers, almanacs, genealogies of families so obscure they'd probably forgotten themselves. She hated that Sef had given her white gloves to handle the documents, because her fingers were made for knives and lockpicks and dough, not for touching paper like it might apologize.

Mostly she hated that they were doing this at night, by candlelight, like criminals, when technically Paloma had the Dowager Queen's blessing and Sef had the archivist's keys and Bramble had already picked two locks they hadn't needed to pick, just for practice.

"We're allowed to be here," she said, for the fourth time.

"Allowed is a strong word," said Bramble, upside down, hanging off a ladder by his knees with a lantern hooked to one foot. He was a thin man built entirely of angles and appetite, with flour permanently ghosted into the creases of his knuckles no matter how he scrubbed. "Permitted, maybe. Tolerated. The Queen's steward said we could consult the sealed collections *pending review*, which in my experience means someone upstairs hasn't decided yet whether this is scholarship or treason."

"It's both," Marisol said. "Everything worth doing is both."

From somewhere deep in the stacks, Hobb's voice drifted out, dry as the dust around him: "In my day it was only treason."

Marisol snorted despite herself. Old Hobb had been down among the pre-Founding records since sundown, moving through them with the slow, deliberate patience of a man who had once been paid to read other people's mail. He'd told them nothing about what he was looking for. He'd told them nothing about a lot of things, lately. There was a new weight on him since the sigil-burn, since the drone-thing from outside had crossed the border and marked Paloma's shoulder with a brand that glowed faintly when the moon came up. Since the word *Ilvane* had entered their lives like a stone dropped in a well whose bottom nobody wanted to find.

Paloma was three shelves over, working through a run of agricultural censuses with the grim focus they brought to everything, including arsenic. Marisol watched them turn a page and thought—not for the first time—that it was obscene how calm they looked. Marked by imperial magic. Named in a prophecy book a hundred years old. And still they sat there cross-legged on the floor with their sleeves rolled up, reading about barley yields like the fate of the valley depended on knowing how much rain fell in the year the third bridge was built.

Maybe it did. That was the thing about Paloma Brightwater. You couldn't rule anything out.

"Find me something," Marisol muttered, and went back to the ledger in front of her: *Household Accounts of the Western Wing, Year 214*. Sheep, candles, salt, more sheep. A hundred years of sheep. If the Crooked Garden—the door out of Vell, the living gate the Dowager Queen swore existed, the only way past Ilvane and her hunger—was written anywhere in this building, it was buried under an avalanche of mutton.

The trouble with hope, Marisol had learned young, was that it looked exactly like work.

* * *

She found the first thread an hour before midnight, and it was so small she almost missed it.

She'd moved on from sheep to kitchens—a natural migration for her, and besides, if you were going to hide something important in a palace archive, you hid it in the boring parts. Nobody censored the grocery lists. She'd pulled a stack of kitchen ledgers spanning the years around the Founding, when the valley's borders had closed and the empire's maps had lost them, and she was skimming entries with half her attention when her eye snagged on a line of ink darker than the rest.

*One (1) door-hinge oil, pressed from nightfruit. Do not substitute.*

Nightfruit. Marisol set down her candle.

There was no such thing as nightfruit. She knew every ingredient that grew in the Valley of Vell, or had grown in it within living memory—she'd been cooking professionally since she was eleven and thieving professionally since she was nine, and the two trades shared a shopping list. Nightfruit wasn't in it. Neither was the next item: *moon-salt, harvested at ebb*. Nor the next: *glasswort honey, comb of the western hive*. Nor the one after that, which stopped her heart for a full three seconds:

*Flour, ground from the grey wheat. Enough for seven.*

Grey wheat didn't exist. Grey wheat had never existed. Wheat came in gold and red and the disappointing brown of bad harvests, but never grey, and never in quantities measured for exactly seven people, unless—

Unless somebody had been planning a meal for seven specific somebodies, a hundred years ago, using ingredients that could not be bought anywhere in the world.

"Paloma," she said, and her voice came out strange, and Paloma's head came up instantly, the way it always did, like they'd been waiting for her call without admitting it. "Come look at this. Bring your gloves and your optimism."

They came. They read over her shoulder, close enough that she could feel the warmth of them, and she heard their breath change when they reached the grey wheat line.

"That's a recipe," Paloma said slowly. "That's a *shopping list*. Someone wrote down what they needed to—" They stopped. Marisol watched them get there, watched it land. "To open a door."

"To cook a door open," Marisol corrected. "Look at the format. It's a mise en place. Quantities, sequence, substitutions forbidden. This isn't ritual language, this is *kitchen language*. Somebody stood in a kitchen a hundred years ago and wrote down how to feed the Crooked Garden."

The ledger's front page gave a name: *Maren Ostrova, Under-Cook, Household of the Queen Regent.* Below it, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *Being a true account of the last service, kept against the day it is needed again.*

"The last service," Paloma repeated.

"Somebody cooked a meal," Marisol said, "and then somebody walked through a door, and then nobody wrote any of it down anywhere else, because writing things down is how the empire finds you." She tapped the page. "This cook hid the recipe inside a hundred years of grocery lists and hoped like hell someone would eventually need it."

"Hoped someone would *read* it," Paloma said quietly. "There's a difference."

Yes, there was. Marisol knew that difference intimately; she'd spent her whole life betting on it. Thieves and cooks were the same profession wearing different aprons: both of them leaving messages for strangers across time, trusting that someday, someone hungry enough would come looking.

* * *

By the second hour past midnight they had pulled every ledger Maren Ostrova had ever touched, and it wasn't many. Under-cooks did not get their own shelves. Her handwriting surfaced in six volumes total, scattered across a decade like breadcrumbs, and each volume held only fragments: a line here, a margin note there, always in that same dark ink, always about ingredients that did not exist.

*Bitter herb, seventh cutting.* Marisol knew bitter herbs; there were eleven in common use and none of them required cuttings numbered past four.

*Water, drawn backward from the well.*

*A memory, finely chopped.* — and beside that one, in different ink, someone else's hand, presumably a censor's or a superior's: *Ostrova, this is a kitchen ledger, not a madhouse. Strike it.*

But she hadn't struck it. She'd written underneath, small and stubborn: *It is the most important line. Everything else is garnish.*

Marisol read that twice and felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name. She'd spent years tasting lies for the crown—Marisol's particular gift, the reason she'd been recruited to the tasters' hall at fifteen, was that falsehoods had a flavor to her, oily and bright, like rancid citrus—and she knew truth when she met it, even dead truth, even truth a century cold. Maren Ostrova had believed every word she wrote. Believed it hard enough to defy whoever held the pen above her.

"She was one of us," Marisol said. It came out flat, but Paloma looked up anyway, and Bramble stopped climbing down his ladder, and even Hobb emerged from the pre-Founding stacks with dust on his shoulders like snow.

"A cook?" Bramble asked.

"A taster." Marisol turned the ledger toward them and pointed at the household roster copied inside the cover. *M. Ostrova — Office of the Mouth, Second Chair.* "Second chair. That's poison duty. That's swallowing the risky dishes so the Queen doesn't have to." She heard her own voice sharpen. "A hundred years ago there was another poisoned mouth standing in a kitchen, writing down how to vanish, and she hid it where only another kitchen person would ever think to look."

Silence took the room. Candles guttered. Somewhere far off, the palace clock counted a quarter hour into the dark.

Hobb came forward slowly, joints cracking, and bent over the ledger. For a long moment he said nothing, and Marisol—who tasted lies, who could hear a falsehood the way other people heard a wrong note—waited for the oily brightness of deception in whatever he said next.

What she got instead was grief. Grief has no flavor; that's what makes it worse than any lie.

"I remember her name," Hobb said. "From the old rolls. Ostrova. She resigned her post the year I took mine. Everyone said she'd gone soft in the head, talking about doors." His thumb moved over the dark ink, careful as a man touching a scar. "Nobody laughed where she could hear it, though. Funny thing about the ones who talk about doors. People stop laughing when they're alone."

"You knew of her," Paloma said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact down between them, like a card turned face-up.

"I knew *of* her." Hobb straightened, and his eyes, when they met Marisol's, were clear and terrible and honest, and she wished briefly and violently that he would lie to her instead. "I know less than I should and more than I want. Ask me again when we've found the rest of her recipe. Some confessions keep better than others."

* * *

They worked until the candles burned down to puddles. By the end they had assembled, from six ledgers and a hundred years of grocery lists, perhaps a third of a recipe.

Seven courses, they thought—seven kept recurring, seven plates, seven chairs, *enough for seven*, and Marisol caught Paloma counting the company on their fingers and coming up one short, and saying nothing about it, and Marisol saying nothing about them saying nothing.

The ingredients were the problem. Moon-salt, nightfruit, glasswort honey, grey wheat, backward water, the seventh cutting of a bitter herb that grew nowhere—all of it gone, or never real, or real only in places the empire had eaten before it ate its maps. Marisol sat back on her heels amid the paper ruins and felt the familiar sourness rise in her throat that she'd learned to recognize as hope's ugly twin: the certainty that you'd come this far for nothing.

Except.

"Wait," she said. "Wait, wait, wait." She grabbed the oldest ledger, the one with the water-stained cover, and flipped to the flyleaf, where Maren Ostrova had copied out—in her dark, stubborn ink—a list of pantry sources. *Moon-salt: the flats beyond the eastern wall, at ebb, take only what sings.* The salt fields. The dead ground at the valley's edge where erased things went, according to every frightened story Marisol had ever been told to keep her indoors at night. *Glasswort: the drowned orchard, west row, the trees that remember being sweet.*

Ghost-orchards. Half-remembered songs. Places that shouldn't exist, holding ingredients that couldn't exist, growing wild in the exact direction of the border where an imperial mage was currently standing, Marisol had no doubt, sniffing the wind for the taste of their absence.

"She left us a shopping route," Marisol said, and heard her own voice shake, and hated it, and let it. "The crazy, magnificent, dead woman left us a *shopping route*. Through the salt fields. Past the border. Straight toward the thing that eats countries."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. Then they smiled—that wry, crooked, infuriating smile that Marisol had decided sometime in the last week she would follow straight into hell if it came to it, and had been furious at herself about ever since.

"Well," Paloma said. "We are, all of us, professionals at swallowing things that could kill us."

Bramble laughed. Even Hobb's mouth twitched. And Marisol gathered the six ledgers into her arms like a bundle of kindling, like a bundle of bombs, and thought: *a hundred years ago a woman with poison scars on her tongue bet everything that someone would find this and finish it.*

Hope, it turned out, wasn't a feeling. Hope was archaeology. Hope was digging through the garbage of the dead until your hands bled, on the faith that they'd left you something better than bones.

They had nearly reached the archive door when Hobb stopped walking.

He stood very still in the candlelight, looking back at the stacks, and Marisol felt the hair rise on her arms before he said a single word—because she tasted it then, faint and unmistakable, drifting through the dead air of the sealed room like smoke through a keyhole.

A lie. Fresh. Close. Oily and bright and *afraid*.

"What is it?" Paloma whispered.

Hobb didn't answer. He was staring at the shelf behind them—at the gap where the prophecy book, the book with tonight's date and Paloma's face beside a door made of vines, was supposed to be locked in Sef's keeping.

The gap was empty. The lie in the air was getting stronger.

And from the corridor outside, soft as a footstep, came the sound of pages turning.
