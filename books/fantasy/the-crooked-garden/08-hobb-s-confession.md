## 8. Hobb's Confession

The fire in the waystation kitchen had burned down to a red heart, and Hobb sat closest to it because his bones ached and because sitting close to a fire was the last honest pleasure a man of sixty years could claim without apology.

He had been watching Paloma all evening.

Not the way Marisol watched people—quick, appraising, like she was tasting them. Hobb watched the slow way, the way he watched a pot that might be about to boil over. And Paloma had been boiling over for two days now, ever since the salt fields, ever since Marisol's wound had refused to close in air that didn't know how to hold time together. The girl—no, not a girl anymore, though Hobb's knees insisted everyone under forty was a girl or a boy—Paloma kept looking at him with a question they hadn't asked yet.

Hobb knew that look. He'd worn it himself, once, on another face, in another country.

That was the trouble with living long enough. You ran out of other people's stories to hide behind.

* * *

They were eating Bramble's stew, which tasted mostly of salt and stubbornness, and Marisol was arguing with Sef about whether the ledger's seventh ingredient could be substituted.

"—and I'm telling you, no cook in a hundred years has written *moon-salt* when they meant *sea-salt*, archivist. Cooks are precise. Cooks are liars about everything except ingredients."

"Cooks are poets," Sef said, "when the truth would get them hanged."

"Then what's this one lying about?"

"Maybe the moon," Bramble said from the hearth, deadpan, and Marisol threw a crust at her head.

It was good noise. It was the noise of people pretending they weren't afraid, and Hobb had eaten enough meals in enough kitchens to know that this pretense was the most nourishing thing on any table. He let it wash over him for a while. He let himself pretend too, one more hour, that he could carry what he carried to the grave, the way he'd planned, the way he'd promised himself in the dark of forty years.

But Paloma set down their spoon. And looked at him. And asked.

"Hobb. When we find the Garden. When we're standing at the gate." They hesitated, and Hobb loved them for the hesitation, because it meant they already suspected the answer would cost something. "How do you know the door takes memories? Nobody in Vell has ever been through it. Nobody remembers anyone going through."

The kitchen went quiet the way a kitchen goes quiet when a knife slips—not all at once, but in the half-second before the blood.

"I know," Hobb said, "because I've paid it."

Marisol's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Sef went very still, the stillness of a man who catalogued things for a living and had just heard a book open that wasn't supposed to exist.

Bramble, who missed nothing, quietly moved herself between Hobb and the door. Not to stop him leaving. To stop anything else coming in.

"Tell us," Paloma said.

And Hobb, who had swallowed every poison an empire could brew and never flinched, found that this—the plain truth, unseasoned—was the one thing his throat refused to let down easy.

* * *

"You have to understand what I was first," he said. "Before you understand what I did. I was hungry. That's the whole of it. I was nineteen and hungry, and my country—" he stopped, corrected himself, made himself say it—"my country was already dying. Not Vell. You've never heard its name because there is no name anymore. There can't be. A name needs mouths to hold it, and Ilvane took the mouths."

"The Mage-Censor," Sef said. Not a question. He'd read the margins of the prophecy book; Hobb had seen him reading them, late, by candle stub.

"She wasn't Censor then. She was younger. Hungrier than me, even. She came through our border the way water comes through a roof—you don't see the leak, you just wake up one morning and everything you own is soft and rotting." Hobb turned his hands over, looked at the palms as if the story were written there. "I was a taster. Same oath you swore, Paloma. Same words. *My mouth before the crown's. My death before the realm's.* I said them with my whole chest, the way you did."

"And then?" Marisol asked softly. She, of all of them, knew how oaths bent.

"And then her soldiers took the palace, and she kept the tasters, because a mage who eats countries is careful about what she puts in her own mouth. Poisoned wine from a rival's hand. Bitter herbs from a conquered province. She needed someone to swallow first." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound, like a bone breaking underwater. "Do you know what immunity costs? It isn't bought in doses. It's bought in *willingness*. Every dish I survived, I survived because part of me wanted to live more than I wanted my oath to mean anything. And every dish made the next one easier. That's the arithmetic nobody tells you. Survival compounds. Like debt."

Paloma's face had gone very carefully blank. Hobb recognized that too. It was the face of a person doing the same arithmetic.

"She fed well off us, those first years. Our grief seasoned everything. Grief is the finest spice there is, if you're the kind of thing that eats nations. But grief runs out. A country can only die once. And when we were nothing but a rumor of a flavor in her memory, she grew hungry again—and here is where I stop being only a victim, so listen close, all of you, because I will not say this part twice."

He stood. His knees cracked like green wood. He walked to the window, where the night over the salt fields pressed against the glass like a face.

"There was another nation. East of us. Across the shallows. Small, green, loud with birdsong—a place so full of life it seemed to brag about it. And they had a door. Their own Crooked Garden, grown into their own hills, older than ours, maybe the same door wearing different hedges—I don't know, I never saw it open. But I knew where it was."

"How?" Sef asked. His voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp.

"Because I was her taster, and tasters hear everything. Kings whisper over soup. Generals mutter over wine they've watched me drink. And one night a cartographer drank too much and talked about a hedge-maze on the eastern hills that appeared on no map and ate his compass needle." Hobb's reflection in the dark glass looked, to himself, exactly like the man he had been: young, frightened, calculating. "She asked me. Do you understand? She didn't torture it out of me. She sat across a table and poured me wine I'd already tasted and she *asked*, in that voice like honey over a blade, and she said: *you've kept me alive this long, old friend. Keep me alive a little longer.*"

"What did she offer you?" Paloma asked. Quiet. Not accusing. Which was somehow unbearable.

"Immunity," Hobb said. "Complete. Permanent. Her own working, poured into my blood like a second spine. No poison could touch me. No magic could unmake me. I would walk out of the eating of my country untouched, insured, *safe*—the one thing every soul in Vell dreams of being and none of us will ever be." He turned around. The firelight caught the wet in his eyes and he didn't wipe it away; they deserved to see it. "I told myself it was survival. I told myself a dead taster saves no one. I told myself I'd find a way to warn them after. Forty years of telling myself things, and I believed every one of them right up until the moment I didn't."

"And you gave her the location," Bramble said. Flat. Not cruel. Just true.

"I drew her a map," Hobb said. "I described the hills. I remembered aloud, and she drank the remembering out of the air like a woman drinking broth. Three weeks later the birdsong stopped. Three weeks later there was no east. Sailors who'd traded there forgot they'd ever loaded cargo. Mothers who'd buried children there forgot they'd had children." His voice finally broke, and he let it. "I have outlived poisons that kill in a breath. I will outlive whatever kills me. That was the bargain. My survival, purchased with everyone else's ending. And the worst of it—the thing I need you to understand, Paloma, you especially—is that I'd do the arithmetic again. Hunger doesn't care about your oath. Fear doesn't care about your honor. I was offered life and I took it, and every day since has been the interest coming due."

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The stew went cold. The fire settled. Outside, the wind moved through the ghost-orchards of the salt fields, carrying that thin, wrong birdsong that belonged to things that no longer existed anywhere except in the air's bad memory.

It was Marisol who moved first. She crossed the room and sat down across from Hobb, and she looked at him the way she looked at a dish she'd been served by an enemy: carefully, completely, hunting the poison.

"My village sold me to a lord's kitchen when I was seven," she said. "I've tasted lies ever since—they come up bitter, like copper and rot. You know what I taste right now, old man?"

"No," Hobb said. "Tell me."

"Nothing." She held his eyes. "You're not lying. That's the hell of it. Every word is true and clean as spring water." She leaned back. "So the question isn't whether you're honest. The question is what we do with an honest man's crime."

"It's not yours to forgive," Hobb said. "That's what I'm telling you. Whatever forgiveness exists for what I did, it died with the east. It lives with no one in this room. You can't give me absolution and I won't insult the dead by asking for it."

"Then why tell us?" Sef asked. "Now. On the road. With hunters behind us and Ilvane closing on the valley like a hand closing on a fly."

Hobb looked at Paloma.

"Because she's coming for you," he said. "You felt it on the salt fields. That sigil she burned into your skin—that wasn't a mark, child. That was a *taste*. She sampled you, the way I sampled her poisons for six years. And I know how she eats. She starts with the ones who smell interesting." He lowered himself back into his chair, heavily, an old man's descent. "And because she'll make you an offer. Sooner or later, alone, when none of us can hear it. She always does. It's how she hunts. She doesn't break doors—she talks people into opening them. She offered me life. She'll offer you something tailored to the shape of your wanting. And when she does, I need you to remember that I sat where you're sitting, and I said yes, and the yes tasted like nothing at all."

"Like immunity," Paloma said.

"Like immunity," Hobb agreed. "The sweetest poison there is. It works instantly, and it kills you slowly, and it kills everyone around you first."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. Then they rose, crossed the room, and did the thing Hobb had not expected and had no armor against: they put their hand on his shoulder, the scarred shoulder, the marked one, and left it there.

"We're not forgiving you," they said. "You're right—it's not ours to give. But you're in this kitchen, and this kitchen is ours, and nobody eats alone in it. That's not absolution. That's just supper."

Hobb bowed his head over their hand and wept, silently, the way old men do, as if crying were a choreography learned late and practiced badly.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Sef stayed awake beside the banked fire with the prophecy book open on his knees. Hobb heard the pages turn, turn, turn—and then stop.

"Old man," Sef said quietly. "Come look at this."

Hobb crossed the room. In the margin of a page dated a hundred years past, in faded brown ink, someone had written a single line in a cramped, hurried hand:

*The taster of the east opened the door for her. May the next door be harder.*

Below it, newer—ink still dark, as if written yesterday—another line had appeared, in handwriting Hobb recognized with a chill that no immunity could blunt.

His own.

*I am sorry,* it read. *I am trying to pay.*

Sef looked up at him. "When did you write this?"

"I didn't," Hobb said. "Not yet."

Outside, far off across the salt flats, something began to sing.
