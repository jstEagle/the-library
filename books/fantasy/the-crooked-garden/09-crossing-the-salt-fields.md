## 9. Crossing the Salt Fields

I learned to steal by learning what people watched. Nobody watches the kitchen girl with flour on her knuckles and a limp she exaggerates for sympathy. For eleven years in the palace of Vell, I was furniture that could pick locks, and I liked it that way. Invisible is safe. Invisible is a kind of home.

So when the salt flats opened up before us at dawn—white to every horizon, cracked like old porcelain—and I felt the strange pull in my chest that meant *this place wants you forgotten*, I understood it immediately. I'd been living on the salt flats my whole life. I just hadn't known they had a geography.

"Stay close," Hobb said, which was the fourth time he'd said it since we left the palace road. He walked with his staff planted like a surveyor's stake, measuring each step before he trusted it. "The flats don't take things all at once. They take them in pieces. A name first. Then a face. Then—"

"Then nothing," Marisol finished, walking backward ahead of us so she could grin at everyone at once. She had her sleeves rolled up despite the cold, because Marisol had never met weather she wouldn't argue with. "Then you're just weather too. My grandmother used to sing about this place. *Where the songs go when nobody sings them.*"

"Your grandmother sang about everything," Paloma said.

"And she was right about most of it."

Paloma didn't answer. They were doing the thing they did now—the new thing, the thing that had started the night Sef brought them that book—where their eyes went distant and their hand drifted up to touch their own cheek, as if checking that their face was still where they'd left it. The sketch in the prophecy book had done something to them. It had taken the small controlled risks they'd built their whole life on and shown them the cliff edge underneath.

I knew the feeling. I'd spent eleven years being furniture, and then one day the Dowager Queen's spymaster had caught me elbow-deep in his strongbox, and instead of hanging me he'd said, *You have useful hands. The kitchens need a cook who can also count.* And I'd spent three years waiting for him to change his mind about the hanging part. Waiting is its own kind of wound.

Sef walked beside me with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. He hadn't let go of it once since the border incident—not to eat, not to sleep, not even when he fell into the stream two days back and came up sputtering with one arm clamped over it. I'd offered twice to carry it. He'd looked at me both times like I'd offered to carry his actual heart out of his chest, and maybe I had.

"How far?" I asked him.

"The ledger says the waystation sits at the valley's western edge." He kept his voice low, the way he did in the archives, as if the books might be sleeping. "Three days' walk across the flats. Then the hills."

"And the Garden after that."

"And the Garden after that."

Marisol spun on her heel and walked forward properly, falling into step beside Paloma. "Cheer up, taster. If the flats eat your name, I'll give you one of mine. I've got extras. Marisol Vane-who-broke-the-Duke's-window. Marisol Vane-who-kissed-the-ambassador's-son-and-lived. Marisol Vane-who-tastes-lies-in-the-stew, which is why nobody invites me to dinner anymore."

"You taste lies," Hobb said, without looking up from the ground.

"I taste lies."

"What do I taste like right now?"

Marisol stopped walking. That was the thing about her—she joked like breathing, but she never joked past a real question. She studied Hobb the way she studied a suspicious sauce: head tilted, eyes narrowed, tongue pressed to the roof of her mouth.

"Like regret," she said finally. "Old regret. Cooled and congealed, with a skin on top."

Hobb nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed a recipe he already knew by heart, and kept walking.

* * *

By midday the flats began showing us what they were.

It started small. A tree on the horizon where no tree should be—no water out here, no soil worth the name—but there it stood, an orchard tree heavy with fruit that was almost apples. Almost. The shape was right but the color slid away when you looked straight at it, gold going gray going gold again, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Ghost-orchard," Hobb said quietly. "Don't eat the fruit."

"Why not?" Marisol asked, already drifting toward it, because Marisol.

"Because it isn't fruit. It's the memory of fruit. An orchard that was erased somewhere else and died here, at the edge, trying to get home." His voice did something I'd never heard it do—it went gentle. "Eat it and you'll be full of someone else's hunger. You'll spend the rest of your life tasting a country that doesn't exist anymore and wondering why nothing else satisfies."

Marisol came back to us without another word. But she looked at the tree a long time as we passed it, and I saw her lips moving, and I would have bet my best lockpicks she was singing to it. Giving it a song, since it only had half of one.

There were more, the further west we went. A bridge with no river under it, its stones remembering water. A child's shoe, sitting upright in the salt, still holding the shape of a foot. Once, faintly, on the wind, a melody—four notes, rising, then a gap where the fifth note should have been, then silence. We all heard it. We all stopped. None of us said anything, because what was there to say? Somewhere behind us or beyond us or nowhere at all, a song had been murdered, and this was the place its body washed up.

Paloma crouched by the shoe for a long moment. When they stood, their jaw was set in the particular way I'd learned meant they'd made a decision and were not accepting arguments.

"Faster," they said. "We walk faster."

We walked faster.

* * *

The hunters came at dusk on the second day, and afterward I would keep replaying it, looking for the moment I could have caught earlier. There wasn't one. That was the worst part. There was never one.

I smelled them first—or rather, I smelled the absence of them. Out on the flats everything has a smell, even the ghosts: the almost-apples sweet and wrong, the salt itself sharp as struck flint. But off to our north there was a patch of air that smelled of nothing at all, a hole in the world's scent, and my hands knew what that meant before my head did. Nothing-smelling means warded. Warded means hunting.

"Hobb," I said. "North. Tell me you smell that."

He lifted his head like an old hound. His face went the color of the salt.

"Off the path," he said. "Now. Flat down, all of you, and *quiet*—"

The light hit before he finished.

It came out of the nothing-patch like a spear thrown by the dark itself—a thread of pale silver, thin as wire, humming. It crossed the distance between the ridge and our column faster than a scream, and it went through the space where Marisol had been standing half a heartbeat before, because Marisol had turned to say something clever, and for once in her life the cleverness saved her.

But the wire was not alone. Three more threads fanned out from the ridge, unhurried, patient, the way a fisherman works a line. And I understood, watching them move, that these weren't attacks. They were questions. *Where are you? Where are you? Where are you?* Each thread that touched ground bloomed into a small ring of silver script that hung in the air and pulsed, and each pulse made my teeth ache and my eyes water, made the ghost-orchard behind us shudder and drop its impossible fruit.

"They're mapping us," Sef breathed. He had the book out of its oilcloth without any memory of deciding to unwrap it. "Sigils. Surveyor's sigils—they're charting the valley's edge, they're finding the holes in the—"

One of the threads found him.

No. That's not true, and I won't write it that way, even in my own head. Threads don't find. Threads are aimed. One of the threads was aimed at Sef, and it would have gone through him like the others went through salt, except that Paloma moved.

I've thought about that a hundred times since. Paloma Brightwater, who had spent their whole life avoiding exactly this kind of risk, who swallowed poison in careful measured doses because controlled danger was the only danger they could bear—Paloma stepped sideways, into the wire's path, and took it across the shoulder.

The sound they made wasn't a scream. It was worse. It was surprise—a short, punched-out *oh*, the sound of someone discovering a new flavor, and I will hear that *oh* until I die.

They went down in the salt. The silver script crawled off the wire and onto their collarbone, spreading like frost across a window, writing itself into their skin in letters none of us could read. Sef was on his knees beside them instantly, the prophecy book abandoned in the dirt—I'd never seen him let go of it—and his hands hovering over the sigil, shaking.

"Burn it out," Hobb said. He'd reached us, staff planted, and his voice was terrible in its calm. "Sef. You've read the border texts. Burn it out or it finishes writing, and if it finishes writing, everything Ilvane is will know exactly where we are, every hour, forever."

"I don't know how—"

"You know how. You're an archivist. You know how fire treats paper. Do it to the paper of them."

Sef looked at Paloma's face. Paloma, white as the flats, gritted out: "Do it."

And here is the thing I did, the thing I keep doing arithmetic on in the dark hours: while Sef cupped his hands around the sigil and whispered the burning words and Paloma bit down on Marisol's belt-leather and screamed through their nose, I moved toward the ridge.

Not bravely. Let me be clear about that, because Marisol tells it bravely and I let her, and every time she tells it I feel like a thief twice over. I moved toward the ridge because I am a coward with professional skills, and cowards with professional skills know that the only thing worse than being hunted is being hunted by something you can't see. I wanted to see it. Seeing is controlling. Stealing is just seeing with your hands.

I got low behind a salt ridge forty yards out and looked up at the crest, and there they were.

Three shapes, standing in the last red light. Not soldiers. Not animals. They were made the way the ghost-orchard was made—almost-things, memories wearing bodies, except these memories belonged to Ilvane. Hunters she had eaten and kept. Their outlines kept sliding, one becoming another becoming the first, and where their faces should have been there was smooth blankness, like pages torn out. In the chest of the middle one, a small silver light pulsed in time with the sigil on Paloma's shoulder.

They weren't looking at me. They were looking past me, at the company, at the little knot of us around Paloma's fallen body, and the pulse in the middle hunter's chest was steady and slow and patient as a clock.

I could have thrown a stone. I had stones. I had knives. I had eleven years of knowing exactly where soft things keep their soft parts.

Instead I lay flat in the salt and memorized them—their spacing, their stillness, the way the light pulsed—and I crept back the way I'd come, because that is what Bramble does. Bramble gathers information. Bramble survives. Bramble does not throw the stone.

I hate her a little, that version of me. I keep her anyway. She's the one who's still alive.

* * *

The sigil came out of Paloma's shoulder in a curl of smoke that smelled like burnt sugar and something older underneath, something like libraries. Afterward there was a mark left behind—not a scar exactly. A scar is closed. This was a small silvered patch of skin, the size of a coin, that shimmered when the light hit it wrong, and no amount of salve or time changed it.

"It's dead," Sef said, for the tenth time. He hadn't stopped touching it since it cooled. Two fingers, feather-light, over and over, checking. "Burned out. It can't report anymore."

"But it happened," Marisol said.

She was standing apart from us, arms crossed, looking west at the darkening flats. Behind us the hunters had withdrawn at full dark, the threads of silver reeling back over the ridge like fishing lines pulled in. We'd broken camp in the dark and walked another two miles on Hobb's insistence, and now we'd finally stopped, and Marisol had finally stopped performing, and what was left was just a woman standing in the cold with her arms crossed.

"It happened," she said again. "She knows we're here. Maybe not where—we burned it out, fine, wonderful, Sef, you beautiful nerd, well done—but she knows *someone* is crossing her flats toward her door. She'll stop asking politely now."

"She was never asking politely," Paloma said. Their voice was rough. They were sitting up against a pack, shirt off to the waist, and the silvered mark caught the firelight and threw it back wrong. "Those threads were surveyor's tools. Questions. The next ones will be answers."

Nobody laughed. Even Marisol.

Hobb was grinding herbs in a mortar with the slow, deliberate rhythm of a man keeping his hands busy so his mouth wouldn't start confessing things. He'd been doing it since the attack. I'd seen that rhythm before, in the palace kitchens, on the nights the Dowager Queen's health frightened him. Guilt-work, I called it. Cooking as penance.

"Let me see the wound," he said, and knelt by Paloma, and I watched him go still.

"What?"

"It's not closing." He said it quietly, but the flats are quiet places and quiet carries. "The burn. Look at the edges. Day-old edges. It should be knitting."

Paloma craned to look. So did I, and I saw it, and my stomach dropped through the salt and kept dropping. The wound was clean—Sef's burning had been precise—but it sat in Paloma's flesh like a thing preserved. No swelling. No sealing. Just an open fact, fresh as the hour it was made.

"Outside time," Hobb murmured. "Her magic doesn't live inside Vell's time. It comes from outside, from the empire, from everywhere the maps still remember. And a wound made outside time—" He stopped. Started again. "It heals when time touches it. That's what healing *is*. But here, inside the erasure, where time goes thick and slow—"

"Say it plainly, old man," Marisol said, and her voice cracked on *old man*, and I realized she was scared, really scared, for the first time since I'd known her.

Hobb looked up at her, and then at Paloma, and his face was the saddest thing I saw on those flats, and I saw a lot of sad things there.

"It won't close," he said. "Not here. Not until we cross into a country that exists. Every day we stay in Vell, that wound stays exactly as open as it is right now. And if it opens further—if anything tears it—there's no healing coming. Not here. Not for anyone hurt by her magic."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, the four-note melody rose on the wind again, and paused where its fifth note used to be, and did not finish.

Paloma looked down at the silvered mark on their shoulder, then at the raw-edged wound beneath it, and I watched them do the arithmetic we were all doing. Three days to the waystation. More to the Garden. And behind us, somewhere in the dark, three almost-things with torn-out faces, no longer asking questions.

"Well," Paloma said at last, and their voice was steadier than mine would have been, steadier than it had any right to be. "Then we'd better not bleed."

Marisol laughed—one sharp, startled bark—and then pressed her hand over her mouth like she'd bitten something bitter, because laughing at that felt like a lie, and Marisol Vane cannot abide lies. Not even her own.

I sat down at the fire's edge and put my back to the darkness and my eyes on my family—all five of them, scarred and scared and silver-marked and stubborn—and I made myself a promise, the kind I'd never made anyone in eleven years of furniture-life:

Next time, I throw the stone.
