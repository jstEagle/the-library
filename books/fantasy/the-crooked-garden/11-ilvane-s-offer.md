## 11. Ilvane's Offer

Ilvane had eaten six nations, and she could tell you what each of them tasted like.

Cael Morath had been oysters and cold iron. The Weeping Steps had been salt and something almost like cinnamon, if cinnamon had been weeping. Little Anselm, the first, the easiest, had tasted of milk and new bread—she'd been young then, and the Mage-Censors before her had done most of the work, and she had swallowed a country the way a child swallows a plum, whole and greedy and without thought for the pit.

Vell tasted of nothing.

That was the trouble with it. That was the fascination.

She stood at the valley's edge where the salt fields began their white burn toward the horizon, and she let the absence roll over her like weather. Most mages found this place unbearable. The magic drained out of them within an hour, pooling somewhere beneath the soil like spilled wine soaking into a tablecloth, and they went home gray-faced and muttering about cursed ground. Ilvane had been standing here for three days. She did not drain because she was not, properly speaking, full of anything anymore. She was a vessel shaped by her appetites, and appetites do not leak.

"Report," she said, without turning.

The hunter familiar came down out of the dark sky on wings made of folded survey-parchment, its sigil-eye still smoldering from where someone in the valley had burned it half to death. It landed badly. It knelt, which familiars only do when they have failed, and it projected its findings directly into her mind: a kitchen. A girl with soot on her cheekbones and a boy with ink-stained fingers. A sigil drawn and burning. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the taste of the prophecy book—the old one, the sealed one, the one that should have crumbled to ash a century ago along with every other book that dared to name Vell's ending.

*They know,* the familiar said, in the voice of a ledger being read aloud. *They know about the Garden.*

"Of course they know," Ilvane said. "The Dowager Queen has known for forty years. She's been sitting on that knowledge like a hen on a stone egg." She turned at last and looked out over Vell—not the border, but the valley itself, which she could see now that her hunters had chewed a hairline crack in the world's skin. Lamplight in windows. Smoke from bread-ovens rising straight up in the windless evening. A country going about its small, doomed business, unaware—or worse, aware and pretending otherwise—that it was already on her plate.

She had expected fear. Fear was the usual seasoning. Nations on the edge of being forgotten always smelled of fear in their final years, a high nervous scent like copper and sweat, and it made the eating easier or harder depending on her mood.

But Vell did not smell of fear. Vell smelled of *contentment*. Bread and lamp-oil and clean linen and people who had made peace with being no one, nowhere, to no one. It was the most infuriating thing she had ever tasted.

"It thinks it's safe," she said softly. "A hundred years inside its own borders, and it has convinced itself that invisibility is a blessing."

The familiar waited. Familiars were good at waiting; they were made of paperwork, essentially, and paperwork's whole nature is patience.

"No," Ilvane said. "We won't breach yet. Send word to the Censorate: hold the perimeter. I want the door found first. A nation eaten through its own back door tastes of surrender, and surrender—" she smiled, and the salt fields dimmed slightly, as they always did when she smiled, as if the light itself preferred not to watch, "—surrender is the finest flavor there is."

*And if they will not surrender?*

"They will," said Ilvane. "I've never met a people who wouldn't. Not when the offer is made correctly."

* *

She went in alone.

This was not arrogance, whatever her hunters believed. It was method. Empires send armies, and armies make nations dig in and remember what they love. But one woman, unarmed, walking openly up the valley road at dusk—that makes a nation *curious*, and curiosity is a far softer thing to work with than courage.

The wards should have stopped her. She felt them as she crossed the inner line, a sensation like walking into a spiderweb woven from pure negation, magic unraveling off her skin thread by thread. She let it happen. Let them see her stripped. A mage entering Vell was just a woman; a woman was just a mouth; and mouths, Ilvane had learned across six centuries, could be fed.

They found her at the market square in Lower Vell, of course. Word traveled fast in a country the size of a dinner plate. By the time she reached the fountain—a modest thing, carved with fish whose symbolism had probably meant something before the erasure—there were guards with pikes, and behind them a scatter of civilians holding brooms and rolling pins and one memorable cleaver, and at the front of them all, pushing through with the particular authority of someone who had spent a lifetime tasting other people's food and surviving it, stood the taster from the familiar's report.

Paloma Brightwater. The name had come to her through imperial records that should not have contained it—records that predated the erasure, scribbled in a census-taker's margin. *Brightwater, infant, origin unknown.* She had wondered about that for years. Origin unknown, in a country where everyone's origin was the same four hundred square miles. It had nagged at her the way a bone caught in the teeth nags.

Now here they were, soot still faintly on their cheekbones, chin up, eyes doing the quick taster's arithmetic—*is this person poisoned, is this person poison, can I survive either answer.*

"You're a long way from your empire," Paloma said.

"I'm exactly where I intend to be." Ilvane spread her empty hands. "No soldiers. No hunters. Just me, and a request."

"We don't grant requests from things that eat countries."

"Mm. And yet you're listening." She let the smile come again, smaller this time, calibrated. Around the square, the pikes wavered. Fear was useful, but doubt was better, and doubt was what she planted now, gently, the way one presses a seed into soil. "I would like to speak with you alone, Taster Brightwater. You may bring your knife. You may even use it—I promise you it will not matter, and I promise you I will not retaliate. Those are two promises I have kept for six hundred years, and I am very tired of keeping them, but I keep them anyway."

"Why me?" Paloma asked. And there it was—the question she wanted, asked exactly as she'd hoped. Not *why should we trust you*, which was a wall, but *why me*, which was a door.

"Because," Ilvane said, "you are the only person in this valley who understands my work."

* *

They gave her the palace's small dining room, because Paloma insisted on neutral ground with witnesses nearby, and Ilvane agreed to everything because agreement was cheaper than argument and twice as disarming. Two guards at the door. The Dowager Queen's spymaster pretending to dust a shelf he had clearly never dusted in his life. And between Ilvane and the taster, a table set—for some reason—with a single plate, a single cup, and a carafe of valley wine.

Paloma sat across from her and did not touch any of it.

"You'll want to test the wine," Ilvane said. "Go ahead. I'll wait."

"I don't need to test it. I know the vintage. I know the cellar it came from." Paloma poured, held the cup to the light, swirled it with a professional's wrist—and then, deliberately, set it down untasted. "But I'm not drinking with you. That's the first rule of my oath. Never share a table with someone who wants something."

"A sensible oath." Ilvane leaned back. "And how many times have you broken it?"

The flicker in those eyes was worth the whole journey. "Once or twice."

"Then you already know what I've come to say, because you've said it to yourself in the dark, in the kitchens, at three in the morning, when the honey cake sits in front of you and your hands shake and nobody watches." Ilvane let the silence stretch. "You understand appetite, Taster. You have built a life around swallowing small deaths so that others don't have to. Every dish you've ever tested, you tested *instead of someone else*. Do you know what that makes you, in the grammar of empires?"

"Tired," said Paloma.

"Necessary." Ilvane leaned forward. "It makes you necessary. And necessity is the one currency that never devalues. So let me make you an offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm a monster, because I assure you, I decided that about myself long ago and arrived at more interesting conclusions."

She laid it out the way she laid out all her offers: slowly, warmly, like a meal served course by course.

"The prophecy book names tonight. It names you. It shows a door made of vines, and it shows you standing at that door, and I have read enough prophecy in six centuries to tell you what it does *not* show: what happens after. Books like that never show after. Because after, for Vell, is me." She said it without cruelty, the way a physician says *the growth is malignant*. "In eleven days—twelve, if your Dowager's wards hold longer than I expect—I breach this valley, and everything in it comes apart. Not dies. *Unravels.* Streets forget their own names. Children fade mid-laugh. Your archivist's beautiful handwriting will outlive his hands. I have watched it six times, and each time I told myself it was necessary, and each time the taste was exquisite, and neither of those facts cancels the other."

"And your offer," Paloma said flatly, "is that I hand over the book and you eat us politely."

"My offer," Ilvane said, "is that you *dine* with me instead."

She watched the words land. Watched the taster's jaw tighten and their hands go still on the table—still the way a person goes still when something has struck close to the bone.

"I can give you what I have," she continued softly. "Immunity. True immunity—not the taster's trick of building tolerance one poison at a time, one scarred liver at a time, until you die at fifty with veins like old rope. I mean the real thing. Nothing made by man or mage can harm you. You would never flinch over a plate again. You would never swallow another death for anyone." She paused. "And more than that. Memory. I remember six nations, Taster. Every street of them. Every song. When I ate little Anselm, I took its four million lives into myself, and they are *alive in me*—their weddings, their funerals, their terrible poetry. A nation eaten by me is not destroyed. It is *preserved*. In the only vault that lasts."

"That's not preservation," Paloma said quietly. "That's a cage with you as the bars."

"It is the only preservation there is!" For a moment—half a heartbeat—the elegance cracked and something older looked out, something hungry and hollowed, and then the mask returned, smoother than before. Ilvane breathed. Recalibrated. This one was sharper than the usual tasters. Good. Sharp things were worth collecting. "Forgive me. Six hundred years of being misunderstood makes one... emphatic. Let me put it plainly, then, as plainly as I know how.

"Give me the book. Tell me where the Crooked Garden stands—or better, walk me to it yourself, since the door seems inclined to know you. In exchange, I take Vell gently. No breach. No unraveling. No fading children. The valley simply... ends, the way a dream ends, painlessly, and wakes up inside me, where I will keep it. Every soul in Vell continues—in memory, in me, perfectly, forever. And you—" she smiled, and this time she let all the warmth she had left pour into it, and it was, she knew, considerable warmth, warped but real, "—you come with me. To the capital. To my table. As my taster, my equal, my heir, if you live long enough to want it. You will eat dishes from six dead nations cooked by the last chef who remembers them. You will be *seen*, Paloma Brightwater. By emperors. By history. By me. Isn't that what none of you have ever been? Seen?"

The room was silent. The spymaster had stopped pretending to dust. One of the guards had gone pale.

Paloma looked at her for a long moment, and Ilvane saw the temptation working in them—she was expert at seeing it, it was her favorite sight in all the world, that brief bright struggle between the self one has and the self one is offered. The taster's eyes dropped to the untouched wine. Their throat moved.

"You really think," Paloma said at last, "that being remembered by the thing that ate you is better than being free and forgotten."

"I think," said Ilvane, "that freedom is a story forgotten things tell themselves. I have eaten six nations' worth of freedom, Taster. It tastes like nothing. Like this valley. Like air." She rose, unhurried, and pushed a small card of black vellum across the table—blank, except that it would bear writing only for the one who held it. "Three days. That is my mercy, and I do not offer mercy often, so mark it well. Three days, and then I stop asking."

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back.

"For what it's worth—and it is worth something, though you won't believe me—you are the first person in six hundred years I have offered a seat rather than a place on the plate. Consider what that says about you. Consider what it says about what you could become."

She walked out into the Vell night, unarmed and unbothered, past the pikes and the pale faces, and the valley's negation peeled gently at her edges like a tide worrying a cliff.

Behind her, in the small dining room, Paloma Brightwater sat alone with an untouched glass of wine and a blank black card, and began—to Ilvane's certain knowledge, for she had seeded the room with one small listening charm, a cheat, yes, but cheats were simply seasoning—to lie.
