## 12. Refusal, With Garnish

Ilvane poured the tea herself.

That was the first obscenity of it. A woman who had eaten six nations whole, who could unmake a border with a flick of her ring finger, standing in the ruined waystation kitchen in a traveling coat worth more than Vell's annual harvest, warming a chipped pot over a stove she had no business knowing how to light. She moved like someone who had studied domesticity from a great distance and found it charming, the way one finds a dancing bear charming.

"Sit," she said. "You've been walking for two days. Your feet are bleeding through your left boot—I can smell the iron."

Paloma sat. Not because she told them to. Because their legs had been negotiating surrender since the salt fields, and this was the first chair they'd seen since Marisol's wound started refusing to close.

The tea was chamomile. Of course it was chamomile. Paloma lifted the cup and did not drink it, and Ilvane watched them not drink it with an expression of genuine professional appreciation.

"You don't test anything anymore," Paloma said. "Do you. You just know."

"I taste intention." She settled across the table, folding her hands like a grandmother at a funeral. "Yours is very loud, taster. It says: *keep her talking.*"

*My intention also says that,* Paloma thought, and hated how transparent they were, and kept talking anyway.

Outside, the wind off the salt fields made the ruined kitchen's broken shutters knock against their frames like polite, persistent visitors. Somewhere beyond the eastern wall—two rooms, a collapsed roof, a garden gone to seed a century ago—the others were sleeping. Hobb on watch. Bramble curled around the ledger like it was a warm animal. Sef with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest even in sleep, the way other men held lovers or weapons.

Marisol was in the pantry, fevered, her wound knitting itself wrong because time out here didn't know how to hold a cut together. Every hour Paloma spent at this table was an hour Marisol bled crooked.

So: keep her talking. But not forever.

"You offered me something," Paloma said. "Before you got sentimental about tea."

"I offered you everything." Ilvane turned her cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, precise as a clock hand. "Immunity. You understand immunity better than anyone alive, I think. You've built yours drop by drop, poison by poison, for years. I'm offering you the finished version. Nothing will ever hurt you again. No blade, no toxin, no age, no grief that lasts longer than a season." She smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the worst thing about her. "And empire, of course. A place at my table. The empire has excellent tables, Brightwater. You'd be amazed what survives in the cellars of nations I've eaten. Wines from countries that no longer exist. Honey from orchards only I remember."

Paloma's mouth watered.

They were ashamed of it later—ashamed in the specific, sour way of a person who has discovered a trapdoor in themselves—but in the moment, sitting in the ruined kitchen with their feet bleeding and their friend rotting gently in a pantry, their mouth *watered*. Honey from orchards only she remembers. A tongue that would never burn again. A body that would never have to swallow another measured dose of arsenic and smile at a queen who might be lying to them.

"You eat countries," Paloma said.

"I *preserve* countries." Ilvane's voice didn't sharpen; it softened, which was more frightening. "Do you know what happens to a forgotten nation when no one eats it? It rots. It frays. Its songs lose their words and its dead lose their names and eventually there's nothing left but a shape on the land where grief used to be. I am not a destroyer, taster. I am a *cellar*. Everything I take, I keep. Perfectly. Forever." She leaned forward. "Vell is already dying. You feel it. The edges going soft. Your Dowager Queen pretending the valley isn't smaller than it was when she was crowned. I can end that slowly, the way it's ending now—or I can end it cleanly, tonight, and every soul in Vell goes into my keeping intact. Whole. Remembered." A pause, delicate as a garnish. "Except you. You walk out. Immortal. Fed. Free."

"And all I have to give you is the book."

"The book," Ilvane agreed. "The little prophet that's been leading you by the nose across my salt fields. Where is it?"

And here was the moment. Paloma had known it was coming since she'd said *sit*, had felt it approaching the way a taster feels the almond note under honey—thin, sweet, and underneath it, cyanide. She had rehearsed nothing. That was the terrible discovery of the next three seconds: she needed no rehearsal. The lie arrived fully dressed, smiling, holding out its hand like an old friend.

"It's not with us," Paloma said. "Sef hid it before we crossed the salt fields. He's the archivist—he thinks in fail-safes. If we don't come back, if anything happens to any of us, the book surfaces on its own. In the palace. In the Dowager Queen's hands. And then every taster, every cook, every servant who can read a recipe reads it, and the Garden's location stops being a secret worth eating anyone for."

It came out smooth. It came out *easy*. And Paloma sat there tasting their own voice the way they tasted a suspect dish, cataloguing the notes—*fluency, plausibility, a whisper of truth for body*—and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water down the spine, that they had always been able to do this. That years of swallowing poison had trained not just the stomach but the mouth. That survival had soaked so deep into them it now came out of them seasoned, plated, ready to serve.

*How easily it comes,* they thought. *How easily I lie to save my skin and call it strategy.*

Ilvane considered this. Her cup completed another slow rotation.

"You're lying," she said pleasantly.

"Yes," Paloma said.

Her eyebrows rose—a fraction, a millimeter of genuine surprise, and Paloma clung to it like a rope thrown into floodwater.

"But," Paloma went on, heart slamming against its ribs, "you should notice *what* I lied about. I didn't say where the book is. I said what happens if you take it from me. Because that part's true whether or not the book exists. Kill me, search me, crack open Sef's ribs for his oilcloth bundle—and find nothing, or find the book, and either way the Dowager Queen learns within the week that the empire knows about the Garden. Either way, Vell burns its own archives, scatters its own people, salts its own trails. You'd get your meal, Mage-Censor. You'd just get it gristled and cold, with half the meat gone bad in the panic." They lifted the untouched tea and finally drank, because a taster never refuses a cup twice—it looks like fear. Chamomile. Bitter at the edge. She'd laced it with something mild and truth-loosening, and Paloma let it sit on their tongue like a professional courtesy between colleagues. "I'm not offering you the book. I'm offering you a better dinner. Patience."

Silence. The shutters knocked. In the pantry, faintly, Marisol murmured a name in her fever—Bramble's name, or maybe Bramble's brother's; Paloma couldn't tell.

Ilvane laughed.

It was a real laugh, low and unguarded, and it transformed her face into something almost lovable, and Paloma understood in that instant how six nations had walked into her mouth without ever quite believing she was hungry.

"Oh, you're *wonderful*," she said. "Do you know how long it's been since someone negotiated with me? They beg. They pray. One king offered me his entire harem, which tells you everything about why his nation deserved what it got." She stood, smoothing her coat, and the lamplight bent toward her slightly, the way plants bend toward a window. "Very well, taster. I'll give you until the moon sets tomorrow night. Bring me the book—or bring me a reason better than the one you just served me, and believe me, I will taste it." At the door she paused, silhouetted against the starless dark of the valley's edge, and added, almost tenderly:

"For what it's worth, I hope you run. The ones who run taste better."

Then she was gone—not through the door so much as *out* of the room, the way a word leaves a page, and the kitchen was just a kitchen again, cold and ruined and smelling of dust and old thyme.

Paloma sat alone with two cups of tea and shook for a long time.

* * *

They found Sef awake.

Of course he was awake. He was sitting on the collapsed garden wall behind the waystation with the oilcloth bundle in his lap and the moon coming up fat and white behind him, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been sitting somewhere long enough to hear everything.

"How much," Paloma said.

"All of it." Sef didn't look at them. He was turning the prophecy book over in his hands, not opening it, just weighing it, as if its mass had changed. "She was loud, for a monster. Or you were. I couldn't tell which."

"Sef—"

"You told her the book was hidden in the palace." Now he looked up, and his eyes in the moonlight were the gray of paper about to be written on. "You told her the Dowager Queen has it. You bought our lives with a lie that puts a target on everyone we love, in a city we can't reach, to warn a woman who can't fight her." His voice stayed level. That was the worst part. Sef angry was loud and forgivable. Sef quiet was a locked door. "And it worked. She believed you. Do you want to know the part that keeps me sitting out here instead of sleeping?"

"Tell me."

"She asked if you were lying, and you said yes, and she *believed the rest anyway*." He laughed, short and airless. "Because you're good, Paloma. You're so good it doesn't matter what's true. You could sell her the sun and she'd check the sky for shadows after."

Paloma climbed up onto the wall beside him. Their boots screamed. Below them the dead garden sprawled, rose bushes gone feral and gray, a sundial whose gnomon had rusted away so that it told no time at all—which felt, lately, like most things.

"I hated it," Paloma said. "If that helps."

"It doesn't."

"I know it doesn't." They pulled their knees up, winced, kept going. "But I need you to hear the whole dish before you send it back. She offered me immortality, Sef. A seat at her table. All I had to do was hand over the book and let her eat Vell *cleanly*, and some part of me—that part that's swallowed arsenic every morning for nine years and called it a career—some part of me wanted to say yes. So yes, I lied. I lied fast and I lied well and I'll probably lie again before this is over, because that's what I am. I'm not brave. I'm *preserved*. There's a difference, and I need you to know the difference before you—" they gestured at the bundle in his lap, at himself, at the whole ragged enterprise—"before you decide what I'm worth."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. The moon climbed. Somewhere in the house, Hobb coughed in his sleep, a deep wet sound, an old man's sound.

"When I found the book," Sef said finally, "the day everything changed—you remember what I said? Before I showed it to you?"

"You said it was against protocol."

"I said it was against protocol, and then I brought it to you anyway, because—" he stopped, jaw working, and Paloma realized with a small, painful shock that Sef was frightened, genuinely frightened, not of Ilvane but of something closer and less survivable. "Because I trusted you. That was the whole protocol. That was the entire archive. One archivist deciding one person was worth breaking a hundred years of rules for." He held the book out—not giving it, showing it, the way you show a scar. "And tonight I heard you lie to the thing that ate six nations, smoothly, instantly, without a single tell, and I thought: *how many times has that voice lied to me?* Small lies. Kind lies. Taster's lies. *No, my lord, the wine is fine. Yes, my lady, the queen enjoyed the soup.* How would I even know?"

"You'd know," Paloma said. "You always know. You knew I was afraid of the dark until I was twelve. You knew I liked you before I did."

"That was before I learned what you sound like when you're being *effective*."

The words landed like a dropped knife—no malice in them, which somehow drove them deeper. Paloma looked away, at the sundial telling no time, and felt the family they'd spent ten days assembling shift subtly around them, the way a table shifts when one leg is shorter than the others. Marisol trusted them because Paloma had carried her. Bramble trusted them because Paloma hadn't flinched at the thefts. Hobb trusted them because shame recognizes shame. And Sef—

Sef had trusted them because Paloma had once shared their bread ration during a palace shortage and never mentioned it, and now that same man was looking at them like a dish he wasn't sure he should swallow.

"I'm going to say one more thing," Paloma said, "and then you can hate me quietly until dawn. The lie was wrong. The *method* was right. She can't be beaten straight—Hobb told us that, Hobb who served her, Hobb who would know. She can only be fed. So I fed her. Time, doubt, a reason to wait. Tomorrow night when the moon sets, she expects the book. Which means tomorrow night, while she's waiting for a delivery that's never coming, we're walking through a gate she thinks we haven't found yet." They turned and took Sef's wrist, feeling his pulse jump under their fingers, quick as a bird's. "I lied to save Marisol's life and yours and mine and every person asleep in that house. I'd do it again. I'll hate myself efficiently, in private, on schedule. But don't you dare stand on that wall and pretend you wouldn't have done the same, because I *saw* your face when you burned the sigil out of my arm, Sef. You lied to me for three days about how much it would hurt. We are all crooked. That's rather the point of the garden."

Sef exhaled. It came out shaky, which was its own kind of honesty.

"If you lie to me again," he said, "about anything that matters—"

"You'll feed me to the gate yourself. Understood."

"And Paloma?" He finally, finally leaned his shoulder against theirs, warm through the ruined coat. "The part where you wanted to say yes to her. Thank you for telling me that. Don't ever stop telling me that. The day you hide the wanting is the day I stop following you."

They sat together on the wall until the moon was high, saying nothing, which was its own treaty.

* * *

Inside, past midnight, Paloma checked on Marisol. The wound had spread a bruise the color of storm-water up her side, and her fever-talk had shifted from names to numbers—she was counting something, endlessly, in a language Paloma didn't know. Bramble slept sitting up against the pantry door, one hand resting on Marisol's ankle like an anchor line.

In the main room, Hobb sat by the dead hearth, awake, whittling nothing.

"She'll come back early," the old man said, without looking up. "Monsters always do. They say when, and then they come early, to catch you mid-run."

"Then we leave before the moon sets," Paloma said. "Tonight. Now. While she's savoring her patience."

Hobb's knife paused. He looked up, and in his ancient face there was something new—not hope exactly, but the place hope is quarried from. "You know where the gate is."

Paloma reached into their shirt and drew out the scrap of cook's ledger they'd torn free in the archives, the page with ingredients that hadn't existed for a hundred years, and beneath them, in faded brown ink, a list of directions written like a recipe:

*A garden needs a wall. A wall needs bones. Follow the oldest graves uphill until the hedge smells of every country you've lost.*

"Hobb," Paloma said, "start packing the pots. We're going to need to cook."
