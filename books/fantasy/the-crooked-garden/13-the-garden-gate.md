## 13. The Garden Gate

The ledger said *follow the salt until it forgets to be salt*, and for two days it did nothing of the kind. The flats stretched white and endless, crunching underfoot like old porcelain, and Sef began to suspect the cook who wrote those pages a century ago had been either a genius or a liar, and that the difference hardly mattered now.

Then the ground changed.

It happened between one step and the next—the way everything happened in Vell, where borders were not lines but moods. The salt gave way to dark loam, soft as cake crumb, and Sef felt it in his teeth before he saw it: a hum, low and patient, like an oven holding its heat through the night.

"Stop," Marisol said, though they had all already stopped.

Ahead of them, out of the flat horizon, rose a wall of green so deep it looked black against the pale sky. A hedge—but no hedge grown by any gardener's hand. It climbed forty feet without a single gap, its branches woven in patterns too deliberate to be chance and too strange to be design: spirals that turned inward on themselves, arches that seemed to lean toward whoever looked at them longest. And threaded through the green, pale as knucklebones, ran shapes Sef's eyes refused at first to understand.

Bones. Long bones, curved bones, ribs of enormous beasts—or of things that had never been beasts at all—grown into the living wood like the trellis had been planted in a graveyard and never told the difference.

"The neighbors," Hobb said quietly. He had not spoken much since his confession, and when he did, his voice came out sanded down, careful. "The six nations Ilvane ate. This is what's left of them. She took their names, their maps, their memories—and whatever she couldn't digest, it ended up here. Growing."

Sef touched the ledger in his coat pocket, its leather gone soft as ear from a hundred years of waiting. He was the archivist. Cataloguing the dead was supposed to be his trade. But he had never once imagined the dead could be *architectural*.

"It's beautiful," Bramble said, and then, catching herself, "I mean—it's horrible. It's both. Can it be both?"

"It can be both," Paloma said. "Most true things are." They stood at the front of the little company, as they always somehow did, small and unremarkable in their food-taster's grey, looking up at the hedge with an expression Sef couldn't read. "Let's find the door."

* * *

There was no door. Not at first.

They walked the outer wall for hours, Bramble running her thief's fingers along every branch, searching for seams, hinges, anything. What she found instead were graves.

The first one was almost invisible—a flat stone set into the hedge's roots, moss-eaten, with letters worn to whispers. Sef knelt and brushed the moss away with his sleeve, the way he'd brushed dust from ten thousand manuscripts, and felt his throat close.

It wasn't Vellish script. It wasn't imperial script. It was something older, angular, beautiful—and beneath it, in Vellish, someone had carved a translation, the way you'd leave a note for whoever came after:

*I brought my grandmother's recipe for mourning bread. The gate asked for her name. I gave it. The gate kept the bread.*

"Someone tried," Marisol said behind him. Her voice had lost its usual blade-edge. "A hundred years ago. Someone got this far and—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. They all understood the grammar of graves.

There were more. Eleven more, over the course of the afternoon, each stone set into the roots like teeth in a jaw. Each one carried the same shape of story: a traveler, a toll, a failure. One had come with gold. One had come with blood—one of the stones was stained dark still, and Sef chose not to examine why. One had come, heartbreakingly, with a child's drawing, pressed flat under glass, of a house with a crooked chimney. *The gate asked for home,* that carving read. *I offered the picture. It was not enough. Nothing less than the thing itself is enough.*

Sef catalogued them. It was the only thing he knew how to do with dread—write it down, give it a number, put it in order. Grave one: the mourner. Grave two: the merchant. Grave three: he stopped writing numbers after five because his hand was shaking too badly.

"They all failed," Hobb said, standing over the last stone. "Every one. Because the gate doesn't want what you carry. It wants what you *are*."

"Cheerful," said Marisol.

"I was here before," Hobb said. "With her. We never found the gate itself. Only these." He laid one broad, scarred hand flat on the stone, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person. "I used to think that meant we were spared. Now I think it means we weren't worthy of failing properly."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

* * *

Dusk came strangely under the hedge—the light didn't fade so much as get *absorbed*, pulled into the green until the whole wall glowed faintly from within, like a lamp seen through leaves. And it was then, with the last light dying, that Sef saw the door.

He would swear afterward that it hadn't been there a moment before. That was the nature of the place, he decided: the Crooked Garden showed itself only to eyes that had given up looking. He had turned away from the wall, defeated, ledger open on his knee, trying to transcribe the mourner's inscription by feel—and when he turned back, there it was.

An archway, perhaps eight feet tall, framed in bone-white wood that might once have been rib or might once have been rafter. Beyond it, darkness—not the darkness of night but the darkness of *elsewhere*, a dark with depth and weather in it, faintly moving. The hedge grew right up to the frame on both sides and then simply stopped, obediently, as if the door had been there first and the garden had grown around it out of respect.

And across the archway, written in that same angular script with a Vellish translation carved beneath, ran a line of text:

*Nine courses serve the king of nothing. Name them, and enter.*

"A riddle," Bramble groaned. "Of course it's a riddle. Why would an eldritch door made of dead nations just *open*?"

"Not a riddle of words," Sef said slowly. His heart had begun to beat in a rhythm he recognized from the archives—the rhythm of a man on the edge of understanding something old. "Read it again. *Courses.* Not questions. Courses."

Paloma stepped up beside him. In the hedge-light their face looked younger and older at once. "A menu," they said. "It's a menu."

"A menu," Marisol repeated flatly.

"The ledger." Sef pulled it out with hands that were nearly steady. "The cook's ledger. I thought the recipes were nonsense—ingredients that don't exist anymore, dishes with no names, measurements in things like *griefs* and *winters*. But look." He turned to the final page, the one he'd skipped a hundred times because it wasn't a recipe at all. It was a list. Nine items, each crossed out except the first, written in that same angular hand:

1. *What hunger was before it learned shame.*
2. —
3. —

The rest were illegible, dissolved into the paper like salt into water. But the first one sat there, clear as a bell, and Sef watched Paloma read it and saw something move behind their eyes—something that might have been recognition, or might have been fear, and with Paloma it had always been hard to tell the difference.

"That's not a dish," Paloma said softly. "That's a memory. Or a feeling. The nine courses aren't food. They're—" They stopped. Started again. "Tomorrow. Chapter fourteen of whatever this is. We eat our worst memories to open a door. I'd bet the honey cake I can't taste anymore."

"You can't know that," Sef said.

"I'm a taster, Sef. I know flavors before I know facts." Paloma reached out and touched the archway, lightly, two fingers against the bone-white wood. The hedge shivered along its entire forty-foot height, a ripple running through the green like wind through wheat, and somewhere deep inside the maze, something exhaled.

The door did not open. But it had noticed them. Sef was certain of it the way he was certain of catalogues and dates: the Crooked Garden knew they were there, and it was waiting to see what they would bring to the table.

Behind them, far off across the salt flats, the sky flickered—a light that wasn't lightning, a color that didn't belong to any fire. Marisol turned toward it, and her lie-taster's tongue must have caught something on the wind, because her face went grey.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Hobb didn't turn around. "Less than we need," he said. "More than none. With her, that's always the arithmetic."

Sef looked back at the archway—at the graves at its feet, at the riddle of courses above it, at the darkness beyond that was not death but *door*—and he thought about the mourner who had brought bread and lost a grandmother's name; about the child's drawing of a crooked chimney that had not been enough; about Hobb, who had walked away unworthy of even failing.

Nine courses. Nine people had died trying, and the gate had eaten their offerings and kept its door shut. Whatever the gate wanted, it wanted it whole, and honest, and served hot.

"Then we'd better learn to cook," Paloma said, and turned back toward the camp, already pulling the ledger from Sef's hands, already reading, already planning—as if the sky hadn't flickered, as if the empire weren't coming, as if the price of the door weren't written plain in eleven languages on eleven stones.

Sef stayed a moment longer. He copied the riddle into his own notebook, in his own careful hand, and beneath it he wrote the date, because that was his job: to write down what happened, so that someone, someday, would know.

Then the hedge-light dimmed, the door went dark, and somewhere behind them in the flats, the flickering sky began, very quietly, to spread.
