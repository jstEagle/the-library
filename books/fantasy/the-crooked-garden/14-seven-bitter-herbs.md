## 14. Seven Bitter Herbs

**Paloma**

The gate had no lock, no handle, no seam. It was only a gap in the hedge where the vines knotted themselves into a shape that might have been a face, if faces grew from bone.

The riddle had been waiting on a stone tablet at the maze's entrance, and Sef had translated it twice to be sure he wasn't inventing it, because the alternative was unbearable:

*Seven mouths, seven sorrows. Eat what you most regret. The gate opens for the honest tongue.*

"Seven," Bramble said flatly. "There are six of us."

The Dowager Queen stepped out of the shadow of the hedge. She had followed them the whole way from the salt fields without anyone noticing—old woman, plain gray coat, walking like someone who had done this walk before and failed it.

"Seven," she agreed.

* * *

**Hobb**

They built the kitchen in the hollow before the gate because the tablet demanded it: *the meal must be cooked where it will be eaten.* The ledger's last page gave them nothing useful except a list of vessels—a black pot, a spoon of horn, bowls that had never held food—and an instruction in a dead dialect that Sef read aloud with his voice shaking: *season with memory. The pot knows how.*

The pot knew how.

When Hobb set it over the cold hearthstones, it warmed by itself, gently, the way a hand warms when you hold it too long. And when he stood over it and thought about what he must think about, the smell rose up and found him across forty years.

Mutton broth. Cardamom. The imperial kitchens at Vaskarren, where he had been young and hungry and so afraid of dying that he had made dying everyone else's problem.

He didn't remember choosing to speak. The words came up like broth through a cracked pot.

"There was a country called Sennet," he said. "Little place. Terraced farms, bells that rang wrong on purpose because they liked the sound. I tasted for the Censor's table there—Ilvane, though she had another name then, or none. She was kind to me. That's the part nobody believes. She asked me questions like she cared about the answers. Where did your mother learn to cook. What did you want to be before this."

His hands were steady. He noticed that and hated it. Forty years of immunity, and the one poison that had ever gotten through was sitting in his own chest.

"She asked me if I knew where the people of Sennet would go, if their door opened. And I told her. Because she'd promised me immunity—real immunity, not this slow suicide we call a trade. A life without counting every mouthful. And I wanted it. Gods help me, I *wanted* it more than I'd ever wanted anything, and I told her about the terraces, the bell tower, the shrine above the eastern valley where the door stood."

Marisol made a small sound and stopped herself.

"They came through the shrine at dawn," Hobb said. "I watched from her tent. They looked so ordinary. Farmers with their animals. A bride in yellow. And she ate their country while they were still crossing the threshold, and everything they were carrying turned to ash in their hands, and then *they*—" His jaw worked. "Then they were ash too. And she gave me my immunity, and I've spent forty years tasting other people's plates so I'd never have to taste what I did."

He ladled the broth into six bowls and one horn cup, and his hand did not shake even once.

"I'll go first," he said. "It's mine anyway. Been carrying it long enough."

He drank.

For a moment nothing happened, and Paloma thought—foolishly, hopefully—that it had failed. Then Hobb sat down very suddenly on the hearthstone, bowl still in his hands, and his eyes went somewhere else entirely. Paloma saw him see it: the bride in yellow, the ash, the bell ringing wrong on purpose. He wept the way old men weep, silently, as if out of practice, and when it passed he looked around at all of them and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"It doesn't take it away. Understand that first. Whatever this does—it doesn't forgive you. It just makes you say it out loud."

"That's enough," Sef said quietly. "That's what it wants."

* * *

**Marisol**

Marisol went second because she refused to let Hobb's courage stand alone, and also because standing in line felt like being back in the orphanage at Corvel, waiting to be fed, and she would die before she waited in a line again.

Her dish came up smelling of rain and river mud, and her stomach dropped before she'd taken a single bite.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, absolutely not. Ask me anything else. Ask me about the man I poisoned in Tallow Street, ask me about—"

"You can't trade," Sef said. He sounded genuinely sorry about it.

She ate it standing up, defiant, like a toast.

The river took her instantly. Not the memory of the river—the river itself, cold and brown and closing over her head. She was nine years old again, in the flood year, in the water with her little brother's collar twisted in her fist, and the current was pulling him and pulling her and she could feel her grip going, feel the exact moment her fingers decided they valued her own breath more than his—

She surfaced on her knees in front of the fire, soaked in sweat, gasping like she'd actually been drowning, and Bramble had her shoulders and was saying her name in the firm cook-voice she used for pan fires.

"He drowned," Marisol said. "You all know that story. I tell it as a joke—the flood took him, bad luck, poor kid. But it wasn't the flood. It was my fingers. I let go. I *felt* myself let go." She laughed, high and awful. "Twenty years of jokes and I let go."

Bramble held her until she could breathe like a land creature again. When Marisol finally pulled back, her eyes were red but level.

"Well," she rasped. "Gate should open wide for me. Nobody's got worse than that."

Nobody argued. Nobody wanted to test it.

* * *

**Sef**

Sef's worst memory surprised everyone, himself most of all. He had expected the prophecy room—he'd expected shame about tonight, about bringing the book to Paloma against every rule he'd sworn to keep. Instead the pot breathed out chalk dust and lamp oil and the particular silence of a schoolroom in the lower quarter, and he understood with horror exactly which day the pot intended to serve him.

He was twelve. There had been a girl named Ovi who couldn't read, who'd been moved three times down the class ranks, and the tutor had asked for a volunteer to read the harvest decree aloud, and Sef had known—*known*, the way children know things they pretend not to—that if he read it well, loudly, beautifully, the tutor would finally see him. So he read it. He read it gorgeously. And afterward Ovi had looked at him with something worse than hatred, with recognition, with the understanding that he had used her illiteracy as a stair, and she'd left the school that winter and he'd heard years later she'd gone into service in a house where—

The memory stopped there. The pot was merciful in only one direction: it served the whole dish but never the course after.

He came back to himself with chalk dust on his tongue and tears he hadn't authorized, and Paloma squeezed his wrist and said nothing, which was the correct thing, and he loved them a little more for it.

"One thing," he managed. "One thing I learned. Shame isn't loud. It's quiet. It reads beautifully."

* * *

**Bramble**

Bramble tried to cheat.

Everyone knew she would; she announced it. "I'm a thief. Worst memories are inventory, and I don't surrender inventory." She proposed alternatives. She proposed lying to the pot. She stood over it and recited a false confession about stealing a silver candlestick in 739, delivered with such conviction that Sef almost believed her.

The pot did not steam.

So she stood there in the firelight with five people watching, and her bravado ran out the way water runs out of a cracked jug, slowly and then all at once, and she said, "Fine. Fine. But if any of you ever repeat this I'll cut your apron strings while you sleep."

Her dish smelled of bread. Fresh bread, good bread, the kind she baked now, and that was the cruelty of it.

"I had a family," she said. "Before the guilds, before the streets. A mother who baked. I stole from her—not money, not jewels. I stole her recipes. Wrote them out word for word, sold them to a rival bakery for coin to buy my way into the thieves' guild, because I was fourteen and I thought the guild was a family and the real family was boring." Her voice went flat and hard, a blade laid on a stone. "She never changed the recipes. Never wrote new ones. Kept making the same loaves for thirty years like she was leaving the door open for me. And I never went back, because going back meant being the person who'd sold her, and staying away meant I got to keep pretending I'd been someone else entirely."

She ate the bread-memory in four furious bites and cried without changing expression at all, tears just falling off her jaw like the bread had salted her, and when it was done she wiped her face with her sleeve and said, "Right. Who's next? Let's get this misery over with before I remember I'm armed."

* * *

**The Dowager Queen**

The old woman's sorrow was the strangest of all, because it was not sharp. It came up from the pot as the smell of clean linen and cold tea, and she drank it with the composure of a woman taking prescribed medicine.

"My worst memory is a Tuesday," she said. "Nothing happened on it. My husband was alive, the valley was whole, my son was coming to dinner. Nothing happened, and I was bored. I remember sitting at the window wishing for something to *happen*." Her eyes went distant. "Two weeks later the empire's mages came to the border. Everything after that—the erasure, the maps, the century of being nothing—I have spent ninety years believing it was my fault. That the universe heard a bored queen and obliged."

She set down her cup. "I know it's irrational. Knowing has never once helped."

* * *

**Paloma**

Six bowls empty. Six people kneeling or swaying or wiping their faces in the hedge-shadow. The gate had not moved.

"Seven mouths," Sef whispered. "We're six."

"No," the Dowager Queen said, and looked at Paloma. "We were always seven. You've known since the salt fields that this was yours to do."

Paloma approached the pot the way they'd once approached a plate laced with nightshade: professionally, respectfully, with the private wager that they would survive it. They leaned over the black iron and thought about what they most regretted, casting around among the usual candidates—every person whose food they'd failed to catch, every risk they hadn't taken, the years spent content inside walls—and waited for the smell.

What rose instead was honey cake. Their own oath-day feast, the sweetness of the kitchens, home in edible form—and underneath it, faint as a fingerprint under varnish, something else. Salt. Wind. A spice they had no name for, because no name for it existed anywhere in Vell.

"That's not right," Paloma said. "That's not my worst memory. That's my *best*—"

"Eat," the Dowager Queen said softly. "It knows better than you do."

Paloma ate.

And the worst memory arrived, and it was not a thing they had done. It was a thing they had *been*, without ever knowing: a darkness before Vell, a cold border crossed in the bottom of a market cart, a woman's heartbeat through blankets that smelled of that nameless spice, a wail smothered by necessity—and then the valley swallowing them whole, kind and warm and magicless, a pocket of safety wrapped around a child who had never belonged to it. The worst memory was the truth itself: *you were born outside. Every day of your life in Vell was a day spent inside a house that was never yours.* The kitchens, the oath, the honey cake, Sef's laugh—all of it lovely, all of it borrowed, all of it resting on a lie of silence that two people had kept for twenty-five years.

Paloma came back to the fire on their hands and knees, shaking, and the first thing they saw was the Dowager Queen's face—and the guilt in it was total, ancient, unmistakable.

"You knew," Paloma said. "You've always known."

The gate, behind them, shivered. Somewhere in its knotwork of bone and vine, a single leaf uncurled—an inch of opening, a crack of starlight through green.

But the Dowager Queen did not look at the gate. She looked only at Paloma, and her mouth opened, and whatever she had carried for twenty-five years began, at last, to come out.
