## 15. Born Beyond the Border

The Dowager Queen did not send for me. She came to me.

I knew it was her by the way the kitchen went quiet—not the hush of respect but the hush of a held breath, every scullery boy and spice-grinder suddenly finding urgent business elsewhere. She moved through my domain like weather. Like frost coming down a valley.

She was not dressed as a queen. That was the first wrong thing. No mourning silks, no crown of blackened silver, just a plain gray traveling coat buttoned to the throat and boots that had seen actual mud. In ninety years I had never seen the Dowager's boots.

"Walk with me," she said.

"Your Majesty, I have a consignment of—"

"It can wait." Her voice was soft in the way that poison is soft, that deep water is soft. "Or it cannot. Either way, walk with me."

I walked. It is strange, the things you notice when your world has tilted on its axis. I noticed that she smelled of dried lavender and old paper, like Sef's archive. I noticed that she walked with a slight hitch on the left, an old injury no one had ever thought to mention in any history I'd read. I noticed that she took us not toward the palace but away from it, down through the terraced gardens, past the tanneries, out along the low wall where the valley's edge shimmered its particular nothing—the border that was not a wall, only a place where Vell stopped being worth noticing.

The salt fields lay beyond it, pale as a scoured plate.

"You've read the prophecy book," she said. Not a question. Of course she knew; she was the Dowager Queen, and knowing things was the whole of her profession.

"Sef showed me. A door made of vines. My face beside it."

"And you've been wondering why." She stopped at the wall and set both hands on the stone, looking out at the salt. "You've been wondering why a hundred-year-old book would draw the face of a food-taster. You are clever, Paloma Brightwater. Cleverer than your position requires. So let us not pretend you haven't already guessed something terrible."

I hadn't. Or I had, in the way you half-dream a thing and wake refusing to name it. "Your Majesty—"

"Ilva," she said.

The word landed strangely. Not a name I knew. And yet some animal part of me flinched at it, the way you flinch from a dish that smells almost right and is therefore deeply wrong.

"That was the name of the nation Ilvane ate first," the Dowager said. "Before she learned to eat countries, she ate one small enough to swallow whole. Ilva. It sat where the salt fields sit now—or rather, the salt fields sit where Ilva used to be. When a nation is eaten, the empire doesn't take its land. The land *goes*. It becomes absence. Salt. The flavor of nothing." She turned to look at me, and her eyes were wet, which frightened me more than anything else that morning. "You were born there."

* * *

I want to tell you I argued. I want to tell you I laughed, or demanded proof, or did any of the things a person does when their entire self is handed back to them in pieces they don't recognize. But I had spent twenty years swallowing poisons in small doses, and I suppose somewhere in all that practice I had learned the trick of it: don't fight the toxin. Let it in. Study it as it works.

So I stood very still and let it work.

"My parents," I said finally. My voice sounded like someone else's. "In the orphan registry, my parents are listed as Brightwater. Fishers, from the lake district."

"The registry was amended. By me." She said it without flinching. "There were four infants carried across the border in the last week before Ilva finished dying. Three of them were too young to remember, and one of them—" her hand trembled against the stone, "—one of them did not survive the crossing. The absence is hard on the very young. It is like a fever. Most burn through it. Some do not."

"Four infants," I repeated. "And I'm supposed to believe I'm one of them because a queen says so."

"No," she said. "You're meant to believe it because of this."

She drew something from inside her coat. Small, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string gone brittle as straw. She put it in my palm and closed my fingers over it, and her hands were cold.

I unwrapped it there on the wall with the salt wind pulling at the cloth. Inside was a wooden charm on a frayed cord—a crude little thing, whittled by someone impatient, a bird with too many wings. And burned into the back of it, in a script I could not read and yet somehow, horribly, could almost sound out:

*Paloma.*

My name. Not Brightwater. Just the one word, the private one, the one even Sef didn't use because I'd never told anyone I had another. Except I hadn't known I had another. Had I?

"You were wearing it when you crossed," the Dowager said. "Whoever carried you tied it around your neck and wrote your name into it so that somewhere, in some country that no longer existed, you would still be somebody. I kept it. I should have given it to you years ago. I told myself you were better off unburdened." Her jaw tightened. "That was cowardice dressed as kindness, and I have worn that costume so long I forget sometimes that it isn't skin."

"Why?" The question came out cracked. "Why lie? Why hide it?"

"Because of what you are." She faced the salt fields fully now, and I saw how old she looked with her back straight—older than ninety years, older than the valley. "Vell survives because nothing here draws attention. No magic. No borders on maps. No anomalies. But you, Paloma—you are an anomaly. A child of an eaten nation living inside a hidden one. The empire's magic cannot see Vell, but it can see *you*, faintly, the way you can see a candle through a shuttered window. For twenty-two years we have kept you indoors, metaphorically speaking. Kept you in kitchens, in cellars, in the small safe rooms of a small safe life. And now a prophecy book has drawn your face at a door, and a mage-censor is crossing our salt, and I am out of shutters."

The charm sat in my hand, light as a dead moth. Too many wings. I thought of the bird trying to fly in six directions at once and going nowhere, and something in my chest folded up small.

"So the prophecy names me," I said slowly, "because I'm not really from Vell."

"The Garden opens for those who belong nowhere," she said. "That is its cruelty and its mercy. Everyone born inside a nation carries that nation's weight—the Garden will not hold them; they are already held. But a child of nowhere, a child whose country was eaten out from under them before they could stand—" her voice caught, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, visibly, expensively, "—such a person is a key cut by the lock itself. Ilvane made you, though she never knew it. Every nation she devours, she makes another key. Somewhere, in whatever dark pantry she keeps herself, there are others like you. Survivors. Keys. And she hunts them, I expect, without ever understanding why her meals keep leaving doors unlocked behind them."

* * *

We walked back slowly. Neither of us spoke for most of it. The terraces rose around us green and ordinary, and I looked at each familiar thing the way you look at a face across a deathbed: the persimmon tree with the crooked trunk that we'd climbed as kitchen children; the smokehouse where Hobb taught me to cure eel; the bell tower that rang the hours in a pattern no other country used because no other country existed to compare it to.

Mine. None of it mine.

"Do you remember them?" I asked at last. "Ilva. Before."

"A little." She said it carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "I was young when it happened. We traded with them—salt fish for their orchard fruit, cloth for their honey. Their honey was famous. Dark, almost bitter, with a finish like—" She stopped.

"Like ours," I said.

"Like ours," she agreed quietly. "Because our bees are descended from theirs. Half of what Vell is, is Ilva continued by accident. Recipes, songs, surnames. We are what survived of them without meaning to be. And you—" she glanced at me sideways, "—you are what survived of them *on purpose*. Someone ran toward the border with you in their arms while the sky was coming apart. Someone chose your life over their own memory. Do you understand what I'm telling you? You are not an accident, Paloma. You are the most deliberate thing in this valley."

I stopped walking. I don't know why exactly, except that my legs decided for me.

"Someone chose," I said. "And I'll never know who."

"No," she said. "Grief without a grave. I know the shape of it. I'm sorry. It's the worst shape there is."

We stood there among the persimmons, two people pretending to be composed, and then—this is the part I keep turning over, the part I didn't expect—she reached out and took my hand. The Dowager Queen of Vell, who had not touched a servant in living memory, held my hand in both of hers like I was something breakable and precious and hers.

"When you open that door," she said, "and you will—I need you to know that Vell does not follow you because you lead. Vell follows you because you are the only one of us who belongs on both sides of it. Born outside, raised within. You are the hinge, child. Doors swing on hinges."

* * *

Sef found me at dusk in the tasting chamber, where I had gone because it was the room where I best understood myself. Rows of vials. Ledgers of doses. Twenty years of controlled ruin written in my own neat hand. If I was a key cut by some monster's appetite, at least I was also this: a person who knew exactly how much poison a body could carry and still stand.

He didn't ask what the Dowager wanted. He'd heard, of course—the palace was a sieve. Instead he set the prophecy book on the table between us, open to the page with the vine-wrapped door, and he looked at the sketch of my face for a long time.

"You knew," he said quietly. "Some part of you always knew you didn't fit."

"Everyone knows I don't fit. I taste lies for a living, Sef. I hear the seams in everything."

"Not like this." He turned the page, and there it was again—the marginalia, the looping annotations in that century-old hand. "Look at the note under your portrait. I couldn't parse it before. Now I can." He translated aloud, his voice careful, archivist-careful, each word placed like a stone in a ford:

*"The gate knows its own. Feed it seven bitter herbs, and it will open for the one who was never counted."*

Never counted. Not of any census. Not of any nation. A face in a book a hundred years old, waiting for a person who wouldn't be born for seventy-eight more of them, because the book wasn't predicting me. It was describing a kind of person. And Ilvane, eating country after country, had been manufacturing that kind of person for a century without knowing she was forging keys.

"She's coming," I said. "Through the salt fields. Marisol's wound won't close, Hobb hasn't slept since his confession, and Bramble says the hunters circle like gulls over a kill. We have days. Maybe less."

"Then we move tonight." Sef closed the book and rested his hand flat on the cover, and when he looked up at me there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the flirt, not the archivist, something rawer. "Paloma. Whatever you learned today—whatever she told you—it doesn't change—"

"It changes everything," I said. "It's the only thing that's ever changed anything. I'm not Vellish, Sef. I'm the ghost of a country that doesn't exist, wearing a stolen name, and the only reason my friends aren't dead in a salt field right now is that I happen to be shaped like a door."

"That's not—"

"It *is*." I heard my own voice go sharp and hated it. "But here's what else it is." I picked up the wooden bird, the too-many-winged thing, and looped its cord over my neck where it had apparently belonged for twenty-two years. "It means Ilvane didn't get all of Ilva. It means somewhere in her eaten silence there's a hole the exact shape of me, and I am going to walk through that garden gate and make sure she chokes on the rest of what she stole."

Sef stared at me. Then, slowly, he smiled—that crooked, dangerous smile I'd been flirting across ledgers at for years—and said, "There she is."

"There *who* is?"

"Exactly," he said, and picked up the book. "Pack light. We leave within the hour. Bramble's already stealing provisions, which is how you know she's optimistic."

Outside the tasting chamber's narrow window, the first stars were coming up over the terraces, and beyond them, far off, the salt fields glowed faintly under the moon like the memory of a sea. Somewhere out there, a woman who ate nations was tasting the wind.

Let her come, I thought, with the bird-charm cold against my sternum. Let her come and meet the last course of Ilva.

I had spent my whole life learning to survive poison. It was time to find out what I was poisonous *to*.
