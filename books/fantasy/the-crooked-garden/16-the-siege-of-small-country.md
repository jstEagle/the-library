## 16. The Siege of Small Country

### Paloma

The first sign was the bread.

Paloma had risen before dawn to bake in the waystation kitchen—their kitchen now, the one they'd coaxed back to life with memory and salt and a stove that lit without fire—and when they pulled the loaves from the oven, the crusts were wrong. Not burnt. Not raw. *Unfinished*, as though the wheat itself had forgotten how to be wheat halfway through becoming flour. The crumb inside was gray and tasted of nothing at all.

Nothing at all. In Vell, where every pear had a name and every grandmother's stew had a feud attached to it, nothing had ever tasted of nothing.

They carried a loaf up the hill to the Garden gate anyway, because Marisol demanded breakfast and Marisol shot first and apologized never. The hedge-maze rose against the paling sky, grown from the bones of six erased nations, its crooked green walls humming that low note Paloma had learned to hear in their teeth. The gate stood sealed among the hedges, waiting on a riddle of courses none of them had solved yet.

Sef was already there, the prophecy book open across his knees, his ink-stained fingers moving down a page that hadn't existed three days ago.

"Paloma." His voice came out flat. "You need to see this."

The new page didn't have a sketch this time. It had a list. Dates going backward like a countdown, each one struck through in ash-gray ink except the last:

*Tonight.*

And beneath it, in handwriting that looked disturbingly like Sef's own: *The valley ends the way water ends. Not all at once. From the edges in.*

Marisol stood at the maze's outer wall, her hand pressed flat against the hedge. "Do you feel that?" she said. "It's warm. It's been cold as a corpse since we got here."

Hobb leaned on his walking stick and said nothing, which from Hobb was a scream.

Then the horizon flickered.

Not darkened—*flickered*, like a candle guttering, like the world beyond the salt fields had been a painting all along and someone had bumped the frame. For half a heartbeat Paloma saw what was actually there: not hills, not the familiar fold of the Vellish mountains, but a vast gray plain under a vast gray sky, and something moving across it. Something with too many joints and a light at its center like a swallowed star.

The horizon snapped back. The mountains returned. But everyone at the gate had seen it, and no one spoke, because speaking would make it true.

"It's here," Bramble said quietly. She'd come up from the treeline without a sound, as she always did, her thief's eyes already counting exits. "She's here. I felt the border go last night. Like a tooth coming loose."

"The wards held for a hundred years," Hobb said.

"The wards were never tested by *her*," said Hobb, and then he closed his mouth so hard his teeth clicked, because he had tested them, once, for her, and they all knew it.

Paloma set the ruined loaf down on the grass. "Then we hold the gate."

"With what?" Marisol's laugh was sharp enough to cut. "We're tasters. We're cooks. One retired burglar and an archivist who faints around blood. Against the woman who ate six countries."

"We're people she can't digest," Paloma said. They heard their own voice come out steady and were briefly grateful to whatever part of them had learned to lie with its spine straight. "Ilvane eats what's forgotten. So we remember out loud. All night. Every story we've got. If Vell is going to unravel, it can unravel around a table full of people saying its name."

Sef looked up at them, and there was doubt in his face still—he hadn't forgiven the lie about the book, maybe never would—but beneath the doubt was something older and more stubborn. "I'll start with the founding," he said. "I know all nine hundred years of it."

"Good," said Paloma. "Because I think we're going to need every single one."

### Ilvane

Mage-Censor Ilvane stood at the wound she had made in the world and savored it.

The border of Vell had been the finest vintage she'd tasted in two centuries—a nation aged a thousand years in the dark cask of its own absence, untouched, unremembered, unspoiled. Other censors drank power the way soldiers drank: fast, hot, burning. Ilvane sipped. She had learned patience in the ruins of Kessarin, the first country she'd eaten, back when she'd still wept between courses.

Now she stepped through the breach, and magic poured into the valley behind her like water finding a hull.

She felt it spread. Felt the valley's long-held breath begin to fail. A place sustained by nothing but its own stubborn continuity could not survive contact with the empire's truth; the two realities could not occupy the same ground, and hers had eaten six nations' worth of ground already. The magic moved through Vell the way rot moves through fruit: gently, almost lovingly, from the outside in.

Behind her, her hunters fanned out—creatures she had made from the last undigested scraps of Kessarin, shapes like herons if herons had been designed by grief, their wings leaving streaks of forgetting across the sky. She did not need them for the valley. The valley was already dying. She needed them for the door, and for the people standing at it.

The prophecy child. The taster. She had met them in the orchard, offered them a seat at her table, watched them lie to her face with admirable fluency. She had let them go, because a meal is best taken when it has finished seasoning itself—and because there was something in the taster she could not yet name. Something that had touched her raw magic in the orchard and had not died. That interested her. Appetite was, above all things, curiosity.

She walked into Vell along a road that was trying very hard to keep existing.

Halfway to the first village, the road gave up. The stones beneath her feet went smooth and featureless, then soft, then simply *absent*, and she walked on air over a gap where a mile of highway had been, watching below her as a farmhouse forgot its own walls. The house didn't collapse. Collapse required a memory of having stood. It simply became less, and less, and then a smear of color, and then nothing, and somewhere inside it she felt a family wake in the dark and not know the names of their own children.

Ilvane paused, and—because she was civilized—gave the moment its due silence.

Then she raised one hand, and her hunters wheeled toward the Crooked Garden, and she followed at her leisure, the way one follows the smell of a feast nearly ready.

### Sef

By midmorning, the stories were failing.

Sef stood on the hill above the gate with the prophecy book under his arm and his voice worn ragged, telling the company the tale of Queen Ameline and the Nine Hundred Bakers—the founding myth every Vellish child learned before they learned their letters. He had told it a hundred times. He knew it the way he knew his own hands.

He got to the fourth baker and couldn't remember her name.

"It was—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "Brenna. No. Brenna was the sixth. The fourth was—" The name wasn't gone the way forgotten things are gone; it was gone the way a word goes when you stare at it too long, dissolved into shapes. Around him the hillside had begun to do the same. The wildflowers were still there, but their colors were draining toward gray, and the old boundary stone at the hill's foot now read only a smooth blank curve, its centuries of carved names wiped clean.

"Sef." Marisol caught his elbow. Her brashness had burned off entirely; underneath it she was pale and precise. "Your nose."

He touched his upper lip and his fingers came away red. Above the salt fields, the sky had developed a hairline crack of light, and through the crack poured a pressure he felt in his molars, in the roots of his hair, in the backs of his eyes. Magic. Actual magic, imperial-made, flooding across a border that had kept it out for a thousand years. Sef had spent his life among books that described such things. Nothing had described *this*: the sensation of being a page on which two incompatible texts were being written at once.

Down in the valley, Lowmarket was screaming.

They could hear it even from the hill—not the people, though the people were screaming too, but the market itself, the sound of a place losing its own definition. Sef had catalogued Lowmarket's archive: four hundred years of stall-deeds and spice-taxes and the famous Bell of Fair Measure. Now the bell rang with no one pulling it, and the sound came out wrong, arrhythmic, a bell forgetting what fairness sounded like.

"People are fading," Bramble reported. She'd been down among the rooftops and back faster than should have been possible. "Slow, at the edges of town. Old Ferro the cooper went translucent at dawn. His daughter's holding his hand and he keeps asking her who she is. The Dowager Queen's sent riders telling everyone to get to the palace cellars, but—" Bramble's jaw tightened. "Cellars won't hold. I've seen this before. Places eaten. You can't hide from it underground. It comes down like water."

"Then we don't hide," Paloma said. They had come down the hill with the ruined loaf still in their hands, absurdly, as if bread were a talisman. "We bring them here. To the gate. Everyone who can walk. If the Garden is a door out of Vell, then it's the one place in this valley that isn't Vell—it grew from the other nations, from *outside*. Maybe it holds when everything else lets go."

"And if it doesn't?" Marisol asked.

"Then we all stand together when it goes," Hobb said, "which is more than I earned and more than most get."

No one argued. There was no time to argue. The second hunter came over the mountains then, low and silent, its heron-grief wings spanning the whole sky, and where its shadow crossed the fields the fields turned to gray velvet, and Sef understood with terrible clarity that the shadow was not a shadow. It was the hunter's *appetite*, cast ahead of it, tasting.

"To the gate!" Paloma shouted, and the company ran.

### Marisol

Marisol had tasted lies her whole life—copper and rot at the back of the tongue—and so she knew, running for the gate with a hundred frightened villagers strung out behind her, that the words the hunters sang were lies, and that the lies were working.

The creatures didn't roar. They *sang*, a thin sweet note like a mother calling a child home, and the song slid into the ears of the fleeing people and told them: *there is no hurry. There is no hunt. There is no you.* Marisol watched a farmer slow to a walk, then a stop, then sit down in the middle of the road with a smile of perfect peace, and by the time she reached him he weighed nothing in her arms. Less than nothing. She was holding the shape a man leaves behind.

"Keep moving!" she bellowed, and her voice cracked on it. "Don't listen! Plug your ears! Sing over it—sing anything, sing *rude songs*, sing louder than it!"

And because Marisol Brightwater—no relation, no relation to anyone, that was rather the point of her—had never in her life failed to be the loudest thing in any given valley, she planted herself in the road and began to sing the filthiest tavern ballad she knew, at full volume, in a voice that could curdle cream at forty paces.

It worked. Gods help them, it worked. The villagers took it up—first out of terror, then out of something like defiance, five hundred frightened people bawling a song about the miller's wife and the tax collector until the hunters' sweet note drowned under human noise. Marisol laughed, wild and hopeless, and kept singing, and that was when the arrow took her.

Not an arrow. A quill—one of the hunters' feathers, hardened and hurled, trailing forgetting like a banner trails wind. It punched through her shoulder above the breastbone, and Marisol sat down hard in the road and looked at the wound and understood two things at once.

The first was that it didn't hurt, which was bad. Pain meant your body remembered itself. This wound was being *edited out*.

The second was that she could taste it. Copper, yes, blood, yes—but under it, the flavor of the feather's magic, and the flavor was *familiar*. She had tasted it before, years ago, in a wine served at a treaty dinner she'd survived while the ambassadors signing it had not. Imperial poison. She'd built her immunity to it dish by dish, same as Paloma, same as all of them, the whole poisoned-mouthed brotherhood of the royal table.

Her body knew this poison. Her body had spent ten years learning to refuse it.

"Get up," she told herself, through her teeth, and got up.

### Hobb

Hobb had not run in thirty years, and he ran now, and hated himself for how much of his breath was sobbing.

He had done this before. That was the thing no one said, the thing he had confessed at the fire with his shame laid out like a butcher's cut: he had stood at another nation's gate while the hunters sang, and he had opened his mouth and told Ilvane where the door was hidden, because she had promised him immunity, and because he had wanted to live more than he had wanted anything else in the world. Kessarin had died around him politely, in order, like guests leaving a banquet. He had tasted the last of its air and found it sweet, and that sweetness was the damnation he carried.

Now he stood at the Crooked Garden's gate with the survivors of Vell pouring past him—five hundred, seven hundred, more, faces he'd known at market, children whose honey cakes he'd sampled—and the hedge walls were *glowing*, drinking the chaos, holding. The Garden was not Vell. The Garden was grown from the bones of the eaten, and the eaten, it seemed, remembered how to shelter the hunted.

But the gate was shut. And the riddle of courses remained unsolved, and Ilvane was coming up the valley road at her leisure, unhurried, elegant, certain, and Hobb knew that walk. He had walked beside it. She always arrived exactly when the meal was ready, because she had never once in two hundred years needed to rush.

"Hobb." Paloma appeared at his side, blood on their sleeve that might have been Marisol's, ash in their hair. "The riddle. The ledger. You were her taster—you were *inside* her head. What does she want? What opens a door for someone like her?"

"Nothing opens a door for someone like her," Hobb said. "Doors open for the hungry and the grieving. She's only hungry." He gripped his stick until the wood groaned. "But the gate isn't for her, is it. It's for us. Seven bitter herbs, the ledger said. A dish brewed from your worst memory, one for each of you. You've fed it six." He looked at the gate, and then at Paloma, and his old heart turned over like a stone in a field. "It's waiting on yours, child. It's been waiting on yours since the day you arrived."

Above them, the sky cracked wider, and light like the memory of daylight poured through, and the first true voice of Mage-Censor Ilvane rolled across the hillside, warm as a hostess greeting guests:

"*Dinner is served.*"

Paloma turned toward the gate, and Hobb saw them square their shoulders the way they did before swallowing poison, and he thought: *there it is. There's the seventh course.*

The gate began, very softly, to hum.
