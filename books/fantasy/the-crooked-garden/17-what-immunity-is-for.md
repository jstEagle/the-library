## 17. What Immunity Is For

The valley was forgetting itself street by street, and Paloma Brightwater was hiding in a pantry.

Not hiding, they corrected themselves, pressing their back against a shelf of empty jars while the world outside shuddered like a struck bell. *Tactically retreating.* There was a difference. The difference was that hiding meant you were afraid for yourself, and Paloma was afraid for everyone else, which was somehow worse.

Through the pantry's cracked door, the kitchen of the waystation glowed with the wrong kind of light. The stove Bramble had lit without fire still burned—a soft gold flame that ate nothing, needed nothing—but around its edges the shadows had begun to move in ways shadows shouldn't. Magic was pouring through the breach at the valley's edge like water into a holed hull, and where it pooled, things came loose. The mortar between stones forgot it was mortar. A copper pot on the shelf beside Paloma's head had gone translucent at the rim, as if it were deciding whether it had ever been real.

Paloma could feel it too. That was the horror of it. For twenty-six years, Vell had been a place without magic, and Paloma had been a person without any sensitivity to it—dull as a spoon, blessedly, mercifully dull. Now the flood was everywhere, soaking through everything, and even a spoon could feel itself drowning.

It felt like swallowing.

That was the thought that arrived, unbidden, absurd, in the dark of the pantry: *it feels like swallowing.*

* * *

They had swallowed things most people couldn't imagine. That was the trade. The taster's oath wasn't poetry—it was a schedule. Arsenic on the first of every month, in honey, because the Dowager Queen believed sweetness made the medicine go down easier. Foxglove in spring, when the gardens bloomed and ambitious cooks got ideas. Antimony, hemlock, lead-white sugar, the gray powder scraped from rat-bait cakes that Marisol called "the breakfast of champions" right up until the morning she vomited blood and stopped joking about it.

Every dose taught the body a new language. Every dose said: *this thing that kills is also a thing you can learn.* Not conquer—tasters didn't conquer anything; that was the whole point of them. They *accommodated*. They made room inside themselves for the unbearable, dose by dose, year by year, until the unbearable became furniture.

Paloma had hated it. Every single day of it. The cramping nights, the numb fingertips, the way food—the one great joy of their life—had been permanently married to death. Other children dreamed of knighthood or wealth. Paloma had dreamed of eating a meal they hadn't tested first, one time, just once, without counting heartbeats afterward.

And now, crouched in a pantry while an empire's magic dissolved the walls of reality, they pressed two fingers to the air and found it tasted familiar.

That was the thing no one else could do. Everyone else in the company flinched from the flood like it was fire. Sef's hands shook so badly he'd dropped the prophecy book twice. Hobb had gone gray and silent, and Hobb did not go silent. Even Bramble, who feared nothing with legs, kept her back to the wall and her eyes moving.

But when the raw magic drifted through the pantry crack and touched Paloma's skin, they catalogued it the way they catalogued everything: bitter top note, metallic finish, a slow burn like arsenic in honey. *Foxglove,* they thought wildly. *It tastes like foxglove and something older underneath.*

Raw magic was poison. Of course it was. It was power without form, appetite without mouth—and Ilvane was the empire's distillation of it, centuries of eaten nations rendered down to a woman-shaped concentrate. To touch her magic unprotected was to die, the same way drinking a taster's tray died. Everyone knew this. It was why no one fought mages hand to hand. It was why the company's plan, such as it was, had consisted entirely of running.

Everyone knew this except, apparently, Paloma's body.

* * *

They found Sef in the great room, kneeling over the prophecy book with a stub of candle, though the stove-light made the candle pointless. He looked up when Paloma came out of the pantry, and his face did the thing it always did—that complicated arithmetic of relief and worry and something warmer he never named, because archivists didn't name things, they filed them.

"You're supposed to be resting," he said.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." Paloma sat down across from him, close enough that their knees touched. "Sef. I need you to tell me exactly what happened when I grabbed the hunter's net."

He flinched. In the battle at the salt fields' edge—or what used to be the salt fields, before the unraveling turned them to gray static—one of Ilvane's hunters had thrown a net woven from pure spellwork, the kind of thing that unmade whatever it caught. Paloma had torn it off Marisol with their bare hands. Everyone had seen it. No one had said anything, because there had been no time, and then there had been no breath, and now there was only this strange lit kitchen at the end of the map with the world dissolving outside.

"It should have taken your hands off at the wrist," Sef said quietly. "I've read about those nets. In the Censorate records I stole, before—" He stopped. Started again. "They don't burn out. They don't let go. You held it and it *let go of you*, Paloma. Nets don't do that."

"I know." Paloma flexed their fingers in the stove-light. Faint silver tracery still marked the palms, like scars from a burn that never broke the skin. It didn't hurt. It hadn't hurt at all. "It tasted like foxglove."

"...What?"

"The magic. When it touched me." Paloma heard their own voice go distant, the way it did when they described flavors to the Dowager Queen. Professional. Detached. "Bitter up front. Metallic finish. Slow burn, delayed onset—that's arsenic, the delayed part, arsenic always lies to you for an hour before it tells the truth. And under all of it, something old and sweet, like honey that's started to turn." They looked up. "Sef. Twenty-six years of poison. Do you understand what that means?"

He was pale. He was always pale, but this was different—this was the paleness of a man watching a page rewrite itself. "Your body has been learning poisons since you were six years old."

"Not learning. *Accommodating.* Making room." Paloma stood, and the strange lightness in their chest wasn't fear anymore, or not only fear. It was the feeling of a lock finding its key after years of rattling. "Ilvane's magic isn't different from her poisons. It's the same thing, bigger. She eats nations the way arsenic eats a body—cell by cell, memory by memory, until there's nothing left to argue. Everyone treats her like weather. Like a force of nature you can only survive by being elsewhere." They laughed, and it came out ragged. "But I have spent my whole life being *elsewhere*. Inside my own body. Making space for the unbearable. Dose by dose. Year by year."

"Paloma." Sef rose slowly. "Say what you're saying."

"I can touch her." The words came out steady, which surprised them, because nothing had ever felt less steady. "Her raw magic. The nets, the spells, maybe—if I'm right—her herself. It'll taste like dying. It might be dying. But it won't be instant, and it won't be certain, and for anyone else in this valley it would be both." They spread their scarred-silver hands. "Twenty-six years I hated this. Every swallow. Every night curled around a cramping gut, hating the oath, hating the kitchens, hating that my whole life was built out of small controlled risks that never once felt like courage. And it turns out—" Their voice cracked, finally, on the last of it. "It turns out it was preparation. All of it. Every dish. I wasn't being poisoned, Sef. I was being *built*."

* * *

Hobb was the one who confirmed it, because Hobb had been on the other side. He sat by the impossible stove with his ruined hands wrapped in cloth—he'd burned them pulling Bramble out of a collapsing doorway—and listened to Paloma explain it twice, his old eyes getting wetter each time.

"Ilvy—" He stopped, corrected himself with visible pain. "*The Mage-Censor* keeps her power raw. Unrefined. Most imperial mages refine it, bottle it, make it safe to carry. She never has. She says bottling it wastes the flavor." His mouth twisted. "She talks about it like wine. Like vintages. I used to taste her spells for her, back when I was hers. Little sips, to check they weren't sabotaged. One sip cost me these." He unwound the cloth from one hand: skin smooth and shiny as wax, nerveless, decades dead. "One sip a day for three years, and look at me. You're telling me you tore apart one of her nets bare-handed and your worst complaint is that it tasted like a flower."

"It was a very rude flower."

Hobb laughed, and then he wept, briefly and without drama, the way old men do—as if crying were a chore long overdue. "Child," he said, when he could speak, "do you know how many people she's fed on? Six nations. Millions of mouths, millions of memories, and she drank them down neat. And the whole time, somewhere in a little valley she couldn't find, there was one person being marinated against her. One person her poison couldn't take." He gripped Paloma's wrist with his dead hand, which was somehow more moving than a living grip. "If you believe in anything, believe that wasn't an accident."

Paloma wanted to say they didn't believe in anything. But standing in a kitchen at the end of the map, with a prophecy book open on the table to a sketch of their own face beside a door made of vines, belief was getting harder to avoid.

* * *

Marisol found them last. She came down the waystation stairs leaning on Bramble, the wound in her side bound in strips of ledger-paper soaked in the impossible recipe's broth—the only thing that slowed the unraveling where the hunters had cut her. Outside time, Sef said, wounds didn't close; they just waited. Marisol's wound was waiting. She was furious about it, in the loud, bright way that was her armor.

"So the plan," she said, lowering herself onto a bench with a hiss, "the actual plan, the one we're going with, is that our food-taster walks up to the most powerful mage in the empire and *touches her*."

"That's roughly the shape of it."

"Roughly." She barked a laugh that cost her something. "You know what I taste, Brightwater? Lies. Always lies, since I was a kid, since before I knew what it was. Every lie tastes like pennies and pond water, and I have never once in my life been able to turn it off. I taste my own lies too. Do you know how many lies I've told to survive? I'm *marinated* in them." Her dark eyes fixed on Paloma, fierce and wet. "So when I tell you that everything you just said tasted true—all of it, every word, even the joke about the flower—I want you to understand what that's worth coming from me."

The kitchen went quiet. Outside, the valley groaned—somewhere north, another street was forgetting its own name; they could hear it, a sound like a word being swallowed. The stove's fireless flame bent toward the door, as if listening.

Paloma looked at their hands. Silver-traced, steady, twenty-six years of swallowed death written into them in a language only their body could read. They had spent their whole life believing endurance was the absence of purpose. That they were the valley's spare part, its insurance policy, the person who existed so that better people wouldn't have to die. Tasters weren't heroes. Tasters were the cost of doing business, paid daily, thanked rarely.

But the poison had never been the punishment. It had been the training.

Every dose had carved out a little more room inside them—not emptiness, never emptiness, but *capacity*. Space for the unbearable. And Ilvane was the most unbearable thing the world had ever produced, a woman made of everything the empire had ever devoured, and tomorrow—at the gate, at the Garden, at the crooked green door that only opened for those willing to pay—someone was going to have to stand in front of her and not die.

Paloma stood, and rolled their shoulders, and felt the whole strange architecture of their life settle into place around them like a key turning.

"Bramble," they said. "How much of that recipe's broth is left?"

The thief-turned-cook eyed them. "Enough for one more cooking. Why?"

"Because if I'm going to touch a mage who eats countries," Paloma said, "I want to do it on a full stomach. And I want the last dish I ever learn to cook to be the one that beats her."

Marisol grinned through the pain. Hobb bowed his gray head. Sef took Paloma's silver-marked hand and held it, saying nothing, filing nothing, just holding it.

Outside, far away at the valley's edge, something vast and elegant and hungry turned its attention toward the Crooked Garden—and began, unhurried, to walk.
