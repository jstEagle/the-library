## 18. The Last Course

The gate of the Crooked Garden stood open a hand's width, and through that gap came a smell I will never describe correctly, though I have tried every night since: green things growing through bone, rain on stone that had never been rained on, the particular sweetness of a door deciding whether to be a door.

Behind me, Vell was coming apart.

I could hear it happening. Not with my ears—with my teeth. A low grinding hum, like a millstone turning somewhere under the valley floor, and every few seconds a *pop* of absence as another street forgot its own name. The bell tower in Lower Vell had stopped being a bell tower an hour ago; now it was just a shape people remembered out of habit, and the habit was fraying. Marisol was down there somewhere, holding the market square with a kitchen knife and a voice like a whip. Bramble had gone back for the children from the tannery row. Sef was at my shoulder, the prophecy book strapped to his chest like a breastplate, and Hobb—

Hobb was on his knees by the gatepost, doing the only thing left to do. Praying to a garden.

And coming up the road through the unraveling fields, unhurried as a woman strolling to a dinner party, came Mage-Censor Ilvane.

She had not rushed once, in all the time I'd known of her. That was the worst thing about her. Floods rush. Fires rush. Ilvane walked, because she had never once met a thing that required hurry. Six nations sat inside her like swallowed stones, and she wore their weight the way a rich woman wears pearls: as proof that someone had paid for them.

She stopped twenty paces from the gate. The magic around her moved like heat off summer cobbles, bending the ruined air, and where her shadow fell, the salt-crusted ground forgot how to be ground and simply became *absence*, smooth and gray and hungry.

"Paloma Brightwater," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like a dish she was considering ordering. "The taster. The little spoon that survived everything."

"I'm not little," I said, because if you're going to die at a garden gate you might as well die accurately.

Something flickered across her face—not amusement, exactly. Interest. The interest of a connoisseur finding an unexpected note in a familiar vintage.

"No," she agreed. "You're not."

* * *

Here is what happened before she arrived, and I have to tell it plainly because there is no time for it to be beautiful:

When she breached the border, magic flooded into Vell like water into a hull, and I was standing closest to the tear. It hit me the way a wave hits a swimmer who has spent thirty years learning to hold their breath. Every poison I'd ever swallowed—every arsenic-laced honey cake, every drop of nightshade in the morning tea, every carefully measured ruin the royal kitchens had fed me since I was nine years old—rose up in my blood and met it.

Raw magic is a poison. Nobody in the empire knows this, because nobody in the empire survives it long enough to learn. But I am a taster. Tasting is what I am *for*. And when Ilvane's power broke over me and found nothing in me weak enough to kill, it did something I still don't fully understand.

It told me what it tasted like.

Not metaphorically. I stood in the flood of a mage-censor's hunger and my tongue—my poor, scarred, professional tongue—read it like a ledger. And underneath the iron and ozone and the cold fat flavor of eaten countries, underneath all of it, there was one note so faint I almost missed it.

Bread. Woodsmoke. River water in clay pots. A song sung off-key by many voices.

A kitchen.

Ilvane's magic had a memory buried in it, deep as a pit, and the memory was of a place that had fed her before the empire did. Before she was the empire's mouth, she had been somebody's daughter standing in somebody's kitchen, waiting for bread.

She had eaten her own home first. Of course she had. You always eat what's closest.

* * *

"You're wondering why I'm offering again," Ilvane said now, tilting her head. Behind her, the sky over the salt fields was the wrong color—the color of a page with the words scraped off. "You refused so beautifully last time. All that moral compromise. I enjoyed it more than you know; hypocrisy has such a bright flavor."

"I'm not wondering anything," I said. "I'm cooking."

Her eyes went, for the first time, to what was beside me.

We had dragged the waystation stove from the Kitchen at the End of the Map—Hobb and Sef and I, three days ago, when the world still held together enough for dragging things. It stood now before the garden gate, black iron, lit without fire, burning on nothing but intention and the memory-seasoning we'd learned to feed it. On its surface sat a single pot, lidded, breathing steam that smelled of woodsmoke and river water and bread.

Ilvane looked at the pot for a long moment.

Then she laughed. It was not a cruel laugh, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. It was genuinely delighted, the laugh of a woman who has just been served something unexpected at a table where she expected to do the serving.

"A last meal," she said. "For me. You're going to *feed* me."

"You've been eating your whole life," I said. "One more dish won't kill you." I lifted the lid.

The steam rose straight up, unbothered by wind, and the smell of it rolled across the dead ground—and the dead ground *remembered*. For ten feet in every direction, gray absence bloomed briefly into grass. Just grass. Just for a moment. Then it faded, and Ilvane's smile faded with it, and I knew I had her.

Because I hadn't cooked this from a recipe. There was no recipe. I had cooked it from the taste of her own magic, the memory she'd swallowed so deep she'd forgotten she was carrying it, and I had seasoned it the only way the stove accepted anymore: with one of mine.

I had given it the taste of honey cake. The first one. Arsenic and all. The small controlled risk that made me who I am, poured into the pot like salt, gone forever from my mouth even if I lived through this night. I didn't mourn it yet. There wasn't time. There would be time later, and God help me, there would be so much time.

"What is it?" Ilvane asked. Her voice had changed. The connoisseur's drawl was still there, but underneath it, faintly, horribly, was hunger. Real hunger. The kind that isn't about power.

"It's your first memory," I said. "The kitchen you stood in before you were anyone's weapon. The bread. The river water. The song your people sang off-key." I set the lid down gently, like setting down a sleeping child. "You didn't destroy it when you ate your country. You kept it. Buried it so deep in your magic that you can't reach it anymore. But I could taste it. I'm a taster. Finding the hidden ingredient is the whole job."

The wind stopped. The grinding hum of Vell's unraveling seemed, for one suspended moment, to lean in and listen.

"You're lying," Ilvane said softly. "This is a trick. Poison. You're a poisoner; it's the oldest trick in—"

"It's not poisoned," I said. "If I wanted you dead I'd have put it in the steam. Breathe it in deep. Go on. Tell me I'm lying."

And here is the thing about Ilvane that no one in the empire understood, the thing I only understood because I had spent my whole life studying mouths: she was starving. Not for countries—for countries are just food, and food is just fuel. She was starving for the one meal she could never have again, the one she had eaten without knowing it was the last, the way everyone eats their last ordinary evening. Every nation she devoured was an attempt to taste it again. Every map she erased was a search of the pantry. And the pantry was empty, and had been empty for two hundred years, and she had grown so elegant and so cold because elegance and coldness are what hunger becomes when it gives up hope.

She stepped forward.

Sef grabbed my arm. "Paloma—" His voice cracked down the middle. "Whatever you're doing—"

"Trust me," I said, which was a terrible thing to say, because he had watched me lie to save us all two nights ago, and trust was exactly the thing I'd spent. He let go anyway. That's the kind of man Sef is. He lets go anyway.

Ilvane crossed the last ten paces and stood over the pot, and up close, without the heat-shimmer of her power between us, I saw what six nations had done to her face. They hadn't aged it. They'd *hollowed* it. She was beautiful the way a goblet is beautiful: shaped entirely by what had been taken out.

She took the spoon.

* * *

I want to tell you she wept. I want to give you the clean story, the story where the monster tastes her humanity and dissolves into tears and the day is saved by feeling. But I'm a cook, and cooks don't serve clean stories. We serve true ones.

She ate. One spoonful. Two. Three.

And her face did something I have seen maybe twice in my life, both times in dying men: it went completely, perfectly calm. The calm of a person who has stopped bracing. Her shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time since she'd entered my valley. Her eyes lost their focus on the horizon—on the next country, the next course, the next hunger—and fixed instead on something twenty years away and four thousand miles east, in a kitchen that existed nowhere now except in a pot of stew.

"Oh," she said.

Just that. *Oh.* The smallest word in any language, and it came out of her like something being born.

"There you are," I said quietly. "That's the taste. That's what you've been looking for. It was never in the maps, Ilvane. You can't find a home by erasing everywhere else. You had it the whole time. You just ate it too fast."

Her hand shook. The spoon clicked against the bowl. Magic was bleeding off her in slow waves now, uncontrolled, and where it touched the ground the gray absence bloomed into grass and stayed bloomed, because the magic was forgetting how to be hungry. Behind her, far off across the salt flats, I heard a hunter-thing shriek and come apart as its mistress's attention slipped its leash.

"The song," she whispered. "They sang it wrong. My mother said they sang it wrong. She was right. They sang it—" Her voice failed. She took another spoonful, and another, faster now, greedy, and I let her, because this was the whole point, this was the entire terrible mercy of it: I was feeding her the thing she had spent two centuries trying to devour her way back to, and every spoonful filled her and emptied her at once, because you cannot get full on a memory, no matter how well it's seasoned.

"Why," she managed. "Why would you cook this. For me. Your enemy. I came to eat your home."

"Because I'm a taster," I said. "My whole life, people gave me poison and called it duty, and I swallowed it and got stronger. You know what I figured out, standing in your flood? Immunity isn't armor. It's not for keeping things out." I nodded at the pot. "It's so you can hold what would kill anyone else. So you can carry it to the table. Somebody had to carry your first memory back to you, Ilvane. Nobody else could touch it without dying. Believe me—I checked. It nearly killed me just tasting it."

She looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment—just a moment—there was no mage-censor in her face at all. There was a girl in a kitchen, listening to a badly sung song, waiting for bread.

"I ate them," she said. "Six. I ate six countries and I never once—" The spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "It never once tasted like this."

"That's because you were eating other people's homes," I said. "Yours was the only one that could ever fill you. And you had it first."

The pot steamed. The garden gate behind us groaned wider—an inch, two inches—as if it could smell what was happening and approved. Down in the valley, the grinding hum slowed. Not stopped. Slowed. Somewhere below us, a street remembered its own name.

Ilvane set down the spoon.

And then she did the last thing I expected, the thing I will be untangling for the rest of my life: she reached past the pot, took my hand—her bare magic against my poisoned, hardened, thirty-years-in-the-making skin—and pressed something into my palm. A small object, cold, dense, humming faintly.

A key. Black iron, crooked as a question mark.

"The toll," she said hoarsely. "The garden takes a memory of home. Yours. All of yours—that's the price for a door this wide, for a whole people. I know. I've stood at gates like it before." Her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them, and I think she didn't know how. "But the key chooses who turns it. It chose you the moment you walked into my flood and lived. Feed the stove whatever it asks. Pay what it demands. And when you forget—" her grip tightened on my hand, hard enough to hurt, hard enough to matter— "*when you forget, and you will, remember this much: the honey cake was worth it. Whatever else you lose, keep that. Keep the knowing that the risk was worth it.*"

Then she stepped back, and drew herself up, and the cold came down over her face again—but it was a different cold now. A chosen cold. The cold of a woman putting a mask back on because the work isn't finished.

"The empire will send others," she said. "Censors younger and hungrier than me. I can slow them. I cannot stop them. Get your people through the door, taster." She turned toward the smoking ruins of the border, toward the empire, toward whatever waited for her there—court-martial, probably, or worse, the discovery that the great Mage-Censor had been fed her own childhood by a valley girl with a wooden spoon. "Tell no one I faltered."

"I won't," I said. Which was a lie, because I am telling you right now, and I will tell it for the rest of my life, however long the garden leaves me.

She walked away into the wrong-colored sky, and the ground remembered itself behind her footsteps, blade by blade of grass, like a tide going out in reverse.

* * *

Sef came to stand beside me. Hobb rose off his knees. Behind the gate, the hedge-maze of bones shifted and sighed, and the gap in the vines widened to the width of a cart, then a wagon, then a road.

"She's gone," Sef said, staring after her. "You fed her and she *left*. Paloma, what did you—what was in that pot?"

"My best memory," I said. "And hers." I turned the black iron key over in my fingers. It was warm now. It was getting warmer. "Sef. Go down to the square. Tell Marisol, tell Bramble, tell the Dowager Queen, tell every soul in Vell who can still hear their own name: the door is open. Everyone comes. No one stays. Bring the ledgers, bring the dogs, bring the sourdough starter if anyone was mad enough to pack it."

He hesitated, the book against his chest, his eyes full of a question he was too kind to ask—*will you still know me tomorrow?*

"Go," I said gently. "I'll catch up. I have a bill to settle first."

He ran. Hobb squeezed my shoulder once, wordless, and followed him down toward the unraveling streets, and then it was just me and the stove and the gate and the key growing hot as a coal in my fist.

The garden's voice came up through the ground, through the soles of my boots, patient as roots: *Toll.*

"I know," I said aloud. I set the key in the lock. It fit the way a memory fits a mouth. "I know exactly what it costs. Start with the kitchens. Take the oath. Take the honey cake. Take all of it."

I thought of my mother's hands. I thought of the Dowager Queen's confession, still fresh as a wound. I thought of a valley that had never been allowed to exist, and the people in it who deserved, more than anything, simply *to be seen*.

The gate began to swing wide.

The key turned, and the paying began.
