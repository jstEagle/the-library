## 19. Toll

The first thing the Garden took was the smell of bread.

I didn't understand it at first. The gate had opened, the hedges had parted like a crowd making way, and I stood on the threshold with my hand still on the key—on Sef's hand, actually, because he'd come up beside me without my noticing, the way he always did—and then the air simply went *blank* in one particular place. A hole shaped like yeast and warmth. I reached for the memory of the palace ovens at dawn, the first batch of the day, that smell that meant *someone will eat today*—

And found nothing. Not an absence, exactly. Absences have edges. This was smooth. Polished. As if the ovens had never existed, as if no one had ever kneaded dough in Vell, as if the concept of rising were something I'd only heard described.

"That's it starting," Hobb said quietly behind me. He'd seen this before. He'd paid this before, or watched someone pay it, back when he served the woman who ate countries. His voice had the flatness of a man describing weather he couldn't stop.

"It's taking them one at a time," I said.

"It's taking what you offered." Sef's fingers tightened over mine on the key. "You told it where to start."

I had. *Start with the kitchens.* Foolish, generous, precise Paloma, laying out the menu for my own devouring. But there had been a logic to it, buried under the panic: if I had to lose everything, I wanted to choose the order. A taster's last dignity is knowing what's in the dish.

Behind us, the valley was coming.

I could hear it—the whole of Vell moving at once, ten thousand footsteps on the road from the city, the Dowager Queen's carriage groaning under its own age, children asking questions their parents couldn't answer because the parents didn't know either. All they knew was that the sky over the border had torn open three days ago and magic had come pouring through like seawater into a hull, and the streets had started forgetting themselves—the Cobbler's Row sign unlettering letter by letter, the fountain in Coronet Square running backward because it had forgotten which way down was—and Ilvane's hunters were at the edge of it all, patient as tide.

We had maybe an hour. Maybe less.

The gate needed to stay open that long. And the gate stayed open only as long as I kept paying.

"Toll," said the garden again, up through my boots, through my teeth. Patient as roots. Hungry as roots too, I thought—roots don't ask permission, they just take, slowly, until the ground is all theirs.

"I'm here," I said. "Keep going."

* * *

It took the oath next.

I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave—not pain, exactly, but a strange lightness, a socket where weight used to live. One moment I could recite it: *My mouth is the wall. My body is the bridge between the pot and the crown. What I swallow, the sovereign need not.* Words I'd spoken every morning since I was fourteen, kneeling on cold kitchen tile with the other apprentices, our voices fogging the air. Words that had been the spine of my entire life.

Gone. I tried to reach for them and my mind slid off, the way a knife slides off a wet whetstone. I knew—I knew in the way you know a room exists behind a closed door—that somewhere in me there had been an oath. I could see its shape from the outside: solemnity, cold tile, the taste of iron and rice. But the words themselves were sealed now, behind glass, in a country I would never visit again.

"Paloma." Marisol caught my elbow. Her wound from the salt fields had finally closed—closed the moment we crossed into the garden's ground, time resuming around her flesh like water filling a footprint—but she still moved carefully, like someone relearning her own body. "How much can you give before there's nothing left of you to walk through?"

"Ask the garden," I said. "It's the one keeping count."

She didn't laugh. None of them did. Bramble stood at the edge of the hedge with her knives out, watching the road down the valley, counting heads the way she counted locks—professionally, grimly, without hope of picking any of them. Hobb had his hands folded like a man at a funeral. Sef hadn't let go of the key. Of my hand.

"Your mother's hands," Sef said softly. "You told me once. That's the memory you'd keep, if you could only keep one."

Had I told him that? I must have. There was so much of my life I'd handed to him in pieces, across archive tables and kitchen doorways, flirting the way archivists and tasters flirt—in marginalia, in borrowed tastes, in long silences that meant more than speeches.

"Yes," I said. "Save that for last. Tell me when we're through. Tell me what I lost, after. Someone has to remember it for me."

"I'll remember everything," Sef said. "I've been writing it down since the prophecy room. Every day of you I could get." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it by adjusting his spectacles, which did not need adjusting. "I'm going to be insufferable about it, too. You'll wake up in some foreign field with no idea who you are and I'll be there with eleven notebooks."

"Twelve," I said. "Be honest."

"Fourteen."

The garden rumbled. *Toll.*

And it took the honey cake.

* * *

I want to describe what that was like, and I find I already can't—not fully, not anymore. I'm writing this part later, from the other side, from Sef's dictation and my own hollowed-out certainty that something sweet used to live here.

Here is what I salvaged, from the notebooks, from the others' faces:

The honey cake was the first thing I ever tasted as a royal taster. Arsenic-laced, third course of the autumn feast, my fourteenth year. I remembered—it seems I remembered, once—the specific terror of it, the chalk-dry crumb, the way Master Corvin watched my throat for the swallow. I remembered surviving. I remembered walking out of that hall changed, marked, belonging to something for the first time in my life.

The garden took the taste first. Then the terror. Then the hall itself, the banners, the watching faces, Master Corvin's eyebrows. It took it in layers, like peeling an onion toward the heart, and each layer went with a small silent *click* I felt in my sternum, and each click left me lighter and less.

By the end I was holding onto Sef's arm with both hands, standing in a doorway made of living vine and bone, and I could not have told you why my eyes were wet. Grief with no object. Mourning with no corpse. Just the shape of a loss, worn smooth, like a stone in a river that has forgotten it was ever a mountain.

"There's a cake," I heard myself say. "Isn't there? There was a cake. Why do I know there was a cake?"

"Because I won't let you forget that you forgot," Sef said fiercely. "That's the deal. You lose the memories. I keep the losing."

*Toll,* said the garden, and reached deeper.

* * *

It took the kitchens whole, then—a decade of mornings compressed into a single swallowing. The scullery boys' songs. The burn scar on my forearm from the render-pot, shaped like a comma, a pause mid-sentence. The exact weight of a properly balanced tasting spoon. The name of the head cook, whose face I can no longer summon but whose disapproval, apparently, I feared more than poison.

It took the Dowager Queen teaching me cards in the winter of my eighteenth year, and cheating, and letting me catch her cheating, and laughing like a girl though she was older than the valley's erasure.

It took the first time I met Marisol—no, wait, that one it tried to take and *couldn't*, and I felt it strain against the memory like a dog against a chain, because that memory wasn't only mine. It lived half in her, half in Hobb, half in the smoke-stained ceiling of the tasters' quarters where we'd sat sharing stolen wine the night we decided to run. Shared things held. The garden could empty my house but not the neighbors' houses where my things had been copied.

So I did the only strategic thing left in me, the taster's instinct, the hoarder's trick: I started *sharing*. Out loud, fast, while the gate drank.

"Sef—the night you brought me the prophecy book, your hands were shaking so hard you dropped it twice, and you blamed the stairs, and there were no stairs—"

"I remember," he said, wet-eyed, gripping back.

"Hobb—you cried at Marisol's joke about the emperor's hat, you absolutely cried, don't deny it—"

"A man can sweat from his eyes," Hobb said, and his voice was wrecked, and everyone laughed, and the laughter went into the shared air where the garden couldn't reach it.

"Bramble—you slept through the siege alarm. Through it. We had to carry you—"

"I was conserving energy for the important parts," Bramble said, and grinned, and the grin was a thing with five owners now, and safe.

Every memory I spoke aloud became ours instead of mine. The garden snarled—I felt it snarl, a root-deep frustration—and reached for the ones I hadn't shared yet. The private ones. The mother's hands.

* * *

The people of Vell came up the road like a river remembering it was a river.

I saw them through the gate's arch: the whole valley, streaming toward the door that had waited a hundred years under the bones of six eaten nations. The Dowager Queen at the front, impossibly upright, refusing every offered arm. Children carried on shoulders. The baker—some baker, a baker, I knew the trade and not the man—still in his apron, carrying nothing but his hands. Ten thousand people who had never been allowed to exist, coming to exist.

And the gate drank me to hold itself wide enough for them.

I understood the arithmetic then, standing in the doorway with my life pouring out of me course by course. The toll wasn't a fee. It was a *proportion*. The door was as wide as the payment was deep. A coin would open it for one. A memory would open it for a family. But a whole homeland passing through—a nation stepping from invisibility into sight—required a life's worth of home poured out like wine, cup after cup after cup, until the channel ran clear.

Fine, I thought. Fine. I had spent my whole life swallowing things so other people wouldn't have to. This was just the largest dish ever set in front of me.

"Toll," said the garden, gentler now, almost tender, the way a nurse is tender drawing blood.

"Take it," I said.

It took the valley itself. Not the fact of it—I'd lose that too, soon, I knew—but the *feel*: the particular gray-green of the light in Vell, the way rain sounded on slate roofs, the smell of the salt fields at dusk. It took my sense of the borders, the hedges, the hiddenness. It took the knowledge of what it meant to be erased, and with it went my anger, and I grieved the anger's going almost more than anything, because the anger had been load-bearing. Who was I, without the grievance that had built me?

Someone from nowhere, said the garden. That's who opens doors.

The road emptied. The last of Vell came through—the stragglers, the doubters, the old man who'd refused to leave his house until the house itself began forgetting him, its walls going translucent like breath on glass. He stumbled through the gate weeping and patted my shoulder as he passed, and said, "Thank you, child," and I smiled at him with no idea who he was, and both of those things were true at once, and both of them hurt.

Then the garden turned to the last dish.

* * *

My mother's hands.

I had saved them the way you save the best bite. Flour to the wrists. The ring she wore—had worn?—a thin band with a crooked setting. The motion of her kneading, which was also the motion of the world turning, which was also, I understood now, the motion of a smuggled infant being rocked by a woman who was not her blood but chose her anyway, because the Dowager Queen had confessed it days ago: born beyond the border, from a nation already eaten, from nowhere, from the exact nowhere that doors open for.

Wait. That wasn't my mother. My mother was—

The garden waited. Even roots can wait.

Sef's hand was on my cheek. "You don't have to narrate this one," he whispered. "Some things get to be private even at the end."

But private things were the only things left that the garden could take, and the gate was still open, and the last families were still crossing, and somewhere behind us on the road Ilvane's hunters had stopped pretending to be patient.

"No," I said. "Take it. Take all of it. That's the point of me."

And it did. And I stood in the doorway of the Crooked Garden, holding a key I no longer recognized, looking at a man with kind eyes and fourteen notebooks who was crying and saying my name—my name, which suddenly sounded foreign, borrowed, a coat I'd been wearing so long I'd mistaken it for skin—and I felt the final click in my sternum, and the gate, paid in full, began to close behind the last child of Vell.

"What was it?" I asked him. My voice sounded young. "What did I pay? Tell me on the other side. Tell me everything."

He pressed the key into my palm and folded my fingers over it, and kissed my knuckles like sealing a letter.

"Everything," he said. "Come home."

Beyond the hedge, past the bones of six dead nations, the light of an unmapped world was waiting—and the first thing I saw in it was a hillside, green and utterly strange, and strangers with familiar eyes reaching out their hands.

I stepped through, carrying nothing, owed everything.

The gate shut on the valley I could no longer name, and ahead of us, on a hillside above a country that appeared on real maps, ten thousand people who had never been allowed to exist stood blinking in the sun—seen, at last, by everyone.
