## 20. Through

**Marisol**

The door was a hedge, and the hedge was singing.

Marisol had no better word for it. The Crooked Garden's gate had opened like a mouth—branches peeling back, petals shivering—and beyond it there was not another corridor of green but a hillside, gold with late light, sloping down toward a river valley where smoke rose from chimneys that belonged to someone else. Someone real. Someone on a map.

She stood at the threshold and could not make her feet move.

Behind her, Vell was coming apart. She could hear it—the sound of a country forgetting itself. Streets unspooling like thread pulled from a hem. The bell tower in Lowmarket ringing a note that had never been cast. People running through lanes that grew soft underfoot, their shadows thinning even as they ran.

"Go," Hobb said beside her, his voice cracked down the middle. He was carrying a child on each hip, two of the baker's granddaughters, their faces pressed into his neck. "You first. You're the fastest."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not leaving me. You're going through and holding the other side so I know it holds." His eyes were wet and steady. "That's what tasters do, girl. We go first."

So Marisol went first.

The moment she crossed, the world *arrived*. That was the only way she could describe it afterward, when she had words again. In Vell she had always felt slightly muffled, like a voice speaking through a wall—even to herself, especially to herself. Here, every sensation landed at full volume: wind with actual weather behind it, grass that smelled of cow fields and rain, a sky so wide it made her dizzy. And underneath everything, faint as a pulse, the sense of being *counted*. Of existing somewhere outside her own borders.

She turned and held out both hands.

They came through in a river after that. Farmers with their seed sacks. The Dowager Queen carried on a litter by four kitchen boys who refused to put her down. Cooks still in their aprons, flour ghosting their sleeves. Children, dogs, goats, one indignant peacock that had apparently decided exile was preferable to dinner. Bramble came through last of the company, bleeding from a cut on her scalp, hauling a sack of the Garden's seeds over her shoulder because—"if we're starting over, we're starting with good stock."

Marisol counted them. It was the thing she did; she tasted truth, and she counted what was true. Two thousand and some. More than half of everyone. Behind the gate, the rest were still coming, and behind *them*—

The hillside darkened.

Not cloud-dark. *Absence*-dark. Ilvane had reached the border, and the border was no longer a line anyone could hold. Marisol watched the edge of Vell simply stop being—a field here, a chapel there, folding up like paper held to flame—and understood, with her whole poisoned mouth, that whatever Paloma was doing back there at the gate, they were losing time.

"Hobb." Her voice came out small. "How long do we have?"

Hobb set the children down and looked at the hillside, at the river, at the road below where a shepherd had stopped dead, staring up at two thousand people appearing out of nowhere on a hill that had been empty an hour ago.

"Long enough," he said. "It has to be."

It was the least convincing lie she'd ever tasted. She took his hand anyway.

* * *

**Bramble**

Bramble had stolen things her whole life: purses, recipes, a year of her life from a man in Casker's Row who deserved it. But she had never stolen anything like this—a whole nation, walked out the back door of existence while the front door burned.

Her job, self-assigned, was the rear guard. Which meant standing just inside the gate while the last of Vell poured past her, watching the darkness come, and trying very hard not to think about what was taking so long.

Paloma was still in there.

She could see them, far down the crooked green corridor of the Garden: a small figure standing at the heart of the maze with their arms raised, holding the gate open the way you'd hold a door for a procession too large to ever finish. Light poured off them—or maybe it poured *into* them; Bramble couldn't tell anymore. Every person who passed through the gate seemed to take a little of Paloma with them, or leave a little of themselves behind. The toll. The memory-toll. Except Paloma wasn't paying once. They were paying continuously, a coin a second, holding the price of two thousand admissions in their body.

"Bramble!" Sef's voice, ragged. The archivist came stumbling up the path with an armload of books strapped to his chest like armor, his glasses cracked, his face streaked with ash. "Have you seen—did they come through—"

"Not yet."

"They have to." He said it like arithmetic. Like if he stated it plainly enough the universe would show its work. "The prophecy names them at the door. At the door, Bramble. Not through it. I've read it four hundred times. There's nothing after the door. There's no page that says they—"

He stopped. Bramble watched him understand what he'd just said, and she watched him decide, visibly, furiously, to refuse it.

"Then we stand here," she said, "until they walk through."

And they stood there. While the sky behind them curdled. While the hedge walls began to gray at the tips, the Garden itself being eaten now, its roots in dead nations finally claimed. While the last families came through—old Hobb, weeping without shame; Marisol dragging a limping stranger; the Dowager Queen walking on her own feet, refusing the litter, chin high, a queen entering exile the way she'd entered everything.

The gate began to close.

No—it began to *narrow*, the way a throat narrows, the green walls leaning in, and through the closing gap Bramble saw Paloma turn and look back at the valley one last time. Look at it the way you look at a face in a photograph of someone already gone.

Then Sef shoved past her and ran into the narrowing gate, and Bramble, swearing fluently in three thieves' cants, ran after him.

* * *

**Sef**

Later—hours later, days, he would never be able to order those hours—he would remember this: the weight of the books against his chest, and the weight of Paloma against his chest, and how the second weight was the only one that mattered.

He found them ten steps inside the gate, on their knees on a path made of woven bone-hedges, and they were *empty*. Not dying—worse. Dying keeps your shape. Paloma had been poured out. Their eyes were open and tracking, but everything behind the eyes had been spent, paid out coin by coin, memory by memory, until what remained was a person-shaped vessel with a stranger's calm.

"Sef," they said pleasantly, as if greeting him at a market stall. "Hello. Do I know you?"

The words went through him like a blade finding the seam in armor. Fourteen years of shared meals in the archive stacks. The flirtation he'd been too cowardly to name and too stubborn to end. The night they'd sat up cataloguing the sealed room together and he'd shown them the prophecy book and their hand had found his hand and neither of them had moved it until dawn.

"Do I know you?" they asked again, gently, because he hadn't answered, because he was crying too hard to answer.

"Yes," he managed. "Yes. You're—you're the royal food-taster. You're the bravest person in Vell. You swallowed poison every morning for eleven years so the queen could eat breakfast, and you complained about the honey cake the entire time, and you—"

"That sounds like someone worth knowing," Paloma said, and smiled at him, and the smile was kind and total and belonged to no one.

The gate was closing. He could feel it at his back, the hedge-walls knitting, the toll collected, the transaction complete. Through the final gap he saw the valley go—not burning, not screaming, just *unwriting*, the way a word fades from a page left in the sun—and then the gap sealed, and the Crooked Garden stood whole and silent around them, and Vell was nowhere at all.

Sef knelt in the bones of six dead nations' hedges and held the person he loved, who no longer knew him, and discovered that grief and arrival can occupy the same body at the same time. That you can be saved and ruined in a single breath. That *through* is not the same as *over*.

"It's all right," Paloma told him, patting his arm with the serene authority of someone comforting a stranger. "Whatever happened, it's over now. And you're here. Whoever you are."

"My name is Sef."

"Sef." They tried it out. Their brow creased—the first crack in the calm, the first flicker of something reaching for something and finding air. "Why does that taste familiar?"

"Because I read to you," he said. "For years. Bad poetry, mostly. You fell asleep every single time and pretended you hadn't."

"I don't remember."

"No."

"But it feels—" They pressed a hand flat against their own sternum, frowning. "There's a feeling. Like a warm plate. Like a kitchen I can't picture. Is that—is that you?"

"That's us," Sef said. "That's all of us. Come and see."

* * *

**Hobb**

The hillside above the river was full of Vell by sundown, and the wider world had noticed.

Hobb stood at the edge of the camp—they were calling it a camp, though it was really two thousand people sitting very quietly in the grass, waiting to find out if they existed—and watched the lanterns of the town below multiply. Watched riders come up the valley road, slow and wary, torches bobbing. Watched the Dowager Queen rise to meet them without any crown at all, wearing a cook's borrowed shawl, and heard her say, in a voice that carried across the whole hillside:

"We are the people of Vell. You will not find us on your maps. We are asking you to draw us there."

A silence. Then the lead rider, a woman with grey in her braid and a surveyor's badge on her coat, looked down at the map in her saddlebag, looked up at the impossible crowd on the empty hill, and said the sentence Hobb would carry to his grave:

"There's a blank space here. There's always been a blank space here. We thought it was nothing."

"It was us," the Queen said. "We were the nothing. And we are finished being nothing."

Hobb turned away before he wept openly. He was old; he'd earned the right to weep privately, at least.

He found Marisol at the cookfires—of course there were cookfires; you could scatter a nation to the winds and within an hour its cooks would find each other and start feeding people, this was the deepest law he knew—and beside the fires, wrapped in blankets, sat Paloma, blinking at the flames like a newborn bird.

Marisol was teaching them her grandmother's soup. Step by step, ingredient by ingredient, patient as spring.

"And you just let it sit? No spell?"

"No spell. There's no magic anywhere, Marisol, I'd know—I mean, I wouldn't know, but—"

"Salt," Marisol said. "Fat. Time. That's the whole religion."

Paloma tasted it. Hobb watched from a distance as they went still, spoon halfway to their mouth, staring into the steam with an expression he recognized from a hundred years of mirrors: the look of someone tasting a ghost.

"What is it?" Marisol asked softly. "What do you taste?"

"I don't know." Paloma's voice wavered. "Something sweet. Something with honey in it. Why does soup remind me of honey cake? Why do I want to cry about a cake I've never eaten?"

Hobb walked away then, because some griefs are private even when they belong to everyone.

But at the edge of the firelight he stopped, and looked back, and made himself look properly—at the taster who remembered nothing, surrounded by everyone they'd forgotten, being fed soup by a liar and guarded in silence by a thief, while an archivist hovered near with a blanket and a broken heart and a book of prophecies clutched under his arm like a child's toy.

They had lost everything, all of them. The valley, the kitchens, the oath, the names of streets. Paloma had lost more—had paid for everyone else's keeping.

And yet here they all were, on a hillside in a world that had finally agreed to see them, building a circle of firelight out of nothing, which was the oldest recipe there was.

Hobb sat down at the fire. He took the bowl Marisol handed him. He ate.

* * *

**Paloma**

In the smallest hours, when most of the hill had finally slept, Paloma could not.

They sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life, watching the stars—which were wrong, which were many, which were *named*, because Sef had pointed up and recited constellations from a book and the sky had obligingly matched. A sky with footnotes. A world with maps.

Sef sat beside them. He had not left their side since the garden. Paloma didn't know why, exactly, except that it felt like gravity, like a law of nature: this person is near you; this is correct.

"I keep almost-remembering," Paloma said. "It's like a word on the tip of my tongue. All night. A valley. Green hills. Was there a valley?"

Sef was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was careful, the way you speak around a wound.

"There was a place," he said. "It was small, and it was hidden, and it was the best place I've ever known. And you held its door open so that everyone inside it could live. Everyone but you."

"That doesn't sound fair."

"It wasn't."

"Did I complain about it?"

A surprised laugh broke out of him, wet and sudden. "Constantly. About everything. Especially the honey cake."

"The honey cake." Paloma closed their eyes. There it was again—that ache with no address, sweetness shaped like sorrow. "I wish I could grieve properly. For it. Whatever it was. It feels rude, enjoying this soup, this blanket, this—sky—when I'm supposed to be mourning a home I gave away. But I don't know what I'm missing. How do you mourn a hole?"

"You don't," Sef said. "You fill it. Slowly. With new things. That's what the living do."

Down the hillside, the fires burned low. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed. Somewhere Marisol was arguing with Bramble about whose turn it was to sleep. Somewhere old Hobb kept watch, because men like Hobb always kept watch, paying for old sins in the only currency they trusted.

Paloma leaned, experimentally, against Sef's shoulder. He went very still, then carefully, carefully, let his head rest against theirs.

"I don't know you," Paloma murmured. "But I know you. Does that make any sense?"

"Every sense," Sef whispered. "It makes every sense there is."

Above them, the unnamed stars wheeled over a hillside that was, as of tonight, on no map at all—and Paloma Brightwater, born nowhere, citizen of nothing, keeper of no memories but their own future, watched the east begin to pale and thought:

*A blank space. We'll fill it.*

Far down in the grass, half-buried beneath Sef's discarded cloak, the old prophecy book lay open to a page no one had written yet—and in the grey pre-dawn light, faint as breath on glass, fresh ink was beginning to bloom.
