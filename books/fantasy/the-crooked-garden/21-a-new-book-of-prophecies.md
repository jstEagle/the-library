## 21. A New Book of Prophecies

The kitchen they rented in Corvane was too small for four people and exactly the right size for grief.

Sef had learned this in the first week: that a room could be measured not in paces but in absences. There was no room here for the Dowager Queen's forty years of silences. No room for Hobb's garden of regrets, which he tended now like an old man watering dead flowers. No room for Marisol's wound, which had finally closed but left a pale seam across her ribs like a scar on a map where a river used to run. And no room—no room at all—for what Paloma had paid.

But it was a kitchen, and Sef had chosen it for that reason alone, over three larger rooms with better light. Because Paloma did not remember Vell, but their hands remembered cooking. He had watched them stand in the doorway of a dozen apartments in this foreign city, foreign to him anyway, and go still as a compass needle only when the landlady said *and here's where you'd cook*. So he rented the small one with the crooked stove and the window that looked out on a wall, and told himself the wall was better than a view. A view would have been somewhere else. The wall was just the wall.

It was Bramble who found the book again.

They had carried very little through the Garden—what do you carry when you are carrying a whole nation's worth of people behind you?—but Bramble, being Bramble, had carried things. A set of lockpicks worn smooth as river stones. A knife she used mostly for cheese. And wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, because she said you never abandoned a book, even one that had ruined your life: the prophecy book from the sealed room beneath the palace of Vell.

*Had been* the palace of Vell. Sef was still learning to speak of home in the past tense, and failing most days.

Bramble kept the book in the cupboard above the stove, between a tin of salt and a jar of pickled lemons Marisol had bought from a market stall because the vendor's accent reminded her of someone. Nobody opened it. The book had done its work; its pages had named a date and drawn a face and been right about both, and rightness like that leaves a taste in your mouth you don't rush back to.

Then, on a grey morning six weeks after the crossing, while Paloma was at the fish market and Marisol was sleeping off a night shift at the harbor infirmary and Hobb was sitting in the square doing his slow, wordless penance of feeding pigeons, Bramble went up on a stool to get the salt and came down holding the book like it had bitten her.

"Sef," she said. Her voice had a particular quality when she was frightened, which was almost never: it got quieter. "Sef, come look at this."

He was grinding pepper. He set down the pestle. Later he would remember that—the pepper, half-ground, left on the counter—and how ordinary the moment was before it wasn't.

The book lay open on the table. It was old; it had always been old, its cover cracked leather gone soft as skin, its pages the color of weak tea. But the pages at the back—the ones that had been blank since the night Sef first broke protocol and carried it to a food-taster who should have been asleep—were no longer blank.

Ink was moving on them.

Not drying. Not smearing. *Moving*, the way water moves under ice, the way a worm moves under soil. Letters surfacing letter by letter, a line assembling itself out of nothing while they watched, in a hand none of them recognized and all of them somehow knew.

"What date is that?" Bramble whispered.

Sef read it twice. His mouth had gone dry in the specific way it did before bad news, a taster's reflex, the body bracing for poison. "Spring," he said. "Next spring. The seventeenth of Thaw-month."

"That's—" Bramble counted on her fingers, though there was no need. "That's months away."

"I know."

"And it says—" She turned the page toward him. Below the date, in that same surfacing hand, were words. Not many. Prophecies, Sef had learned, were stingy things; they gave you a coin and let you imagine the fortune. The line read:

*A door in the drowned terraces. A girl who counts stars. Ask her what she lost.*

Below that, fainter, still forming, another date. And below that, the ghost of a third, ink rising through the paper like something climbing toward air.

Sef sat down slowly on Bramble's stool, which creaked under him, which was real, which was made of wood cut from trees that grew in a country that existed on maps. That was the thing he kept coming back to, these weeks. Everything here existed. Every door had a street on the other side of it. Every stranger they passed in the market had a grandmother, and the grandmother had a grave, and the grave had a stone, and the stone had a name, and all of it was written down somewhere, registered, remembered. He had spent his whole life in a valley that was erased so thoroughly that even its own survivors' memories had been the only map remaining. He did not think he would ever stop being astonished by gravestones.

And now the book was writing again.

"It's not finished," he said. "Vell. Our story. It's not—it wasn't the end. It was just a chapter."

Bramble looked at him the way she looked at locks she intended to open. "Other doors," she said. "That's what it means, isn't it. Other nations. Other valleys like ours." Her jaw tightened. "Like Ilvane."

The name dropped into the kitchen and sat there between them. They did not say it often. Ilvane was dead—or unmade, or dissolved, or however you described what happened when a mage who ate countries was fed her own first memory at a crooked gate and found herself starving for it. Paloma had served her that dish with steady hands, and Sef had watched from the hedge-shadow, and what he had seen in the Mage-Censor's face in that last moment was not defeat. It was homesickness. It was the look of someone who has eaten everything in the world except the one meal that mattered, being offered it at last, too late, in too large a portion.

She had faltered. She had fallen. The Garden had taken her the way it took tolls from everyone: entirely.

But a woman like that does not work alone. Sef had thought about this more than he wanted to. An empire that unmakes nations does not employ only one pair of hands. Somewhere beyond the maps of their new life there were other censors, other surveyors, other mouths tasting absence and calling it vintage. And somewhere—here, in this book, in ink still wet with futurity—there were other valleys. Other kitchens. Other tasters swallowing poison one dish at a time, believing themselves safe inside borders no one else could see.

"A girl who counts stars," Bramble read aloud. "In drowned terraces. Where's that?"

"Somewhere," Sef said. "Everywhere. It doesn't give places. It gives dates." He touched the page and the ink was cool, cooler than paper should be, cool as the air near the Garden gate had been. "Dates and people. That's all it ever gave us. One date. One face." He had drawn that face himself once, early on, copying the sketch into his own notebook so he wouldn't have to keep opening the book. Paloma's face, younger than prophecy, looking over their shoulder at a door made of vines. He still had the notebook. He had not been able to throw away anything from Vell. He suspected he never would.

The front door banged open and cold air came in with Paloma and the smell of the fish market—brine and ice and strangers' voices speaking a language whose music Sef was only beginning to hear rather than merely endure.

"You'll never guess what the fishmonger gave me for free," Paloma announced, setting down two baskets. Then they stopped. Sef watched them stop—the way their whole body went quiet, the way it had gone quiet in the kitchens of Vell whenever a dish smelled wrong. Some instincts survived the loss of every memory that built them.

"What," Paloma said, "is that."

"The book," Bramble said. "It's writing again."

Paloma crossed the room and stood over the page, and Sef stood beside them, close enough that their sleeve brushed his arm, and together they read the new lines while the ink continued its slow emergence, patient as roots.

"Another door," Paloma said at last. Their voice was careful. Since the crossing, since the toll, they spoke carefully about anything connected to home—not because they grieved it, but because they couldn't, and the inability itself was a kind of wound they were polite around, the way you're polite around a burn. "Another valley. Another—" They stopped. Started again. "Someone else is going to have to do what we did."

"No," Sef said. It came out harder than he meant. "No. Someone else is going to have to know what we know. That's different."

Paloma looked at him. They did not remember the archives, or the oath, or the honey cake laced with arsenic that they had eaten smiling. They did not remember him. That was the arithmetic of the toll, and Sef had accepted it, had held them on the hillside above the real map while an entire nation poured through a door behind them, and had thought: *I will be a stranger to you, and I will stay anyway.* He had imagined that was the hardest sentence of his life.

Now the book was offering him a second one.

"There will be others like us," he said. "Tasters. Archivists. People standing in sealed rooms finding things they shouldn't. People at borders that don't exist on any map, keeping whole countries alive by remembering them." He put his hand flat on the page, over the forming third date, feeling the faint tremor of ink arriving. "And if nobody finds this book—if nobody goes—then those valleys don't get a Paloma. They just get eaten. Quietly. The way we almost did."

"We didn't almost," Paloma said. "We did. We were eaten. We just—" They gestured vaguely at the kitchen, the crooked stove, the wall outside the window. "Got digested somewhere nicer."

Bramble laughed despite herself, a short sharp bark. Even now, Sef thought, even hollowed out, Paloma was funny. Grief had not managed to take that. Perhaps it couldn't. Perhaps humor lived in whatever part of a person the empire's magic couldn't reach.

* * *

That evening they gathered, the way they had begun to gather without deciding to, four chairs pulled to one table because a table with four chairs was the closest thing to a country they could build in a rented room.

Hobb arrived last, pigeon feathers on his coat, and listened without interrupting while Sef laid the book open and told them what it was doing. The old man leaned close, squinting. His eyes were rheumy and his hands shook and he was immune to every poison known to the art of poisoning except the one he drank daily, which was memory.

"Drowned terraces," he murmured. "I've heard that phrase." He said it the way he said everything now, flatly, a man who had burned his credibility along with his innocence at another nation's gate a hundred years ago. "Or I heard something like it. Before. When I was—" He stopped. He always stopped there. "It might be nothing. My past is mostly nothing now. I sold the useful parts."

"You kept the parts that mattered," Marisol said. She said it every time. It was becoming liturgy between them, call and response, forgiveness renewed nightly like bread.

"It might be nothing," Hobb repeated. "But drowned terraces—I'd stake my last immunity it's south of here. Coastal. Rice country, maybe. Or it was rice country before the drowning." He looked up, and his old eyes were wet and fierce. "There were seven of us, you know. Nations. That I know of. Ilvane ate six before Vell. Six doors, six gardens, six tolls. Someone paid each one. Someone walked through each time, leading their people out, forgetting their home so their people could keep theirs." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "Six someones. And not one of them left a note."

The kitchen was silent. Outside, the city went on being a city—carts, bells, a drunk singing three streets over, all of it magnificently indifferent to the fact that the world contained more erased countries than anyone had counted.

"Not one of them," Sef said quietly, "had a book that wrote itself."

He looked down at the page. The third date had finished surfacing while Hobb talked. Two dates now, fully formed, stacked like courses. Two doors. Two faces, presumably, waiting to be sketched in the margins by whatever hand drew such things—a hand Sef had once believed belonged to a dead prophet and now suspected belonged to the Garden itself, or to whatever older, stranger thing the Garden grew from.

Two dates. And the book was not stopping.

"Spring," Marisol said, tapping the first date. "That's the drowned-terrace girl. We could make it. It's far, but we could make it."

"We?" Bramble raised an eyebrow. "Since when are we going anywhere? I just learned which market sells decent saffron. I have a *route*."

"Since the book started writing in our cupboard," Marisol said. "You want to leave it to chance? To some local archivist stumbling into a sealed room and having our luck?"

"Our luck nearly killed us," Bramble said.

"Our luck saved us," Sef said. "Both things are true. That's what I keep—" He paused, searching for it. "That's what I keep trying to say. Everything that saved us was also the thing that endangered us. The oath. The book. The poison. Paloma's—" He glanced at them. Paloma nodded slightly, granting permission for their own history. "Paloma's whole life of swallowed poison. It was all preparation wearing the costume of suffering. Maybe that's the pattern. Maybe that's what the Garden grows from. People who took small terrible risks every day until they were ready for the large one."

"So we find the next ones," Marisol said. "Before the empire does. We become what nobody was for us." She grinned, and the scar on her ribs caught the lamplight. "A company of poisoned mouths, second edition."

"It's suicide with extra steps," Bramble said. She was already turning the book toward herself, already reading the second date, already memorizing it. Sef hid a smile. Bramble's refusals had always been the sound of her yes arriving early.

Paloma had not spoken for a while. They sat with their hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold, looking at the page, at the moving ink, at the dates of other people's catastrophes and salvations. Sef watched them and felt the old ache that had become, over six weeks, almost companionable—the ache of loving someone who did not remember being loved by you. He had decided not to tell them. About the archives, about the flirting over catalogued poisons, about the night he broke every rule he'd sworn and carried a forbidden book to a food-taster's door. What good would it do? It would be a story about two people who no longer existed. And yet—

And yet Paloma looked up suddenly, catching him watching, and said, "What? Why are you looking at me like I'm a recipe you can't decipher?"

"Because you are," Sef said. "The best kind. The one with ingredients nobody stocks."

Paloma snorted, and something passed across their face—brief, unplaceable, the ghost of recognition without content, the feeling of family arriving ahead of its memory. Sef had seen it before. He lived for it, if he was honest, which he tried not to be.

"Fine," Paloma said. "South. Drowned terraces. A girl who counts stars." They drained the cold tea and grimaced. "But I'm not eating anything a prophecy tells me to eat. I've retired."

Nobody laughed quite hard enough, because they were all thinking the same thing: that retirement was a word for people whose stories were over, and the book on the table had just made it plain that theirs was not.

Later—much later, after Marisol and Bramble had gone up to bed arguing cheerfully about pack lists, after Hobb had fallen asleep in his chair as he did every night and been covered with a blanket as he was every night—Sef stayed at the table alone with the book.

The candle was low. The kitchen was warm from the banked stove. He turned to the last page, the one where the third date had surfaced, and found that it had company now: below the date, faint as breath on glass, a sketch was forming. He bent close.

It was not a face this time.

It was a kitchen. Small, crook-stoved, window onto a wall. Four chairs at a table. And in the corner, rendered in a few sure strokes, a figure bending over an open book by candlelight—a figure with ink on his fingers and an archivist's stoop, unmistakable, undeniable, drawn as if from life.

As if from *this* life. As if whoever wrote the future had been standing in this exact room, tonight, watching him watch it.

Sef sat very still. The candle guttered. On the page, beneath the sketch, one final line surfaced, letter by letter, in that hand that was no one's and everyone's:

*The reader is part of the reading. Pack lightly. You are already expected.*

He looked at his ink-stained fingers. He looked at the cupboard where the salt and the pickled lemons and the book lived. Somewhere south, in drowned terraces, a girl was counting stars over a country that did not exist, waiting for someone to ask her what she had lost.

Sef blew out the candle and went to wake the others.
