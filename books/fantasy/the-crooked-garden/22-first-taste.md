## 22. First Taste

The kitchen was too small for what Paloma intended to do in it.

That was the first thing they noticed about it, three weeks after Sef had signed the lease on the narrow house above the tannery lane—three weeks of scrubbing soot from the ceiling beams and coaxing the iron stove to draw properly and arguing with Bramble about whether the window over the sink should face the street or the garden. The room was barely wide enough for two people to pass each other without turning sideways. The counter was a plank of scarred oak that some previous tenant had burned a ring into, as if a pot had once been left too long and no one had cared enough to sand the memory out.

Paloma cared about things like that now. They didn't know why.

They stood in the middle of the little kitchen at dawn, before anyone else was awake, with their sleeves rolled and their hands flour-white, and they let themselves feel the size of the room the way you'd test a bruise. Too small. Too low-ceilinged. The stove smoked when the wind came off the harbor, and the chimney needed sweeping again, and there was a draft under the door that no amount of rag-stuffing would kill.

It was nothing like the kitchens they had dreamed of for twenty years without knowing they were dreaming of them.

*You don't dream,* Paloma reminded themself, cracking eggs into a bowl one-handed. *You cook.*

The eggs came from the market woman on Cordwainer Street who had taken a liking to Marisol because Marisol haggled like a woman raised by wolves and the market woman respected that. The butter came from a farm upriver, salted almost past bearing, which Hobb said reminded him of something he couldn't name and then went quiet in the particular way he went quiet, which everyone had learned to leave alone. The leeks were Bramble's contribution, acquired through channels Bramble declined to specify, though Paloma suspected the truth was embarrassingly legal: Bramble had simply charmed the greengrocer's daughter, which was its own kind of theft.

And the honey—the honey had cost Paloma an entire afternoon of walking, because they had passed four stalls selling honey and turned away from all of them, heart hammering, before finding a fifth whose jars were plain clay instead of glass, and even then they'd had to stand there breathing until the shaking stopped.

Honey cake. They didn't know why honey cake mattered. They only knew that when they thought about it, someplace behind their sternum ached like a tooth, sweet and ruined, and that they could not stop thinking about it anyway.

* * *

By midmorning the whole crooked family had found excuses to drift into the kitchen.

Marisol arrived first, ostensibly to return a borrowed knife, actually to supervise. She installed herself on the one stool and narrated everything Paloma did wrong, which was her way of saying she was glad to be alive. Her arm still bore the wound from the salt fields—a thin white line now, closed at last after weeks of refusing to heal—but she rubbed it when she was nervous, and she was nervous this morning, and Paloma pretended not to notice.

"You're burning the butter," she said.

"I am *browning* the butter."

"You're burning it. There's a difference and it's called attention."

Paloma pulled the pan off the heat a half-second early and gave her a look. Marisol grinned. She tasted lies; she also, apparently, dispensed them freely, because she had told the landlady she was Paloma's sister, and told the fishmonger she was a widow, and told Hobb last Tuesday that his new coat made him look distinguished, which was the cruelest lie of the three.

Bramble came in through the back door with flour already on their sleeves, having heard—through channels—that baking was happening and that nonattendance would be noted. They were small and quick and had never quite stopped moving like someone who might need to leave through a window, but lately the windows they eyed were open ones, ones with gardens under them, and sometimes they just looked out of them for a while instead.

"Smells like," Bramble began, and stopped, head tilted, like a bird hearing a far-off bell. "I don't know what it smells like. Something. Something I—" They shook it off. "Something good."

"That's the plan," Paloma said.

Hobb came last, as he always did, easing himself onto the bench by the wall with the groan of a man whose body had survived everything except comfort. He was old enough that his immunity had become legend among them—he could drink hemlock tea with his breakfast and complain only about the taste—but there were mornings when his hands shook, and this was one, and Paloma set a cup of barley tea within reach without being asked, and Hobb looked at the cup for a long moment before drinking, as if it had been placed there by someone he owed.

"It's good," he said finally, of the tea, which was a lie, because the tea was terrible, and Marisol snorted, and the morning tipped over into noise.

Sef arrived last of all, because Sef was always arriving last of all now, coming down the stairs from the front room where the prophecy book lived under lock and cloth and three separate arguments' worth of precaution. He had ink on his fingers and that look on his face—the look Paloma had learned meant *the book has been doing something again*—but today he folded the look away and put it in his pocket and rolled up his sleeves instead.

"What can I do?"

"Zest," Paloma said, handing him a lemon and a knife. "And don't cut yourself. You're the only one of us who can read."

"I'm not the only one who can read."

"You're the only one who reads *voluntarily.*"

Marisol laughed so hard she nearly fell off the stool. Bramble stole a piece of lemon zest and got smacked with a spoon. Hobb observed, to no one, that in his day apprentices lost fingers for less, and Sef said something dry about how in Hobb's day the ovens were fired with the bones of the unrepentant, and the kitchen filled up with talk the way a cup fills with water—easily, completely, to the brim.

Paloma worked in the middle of it and felt, for the first time since waking on that hillside with a name and a trade and a family and no country, something that might have been home.

* * *

The cakes came out of the oven golden and slightly lopsided, because the stove heated unevenly on the left side and nobody had fixed it because fixing it would have required agreeing on anything, and this family agreed on nothing except each other.

Paloma set them on the scarred oak counter to cool, and the smell rose up and filled the little room, honey and lemon and browned butter and something underneath, something fainter, like a note held under water—and Paloma's hands went still.

There it was again. That ache behind the sternum. That tooth-shaped sweetness.

They knew this smell. Not the cake—they'd invented the recipe that morning, more or less, working from instinct and whatever the market had—but the *smell*, the whole cloud of it, warm and specific, tied to a place. A big room. Stone floors, maybe, or wood worn smooth. Many people. Laughter that wasn't laughter exactly but something adjacent, the sound of people who were safe enough to be loud. A voice saying something kind. An oath, maybe? The word *oath* surfaced and sank like a coin dropped in dark water, and Paloma reached for it and caught nothing but the ripple.

"Paloma?" Sef was watching them. He watched them a great deal these days, with a patience that Paloma had decided not to examine too closely because examining it made their chest do things. "Are you all right?"

"I don't know," Paloma said honestly. "This smells like somewhere I've been."

"You've been lots of places."

"No." Paloma shook their head slowly. "Not places I've *been.* Somewhere I—" They searched for the word and couldn't find it, and the not-finding hurt worse than the ache itself. "Somewhere I *belonged.* Before. Somewhere I can't—"

They stopped. There was a rule, unspoken but absolute, that none of them named the thing they had paid. The toll. The memory. Whatever it had been—and Paloma had woken on the hillside knowing only that it had been large, that it had been everything, that the hollow where it used to live was shaped exactly like the shape of their life—it was gone, and digging at the empty socket only made them bleed.

But the smell. The smell slipped past the toll like a smuggler through a gate.

"Here." Hobb levered himself up and took the knife from Sef's hand, cutting the first cake into generous wedges with the steadiness of long practice. "Eat first. Think after. That's the whole of my philosophy and it's kept me alive past every man who disagreed with it."

He handed plates around. Marisol took hers with both hands, which she did sometimes, as if food were a living thing that might startle. Bramble ate standing up, back to the wall, out of habit, then caught themself and moved deliberately to sit on the bench beside Hobb, in the open, where anyone could see them. It was a small act of courage and everyone noticed and no one remarked on it, which was its own kind of grace.

Paloma took the last wedge and held it a moment, feeling the warmth of it through the plate.

Then they bit.

* * *

Honey. Lemon. Butter gone deep and nutty at the edges. Crumb soft as an apology.

And beneath it all, unmistakable now, undeniable: an echo. A vast and vanished echo, the way a shell holds the sea—not the sea itself, not anymore, but the proof that it had once been there, that something enormous had sounded against them and withdrawn. Paloma stood in the too-small kitchen with cake in their mouth and tears running silently down their face, tasting a home they could not name, could not picture, could not mourn properly because mourning requires a shape and theirs had been taken as payment.

The others had gone quiet. They knew. All of them knew what this was. They had each paid something at that crooked green gate—Hobb most of all, who would not say what, only that he slept better now, which frightened him—and they recognized the posture of a person meeting the ghost of their own price.

It was Marisol who moved first. She always did. She crossed the little kitchen and put her hand flat between Paloma's shoulder blades, right over the ache, and left it there, warm and heavy and real.

"Don't chase it," she said quietly. "Whatever it is. Chasing it will eat you. Believe me—I spent a month chasing mine and it led me in circles and I came out with nothing but wet boots."

"But I can almost—"

"Almost is the toll's favorite word." She squeezed. "Let it be a flavor. Flavors are allowed to stay."

Paloma swallowed the bite. Swallowed again. And then, because they were who they were—a taster, a survivor, a person built out of small controlled risks—they did the bravest thing available to them.

They took another bite.

And another. Standing in their own kitchen, in their own house, above their own street in a city that appeared on maps, in a world where strangers could see them and remember them and write their name down if they chose—they ate the whole slice, and the echo sang through every mouthful, grief and sweetness braided together so tightly they could not be separated, and Paloma understood, finally, that they were not going to get the home back.

And understood, in the same breath, that they were going to build one anyway.

* * *

"So," Paloma said, wiping their face with the heel of their hand, voice rough but steady. "This kitchen is too small."

"It's a closet with ambitions," Marisol agreed.

"The stove smokes. The counter has a burn ring. The window faces a tannery."

"A very scenic tannery," said Bramble.

"And the lease is month to month, and the rent is robbery, and the stairs will kill Hobb eventually."

"They'll have to catch me first," Hobb said placidly, refilling his tea.

"Which is why," Paloma went on, and here they made themselves look at each of them in turn, because this next part mattered and they wanted witnesses, "we're going to find a bigger one. With a proper hearth and a garden out back and a table that seats more than four. Because I intend to keep feeding all of you for a very long time, and I intend to teach whoever wants to learn, and I intend—" their voice caught, and they pushed through it, "—I intend for this to be a place where people come to be safe. Wherever we put it. Whatever we call it. I don't remember what safe used to feel like. But I know what it feels like now, and it feels like this room, and I want more rooms like it."

Silence. The good kind, the full kind.

Then Bramble, softly: "A school?"

"A kitchen," Paloma said. "That happens to teach. That happens to shelter. That happens to feed anyone who walks in hungry, no questions, no ledger." They glanced at Sef. "No books either. Well. Some books."

"One or two," Sef said, deadpan, and his eyes were shining.

"We'll need money," Marisol said, already scheming, already rolling up her sleeves in spirit.

"We'll need a building," Bramble said, already casing imaginary windows.

"We'll need a name," Hobb said, and smiled his rare, whole smile. "Every place worth keeping has a name."

They fell into it then, all of them talking at once, arguing happily about names and neighborhoods and whether Marisol could be trusted with the accounts (she could not) and whether Bramble could be trusted with the shopping (define *trusted*) and whether Hobb would finally consent to sit near the fire like a civilized person (he would not). Outside the little window the afternoon light came slantwise off the harbor, gilding the tannery roofs, touching the ordinary street where ordinary people walked, visible, remembered, real.

Paloma stood apart for one moment longer, one hand resting on the warm flank of the stove, and let themself feel it all: the echo of the nameless home singing low and constant under their ribs, and the new thing growing over it, root by root, meal by meal, person by person. Not a replacement. Nothing replaced it. But a second garden, planted in different soil, tended by the same hands.

*Come on, then,* they thought—to the echo, to the hollow, to whatever had been paid and whatever had been saved. *Watch this.*

They turned back to their family, picked up the knife, and began the second cake.

* * *

It was Sef who found it, later that night, after the dishes were done and the others had gone up to bed and the kitchen held only the two of them and the banked stove's orange breath.

He had brought the prophecy book down to show Paloma something—he'd been carrying it carefully for weeks, showing them things, patient with their inability to remember why the book mattered—and he had opened it to the newest pages, the ones that wrote themselves, the ones that had been blank since the hillside.

They weren't blank anymore.

"Sef?" Paloma said, because he had gone very still, ink-stained fingers hovering above the page. "What is it?"

Sef looked up at them across the little kitchen, and his expression was one Paloma had seen once before, long ago—or so they imagined, or so the ache suggested—in a sealed room full of old paper, wonder curdling into dread.

"New pages," he said quietly. "New dates." He turned the book around so Paloma could see the fresh ink still drying, still shining, still writing itself letter by letter at the bottom of the page. "And Paloma—there are other doors. Other valleys. Other people being erased right now."

The ink crawled on. Somewhere beyond the harbor, beyond the maps, something forgotten was waiting for someone willing to pay.

Paloma set down the knife.
