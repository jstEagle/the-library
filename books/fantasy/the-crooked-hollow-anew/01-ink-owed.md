## 1. Ink Owed

There are three hundred and eleven ways to be in debt in Crooked Hollow, and I have personally catalogued two hundred and ninety of them. The remaining twenty-one are the ones the Ledger Guild doesn't write down, which is how you know they're the expensive ones.

My own debt fit neatly into category one: money owed, principal and interest, to Odell Vane, guildmaster of the Ledger Guild of Crooked Hollow, keeper of impossible things. Fourteen years, four months, and — I checked the column twice, because hope makes sloppy arithmetic — nine days since he bought my family's ruin off a magistrate's desk for the price of a modest house and turned it into the price of my entire life.

I was tallying it again when the thunderclap escaped.

* * *

The vault beneath the guildhall holds the inventory nobody else will insure: the bottled weather, the bottled griefs, the assorted miracles that arrive in this city the way cats arrive at fishmongers' doors — uninvited, yowling, and eventually someone's problem. My problem, specifically, because I am the only scribe with steady enough hands and dull enough dreams to count them.

Item forty-one, shelf six: one thunderclap, bottled, provenance unknown, acquired from a fisherman who swore it fell out of a clear sky over the canal and into his net. It sat in its stoppered green glass like a fistful of grey wool, humming faintly, the way a sleeping dog hums before it bites.

I had just written *condition: stable* when the cork decided otherwise.

The sound it made was not loud so much as *total*. For one full second the vault contained nothing but noise — my bones were percussion instruments, the inkwell jumped a full inch off the desk, and somewhere above us a clerk screamed, then apologized to no one. Then silence rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

I picked myself up off the floor, retrieved my pen from behind a jar of preserved moonlight (item twelve; do not shake), and wrote:

*Condition: less stable than previously recorded.*

That's the job, really. That's all of it. The world produces things that shouldn't exist, and the Guild writes them down so that they continue to, because in Crooked Hollow an unwritten thing has a way of unwriting itself. We are not merchants. We are not magicians. We are bookkeepers for the impossible, and we are very, very good at pretending that's a respectable profession.

I am good at pretending generally. Fourteen years of practice.

* * *

At midday I climbed the stairs to the counting room, where the debt lives.

It's a real ledger, my debt. Bound in grey calfskin, ruled in Odell's own hand fourteen years ago, updated quarterly in mine. *Mara Quintana. Orphaned by fire, aged eleven. Debt assumed: 840 crowns, 12 shillings.* And then the interest, patient as rot, line after line after line, year after year, so that the number at the bottom has never once gone down. Not once. Not even the winter I worked nineteen-hour days transcribing shipping manifests until my fingers cracked and bled onto the vellum, and Odell docked me for staining the pages.

I don't hate him. That's the strange part, the part I've stopped examining too closely, the way you stop probing a tooth that aches only sometimes. He fed me. He taught me letters and ledgers and the difference between a debt and a punishment, though lately I've begun to suspect there isn't one. He called me *his best investment* once, at a guild dinner, laughing, and everyone laughed, and I laughed too, because what else do investments do?

The counting room smelled of tallow and cold tea. Odell sat behind his desk like a stone that had learned to wear waistcoats — broad, grey, immovable, with small sharp eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. He was fifty-something or sixty-something; he had been both for as long as I'd known him.

"Quarterly audit," I said, setting the grey ledger on his desk. "You'll want to initial page—"

"Page ninety-one. Yes." He didn't look up from the letter he was sealing. "And the vault?"

"The thunderclap discharged."

His pen paused. "Anyone hurt?"

"My dignity. Slightly singed."

"Add it to your expenses." Which was a joke, because my expenses went on the same side of the ledger as everything else — the wrong side. He signed the seal, pressed it, and finally looked at me with those eyes that had watched me grow from a soot-streaked child into a woman who could smell an arithmetic error through a closed door. "Anything else in the audit give you trouble?"

"No," I said.

Which was true, right up until three hours later, when it wasn't.

* * *

The routine part of the quarterly audit is simple: verify the impossible inventory against the master catalogue, verify the debts against the interest schedules, verify the seals against the seal registry. I have done it forty times. I could do it drunk, blindfolded, or dead, and some mornings I suspect I have done all three.

The folios live in the long gallery behind the counting room — shelves of grey and black bindings going back further than the Hollow's charter, further than the kingdom's, if you believe the dates inside them, and I did believe the dates inside them because believing dates is my whole personality.

I was working through the sealed section. Sealed folios aren't forbidden, exactly; they're just expensive to access, requiring a guildmaster's countersignature, which is a way of saying forbidden with extra steps. But audits require checking the seals themselves, and checking a seal means holding the book, and holding a book means feeling whether its weight matches its entry in the catalogue.

Folio V-9 was light.

Not dramatically. Not the weight of missing pages — a razored block would announce itself, would make the covers sag. This was subtler. This was the weight of a book whose binding had been re-sewn, expertly, decades ago, by someone who knew exactly how much thread to pull to keep the spine honest.

I set it down. I picked it up again. I told myself I was imagining it, and then I opened it, because I have never once in my life successfully told myself anything.

The entry was dated in our standard hand — guild cipher, the numbers clean, the abbreviations correct. But the *content*, the description of the item received, was written in no alphabet I know.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the one thing I own outright. I know the kingdom's script and its predecessor. I know the merchant shorthand of the canal trade, the smugglers' knot-code, the temple script the priests use to bill miracles. I know the old coastal runes well enough to read a tombstone. This was none of them. The letters were tall and narrow and leaned together like people sheltering from rain, and they moved when I looked directly at them — not physically, I don't mean physically, I mean my eyes kept sliding off them the way they slide off a word you've read ten thousand times until suddenly it looks like scratches.

Except they weren't scratches. They were *writing*. Someone had sat at a desk like mine, with a pen like mine, and meant something, and the meaning was locked behind a door in my own head.

And here is the detail that took the floor out from under me: the entry was dated. Dated in ordinary numerals, in our ordinary system. And the date was *impossible*. Because the date was from a year that does not appear anywhere else in the catalogue — a gap, a jump, the kind of thing I'd noticed years ago and filed under *scribal error* because the alternative was worse.

There were other entries in the same hand. Dozens. All in that year. All in that leaning, rain-sheltering alphabet. And then the next folio began, in ordinary script, as if nothing had ever been written in any other.

I stood in the long gallery with the lamp burning low and my heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional, and I did the thing I always do when the ground moves: I reached for my pen.

I copied one line of the strange alphabet into my pocket notebook. Just one. A scribe's instinct — if you cannot understand a thing, record it, and understanding can come later, or someone else can come later, or at minimum the thing cannot claim it was never there.

Then I heard the floorboard at the end of the gallery creak, and I closed the folio with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a cell door.

Odell stood at the end of the shelves with a lamp of his own, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to read what crossed his face in that half-second before he arranged it into blandness. Not anger. Anger I could have handled. It was something older and tireder — the look of a man finding a door open that he had spent a very long time making sure stayed shut.

"Quintana," he said. "Audit running late?"

"Folio V-9," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The binding's been re-sewn. Pages are missing. That's a catalogue discrepancy. It goes in the report."

A long pause. Rain started against the high windows, soft as ash.

"Put it in the report," he said at last. "Note the discrepancy. Note that it predates current management." And he turned and walked away down the gallery, and his lamp left a moving stripe of gold along the spines of all those grey books, and every one of them suddenly looked like it was keeping a secret.

I wrote the discrepancy in the report. I wrote it in my neatest hand, the hand he taught me, the hand he owns along with everything else.

Then I sat alone in the gallery after he'd gone, with the rain coming down and the strange copied line glowing up at me from my notebook like a coal, and I thought about the twenty-one kinds of debt the Guild doesn't write down.

I think I may have found number twenty-two.

Because here is what I couldn't stop noticing, in the end, when I finally let myself notice it: the year those impossible entries were written — the year that doesn't exist anywhere else in the catalogue — is the same year, to the season, that Odell Vane bought a ruined family's debts off a magistrate's desk and acquired himself a daughter-shaped investment.

And tomorrow night, if the rain keeps up the way the canal-men swear it will, the Guild opens its doors for the autumn intake, and anything — anyone — washed down from upstream comes knocking at ours first.

I intend to be the one who answers.
