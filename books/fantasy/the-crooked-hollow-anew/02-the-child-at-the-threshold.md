## 2. The Child at the Threshold

The rain came down on Crooked Hollow like a debt collector—relentless, impersonal, and arriving exactly when you could least afford it.

I was in the counting room, which is what the guild called the long chamber on the second floor where we tallied things that had no business being tallied. That night's inventory included a bottled thunderclap (item 447, one of three, do not shake), a memory suspended in amber that hummed when you held it too long, and a jar of fog that, according to its acquisition ledger, had been purchased from a drowned man for the price of a funeral. I wrote *condition: stable* next to each entry because that was my job. The truth was more complicated. Nothing in this building was stable. Stability was just a story we told the ink.

My candle guttered. Outside, the storm had teeth.

"You're still here." Odell Vane stood in the doorway with his coat already buttoned, silver at his temples dark with rain. My guildmaster. My creditor. The man who had, eleven years ago, bought the paper that said I belonged to him until such time as my family's ruin was paid off—at current interest rates, roughly never. "Go home, Quintana."

"This is home," I said, and didn't look up from the ledger. It was easier to say it to the page than to his face.

He made the sound he always made—a short exhale through the nose that managed to be both fond and dismissive—and left. I heard his boots on the stairs, heard the front bolts drawn, and then I was alone with the ledgers and the storm and the particular silence of a building full of impossible things pretending to sleep.

That was when the knocking started.

* * *

It came from the main doors, three floors below, and it was small. That's the only word for it. Not weak—small. The kind of knock a person makes when they've already decided no one will answer and are knocking anyway because stopping would mean admitting it.

I should have ignored it. Guild policy was explicit: after hours, all inquiries were referred to the wardens, and the wardens' policy was explicit in turn: nothing that knocked after midnight got in, on the principle that anything desperate enough to knock after midnight was dangerous enough to be worth keeping out. I had watched them turn away beggars, madmen, a woman who claimed her shadow had been stolen and wanted it appraised. I had watched and said nothing, because saying nothing was what my debt cost, and I paid it daily like rent.

But the knock was small, and the rain was loud, and something in me—the part I kept locked in the counting room with the bottled thunderclap—got up before I did.

The main hall was black except for the seal-glass window above the doors, where lightning kept flashing the guild's sigil onto the floor: a circle broken by a crooked line. Every time the thunder rolled, the light shivered and reassembled itself, patient as arithmetic.

I opened the wicket gate in the door's iron grille.

A girl stood on the step. Eight years old, perhaps, though hunger ages children unpredictably. She was soaked to the skin, barefoot, and she looked up at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the flat, practiced patience of someone who has learned that hope is a luxury item.

"Guild's closed," I said. "Come back—" To where? In daylight? In this? I heard how it sounded and hated myself for it.

She answered.

Not in Hollow Cant, not in High Tongue, not in any of the six languages I can read or the four I can fake. The sounds came out of her rounded and strange, vowels held long, consonants soft as river stones, and they meant nothing to me and everything to her. She said maybe ten words. Her voice cracked on two of them. Then she held up her hand.

In her palm was a scale. A dragon's scale, broad as my thumbnail, black shot through with veins of deep red—and it glowed. Not reflected the lightning. Glowed. From inside. Like a coal that had decided against going out.

Every hair on my arms stood up. I have handled impossible inventory for eleven years. I have catalogued relics that whispered and keys that bit. But this was different, because the scale wasn't an object sitting in a girl's hand. It was answering her. As her fingers trembled with cold, the glow pulsed faster, steadier, like a heart trying to warm her from the outside.

"Where did you get that?" I asked.

More words in the old-sounding tongue. She pointed at the scale, then at herself, then—this is the part I keep returning to—she pointed past me, into the hall, at the seal-glass window, and said one word with unmistakable emphasis. The same syllable twice, rising.

Lightning flashed. The sigil blazed on the floor between us, the broken circle, the crooked line.

She was pointing at it. And whatever she was saying, she was saying it like a name.

* * *

"Wardens!" My voice came out rougher than intended. "Wardens, there's—"

Hask and Merrin came down the night stair with lanterns and the particular slowness of men who resent being needed. Hask was the taller; Merrin was the one who'd once turned out the woman with the stolen shadow. They looked at the girl through the grille the way you look at weather.

"Street child," Hask said. "Storm'll take her or it won't."

"She's hurt," I said. She wasn't, visibly. But her feet were bleeding, and I'd decided this counted.

"Mara." Merrin used my name the way Odell used it—correctly, distantly, a label on a drawer. "You know the rule."

"I know the rule." I knew every rule in this building. Rules were load-bearing walls in the architecture of my debt. "I'm asking about the scale."

They looked. To their credit, they actually looked. The glow had settled into a slow ember-pulse now, and in its light the girl's face was very calm, very tired, and very much not going anywhere.

"Catch-trade," Merrin said finally. "Some canal peddler's trinket. Turn her out, Quintana. Vane doesn't pay us to adopt strays."

"He doesn't pay me at all," I said, "he lends to me," and before I fully understood what my hands were doing, I had unhooked the wicket's latch and pulled the door open six inches, enough for cold wet air to pour through, enough for a child to fit if a child were small and determined and had already begun to move.

Hask grabbed my shoulder. "Quintana—"

"She'll freeze." Stupid reason. There were a hundred shelters in the Hollow. "She spoke to the seal." Worse reason. "Look at the scale." Worst reason of all, because looking at the scale was the one thing I couldn't stop doing, and the longer I looked, the more certain I became that I had seen its shape before—not the object, but the curve of it, the specific arc—in the marginalia of folios so old their chains had rusted. Sealed folios. Folios I was not permitted to open.

The girl slipped through the gap like water finding a crack. She stood in our hall, dripping on four hundred years of imported marble, and looked up at the sigil window and said her doubled syllable again, softly this time, the way you'd say a prayer you weren't sure was still listening.

And the seal-glass—old glass, warded glass, glass that had shrugged off lightning and worse—flickered. Just once. Like a lamp catching a draft.

Merrin swore in three dialects.

"That's not catch-trade," Hask said quietly.

"No," I agreed. My heart was doing something unsanctioned. "It isn't."

* * *

We put her in the receiving room because it was closest and had a fire, and by "we" I mean I carried her, because her legs had finally admitted what the rest of her had been denying for miles. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and rain and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like the air before a storm breaks somewhere far away.

Merrin fetched blankets without being asked, which told me he was more frightened than he wanted anyone to know. Frightened men follow rules. Terrified ones make exceptions.

"What do I write?" I asked Hask, pen already in hand, because ritual is how I keep my hands steady. "Incident log. There has to be an entry."

Hask stared at the fire, at the girl, at me. "Write 'foundling, temporary custody, pending guildmaster review.' And Mara—" he lowered his voice, glanced at the sleeping shape under the blanket, small and breathing and glowing faintly at the hand, "—write it small. Some entries are better found late."

That was the first honest thing a warden had ever said to me.

I wrote the entry. Foundling, temporary custody, pending guildmaster review. Small. And then, because I am a scribe down to the marrow and a scribe records what is, I added a line I probably shouldn't have:

*Subject speaks unknown language. Initial phonetics consistent with folios sealed under writ, ref. pre-kingdom holdings.*

I sat back and read it and felt the first crack go through me—not loudly, not dramatically, just a fine hairline fracture running through eleven years of quiet compliance. Because here is what I realized, sitting in that warm room while the storm tried to get in and failed: I had spent my whole tenure in this guild making myself invisible. Invisible scribes don't ask questions. Invisible scribes tally the thunderclaps and the amber memories and the jarred fog, and they pay their interest, and they survive. It was a good strategy. It had kept me alive.

And tonight, for a child whose name I didn't know, in a language older than any kingdom, I had opened a door I had no authority to open, in front of witnesses, and written the evidence down in my own hand.

Odell Vane would see that entry by morning. He would see the date, the hour, my signature. And I found—as I sat there watching the coal-light pulse in a sleeping child's fist—that I could not make myself regret it.

The girl stirred. Opened her eyes. Looked straight at me across the dying fire, and said the doubled syllable again—but this time she pointed at me when she said it.

At me. Not the seal. Me.

I don't know what she named me. But the scale flared when she said it, bright as a struck match, and somewhere far beneath the Hollow—deeper than the cisterns, deeper than the foundations, in a dark I had never once thought to wonder about—something answered.
