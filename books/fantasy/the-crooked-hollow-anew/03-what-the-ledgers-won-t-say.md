## 3. What the Ledgers Won't Say

There are three kinds of silence in the Ledger Guild of Crooked Hollow, and I have learned to tell them apart the way a sailor tells weather by the taste of the wind.

The first is the silence of work: quills scratching, abacuses clicking, forty scribes breathing in unison over their columns. It's not really silence at all, but it functions as one, and after eleven years I find it more restful than actual quiet.

The second is Odell Vane's silence, which is a held breath. When the guildmaster goes quiet, it means he is weighing something—usually money, occasionally mercy—and whichever way the scale tips, someone in the Hollow will feel it.

The third kind I had never heard before the night the child came to our door.

It was the silence of a folio that should have had words on its pages and didn't.

* * *

I want to be clear about something: I did not set out that morning intending to commit a crime. I set out intending to do my job, which is a different thing entirely, though I grant the border between them has always been drawn in pencil.

My job, as senior tally-scribe of the Impossible Inventory, was to reconcile the guild's holdings against the master rolls. The Impossible Inventory is exactly what it sounds like—the things Crooked Hollow trades that no sane market would price. The bottled thunderclap from my first week. A memory sealed in amber, which hums when you hold it up to your ear like a shell, though what it hums depends on who's listening; Bramble swears his sings a drinking song, and I've never had the heart to tell him mine weeps. A jar of last year's fog, sold three times and returned four. A key that fits no lock yet, which Odell keeps wrapped in oilcloth because, he says, keys are promises and this one hasn't been made yet.

Every item gets an entry. Every entry gets a folio reference. And every folio reference must resolve, or the audit fails, and if the audit fails, the Crown's assessors descend on us like white-collared locusts, and the guild bleeds fees until we're all working for free.

So when entry 4471—a vial of riverwater that flows uphill, acquired from a drowned man who wouldn't say where he'd drowned—pointed me to Folio Vess-9, and Folio Vess-9 turned out to be *missing*, I did what any competent scribe would do.

I went looking for it.

* * *

The sealed archive sits beneath the counting floor, down a stair that smells of cold stone and older ink. Access requires the guildmaster's seal, which is why the door has a lock shaped like a closed mouth—I've always assumed some long-dead locksmith had a sense of humor—and why I stood in front of it for a full quarter hour telling myself stories.

I was checking a cross-reference. Scribes check cross-references. If anyone asked, and no one would ask, because no one ever asks the tally-scribe anything beyond *does it balance*, I was performing due diligence.

The lie fit so comfortably I almost believed it myself, which is how you know you've been poor a long time. Comfortable lies are a luxury. I've only ever been able to afford uncomfortable ones.

The lock took my hairpin, a prayer to no particular god, and eleven years of watching Odell's hands. He turns the key left, then right, then presses his thumb below the keyhole where there's a mechanism I've never seen but have inferred from the way he pauses, the way a man pauses before saying a name he shouldn't.

Left. Right. Press.

The mouth opened.

* * *

Inside, the sealed archive is smaller than rumor makes it. Rumor gives it cathedral proportions; reality gives it perhaps thirty feet of shelving, dim lamplight, and dust with a geological patience about being disturbed. The folios stand in ranks, spines lettered in a hand I recognized immediately, because I've spent half my life trying to copy it and failing: Odell's early script, back when his letters still reached for something.

Vess-1 through Vess-40. Forty folios under a single designation, which was itself strange—the Impossible Inventory sprawls across hundreds of designations, each item lovingly categorized. One designation holding forty volumes meant one subject mattered enough to be systematic about.

I pulled Vess-9's neighbor, Vess-8, just to orient myself. Sealed folios are supposed to be catalogued even if their contents aren't readable. I opened it expecting inventory entries, trade records, something.

What I got was a page that began mid-word—*"...and the bells rang for three days, and the water in the canals ran sweet, and every child born that season was said to carry the light of"*—and then stopped.

Not ended. Stopped. The page after it was blank at the top third and then resumed, mid-sentence again, with text in an alphabet I had never seen in eleven years of handling every script that passes through Crooked Hollow. Curved letters, upright letters, letters that looked like they'd been grown rather than written, like ivy trained along a trellis.

And between them, the ghost of what had been removed. You can't razor pages out of a ledger without leaving evidence. The stubs remain at the binding, thin as fingernail parings. The blade had been sharp and the hand patient: whoever cut these pages had done it slowly, one at a time, so the knife wouldn't jump or tear. This wasn't vandalism. This was surgery.

I checked Vess-7. Cut. Vess-6. Cut. I pulled them in a run, faster now, my breath loud in the little room, and found the same wound repeated—decades of it. Some gaps were single pages. Some were whole gatherings, entire signatures sliced away, leaving the surviving text like a man standing in a doorway missing the wall around him. The earliest cuts were cruder; the later ones elegant. Someone had gotten better at it over time. Someone had practiced.

Do you know what that means? It means it wasn't one terrible night. It was a career. Years of returning to these shelves with a blade, keeping pace with whatever kept needing to disappear.

I sat down on the floor among the opened folios, which is against about six guild regulations, and did the arithmetic I'm built for. Forty folios. Conservatively two hundred pages cut per volume across the run. Four thousand pages gone. Four thousand pages of a history that began mid-word about bells ringing and children carrying light, and somewhere out there, a girl had stood in our doorway speaking letters like ivy, and the word she'd used for herself—

*Ashen-vess.* Ash of Vess.

I hadn't understood it then. I understood it now, sitting in the dust with the ghosts of pages under my hands.

* * *

Here is the thing about dread: it doesn't arrive all at once. It arrives the way water finds a cellar—through cracks you didn't know the foundation had, quietly, while you're busy with other things, until one morning your boots are soaked and you can't point to the moment it started.

I put the folios back. I aligned the spines. I wiped my fingerprints from the dust with my sleeve, which fooled no one, least of all the dust. And I climbed the stairs rehearsing innocence, which is a performance I've never had talent for, because my face has always been better at numbers than at lies.

Odell was waiting at the top of the stairs.

He was standing at his desk with a lamp turned low, going through the day's tallies, and he did not look up when I emerged. That was the first wrong thing. Odell always looks up. Odell notices everything—that's the whole architecture of him, a man built entirely of noticing. Eleven years, and he'd caught me miscounting by a single copper from across a room.

"Master Vane," I said. "The audit reconciliation. Entry 4471 references a folio that appears to be—" I chose the word carefully, the way you choose footing on ice, "—misfiled."

"Misfiled," he repeated.

"Or damaged. Or—" *Razored out by the yard,* said the part of my brain that had never once been useful at moments like this. "In need of restoration."

He set down his pen. He looked at me then, and his face was doing something I had no column for. Not anger. Not the weary disappointment he saves for clerks who salt the books. Something older and tireder than either, the look of a man who has been waiting for this exact conversation for years and hoped, against all his ledgers, never to have it.

"How much did you see?" he asked.

And here is where I should tell you I lied brilliantly. Here is where the clever scribe, the woman who can forge a bishop's signature well enough to fool the bishop, deploys her gifts.

"I saw a gap," I said. My voice came out small. "In Folio Vess-8. Pages cut. Decades of them, by the look of the bindings."

All of it. I told him all of it, standing in the lamplight with dust still on my sleeve, because apparently my loyalty has a crack in it too, and the truth leaks out through it whether I consent or not.

Odell was quiet for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, the evening shift packed up, chairs scraping, voices fading, the guild emptying itself toward supper and the rain that had started again against the windows.

"The child," he said finally. "Where is she sleeping?"

The question landed sideways, and it took me a moment to understand it was an answer. "The west loft. With Marta watching the door."

"And the scale she carries?"

"In my strongbox. Wrapped in wool." I hesitated. "It's warm. It's been warm since she gave it to me, and strongboxes aren't supposed to—"

"I know what it is." He said it flatly, without heat, and somehow that was worse than shouting. He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment—just a moment—he looked less like the guildmaster of Crooked Hollow and more like a very old man standing in front of shelves full of things he'd cut out himself. "Mara. Listen to me. There are entries in this guild that exist only so that other entries can be forgotten. The gaps are not damage. The gaps are the record."

"That's not how records work."

"No," he agreed softly. "It isn't. It's how erasure works, and erasure is also a kind of bookkeeping. The most expensive kind." He straightened, and the guildmaster reassembled himself around the tired old man like armor going on plate by plate. "You found nothing tonight. The folio was misfiled; you corrected the error; the audit stands. Do you understand me?"

I understood that he was asking me to lie in the exact place where I'd just confessed the truth, and that the request was so perfectly circular it might have been designed by an accountant.

"What was Vessia?" I asked.

His hand stopped on the lamp key. Just stopped, mid-motion, the way a heart stops in the bad ballads.

"I don't know what you mean," said Odell Vane, who had taught me my letters, who had bought my debt off a slaver's clerk when I was fourteen and never once mentioned it, who had raised me on double-entry and honesty and the guild's one commandment: *the record is sacred.*

"You flinched," I said. "That's how I know."

For a moment I thought he would tell me. His mouth opened. Whatever lived behind his teeth pressed against them like a prisoner at a gate.

"Go home, Mara," he said instead. "Sleep. Tomorrow we count what can be counted."

I went. Of course I went. What else does a debt-bound scribe do when the man who owns her future asks her, gently, to walk away?

But at the door I looked back, and he was still standing there in the low lamplight, and he had taken down Folio Vess-1, the oldest of them, and he was holding it against his chest the way you hold a thing you're deciding whether to bury or to burn.

* * *

I did not go home.

I went to the west loft instead, where Ash lay curled under Marta's spare blanket, small as a comma in the great sentence of the room. The scale sat warm in my pocket. She was awake—I could tell by her breathing, the careful shallow rhythm of a child pretending—and when I sat beside her, she opened her eyes and looked at me with the patience of someone who has already lost everything and is simply waiting to learn what's next.

"Ashen-vess," I said, the only words of hers I knew.

Her face changed. Not a smile—something before smiles, something older. She reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out a second scale, twin to the one in my strongbox, and pressed it into my palm. Warm as a held hand. Then she pointed at the floor beneath us—at the guild, at the stone, at whatever lay underneath the Hollow's foundations—and said a word I didn't know, but said it the way you say *home*.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard Odell's footsteps cross the counting floor toward the sealed archive stair.

Going down. Again. Tonight.

I closed my hand around the scale, and it pulsed once against my palm, like a heartbeat answering a question I hadn't asked aloud yet:

*What is buried under the guild?*

Tomorrow, I decided, I would stop counting what can be counted.
