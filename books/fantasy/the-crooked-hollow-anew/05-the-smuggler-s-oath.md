## 5. The Smuggler's Oath

There are three rules to running cargo on the Hollow's canals, and I've broken all of them, but never on the same night.

Rule one: don't carry anything that breathes. Rule two: don't carry anything that glows. Rule three: don't let Mara Quintana talk you into breaking the first two.

I want it noted for posterity that I held out for nearly four minutes.

* * *

She came to my boat at the low tide moorings under Tannery Bridge, in that hour when the fog comes off the water thick enough to carve your initials in, and she had a bundle wrapped in her own coat slung against her hip like a sack of contraband flour. I knew before she said a word. You spend enough years moving things that shouldn't be moved, you develop a nose for it. The bundle was breathing. The bundle was also, judging by the faint orange seep of light through wool, glowing.

"Bramble," she said.

"Mara."

"I need a favor."

"Those are two words that have historically cost me money, blood, or both."

"This one might cost more."

And there it was — the thing about Mara Quintana that nobody at the Ledger Guild ever seems to notice, because she's made herself into furniture over there, quiet as a filing cabinet. She doesn't lie. Not because she's too good for it, but because she thinks lying is inefficient. So when she says *this one might cost more*, what she means is: *run, you idiot*, and what I hear — because I'm an even bigger idiot than she gives me credit for — is *stay*.

"Show me," I said.

She unwrapped the coat.

The child was maybe eight years old, asleep or pretending to be, with soot-grey hair cropped short and a smudge of something dark across her cheekbone that wasn't dirt. In her cupped hands, even sleeping, she held a scale. A dragon's scale, if you'll believe me, though I didn't yet, big as a playing card and glowing from within like a coal banked for the night — that deep patient orange that says *I am not out, I am only waiting.*

"She turned up at the guild door three nights ago," Mara said. "Speaking a language nobody knows. Except—" she hesitated, and Mara Quintana hesitating is like the canal freezing in high summer; it means something underneath has stopped flowing right, "—except it turns out somebody knew it once. Long enough to write it down. And then long enough to cut it back out again."

"Cut it out of what?"

"The ledgers." She said it flat, the way you'd say *the roof leaks* or *the bread's gone stale*. "Decades of pages, razored out. Somebody wanted a whole country forgotten, Bramble, and they used my guild's books to do it."

I looked at the child. The child opened her eyes.

I've been looked at by a lot of things in my line of work: customs officers, knife-happy dock bosses, one customs officer who was also a knife-happy dock boss. None of them looked at me the way this girl did. She wasn't afraid. That was the unsettling part. She looked at me the way you look at weather — taking my measure, deciding whether I was rain or sun. Then she said something in that language of hers, three syllables, soft as a question, and the scale in her hands brightened like it agreed with whatever she'd asked.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"I don't know. But Odell Vane went white as chalk when he heard it, and Odell Vane doesn't go white. He goes *careful*. There's a difference, and I've spent eleven years learning it."

"So the guildmaster knows what she is."

"The guildmaster knows what she *was*. Past tense. That's rather the whole problem." Mara shifted the girl higher on her hip — the girl let her, which told me something too; children don't lean on people they don't trust, and this one leaned like Mara was load-bearing. "The wardens want her turned out. Renn's Inquisitors are coming — grey coats at the gate, inspection of every record, every room. If they find her in the guild, they find her. If they find her anywhere in the Hollow, they find her. She needs to be somewhere that isn't anywhere."

"And you thought of me." I put a hand over my heart. "Mara, I'm touched. Also insulted. Mostly touched."

"You're the only person I know who can make a thing disappear on the water and bring it back when it's safe."

"That's rule one I'd be breaking. No live cargo."

"I know."

"And rule two. Nothing that glows."

"I know."

"Rule three?"

She almost smiled. It was a small, tired, real smile, the kind you only get from people who've watched you do something stupid and loved you anyway. "Rule three is mine. Don't let Mara Quintana talk you into anything."

"Well," I said, and looked at the scale, which pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat in a warm room, "you haven't talked me into anything yet. Let's see how long you keep that streak."

* * *

Here's what people get wrong about smugglers, mostly because we encourage them to get it wrong. They think we're in it for the coin. Coin's fine. Coin buys rope and rum and keeps the boat's hull patched. But nobody risks the noose year after year for coin. You can make coin honestly, if you're willing to be bored to death doing it.

No — what keeps a smuggler smuggling is the same thing that keeps anyone at any crooked trade: the conviction that the rules are wrong. That somewhere up the chain, some fat ledger-keeper decided what counted as legal, and his decision served him, and everybody downstream just swallowed it. Every smuggler I've ever known is running a private argument against somebody else's ledger. We just express it in cargo.

So when Mara sat in my cabin that night, drinking tea I watered down because she'd only nurse it anyway, and laid it out — the erased country, the razored pages, the Inquisitors coming to make the erasure permanent — I didn't need convincing. What I needed was to know she believed it. Because Mara believing a thing is rarer than dragon's scales, and worth more.

"You've seen the gaps yourself?" I asked.

"With my own eyes. Whole years missing. And the stitching where pages were removed is old, Bramble. This wasn't done last month. It's been sitting in our shelves since before I could write, and everyone who might have remembered why is either dead or very carefully not talking."

"Including Vane."

"Especially Vane." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "That's the part I can't — he raised me. Bought my family's debt when there was nothing left of us to buy. Gave me a desk and a trade and a place to stand. And now I'm sneaking around his own archive behind his back, and I don't know if I'm betraying him or finally understanding him, and those feel horrifyingly similar from the inside."

I let that sit. Some things you don't answer; you just keep the tea hot and let a person hear themselves.

Then the curtain twitched and the girl came out from the bunk where I'd stowed her, dragging the blanket like a small ghost, and stood between us looking from Mara to me and back with the grave patience of someone deciding whether these two adults were going to be adequate.

"Ash," Mara said softly. "It's all right. This is Bramble. He's — " she paused, hunting for a word, "— a friend."

"*Friend.*" The girl tried the word out. Her accent was strange, vowels shaped wrong, like a song transposed into a key it wasn't written for. "Frend. Bramble-frend."

"That's me," I said. "Bramble-frend, at your service. And you're Ash?"

"Mara named me that," she said, and then, with the terrible precision of children, "because of the fire. Everything I come from is ash now. But ash is what's left after, so it's not nothing."

I set down my cup.

Eight years old, maybe. Maybe six. Saying *everything I come from is ash now* the way you'd say *it's Tuesday*. And the scale in her fist glowing steady, steady, like it was keeping time with something enormous and far away.

"Well," I said, when I trusted my voice, "Ash. First lesson of the canals: anybody who feeds the boat's cat outranks me. His name is Admiral, he's a liar, and he'll pretend he hasn't eaten. Second lesson: belowdecks there's a false floor under the cargo hold. Under that there's a space big enough for a girl and her — " I nodded at the scale, "— her coal. Nobody looks under a smuggler's floor. It's the one courtesy of the trade."

She considered all this with great seriousness. Then she climbed onto the bench beside me, held up the scale, and pressed it briefly against my knuckles. Warm. Warm as a hand.

"Frend," she said again, decisively, like a judge striking a gavel, and that was that. I was sworn in. I didn't recall the oath having words, but oaths rarely do. The real ones are just moments you can't take back, and I felt this one settle into my chest like ballast.

* * *

But I'm a businessman, even a crooked one, and I had one condition. Not coin — I told her to keep her guild scrip, it spends badly outside the walls anyway. One truth. From the ledgers.

"One truth," Mara repeated.

"You say there's a country that got cut out of the books. Fine. I believe you believe it. But I've got a hull full of honest contraband and a reputation built on knowing exactly what I'm carrying and why. So here's my price: when you crack those razored gaps open — and you will, because you've got the face of a woman about to do something magnificent and illegal — you tell me one true thing out of them. Anything. A name, a street, a reason. One truth from the erased country, freely given, no guild seal on it. That's my fee."

"Why?" Ash asked. Children always ask why, and adults always flinch, and I've decided the children are onto something.

I looked at the lantern, because it's easier than looking at people when you're being honest. "My gran used to sing," I said. "Songs nobody else sang. Lullabies with words that weren't Crown-tongue. When I asked her where they were from she'd say *from before*, and change the subject, and pour another cup. She died when I was twelve, and I've spent twenty years telling myself *from before* meant nothing. Now a child turns up speaking a language older than any kingdom, and I'm wondering if my gran was singing to a country that somebody murdered, and I never even asked her properly." I shrugged, aiming for careless and probably missing. "One truth. That's cheap, Mara. You know it's cheap. I'd do it for free and we both know that too, but a smuggler's got a brand to maintain."

Mara reached across the little table and gripped my wrist, hard, the way she does — like she's checking a pulse, like she needs proof you're still there. "One truth," she said. "Sworn."

"Sworn," I said. "Now get some sleep. Both of you. Tide turns at dawn and I know a stretch of dead water behind the salt warehouses where the wardens' patrol logs have a gap exactly the size of my boat."

* * *

We ran the dead water for six nights. Six nights of drifting with the lamp doused, listening to the grey coats' boots on the warehouse quay overhead, Ash learning card games in the cabin and beating me at most of them through what I can only describe as cheating of unusual purity — she hadn't learned the rules well enough to know she was breaking them, and somehow that made it worse.

On the sixth night she came up on deck while I kept the sweep gentle, stood at the rail, and pointed at the black water.

"Bramble-frend," she said. "Under here. Streets."

"Just cisterns and drowned foundations, little ember. Old city sank a long time back."

"No." She shook her head, and the scale came out of her shirt and lit the whole deck orange, and for one heartbeat — one — I swear on the Admiral's nine lives I saw it. Down through the black water: a street. Cobbles. A wall with letters cut into it in that alphabet older than kingdoms. Lamplight, or something like lamplight, far down, the way a town looks from a hill at dusk.

Then it was water again, plain dumb water, and my heart was going like a drum in a parade.

"They're still there," Ash said quietly. "They're waiting to be remembered. That's what the fire does. It remembers." She looked up at me, and her eyes reflected the scale's glow, and she smiled — a small, old, terrible smile. "You gave an oath, frend. One truth. I think the country wants to pay its debts early."

I stood at the tiller of my crooked little boat on a dead-black canal, holding cargo that breathed and glowed, three rules broken clean through, and realized I wasn't frightened anymore. That should have frightened me.

Because at dawn, when I poled us back toward the tannery moorings, there was a figure waiting on the bridge. Grey coat. Grey hat. Standing very still in the fog like a nail someone had hammered into the morning.

Mara saw him the same moment I did. Her hand found my sleeve.

"That's Renn," she whispered. "Inquisitor Halvard Renn. He's early."

Belowdecks, the scale flared once — bright, alarmed, awake — and went dark as a closed eye.
