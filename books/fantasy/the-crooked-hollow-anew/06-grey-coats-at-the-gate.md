## 6. Grey Coats at the Gate

The knock came at dawn, which was the first wrong thing about it. Nobody knocks on the Ledger Guild's door at dawn. Debtors come at dusk, ashamed and quiet. Merchants come at midday, loud and smelling of pepper. And the wardens who collect for the Crown come whenever they please, because that is what power means: never having to check the hour.

But dawn, with three sharp raps and no voice behind them — that was new.

I was already awake. I had been awake since the fourth bell of the night, when Ash had whimpered in her sleep under the floorboards and I'd gone down with a candle to stroke her hair until she settled, murmuring nonsense syllables of a language I couldn't speak but was beginning, against all my training, to understand by feel alone. The girl slept like someone who had once slept badly for a very long time and was making up for it now, all at once, in a cellar beneath a guild of liars.

So when the knock came, I was standing at the counting table with ink on my fingers and no good reason to be there, and I felt the knock land somewhere behind my ribs.

Mistress Corren answered the door. I heard her voice go flat and careful — she had two registers for strangers, obsequious and hostile, and this was neither — and then boots in the hall, more than one pair, and then Odell Vane's door opening above me with the particular creak it made when he moved quickly, which he almost never did.

I did not run to the window like a guilty woman. I want that recorded. I walked, at the pace of a scribe with ledgers to balance, and I looked out through the slats of the shutters into the grey drizzle of Crooked Hollow's shortest morning.

Three of them. Grey coats, travel-cloaked, mud to the knee. Two stood by the gate like furniture. The third was already inside the courtyard, shaking rain from his shoulders, and even from above I could see he was different from the other two. He stood like a man reading a room. His coat was better cut. His hat was off, held against his chest, dripping politely.

"Guildmaster Vane," his voice carried up through the floorboards, smooth as tallow. "Halvard Renn. Office of Inquisition. I'm told you keep the most complete records in the Hollow."

"I'm told a great many things," Odell said. "Most of them false."

"Then we'll get along."

* * *

They gathered in the audit hall, and I was summoned, because of course I was. That is the particular cruelty of being indispensable: you are always present for your own endangerment.

Renn sat at the long table with his gloves folded beside him and his hands bare, which unsettled me more than any weapon would have. An armed man is making a show. A man who takes his gloves off intends to touch things.

"Mara Quintana," he said, before anyone introduced us. "Senior reconciler. Eleven years' service. No blemishes on record." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, like everything else about him. "You've audited half the trade houses in the Hollow. Your work has been described to me as 'impossibly clean.'"

"Thank you," I said.

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." He turned to Odell. "By order of the Crown, I'll inspect your full holdings. Every folio, every sealed drawer, every private ledger. Beginning today."

"Beginning today," Odell repeated, "is a large request. We have active accounts. Interruption of reconciliation—"

"Inquisitor," Renn said mildly. "And interruption is rather the point. Something was carried through your city recently. Something small." He let the silence do its work. "A child, perhaps. Or something that looks like one. The Crown has an interest in items of unusual provenance, and the Ledger Guild, guildmaster, is where provenance goes to hide."

My pen did not stop moving. I had trained myself for moments like this — not this exactly, nothing like this exactly, but for the general condition of being watched while knowing something — and the trick, I have found, is to give your hands a job so absorbing that your face can go elsewhere. My hands balanced the Ferrow account. My face was bored. Somewhere beneath both, my heart was hammering out a rhythm I hoped wasn't audible through the floorboards, because Ash was directly below us, asleep, with a scale that glowed like a banked coal tucked against her chest.

"The Guild complies with all lawful inspection," Odell said. His voice was its usual dry gravel, but I have known him twenty years and heard every weather it could produce, and this was storm-glass weather. Flat. Pressured. "You'll find our records in order."

"I'm sure I will," said Renn. "That's what worries me."

* * *

He began with the public folios, which was either courtesy or strategy, and by noon I had learned it was strategy. Renn read the way a fisherman reads water. He didn't look at entries; he looked at *gaps*. He'd run his finger down a column and stop where nothing was written, and ask, "Why does the Marlowe account skip the third of Frostmonth?" and some poor clerk would stammer that the third of Frostmonth was a holiday, and Renn would say, "Was it," in a tone that made holidays sound like conspiracies.

By late afternoon he had worked through to the restricted shelves, and Odell produced the keys, and I watched the guildmaster hand over thirty years of secrets with the expression of a man paying a ransom in coins he'd counted himself.

That was when Renn said, casually, without looking up: "Your private collection, Mistress Quintana. The sealed acquisitions. Bottled thunderclap, memory in amber, the usual curiosities. Where are those catalogued?"

"Restricted annex," I said. "Separate inventory."

"I'd like to see it."

"You'll need the second key," Odell said. "It's kept—"

"With you," Renn finished pleasantly. "Of course it is. Fetch it, then. Take your time."

Odell took his time. He took so much time that I understood he was taking it deliberately, and I used every minute of it, because I had finally understood what Renn was actually hunting, and it was worse than Ash.

He was hunting the *entry*. The one I'd found weeks ago, written in no alphabet I knew, the entry that had started all of this — the impossible inventory item logged in a script older than any kingdom. If the Inquisition knew such an entry existed, they didn't need the child. They needed only the page. And pages can be seized faster than children can be hidden.

When Odell returned with the key, walking slowly, breathing carefully, I had already made my decision, and the decision was this: tonight, whatever it cost, the entry would stop existing.

* * *

Renn dined at the guild that night. He insisted it was no imposition; he simply preferred to continue working after supper, and would the guildmaster object to lamplight in the audit hall past midnight? Odell did not object. Odell poured the wine himself, which he does for perhaps three people a year, and I recognized the gesture for what it was: a man keeping the wolf close enough to watch its teeth.

I served the wine. This was also deliberate, though whose deliberation, mine or Odell's, I couldn't say. What I learned from serving it: Renn drank little. He asked questions the way other men make conversation. He wanted to know how long I'd been with the guild, whether my family had been Hollow-born, whether I found the work satisfying.

"My family were farmers," I said. "Upstream. There was a flood."

"Ah," he said, with a softness that made my skin crawl, because it was genuine. "The Quintana farm. Yes. I've read that file." He swirled his wine. "Debts after the flood. The guildmaster bought them. Generous man, Odell Vane."

"Very," I said.

"And loyal servants are worth their price." He set down the glass. "Tell me, Mistress Quintana — if you found something in these ledgers that shouldn't exist, something the Crown had declared... irregular. Would you report it?"

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the hall. Downstairs, under the boards, a child was sleeping who had once had a country.

"I reconcile accounts," I said. "I don't judge them."

"A careful answer."

"I'm a careful woman."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and it frightened me more than the questions had, because for one moment Halvard Renn stopped being an office and became a person, tired around the eyes, and people can be reasoned with, which meant he could also be *betrayed*, and I did not want to think about which of those I was preparing to do.

* * *

He retired to the guest room Odell offered — refusing it would have been suspicious, accepting it was a catastrophe — and the house went dark by degrees, lamp by lamp, until only the audit hall burned.

I waited two hours. I know because I counted the bells, and between bells I listened to the rain, and beneath the rain, faintly, I listened for a child's breathing through the floor and heard it, steady, and that steadiness is the only reason my hands were calm when I unlocked the restricted annex and took out the folio with the impossible entry.

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery: it is not drawing. It is *accounting*. You cannot fake an old page — the paper remembers, the ink remembers, the damp of forty years lives in the fibers — so you don't fake the page. You fake the *entry*. You find a blank space in a genuinely old ledger, a margin note half-erased, a cancelled line, and you write over it, in period ink, mixed from period recipes, with a period nib worn to the right width. And then you commit the true sin of the forger's art: you make it boring. Nothing incriminates itself like importance. The entry must be dull. Dull as salt. Dull as a Tuesday.

The original entry — the one in the old alphabet, the one that had hooked me like a fish — I could not erase. Erasure leaves ghosts; I know, I've spent my life finding them. So I did the next best thing. I buried it. I added, above and below it, in the same cramped antique hand, three lines of perfectly ordinary Vessian-adjacent merchant script — prices, weights, a dispute over tallow — so that the strange alphabet sat in the middle of a mundane passage like one odd word in a familiar sentence. Not hidden. Explained away. Any reader would assume the whole passage was one language, half-legible, of no account.

Then I forged the *counter-entry*: a duplicate record in the main catalogue, dated years back, describing the item as "scrap folio, foreign script, value nil, retained for binding stock." Value nil. Binding stock. I have never loved four words more.

I worked until the fifth bell. My neck ached. My eyes burned. Twice I heard footsteps in the hall and put out my lamp and stood in the dark with my heart going like a smith's hammer, and twice the footsteps passed, one set heavy (a warden doing rounds), one set soft and slow (Odell, sleepless, pacing — or perhaps standing outside my door deciding whether to stop, and choosing not to, which was its own message).

At the sixth bell, grey light came up over the rooftops, and I blew out the lamp and looked at what I'd done. A clean lie, sitting in forty years of honest paper, indistinguishable, permanent. Eleven years of scrupulous service, and it had taken me one night to learn that honesty is just a forgery you haven't needed yet.

Under the floorboards, Ash stirred. I lifted the plank and went down to her, and she blinked up at me in the candle-dark and said something soft in the old tongue, and I didn't know the words, but I knew the tone. It was the tone children use when they wake and need to know, before anything else, that the day is safe.

"Not yet," I whispered, in my own language, stroking her hair. "But today, maybe. Today we get cleverer."

She took my finger in her whole small hand, the way children do, as if holding on were a complete philosophy, and closed her eyes again.

* * *

Renn appeared at breakfast immaculate, as though he had slept in a tailor's shop instead of a guild guest bed, and announced over porridge that he would conclude his inspection by evening.

"One thing troubles me," he said, buttering bread with terrible gentleness. "Your restricted annex. The catalogues are thorough. Impeccable. But—" he paused, savoring it, "—there's a shelf gap. Folio eleven, position nine. Dust everywhere else, dust shadow there. Something was removed. Recently."

The porridge in my mouth turned to clay. *Folio eleven.* I had checked the entry. I had not checked the *shelf*.

Odell, to his eternal credit, did not look at me. He looked at his tea. "Removed for restoration," he said. "We send fragile items to a binder in Lowmarsh."

"Do you," said Renn. "How convenient. I'm traveling to Lowmarsh myself within the fortnight." He rose, drew on his gloves — the gloves, at last, the gloves meant the performance was over — and smiled at us both with perfect, dreadful courtesy. "Thank you for your hospitality, guildmaster. Thank you for your care, Mistress Quintana. The Crown values diligence." At the door he paused. "Oh — and if a child should turn up in the Hollow. Small, dark-haired, speaks oddly. The reward is substantial. Enough," he said lightly, "to clear any debt."

The gate closed behind him. The rain kept falling. And Odell Vane stood in his own hallway, very still, looking at the spot where the Inquisitor had been, and said, in a voice I had never heard him use — quiet, and old, and afraid:

"He knows about Lowmarsh. Mara. He knows about Lowmarsh, and I have never told anyone about Lowmarsh."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, a child began to hum a song in a language older than any kingdom — and I realized, with a cold that started in my spine and spread outward, that I had heard the melody before. In the sealed folios. In the margins. In the handwriting of whoever had razored out the pages, decades ago, leaving only the tunes behind.
