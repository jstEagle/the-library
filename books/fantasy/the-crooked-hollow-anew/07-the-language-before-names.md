## 7. The Language Before Names

I am going to tell you a thing that is true, and then I am going to tell you another thing that is true, and you will not believe they can both be true at the same time, because grown people never believe that.

Here is the first true thing: my country folded up like paper.

Here is the second true thing: it is still here.

You see? Both. Both at once. That is how fire works. Fire does not stop being fire when the candle goes out. It just waits in the dark for somebody to strike.

* * *

Before, there was Mama's voice.

That is where everything starts, so that is where I start. Mama had a voice like bread coming out of an oven — warm all the way through, even when she was cross. Especially when she was cross. When she was cross she said words like *sennet-varra*, which means "little troublemaker, come here," and she said them soft, so soft, and her hands were floury from the morning loaves and she would pull me against her apron and hold me there until the crossness went out of both of us.

Our house was on Vessia-Street. Not a street called Vessia. It was just called Street, because in Vessia we did not need to name our streets after anything. They were ours. Why would you write your own name on your own hand?

The bell tower stood at the end of Street. Every morning the dragon sang to the bell and the bell sang back, and if you put your palm flat on the cobblestones you could feel the two songs shaking hands down under the ground. I used to do that every day. Palm flat. Eyes shut. The whole city humming like a sleeping cat.

Mama said the dragon was older than the word for *older*. She said the dragon remembered the country out loud, every day, because that is what dragons do — they do not keep memories inside like people do, all cramped up and secret. They say them. Big and slow. A memory the size of a country has to be said out loud or it gets lost, like a stitch dropped in knitting, and then the whole sweater comes undone.

I did not know then that a sweater can be undone on purpose.

* * *

The fire did not come from outside. That is the part nobody believes. People think fire comes from outside, from enemies, from the sky. But this fire came from inside. From paper.

There were men in grey coats who carried no swords. They carried ledgers. I remember thinking they looked like scribes, like the kind people who came to Papa's shop to buy ink, and I remember being glad, because scribes were safe. Scribes were the safest people in the world. My whole family thought so. We were a family of ink-people. Papa ground pigments. Mama wrote letters for neighbors who couldn't write. I was learning my letters on slate.

The grey-coat men opened their ledgers and began to read us out of the world.

I cannot say it better than that, though I have tried many times, in the dark, under floorboards, in a language only one other living person knows. You cannot read a country out of the world. But you can read its *name* out. And a country without its name is like a candle without its wick. All the fire is still there. It has nowhere to stand.

They started small. Street names first. Then shop signs. Then the bells went quiet mid-ring, like someone had put a hand over a mouth. Then Mama's face got strange, like she was trying to remember a dream while standing inside it.

Then the folding started.

I watched our street fold. I want you to understand: I did not watch it burn. Burning would have been honest. Folding is worse. The houses leaned toward each other the way paper leans when you crease it, and the crease went right through the bakery and the well and the bell tower, and the bell tower made one sound — half ring, half scream — and then it was flat. Flat as a page. Flat as nothing.

Mama pushed me into the cellar and pressed the scale into my hand.

It was warm. It was always warm, ever after. It came off the dragon's smallest toe, Mama said, shed years before I was born, kept on our mantel because it glowed pretty in winter. She wrapped my fingers around it and said the old words, the ones grandmothers say and children forget, and I did not forget. I have never once forgotten. Forgetting is a door and I nailed mine shut.

"Keep the ember lit," Mama said. "A country isn't land. A country is remembering. As long as one person remembers out loud, we are not gone."

Then the cellar ceiling folded too, and Mama was on the wrong side of the fold, and I have been on this side of it ever since.

* * *

Do you know what it is like to walk through a country that is pretending not to exist?

Everything is still there. The river. The hills. The smell of rain on stone. But the names are gone out of things, and things without names go quiet, and quiet things start to fade at the edges like a drawing left in the sun. I walked for a long time — I don't know how long; time also got folded, a little — through fields that did not know they were Vessian fields. Sheep looked at me and forgot me between one blink and the next. Once I found a milestone with writing still on it, deep-cut letters the grey coats missed, and I sat beside it all night with my thumb in the groove of the first letter, which was the letter that begins the word for *home*, and I slept better than I had slept in months.

Because that is the trick of it. The men in grey could razor the pages and fold the country and unname the streets. But they could not get inside my mouth.

My language lived in me. Old tongue. First tongue. The tongue from before kingdoms, Mama called it, back when words were young and strong and meant what they said. Every word I knew was a little coal. And coals, if you keep them together and breathe on them, stay alive.

So I talked to myself. Out loud. Every day. I named everything I saw, in the old tongue, quietly, like planting seeds. *Stone. Rain. Door.* If I named it, it remembered itself a little. If it remembered itself, it held on a little longer.

Maybe that sounds like a small magic for such a big grief. But I was eight years old and alone, and you take the magic you have.

* * *

Now I must tell you about the rain night, and the door, and the seal.

I had walked farther than I meant to. The unnamed lands blur at the edges, and edges are slippery, and I fell — I fell out of the fading country into a loud wet city full of lights that hurt and voices in a language with too many hard corners in it. Crooked Hollow, the voices said. Crooked Hollow, crooked hollow, crooked. Everything there leaned. Even the buildings leaned, like the whole town was listening sideways.

And in the middle of all that leaning noise there was one building that did not lean. It stood straight. It stood straight the way a soldier stands straight, which is to say: it was hiding something.

Above its door burned a lamp, and under the lamp was carved a seal, and I stopped walking because my feet stopped before I told them to.

I know that seal.

You spend your whole life seeing a thing on coins and lintels and the spines of books and the wax on important letters, and you think it is decoration. A flower. A knot. Pretty nonsense. That is what I thought too, until the night the grey coats came, when Mama grabbed the seal-stamp from Papa's desk — the one he used for the guild contracts, the ones from across the water — and pressed it into hot wax and said, *if anyone asks, show them this, they cannot touch a sealed child.*

I asked her why. I was seven and full of questions.

"Because," Mama said, and her voice did the oven-warm thing even though her eyes were doing something else, "that is not a flower, little ash. That is the bell tower and the dragon, curled around each other. That is our sigil. Somebody very old gave it away once, or sold it, or lost it — and now strangers wear our country on their door."

Strangers wore our country on their door. And on that rain night, soaked to the bone, coal-hot scale clenched in my fist, I stood in front of that door and understood the second true thing for the very first time:

The country had folded up. But pieces of it had been carried out, before the folding, by people who maybe did not even know what they were carrying. And one of those pieces was hanging over this door, glowing faintly under the lamp, waiting.

The scale in my hand pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heart recognizing another heart across a crowded room.

I knocked.

* * *

The woman who opened the door had ink on her fingers and tiredness all the way down to her shoes, and when she looked at me she did not do the thing grown people usually do, which is look *through* children as though we are weather. She looked *at* me. All the way at me.

She said words in the corner-language. Hard-cornered words. Asking who I was, I think, or where my mother was, and I could not answer in her tongue, so I answered in mine. I said the greeting-words, the ones you say to a stranger at your threshold so that the stranger becomes a guest and the guest becomes safe:

*Vessa-lume, vessa-tarn. My fire to your fire. My door is yours.*

Her face changed. Not big, like in stories. Small, like a candle flame bending when someone walks past. She heard the shape of the words even though she couldn't have known their meaning — some meanings live in the sound itself — and behind her, deeper in the house, a lamp flickered, or maybe it was more lamps than lamps, maybe it was the seal above the door waking up after a hundred years of being mistaken for a flower.

Other people came. A big man with canal-water in his boots. An old man with a ledger-face and eyes like locked drawers. They argued about me in their hard-cornered tongue, and I stood on the wet stones and held the scale and waited, because I had learned that waiting is most of what the powerless have, and if you wait well, sometimes the right thing happens.

The ink-fingered woman knelt down to my level. Rain ran off her roof onto both of us equally. She pointed at herself and said a word — *Mara*, her mouth making it round and careful — and then she pointed at me.

Nobody had asked my name since the folding. Names need a country to stand in, mostly. But her door had our sigil on it. Her door was, somehow, a piece of standing ground.

I said my name in the old tongue. Just the small-name, the hearth-name, the one Mama used when I was sleepy. The big ceremonial name I kept hidden, the way you keep a second coin sewn into your coat lining.

The woman tried to repeat it and got it wrong, softly, the way you get a song wrong the first time, and then tried again, and the third time it came out as *Ash* — which is not my name, but which is not *not* my name either. In the old tongue, *ash* is what fire leaves when it has finished protecting the wood. It is also what fire leaves behind so that the ground knows to be fertile.

Ash. I decided in that moment, standing in the rain under a stolen sigil, that I would let them call me that. Ash. What remains. What remembers being fire.

* * *

They put me in a room with a real bed, which I did not trust, because beds are for places that exist, and I did not fully believe this place existed yet. So I lay on top of the covers with my boots on and the scale under my pillow, and I listened to the strange city settle around me like a large animal lying down.

Under the floorboards, I found later, there was a space. Grown people think spaces under floorboards are for hiding things from. But I grew up in a folded country. I know that empty spaces are really doors wearing a disguise. Under those boards was a door-shaped dark, and I slept there some nights after, curled around my coal, breathing on it.

Breathing on it, and saying my lists.

*Stone. Rain. Door. Mother. Bell. Dragon. Home.*

Every night, the list. Because a list is a rope thrown backward across a chasm, and as long as I hold my end, somewhere on the other side, someone might catch theirs.

Some nights the scale flared when I said certain words. *Dragon* made it pulse slow and deep, like a drum under water. Once — only once, that first week — it flared so bright that pictures came with the light, pictures that were not mine exactly but were mine *now*: streets singing, a bell tower whole, a vast coil of shadow-and-gold wrapped three times around the tower's base, one enormous eye opening, finding me across impossible distance, and regarding me with an expression I did not yet have the words for.

I have the words now. The expression said: *you took your time.*

And underneath that, quieter, older, worn down like a step climbed by a thousand years of feet, the eye said something else. Something that made me press the scale to my chest and cry the way you are allowed to cry only when nobody can hear.

It said: *almost done. Almost done. Hold on.*

Dragons do not say *help*. Dragons are too proud and too old. Dragons say *hold on* and mean *come quickly.*

* * *

In the days after, I learned the shape of the place that had taken me in. Ledgers everywhere — stacks and stacks of them, paper on paper on paper, the whole building breathing ink. The ink-woman, Mara, worked at a high desk with a green glass lamp, and she counted things that should not be countable — thunderclaps in bottles, summers kept in amber — and wrote numbers beside them as though numbers could tame them. I liked her for that. Numbers are just another way of saying *I see you* to a wild thing.

I could not read her letters. Too many hard corners. But I could read her ledger-sounds, the scratch of her pen, and I learned which scratches meant *safe* and which meant *trouble*. Trouble had a faster scratch. Trouble scratched like rain on a window in a hurry.

And I noticed — because children notice everything, we are built for it, noticing is how we survive being small — that Mara sometimes pulled down a folio with a broken spine, and looked at the place where pages should be, and there were no pages. Only a razored gap, smooth as the fold in my country. She would put her finger in the gap the way I once put my thumb in the milestone's letter.

She did not know she was touching the same wound I carry.

I wanted to tell her. My whole chest was full of telling. But telling requires a shared language, and hers had hard corners, and mine had none left to give away cheaply. So I did the patient thing. The coal thing. I sat at the foot of her desk some evenings, saying my lists quietly, planting my seeds, and I watched her finger find the gaps again and again, and I thought:

*Soon. Soon you will ask. And when you ask, I will open like a door.*

The scale agreed with me. It had begun to glow brighter each night, warmer each morning, as though something far away had turned its attention fully toward this crooked leaning town, toward the sigil on the door, toward the razored gaps in the shelves, toward me — the last mouth that still spoke the country aloud.

Hold on, the eye had said.

I was holding. But the scale was getting hotter, and hotter is a word that only ever ends one way.

And on the morning the grey coats came to the gate of Crooked Hollow — I felt it before anyone told me, a cold sliding down my spine like a crease beginning to fold — I understood that soon was no longer soon enough.

Some doors, if you wait too long, have to be kicked.
