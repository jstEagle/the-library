## 8. Fire That Remembers

I want it on the record—my record, the one I keep in my head where Odell Vane can't audit it—that I did not intend to touch the scale.

I had rules. Rules were how a debt-bound scribe survived twenty years in the Ledger Guild without becoming one of the ledgers herself. Rule one: don't steal. Rule two: don't get caught not stealing. Rule three: don't touch anything that hums, glows, or is filed under *Pending Classification*, because in Crooked Hollow that category contained a bottled thunderclap, a jar of last winter's fog that wept when you opened it, and at least one object that had been classified as "a chair" for so long that everyone had forgotten it was lying about.

The scale was none of those things. The scale was worse. It was honest.

It sat in my palm like a coal that had decided to be patient. Ash had pressed it into my hand an hour earlier—"You hold. Ash sleep"—and then fallen asleep on Bramble Fenwick's coat in the corner of my counting room like a cat who had chosen her household and expected the paperwork to follow. The girl slept the way soldiers and children sleep: completely, with one hand curled around nothing, guarding a country that no longer existed.

And the scale warmed against my skin, and I thought, *just a moment longer,* which is how every disaster in history begins.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about impossible inventory: after enough years, you stop flinching at it. You develop a professional detachment. A memory sealed in amber comes across your desk and you note its weight, its provenance, its storage requirements (*do not expose to direct grief*), and you move on to the next line item. You become very good at cataloguing miracles without letting them catalogue you.

So when the scale flared, my first instinct was bureaucratic. *Unregistered light source. Note for the morning tally.*

My second instinct arrived about half a heartbeat later and knocked the first one flat.

Light poured out of the scale—not out of it, exactly; through it, as though the scale were a keyhole and something enormous on the other side had finally noticed me looking—and the counting room went away. The smell of ink and mildew went away. Bramble's snoring went away, which was honestly the most miraculous part.

I was standing in a street.

Not standing—*being*, the way you are in a dream, except this was no dream I'd have had the imagination to make. Cobbles the color of river pearls, worn into gentle waves by centuries of feet. Houses leaning together overhead like old friends sharing secrets, their shutters painted colors I didn't have names for—a blue that was almost singing, a green that tasted of pears. Banners hung from balconies, stitched with a sigil I knew intimately from ten thousand wax seals and never once thought to question: the coiled serpent-and-star of the Ledger Guild.

Except here it wasn't a guild seal. Here it was everywhere—carved over doorways, set into the cobbles, worked into the iron of the lampposts. Not a merchant's mark. A country's crest.

People moved through the street. That was the part that undid me. Not the architecture, not the impossible sky, which was the gold of late afternoon stretched thin as leaf. The people. A woman arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger. Two boys racing a hoop down the center of the road, scattering pigeons. An old man on a bench feeding crumbs to a bird that was definitely not any species I could name—it had too many wings and an expression of profound judgment—and he was laughing at it.

They couldn't see me. I walked among them like weather, like a draft under a door, and they went on living around me, and every single one of them was speaking the language Ash spoke. It poured past me, musical and round-voweled, and I understood it the way you understand music—not word by word but whole, the way meaning arrives before translation.

A bell rang somewhere above, and I looked up.

The bell tower rose from the heart of the city, white stone gone honey-colored with age, and coiled around it—around the whole tower, seven times, tail anchored somewhere below the earth and head resting near the belfry like a cat draped over a favorite chair—was a dragon.

She was vast in the way mountains are vast, in a way your mind files under *landscape* rather than *creature*. Her scales were the deep red of banked embers, and between them ran veins of light, slow pulses traveling up her length like breath, like tide. She wasn't sleeping. I want to be clear about that, because everything else I understood later, but I understood that immediately. Sleeping things have a looseness to them. She was *attending*. Her eye, large as a cart wheel, was open, and she was watching the street below—the woman and the fishmonger, the boys with their hoop, the old man and his rude many-winged bird—with an attention so complete it was nearly a form of love.

And she was singing. Or the tower was singing through her, or she was singing through the tower—I couldn't tell where one ended and the other began—but the sound came up through the cobbles into my borrowed feet, and it was the song Ash hummed sometimes when she thought no one was listening. The one that made Bramble go quiet and stare into his ale.

The city sang back. Not all at once, not in unison—that would have been eerie. But bits of it. The woman haggling picked up a phrase of melody between offers. The boys turned their race into a rhythm. Somewhere above, a window opened and a voice joined for a bar before life pulled it away again. The song wasn't a performance. It was a *maintenance*. It was what this place did instead of breathing.

I stood in a street in a country that has never appeared on any map drawn in the last two hundred years, and I thought, with the full clarity available only to people who are not really there:

*This is Vessia.*

* * *

Then the light guttered.

It happened slowly enough that I felt it happening, which was crueler than if it had simply cut off. The edges of the vision browned like paper held too near a flame. The pearl cobbles dimmed. The dragon's glow-pulses slowed—one beat, then a gap, then another beat, arrhythmic, a heart learning to stumble—and as the light withdrew from her scales I saw what I hadn't let myself see before: that she was thinner than a creature that size should be. That the coils around the tower had gaps in them, places where scale showed stone beneath, or where there was no stone either, just a soft absence like a hole in a story.

The old man on the bench faded first. Then the boys. The fishmonger's stall went translucent, the fish inside it still turning silver, still fresh, still *there*, just less so. The woman's voice stretched thin and kept arguing anyway, because whatever else was failing, the argument was clearly a matter of principle.

Last of all, the dragon's eye found mine.

I don't know how. I was nobody, a draft, a rumor of a person, and she looked directly at me across the dying gold air of her city, and her eye was ancient and tired and absolutely lucid, and what passed from her to me was not words. It was arithmetic. It was the look of an accountant reviewing a ledger that has run dry, and the figure at the bottom of the final column was small, and shrinking, and she knew it to the digit.

*How long?* I tried to ask, and had no mouth to ask it with.

But the scale answered anyway, because the scale was hers—or part of her, shed or given or lost, I didn't know yet—and as the vision folded up like paper, like a country folding up like paper, the heat in my palm gave one last pulse, and I felt it the way you feel a debt come due:

*Soon.*

* * *

I came back to myself sitting on the floor of my own counting room, which was undignified, and holding a scale that had gone cool as a river stone, which was terrifying.

Bramble was crouched in front of me with his hands hovering an inch from my shoulders, the way he does when he wants to shake someone but suspects it would be counterproductive. His face had gone the color of good vellum.

"You went somewhere," he said. "Your eyes went somewhere. Mara. Your eyes left."

"I'm fine," I said, which was rule four of survival in the Hollow: deny everything until denial becomes structurally unsound.

"Mara."

"I'm *fine.*" I got my feet under me and stood, and the room tilted politely and settled. Ash hadn't woken. She lay in the corner with the coat pulled to her chin, and even asleep her brow was furrowed, as if the dream she was having required negotiation.

I looked at the scale in my palm. Cool now. Quiet. But I had held it while it burned, and I knew what I knew.

"Bramble," I said. "What do you know about dragons?"

He blinked. "That they're extinct?"

"That's what the ledgers say."

"Well." He scratched under his cap. "Ledgers have been wrong before. Once. Possibly twice. There was that incident with the entire missing decade."

"The ledgers aren't wrong," I said. "They're *edited*. There's a difference, and the difference is a knife." I turned the scale over. From underneath, in certain light, you could see a faint tracery across its surface—lines, fine as script, running in patterns that repeated and branched. I'd assumed it was natural figuring, the way you assume things in this job. Now I looked at it properly and saw it for what it was. Writing. Or a map. Or both. In this city those might be the same discipline.

"And here's the thing," I went on, and heard my own voice drop into the register I use for reading difficult clauses aloud, the one that means I've stopped being wry because wryness requires a margin of safety and my margin had just been spent. "Whatever I saw in there—it isn't gone. It's *failing*. There's a difference, and the difference is time."

Bramble sat down heavily on my stool, which squeaked in protest. "Saw what? What did you see?"

"A city. Streets. People. A bell tower with a dragon wrapped around it like she was keeping it warm." I closed my hand over the scale. "She was keeping more than the tower warm, Bramble. I think she was keeping all of it warm. Every cobble. Every argument. That whole place exists because she remembers it out loud, and she is running out of voice."

The silence that followed had texture. Outside, rain worked at the shutters. Ash murmured something in her sleep—a word, maybe, or half of one—and rolled toward the wall.

"So it's real," Bramble said finally. "Vessia. It's not a story, it's not a smuggling route somebody invented to explain cargo that shouldn't exist. It's real, and it's *dying*, right now, tonight, while we sit here discussing it."

"Yes."

"And the child is from there."

"Yes."

"And the Crown sent grey coats to make sure no one ever says the name out loud."

"*Yes.*"

He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he wore the expression I'd seen him wear exactly twice before: once before he testified against the Tollhouse gang, and once before he married—and divorced, in under a year—the lighthouse keeper's daughter. The expression of a man about to do something brave and stupid and character-defining.

"All right," he said. "So we're doing this. Obviously we're doing this, I'd just like it noted that I was consulted, however briefly, before being swept into catastrophe."

"Noted," I said. "Witnessed and recorded."

"Good. Because here's my problem, Mara." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped to the register smugglers reserve for cargo and confessions. "If a whole country can be erased—ledgers razored, maps redrawn, songs outlawed until nobody alive remembers the tune—then it can happen again. To anywhere. To the Hollow. And the only people who'll see it coming are the ones who've already seen it happen once." He nodded at Ash. "Her. And whatever's left of that dragon."

The scale pulsed once in my palm. Faint. A coal remembering it used to be a fire.

Or—no. Not remembering. *Reminding.*

Because that was the thing I'd carried back out of the vision, the thing sitting in my chest now heavier than any debt figure Odell Vane had ever entered against my name. The dragon's eye, finding mine across a dying street. The arithmetic in it. She hadn't been looking at me by accident. Scales don't fall off healthy creatures. That scale had been *sent*—shed deliberately, carried somehow across an erasure, into a flood, into the arms of a child who spoke the language before names—and it had waited in my counting room, filed under pending classification, until someone held it long enough to look through the keyhole.

She had shown me Vessia whole so that I would understand what was being lost. She had let me watch it gutter so that I would understand how fast.

And I was a scribe. Recording things was the one skill I had ever been permitted to own.

"Bramble," I said. "How quickly can you get word to your canal contacts? All of them. The ones who owe you, the ones you owe, the ones who just owe generally."

His eyebrows climbed. "Depends what the message is."

"'The ledger is open.' Nothing else. They'll know it's serious if it's strange."

"They'll think it's a trap."

"It probably is one," I said. "Just not ours."

He snorted, stood, and gathered his coat from around Ash with the exaggerated care of a man defusing something. She stirred, blinked up at him with eyes the color of the vision's last gold light, and then—this is the part I will carry to whatever grave I eventually fill—she looked at me, and at the scale in my open hand, and her whole face changed. Not fear. Recognition. The way you look at a door you'd stopped believing was in the wall.

"You saw," she said. Not a question. In trade-tongue, halting, but clear.

"I saw," I said.

Her chin trembled, once, and she mastered it the way children master things that should fell adults. "She is almost done," Ash said. "Mama said. Before. She said the fire goes down and down and when it is gone—" She made a gesture, small hands closing into fists and opening on nothing. Folding up like paper.

"When it's gone, it's gone forever," I finished. "Unless."

Ash tilted her head. "*Unless?*"

And there it was, the hook under my ribs, the urgency with teeth, the thing that had curdled all that wonder into something with a deadline. Because I had seen the city, and I had seen the gaps in the dragon's coils, and I had spent twenty years in a building full of sealed folios and razored pages and entries written in alphabets that predated every kingdom on the current maps. Erasure leaves scars. Scars can be read. If a country could be unwritten, then somewhere—in the ledgers, beneath the Hollow, in the spaces where the ink had been cut away—there had to be a record of how. And if there was a record of how it was undone—

There might be a record of how to undo the undoing.

"I don't know yet," I told Ash honestly, because she deserved honesty more than comfort. "But I know where to start looking. And I know we don't have long."

Outside, the rain redoubled against the shutters, and somewhere across the dark rooftops of Crooked Hollow, I would have sworn I heard—very faintly, under the water-noise, under the wind—a bell tolling once, in a pattern no bell in this city used.

Counting down, maybe.

Or calling roll.
