## 10. Ledger of the Erased

**Mara**

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a guild archive at three in the morning, and I have come to know it the way sailors know weather. It is not peace. It is the silence of a room holding its breath around a secret, and I had spent four nights inside it with a candle stub, Ash's alphabet scratched onto scraps of vellum, and the razored folios spread out before me like a crime scene.

Which, I suppose, is exactly what they were.

The gaps were not random. That was the first thing I'd proven to my own satisfaction, and proving things to my own satisfaction is one of the few pleasures debt has left me. Odell Vane was many things—stingy, secretive, capable of a silence that could curdle milk—but he was not careless. The pages missing from the sealed folios followed a pattern: every entry dated in the month of the Long Frost, across forty-one consecutive years. Every entry touching on the cisterns. Every entry, when you held the surviving margins up to candlelight and looked for the ghost of pressed ink on the facing page, mentioning a word that recurred like a heartbeat.

I had copied that word out phonetically forty times before I admitted I couldn't pronounce it. The old tongue didn't have letters so much as intentions. Ash had laughed at my attempts—the first time I'd heard her laugh, a small startled sound like a coin dropped in an empty room—and then she'd taken my quill and drawn the character properly: a vertical stroke with two arms, like a person standing in a doorway.

"Door," she'd said. Then, tapping the second character beside it, the one shaped like water running downhill: "Out."

*Door Out.* Two words, repeated across four decades of mutilated ledgers, always in the same context, always near references to the drowned places under the Hollow.

I sat back and rubbed my eyes until the candle flame smeared into a yellow comet. Somewhere above me, the guild slept. Somewhere across town, Bramble Fenwick was presumably asleep too, or drunk, or both—Bramble considered those compatible activities—and I was about to do something spectacularly stupid, which was send for her at dawn.

Because here was the problem, the one that sat on my chest like a cat that had decided I owed it money, which in this town was a familiar feeling. Everything I found in these folios belonged, by every law of contract and custom I had ever transcribed, to Odell Vane. My loyalty was purchased. It said so, in ink, in his ledger, in a figure I had been counting down for eleven years and was still, by my own arithmetic, nine years from zero. Nine more years of being the woman who kept his books and asked no questions, in exchange for being the woman he'd pulled out of the gutter when her family's name went up in smoke along with their print shop.

And yet.

And yet there was a child sleeping upstairs who spoke a dead country's language, and a dragon dying somewhere I couldn't see, and an Inquisitor named Renn who had looked at me across his inspection table with eyes that said *I know what you're hiding and I haven't decided what to do about it*, and nine years suddenly felt like a very long time to spend pretending the world wasn't ending.

I trimmed the candle and kept working. What else does a scribe do?

* * *

**Bramble**

Now, I want it understood that I am not a brave woman. Brave people get memorialized. I intended to get old, which requires a completely different skill set, mostly involving running away at the correct moment and knowing which canals freeze thin.

So when Mara Quintana turned up at my mooring at an hour when honest smugglers are asleep and dishonest ones are working, I should have told her no. Instead I made tea, because apparently eleven years of friendship means that when she looks at me with that particular expression—the one that says *I have done something reckless and need an accomplice*—my hands start moving toward the kettle without consulting the rest of me.

"You've been in the sealed folios," I said. Not a question. You don't survive the Ledger Guild's orbit without learning to read Mara Quintana, and right now she read like a confession.

"I've been studying the gaps in the sealed folios," she corrected. "Legally distinct."

"Is it? Legally?"

"Almost certainly not."

I drank my tea. Outside, the canal did its slow brown breathing against the hull. A heron stood on the piling looking judgmental, which is the whole profession of herons.

"Ash is asking questions," Mara went on. "About home. And I can't answer them, Bramble, because the answers were cut out of the record with a razor sometime before I was born. But the gaps have edges, and edges leave marks." She set a page on my table between the teapot and the bait knife. "This word. It recurs forty-one times. Ash says it means 'door.' This one means 'out.' And this—" she tapped a third glyph, coiled like a sleeping snake, "—she says means 'under,' but only when you're talking about water."

"So we're looking for a door out, under the water."

"We're looking for where Vessia went," Mara said quietly. "That's what the door is. Where a whole country goes when the Crown decides it never existed."

I looked at the glyphs. I looked at her face, which had the specific grey tinge of a woman who has spent four nights in an archive and found something worse than nothing. And I thought about the scale Ash carried, glowing like a banked coal, and how the wardens' dogs whined when she passed, and how Renn's grey coats had come to the gate once already and would come again.

"Mara," I said, "you know I'd walk into fire for you. Mostly because you'd owe me and I collect. But if we find this door—if it's real—whose side of it do we stand on? Because I've got a boat, a crew of two idiots, and a sister in Tallowmark who thinks I'm respectable. I can't be loyal to two countries. Nobody can."

"That," said Mara, "is exactly the problem."

At least she was honest about it. Honesty is underrated among conspirators.

* * *

**Mara**

We worked through the method over the next three days, and I will record it here as precisely as I can, because if this chapter of my life ends with me in irons, someone ought to know the scholarship was sound.

The razored pages left impressions. Ink pressed into the facing leaf, mirrored and faint, recoverable with lamp-oil washes and a jeweler's glass I borrowed from a pawnbroker whose name I will take to my grave. Forty-one years of ghost-entries. Most were mundane—cistern maintenance costs, dredging contracts, complaints about flooding—but each one ended with the same formula, written in the old script rather than trade-tongue, as though whoever kept those entries had wanted them unreadable to the Crown's clerks even before the razors came.

Ash translated for us in the evenings, sitting cross-legged on Bramble's deck with her scale in her lap, pronouncing words that made the canal water ripple in ways water shouldn't. She was patient with us. Children often are, with adults learning to crawl in languages they walked in as infants.

"This one says the door sleeps beneath the singing stair," she read one night, frowning. "But the stair isn't a stair anymore. It says the Hollow built its gutters over the country's throat."

"The cisterns," Bramble said. She'd gone still in the way she did when the smuggling part of her brain lit up. "The old cisterns under Tanner's Row. They're older than the guild charter—everyone says so, nobody knows why. There are galleries down there the water company doesn't have maps for. Sealed since the flood of my grandmother's day."

"And the singing stair?"

Ash looked up at me, and there was something in her face that made my chest hurt—an eight-year-old trying to carry a weight built for shoulders twice her size. "The bell tower," she said. "In Vessia there was a bell tower, and the stairs sang when you climbed them because the steps were tuned. Mama said—" Her voice caught. Bramble, without appearing to move, put a hand briefly on the girl's shoulder, and Ash steadied. "Mama said the stairs remembered every foot that climbed them. That's why the country couldn't be fully erased. Things that remember fight back."

I wrote that down. Of course I wrote it down; I'm a scribe, writing things down is my spine. But underneath the transcription, some quieter part of me heard what she'd actually said: *the country couldn't be fully erased.* Which meant the erasure was failing. Which meant somewhere, a dragon was losing that fight, and the Crown's Inquisitors knew it, and that was why Renn had come to our gate with his grey coats and his polite, terrible questions.

Two loyalties. I kept turning the phrase over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit. On one side: Odell Vane, who had bought my ruin and my gratitude in the same transaction, and whom I loved in the complicated way you love a parent who has never quite told you the truth. On the other: a dead country, a living child, and a truth that had been stolen from the public record by men with razors and royal warrants.

You cannot serve both. Ledgers don't balance that way. I knew it the way I knew my sums.

I just didn't yet know which column I'd write myself into.

* * *

**Bramble**

Here's a thing about Mara Quintana that most people miss, because she's quiet and precise and apologizes when other people bump into her: she is the stubbornest creature ever to hold a quill. Once she fixes on a truth, you might as well argue with mortar.

So by the fourth evening I'd stopped pretending I had a choice, and started doing what I'm actually good at, which is logistics. If there was a door under the drowned cisterns below Tanner's Row, then somebody had to know the way in, and the water company's sealed maps weren't going to walk themselves out of the surveyor's office. Also somebody had to think about boats, rope, lamps, food, and the strong likelihood that everything would go wrong in the dark while wet.

I made lists. Mara checked my figures. Ash sat between us drawing the old characters in spilled tea, teaching them to the paper, and I swear the paper listened.

It was during the third list—*things that float*—that Mara went quiet in a way I didn't like.

"Bramble," she said. "When we find it. The door. What do you think happens to the Hollow?"

I hadn't thought about it, which was a lie, because I'd thought about nothing else since she'd first said *where Vessia went*. "Depends what's keeping it shut," I said. "Or open. Or whatever doors do."

"If Vessia comes back—if it unfolds, the way Ash describes—it has to go somewhere. Countries don't return into thin air. They return into space. Into streets. Into—" She stopped.

"Into the Hollow," I finished. "Built on top of it. Gutters over the country's throat, the girl said." I looked down at my deck, my mooring, my stretch of canal that I had privately considered mine for twenty years, and felt the ground shift under assumptions I hadn't known I was standing on. "So we might be digging up a country that's lying under our feet like a floor. And when it stands up..."

"Everything built on top gets moved," Mara said. "Or broken. Or burned. Ash's scale showed me the dragon, Bramble. It's dying. Whatever it's been holding together is coming apart whether we open the door or not. The only choice we get is whether anyone's ready when it goes."

I poured two fingers of the good brandy, the one I save for funerals and tax inspectors, and gave us each half. "You know what my loyalty says?" I said. "My loyalty says keep my head down, run my cargo, stay small enough that history doesn't notice me. That's kept me alive a long time."

"But?"

"But history's already noticed. It noticed Ash the minute she knocked on your guild door. So." I raised the cup. "To the drowned stair, then. May it sing better than it floods."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. She was doing that thing again, the thing where her face wanted one thing and her debts wanted another, and I watched her lose the argument with herself in real time.

"There's one more thing," she said. "Before we go down there. I have to ask Odell what he knows. He caught me in the folios weeks ago and said nothing—that's not mercy, Bramble, that's a man deciding whether to stop me or use me. I can't walk under the Hollow carrying a question that big without putting it to him."

"And if he's the one who ordered the razors?"

She looked at the candle flame for a long moment.

"Then I'll finally know which side of the ledger I'm on," she said. "Won't I."

* * *

**Mara**

I went to him at dusk, when the guild hall smelled of lamp oil and cold mutton and the clerks had gone home to their suppers. Odell Vane sat in his office the way he always did, behind a desk worn smooth by sixty years of other people's transactions, a small dry man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that had seen every kind of lie and priced most of them fairly.

"Master Vane," I said. "I need to ask you about the Door Out of Vessia."

I had rehearsed gentler openings. They all died in my throat, because I am a scribe, and scribes believe that precision is a form of respect.

He didn't flinch. He set down his pen, aligned it with the desk's edge—a habit of his, ordering small things when large ones came loose—and looked at me for a long time. Behind him, the window showed the rooftops of Crooked Hollow stacked against a bruised sunset, all of it sitting, I now knew, on the throat of a country that was being strangled slowly enough that no one heard.

"You've been reading the ghosts," he said at last. "The impressions in the facing leaves. I wondered when you'd find the technique. Your mother used it, you know. Before the shop burned."

"My mother—" The word came out cracked.

"She audited for me too. Best I ever employed." He folded his hands. "Sit down, Mara. You deserve the whole entry, not the margin. But understand what you're asking for. Because the last time I told someone about that door, the Crown razed a print shop to the ground and orphaned a girl, and I have spent eleven years telling myself that buying her debt was penance instead of possession."

The candle guttered. Somewhere overhead, faint as memory, I could have sworn I heard a child humming in a language older than any kingdom—and beneath our feet, deeper than the cisterns, something vast and patient shifted in its sleep, and began, very softly, to count down.

Odell opened the locked drawer.

"Ask your question properly," he said, "and I'll answer it properly. But decide first, girl—which name are you serving tonight? Mine, or yours?"

And God help me, for the first time in eleven years, I knew the answer before I knew the cost.
