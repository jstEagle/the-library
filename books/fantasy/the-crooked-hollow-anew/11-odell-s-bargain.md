## 11. Odell's Bargain

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before an Inquisitor knocks on your door. I have heard it twice in my life. The first time was forty years ago, and it smelled of lamp oil and burning paper. The second time was tonight, and it smelled of rain on old stone and the cheap tallow candle I'd been using to avoid lighting the good ones—because if the guild is going to burn, let it burn down to its last bad candle first.

I was in my office doing what I do best, which is lying to paper. The ledgers of the Ledger Guild are honest documents in every particular except the ones that matter. Every bolt of cloth weighed, every bottled thunderclap catalogued, every memory in amber appraised to the copper. It is only the gaps that lie, and I am the man who maintains them. Forty years of razored pages, forty years of ink that says *nothing here* in six different hands so that no single clerk could be blamed for the absence.

Mara thinks she found one gap tonight. Clever girl. She found the seam of a coat with ten thousand stitches, and she pulled one thread, and now everything is coming apart at the speed of guilt.

The knock came at the third bell. Two raps, precise as a signature.

I knew before I opened it that it would not be Renn. Renn knocks like a man who hopes no one is home. This knock belonged to someone who hoped very much that I was.

"Guildmaster Vane." Inquisitor Sereth Corvane stood in the rain with her grey hood back and her face bare, which is a courtesy Inquisitors extend only when they want you to see exactly how much they are not smiling. She is younger than Renn by fifteen years and more dangerous by thirty, because Renn doubts himself and Corvane has never doubted anything except other people. "May I come in? Your roof leaks."

"Everything in this building leaks," I said. "It's how we keep the rent low."

She stepped past me into the hall, dripping on stones that had survived three floods and one smallpox epidemic, and looked around with the unhurried appetite of a woman reading a room the way I read a ledger. Line by line. Looking for the entry that doesn't balance.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about me, since I doubt anyone will write it down after tonight: I did not become a liar out of cowardice. I became one out of arithmetic.

Forty years ago I was a junior assessor with good handwriting and a talent for forgetting things quickly, which made me valuable. When the Crown came to the Guild and said *there is a country that must stop having existed*, they did not send soldiers. They sent clerks. Erasure is bookkeeping before it is anything else. First you remove the treaties. Then the trade routes. Then the maps. Then the names from parish rolls, the songs from songbooks, the words from dictionaries—you would be astonished how much of a country lives in dictionaries. And then, finally, you go door to door through the memory itself, folio by folio, and you razor out the pages where a place called Vessia used to be.

I razored for eleven days. My hands bled. Not from the work—from holding them still while older men did the actual cutting, because I was too junior to be trusted with a blade and too honest-looking to be trusted with nothing. So I held pages flat. That was my great crime. I held the paper steady so it could be cut cleanly.

And when it was done, the Guild gave me a choice: forget with everyone else, or stay and keep the gaps. Someone had to maintain the erasure. Gaps decay. New clerks find seams. Records drift against each other like ice floes and open up cracks where a whole country can leak back through.

I stayed. I told myself it was penance. Some nights I still believe that.

Then a fishmonger named Quintana drowned with his wife when the river took their boat, leaving behind a daughter of nine with quick eyes and no family and debts her father had signed in a desperate year, and I bought the paper. All of it. Every copper. I told myself that too was penance—that I would raise her inside the walls of the thing I helped build, and keep her safe, and never let her near the gaps.

You see the flaw. You saw it before I did. Everyone sees it before I do; it is my defining feature. I bound a child to the vault of secrets with a chain made of her own name, and I called it kindness, and then I spent thirty years watching her count her debt in a ledger I could have torn up any morning, and I never tore it up, because the debt was the only leash I had on my own conscience. As long as she owed me, I owed her. That is not arithmetic. That is cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat.

And now there is a child asleep under my floorboards who speaks the language I spent eleven days helping to kill, and the Crown has sent Corvane, and the arithmetic has finally, finally stopped balancing.

* * *

Corvane sat across from my desk without being offered a chair, which is an Inquisitor's way of saying the chair was always hers. Rain ran off her cloak onto my floorboards—the same floorboards, I noted with distant hysteria, beneath which Ash was sleeping not twenty feet away, breathing the slow breath of an exhausted eight-year-old.

"You received my colleague Renn two weeks ago," she said.

"I receive many people."

"He filed a report."

"I'm sure it was thorough."

"It was," she agreed, "suspiciously thorough. Suspiciously clean. Renn has a habit, Guildmaster, of finding exactly enough to justify his journey and not one item more." She produced a folded paper from her sleeve but did not open it. "He inspected your records and reported no anomalies. I have since learned that a child was seen at your gate on the night of the storm. A child speaking, according to a warden's deposition—" she unfolded the paper after all, delicately, like a man defusing something, "—*a language the deponent could not name but which hurt her ears to hear.*"

"That warden," I said, "has been dismissed. Drunk. He also swore the rain was falling upward."

"The rain does strange things around that language. Or so the oldest files say." Her eyes came up. Grey eyes. The Crown picks them grey on purpose; I'm convinced of it. "The oldest sealed files. Which are kept here. Under seal. By you."

"By the Guild."

"By you, Vane. Let's not hide behind architecture." She leaned forward. "Here is what happens next, and I will say it once, plainly, because unlike Renn I don't enjoy the theater of this. If the heir of Vessia is alive, the erasure is incomplete, and an incomplete erasure is not a scandal—it is a wound. Countries leak. People remember. Grandmothers sing songs they cannot source. And the Crown's answer to a leaking wound is not ointment. Do you understand me?"

"Burn it cauterized," I said. "Child and all."

"The child needn't burn. The child can simply stop having been. We have done it to larger things than a girl." She said it without cruelty, which was the worst part. Cruelty at least admits the thing exists. "But the Guild—your Guild, your records, your clerks, your thirty years of quiet service—will burn with her, because the fire doesn't read ledgers. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the Guild demonstrates that its loyalty to the Crown outweighs its sentimentality. Give me the child, Vane. Tonight. And tomorrow morning this building stands, your clerks keep their posts, and your name appears nowhere in what follows. You will have bought your life's work with one small life. Clerks do this sort of arithmetic all day, I'm told. You should be very good at it."

I looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below us, a floorboard settled—or didn't—and my heart performed a maneuver I felt in my teeth.

"You'll want tea," I said. "This will take some time. The child isn't here."

"No?"

"No. But I know where she is. And I'll tell you—but not tonight, and not for free, and not until I've made arrangements to protect my people. Because if I simply hand her over, Corvane, what stops you from burning the Guild anyway? Loose ends. A guildmaster who knew about the heir for weeks and said nothing. You've just told me yourself: the fire doesn't read ledgers. I need guarantees. Written ones. Sealed ones. In triplicate—we're clerks, after all. It's how we say *I love you*."

Something flickered behind the grey eyes. Respect, possibly. Or the recognition of a fellow professional. Either way, she inclined her head a fraction.

"Dawn," she said. "You have until dawn. Bring me the location, bring me the seals, and the Hollow keeps its guild." She rose, collected her hood, and paused at the door. "Vane. A word of professional courtesy. Renn's report was clean because he wanted it to be. I've opened an inquiry into him. If you're shielding something with that man, know that his protection has an expiration date."

She left. I listened to her boots fade into the rain, and then I sat down at my desk and put my face in my hands for exactly sixty seconds, which is the amount of grief a guildmaster can afford per incident.

* * *

Then I went downstairs and lied to the second person that night.

Mara was in the counting room with a lamp turned low, surrounded by folios, and the look on her face when I came in told me she'd heard the front door. She stood up too fast. Guilt and defiance fighting for possession of her chin. Thirty years I've watched that chin.

"She's gone," I said. "Corvane. She wants the child."

"I know. I heard enough." Mara's hand drifted toward the floorboard seam near the cold hearth, an unconscious gesture, a mother hen pretending to be a scribe. "Odell, we have to move her. Tonight. Bramble can take her by the canal—"

"Yes."

She blinked. I imagine I am not often agreeable, and certainly never promptly. "Yes?"

"Yes, move her tonight. Take the south route, under the tannery bridge, not the north—the north locks have been watched since Renn's visit, I'd wager my good candles on it. Go now. Don't pack. Don't tell the night clerk. And Mara—" I caught her arm, and she flinched, and I let go, because I deserved the flinch and worse. "Don't come back here afterward. Not for a few days. Not until I send word."

"What are you going to do?"

What was I going to do. I was going to sit at my desk and forge a location. I was going to write down, in my best hand, the address of a derelict rope-walk on the far side of the Hollow that had been empty for nine years, and seal it with the Guild's stamp, and give it to Corvane at dawn, knowing full well that when her men kicked in its doors they would find dust, rats, and a guildmaster whose credibility died in that moment. And Corvane does not forgive wasted hours. She especially does not forgive them when she realizes she's been played by a bookkeeper.

Meanwhile I had told Mara to run south, and I would tell Corvane east, and somewhere in between those two lies I intended to buy four or five hours, which is either enough time for a smuggler to put a child beyond reach or exactly enough time for everything to go wrong in a new and interesting way.

"Paperwork," I said. "It's what I'm for."

"Odell." She was crying, or nearly, and furious about it. "Whatever you're planning—if you're covering for us, if you're spending yourself for us—I won't—"

"You will," I said, "because you owe me."

It came out crueler than I meant it, or perhaps exactly as cruel as I meant it, because cruelty travels faster than tenderness at this hour of the night, and I needed her moving. Her face closed like a ledger snapping shut. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, picked up the sleeping bundle of blankets from under the floorboards—a small weight, a coal-warm scale glinting at its edge—and carried the child of a murdered country toward my back stairs without another word.

At the bottom step she paused. "Her name is Ash," she said. "We named her. You should know, in case—" She didn't finish. There was no need. *In case* is the native language of this entire city.

"Ash," I repeated. Good name. Short. Easy to carve somewhere permanent.

Then the door shut, and I was alone with the lamps and the ledgers and roughly five hours of life remaining, by my own estimate, and I did the only thing I have ever been truly excellent at.

I sat down, uncapped the good ink, and began to lie.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, the way dawn does when it knows what's expected of it. Corvane arrived with four grey coats and a face like a balanced column of figures. I handed her the sealed document with both hands, the way one surrenders a sword.

"The rope-walk on Tanner's End," I said. "She's held there. Smugglers. Fenwick's people."

She broke the seal and read, and I watched her read, and I thought: *forty years of honest lies, and this is the one that kills me. Fitting. At least it's in a fine hand.*

"Guildmaster," she said, folding the paper away, "if this is true, you've saved your guild. If it's false—" She smiled, briefly, and it was the most frightening thing I had seen all week, including the bottled thunderclap. "Well. Then you'll have taught me something about clerks."

They marched out into the wet street, eastward, toward dust and rats and my rapidly shortening future. I watched them go from the doorway, and then I turned back inside, took down the oldest folio in the restricted case—the one with the sigil on its spine that Mara still hadn't asked me about—and began, with steady hands, to write the letter that would either save everyone or serve as my confession.

Outside, far to the south, a boat was already sliding through the dark water. And far to the east, grey coats were quickening their pace, because Inquisitors always know, eventually, when the numbers don't add up.

I had given myself until noon. I intended to spend every minute of it.
