## 12. Down Through the Drowned Streets

There is a particular smell to a city's secrets, and it is not parchment or lamp oil as I had always assumed. It is wet stone and old iron and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a cellar no one will claim. The cisterns beneath Crooked Hollow smelled of all three, and I stood at the top of the maintenance stair with a lantern in one hand and my entire opinion of Bramble Fenwick's planning in the other.

"This," I said, "is your route."

Bramble peered past me into the dark with the expression of a man appraising a horse he'd already paid for. "It's *a* route."

"It's a hole full of water."

"It's a hole full of water that the Inquisition doesn't know about, which makes it the finest road in the Hollow." He shouldered his pack and started down the stairs without waiting for agreement, because Bramble has never once in his life waited for agreement. "Mind the fourth step. It lies."

"Everything down here lies," I said, but I followed him, because the alternative was staying above ground where grey coats walked the streets in pairs, counting doors.

Renn came next, moving carefully, his Inquisitor's coat traded for a plain wool thing that made him look like a schoolmaster who'd lost an argument with his laundry. He carried the second lantern and said nothing, which I was learning was how Renn apologized—by being useful and quiet about it. Ash came last, holding my hand, her other hand closed around the scale.

The scale had been warm all day. Not hot. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and Ash kept glancing at it the way you glance at a door you're not sure is locked.

The fourth step did, indeed, lie. It gave under my boot with a crunch like old bread, and I caught myself on the rail while Bramble called up, cheerfully, "Told you."

* * *

You would think, having lived in Crooked Hollow my whole life, that I would know what lay beneath it. But the Hollow is a city built on its own denials. We pave over things. We ledger over things. The Ledger Guild itself sits on foundations nobody questions because questioning foundations is bad for business, and business is the only scripture this town reads regularly.

So when the stairs ended and the lantern light opened out onto a vaulted gallery half-drowned in black, still water, I confess I stopped like a fool and stared.

Arches. Dozens of them, marching away into the dark, each one carved from pale stone gone green-black at the waterline. And on every arch, cut deep enough to survive the flooding, ran letters.

Not our letters.

I knew that now. Three weeks ago I'd have called them decorative grooves and moved along. But I had spent those three weeks with sealed folios open on my knees at two in the morning, teaching myself an alphabet that officially did not exist, and so I read the arches the way you read a name scrawled on a doorpost: *Vessin-kaal. Vessin-mora. Vessin-teth.*

Streets. They were street names.

"Bramble," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened by the vaults. "Do you know what these say?"

"I know they don't say anything in any language I've been fined in," he said. "Which is why I like it down here. Nobody's ever collected a toll on me in this hole."

"They're streets." I put my hand flat against the nearest arch, cold as a dead man's cheek. "*Vessin-kaal.* If the pattern holds—and it does, I've seen it in the folios—that's Kaal Street. This was a street, Bramble. People walked here. There were shopfronts. There were—" I stopped, because my throat had decided to be difficult.

Ash tugged my hand. She was looking up at the arch with an expression I couldn't parse, and then she said something soft in the old tongue, one word, twice, and touched the carving with two fingers the way you'd touch a scar to check if it still hurt.

"What did you say?" Renn asked quietly. He'd been learning too, in his guilty, methodical way. He carried a little notebook now, filled with transliterations in his precise Inquisitor's hand, and the sight of it still made something in my chest twist sideways.

"Home-street," Ash said. Then, after a moment, correcting herself with the terrible precision of children: "Home-*that-was*-street."

Nobody had anything to add to that. We stood in the dark under a drowned street and let a child tell us where we were.

* * *

The water was the problem. Of course the water was the problem; water is always the problem, right up until it isn't.

The gallery floor sloped down ahead of us, and somewhere around the third arch the stone gave way to black water that swallowed the lantern light whole. Bramble tested it with a pole and reported, with the breezy fatalism that had kept him alive through twenty years of smuggling, "Waist-deep for me, chest-deep for you, over-the-head for the little one. Cold as a tax collector's heart."

"We can't carry her through that," I said. "She'll freeze before we're halfway."

"She won't freeze," Ash said.

We all looked at her. She was staring at the water with her head tilted, listening, the way she sometimes listened to rooms before entering them—as though checking whether they were friendly.

"The water remembers," she said. "It's been lying here a long time. Lying on top of everything." She frowned, hunting for words, because the old language had words for things ours didn't and ours had words for things hers didn't, and she lived in the gap between them. "It's tired of being on top."

"Ash," Renn said gently, in the tone he used when he thought he was managing a situation, "water doesn't—"

She stepped forward and spoke to it.

I want to be precise about this, because later, when people asked—and they asked for years—I never wanted to have exaggerated. She didn't shout. She didn't gesture. She spoke the way you speak to a dog that knows its name: low, certain, affectionate. Two sentences in the pre-kingdom tongue, and the second one I understood, because by then I knew maybe forty words of a language older than every kingdom, and one of the words I knew best was *wake.*

The water moved.

Not waves. Not a current. It *parted*—a slow, reluctant division running out from where Ash stood, a channel opening through the flooded gallery like a seam being unpicked, the black water drawing back on either side until there was a path of bare, glistening stone leading down into the dark. The water stood at the edges of it, trembling, held there by nothing, and the sound it made was the sound of a crowd going quiet.

My lantern shook. I'm not ashamed to say it. My lantern shook and my knees had opinions and Bramble said, very quietly, in a voice stripped of all its usual varnish:

"Well. That's new."

"No," Renn said. He had gone the color of paper. He was looking at the parted water the way a man looks at a debt he'd hoped was forgiven. "No, it isn't. The old reports—the sealed ones—they mention the heir commanding the rivers. We were taught it was allegory." He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "We were taught a great deal was allegory."

Ash looked back at us, suddenly uncertain, suddenly eight years old again, her hand tight around the scale. "Is it wrong? Did I do it wrong?"

"You did it perfectly," I told her, and meant it, and knelt to look her in the eye. "But listen to me. When we get where we're going, you rest. You don't spend yourself for our comfort. Understood?"

She nodded, solemn as a guild auditor, and took my hand again, and we walked down the middle of a drowned street between walls of standing water, and I did not look closely at the reflections in those walls, because I had the distinct feeling that some of them were not ours.

* * *

We walked for hours. Or perhaps for one hour that felt like several; time down there was a rumor. The channel Ash had opened narrowed and widened with the old street's moods, and twice we had to wait while she rested on a dry stoop—there were stoops, doorsteps rising out of the flood like the backs of patient animals—and ate dried plums and complained, in the universal dialect of children, that walking was boring.

Boring. Beneath her feet, a country.

At the fifth arch I found a shopfront. That's the only word for it—a recessed doorway with a lintel, and above the lintel a carved sign whose letters I could mostly read. *Oren & Daughters, Bindery.* A bookbinder's shop, drowned for longer than the Guild had existed, and I stood there with my hand on the doorframe feeling like a burglar at a funeral.

"They bound books here," I said. Nobody answered, because what was there to answer. "Somebody's daughters learned their trade on this exact spot, and the Crown wrote the whole street out of existence, and now I'm standing in their doorway with a smuggler and a defected Inquisitor and a child who can part water, and I don't even know their names."

"The ledgers might," Renn said quietly. "Somewhere. The razored pages—we only recovered fragments. But names are hard to erase completely. Names stick." He paused. "That was supposed to be a comforting observation. I'm told I'm poor at those."

"It's the truest thing anyone's said all night," I said. "Keep failing exactly like that."

Bramble, meanwhile, had gone ahead to scout and come back wearing the expression he wore when he'd found cargo worth the risk. "There's a junction ahead," he said. "Gallery splits three ways. And there's a marker—stone post, waist-high, standing in the shallows. Carved all over. Your girl's alphabet, Mara, not mine."

The marker stood where three drowned streets met, and it was taller than Bramble had made it sound—shoulder-high, furred with silt, its carvings worn but legible to someone who'd spent three weeks squinting at razored gaps. I brushed the mud away with my sleeve and read aloud, slowly, translating as I went, getting it wrong twice and letting Ash correct me once.

"*Below the meeting of waters, the Door keeps its own counsel. Knock with fire. Answer with a name.*"

The last two words seemed to echo longer than they should have. Water has good acoustics. That's what I told myself, anyway.

"Answer with a name," Renn repeated. His notebook was out; he wrote it down, then looked at what he'd written as if it had bitten him. "That's specific. That's uncomfortably specific."

"It's a riddle," Bramble said. "Old doors love riddles. Probably means you knock politely and state your business. Like a doorman."

"Doors under cities don't have doormen," I said.

"Everything has a doorman if you're rude enough to it," Bramble said, and started checking the rope coils on his pack, and refused to meet anyone's eye, and I loved him a little for the cowardice of his optimism.

Ash hadn't spoken. She was looking at the marker with the scale held up against it, and the scale was glowing—not coal-warm now but bright, throwing orange light across the carvings, and where the light fell, the worn letters darkened and sharpened, as though the stone remembered being new. Ash traced one line with her finger and read it without hesitation, fluently, the way I'd never manage in ten years.

Then she went very still.

"Ash?" I crouched beside her. "What does it say?"

Her voice, when it came, was small. "It says the Door is asleep. And it says—" She looked up at me, and in the scale-light her eyes were enormous. "It says it misses being knocked on. It says it's been waiting so long it forgot who it opens for." Her chin trembled, just slightly. "Mara. It says it's lonely."

And there, in the drowning dark beneath a city that had been built on top of a murder, with grey coats somewhere overhead and a traitor possibly among us—because I had begun, by then, to count our number and wonder which face didn't fit—I felt the strangest thing. Not fear. Fear was there, threaded through everything like wire, claustrophobia pressing in with the wet stone smell, the knowledge that the water could close over us at any childish whim of exhaustion or ambush. All of that, yes.

But underneath it: pity. Pity for a door.

"All right," I said, and heard my own voice go steady, the ledger-clerk's voice, the one that survives audits and funerals. "Then we'd better not keep it waiting much longer. Which way, Ash?"

Ash turned toward the left-hand gallery, where the water ran deepest and darkest, and the scale flared once in her fist like a heartbeat agreeing.

"That way," she said. "It's happy we're coming."

Behind us, far back along the drowned street, something made a sound. Not water. Not stone settling. A boot, or something shaped like a boot, on a submerged doorstep—and then silence, deliberate and total, the silence of someone who has realized they've made noise and is now being very careful.

Bramble's hand found my shoulder in the dark and squeezed once.

"Don't stop," he breathed. "Don't run. Walk."

We walked.
