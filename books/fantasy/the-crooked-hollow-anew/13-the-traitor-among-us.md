## 13. The Traitor Among Us

There are three rules to running cargo on the canals of Crooked Hollow, and I learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never carry what you can't swim away from. Two: never trust a man who smiles before he's paid you. Three: never, under any circumstances, let anyone know your route — not your mother, not your lover, and certainly not the nervous clerk who asks too many questions while pretending to count barrels.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday, because I trusted a man named Pellum Ostrey, and because I'm a fool with a boat and a soft spot for children who talk in dead languages.

Let me back up.

* * *

The plan, as Mara laid it out in the back room of the Ledger Guild with the lamps shuttered and Ash asleep under a table like a cat who'd learned to distrust doors, was simple enough to fit on a coin.

The ledgers had given us the way down. The drowned cisterns beneath the Hollow, where the street names still swam in Vessian on the old stonework. The Door Out of Vessia — though the more I heard about it, the less I liked the word *out*. Doors that open onto erased countries tend to want payment, and payment, in my experience, is always collected from the person least able to afford it.

But that was Mara's problem, and Odell Vane's problem, and the dying dragon's problem. My problem was smaller and more practical: get a child, a scribe, a fugitive inquisitor, and myself from the Guild's back canal gate to the cistern entrance under the Tanner's Reach without being seen by a single grey coat.

"I know a way," I said. "The flood tunnels. They're technically smuggling routes, which means they're technically mine."

"Technically," Renn said. He was sitting with his back to the wall, the way soldiers sit, like the wall might need defending. He'd been a fugitive for four days and he still hadn't learned to relax. Inquisitors. Give them a lifetime of sin and they'll still sit like the furniture is reporting on them.

"Technically," I agreed. "The Wardens' Office thinks the flood tunnels collapsed in the Sinking Year. The Wardens' Office is wrong, because I have spent eleven years making sure they stay wrong. There's a stretch of the old Vessian quarter that runs under the Reach — dry, mostly, if you don't mind the smell of a century of things I'd rather not name."

Mara looked at me across the table. She had ink on her fingers and exhaustion in the set of her mouth, and she was, I want it noted, the only person in that room who looked like the weight of two countries was actually resting on her shoulders. The rest of us were just carrying luggage.

"Bramble," she said. "Are you sure? Once you're in, you're in. Renn's already burned. I'm already burned. You could still walk away and be nobody's smuggler but your own."

"I could," I said. "But then who'd carry the torches? And the brandy? And the child who can talk to water?"

Under the table, Ash stirred. She'd taken to sleeping with the scale in her fist, and sometimes in the dark it glowed through her fingers like she was holding a coal that loved her. She looked up at me with those too-old eyes and said something in the old tongue, soft, and Mara translated it in that careful voice she used for translations:

"She says, 'The boat woman keeps her promises.'"

"Well," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing in the middle of the word, "the boat woman does."

That was Tuesday. I told Pellum Ostrey my route on Wednesday, and I'll tell you exactly why, because the why matters more than the what.

* * *

Pellum had been a guild clerk for twenty-two years, which is long enough to grow moss on a man. He was the one who'd first shown Mara the razored gaps in the sealed folios — I learned that later, and it made what came after worse, or better, or both. He was a small, damp, apologetic sort of man who counted everything twice and apologized to the barrels when he moved them. He had a wife with bad lungs and two daughters, and he'd once, years ago, looked the other way while I moved a shipment of untaxed brandy through the Guild's receiving dock, because I'd slipped him enough to cover a winter's coal and he'd slipped me a nod.

So when he cornered me by the canal gate on Wednesday — nervous, wringing his hands, asking in that awful casual way nervous people ask — I told him. Not all of it. Just the route. The Reach, the flood tunnel, the hour.

"Purely for the ledgers," he said. "The Guild will want a record of, ah, of the movement of persons through Guild-adjacent passages."

"Pellum." I put a hand on his shoulder and felt him flinch like a struck bell. "There is no record. There is no movement of persons. There is a girl and a scribe and a walk in the dark, and if a single grey coat is standing where you're standing right now, in about a year, I will find you, and I will not hurt you, but I will tell your wife about the brandy."

He laughed. It was a terrible laugh, the laugh of a man rehearsing. I mistook it for relief.

Here's the thing I've had a long time to think about since: I don't think Pellum betrayed us because he was wicked. I think he betrayed us because he was small, and the Inquisition is very large, and they'd found the two softest things in his life — his daughters — and held them up like coins. I've been angry at that man every hour since, and I've also, in my worst moments, understood him completely, and I hate that understanding more than I hate him.

He asked me the route twice. I should have noticed that. People who are keeping records ask once. People who are memorizing ask twice.

* * *

We went down at the turn of the tide, which is the only honest hour in Crooked Hollow — the water stops pretending to go anywhere and just sits there, thinking.

I led. Behind me came Mara with a shuttered lantern, then Renn with Ash on his back, which was a sight I never expected to see and will carry to my grave: a Crown inquisitor, his grey coat swapped for plain wool, carrying a Vessian child through the dark like she weighed nothing. Ash had her arms around his neck and the scale in her fist, and every few steps it pulsed, faint and warm, like a heartbeat under a blanket.

"Smells like a century of things you'd rather not name," Renn observed.

"I did warn you."

"You did. I assumed smuggler hyperbole."

"I never exaggerate. Exaggeration is a form of record-keeping, and I don't keep records. That's Mara's job."

Behind us, Mara made a sound that in a healthier week might have been a laugh.

The tunnel under the Tanner's Reach was exactly as I'd left it: dry-ish, low-ceilinged, walled in stone so old the chisel marks had softened into something like waves. And there — I saw Mara's lantern hand drift toward it — a stretch of wall where letters ran in a band, worn but stubborn. Vessian. Ash made a small sound against Renn's neck and reached out one hand toward the letters as we passed, the way you'd reach for a sleeping relative.

"What does it say?" Renn asked quietly.

Mara translated slowly. "It's a street name. *Ember Row.*" A pause. "There's a street in the Hollow called Tanner's Row. Same street. They just — renamed it. Over it."

"Countries do that," Renn said. "Rename things. It's cheaper than remembering them."

We walked on. I want you to understand how good it felt, that stretch of the walk. We were four people and a coal-warm scale moving through the bones of a murdered country, and we were going to fix it, and I was the one who knew the way. I've spent my whole life being the man who knows the way to places that officially don't exist. It's the only kind of pride I have, and for about an hour that night, it was enough.

Then we came to the bend at the old cistern junction, where the tunnel splits three ways, and the scale in Ash's fist flared — not the warm pulse, but a hard, urgent red — and Ash said one word, loud, in a language none of us spoke but all of us understood.

"*Down!*"

I've been ambushed twice before in my career. Both times by rival smugglers, both times over cargo, both times resolved with shouting and one memorable thrown boot. This was not that.

The crossbows spoke from the dark of the left-hand tunnel — the one I had NOT told anyone about, which is how I knew, in the single cold second before the bolts came, exactly what had happened and to whom I owed it. Three bolts. Grey coats don't fire three bolts at smugglers. Smugglers get arrested. Grey coats fire three bolts at fugitives they've been ordered to make permanent.

I didn't think. I want that on the record — my record, the one I keep nowhere, the one that's just me. I didn't think. I heard the child scream the warning and I heard the strings release and my body did the arithmetic without me: Renn had Ash on his back, which meant his hands were full, which meant the small warm thing was the one standing in the line of fire.

I stepped left. Into it.

The bolt took me high in the chest, left side, and I will tell you honestly that the first thing I felt was not pain. It was surprise — a hard punch of it, like the canal in January — and then the pain arrived, late and enormous, and I went down onto stone that smelled like a century of things I'd rather not name.

* * *

Here is what I know of the fight, which is not much, because I spent most of it on the floor conducting negotiations with my own lungs.

I know Renn turned so his back took whatever came next, and I know Mara did something with the lantern — threw it, I think, or swung it — and I know there was shouting, grey-coat shouting, the flat procedural voices of men reading from a list. I know Ash was screaming my name — my actual name, *Bramble*, in her mother-tongue it comes out different, softer, like *bramble* means something kind — and I know that the water in the cistern junction, the black standing water, rose.

I know this because I was lying next to it. It rose like something standing up out of a chair. It came over the lip of the cistern in a wall, and it did not behave like water. It behaved like a dog that has heard its person cry out. It went over the grey coats in a cold rush, and the screaming changed key, and then there were boots running — theirs, away, downstream into tunnels I'd have paid good money to see them navigate in the dark.

And then it was quiet, and Mara was on her knees next to me, and her hands were pressing my chest and they were shaking, and she was saying my name in the ordinary way, over and over, like a tally she couldn't balance.

"Don't," I said. It came out as mostly bubbles. "Don't do the voice. The one where you're reading bad news off a page."

"Shut up, Bramble."

"There she is."

Renn had Ash. Ash had the scale, and the scale was burning — actually burning, throwing light like a forge, and the child was holding it against my chest, against the wound, and saying words in the old language, fast, urgent, a whole litany of them, and I felt — this is going to sound like brandy, and I hadn't had any — I felt the fire go *looking*. Through me. Not healing, not yet. Searching. Like a finger running down a ledger, finding my line, checking whether I was owed more time.

"Is it working?" Mara asked, and her voice cracked clean in half on *working*.

Ash looked up at her, and the child's face was streaked with tears and lit from below like a hearth, and she said something long and low. Mara listened, and then translated, and her voice was the flattest I've ever heard it, which is how I knew it was the worst news she'd ever delivered:

"She says the fire remembers me. She says it will hold the wound shut. But she says — " Mara stopped. Started again. "She says it can't do more than that. It's saving itself. It's saving itself for the dragon."

"For the door," Renn said quietly.

"For whatever's coming," Mara said.

The scale dimmed to its usual coal-glow. The pain in my chest settled into something deep and slow, a fire banked for the night. I could breathe. It cost me, but I could breathe, and I lay there on the stones of Ember Row and laughed, or made the sound that passes for laughing in a man with a bolt-hole in him.

"Bramble." Mara again. She'd gone very still. "What's funny?"

"Pellum," I said. "He asked me the route twice."

I watched her understand it. I watched it land on her the way the bolt had landed on me — the punch first, the pain after. She'd known Pellum twenty years. He'd shown her the razored pages. He'd been, in his damp apologetic way, the first crack of daylight in the whole rotten archive.

"He has daughters," she said.

"He has daughters," I agreed. "And the Inquisition found them. And he's a small man, Mara. And they are so very large."

She sat back on her heels. Above us, water dripped from the cistern lip in a slow, counting rhythm. Ash crawled over and put her small hand flat on my forehead, the way you'd check a fever, or bless someone, or both, and said one word. Mara didn't translate it. She didn't have to. Some words you can hear in any language, and that one was *stay*.

"I'm staying," I told the child. "I've got a route to finish. And a clerk to forgive, eventually, which will be the harder journey of the two."

Renn crouched at the tunnel mouth, listening to the dark, and when he turned back his face had gone inquisitor-flat, which on him now meant fear. "They'll report the ambush within the hour. They know the junction. They'll map the rest of the route from Pellum's statement — if he gave it all." He paused. "We have to move. Tonight. All the way down. Before they seal the cisterns."

"Tonight," Mara said.

I looked up at her from the floor, this debt-ridden scribe who'd started all this by refusing to turn a child out into the rain, and I saw what the night had already written on her face. She was done being careful. Whatever came next — the drowned streets, the dragon, the door, the price — she was going to pay it, and God help me, so was I.

"Right," I said, and got my arm around her shoulder, and got my feet under me, which hurt like a second bolt, and stood up. "Somebody carry the brandy. The boat woman's done walking slow."

Above us, far away, through a hundred feet of stone and a drowned country's worth of silence, I would have sworn I felt something shift — a groan in the deep cisterns, like a great body turning over in its sleep. Or waking.

Ash's scale flared once, red as a warning, and pointed *down*.
