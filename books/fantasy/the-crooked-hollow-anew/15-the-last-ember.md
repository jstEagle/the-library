## 15. The Last Ember

The scale stopped being warm and started being *loud*.

That is the only way I can say it. Mara says things are loud when they are loud, like thunder, like Bramble singing in the boat. But this was loud inside my chest, where nobody else could hear it, and it pulled the way a rope pulls when someone you love is holding the other end.

"Which way?" Mara asked. She had my hand. She had been holding my hand since we came down through the water-door, and I think she thought she was keeping me safe, but really we were keeping each other safe, which is a different thing and better.

I held up the scale. It glowed orange at one edge, like a coal breathing.

"That way," I said. "Down. And then down more."

Bramble made a face. Bramble makes faces the way other people make sentences. "Down more," he repeated. "Wonderful. My boots have opinions about down more, and none of them are kind."

His side was still wrapped where the bolt went through him in the dark tunnel. He was supposed to be lying down. He had said, very seriously, that he would rather bleed walking forward than bleeding standing still, and Renn — the grey coat who was not a grey coat anymore — had looked at him for a long moment and then said nothing, which from an Inquisitor is practically a hug.

So we went down more.

* * *

The cisterns under Crooked Hollow are not really cisterns anymore. That is what I learned as we walked. They are streets. Old ones. The water had come in after, the way water does, patient and rude, and now it stood knee-deep in places that used to be somebody's kitchen.

I knew because I could read the walls.

Not with letters. With *names*. The street names were carved over the arches, and they were in my language — the language before names, the language Mama sang to me before the country folded up like paper. I had almost forgotten I knew them. Words come back the way birds come back to a roof. You do not decide. They just land.

"*Vellum Row*," I read, touching the carving. "*The Street of Nine Bells.* *Weaver's Grief.*"

"Weaver's Grief," Mara repeated softly. She wrote everything down, even now, even wet, her little book held up out of the water like a cat she was rescuing. "Ash, what did that last one mean? Literally?"

"A weaver who lost someone," I said. "It means the street remembers her."

Mara stopped writing for a second. Just a second. Then she wrote faster.

Renn walked behind us with his lantern held low. He kept looking at the carvings too, and his face did something complicated each time, like a door opening onto a room full of furniture he recognized from childhood. Once he whispered something under his breath. It was half a lullaby. I turned around and finished it for him, and he went very still, and then he said, "My grandmother would have liked you," in a voice like wet paper, and we kept walking.

The water got deeper. Then, all at once, it got shallower, because the floor rose, and the ceiling rose with it, and the air changed.

It smelled like warm stone. Like bread left in an oven too long. Like the inside of a word.

"There," I said.

* * *

The hall was round, and it had not been drowned. Whatever water ruled the rest of the underworld, it had agreed to stop at this threshold, and the threshold was a ring of tiles laid in a pattern I knew from the inside of my own eyelids: the coiled circle, the wing folded around the bell. The sigil on the guild's seal. The sigil on Mama's ring. The sigil on me, maybe, though mine was under my skin where no one could stamp it.

And in the middle of the hall, the dragon.

I want to tell you she was enormous and terrible and beautiful, and she was, but mostly — mostly she was *thin*. That is the thing nobody tells you about dragons. When they get old, they get old the way fires do: not smaller exactly, but *hungrier*, burning themselves to keep burning. Her scales were the color of old coins and riverbeds and the sky right before snow. Half of her had gone grey as ash, and where the grey touched her, she was see-through, like smoke pretending to be a body.

She was talking to herself.

That is what the sound was, the sound we had heard growing louder for hours, the low rumble through the stones. She was saying names. Streets and rivers and people and songs, one after another, in a voice like a hearth learning to speak. Every name she said, the air above her flickered, and in the flicker I saw things: a market with striped awnings. A bridge with lanterns. A woman laughing while dough rose on a windowsill.

"She's remembering Vessia aloud," Mara breathed. "That's how it's still — that's how any of it exists."

"The erasure took the records," Renn said quietly. He had taken off his grey coat somewhere below and now just looked like a tired man in a good shirt. "Took the maps, the histories, the names from every ledger in every kingdom. But it couldn't take *her*. She was never written down. She was the writing."

The dragon's eye opened.

It was gold, and it was kind, and it was so tired that looking into it felt like being handed something heavy without being asked first.

"*Little ember*," she said.

Her voice did not go into my ears. It went into my ribs. Bramble grabbed Mara's arm. Renn put his hand on his sword and then, wisely, took it off again.

"You know me?" I asked.

"I named your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother, and hers, and hers." The dragon's head came down, slow as a season changing, until her breath moved my hair. It smelled like every fire I had ever been warm beside. "I have been waiting a long time to say the next part, little ember. I am sorry it comes so late, and so heavy."

* * *

She told us things while we sat on the warm tiles, because dragons do not hurry and neither, it turns out, does grief.

Her name — her true name, the one she let us hold for a moment and then take back, the way you let someone smell bread but not eat it — was older than the kingdom that erased her country. She had been Vessia's keeper before Vessia had walls. When the Crown came with its ink and its inquisitors and its terrible clean idea of a world with one story in it, the Vessians had not fought with swords. Swords lose to swords. They had brought their whole country to her, every song and street and grandmother, and asked her to *hold* it, the way you ask the sea to hold a message in a bottle.

"And I said yes," the dragon said, "because I loved them, and love is the only yes that cannot be unsaid."

"So you've been down here," Mara said. Her voice wobbled. "Underneath us. All this time. While the Hollow was built on top of you. While the guild stamped its seal on—" She stopped. Her hand went to the ledger in her coat, the one with the razored pages, the one Odell Vane had caught her reading and said nothing about.

"While the debt was counted," the dragon said, gently, in the way that meant she saw all of it, the whole shape of Mara's life, and was not judging, only sad. "Yes, scribe. I felt every footstep. I remembered every name they cut out of your books, so it would not be lost. That is what I am doing now. That is all I have been doing for a hundred years. Remembering aloud. A country is only ever one generation of forgetting away from dying, and I am the last one who remembers, and I am—" she paused, and the pause was worse than anything she could have said, "—nearly done."

The grey part of her shifted. Underneath it, I could see straight through to the wall.

Nobody said anything. Bramble sat down heavily on a tile, which for Bramble is a prayer. Renn took off his glove and pressed his bare hand flat against the floor, like he was taking an oath to the stone itself.

"But the Door," Mara said finally. "There's a Door Out of Vessia. Beneath the Hollow. We found the references, we decoded—"

"You found the *front* of it," the dragon said. "A door has two sides, scribe. And a lock. And a price. But none of that matters yet, because a door is only wood and hinges unless there is a key with blood in it." Her great eye turned to me. "Unless there is an heir."

My chest went cold and hot at the same time, which I did not know was possible.

"I'm not—" I started. "I'm just Ash. I'm eight. I can't even reach the high shelf."

"You are the last leaf of a tree they burned to the roots," the dragon said. "You speak the tongue before names. You carry my scale, which I gave to your mother's line the day Vessia folded, so that someday it could find its way home. And you are eight." Something moved across her face that on a person would have been a smile. "Every heir in history has been too small for what they carried. That is what heirs *are*, little ember. Too-small people carrying too-big things, until the carrying makes them big enough."

"I don't want to be big enough," I said, and my voice cracked right down the middle, and I hated it. "I want my mama. I want our kitchen. I want the country back the way it *was*, with everybody in it, not — not a door, not a price, not a flame. Why does everything cost something? Why can't one thing just be *given*?"

The hall was quiet except for the drip of far-off water counting seconds.

Then the dragon lowered her head all the way to the tiles, so that her forehead nearly touched mine, and her voice went soft, softer than the lullaby, softer than paper folding.

"Because you were given one already," she said. "Your mother gave you the scale. Your country gave you the tongue. I am giving you the last of me. Everything that survives, survives because someone gave it. That is not the tragedy, little ember. That is the *mechanism*."

* * *

She stood up then, all of her, and the hall filled with her shadow, and I understood that what happened next was going to happen whether I was brave or not, so I decided to be brave anyway, because if you have to do a thing you might as well do it standing.

"This is the rite," she said. "Hear it, witnesses. Scribe. Smuggler. Turned coat." Renn flinched; the dragon's eye crinkled. "Yes, I know what you are. Good. The world needs more of those."

She drew one claw — gentle, always gentle, the way the biggest things are gentle or they break everything — and touched it to the glowing scale at my throat.

"Ash, daughter of Vessia, last leaf of the tree: I name you heir. Heir of the held country. Keeper of the remembering. Warden of the Door." The words sank into me like heat into cold hands. "And with the name, the charge: what I have carried alone, you will carry — but not alone. That is the difference. That is the *only* difference, and it is enough."

"It doesn't feel like enough," I whispered.

"It never does," she said. "Now. My last ember. Are you ready?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Good," she said. "Only fools are ready."

And she breathed.

Not fire the way stories say fire — no roaring, no wall of heat. She breathed out the *inside* of herself, a hundred years of held names and held streets and held mornings, all of it gathered down to one coal-bright point that floated off her tongue like a spark off a log, drifting, drifting, and landing —

— on the scale.

The scale drank it. I felt it drink. It went through the metal and through my skin and into the place where the loudness lived, and suddenly I was not a girl holding a scale. I was a girl holding a *country*. I could feel every street. The Street of Nine Bells rang when I thought about it. Weaver's Grief ached when I touched it. There was a hill with a plum tree, and a ferryman's song with a wrong note everyone loved, and four million names, and I knew them, I *knew* them, all of them, and they were so heavy—

I fell to my knees on the warm tiles and cried, because it was beautiful and it was unbearable and both of those things were true in the same heartbeat, and Mara knelt with me and held me and did not say *shh*, which was right, because some things should not be shushed.

When I could see again, the hall was dimmer.

The dragon lay where she had stood, and the grey had won. Only her outline remained, smoke-shaped, going soft at the edges like chalk in rain. But her eye was still open, and still gold, and still looking at me.

"Little heir," she said, and her voice was thin as the last page of a book. "One thing more. The Door opens soon — I can feel feet on my streets, grey coats, many, coming down. And the price at the door is crueler than you know. Someone will have to pay it. Watch who volunteers. Love them well beforehand." Her gaze slid, deliberate as a signature, to Mara. To Mara, and then back to me. "Promise me you will not let the paying be lonely."

"I promise," I said, not understanding yet.

"Then I am done," said the dragon, "and it was — it was a good country. Tell them. Tell them it was a *good* country—"

And she came apart into embers, thousands of them, rising like reversed snow, filling the round hall with drifting light, and every single one of them, as it passed me, whispered a name — a street, a song, a grandmother, a bell — handing them over, one by one by one, until the last ember winked out against the ceiling and the hall was just a hall, and the silence where she had been was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

I stood up. The scale at my throat burned steady and sure, pointing — not down anymore.

*Up*. Toward the surface. Toward the Hollow. Toward the Door.

And beneath my feet, faint but unmistakable, through the old Vessian stones, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Grey coats, coming down.

"They're here," Renn said, and drew his sword at last.
