## 18. Unwritten

The first thing I notice about being unwritten is that it doesn't hurt.

I had braced for pain. You would too, I think, if you'd spent your whole life learning that everything costs—that ink costs, that bread costs, that a name costs most of all. I had imagined the surrender of my name as a kind of flaying, the slow peeling of Mara Quintana off the bone of me. I had stepped through the Door expecting to be unmade screaming.

Instead there is light like late afternoon through honey, and under my feet a road that is not stone but something warmer, and the smell of bread baking somewhere, which is absurd, because I have not eaten in two days and dead women do not get hungry.

Except I am not dead. That's the first thing the road tells me, or maybe the road is just where I've decided to stand while I work out what I am.

* * *

Vessia is whole.

I want you to understand what that sentence does to a person who has spent months reading its ghost in razored margins. Vessia is *whole*. The bell tower stands at the end of the street with the dragon coiled around it—not the dying, grey-embered ruin Ash led us to beneath the Hollow, but the dragon as it was, scales like hammered copper, one vast eye half-lidded and ancient and amused. The streets run down toward a river the color of struck flint. Laundry lines cross overhead strung with shirts and small dresses turning slowly in a wind that smells of salt and cedar. Somewhere a fiddle is playing a tune I have never heard and somehow know every note of.

People pass me on the road. A woman carrying a basket of pears nods to me the way neighbors nod. A boy chases a dog between the stalls of a market that was erased before my grandmother was born, and the dog barks, and the sound of it is so ordinary that I have to stop walking and press my hand against a wall just to feel the plaster give, because ordinary is the thing I never thought Vessia would get to be again.

This is what my name bought. This is what it cost. Both at once, the way everything in Crooked Hollow is both at once—debt and gift, ledger and lie.

* * *

Here is the mechanism, as best I can explain it from inside, which is like explaining drowning from underwater.

The Door opens onto Vessia only if someone gives up their name entirely. Not a pseudonym. Not an alias to be shrugged off later. The name itself—the whole architecture of Mara Quintana, scribe of the Ledger Guild, debtor of Odell Vane, daughter of a ruined family, the girl who counted impossible things and kept her head down and learned to make herself small enough to survive—all of it surrendered into the threshold, spent like coin, gone.

And so Vessia has me the way a fire has wood. I am here because I am burning.

I walk the streets and they are beautiful and they are real and every hour—I think it is hours; time moves strangely here, like syrup off a spoon—every hour I am a little less sure which parts of me came through the Door.

My hands still know how to hold a pen. But when I try to remember who taught me, the memory slides. There was a guildmaster. There was a man who bought a child's debt and called it kindness and called his guilt something gentler. His face is—there was a beard? No. Clean-shaven. Grey at the temples. I can see his hands counting coins and I cannot see his eyes, and I know, I *know*, that this is not forgetfulness. This is subtraction. Somewhere behind me, in the world I left, my entries are blanking out of the ledgers line by line. My name is coming off the debt rolls. My name is coming off the apprenticeship register. My name is coming out of mouths.

That last one is the one that frightens me. Ink I can abide. But somewhere Bramble Fenwick is trying to say my name and finding a shape in her mouth with nothing in it, and Renn is writing a report and pausing over a blank space where a witness should be, and—

And Ash.

* * *

Ash remembers things differently than ledgers do. I didn't know that when I stepped forward. I knew it only as a hope, thin as thread, and hopes are poor currency even in a country built from them.

But this is what happens:

I am standing in the market square—it is evening now, lamps coming on one by one like the city is opening its eyes—and the light begins to close.

Not the daylight. The *light*. The golden substance of the threshold itself, the thing that holds Vessia open the way a held breath holds a song. It is drawing in at the edges, contracting, and I understand with a clarity that feels like cold water that the Door is deciding. It gave me Vessia. Now it wants to know if I am finished. If the payment is complete. If the woman who walked in has been fully converted into the country she purchased.

I look down at my hands and they are translucent at the edges. Not fading like smoke. Fading like *ink lifted off a page*—the letters going pale, going ghostly, going back to paper. I press my palm flat against a market stall and the wood shows through it, grain and all, and the pear-seller woman looks straight through me and asks her husband whether he's seen her basket anywhere, and I realize she nodded to me earlier out of courtesy to someone who was already mostly not there.

"Wait," I say, and my voice comes out thin, a whisper played at the wrong speed. "Wait, I'm not done. I haven't—there were people. There were people I—"

There were people I what?

I reach for the word and it isn't a word anymore. It's a shape. A small fierce shape with rain in her hair and a scale that glows like a coal, standing in a doorway on a night I can almost—almost—no. Gone. The doorway is gone. The night is gone. Only the shape remains, and the feeling, and the feeling is enormous, it is the largest thing left in me, bigger than my name ever was: *do not let go of the small fierce shape.*

"I don't remember your name," I tell the empty air, and laughing and weeping turn out to be the same motion when you're dissolving. "I don't remember any of your names. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I paid with the wrong—no. No, I paid with the right thing. I paid exactly what it cost. I just wish I could keep the receipt."

Somewhere a bell rings. The dragon's bell, up in the tower, and the sound of it goes through me like a hand through water.

* * *

Then the light tears.

Not gently. The closing seam of the threshold splits along a line no one drew, and through the tear comes a voice—a child's voice, high and furious and cracked with crying, speaking the language older than any kingdom, the pre-tongue, the first words, and though I cannot translate them I understand them perfectly, the way you understand fire understands wood.

She is shouting my name.

She doesn't have it. Nobody has it. I checked; it's not anywhere; I am a blank entry, an erasure, a margin with nothing written in it. And this eight-year-old girl is standing on the far side of the closing light shouting syllables that mean nothing and meaning them *completely*, because Ash does not need the ledger's version of me. She has her own. She wrote it herself, in ember-light, in the old language, in a book that didn't exist until she made it exist, and her version of my name is not the one I surrendered. It's better. It's mine but more so. It is my name the way Vessia is the Hollow's—same bones, new country.

"Ash," I say—or I try to say it, and find I don't have the word *Ash* either, only the shape, only the small fierce shape, and I say the shape with everything left in me. "*Here.* I'm here. I'm mostly here. I'm—"

I am a hand pressed against glass from the wrong side. I am a signature lifting off the page. The light is nearly closed. Through the narrowing gap I can see her—actually see her, small and blazing, hair wild, one fist raised with the scale in it glowing like a captive sunrise, and behind her other shapes: a broad one leaning wrong, hurt, alive; a tall one in grey who should be my enemy and chose otherwise; an old one, stooped, who I think—I think—was kind, once, in the way that guilt is kind.

They are all shouting. I can't hear words anymore. I can hear *them*, though. Voices are harder to erase than names. Someone should write that down. Someone should put it in a ledger: *names fade; voices linger.* I would write it myself but I no longer have hands, exactly, or a self to have them.

The gap is a knife-blade wide. Ash puts her palm against it, against the light itself, and the scale flares white, and I feel her reaching—not for Mara Quintana, debtor, scribe, erased; she can't reach that woman, that woman is spent—but for the other thing, the truer thing, the thing underneath the name that no ledger ever managed to record anyway.

She reaches for the person who opened the door for her on a rainy night when everyone else said turn her out.

And I—

I reach back.

It is not much of a reach. I am mostly light and stubbornness by then. But I have spent my whole life being competent at small invisible things, holding pens steady, keeping tallies true, making myself small enough to survive, and it turns out all of that was practice for this: one final act of precise, weary, desperate competence, threading what's left of me through a closing seam toward a child's open hand.

Our fingers almost touch.

Almost.

The light closes.

* * *

Darkness. Or not darkness—*paper*. I am in the white of an unwritten page, and I am calm, which is the worst part, because calm means I am nearly gone, because Mara Quintana was never calm in her life, she was anxious and wry and tired and stubborn, and whoever is floating here now is none of those things, whoever is floating here is nobody.

*Nobody,* I think, testing the word. It fits too well.

But underneath the nobody, deeper than the name ever reached, there is a residue. A sediment. Things too heavy to dissolve: the smell of rain on a guild door. Bread baking in a country that shouldn't exist. A fiddle tune I never heard and know by heart. And a warmth in one hand, faint as a coal wrapped in ash, where a small fierce shape pressed her fingers against the closing light and did not let go fast enough—or did not let go at all.

Something crossed over with me. Something she pushed through the seam in the last instant, the way you shove a letter through a door before it shuts. I cannot read it. I have no eyes, no hands, no name to read it *with*.

But I can hold it.

Whoever I am now, I can hold that.

And far away—or very near; distance is a rumor in this place—I hear a pen scratching. Not a guild pen. An ember-dipped point dragging across new paper, in the old language, in a hand that trembles with effort and does not stop, writing and writing and writing, and every stroke of it lands somewhere in my chest like a stitch pulling a wound closed.

She is writing me back.

She is writing me into a book that has no guild seal, no debt column, no Crown above it. She is writing me as a citizen. She is writing me as hers.

I don't have a name yet.

But I think—I think I am about to be given one.
