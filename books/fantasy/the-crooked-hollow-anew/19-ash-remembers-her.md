## 19. Ash Remembers Her

The ember does not want to be held.

I learn this the way I learned everything in Crooked Hollow, which is to say by burning my fingers first and asking questions after. The dragon's last flame sits in my palm like a small angry bird, all heat and opinion, and it keeps trying to fly back toward the dark place where the old country used to be. I hold on anyway. I am good at holding on. Bramble says stubbornness is a family trait of mine now, since she's decided she's my family, and families share things, even bad ones.

Mara is fading.

I can feel it happening the way you can feel a tooth being loose — not pain exactly, just a wrongness that your tongue keeps going back to. One moment ago (or one year ago; time inside the Door is a badly kept ledger) she stepped forward and gave her name away, and the Door drank it like sweet tea, and now Vessia is opening up on the other side, green and gold and smelling of bread, and Mara Quintana is becoming nobody.

I know what becoming nobody feels like. I was almost nobody when I came to the guild door in the rain. The wardens wanted to make me nobody faster. They had forms for it, probably. Inquisitors always have forms.

But here is the thing nobody in this whole crooked city understands, not Odell with his guilt, not Renn with his law books, not even Mara with her beautiful careful handwriting: names are not given. Names are *kept*. Somebody has to keep them. That's what my mother told me before the country folded up like paper in her hands. *As long as someone remembers your name, little coal, you are somewhere.* She said it in the old language, the language before kingdoms, the language that sounds like water deciding which way to go.

So I will keep her name. That is the whole plan. It is not a very big plan. Bramble would say it needs more smuggling in it.

* * *

The problem is paper.

Vessia had paper, wonderful paper made from river-reed, but Vessia is on the other side of a door that only opens for people who don't exist anymore, so that's not helpful. The Hollow has paper too, but all of it belongs to the Ledger Guild, and every page of Ledger Guild paper is stitched into the debt-records, and if I write Mara's name on debt-paper it won't be a name at all, it'll be an amount owed, and Mara has spent her whole life being an amount owed. I am not doing that to her. I would rather burn the whole guild down, and I know exactly how, because I have a dragon's fire in my hand and honestly it's mostly enthusiasm.

Renn finds me sitting on the cistern steps with the ember cupped against my chest, glaring at nothing.

"You look," he says, "exactly like the guildmaster did, forty years ago, according to his own confession. Same glare."

"Good," I say. "He deserved to be glared at."

"He did." Renn sits down next to me, which still surprises me every time. Grey coats aren't supposed to sit down next to children. Grey coats are supposed to stand very straight and write things down that ruin you. But Renn's grandmother sang him lullabies in my language when he was small, he told me, and he didn't know what they were until this week, and when he found out he cried in a stairwell where he thought no one could see. I saw. I see lots of things. Being eight means everyone forgets you're looking.

"The Crown's records won't serve," Renn says, following my stare to the ember. "Anything written under Crown seal belongs to the Crown. If we write her there, they own the name again."

"Then we need paper that belongs to nobody."

"There is no such paper."

"There is no such *country* either," I say, "and we're making one of those, so."

He looks at me for a long moment. Then he does something with his mouth that I think is the beginning of a smile getting lost on the way. "What do you need?"

"A book that hasn't been written in yet. And Bramble. And everyone who loved her, standing close enough to remember out loud."

* * *

Bramble brings the book.

Of course Bramble brings the book. Bramble Fenwick can produce anything from inside her coat, up to and including, I am fairly sure, another smaller coat. She comes up the cistern stairs two at a time with water sloshing off her boots and a bundle under her arm wrapped in oilcloth, and she unwraps it with the care other people use for babies or bombs.

"Canal salvage," she says. "Pulled it out of a drowned warehouse three years back. Waterlogged blank folio, no guild stamp, no Crown seal, no owner. Sat in my hold ever since because I couldn't bring myself to sell something with no name on it." She shrugs, which for Bramble is a whole speech about feelings. "Seems it was waiting."

The pages smell like the bottom of the canal, which is to say like secrets, but they're dry now and strong. A book with no owner. A book that belongs to nobody, which means it can belong to everybody, which means —

"It's perfect," I say.

"Yeah, well." She ruffles my hair, which I permit because she bled for me and also because her hands are gentle for a smuggler. "Don't tell anyone I did something sentimental. I've got a reputation. It's a terrible reputation and I've worked hard on it."

Then people start arriving, and this is the part I will remember for the rest of my life, however long the new country lets me live.

They come down into the underworld in ones and twos, the way people come when they're not sure they're allowed. Exiles from the flooded streets. Smugglers who owe Bramble favors. Guild clerks with ink-stained fingers and guilty faces, including the traitor clerk, who stands at the very back wringing his hands, and nobody sends him away, because Mara wouldn't have, and we are doing this the way Mara would have done it, which is to say with room in it for people who failed. Old Ferro the lamplighter, who once looked the other way when a child ran past him in the rain. Women from the night market who fed Ash-and-Mara soup when Ash-and-Mara were hiding. Even the wardens, two of them, who wanted to turn me out at the door in chapter — in the beginning. They take their hats off. Nobody tells them to.

Renn counts them quietly. "Twenty-six witnesses," he murmurs. "In the old law — the real old law, older than the Crown — a name entered before twenty witnesses cannot be struck by any office."

"How do you know the old law?" I ask.

"My grandmother," he says simply. "She taught me my letters from a book she wasn't supposed to have. I thought it was a recipe book." He pauses. "It may also have been a recipe book. Vessians took their cooking seriously."

And then, because the ember is warm against my chest and the book is open on my knees and everyone is waiting, I understand what I have to do, and I am scared of it, and I do it anyway, because that's what everyone else in this story keeps doing, so apparently it works.

* * *

Writing with dragon-fire is not like writing with ink.

Ink goes where you push it. Fire goes where the truth already is. I dip the ember against the first page the way my mother dipped her reed pen, and the flame doesn't scorch the paper — it *finds* it, sinks into it, and letters rise up like they were always hiding there, waiting, in the language before names:

*Here begins the ledger of the country that is being born.*

My hand shakes. The fire steadies it. The fire knows this work. This is what dragons were *for*, I think, before everyone decided they were for hoarding and burning. My dragon spent its whole long dying life remembering a country aloud so the country would stay real. Now I'm the dragon. It's a lot of responsibility for someone who still isn't allowed to cross the canal alone, but nobody ever asks the heir if she's ready. You're just ready or you're ash.

I write the country first. Not Vessia — Vessia is behind us, folded up, resting, and maybe someday it will forgive us, but you can't build a house out of grief. I write what I saw through the Door: green hills with the Hollow's crooked rooftops growing at their edges like lichen, two peoples' streets stitching together at the seam, a bell tower with a dragon coiled around it, except the dragon is a carving now, its work finished, its fire living in a book instead.

Then I write the citizens. Freely given names, one by one. Bramble Fenwick, ferrywoman between worlds. Halvard Renn, who chose conscience over career and got both ruined and saved, which is the usual price. Odell Vane — I write his name even though he's dead, especially because he's dead, because the dead are the ones who most need keeping, and because he held the tunnel door open with his body and his last word was a gift. The clerks. The smugglers. The lamplighter. The wardens. Twenty-six names, and the fire takes each one gently, like hands accepting eggs.

And then the page where Mara should be.

And the fire flinches.

* * *

This is the hard part, so I'm going to say it plainly, the way Ash says things, because I promised myself I'd never hide the worst part the way the ledgers hid Vessia.

There is no name to write.

I reach for *Mara Quintana* and it's like reaching into a drawer that's been emptied. The word *Mara* is a shape with nothing in it. The word *Quintana* tastes like somebody else's memory. She gave it away — willingly, bravely, infuriatingly, without asking any of us, which is the most Mara thing she ever did and I am going to be angry at her about it forever if she ever exists again to be angry at.

The ember gutters in my palm. Small. Doubtful. Fire that remembers needs something to remember, and the world is forgetting her as I kneel there. I can feel it going out of the room — the witnesses blinking, frowning, one woman saying *who are we waiting for?* — and Bramble slams her fist on the cistern stone and shouts, "Mara! Her name is MARA!" but shouting a name doesn't keep it, keeping keeps it, and I don't have it, I never had it, I only ever borrowed it, she let me ride on her shoulders and sleep under her floorboards and she called me *little coal* in a voice like —

Like.

Wait.

She called me little coal. She learned my mother's words. She sat up nights matching my language against razored-out folios, letter by letter, sound by sound, because she refused to let a language die just because it was inconvenient. She wrote my name — my true name, the one from before, the one the Inquisitors hunted — into her private notebook in the old script, spelled correctly, every mark in its place, because she said a name written with care was a name defended.

She remembered *me*. With her hands. In ink. In the language before names.

Names are not given. Names are kept. And she kept mine first.

"Maren," I whisper — no. Not that. I go deeper, down to the oldest shelf, the shelf where my mother's voice lives. I speak the old word for what Mara was to me, the word that means *the one who writes you into the world*, and the ember leaps in my hand like a heart remembering how.

Because that's the secret. The Door took her name from the old country's ledger and the Crown's ledger and the guild's ledger. Every book that ever owned her. But it couldn't take what she *did*, because what she did lives in other people, and other people are not ledgers, whatever the guild thinks.

I put the ember to the empty page and I don't write her name.

I write her *deeds*, in the old language, and let the deeds summon the name the way dawn summons the sun:

*She counted impossible things and made them possible by writing them down.*
*She opened her door in the rain.*
*She forged all night against the grey coats and never once lied to a friend.*
*She forgave the man who bound her, and stood over him when he died, and closed his eyes with her own hands.*
*She gave everything she owned so a child could have a country.*

And on the last line, as the fire roars up bright and glad, the way fires do when they finally get to tell the truth, I write the name that rises out of the deeds on its own, whole and warm, the way a drowned thing rises when you stop holding it under:

*Mara Quintana. Citizen. Founding keeper of this book. Remembered by Ash, daughter of Vessia, who was saved by her, and who saves her back, because that is how a country starts — with people saving each other back.*

* * *

The change comes like tide.

One moment the witnesses are frowning at empty air, and the next — the next, a woman in the crowd gasps and says, "Where's Mara? Has anyone seen—" and then Bramble is laughing and crying at once, ugly and loud, and Renn has to sit down on a step because his legs have stopped taking orders, and the ember in my palm goes soft at last, a coal instead of a flame, tired and satisfied, like a dog after a long run.

And there, at the top of the cistern stairs, silhouetted against the seam-light pouring through the Door — light the color of a country waking up — stands Mara.

Not fading. Not nameless. Looking at her own hands like they're new, and then at me, and her face does about nine things at once, and I only catch a few of them because I'm running, we're both running, and then her arms are around me and she's saying my name, my true name, in the old language, pronounced perfectly, and I'm saying hers, and around us twenty-six people are cheering and one of the wardens is blowing his nose on his hat, which serves him right.

"You absolute idiot," Bramble sobs into Mara's hair. "You gave away your NAME—"

"I heard," Mara says, wobbly. "Apparently. From inside. It was very strange, don't ask."

"I wrote you back," I tell her, into her coat, which smells like ink and rain and home. "In the new book. You're a citizen. Of the country being born. It's official, there were twenty-six witnesses, Renn checked the law."

Mara pulls back and holds my face in her ink-stained hands, and she looks at me the way I imagine my mother looked at the whole country at once.

"Then show me," she says. "Show me what we're building."

Behind her, through the Door, the seam-country stretches green and gold and unfinished, and along its edge, tiny figures are already gathering — refugees walking out of Vessia, Hollow-folk walking in, meeting in the middle like two rivers agreeing to be one river. Somewhere out there a bell tower waits for its first ring.

I take Mara's hand. I have the ember, the book, and everyone I love in one room.

"Come on," I say. "We've got founding to do."
