## 21. New Entries

The new ledger smells of cedar and rain, which is to say it smells of nothing yet, and I find I like that better than I expected. Old ledgers smell of everything that's been done to them—sweat, candle smoke, the sour breath of men counting other people's lives in columns. This one smells only of paper waiting.

I keep it now in a schoolhouse that didn't exist a year ago, at a desk that was a door once, planed flat and given legs. Ash insisted on the door-desk. "Doors should get to rest," she said, with the grave authority of an eight-year-old who has watched a country fold up like paper and unfold again into something no one had planned for. Who was I to argue? I have learned, these past months, that arguing with Ash is like arguing with weather. You can do it. It changes nothing.

The morning light comes through windows Bramble's people salvaged from three different buildings, so the panes don't match and the light arrives in pieces—amber here, green there, one pane of old bottle-glass that throws a coin of violet onto my page every afternoon around four. I have stopped minding. There is something honest about imperfect light. It doesn't pretend the world is whole when it isn't, and it doesn't pretend the world is broken when it's mending.

Mending is the word I've settled on. Not healed. Mended things show their seams, and I've decided I want the seams visible. The Hollow has enough hidden history sewn into its walls to last ten lifetimes of regret.

* * *

The names come in slowly, in ones and twos, like water finding its level.

A woman named Sefa came yesterday with two children and no surname. She stood at my desk turning her cap in her hands and said she'd never been asked for one before, not properly asked, the way you ask someone what they'd like rather than what they are. Her children were Vessian-born, or would have been, if Vessia had been allowed to be anywhere. The boy wanted to be called after his grandfather. The girl, after much deliberation, wanted to be called River, because she liked how it sounded when shouted across water.

"River," I wrote. "Just River?"

"Just River," she said, and grinned with all the teeth she had, and I thought: this is what the ledgers were supposed to be, before the Guild got hold of them. Before debt. Before columns of what people owed instead of what people chose.

That's the difference, if anyone asks me—and Renn keeps threatening to ask me, in his dry way, for the official record he's building out of the wreckage of the Inquisition's files. In the old ledgers, a name was a hook. Something to hang a debt on, a lien, a leash. Here, a name is a gift you give yourself and let others witness. I write them down the way you'd write down a promise someone made to themselves. *Sefa, called mother by two children. River, aged six, likes shouting.*

I add small notes like that now. Odell would have hated it. Odell kept his books clean as bone. But Odell is dead, and I am learning to keep books the way a person keeps a garden—a little wild, a little generous, everything in it alive.

* * *

Bramble runs the ferry.

Of course she does. When I suggested—gently, carefully, the way you approach a woman with a crossbow scar through her shoulder—that she might want to rest, she looked at me the way she looks at customs officials, and said, "Rest? I've been resting since the bolt went in. Six weeks of lying on my back watching a ceiling. If I lie still one more day I'll take root and become furniture." Then she stood up too fast, went grey, sat back down, and added, "But I'll rest *moving*. That's different."

So she moves. Twice a day, dawn and dusk, the ferry crosses the seam—the place where the Hollow's edge meets the country that came through the Door, where the cobbled streets of Crooked Hollow simply... continue, but changed, into lanes no cartographer drew because no cartographer existed when they were made. The locals call it the Seam. The newcomers call it the Threshold. Ash calls it the Doorstep, which is the best name, and is therefore, naturally, not the one catching on.

Bramble ferries people across both ways. Hollow folk going to see the impossible: streets that remember being drowned, fountains that run uphill, a bell tower with no bell that rings anyway at odd hours, softly, like something practicing. And Vessians—or the closest thing to Vessians, the ones the dragon's last flame pulled through from wherever the erased go—coming to see the ordinary: markets, taxes (they're scandalized), bread that costs less than a week's wages.

She charges no fare. She charges stories instead. One true story per crossing, told aloud, and she keeps a little book of the best ones wedged under the tiller. Last week she told me about a Vessian grandmother who crossed just to sit in the middle of the canal and weep, and when Bramble asked if she wanted to go back, the woman said, "Back? Child, I'm not going back. I'm going *across*. There's a difference. Back is a direction. Across is a choice."

Bramble wrote it down. Of course she did. We're all keeping records now, one way or another. It seems to be what survivors of erasure do—we write, compulsively, gratefully, like people who nearly drowned breathing air with their whole chests.

* * *

The scar, when I see it—which is more often than she'd like, because she leaves her shirt unlaced on the left side out of what I suspect is defiance rather than comfort—has healed into something strange. A long pale line, yes, but beneath it the skin took on a faint shimmer, like mother-of-pearl, where the bolt that was meant for Ash passed through flesh that had spent years carrying smuggled things. Ash says the dragon's ember touched everyone in the tunnel that night, some more than others. Bramble says it's just scarring and Ash is being dramatic. But I've seen Bramble's hand pass over water and the water lean toward her, slightly, the way a cat leans toward a familiar knee. She pretends not to notice. She notices.

"You could ask it for anything," Ash told her once, solemn as scripture. "The water. It remembers you."

"It can remember me from over there," Bramble said. "I'm not asking it for favors. Favors are how you end up owing things." She caught my eye then, and something passed between us—not quite a joke, not quite grief. Owing. We had both done our time in that country.

* * *

Renn visits on Thursdays. He says it's for the record. He brings pastries, which is also for the record, though neither of us says whose.

He sits in the corner chair—he brought the chair himself, months ago, installed it without asking, in the manner of a man who spent twenty years taking things away from people and has decided to spend the next twenty putting things back—and he reads through my entries while eating, and corrects nothing, and occasionally says "hmm" in a tone that means approval but sounds like indigestion.

Today he set down his pastry and studied me across the room with those grey inquisitor's eyes that have learned, slowly, painfully, to look like a man's eyes instead of an institution's.

"Quintana," he said. "You know the Crown still doesn't recognize any of this. Officially, there is no seam, no new country, no ferry. Officially, you are a dead scribe with no name, squatting in an unregistered building teaching an unregistered language to unregistered children."

"Officially," I agreed, dipping my pen.

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, the Crown's senior archivist eats almond pastries in that building every Thursday and tells me which of the old Guild's sealed folios are worth unsealing."

"Hmm," said Renn, which meant yes.

There is a law older than the Crown—that's what he cited, that night in the cisterns, standing between us and the Inquisition's crossbows with his warrant held upside down and his voice steady as bedrock. The law of witness. Older than kings, older than ledgers: *what is witnessed truly cannot be unwitnessed.* He built his defense on it, and the Crown's own magistrates, to their eternal fury, found they couldn't argue without arguing against the foundations of every court they presided over. So the Inquisition stands down, officially restructured, unofficially ashamed, and Halvard Renn spends his Thursdays eating pastries and helping me unseal the folios Odell died protecting and died regretting.

"He'd have hated this," I said once, meaning Odell, meaning the wildness of my ledgers, the notes in margins, the names freely given.

"No," Renn said, surprising me. "He'd have hated that he loved it. There's a difference. I knew him a long time, Quintana. Men like us don't repent cleanly. We repent sideways, dragging our heels, making a mess of it." He brushed crumbs from his coat. "His last word was your name. That wasn't guilt. That was a man finally setting down a weight and finding his arms still worked."

I didn't say anything. Some Thursdays I don't say much. He understands. He brought the chair; he knows how to sit in silence.

* * *

Ash comes by every day after her lessons—she teaches mornings now, the old language, to a classroom of children from both sides of the Seam who learn it the way children learn anything: completely, effortlessly, as if it had always been theirs. Which, Ash argues, it always was. Language before names, she calls it. The tongue the world spoke before anyone thought to claim it.

She checks my ledger the way Odell used to check mine—leaning over my shoulder, finger tracing the lines—but where Odell looked for errors, Ash looks for people. "Tell me about this one," she'll say, tapping an entry. *Corvin, ferryman's apprentice, afraid of deep water, crossing anyway.* And I tell her, and she listens with her whole body, and sometimes her scale—still worn on its cord at her throat, still warm, always warm—glows a little brighter, as if it's listening too.

Yesterday she tapped an entry and went quiet for a long time.

"What is it?" I asked.

"This woman. Marta. She gave her name yesterday?" I nodded. "My mother knew a Marta," Ash said. "In Vessia. Before." Her finger stayed on the page. "It's probably not the same one. Names repeat. That's what names are *for*." But she copied the entry into her own book—the new book, the one written in the old language, the founding document, we're calling it, half laughing when we say it and half not—and she wrote Marta's name beside others, and the scale glowed like a coal banked for morning.

Nothing is lost, she says. Not really. Erasure is a violence, not a victory. What was erased can be remembered, and what is remembered can return, changed, wearing new clothes, answering to new names. River, aged six, likes shouting. Perhaps she was someone once, somewhere folded. Perhaps she'll be someone again. Both are true. The ledgers taught me that columns are lies—nothing fits in one column. Everything belongs in two.

* * *

This evening I stayed late. The violet pane did its work, dropping its coin of light on the page, and I wrote the last entries of the day while the schoolhouse emptied and the light went amber and then grey.

The last name of the day belonged to an old man who came in just before closing, hat in hands, canal-damp, nervous. He said he'd lived in the Hollow sixty years and never given his name to anyone freely, only to tax collectors and press gangs and the Guild. He said it felt strange to do it on purpose. He said his name was Piotr, and his mother had sung him songs in a language he'd forgotten until last month, when he heard children shouting it across the water near the ferry landing, and he'd stood there on the bank crying like a fool into his beer.

"What were the songs about?" I asked him.

"Boats," he said, and laughed wetly. "All of them, boats. My mother's family were ferrymen, way back. Before the Hollow was the Hollow."

I wrote: *Piotr. Ferryman's blood. Forgot, and remembered, and wept about it, and is not ashamed.*

He read it upside down and laughed again, and shook my hand with both of his, and left whistling something in a language older than any kingdom.

I sat a while after he'd gone, pen idle, listening to the building settle. Somewhere outside, Bramble's evening ferry horn sounded across the water—one long note, low and sure, the sound of a crossing being made. Somewhere a bell with no bell rang its soft practice-note. Somewhere Ash was writing names in a dead language that refuses to die, and every one of them glowed faintly warm under her fingers.

Peace, I thought. This must be what it feels like. Not the absence of trouble—I'm sure trouble is already drafting letters of introduction—but the presence of choice. I choose this desk. I choose these names. No debt holds me here. The only thing I owe is attention, and I pay it gladly, daily, in full.

I dipped my pen to close the book for the night, and that's when I saw it.

At the bottom of today's page, below Piotr, below the last line I wrote—in ink I did not put there, in a hand I almost recognized, a hand like mine but steadier, like mine but older—three words in the language before names.

I don't read it fluently. Ash is teaching me, Thursdays and Sundays, between pastries and folios. But I know enough now. Enough to translate the first word. Enough to feel the second one land in my chest like a struck bell.

Enough to know that the third word is a date.

Tomorrow's date.
