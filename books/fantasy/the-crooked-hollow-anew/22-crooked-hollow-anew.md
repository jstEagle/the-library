## 22. Crooked Hollow Anew

The schoolhouse did not exist a year ago. I know this because I helped carry the stones for it, and my arms were too small to carry very many, and Bramble laughed at me and put three extra on top anyway and said, *character-building, little ember.* Now the walls stand straight and the roof keeps out the rain and there is a bell in the tower that Mara says rings too loud, and I say a country that was quiet for two hundred years has earned some noise.

I am the teacher. Nobody voted on this. It happened the way important things happen in Crooked Hollow: somebody needed doing it, and then everybody looked at me, and then it was true.

This morning there are nineteen children at the desks. Some are Hollow children with mud still on their boots from the ferry path. Some came through the Door with their mothers, blinking, holding bread they had never seen before, in a country that had never been anywhere until last spring. They sit together now without noticing that this is remarkable. That is my favorite thing about them.

"Say it with me," I tell them, and hold up the slate where I have chalked the word for *morning* in the old letters. "Vess-ah-nu."

"Vess-ah-nu," they say back, nineteen voices, one of them wrong in a way that makes the others giggle.

"Again. Slower. You are not chasing a cart."

"We are not chasing a cart," Renna says solemnly, from the front row, because she is six and takes everything literally, and the whole room laughs, and I let them, because laughing is also the old language, maybe the oldest part.

I write the word again beneath itself, and under that I write what it means: *the light before the light.* My mother used to say it when she woke me before dawn, her hand warm on my hair, in a house that folded up like paper with everything else. I do not tell the children this. Not yet. Grief is a lesson you teach yourself, slowly, in private, for years. But I teach them the word, so that someday, if they need it, it will be there waiting for them, like a lamp left burning in a window by someone who trusted you to come home.

That is what language is. I learned that from a dragon.

* * *

After lessons, I walk down to the guild hall, which is not the Ledger Guild anymore. The sign over the door is new wood, pale as bone, and burned into it in both languages: **THE LEDGER OF THE ERASED — AND THE NOT-YET-WRITTEN**. Mara wanted something shorter. Bramble argued for *STUFF WE REMEMBER*. They lost.

Inside, it smells the way it always smelled — ink and dust and lamp oil — except the dust is winning less than it used to. The great counting room is full of desks now instead of cages of debt-folios. People come here to be written in. Nobody comes here to be written out. We do not do that anymore. It is the only rule, and it is written above the door in letters tall enough that even Renna can read them:

*Nobody is erased here.*

Mara is at her desk, which is Odell Vane's old desk, though she had the top planed down and refinished so it does not look like his anymore. She is bent over a folio with her spectacles sliding down her nose, and her pen is moving in that fast, sure way she has, like the words are already finished somewhere and she is just letting them out.

She looks up when my shadow falls across the page.

"You're late," she says.

"I'm nine years old. I'm exactly on time."

"That's not how time works."

"It's how *my* time works." I climb onto the stool across from her, the one that is mine, worn smooth in the middle from a year of sitting. "Bramble says the ferry ran double today. Forty-one people came through from the seam villages. A man brought a goat."

"A goat," Mara repeats, in the voice she uses for things she refuses to feel about yet.

"On the boat. In the boat? Bramble said I should tell you so you could add it to the census."

"Add it to the—" She pinches the bridge of her nose. "Ash. We are not putting goats in the census."

"Then the census is wrong," I say, "and you always say a ledger that lies is worse than no ledger at all."

There is a pause. Then Mara sighs the big sigh, the one that means she has lost, and pulls a fresh sheet toward her, and writes, in her beautiful clerk's hand: *One (1) goat, brown, unnamed, arrived by ferry, owner: also unnamed, possibly the goat.*

"There," she says. "History is served."

I grin at her. She tries not to grin back. She fails, the way she always fails, the way I have spent a whole year learning every way she fails at being stern, and I love all of them.

* * *

Here is a thing I have not told anyone, not even Mara, not even in the old language, which can hold almost anything:

For a long time after the dragon gave me its fire, I was afraid of it. Not of burning. Of *remembering.* Because the flame remembers everything it touches, and I thought: what if I remember my mother's face so hard that I forget my own? What if keeping a whole country inside you means there is no room left to be a girl?

But that is not how it worked. That is not how any of it worked.

The fire does not take your room. It builds you more.

I found that out on an ordinary evening, months ago, sitting on the schoolhouse steps while Mara locked up, watching her count the day's entries under her breath the way she counts everything — stairs, raindrops, reasons to worry. And I felt the ember in my chest turn over, gentle as a sleeping cat, and I understood: it was not asking me to hold Vessia instead of myself. It was asking me to hold Vessia *and* myself, *and* Mara, *and* Bramble with his scar and his terrible jokes, *and* Renn who sends letters he signs *your reluctant correspondent*, *and* the goat, apparently.

A country is not a weight. A country is a table, and everyone you love gets a chair.

My mother taught me the word for that too. I teach it on Fridays. It is the longest word we have, and the smallest children can say it by winter, every year, and every year I get to hear it again like it is the first time.

* * *

Today is not Friday. Today is better.

Today is the day of the book.

It sits on Mara's desk between us, and it is nothing like the Ledger Guild's books. Those were vellum and iron clasps and locks, made to keep people out and truths in. This one is plain paper sewn with linen thread, bound in dyed leather the color of river clay, because Bramble insisted our first book should look like the ground we stood on. On its cover, stamped in gold leaf that Renn procured through channels he declined to describe, is the sigil we took back from the guild seal: the coiled dragon around the bell tower. Ours now. Not theirs. It was always ours.

"The first page," Mara says, and her voice does the thing it does when she is pretending to be practical about something enormous. "Traditionally, the first entry establishes the ledger's purpose and authority."

"Traditionally," I agree.

"In the old days, the first page of a guild ledger held the master's name. His mark. His claim." Her pen hovers. "We're not doing that."

"No."

"We're doing it differently."

"Yes," I say. "Give it here."

She slides the book across the desk. I open to the first page, white as morning snow, and I feel the ember stir, warm under my ribs, attentive, the way it always is when something matters.

I dip the pen in the good ink — Mara's ink, the black kind, the honest kind — and I write, in the old letters, large and careful, the sentence we agreed on, the founding line, the one that will sit at the head of every record this country ever keeps:

*This book belongs to everyone whose name is in it, and to everyone whose name should be.*

Below it, smaller, in both languages: *No name entered here may ever be removed. No person may ever be asked to pay with their name. Signed in witness, this first day.*

And then the signatures.

Bramble goes first, because he shoved past us all, and he writes his name huge, taking up half the width of the page, and adds underneath: *ferryman, smuggler (retired-ish), survivor of one (1) crossbow bolt, which hurt.* Mara scolds him. He kisses the top of her head. She pretends this did not happen.

Renn signs small and neat, like a man still getting used to signing things honestly, and adds his title: *formerly of the Inquisition; currently of nowhere in particular; glad of it.*

The others come throughout the day — seam-walkers and cistern-divers, the midwife with hands like warm bread, the ex-clerk Pellin who sold us out once and has spent a year carrying stones for every building we raise, and who signs last, after everyone else, and cries a little, and nobody tells him he shouldn't be allowed. He paid. He is still paying. That is what a country is for: giving people somewhere to put down what they carry.

And then, near the bottom of the page, when the lamplight has gone gold and the room has gone quiet, I take the pen one more time.

I write my name first. Ash, heir of Vessia, keeper of the ember, teacher of the word for *the light before the light.* Beside it, in the space I have saved since the day we bound this book, I write the other name. The one I promised, in the dark under the world, in the closing light, when I was small and terrified and she was already mostly gone.

*Mara Quintana.*

Citizen. First citizen, though she would hate me writing that, so I don't. Just her name, beside mine, the way it looks when two people belong to the same place.

Mara leans over my shoulder and reads it, and reads it again, and puts her hand flat on the page over the letters like she is checking they are warm.

"You know," she says quietly, "I spent ten years paying a debt with that name. Ten years thinking it was the only thing I owned, and therefore the thing I'd lose." Her thumb moves along the ink. "Turns out it wasn't a debt at all. It was a door. I just didn't have the key yet."

"You had me," I say.

"I had you," she agrees, and her voice cracks on the second word, just slightly, and I pretend not to notice, because that is also the old language, the part you speak with your eyes forward and your heart wide open.

Outside, the bell rings the hour, too loud, the way Mara likes to complain about and never actually fixes. Down at the quay, Bramble's ferry lantern swings, bringing somebody home. Somewhere in the seam-country beyond the Door, a village that was a memory this morning is a market square by supper, and the smell of their cooking drifts over the water, and it smells like cumin and woodsmoke and *staying.*

I close the book gently, the way you close a door you intend to open again tomorrow.

"Nineteen students tomorrow," I tell Mara. "Maybe twenty. The miller's family came through on the afternoon ferry."

"Twenty," she says, already reaching for a fresh folio, already writing, because that is her love language, ink. "I'll note it. And Ash?"

"Mm?"

She looks at me across the lamplit desk — the scribe who was bought and paid for, who un-bought herself, who taught a whole city that a ledger can be a promise instead of a cage — and she smiles the real smile, the one with no accounting in it at all.

"Welcome home."

I am home. I was born in a country that ended, and I live in one that is beginning, and they are the same country, and I am the same girl, and the fire in my chest is not a burden and never was.

It is a lamp in a window.

And everyone is coming home.

*The end.*
