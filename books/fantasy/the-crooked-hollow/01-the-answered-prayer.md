## 1. The Answered Prayer

There are three rules to guiding a caravan through Thornfell, and I have spent nineteen years learning all of them the hard way.

One: never trust a path you haven't walked in the dark. Two: never trust a merchant who says he's not carrying anything worth stealing. Three — and this is the one that keeps people alive — never pray on the mountain. Not because the old gods were cruel. Because they used to answer, and then they stopped, and the silence afterward was worse than any cruelty could have been.

I was breaking rule three by association before we'd even made the high pass.

"Yara." Doss Kettleby, the caravan master, fell into step beside me as the mules picked their way over scree the color of old bone. He's a round man with a voice like gravel in a bucket and the moral flexibility of wet rope, which is why I like him. "The boy's praying again."

"Which boy?"

"The new one. The one coughing up his lungs since Greyford. Tam. Says prayers under his breath every time the path narrows. You'd think he'd have noticed nobody's listening."

"He'll notice," I said. "Everyone does eventually."

Doss grunted and dropped back to shout at a mule that had opinions about the incline. I kept walking, one hand trailing along the cold rock of the cut, the other resting — as it always did, though I'd deny it under torture — on the hilt of my mother's sword.

The blade is called Vess, though only I know that now, and I only know it because she told me when I was small enough to sit cross-legged at her knee while she oiled the steel. *A swordspeaker names her blade,* she said, *and the blade remembers her.* I'd thought it was just something mothers said, the way they say *eat your greens* and *the river takes what it's owed.* Then she died, and the covenant broke, and the gods went silent all across the nine realms, and I learned that most things mothers say are true in ways you won't enjoy.

I don't speak to Vess. I carry her. There's a difference, and the difference is how I've stayed sane.

* * *

We made the Hollow Stair by late afternoon, that shelf of stone where the mountain drops away on your left for six hundred feet into cloud. It's the worst stretch of the crossing and the best place to lose anyone following you, which is why smugglers pay me what they pay me. The light was going amber and long, gilding the frost on the rocks, and for about ten minutes Thornfell almost managed to be beautiful instead of merely indifferent.

That's when Tam collapsed.

Not dramatically — no crying out, no theatrical stagger. He just sat down on the trail between one step and the next, the way a puppet sits down when someone cuts its strings. By the time I got back to him he was gray as the scree, blood on his lips, and the coughing had gone wet and wrong in a way I recognized from the fever camps two winters back.

Doss crouched beside him. "Greyford fever," he said, with the delicacy of a man reading a ledger aloud. "He's done, Yara. We can't carry him up the Stair."

"I know," I said.

"We can't stay with him either. Light's going."

"I said I know."

Tam's eyes found mine. He was maybe nineteen. He'd joined the caravan at Greyford with nothing but a bindle and a story about a sister in Lowmark who'd take him in, and I'd believed him because I wanted to, because everyone deserves one believing stranger per lifetime.

"Lady," he whispered. His hand scrabbled at my sleeve. "Lady, I'm scared. Nobody's ever — will you say one? For me? A prayer? My gran used to say them but I never learned the words right, and there's no one left who—"

"Boy," Doss started gently, "there's no one to hear—"

"I'll say it," I heard myself say.

Nineteen years of rule three, and I broke it because a dying boy asked me with his eyes and I am apparently still, somewhere underneath the scar tissue, the girl who sat at her mother's knee.

I took his cold hands in mine and I spoke the old words. The ones from before. The covenant-words, the ones the priests burned out of every book and tongue after the Silence: *Hollow-mother, crooked-crowned, hear the small voice in the dark. What was sworn beneath the stone, let it hold; what was broken, let it mend.*

It's a reforging prayer. I didn't think about that until later. I hadn't spoken it since I was seven years old, and my mouth remembered it anyway, the way a mouth remembers its mother's name.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. I felt Tam's grip loosen with disappointment — or maybe that was just death arriving — and I finished the words anyway, out loud, into the amber air, feeling like a fool and a fraud and an archaeologist of grief all at once.

Then the mountain answered.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, and I can't, so I'll describe it badly and you'll have to forgive me.

Light came out of Thornfell itself. Not from above — from within, from deep under the ridge, the way heat comes out of a forge wall. It rose through the cracks in the world in slow golden veins, and where it touched the frost the frost bloomed, actual white flowers opening on black stone in the space of a heartbeat, and the whole pass filled with a sound like a bell being struck underwater. Every mule on the Stair went to its knees. Every person did too, except me, and only because I was already down.

And Vess — my mother's sword, silent for nineteen years, dead steel, ornament, inheritance, burden —

Vess began to hum.

Not rattle. Not ring. *Hum*, low and warm against my hip, the way a cat hums, the way a woman hums when she's working and content, and beneath the hum — threaded through it, unmistakable, impossible — a voice.

*Yara.*

My mother's voice. Saying my name the way she said it, with the little rise at the end, half question, half delight, as if she'd been looking everywhere for me and had finally, finally found me in the last place she thought to check.

The light climbed the far ridge and gathered there, above the tree line, above the snow line, in a column that had no business existing, gold shot through with green, and in its heart — I swear this, and I know what swearing costs — in its heart something moved. Something vast and patient turned toward us the way a sleeper turns toward a window at dawn.

Down the whole length of the Hollow Stair, men who had spent their lives pretending not to believe in anything were weeping openly into the frost-flowers.

"*Yara.*"

I pulled Vess free of her sheath. The blade shone like a held breath. And the voice came again, closer now, or deeper, or both, and it wasn't only my mother anymore — it was layered, many-throated, the way a choir is one voice made of dozens:

*You spoke the words. After all these years, little speaker — you spoke the words.*

"It was for him," I said stupidly, to a sword, on my knees in the snow, while a boy died behind me and a god looked down from a mountain. "It wasn't — I wasn't asking for anything—"

*No,* the voice said, and I felt it smile, if a voice can smile, felt it the way you feel sun through a window. *You never do. That is why it answered.*

Behind me, Tam made a small sound. I turned — and he was sitting up. Gray-faced still, blood on his chin, but breathing easy, eyes wide and clear, staring at the light on the ridge like a man reading his own pardon.

The fever hadn't broken. Fevers don't break like that. Something had reached down the Stair and lifted the sickness out of him the way you'd lift a thorn from a child's foot.

Doss was looking at me. Everyone was looking at me. Twenty smugglers and a resurrected boy, all waiting for the swordspeaker to explain what none of us had seen in nineteen years.

I looked down at Vess, humming warm in my grip, my mother's voice folded inside the steel like a letter I'd never dared open.

"Yara," I said to her, quietly, so only she could hear. "What did you get me into?"

The blade pulsed once, gentle as a hand on my shoulder, and the voice said:

*Come home, little speaker. The Hollow Shrine has waited long enough — and so have you.*
