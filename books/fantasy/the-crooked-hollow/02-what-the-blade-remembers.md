## 2. What the Blade Remembers

The scouts found our trail by noon, which meant I'd been sloppy, which meant I was tired, which meant the light in the mountains had gotten into my head and made a nest there.

Three riders in Vaelric's grey-and-gold, moving along the ridgeline with the patient confidence of men who knew the quarry was limping. I watched them from a fold of rock above the pass while the last of the caravan's mules picked their way down into the treeline. The blade on my back had been humming since dawn — not loudly, nothing Sef's people could hear over wind and mule-complaints, but enough. A thread of sound pulled taut against my spine, like a finger hooked under my collarbone.

*Quiet,* I told it.

It did not quiet. Swords are bad listeners. This one especially, being, apparently, less sword than something else wearing a sword's shape, the way a hermit crab wears a shell it didn't build.

I cut east off the smugglers' road at the split oak — the one with the lightning scar that every runner in Thornfell knows means *trouble behind, choose fast* — and dropped into the ravine country where the trees grow crooked and the streams run cold enough to hurt your teeth. By dusk I'd put a river and two false trails between me and the riders, and by full dark I stood dripping on the shingle below Marrow's Landing, where a single lantern burned green in the window of a boathouse that officially did not exist.

Sef Marrow opened the door before I knocked. She always does. I've stopped asking how.

"You look," she said, "like something the mountain chewed up and reconsidered."

"Your hospitality is legendary."

"It is." She stepped aside to let me in. "Come ruin my floor."

* * *

The boathouse smelled of pitch, tar, and the particular staleness of men who've been hiding from tax collectors for a week. Sef's crew — six of them, plus old Brannic who claimed to be retired but slept armed in the rigging of a boat that hadn't sailed in years — cleared a space by the stove without being asked. Smugglers have rules about swordspeakers. Most of them are superstitious nonsense. Some of them, I was beginning to suspect, were not.

I told them everything. The dying stranger on the high trail, his prayer for his daughter, and the light that came down out of the peaks like a held breath finally released. The way the fever had left him between one heartbeat and the next, weeping, calling a name no one had spoken aloud in twenty years. And then, because keeping secrets from Sef Marrow is like keeping water in your fist, I drew the blade and laid it across my knees.

The humming stopped. In the sudden silence, every person in that room leaned forward without meaning to.

"My mother's," I said. "She carried it through the last years of the covenant. When she died I took it because it was mine to take, and because—" I shrugged, wry even now, especially now. "Because a Dravenmoor doesn't leave a good blade rusting."

"And now?" Sef asked quietly.

"Now it hums. Now prayers get answered. Now Vaelric's priests are offering pardons for its surrender, which tells me the usurper is frightened of a piece of metal, and I don't like working for frightened men for free."

Brannic stirred in his corner. "Show us the fuller," he said. "The groove along the blade. Show it in the firelight."

I almost refused. Then I turned the sword so the stove-light ran down the channel of the fuller, and the room went still in a different way.

Letters. Old letters, oath-script, the kind they used to cut into shrine-stone when the gods still bothered reading. They'd been there all my life. I'd seen them a thousand times and read them never, because everyone knows the fuller of an old blade is just decoration, just craft, just—

"They're not decoration," Brannic said, as if I'd said it aloud. "My grandmother served at the Hollow Shrine. She said the oath-speakers carried their words with them. Cut into the steel. So the covenant could never be lost, only carried."

"That's a story."

"So's everything worth knowing." He leaned in, eyes milky but fixed. "Read it, girl. You're a Dravenmoor. Your mother taught you the old script if she taught you anything."

She had. Nights by the fire, tracing letters in ash, laughing when I got them wrong. I'd thought it was a game. I'd thought a great many things were games.

I read. My voice came out strange, flattened, like someone else's:

*I am Maera Dravenmoor, last speaker of the Thornfell line. I swear the keeping of this blade until a hand comes that can bear what I could not. The covenant broke not by the gods' leaving but by ours. We grew ashamed of needing them, and shame is a wall no prayer can climb. To the one who holds this: you were not asked. That is the cruelty of inheritance. Forgive her who bound you to it.*

The stove ticked. Someone's breath caught. Mine, maybe.

*You were not asked.*

I sat there with my mother's handwriting in steel across my knees and felt the floor of my whole life tilt. Twenty years I'd carried this blade as a keepsake, a burden, a family duty I grumbled about the way you grumble about weather. I'd built myself on top of it — Yara the guide, Yara the cynic, Yara who laughed at priests and took coin from smugglers and called the old gods a pretty story for frightened people. And underneath all of it, unasked, unchosen, a covenant waiting in the steel like a seed under snow.

"I never chose this," I heard myself say.

"No," Sef said. She crouched in front of me, took my hands off the blade gently, like prying fingers from a wound. "You chose to carry it down the mountain every winter. You chose to feed half this crew through two famines. You chose to come here tonight instead of running. Whatever your mother wrote into that steel, the woman holding it is the one you built yourself."

"The woman holding it is what the steel was waiting for. That's worse."

"It's not."

"Sef—"

"Yara." She said my name the way she says it when she means it, low and certain, and I hated how much it helped. "Names can be remade. I should know; I've worn three."

* * *

They came at midnight, or the hour the smugglers call midnight, which is whenever the tide and the whiskey agree. Not soldiers. Priests — three of them in Vaelric's grey, walking down the shingle without lanterns, and the strangest part was that the blade didn't hum at all. It went silent as a held tongue.

Sef's lookouts let them come. Smuggler law: parley first, knives second.

The eldest priest stopped ten feet from the boathouse door and spoke to the night in general, which meant to me. "Yara Dravenmoor. The Radiant Throne extends its mercy. Surrender the relic and walk free — pardon for you, passage for your associates, an end to the hunting. His Grace Vaelric asks only that the old error not be repeated."

"The old error," I called back. "You mean the gods."

"I mean disorder." His voice was smooth as river stone. "The world was given order when the shrines fell silent. Prayer is chaos. Answered prayer is war. Give us the sword, child, and be a woman again — free, small, safe."

*A woman again.* As if answering prayers had unsexed me. As if the choice were between being small and being a weapon.

I looked at Sef. She looked back, and gave me the smallest nod, the one that means *yours to make, and I'll stand wherever you stand.*

Here is what I learned about myself in that moment, standing barefoot on cold shingle with my mother's oath humming awake against my spine: I was angry. Not at the priest. At her. At Maera Dravenmoor, dead twenty years, who wrote *you were not asked* into steel and then died and left me to answer a question I'd never been permitted to hear.

So I answered the only way a Dravenmoor answers anything.

"Tell your Radiant Throne," I said, "that the sword declines his pardon."

The priest sighed, genuinely sorrowful, which frightened me more than threats would have. "Then the vanguard marches at dawn. The Hollow Shrine road will be closed, girl. Whatever you think that blade has promised you — the mountain will not let you reach it."

He turned, and his two companions turned with him, and as they walked back up the shingle the blade began to hum again — louder now, urgent, a voice I finally recognized beneath the sound. Not my mother's voice. Older. The voice of whatever had answered a dying man's prayer on the high trail, singing through steel it had chosen long before I was born.

Behind me, Sef started giving orders in the low voice she saves for weather and war.

"Dawn," she said, catching my eye. "How fast can you climb?"

I looked north, toward the black teeth of the peaks and the hollow between them where everything began.

"Faster than an army," I said. "But not faster alone."

Sef smiled the smile that always precedes disaster. "Good thing you're not going alone, then."
