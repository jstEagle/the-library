## 3. The Usurper's Road

*Yara*

The fires of Vaelric's army could be seen from the high passes three nights before his horns reached them — a smear of orange along the valley floor like a wound that wouldn't clot. I stood at the edge of the smugglers' camp with my mother's blade across my knees and did the arithmetic every oath-bound fool eventually does: how many roads lead away, and how many lead up.

Sef Marrow counted differently. Sef always counted in people.

"You're doing the face," she said, dropping onto the rock beside me with the particular grace of a woman who had climbed it drunk twice and remembered neither time. "The face where you've already decided something terrible and you're rehearsing how to tell me."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Yara. I have watched you lie to border wardens, tax collectors, a priest of the Hollow Creed, and one extremely persistent goat. You are *bad* at it." She nodded at the blade. "It's been humming since dusk. It's humming now. And you keep looking at the Shrine pass like it owes you money."

I turned the sword over. In the firelight the old script along the fuller seemed to move, or maybe that was my eyes, or maybe that was the covenant stirring in its long grave. "The blade remembers the words," I said. "The reforging words. But they have to be spoken at the Hollow Shrine. By a speaker. Alone."

"Alone," Sef repeated.

"And there's more."

"There's always more."

"The speaker gives up her name. Forever. Not death exactly — worse, maybe. The name goes into the covenant. Everything she was becomes part of what holds the realms together." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as ice over a stream. "So whoever climbs up there stops being anyone. The gods come back, the cities stand, and no one remembers why."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, Vaelric's army coughed smoke into the dark.

Sef was quiet for a long moment, which for Sef was roughly equivalent to a eulogy. Then she said, "And your plan is to tell me this and then sneak off at dawn like a character in one of those tragic ballads?"

"That was broadly the shape of it."

"No."

"Sef—"

"*No.*" She said it without heat, which was somehow more frightening than shouting. "You don't get to hand me your grief wrapped in duty and call it a choice. You want to climb to that shrine and unmake yourself? Fine. That's yours to do. But you don't get to decide I'll be better off not watching."

"It's not about better off. It's about—" I gestured helplessly at the valley, the army, the whole crooked world. "Vaelric's vanguard will reach the shrine road by tomorrow night. Someone has to slow them. And I am not asking you to die holding a pass for a woman who won't remember your name."

"You're not asking me anything. That's the problem." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and looked down at me with an expression I'd seen her wear on ships in storms — calm, appalled, entirely resolved. "Get some sleep, Dravenmoor. You'll want your legs."

She walked back toward the tents. She did not say goodnight.

That should have warned me.

* * *

*Sef*

Here's what nobody tells you about loving someone who's made of oaths: you spend half your life learning to read a person who treats honesty like contraband. Yara Dravenmoor could look a customs man dead in the eye and swear the crates held wool when they held enough blackpowder to redecorate a fortress. But ask her whether she loved you and she'd answer with weather reports.

So when she slipped out of camp before first light, moving quiet as regret up the shrine trail with that singing sword on her back, I wasn't surprised. I was prepared. There's a difference. The difference was a packed bag, a borrowed mule, and Brannick covering for me with a straight face he absolutely did not earn.

"She'll be furious," Brannick said.

"She'll be alive and furious. Best version of her."

The trail out of Thornfell's western shoulder was a goat's idea of engineering — switchbacks stacked on scree, a drop on the left that went down far enough to give the clouds vertigo. I made the first ridge by midmorning and caught sight of her a switchback above, small and stubborn against the grey stone, and felt the specific ache of loving someone whose entire personality is self-sacrifice.

I didn't call out. Calling out on scree slopes gets people killed, and anyway, I wanted to see her face when she noticed.

It took her until noon. We rounded a shoulder of rock and there we were, ten paces apart, and she stopped like she'd hit glass.

"You're joking," she said.

"Mule's tied below. I'm not joking."

"I told you—"

"You told me a lot of things. You told me once the Thornfell crossing took four days and it took nine. You told me you didn't snore." I stepped closer. "You don't get to walk into a story where you stop existing and expect me to just — what? Go home? Start smuggling something legal? Become a *farmer*, Yara?"

Her jaw worked. Below us, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading all morning: horns. Not behind us. Ahead and below — the vanguard, already working up the shrine road from the north side. Vaelric hadn't waited for his priests' pardon offer to expire. He'd sent soldiers.

"They'll reach the saddle by dark," Yara said quietly. All the argument gone out of her voice, replaced by the flat tone she used for counting exits. "There's a choke point at the Widow's Stair. Narrow enough that six people could hold it against sixty. Maybe eighty."

"Six people," I said. "You're including yourself in that six?"

"I'm including myself in nothing. I'm going up."

"And I'm going sideways." I pointed east, where a ledge ran along the cliff face toward the Stair. "Brannick and the crew are already moving. They left an hour after I did. We hold the pass, you climb, everybody gets exactly what they wanted except happy."

"That's not — Sef, those slopes are called the killing ones for a reason."

"So rename them. You're good at renaming things." I hated how my voice cracked on it. "Look at me. I know what you're going to do up there. I understand it, God help me, I even think you're right, which is the worst part. But if you think I can stand at the bottom of this mountain knowing you went alone — knowing the last thing you ever did was try to protect me from having to care about you — then you don't know me at all, and I refuse to accept that, because I've spent two years making sure you do."

The wind came off the glacier and shoved between us, cold and indifferent. Above, very faint, the blade was singing again — a thread of sound like someone humming through tears.

Yara closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was crying without any drama whatsoever, which was somehow the most Yara thing imaginable.

"If you die on that stair," she said, "I will find whatever's left of you in the covenant and haunt you personally."

"Romantic."

"I mean it, Marrow."

"I know you do." I pulled my axe off my back and checked the haft the way she'd taught me, years ago, on a night neither of us had named yet. "Go. Climb. Say your words. And Yara — whatever you become up there, however big and strange and holy — some part of you knew my name. Make sure that part wins."

She kissed me once, hard, like sealing a letter. Then she turned uphill and started climbing, and the sword's song rose to meet her, and I stood there exactly four seconds — I counted, because it was all I'd allow myself — before turning east toward the Stair and the sound of horns.

* * *

*Yara*

The higher I climbed, the less the world behaved.

Snow fell upward past the tree line. The blade's hum became words, became my mother's voice saying my name — my true name, the one the covenant would take — over and over, gentle as a lullaby and relentless as tide. Behind and below, steel rang on stone at the Widow's Stair, and I let myself believe, because I had to, that the ringing meant Sef still stood.

At the last bend before the Shrine, I looked back once.

The pass was a ribbon of firelight and falling rock, and against the dark of it I could just make out figures — six, seven, holding. One of them moved like Sef moved, all economy and fury, and I pressed my hand to my mouth and kept climbing, because if I stopped I would go down there, and the world would end politely and forever.

The Hollow Shrine opened before me like a throat of starlight.

And standing in it, waiting, wearing my mother's face and my mother's patience, was something that had died twenty years ago and never gotten the news.

"You brought the blade," it said. "Now let us speak of what it costs."
