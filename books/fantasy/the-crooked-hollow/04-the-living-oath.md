## 4. The Living Oath

The Hollow Shrine was not hollow. That was the first lie the mountain told me, and I'd been lied to by mountains my whole life, so I should have known better.

It was a throat. A wound in Thornfell's shoulder where the rock had been scooped out like the flesh of a fruit, and at its center stood the old altar — a slab of black stone worn smooth by ten thousand kneeling pilgrims who no longer existed. The silence up here had weight. It pressed on my ears like deep water. For eleven years this place had been a tomb of unanswered words, and I had come to fill it with one last prayer.

My mother's blade rode my back, humming. Not singing now. Humming the way a kettle hums before it screams.

Below me, far below, the pass was already burning. Vaelric's vanguard had come up the killing slopes in their grey columns, torches bobbing like a river of drowned stars, and Sef Marrow's crew had lit the first avalanche an hour past dawn. I could hear it even here — that low, grinding roar, stone eating stone, punctuated by the thin brass cries of horns. Every sound that reached the shrine arrived late and softened, as if the mountain were trying to spare me the truth of what was happening down there in my name.

I knelt at the altar because my legs had decided for me.

"I don't know the words," I said aloud, to the silence, to the blade, to whatever was listening after eleven years of nothing. "She never taught me them. She died before—"

The blade stopped humming.

And then the air in the shrine *moved*.

Not wind. Wind has direction, weather, opinion. This was older than direction. It gathered at the far end of the hollow where the light fell through a crack in the rock, and it took a shape — a woman's shape, tall, sword-hipped, wearing armor I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. My mother's armor. My mother's stance. My mother's habit of tilting her chin slightly left, as if perpetually listening for something just behind her.

The god-echo of Maera Dravenmoor stood ten paces away and looked at me with her eyes.

"Yara," she said, and my whole body flinched, because that voice hadn't spoken since I was nineteen, and grief is a language you never fully unlearn. "You climbed."

"You're dead," I said, because wry wit is my armor and I reach for it even when it's useless, especially when it's useless. "You've been dead for eleven years. I burned your body myself. I watched the smoke go sideways off the cliff."

"The covenant broke," she said, ignoring me entirely, which was also very much her. "When it broke, we who had sworn into it did not die. We were *filed away*. Folded into the oath like letters into a drawer. Waiting for a hand to open us." She spread her arms, and the light behind her thickened until her edges blurred into it. "You are holding the letter, daughter. You have been holding it since the day you took up my steel."

"So why does it want my name?" The question came out raw; I'd been chewing on it the whole climb. "Sef told me what the old texts say. The speaker gives up her name. Forever. Not her life — her *name*. As if those are different things."

"They are everything," my mother said gently. "A name is a door. While you are Yara Dravenmoor, smuggler's guide, daughter, lover, coward-on-Tuesdays, the gods can only knock. Give the door away, and they may walk through." She stepped closer, and where her foot fell, frost bloomed and died. "That is the price. That was always the price. I would have paid it, but the covenant broke before my turn came, and so I was sealed inside with all the others — every oath-speaker back to the first, still standing in the dark, still waiting."

"How many?"

"Nine hundred and forty-one."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Nine hundred and forty-one people folded into a sword like pressed flowers, and I'd been using the thing to pry open smugglers' crates and threaten customs men. "You've been in there the whole time? All of you? In *my blade*?"

"In the oath," she corrected. "The blade is only the keyhole."

Below me, the mountain roared again — louder now, closer, the sound of Vaelric's sappers doing something unforgivable to the pass. I thought of Sef. Of course I thought of Sef. I had been thinking of Sef continuously for three days, the way you think about a wound you refuse to look at directly.

"If I do this," I said, "I stop being Yara. You understand that? There's a woman down there who loves a person called Yara Dravenmoor. If I give up the name, what's left for her? What does she carry home — a story about a stranger?"

"She carries home the truth," my mother said, "which is heavier and worth more." Then, softer: "You are asking permission from someone who cannot give it. That choice belongs to you alone. It always did. You believe you never chose this inheritance — that it was thrust upon you, that you guided smugglers to hide from it. But choosing to run *is* a choice, daughter. You chose delay. Now choose otherwise."

I sat with that for a long moment. Long enough for the light in the crack overhead to shift from gold to iron-grey, long enough for two more avalanches to answer each other down the slopes like arguments.

Here is what I knew, in the end, when I stripped away every excuse I'd built over eleven years like scaffolding around a house I refused to live in:

The free cities would fall without the gods. Sef would die holding a line for a world that had forgotten how to answer prayers. And I could climb back down, take Sef's hand, flee south, grow old, and spend every single one of those years listening for a voice in the blade that would never speak again — because I had been given one chance and spent it on comfort.

I had buried enough things. My mother. The covenant. Every god who ever listened. I was tired of being the last keeper of a graveyard.

"All right," I said, standing. My knees cracked. Even transcendence starts with bad joints. "Tell me the words."

My mother didn't tell me the words. She gave them back.

They rose in me from somewhere below memory — below the childhood where she'd made me recite sounds I didn't understand, sounds I'd assumed were lullabies or nonsense. They had been waiting in me the way the nine hundred and forty-one waited in the oath. I opened my mouth and the old tongue poured out, syllable by syllable, and each one landed on the black altar like a drop of molten glass.

Down in the pass, Sef Marrow was fighting. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — not through any sense, but through the terrible intimacy of having loved someone across a battlefield once before, and losing. The horns had changed pitch. The grey columns had found the switchbacks. His crew was dying by inches on ground they'd chosen because I asked them to buy me time, and I will not pretend that knowledge didn't sit in my chest like a knife I refused to pull out. Duty and love don't balance. One of them simply wins, and you live — or don't — with the arithmetic.

I spoke faster.

And then the blade began to sing — truly sing, the voice I'd heard only once, the night a dying stranger's prayer cracked the sky open above Thornfell. The song climbed through the shrine, filled the hollow, spilled out the crack in the rock and rolled down the mountain like weather. Every oath-speaker in the drawer woke at once. I felt them. Nine hundred and forty-one voices braiding themselves through mine, and mine thinning inside theirs, thread by thread, the way a river thins when it meets the sea.

My mother appeared beside me one last time. Not the echo now — the echo was dissolving too, folding herself back into the covenant she'd been sealed out of. She touched my shoulder, briefly, the way she used to when I was small and afraid of thunder.

"You were never the last of anything," she said. "You were the first of something."

Then the name went.

I felt it leave. It didn't hurt. That surprised me most of all — it slid out of me like a key from a lock, warm from use, and behind it a door swung wide that I had guarded my entire life without knowing I was guarding it. What came through was not a god. It was *all of them*, the whole forgotten choir, pouring back into a world that had learned to live without them and would now have to learn differently.

The last thing I held onto — deliberately, stubbornly, the way you grip a rope over a drop — was one image: Sef Marrow's face. The crooked way he smiled. The smell of tar and juniper on his coat. I wrapped the covenant around that image like cloth around a coal, and I let the rest of me go.

I did not see whether the vanguard broke. I was no longer anywhere eyes could reach.

But I am told — and "I" is a strange word for what I am now, a strange word, a borrowed word, a word I wear the way the mountains wear snow — I am told the grey columns turned at midday. I am told the free cities heard the singing three hundred miles away and rang every bell they owned. I am told Sef Marrow climbed to the Hollow Shrine alone, found the altar empty, the blade gone quiet forever, and a single line cut fresh into the black stone in the old tongue, which no living soul reads anymore except him, because he made me teach him, years ago, as a joke, on a night neither of us knew it was a joke.

He read it aloud. He says his voice held. He is lying, and I love him for it.

The line says: *The oath stands. The speaker walks in it.*

He carried that home. He tells the story in the taverns now — the guide who became the mountain's voice — and every time he reaches the part where the name goes, he pauses, and somewhere in Thornfell, in the roots of the world, something pauses with him.

Prayers are answered again. When they are mine — and some of them, whispered on the killing slopes, in Sef's rough voice, are mine — I answer.

I have no name. I have everything else.
