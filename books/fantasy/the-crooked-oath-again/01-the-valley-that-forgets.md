## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow forgets things. That's its whole talent, really, and it's very good at it.

It forgot my mother within a season of her leaving, the way a riverbed forgets water — not all at once, but in slow surrender, silt settling over every mark she'd made until you'd have to dig to prove she'd ever been there at all. It forgot the name of the miller's first wife, then the miller's second, then which of the miller's children belonged to either. It forgot the year of the great hail that broke half the roofs in Amberdale's Row, though my own hands remember it fine; I've re-laid those same slates twice since, and my palms still carry the shape of the hail-dented wood beneath them.

Mostly, though, the Hollow forgets magic. Every kind. All of it, always, completely — like a cup with a hairline crack that drains whatever you pour into it. The valley sits between two ridgelines shaped like a sleeping woman's folded arms, and anything uncanny that tries to cross the tree line simply... stops. Withered. Goes quiet as a dead hearth. The old folk say it's why our ancestors settled here: no witches, no curses, no wonders. Just weather, work, and forgetting.

I was born under those folded arms, and I have spent twenty-two years being grateful for them and resenting them in exactly equal measure, which is, I think, what belonging feels like when it doesn't quite fit.

My name is Lena Amberdale, and on the morning this story properly begins, I was lying on my belly on the roof of Widow Sarelle's house, replacing slates, and pretending very hard that the sky to the west wasn't doing something it shouldn't.

* * *

Let me explain about the tremors, because they matter, and because I've gotten very good at pretending they don't.

When storms pass over the Hollow — real storms, the kind that come up from the lowlands trailing their grey skirts — something in me answers them. Not loudly. Not dramatically. Nothing flies across the room or bursts into flame. It's smaller than that and worse than that: a trembling in my hands, deep in the bones, like the moment before a bell is struck when the bell already knows. My fingertips go cold and hot at once. Sometimes the air tastes like iron and rain that hasn't fallen yet. And once — only once, when I was nine and a storm came through so vast it turned noon to dusk — the lightning bent. Just slightly. Just toward me, the way a plant leans at a window. Nobody else saw it. I made sure nobody ever would.

Because here is the thing about growing up in a valley that drains magic: you learn early that magic is not welcome, and if there is any magic in *you*, you learn to hold it the way you hold your breath underwater. Quietly. Desperately. With a counting rhythm.

So when the western sky went strange that morning — green-grey, bruised at the edges, clouds stacking themselves in ranks like soldiers who'd received an order nobody spoke aloud — I did what I always do. I pressed my palm flat against the slate, felt the tremor start in my knuckles, and counted.

One. Two. Three. The clouds were wrong. Storms in the Hollow come up the river valley, predictable as cart traffic. This one was coming *over* the ridge, straight down, as if it had business here specifically.

Four. Five. Six. My teeth ached. That was new.

Seven—

"Lena!" Widow Sarelle's voice floated up from her garden, cracked and imperious. "If you're going to lie around on my roof all day, the least you can do is fall off in a dramatic fashion so I have something to talk about at market."

"I'm working," I called back, grateful for the interruption, ungrateful for how obviously my voice shook.

"You're gossiping with the shingles. I can hear you." A pause, then, sharper: "And get down before that storm arrives. There's something wrong with it. Been wrong since dawn. Old Toma says his knee's singing hymns."

Old Toma's knee had predicted weather for forty years with the reliability of a rooster, and if it was singing hymns rather than swearing, the storm was a bad one. I drove the last slate-pin home, gathered my tools, and swung down the ladder just as the first fat drops hit the thatch of the cooper's shed across the lane.

The tremor in my hands had settled into a low hum by the time my feet touched grass. Like a struck bell fading. Like something far away saying my name in a language I didn't speak but somehow understood.

I told myself, as I told myself every time, that it was nothing. That everyone felt storms in their teeth. That the Hollow's silence had soaked into me so thoroughly there couldn't possibly be anything left in me worth silencing.

I was very good at telling myself that. I'd had twenty-two years of practice.

* * *

The storm passed by evening without doing damage, which was itself strange — storms that build like that usually spend themselves violently. This one seemed to lose interest, or lose courage, sliding off east along the ridge like a dog called home mid-bark. The Hollow, I thought, had probably forgotten it already. The valley was good at that too. Weather happened *to* it; it declined to remember.

I was at the Drowned Goose — the only tavern in Thornbrook worth the name, mostly because it was the only tavern in Thornbrook — nursing a cider I was making last too long, when the peddler arrived.

His name was Fitch, and he came through twice a year with pins, ribbons, salt, news, and lies, in roughly descending order of quality. He was a small man built like a question mark, with a pack that outweighed him and a smile he wore like armor against the world's disappointment. The whole room turned toward him the way rooms do when news walks in wearing a coat.

"Evening, evening, don't get up, I can see you're all paralyzed with joy." He deposited his pack on the bench beside me because I was the only one who'd shifted to make room, which tells you something about both of us. "Cider. The good jug, not the one you water for strangers."

"We're watering it anyway," said Marta behind the bar.

"Then water it less." He dropped onto the bench, sighed the sigh of a man whose feet had filed formal complaints, and looked at me sideways. "Lena Amberdale. Still mending roofs? Still unmarried? Still the prettiest face in a valley full of people who look like boiled turnips?"

"Still flattering people to soften them up before you sell them tin buttons as silver."

"They *look* silver. That's practically a warranty." He accepted his cider, drank half of it, and his smile flickered — just for a moment — into something tired underneath. "Actually, Lena. You might want to hear this one sober. Or drunk. Dealer's choice."

The room quieted on its own. Rooms do that around Fitch when he drops the patter. He was, whatever else he was, the closest thing the Hollow had to a window, and windows are precious in places that prefer walls.

"Crown criers," he said. "In Brassen, in Corleigh, in every market town from here to the coast. Read from a scroll with the royal seal, big as your fist. New edict out of the capital." He turned his mug slowly on the table. "All persons gifted with unnatural arts are to be declared to the local magistrate. Registered. And thereafter seized for examination by the Crown's Office of Inquiry."

The word *seized* sat in the air like a dropped knife.

"Seized," repeated Tammas the smith, slow and heavy. "For what?"

"For examination." Fitch shrugged elaborately. "Nobody knows what comes after examination. Nobody's come back to describe it. But the criers had lists, Tammas. Names. Towns where the gifted are known to live. And bounty notices." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "Ten crowns for information leading to a seizure. Twenty for a seizure accomplished. Fifty—" he paused, savoring it like a man savoring a bad oyster, "—for a *witch*."

Someone laughed. Nervously, the way people laugh at funerals when the priest says something almost funny. Someone else said, "Well, good thing there's none of that here," and a murmur of agreement went around the room, comfortable and warm and utterly sincere.

That's the Hollow for you. No magic here. Never has been. Safest place in the realm for honest folk.

I sat very still and finished my cider and noticed that my hands were trembling, and that this time it had nothing to do with any storm.

* * *

Fitch walked me partway home afterward, which he does sometimes, and which I let him because the lane past the millpond is dark and because his chatter keeps the silence from getting ideas.

"You went white as milk back there," he said, when we were out of earshot of the tavern. "Don't bother denying it, I've sold ribbons to enough liars to know the color."

"The proclamation doesn't concern the Hollow."

"No," he agreed mildly. "It concerns the realm. Which has a way of concerning everything eventually, like damp." He walked a few steps, boots squelching in the mud. "Your mother," he said, "came from outside. Everyone knows that. Came over the ridge pregnant and alone and never said from where, and left again when you were four, and never came back."

"She died outside," I said. "We think."

"Mm. And nobody in this valley ever asked her a single question, did they? Not one. Welcomed her in, took her mending and her bread recipes, and never once asked where she got the way she could calm a colicky infant just by humming. Or how she always knew when rain was coming three days out. Or—" he glanced at me, quick and sharp, "—why little Lena Amberdale's hands used to shake whenever thunder rolled, according to a certain peddler who sold your foster father a cradle nineteen years ago and has eyes in his head."

My blood went cold and stayed there. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." He stopped at the gate of our house, where lamplight showed warm in the window and my foster father's shadow moved behind it. "Look. I'm going to say something, and then I'm going to sell you some genuinely stolen lace tomorrow, and neither of us will remember this conversation, because that's how we'll survive it. Here it is: the Office of Inquiry doesn't send criers to towns that interest them. They send criers to towns that bore them. And then, later, quietly, they send other men. Grey coats. Iron. Men who carry lists." He set his hand briefly on my shoulder, light as a bird landing. "A valley that forgets is a fine place to hide, girl. Until somebody remembers you."

He tipped his hat and was gone down the dark lane, whistling something cheerful and off-key, his pack swaying like a hunched second self.

* * *

My foster father, Doss Amberdale, was mending a fishing net by the fire when I came in, because Doss was always mending something — nets, fences, chairs, me. He'd found me at four years old, or been given me, depending on which version of the story you believed; the versions changed depending on how much ale was involved. What never changed was this: he took me in when the valley wouldn't quite, fed me when food was short, taught me slates and knots and how to be invisible, and never once asked me about the shaking in my hands, though I know — I *know* — he saw it.

"Doss," I said. "Fitch brought news. Crown criers. An edict about the gifted."

His hands kept working the net. His eyes didn't move. "Mm."

"They're seizing people. People like—" I stopped. Started again. "People who aren't like us. Obviously."

"Mm," he said again, and then, after a long moment, laid the net down and looked at me across the fire, and his face was older than it had been at supper. "How many slates did you finish on Sarelle's roof?"

"Eleven. Why?"

"Because the west slope needs twelve more, and I want you to do them tomorrow, and I want you to do them well, because a roof is a promise you make to a house." He picked the net back up. "And because while you're up there, you'll be able to see the tree line in every direction, and I'd take it kindly if you'd keep an eye on it."

"An eye on it for what?"

"For grey," he said simply, and went back to his mending, and would say nothing more that night no matter how I asked, and the fire burned down, and outside the window the Hollow lay silent under the stars, forgetting everything, holding its breath.

I climbed to my little room under the eaves and lay awake listening to the valley not-remembering, and I thought about my mother, whom I could not picture clearly anymore — brown hair, a laugh like water over stones, gone — and I thought about a word Fitch had used lightly, the way he uses all his words: *witch*. Fifty crowns. And I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

They will come here eventually. Valleys that forget are the first place hunters look, because everyone hides in them.

And beneath that thought, quieter, in the bone-deep place where the tremor lives, something else stirred — something that felt less like fear than like recognition. Like a bell that has waited years to be struck, hearing footsteps approach the rope.

Sleep did not come easily, and when it finally did, I dreamed of a letter burning — though I had never seen any such letter, and would not understand the dream until it came horribly true.

At dawn, I went up onto Widow Sarelle's roof and mended the west slope, all twelve slates, driving each pin home like a vow. And I watched the tree line.

It stayed green all day.

But three days later, at first light, I looked up from the trough where I was washing my face, and Old Toma was standing in the middle of the lane pointing east with a hand that shook worse than mine ever had, and beyond him, at the far edge of the valley where the trees gave way to the pass, something grey had crossed the line that nothing uncanny was supposed to be able to cross.

Iron, walking into the Hollow. Coming down the road toward Thornbrook.

Coming, though I did not yet know it, for me.
