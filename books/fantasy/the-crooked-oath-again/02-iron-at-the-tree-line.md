## 2. Iron at the Tree Line

The trouble with living in a valley that forgets things is that eventually it forgets you too.

I was up on the Kettering roof that morning, replacing slates the winter wind had pried loose, when I saw them come down the north road. Six riders in grey iron, moving at a walk, the way men move when they're in no hurry because they already know how the day ends.

I sat back on my heels and did the arithmetic every soul in the Hollow does without thinking. Magic dies at the tree line. That's the whole point of us. Storms break apart over the ridge like waves on rocks. Charms curdle. Enchanted blades go dull as spoons. Whatever walks into the Hollow wearing power walks out of it naked — or doesn't walk out at all. The old stories said the valley had swallowed a sorcerer-king whole once, and the only trace of him ever found was one very confused boot.

So iron riders were an oddity, not an omen. Probably tax collectors. Tax collectors dressed like the end of the world to make you grateful about the taxes.

I went back to my slates.

That's the thing I keep returning to, all these months later. I *went back to my slates.* The world was coming down the north road in grey iron and I worried about a roof.

* * *

By noon everyone in Duncraggan's Hollow had seen them and formed an opinion. Old Marta held they were deserters from some border war. The miller's boy swore he'd counted twelve, then twenty, then gave up counting when the numbers stopped being impressive and started being frightening. Tam Briddle — no relation to any Tam I'd come to love later, though I didn't know that yet — insisted they were ghost-soldiers, which earned him a clip round the ear from his mother for being morbid before supper.

I kept my opinion to myself, which was my habit with most things. My opinion was: nobody wears armour like that to collect taxes. Nobody wears armour like that unless they expect to be hated, and want to be feared first.

They made camp by the millpond and didn't ask anyone's leave to do it. That told me more than the armour did. In the Hollow, you ask. You ask to graze your goat on the common, to take water from the well, to marry someone's daughter. Asking is the whole architecture of the place. These men simply took ground the way weather takes ground.

And here was the other thing, the thing I noticed and filed away and told no one, because telling people things is how you get asked questions: their horses drank from the millpond and shied away from it. Both ears flat, rolling their eyes, the way horses do around a storm coming. Horses don't like me much either. I'd learned not to take it personally, but I noticed.

I noticed, and I did nothing, and that evening Da called me inside early and bolted the door behind me, and I understood that he had noticed too.

"Sit," he said.

Da — Aldous Amberdale, though I only ever called him Da, and half the valley thought that was his actual name — was not a man who wasted words. He was a cooper by trade and a worrier by vocation, and he had raised me from the age of four with a tenderness he expressed almost entirely through carpentry. He fixed my shoes. He fixed my chair. He fixed the latch on my window twice a year whether it needed it or not, and if you asked him why, he'd say draft, and change the subject.

He set a candle on the table between us, which was itself alarming. We were careful with candles. Then he reached up into the rafters above the hearth, into the gap where the smoke-blackened beam met the chimney stone, and drew down a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

I knew what it was before he unwrapped it. Of course I knew. I'd known since I was seven and climbed up there after a cat, and felt the shape of it through the cloth, and asked, and been told *your mother's*, and watched my father's face do something I had never seen it do before or since, and never asked again.

"You're going to burn it," I said.

"I'm going to burn it," he agreed.

"It's the only thing she left."

"Aye." He knelt and fed the letter into the coals without ceremony, the way you'd feed in a stick of kindling. It caught. Her handwriting — I could see it through the paper's edge, that slanted, urgent script — went brown, then black, then gone.

Something in my chest went with it. I want to be honest here, since this whole account is meant to be honest: I hated him for about ten seconds. A clean, total hatred, like a struck match. Ten seconds is a long time to hate your father. It's long enough to say things. I said none of them, mostly because my throat had closed.

"Why?" I managed.

"Because there are six men in grey iron sitting at our millpond," Da said, "and one of them isn't a man at all, or isn't only one, and tomorrow they're going to knock on doors and ask questions, and anything in this house that smells of her goes in the fire tonight." He looked up at me across the hearth, and his face was terrible and calm, the way it got during floods. "Lena. What have your hands been doing lately?"

My hands, which had been mending roofs and hauling buckets and doing absolutely nothing remarkable for nineteen years, lay in my lap like evidence.

"Nothing," I said.

"Liar," he said, without heat. "When the thunder comes over the ridge, your fingers twitch. You think nobody sees. I've watched you hide it since you were small enough to fit in a flour barrel." He sighed, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and a few borrowed ones. "Your mother wrote me one letter and made me swear two oaths. First oath: raise her girl plain and quiet, teach her nothing, tell her less. Second oath: if ever the iron came into the valley, the letter burns and the girl runs."

"The girl runs where?"

"That," said Da, "she didn't say. Which I've always considered poor planning on her part."

* * *

They knocked on doors the next morning, just as he'd said. Not all of them. They were selective, which was worse. They came to the Hedleys first, who were nobody, who had never been anybody, whose greatest distinction was a prize-winning marrow — and that frightened everyone more than any amount of marching would have, because if the Hedleys were worth knocking for, then anyone was.

I watched from the loft window while pretending to sort wool. There were six of them on foot now, spread along the lane, and a seventh figure who did not knock at all. She stood in the middle of the road in front of the Hedley door while her soldiers went in, and she looked at the village the way a clerk looks at a ledger. Not cruelly. Not kindly. Like sums.

She wore the same grey iron as the rest, but hers fitted better, and she wore it the way other women wear Sunday clothes. Her hair was bound back severe and silver-streaked, and her eyes — even at forty yards, even through a loft window — were the coldest thing in the valley, colder than the pond in deepwinter, and I include the pond's opinion of me specifically.

Later I'd learn her name was Vess. Later I'd learn a great deal about Inquisitor Vess, more than I ever wanted to. That morning all I knew was that when she turned her head and looked directly at our house — not near it, not past it, *at* it — my hands started trembling so hard I had to sit on them.

Not fear. Or not only fear. It was the other thing, the thing I'd spent my life pressing flat, rising up through me like water through chalk. Somewhere overhead, beyond the ceiling, beyond the sky, a storm was building over the ridge. I couldn't see it. I could feel it the way you feel a train through the soles of your feet, if we'd had trains, which we did not, because the crown had decided railways encouraged wandering.

The storm leaned against the tree line and could not get in. And something in me leaned back.

"Stop it," I whispered to my own fingers. "Stop it, stop it, stop."

They stopped. They always stopped. That was the arrangement I'd made with myself at nine years old, shaking under blankets while thunder walked the rim of the world calling my name in a voice only I could hear: *nothing happens here, nothing ever happens here, and if you keep still, nothing ever will.*

Down in the lane, the cold-eyed woman smiled at our house. It was the worst thing I'd ever seen. It was the smile of someone who has found a typo in a very long document.

Then the soldiers came out of the Hedleys' cottage with old Marta between them, gentle as you please, and Marta was saying something indignant about marrows, and the woman in grey consulted a paper she carried, and I saw her finger move down it and stop.

I couldn't read lips at forty yards. I didn't need to. I knew what names were on that list the way you know a storm's coming — not from seeing it, but from the ache in old scars and the taste of copper and the way the birds go quiet.

One of those names was mine.

* * *

Da didn't argue with me, which frightened me more than shouting would have. I'd built a whole speech on the stairs — *I'm not leaving, this is my home, I've mended half the roofs in this valley* — and he dismantled it before I got three words out by simply handing me a packed satchel.

He'd packed it days ago. Maybe years. There were dried apples in it that predated this autumn.

"There's a shepherd's track behind the fell," he said, talking fast and low, the way he talked during floods, hands moving as if he could build the escape route in the air. "It comes out above the tree line on the far side. You remember the line. You cross it, you keep walking, you don't stop walking until—"

"Until what? Da, until *what?*"

"Until someone finds you," he said. "Someone will find you. She promised me that much."

"She. *She.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You mean my mother. The woman whose entire legacy is a burned letter and a bag of ancient apples. Da, I don't even know her *name.*"

"You do," he said quietly. "You've always known it. You just don't let yourself say it, same as you don't let your hands shake." He gripped my shoulders, and his hands were a joiner's hands, scarred and square and steady, and they were shaking too, which undid me entirely. "Lena. Listen to me. This valley was never a home. It was a hiding place. There's a difference, and I should have told you the difference years ago, and I didn't, because telling you felt like losing you, and I'm a selfish old man."

Boots on the path outside. More than one pair. Unhurried.

Da kissed the top of my head, quick and hard, the way you'd strike a flint. Then he shoved the satchel into my arms, turned me bodily toward the back door, and put himself between me and the front one.

"Out," he said. "Now. And Lena—" He glanced back at me once, and everything he'd never said in nineteen years was crowded into his face with nowhere to go. "Whatever you find out there, whatever she was — you were never a burden. You were the best thing I ever built."

The back door opened onto the vegetable garden, the pig run, the fell. Behind me, through the house, I heard the front knock — three blows, measured, courteous, final — and Da's voice saying something calm and useless about marrows, God help him, buying me seconds with small talk.

I ran.

I ran past the pigs, who screamed their objections, past the bean rows, up the sheep track with my lungs full of knives. At the top of the first rise I did the coward's thing and looked back.

Our house looked small and brown and utterly ordinary, smoke rising straight from its chimney because Da had banked the fire before answering the door, because even at the end he was a careful man. Two figures in grey iron stood in our garden. One of them was looking at the trampled beans, reading them like a page.

And standing in the lane, motionless among the startled chickens, the cold-eyed woman lifted her head and looked directly up the fell at me. Forty yards had become four hundred, and it made no difference at all. Across all that distance and all that morning air, I felt her look land on me like a hand on the back of my neck.

She didn't shout. She didn't send anyone after me. She just looked, and then she smiled that ledger-smile again, and turned back toward the house as if I were a matter already settled, an entry already made.

I ran harder.

The track climbed. The trees thickened, then thinned, then gave out altogether at the crest of the fell, where the world ended in wind and bare rock and the grey-green sea of the high moor beyond. I stopped at the exact line — I couldn't help it, some part of me wanted one last look at the only home I'd ever known — and below me the whole valley lay open like a cupped hand: the millpond, the lanes, the rooftops, the little figures moving between them.

Magic died at that line. It always had. Every charm, every curse, every wonder of the wide world came apart at the tree line like fog in sunlight. That was the covenant of the Hollow, the oldest joke in the realm: a valley where nothing unnatural could follow you, sanctuary guaranteed.

Six riders in grey iron had crossed it anyway.

Standing on the boundary with the wind trying to take my hair apart, I finally understood what that meant, and it wasn't that the hunters had broken the rule.

It was that the rule had never protected people like me. The valley forgot magic. But it seemed magic had not forgotten the valley — or rather, something in the wide world had finally remembered *me*, and had sent iron to collect.

Behind me, faint with distance, a thread of smoke rose from the direction of our house that was too black to be a chimney.

Ahead of me, the moor rolled away toward a horizon I'd never once seen past, and the storm I'd felt all morning broke over the ridge at last — and instead of breaking apart at the tree line, the way storms always had, the way storms were supposed to—

It followed me.

Rain hit the bare rock around my boots in a ring, precise as a drawn circle, and in the heart of that grey curtain something vast turned its attention downward, and a voice that was almost thunder and almost my mother's said, quite clearly:

"*Run faster, little storm.*"

So I did.
