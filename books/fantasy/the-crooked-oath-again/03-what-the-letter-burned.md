## 3. What the Letter Burned

My father burned my mother's letter on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Not because Tuesdays are sacred — nothing in the Hollow is sacred except the harvest and the well — but because he'd had twenty years to burn it and chose the one morning I was standing in the kitchen with my hands still smelling of roof tar.

He didn't even use the hearth. He used the candle by the window, the one he kept for reading accounts, and he held the paper in it with a steadiness that told me he'd rehearsed this. That's the thing about Da: he never did anything dramatic without practicing it first, like a man learning a dance he hoped never to perform.

"Da," I said.

"Don't," he said.

"They're already at the tree line. Burning it doesn't unwrite my name."

"I know what it does." The flame climbed the corner of the page. "It keeps them from knowing what you are."

I could have told him they already knew what I was. Inquisitor Vess had read my name off a list in front of half the village, in that voice like a ledger being closed, and lists don't lie about people; lists *make* people. But there was no arguing with a man holding fire, so instead I watched the paper go.

And here is the terrible, shameful truth I have carried since that Tuesday: as it burned, I remembered more of it than I ever had when it was whole.

* * *

I was six when I found it. It lived inside the false bottom of Ma's sewing box, which Da thought I didn't know about, and which I had discovered within a week of learning that boxes have bottoms. Children are smugglers by nature. We traffic in whatever adults hide.

The paper was soft as cloth from being unfolded and refolded too many times, and the ink had gone brown at the folds where her tears — or rain, though I've come to believe there's little difference between some women and rain — had fallen. I couldn't read all of it then. I read it every year after, adding words to my understanding the way you add stones to a cairn, until at fourteen I knew it whole and pretended not to, because knowing it whole meant admitting what it said.

*My darling thunderhead.* That was how it began. My mother called me thunderhead the way other mothers called their daughters lambkin or sweetpea, and I had always assumed it was because I'd been born during a storm, red-faced and howling. I was wrong about that. I was wrong about most things.

*They will tell you the Hollow will keep you safe, and they will be right for a while, and I am sorry that "a while" is the best I can give you.*

*I was an oath-bearer of Veyl. Say the word aloud once, so it lives in your mouth: Veyl. The storm-realm. Not above us, child — through us. Every storm that has ever broken over human ground broke because Veyl let it, and Veyl let it because of the covenant, and the covenant holds because of signatures written in living blood. Mine was one. There were others. There were supposed to always be others.*

*But oaths are crooked things. They bend around the people who carry them, and when the carriers die or hide or forget, the oath bends toward breaking. It is bending now. I can feel it in my hands the way you feel weather in your teeth.*

That last line I understood at fourteen better than I understood anything else in the world, because I felt weather in my teeth too, and in my wrists, and behind my eyes on nights when lightning walked the ridgeline like a sentry who couldn't remember what she was guarding.

*If the covenant dies, both realms go quiet. You would think quiet is mercy. It isn't. A realm going quiet is a lung refusing air. And the ones like us — the gifted, the signed, the storm-blooded — we would not simply lose our gifts. We would lose the part of ourselves the gift grew around. Do you understand? We are not people who have magic. We are places where magic has a home, and if the home burns down, the person goes with it.*

*So I hid you where magic cannot follow. Forgive me. I built your cage out of my love, and I know that someday you will hate the bars, and I know that someday, if I have done anything right, you will walk out of them anyway.*

There was more — names, I think, or directions, a list of the other heirs scattered across the human realm like seeds from a shaken pod — but those pages I never got to read slowly enough. Because at fifteen, Da caught me with the box open, and he didn't shout. He just sat down across from me at the table, this enormous man made small, and said, "Lena. Some doors you open and then you live in the room forever."

And I, being fifteen and an idiot in the specific way fifteen-year-olds are idiots — believing that safety is a place rather than a decision — put the letter back and shut the false bottom and didn't open it again for five years.

Five years. I traded the whole of my inheritance for one more quiet autumn in the valley.

* * *

"You knew," I said to Da, while the ash of the letter drifted down onto the table like grey snow. "You knew what she was. What I am."

"I knew she was frightened," Da said. "Frightened women write frightening letters."

"She wrote the truth."

"The truth." He laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "Lena, the truth is what got her killed. Or taken. Or whatever it was." He turned on me then, and his eyes were wet and furious and twenty years tired. "She came up that pass with you in her arms and blood on her cloak and she wouldn't say whose, and she stayed long enough to see you walking and then she went back out the way she came, and I have spent your entire life being grateful and angry in equal measure. Don't you dare hand me a letter and call it truth. Truth is flour that keeps. Truth is a roof that holds. Truth is you alive at the end of the season."

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter. Storm-light, though the storms hadn't touched the Hollow in twenty years — they broke against the ridges like waves on a seawall and spent themselves elsewhere. Except today the sky looked less like weather and more like intention.

Horns sounded from the direction of the tree line. Two notes, then silence, then two again. I'd heard that pattern described by peddlers: the witch-hunters' sweep-call. They were moving through the village house by house.

"Lena." Da gripped my shoulders. His hands were shaking, which frightened me more than the horns, because in thirty-one years of knowing him I had seen his hands shake exactly never. "The back pasture. There's the drover's path, up under the west ridge. It's steep and it's ugly and no one uses it because no one knows it. No one but you, because you mended the wall along it twice."

"Da—"

"No." He said it before I could say goodbye, before I could say thank you, before I could say any of the ten thousand things, and I think that was deliberate. I think he knew that if I said goodbye out loud, neither of us would move. "Go. And girl—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—if you find what she wanted you to find, you make it worth the burning."

* * *

The drover's path is not a path so much as an argument the mountain once had with itself. It switchbacks up beneath the west ridge through scree and gorse, past the ruins of a shepherd's hut older than the valley's memory, and I know every handhold of it the way you know the gaps in your own teeth. Which is fortunate, because the men in grey iron do not.

I heard them below me before I saw them — iron makes a particular sound on stone, a clank-and-scrape like a pot falling downstairs — and I pressed myself into the shadow of the ruined hut and watched four of them start up the main trail, the sensible trail, the one any reasonable fugitive would take.

Inquisitor Vess was not with them. Somehow that was worse. Somewhere behind me, that cold ledger-voice was reading names off a list, and mine was crossed off already, and the space where my name had been was the space I now had to survive into.

I waited until the clank-and-scrape faded, then climbed.

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing the only home you've ever known: your body betrays you with competence. My feet found the path without my permission. My hands knew the gorse that holds and the gorse that bites. Twenty years of mending roofs and walls and fences had built me a body that belonged to this valley, and that body carried me upward efficiently, faithfully, while the rest of me screamed.

Halfway up, the wind changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left. The wind in the Hollow comes down from the peaks and dies at the tree line — everyone knows this, it's the valley's one miracle, the reason the founders settled here, magic cannot cross into the Hollow and so the Hollow is safe. The wind below me was that dead wind, flat and empty, carrying woodsmoke and fear.

The wind above me was alive.

It came over the ridge in a long slow exhale and it tasted of ozone and far-off rain and something underneath that I have no honest word for — the smell of a room you've never been in but recognize. And my hands, my traitor, faithful hands, began to tremble. Not the hiding-tremor I'd fought all my life in cellars and shuttered rooms whenever storms passed overhead. This was different. This was the trembling of a struck bell.

*Say the word aloud once, so it lives in your mouth: Veyl.*

"Veyl," I whispered, and the wind picked it up and carried it away like it had been waiting twenty years for someone to say it.

Below me, a shout. One of the hunters had found the drover's path — or rather, had found my footprints in the scree, because I'd been in such a hurry to escape that I'd forgotten the first rule of escaping, which is don't leave a signature. Iron clanked on stone, faster now. They'd seen me.

I ran.

The last stretch of the path is a scramble up a spine of bare rock, and I took it on hands and knees with my lungs full of knives, and behind me the hunters were gaining because iron is tireless and grief is not. And then I crested the spine, and the world ended.

Not literally. Though I'd forgive you for thinking so. The ridge fell away on the far side into country I had only ever seen from a distance — rolling moorland going purple into the dusk, a river stitching silver through it, clouds stacked up over everything like a crowd pressing forward to watch. And the wind hit me full in the face, the living wind, and every hair on my body stood up, and the tremor in my hands stopped being a tremor and became a hum, deep and vast, like standing on the skin of a drum.

Magic. Real magic, unfiltered, after a lifetime of drinking nothing. It was like the first breath after nearly drowning, if drowning lasted twenty years.

The hunters reached the crest behind me, and I turned to look at them — I don't know why, some animal need to face the thing chasing you — and I watched the lead one stop dead at the ridge line. Not from exhaustion. From the same wind. It moved through his iron and he flinched, actually flinched, and I saw understanding cross his face: the Hollow's protection ran only one way. Magic could not enter the valley. But the valley's daughter, walking out of it —

I was no longer a girl hiding in a place magic couldn't reach. I was a storm-blooded heir of Veyl standing in a world where her blood meant something, and every hunter on that ridge knew it.

"Amberdale!" the lead hunter shouted. "By authority of the crown, stand!"

I stood, in a manner of speaking. I stood very fast, downhill, at a run.

The moorland took me in. Gorse and heather and hidden rabbit-holes tried their best to break my ankles, and the light failed, and the shouts behind me spread out into a net, and I ran until running became falling became stumbling became a kind of desperate walking, and the whole time the hum in my hands grew louder, answering the darkening sky, and I understood with terrible clarity that I had spent my whole life holding my breath and I had just, finally, exhaled.

Then the lights came.

Torches, ahead of me, downslope near the river crossing — three of them, four, arranged in a curve. Not pursuit. Ambush. They'd expected the fugitive to come down off the ridge eventually, and here was the net, and behind me the iron was clanking closer, and I stood in the open moor between the hammer and the anvil with a storm building in my bones and no idea what to do with it.

The torches began to converge.

And from somewhere off to my left — from a fold of the moor I'd have sworn was empty — came a voice, low and dry as old leather:

"Down, girl. And whatever happens next — don't apologize to it."
