## 4. The Man Who Kept a Broken Oath

There are three things a man learns to count on when his oath dies: the cold in the morning, the taste of cheap ale, and nobody asking him for anything ever again.

I had gotten very good at all three.

For eleven years I'd kept a shack above the treeline on the eastern shoulder of the Amber Ridge, far enough from anywhere that even the tax collectors forgot I existed, which is the closest thing to sainthood this world offers. My days went like this: wake, hate the morning, split wood I didn't need, drink ale I did need, and watch the sky over the Hollow with the particular bitterness of a man watching a door he's been locked out of.

Because that's what the Hollow was. Everyone said so. A valley where magic goes to die at the tree line. You could stand at the ridge — I stood there often enough, God help me — and feel your own skin go quiet the moment you crossed down into it. No hum of covenant-stone. No itch of gift behind the eyes. Just weather, and dirt, and people who wanted nothing to do with any of it.

Most men would call that peace.

I called it a wound with a fence around it.

The mark sat on my left forearm where it had always sat, the old oath-sigil of the keepers, and for eleven years it had been as dark as a burnt wick. Once it had glowed like banked coals whenever an oath was near — sworn or broken or begged. Now it was just a scar that remembered being something more. There are worse fates. I'd made a list over the years. It was a long list, and most of the entries were my own fault.

So understand me when I say that on the night everything changed, I wanted nothing. I was minding my own ruin, as I had for a decade, when the mark woke up.

* * *

It happened at dusk, and it happened like pain.

I was carrying water up from the spring when my arm lit white-hot under the sleeve — the sigil flaring for the first time since the night I failed, glowing through cloth and skin like a coal someone had blown back to life. I dropped the bucket. I swear on whatever's left of my name, I thought I was dying, and part of me was almost relieved.

Then I looked toward the Hollow, and I understood.

Over the valley, past the tree line, past every law of the place itself, lightning was crawling along the clouds. Not falling — *crawling*, like something alive pacing the length of its cage. The Hollow doesn't do that. The Hollow does grey drizzle and honest thunder and nothing else, because magic cannot cross its border. Magic cannot follow anyone in there.

Which meant whatever was making the sky pace wasn't coming in.

It was trying to get *out*.

I should have gone back inside. I want that on the record. Any sensible man — any man who'd learned the lesson I'd paid eleven years of solitude to learn — would have barred his door and told himself the story he needed: not my oath, not my failure, not my problem.

Instead I found myself saddling the mule, because apparently I'm not a sensible man, just one who plays one in his own company.

* * *

I caught up with them two miles below the pass, where the old drover's road drops through scree toward the lowlands.

I smelled them before I saw them — horses, iron, and lamp-oil. Witch-hunters travel with a stink all their own, like a church that's been set on fire. Four riders and six on foot, fanning out across the road with the patience of men who've done this many times and enjoyed it more than they'd admit. And in the middle of them, running like a hare with wolves on either side, a girl.

Not much of a girl, either. Nineteen maybe, twenty. Brown hair escaping its braid, a mend-kit slung across her back like she'd grabbed the first thing her hands found — which, knowing what I know now, she probably had. Roofing nails spilled out of it as she ran, pinging off stones. She ran the way people run who've never had to run before: fast, and wrong, wasting strength on panic.

And around her, clinging to her like smoke, was the storm-sign.

I don't know how else to put it. The air bent near her. Every few strides the wind changed its mind about which way it blew. Twice, static crawled up her arms and the nearest hunter's horse screamed and bucked. She had no idea what she was doing — that much was plain — but the sky knew. The sky was paying attention.

My dead mark burned like a festival lantern.

"Hold," I muttered to the mule, and tied him to a pine, and did the single stupidest thing available to me, which was to step onto the road ahead of her.

"Girl!" I shouted. "Downslope! Left of the cairn!"

She heard me. Of course she heard me; I have a voice built for shouting at livestock. But she also heard the hunters shout behind her, and panic picked the wrong direction, and she bolted right instead — straight into the arms of two of them who'd cut across the scree.

They took her down hard. I'll give them that; witch-hunters are professionals. Iron manacles came out, the grey-iron kind with the etched bands, the kind that bite a gift quiet. One of them knelt on her back while the other cinched the cuff, and she made a sound I've thought about more times since than I'd like — not a scream. A sound of pure, offended disbelief, like the world had broken a promise she hadn't known she'd been counting on.

The tall one holding the manacles laughed. That decided it, really. You can forgive a man his work. It's harder to forgive him for enjoying it.

"Evening," I called, walking down the slope with my hands visible and my gait loose, the way you approach strange dogs and armed men. "Trouble?"

"None of yours, old man." The tall one didn't even look up. "Crown business. Move along."

"Crown business on my road?" I scratched my beard. "That's funny. Last I checked, I bought the easement rights off a dead smuggler, and dead smugglers are famously difficult to renegotiate with."

That got me a look. Good. Eyes on me were eyes not on the girl.

"Name," said the one kneeling, flat as slate. He had an inquisitor's collar — not the grand kind, a field clerk's badge, some functionary sent along to make the paperwork holy. "Now."

"Corren Vale. Oath-keeper, retired." I let the word sit there. *Retired.* It's amazing what a man will claim when the mark on his arm is hidden under a sleeve and burning hot enough to brand cattle. "And unless that girl stole your purse, you've got the wrong quarry. This is the Amber Ridge. Nothing lives here but goats and regret."

"The girl is named property of the crown," the clerk said. "Proclamation of the third month. All gifted persons to be seized for assessment." He produced a paper, and I confess my stomach dropped, because the crown doesn't waste proclamations on empty valleys. Somebody somewhere had finally started counting heads.

"She's gifted, then?" I asked mildly. "Funny. Looks like a roofer's daughter to me. Look at her hands."

They actually looked. People always look. And in the half-second their attention slid, the girl did something none of us expected.

She *pulled*.

I felt it before I saw it — a pressure drop, a taste of copper and rain on the tongue — and then the evening tore open. Lightning came down off the ridgeline like it had been waiting all day for permission, and it hit the scree ten feet from the hunters, close enough to turn iron hot and hair white. Horses went mad. Two men went down screaming, not burned, just blinded and deafened and utterly certain they were about to die. The clerk lost his precious paper to the wind.

And the girl lay there in the manacles with her eyes wide and her breath ragged, staring at her own hands like they belonged to somebody else.

"That wasn't me," she whispered. "That wasn't — I didn't—"

"Move and I'll gut her!" the tall one shouted at the darkness in general, knife out, which is the traditional battle cry of a man who has just realized the fight has changed shape.

I hit him with a rock.

Not a dramatic rock. Not a hero's rock. An ordinary fist-sized piece of granite I'd picked up on the way down, thrown with the unglamorous accuracy of a man who spent thirty years splitting wood. It caught him above the ear and he folded like laundry. Then everything happened quickly and badly, the way fights do: I got the manacle key off his belt, hauled the girl up by the arm, and we ran.

Behind us the clerk was shrieking names and numbers at the night. Ahead of us was scree, and pine, and my mule, who gave us both a look of profound betrayal as I slashed his tether and shoved the girl up onto the saddle.

"They'll follow," she gasped.

"They'll follow tomorrow," I said. "Lightning makes men religious, and religious men sleep badly. Come on."

* * *

We rode until the moon was high, then walked the mule until my knees filed a formal complaint, then stopped in a hollow beneath a shelf of stone where a fire would be invisible from anything except directly overhead. I built one anyway. Cold makes people stupid, and stupid was the last thing either of us could afford.

She sat against the rock with the manacles still on — I couldn't pick the lock without a smithy, and I said so, and she took it about as well as you'd expect — and stared into the flames.

"You're an oath-keeper," she said eventually.

"I was."

"What does that mean? Exactly."

"It means I used to carry words between people who needed them carried. Sworn bargains. Old contracts. When two parties made a binding vow — a real one, the kind with weight to it — a keeper witnessed it, sealed it, and enforced it." I fed a branch to the fire. "There aren't many of us left. The work dried up. Turns out people prefer promises they can break."

"And yours—" She nodded at my sleeve, and I realized the mark was still faintly glowing through the wool, and cursed inwardly. "Yours broke?"

"Mine went dark," I said. "There's a difference, and it's worse."

She waited. She had that quality, I noticed — the patience of someone raised around people who talk slowly. Roofer's daughter, indeed.

"I was sworn to guard an oath-bearer," I said. "A woman of Veyl. The storm-realm." Her breath caught, small and sharp, and I pretended not to hear it. "Eleven years ago I was late. Three days late, because I trusted a shortcut and a liar, in that order. By the time I reached where she'd been, she was gone, and her covenant-mark was cold, and mine went dark the same hour. That's what happens when your charge dies. Your mark goes out like a lamp with the oil poured away." I shrugged, because the alternative was saying it plainly, and I'd rather swallow glass. "Since then I've kept bees I don't like and drunk ale I do. That's the whole profession now."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"You knew her," the girl said quietly. "My mother."

I looked at her properly then — really looked, the way I should have on the road. The storm-sign hung around her still, fainter now, exhausted, but unmistakable to anyone whose arm had once read such things by heat alone. The same pressure in the air. The same copper-rain taste. Eleven years I'd told myself I'd know that signature anywhere, and here it was, sitting in my firelight wearing a mended coat and roofing nails.

"Lena," I said. "Lena Amberdale."

Her whole body went rigid. "How do you know my name?"

"Your mother talked about you the way other women talk about saints." I pulled off my boot because my feet hurt, and because it gave my hands something to do. "Elara Amberdale, oath-bearer of Veyl, last signatory of the covenant. She hid you in the Hollow because magic can't reach you there. She told me once — the last time I saw her alive — 'If anything happens to me, Corren, my daughter stays in the valley until she's ready. Promise me you won't come looking.' So I didn't. For eleven years I kept the only promise I had left, which was to stay away." I snorted. "And then the crown sends hunters in after you anyway, and the sky starts clawing at the treeline, and it turns out staying away was never going to be enough. Story of my life. I arrive too late to prevent things and just in time to clean them up."

"You're saying my mother is—"

"Dead. Yes. I'm sorry. I'm bad at sorry; you'll notice."

She was crying. Silently, the way people cry who learned young that noise gets noticed. I looked at the fire and let her, because some griefs deserve privacy even in company, and because if I looked at her face I was going to see Elara in it, and I'd had eleven years of practice not doing that.

"They're going to keep coming," she said at last, voice scraped raw. "The hunters. The man with the list — there's a list, my name is *on* it. And the proclamation, everyone in the lowlands will—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I can't go back to the Hollow. They breached it. Sanctuary was the one thing it was supposed to be, and they just... walked in."

"Grey iron walks anywhere it's paid to," I said. "The Hollow keeps magic out. It never kept out ambition. Different animals entirely."

"So where do I go?"

And there it was. The question I'd ridden down a mountainside hoping not to hear, delivered by a chained girl with her mother's stubbornness and her mother's storm sitting on her shoulders like a second cloak.

I should have pointed her south, to the coast, to a ship, to anywhere the crown's writ thinned out. I'd rehearsed the speech on the ride. *Forget the realms, girl. Forget the blood. Live small and live long.*

What I said instead was: "You've seen the letter she left you? The one your foster father—"

"Burned." Flat. Wounded. "He burned it before they could find it. I read pieces when I was small. Something about a Stormseat. About heirs. About the covenant dying and needing—" Her hands turned over in her lap, helpless. "I don't remember the rest. I was eight."

"The Stormseat." I said the word and felt my dark mark shiver, like a dog hearing its name after years of being called something else. "Of course it's the Stormseat. She'd leave you the one errand in the world with a body count."

"Then tell me what it is."

"It's the anchor-point. Where the covenant between the human realm and Veyl was signed, sealed, and — if your mother's fragments are true — is currently coming apart at the seams." I stared into the fire. "When the covenant fails, the storm-realm starves, and everything it feeds — every gift, every heir-blood, every scrap of wonder in this world — goes with it. Including you. Especially you, probably. And the crown's answer to all this is to hunt gifted folk like rats before the lights go out entirely." I laughed, short and joyless. "The timing's almost elegant."

"Take me there."

"No."

"You don't get to say no."

"I absolutely do. It's the main privilege of being a disgraced hermit." I held up a hand before she could spend breath on outrage. "Listen to me, Lena Amberdale. I am not a guide. I am not a hero. I am a man who failed the last person who asked him for exactly this, and the distance between those failures is measured in graves. Find another keeper. Find a smuggler, a priest, a very ambitious goat — anyone whose mistakes haven't already cost one Amberdale her life."

"Are there other keepers?"

Silence. The fire crackled. The owl tried again, less hopefully.

"That's what I thought," she said.

"It's not—" I pinched the bridge of my nose. "It isn't safe with me. That's the point. Everything near me ends up late, or lost, or—"

"My mother trusted you."

"She shouldn't have."

"But she did." Lena leaned forward, manacles clinking, firelight catching the storm-grey in her eyes that I'd bet coin she'd never seen in a mirror. "And she's dead, and I'm hunted, and the sky talks to me now whether I like it or not, and the only person in the world who knows what any of it means is a bitter old man in the woods who throws rocks at knights." Her chin came up. Elara's chin. Exact same angle. "So take me to the Stormseat, Keeper. Or admit that your broken oath means less to you than your fear does."

Well. What do you say to that?

I said nothing for a long time, which she correctly interpreted as losing. I drank the last of the ale, which helped nobody. And I looked at the mark on my arm, still faintly aglow for the first time in eleven years, burning steady in the presence of an oath-bearer's daughter like a compass needle that had finally found its north again — and I understood, with the particular misery of a man being dragged back into a story he'd paid full price to escape, that the mark had known before I did.

It had flared the moment she'd neared the tree line. It had been pulling me downhill all night.

"Fine," I said. "FINE. But we do this my way. We avoid roads. We avoid towns. We avoid, above all, standing out — which means you learn to keep that sky of yours leashed, starting tomorrow, because the next hunters won't be four men and a clerk, they'll be forty, and they'll bring iron made specifically for people who throw lightning." I jabbed a finger at her. "And when we get there — if we get there — you find whoever or whatever the Stormseat wants, you finish your mother's work, and you release me from this. Whatever this is." I waved at my glowing arm with disgust. "I retire *once*, girl. Properly, this time."

"Deal," she said, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — the first loosening I'd seen since the scree. Then, because she was her mother's daughter: "Corren. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the rock."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was gone as quick as it came, replaced by something older and heavier, and she curled up against the stone with her chained wrists tucked in, and within minutes she was asleep — really asleep, the boneless sleep of the young, which I resented on principle.

I stayed up and watched the road.

Around midnight, far to the south, I saw them: watch-fires strung along the lowland passes like a necklace of orange beads. Too many for a pursuit party. Too organized for a hunt.

More like a net, being drawn closed.

And above the nearest fires, where the clouds should have been plain and empty, the sky was doing something I hadn't seen since the last days of the old covenant. It was *leaning*. Bending southward, all of it, wind and cloud and pressure, the way iron filings bend to a lodestone — bending toward something out past the river-country, something east.

Toward Brassen, if I had to guess. The river-city.

Toward water.

I looked at the sleeping girl, and the glowing mark, and the leaning sky, and I sighed the sigh of a man whose retirement had officially lasted eleven years and four hours.

"Well," I told the owl. "Let's hope the next heir's friendlier than the last one."

The owl, wisely, declined to comment.
