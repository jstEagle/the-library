## 5. The First Heir

### Lena

Brassen smelled of fish, tar, and money, in roughly that order.

I had never seen a city before. I had seen the Hollow's one street, its chapel, its millpond, and a great deal of sky, which I had assumed was enough world for anyone. Brassen corrected me within an hour. It was built the way rivers build — crookedly, in layers, with older things sinking underneath newer things until nobody remembered what had been there first. Houses leaned on each other like tired drunks. Bridges stitched the two banks together in seven places, and every one of them was crowded with people who looked at strangers the way gulls look at unattended bread.

"Keep your hood up," Corren said, for the eleventh time since the ridge.

"It's hot."

"Keep it up anyway."

He walked like a man trying to take up less space than he did, which was difficult, because Corren was built along the lines of a doorframe. His grey oath-keeper's coat had been dyed brown somewhere along the road, badly, so that in certain light it still bled grey at the seams — a broken promise you could see from across a street. He kept his left hand near his knife and his right hand near me, and I understood without being told that both were the same gesture.

We had been three days out of the hills, sleeping in ditches and eating Corren's terrible travel bread, and this was the plan entire: find the heir of the water-oath in Brassen, convince her to walk a thousand miles to a mountain that was actively dying, and do it all before the crown's hunters found us. When I pointed out the flaws in this, Corren said, "Yes," in a tone that suggested the flaws had been personally introduced to him years ago and they were old friends now.

"How do we even know she's here?"

"Oath-bearers keep to their element when they're frightened. Water folk go to water." He said it the way farmers say *rain comes from clouds*. "Her mother kept the ferry at Brassen. If the daughter lives, the daughter ferries."

"And if she doesn't want to come?"

Corren was quiet long enough for a fishmonger to shout past us about the freshness of his eels. "Then we've lost nothing but time," he said, which was a lie, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so.

The river itself was the widest thing I'd ever seen — wider than the whole Hollow laid end to end, brown-green and muscular, carrying barges and gossip and the occasional drowned crate toward the sea. Ferries crossed it in a constant shuttle, flat-bottomed boats poled by men and women with forearms like ship cable. We stood at the third landing down and watched them, and I tried to feel something holy about it, the bloodline of an ancient covenant keeping its quiet watch over the crossing. Mostly what I felt was damp.

Then I saw her.

She was easy to pick out even among the ferrymen, though nothing about her announced itself. A woman of maybe thirty, short, broad through the shoulders, with sunburnt brown skin and black hair hacked off at the jaw as if she'd done it herself with a boat knife and hadn't cared how it came out. She worked the small passenger skiff, the one that took people across for a copper instead of a silver, and she worked it better than anyone else at the landing — no wasted motion, the pole never once slapping the water where it should have slipped silent. Her passengers got where they were going faster than they expected and couldn't say why.

But that wasn't how I knew. I knew because of the rain.

It had started spitting while we watched, a thin grey drizzle off the river, the kind that means nothing. And around her skiff — only around her skiff, a circle maybe ten feet wide — the drizzle bent. It fell inward, then aside, leaving her and her passengers dry in a moving pocket of clear air, like a hole poked in the weather. She didn't look up at it. She didn't acknowledge it at all. When the pocket drifted too wide she frowned faintly, the way you'd frown at a draft under a door, and it tightened.

"She doesn't know she does it," I whispered.

"Oh, she knows," Corren said. "She just pretends it's ballast luck or good rope. They always have an explanation ready. It's how they survive." Something moved through his voice that I'd learned to hear by then — the sound of a man describing himself. "Watch. She'll dock at the far post, count her coppers twice, and tell anyone who asks that the rain follows the channel wind."

The skiff slid in exactly as he predicted. The woman counted her coppers twice. And when a dockhand asked why she'd stayed dry, she said, "Channel wind," without looking up, and the dockhand nodded as if this were a known feature of rivers.

"That's her," I said. "That's the heir."

"That's the heir," Corren agreed grimly, "and she is going to be delighted to see us."

### Maren

There are three kinds of trouble that come to a ferry landing: drunk ones, rich ones, and ones who ask too many questions about the weather.

The big one in the bad dye job was the third kind. I marked him the moment he stepped onto my landing, partly because he walked like a soldier pretending to be a merchant, and mostly because he stood at the rail watching the rain bend around my skiff with the expression of a man doing arithmetic. Nobody looks at channel wind like that. Channel wind is for sailors and liars, and he was neither.

His companion was worse. Young girl, hood up in summer, standing too still. People who stand too still are either hiding something or hunting something, and the hood said hiding, and the eyes over the rim of the hood said hunting, and I didn't like the combination.

They waited until my last fare of the evening — old Pemmel, going home late from his sister's, snoring before we hit mid-channel — and then the big one stepped up to my post with a copper held out like a peace offering.

"Crossing?" he said.

"Fare's a copper. Fare's still a copper if you talk the whole way. Double if you ask about the weather."

Something flickered across his face. Guilt, maybe. Soldiers wear guilt badly; it sits on them like armor that doesn't fit. "One crossing," he said. "And a word after, if you'll give it."

"I sell crossings. Words are free, which is why I don't deal in them."

The girl climbed aboard without a word and sat in the stern with her hands folded, and I pushed off, and the river took us the way it takes everything, patiently, without opinion. Halfway across the big one leaned on the rail and looked at the water for a while, gathering whatever speech he'd brought, and I gave him time because the current was kind tonight and I'm not a monster.

"My name is Corren Vael," he finally said. "I was an oath-keeper of the old covenant."

The pole nearly went out of my hands.

Not because of the words themselves — those were just words, dead words, the kind that show up in chapel murals and grandmother's warnings. Because of how he said them. You can tell a lot about a person by how they hold a title they've lost. Some men polish it. Some men spit on it. This one held it out flat on his palm like something that had bitten him and might bite again, and waited to see if I'd flinch.

"Don't know what that is," I said.

"Yes, you do."

"The fare's still a copper."

"Maren." The girl spoke for the first time, and her voice was younger than her eyes, valley-flat and stubborn. "Your mother was Sera of the Crossing. She was the last signatory of the water-oath. She died keeping it, eleven years ago, at the ford at Harrowmere, and the covenant has been failing ever since, and you are the last heir of it alive."

The river made a soft sound against the hull. That was all. Rivers are polite that way; they let you have your disasters in private.

I finished the crossing. I always finish the crossing — you don't stop a boat mid-water to argue, that's how people drown, and I had done quite enough drowning for one lifetime already, thank you. I ran us up against the far pilings, tied off, and turned around.

"You've got the wrong woman," I said. "My mother sold cloth."

"Your mother held a ford against a flood with her bare hands," the girl said, "and forty people lived because of it, and the crown called it witchcraft and burned your house for it, and you were sent to an aunt in Brassen and told never to speak of it again."

I looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind us a bell buoy clanked. My hands, I noticed distantly, were steady on the mooring line, which was more than I could say for anything else.

"Who told you that?"

"Your mother wrote it down. Before she died. There's a letter, and a seal-ring, and a list of names, and mine is on it, and yours is next." She pulled back her hood, and I saw what the hood had been hiding: a thin white scar running from her temple into her hairline, and eyes the grey-green of deep water right before it storms. "My name is Lena Amberdale. My mother was Wren Amberdale, and she hid me in a valley where magic can't follow, and the crown found me anyway, and I am asking you —"

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the —"

"No." I held up a hand. "I'm going to say this once, so listen, because I don't repeat myself on the water. Whatever your mothers signed, whatever oaths, whatever realms — I run a ferry. Two banks, one river, six coppers a day. People get on, people get off, everybody pays. That's the whole covenant I keep. You want someone to save the world, try the chapel. They've got chairs."

"The covenant is dying," Corren said quietly. "The storm-realm is starving. When it goes, everything gifted goes with it — and everything the gifted hold back goes loose. The floods, the fires, the storms. Your river included."

"Then the river and I will retire together." I untied his fare from his fingers — he let me, which I appreciated — and jerked my thumb at the bank. "Landing's there. Walk straight, mind the eel carts, don't come back."

The girl — Lena — didn't move. She had the look of someone standing in a doorway deciding whether to knock harder or leave something on the step. I knew that look. I'd worn it myself, once, outside a certain aunt's house, holding a bundle of my mother's things I'd never opened.

"Vess is coming here," she said. "Inquisitor Vess. Grey iron. She has a list, and she reads names off it in every town between here and the mountains, and she pays silver for denunciations. She found me in a valley that magic cannot enter. Do you understand? She walked into the one place in the world where my mother thought I'd be safe, because she wanted it enough to break the rule everyone believed was absolute." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. "There is nowhere to run a ferry to, Maren. I checked."

"Then I'll swim," I said, and turned my back on them, and poled home through the darkening water, and told myself the cold feeling in my stomach was just the evening fog coming off the river.

### Lena

Corren wanted to leave that night. I refused, and to my surprise he yielded, and I understood later that he'd expected to be refused — that some part of him had wanted me to insist, the way you test a rope by leaning on it. So we took a room above a chandlery that smelled of pitch and honest labor, and I lay awake listening to the river breathe against the pilings, and I thought about doors.

Maren had closed hers. But she had closed it like a person, not like a wall. She'd listened to the whole of it — the ford at Harrowmere, the burning, the letter — and her face had done things she would have denied under torture. Grief keeps strange hours. It had been eleven years, and you could see that she'd spent all eleven of them standing guard over a wound, checking it daily, daring it to heal.

"She'll come," I said in the dark.

"She'll throw us off the pier," Corren said. "Go to sleep."

I did sleep, eventually, and dreamed of the Hollow, of the tree line standing in its patient ring around the valley like a sentence nobody had finished. I woke to bells.

Not chapel bells. Alarm bells — the flat, urgent clangor of the harbor watch, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause. Corren was already at the window, dressed, knife out, and the look on his face closed the conversation before I opened my mouth.

Below, at the foot of our street, grey figures were moving through the morning market. Not many. Six, maybe eight, in iron-grey coats, unhurried, spreading out from the landing the way ink spreads through water. And at their center, walking as though the mud of Brassen were a carpet laid for her, a woman in a long dark coat with a face like a closed ledger.

"Vess," Corren breathed. "How — we were three days ahead. Three days."

"Maybe we weren't," I said. "Maybe she was never behind us. Maybe she was ahead the whole time, waiting to see who we'd visit." The thought arrived cold and complete, the way true things do. "She knows the list, Corren. She's always known the list. She isn't chasing us. She's reading us."

Down at the landings, the ferrymen were being lined up. I saw Maren among them, arms crossed, chin up, wearing the particular insolence of a citizen with nothing to hide, and I saw a hunter hold a paper up to her face, and I saw her shake her head.

"We can't fight eight," Corren said. "We can't outrun them to the gate. And we can't leave her."

"No," I agreed. "We can't."

What happened next, I only half-saw, and I have thought about it every day since. The hunters were marching Maren and two other ferrymen up from the water toward the harbor square, toward a wagon that had rolled up with the efficiency of a thing long planned. Maren's eyes swept the crowd once — quick, professional, the scan of a woman counting heads on a boat — and found mine, and I will swear until I die that what passed across her face was not fear for herself. It was fury. Pure, banked, eleven-years-deep fury that the river she'd given her whole life to had brought this to her doorstep anyway, after everything, after she'd paid.

And the river answered her.

I felt it before anyone saw it — a pressure in my teeth, in the scar over my ear, in the old hollow place where my mother's magic slept. The water level at the landings rose four inches in the space of a breath. Then another four. The barges groaned against their lines. Down the length of the great brown river, as far as I could see, the current reversed — actually reversed, the whole patient muscle of it flexing backward against its own nature — and came up the harbor like a hand closing.

It wasn't violent. That was the terrifying part. A wave would have been forgivable, explicable, a freak of tide. This was deliberate. Water climbed the pilings, slid across the boards, curled around the hunters' boots and lifted — gently, irresistibly — until eight armed men in iron-grey coats were floating, flailing, thoroughly undignified, their wagon bobbing away toward mid-channel with the harbor watch swimming after it. Papers scattered and dissolved. Maren stood on the flooded dock with water streaming off her shoulders in a perfect circle that never touched her, breathing hard, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who'd broken into her life.

Every eye in Brassen was on her. Every eye except the cold grey pair at the edge of the square, which had found, with terrible calm, the second-floor window where I stood.

"Corren," I said. "Now."

We went down the back stairs and through the chandlery and out among the eel carts, and the crowd was screaming and laughing and fleeing all at once, and somehow, impossibly, Maren was with us — I never saw her decide; one moment she was a statue on the dock and the next she was hauling us both by the collar into the alley behind the ropewalk, moving with the surefootedness of someone who had grown up knowing every plank of this city that floated.

"Boat," she said. "My cousin's. Under the west stairs. Move."

We moved. Behind us the alarm bells kept ringing, and somewhere in the chaos a cold voice was giving orders that carried unnaturally far, and I did not look back, because I already knew what I'd see: Inquisitor Vess, dry amid the flood, watching us go, filing everything away.

### Maren

Here is what nobody tells you about the gift: it doesn't ask.

Eleven years I'd kept it small. A dry circle in the rain. A skiff that rode a little easier than physics allowed. Small kindnesses, small cheats, nothing that would make a priest look twice. I'd fed it on silence the way you feed a dog under the table, and I'd genuinely believed — I had built my whole life on believing — that it would stay small forever, that it was a thing I owned rather than a thing that owned me.

Then a girl with a scar looked at me across a ferry deck and told me the truth about my mother, and the river stood up.

I didn't call it. Understand that. I have gone over that morning ten thousand times and I know exactly what I did, which was nothing. I was angry, and the water heard the anger, and it came. All of it. Eleven years of it, plus my mother's share, plus whatever vast slow patience runs under the world and answers to people like us. I stood in the middle of a miracle I never asked for, in front of the entire harbor of Brassen, and I knew with total clarity that my life as a ferry-woman was over — that the small warm ordinary thing I'd built plank by plank for eleven years had just been washed away by the very element I'd spent those years serving.

So yes. I was furious. And yes. I was frightened. Both at once, in the same breath, all the way down.

The cousin's boat was a leaky skiff with a bad sail and my cousin's name burned into the tiller, and I stole it without a flicker of remorse, because property crimes hardly register once you've committed public witchcraft. I got us out on the ebb tide with the harbor watch still fishing hunters out of the drink, and I put Brassen behind us at a speed the little boat had no right to, and for a long time none of us said anything, because what was there to say.

Finally the girl — Lena, the storm-child, the reason my life had ended — sat down across from me in the bilge water and said, "You didn't have to do that."

"Don't," I said.

"They'd have taken you. Vess would have —"

"I said *don't*." My voice cracked on it, which I hated. I gripped the tiller and watched the city shrink behind us, the seven bridges, the smoke, the whole shape of the only life I had. "You don't get to thank me. I didn't do it for you. I didn't do it at all. It did it. Do you understand the difference? Because I'm beginning to, and I hate it."

Lena was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed — softer, and sadder, and much older than her years. "I lived in a valley where magic couldn't reach me," she said. "Twenty-two years. I mended roofs. I knew everyone's name. And the whole time I was holding my breath, because I knew it was borrowed, that someday the thing in my blood would come collect." She looked out at the open water ahead of us, at the grey line of the sea. "You held your breath for eleven years. I'm sorry. I truly am. But the breath is out now, Maren. It doesn't go back in."

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. I opened my mouth to tell her she was wrong, that I'd go back, that I'd bribe someone, lie my way home, dry the memory out of the harbor with a well-placed rumor and a fistful of silver.

Then I looked at the big one — Corren — sitting silent in the bow with his badly-dyed coat and his face like a man attending his own funeral, and I looked at the girl with her mother's storm-eyes, and I thought about a paper held up to my face on my own dock, and a cold woman in a dark coat whose gaze I had felt on my back like a blade laid flat between my shoulders.

The river carried us seaward. It didn't ask, either.

"There's a list," I said at last, not looking at either of them. "Of heirs. Yours is on it, mine's on it. How many names?"

"Five," Lena said. "Four living, besides the seal."

"So there are two more after me." I steered us into the deep channel, where the water ran dark and strong, going where it liked, taking me with it. "Fine. Fine. But I'm telling you now, storm-child, and I'm telling you once: I'm not saving any realms. I'm getting you to your mountain, and I'm keeping you alive, because apparently that's what my blood does whether I like it or not. After that, I'm going home."

Neither of them answered. The sail filled. The coast of Brassen sank below the horizon behind us, and ahead the water stretched out grey and endless toward the mountains I'd sworn I'd never see.

Somewhere down in the hold of the stolen skiff, wrapped in oilcloth I'd grabbed without thinking from under my cousin's seat, was my mother's old ferry-box — and inside it, though I wouldn't open it for three more days, a letter in my mother's hand, and a ring with a wave cut into the signet, and the first line of a liturgy I had spent my whole life pretending I didn't already know by heart.
