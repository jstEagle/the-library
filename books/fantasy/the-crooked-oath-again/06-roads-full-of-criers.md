## 6. Roads Full of Criers

I have run a ferry for eleven years, and in that time I learned exactly two things about people: they will pay double to cross a river at night, and they will tell you their whole lives if you pretend not to be listening. So believe me when I say I know how to read a crowd. And the crowd in the market square of Millbrook was reading itself out loud.

A crier stood on the well-head in a coat the color of wet slate, reading the proclamation for the third time that morning. I could hear it from the edge of the square, where I was pretending to inspect a wheel of cheese I had no intention of buying.

"—all persons gifted with unnatural arts to surrender themselves to the crown's officers, or be seized. Any who shelter the gifted shall share their sentence. Any who inform shall receive ten crowns—"

Ten crowns. For a name. I'd watched men haggle down to a halfpenny over fish heads, and here was ten crowns for pointing at your neighbor and saying *her*.

"Ten crowns," said the cheese-woman beside me, not to me, just to the air, in the way people say things they don't want to own up to having said.

"Aye," said the man next to her. "Old Wenna at the mill, her granddaughter's got the knowing. Lights candles without striking them. Sweet girl."

The cheese-woman went quiet in a particular way. The way of someone doing arithmetic.

I bought the cheese. I didn't want the cheese. I paid for it because standing there while two strangers priced a child felt like something I'd have to answer for later, and I already had enough of those.

*You're not from here,* I reminded myself, hauling my purchases back toward the inn where Corren and Lena were waiting. *You're Tamsin Vael, wool merchant's widow, traveling to your sister in Harrow Ford. You have no gift except an unfortunate talent for drowning things you're angry at.* That last part was supposed to be a joke. It wasn't landing, even privately.

Three days on the road since Brassen, and I still hadn't decided whether I'd been kidnapped or rescued. The jury was out. The jury was me, and I was a biased witness.

* * *

Corren had the map spread across the inn table like he was planning a war, which, in fairness, he sort of was — a very small, very cowardly war whose entire strategy was *don't be interesting.*

"We avoid the king's road," he said, tapping it. "Criers post at every crossroads on it now. We take the drover's paths through the hills."

"The drover's paths add four days," I said.

"The king's road adds a gibbet." He said it flat, not cruel, the way you'd report the weather. "Four days is cheap."

Lena sat by the window with her hood up, watching the square. She'd been doing that a lot — watching, cataloguing exits, flinching whenever anyone in grey passed below. She looked, I thought, like a woman trying to remember how furniture worked in a house she used to live in.

"They knew my mother's name," she said, to the window. "In the Hollow. Vess read it off a list. Someone wrote it down years ago and kept it." Her hands were doing the thing again — that small tremor, like a bird trapped under cloth. "Who keeps a list like that?"

"People who are paid to," Corren said. "People who are afraid. Usually both."

"And everyone in that square today," I said, dropping into the third chair, "is going to do the arithmetic. Ten crowns against a neighbor. You want to know what I learned running a ferry? Nobody thinks of themselves as the kind of person who sells out a child. They think of themselves as the kind of person who'd never do it — right up until the moment they do, and then they think of reasons."

Corren looked at me. "That's cheerful."

"I'm a cheerful woman."

"You flooded a dock district yesterday."

"To save our lives."

"And felt terrible about it."

"I felt terrible about the *boats*," I said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and we both let it sit there like a stone nobody wanted to pick up.

Here is what I hadn't told them: I'd grown up in Brassen hearing about the hunt the way you hear about weather in far-off countries. Bad luck for other people. My grandmother made me swear the oath-silence before I could swim — *never show them, never tell them, the river keeps your secrets better than any friend* — and I'd resented her for it my whole life, because it meant lying, constantly, in small ways, to everyone, forever. Lying about why I never bathed at the public house. Lying about why the fish always came to my lines. Lying so long and so smoothly that by the time the hunters actually came to Brassen, I didn't know which parts of me were real.

And then these two had shown up, and in one afternoon I'd torn the roof off all that lying, drowned a dozen boats, and left with strangers. My grandmother would have had opinions. My grandmother was also dead, which I chose to interpret as her being unavailable for comment.

"So we're merchants," I said, because someone had to say something practical before I started confessing things. "Wool. Boring, heavy, unremarkable wool. Nobody robs a wool cart. Nobody looks twice at wool."

"Nobody looks once at wool," Corren agreed. "It's perfect."

Lena turned from the window. "And if someone asks questions?"

"Then Tamsin Vael talks," I said, "because Tamsin Vael has a dead husband, a bad hip, and a sister in Harrow Ford who owes her money, and she will tell you about all three given the slightest encouragement. People flee from a woman mid-anecdote. Trust me. I've been that woman at funerals."

For the first time in three days, Lena smiled. It was small and crooked and gone too fast, but it was there, and I found — annoyingly — that I'd have done quite a lot to see it again.

* * *

The trouble with pretending to be boring is that you have to commit to it completely, and I am, at heart, a spectacularly un-boring person trapped in a boring person's employment.

We did well for six days. Corren drove the cart and played the taciturn drover so convincingly that I suspected it wasn't entirely play. Lena kept the accounts and shrank into her hood at every checkpoint. I talked. Gods, how I talked. I told toll-keepers about my late husband Aldous (drowned, tragically, though I kept the cause vague) and my hip (acting) and my sister Berthe (fictional, but vivid). I complained about wool prices with such authority that a factor in Duncastle asked my advice and then took it.

And the road taught me things, those six days. It taught me that the proclamation had teeth everywhere — criers in every town, notices nailed to church doors, and once, horribly, a pair of stocks outside a village green where a woman sat with a sign reading GIFT-CONCEALER, while farmers walked past with their chins carefully aimed elsewhere. It taught me that fear travels faster than horses.

But it also taught me this:

In Duncastle, when the crier read the list of seized names and a boy in the crowd started crying because his aunt was on it, an old man put his hand on the boy's shoulder and stood there, in front of everyone, until the boy stopped shaking. In front of everyone. That's either bravery or stupidity, and after eleven years of watching people, I can tell you it's rarer than either.

At a ford in the hill country, our axle cracked — Corren swore with the fluency of a man who'd clearly spent years around soldiers — and a drover named Hesk stopped his own train to help us mend it. Refused payment. Fed us stew. Talked the entire time about his daughter, away at her aunt's, clever girl, good with animals, animals always calm around her, funny thing that—

He said it lightly. He said it the way you toss a coin into water: testing the depth.

And I heard it. And I looked at him, this broad, sunburnt stranger who owed us nothing, and I understood that he was asking — not *are you hunted*, nothing so dangerous as words — just: *is the world safe for her? Is anyone?*

"She sounds lovely," I said. "My sister's girl is the same. Animals follow her around like she's made of bread."

Hesk's eyes met mine for half a second. Something passed between us that had no name on any proclamation. Then he tipped his cap, climbed onto his wagon, and rolled away, and neither of us had said a single prosecutable word.

"What was that?" Lena asked quietly, when he was gone.

"That," I said, "was two liars telling each other the truth."

She frowned at that, working it over. Corren just grunted, which from him was practically applause.

There were others. A tavern keeper in a place called Netherby who "lost" our names from his ledger and charged us for two beds instead of three. A woman selling apples who slipped an extra handful into Lena's bag and squeezed her hand — just squeezed it, once, hard — and walked on. Small kindnesses, each one a tiny crime now, each one costing the giver something. That was the part that got under my skin. Kindness used to be free. The crown had put a price on it, and people were paying anyway, out of purses that couldn't afford it.

I lay awake that night in the cart's lee, wrapped in a tarp that smelled of lanolin, listening to Corren take first watch, and I thought about my ferry sitting empty at its mooring in Brassen. About old Pieter who rented me my berth, who'd known me since I was nine. Had he wondered? Had he ever done the arithmetic? Ten crowns against Maren, who pulled his grandson out of the current that spring day?

I decided, staring at the stars, that I'd rather not know. Some ledgers you don't get to audit.

* * *

On the ninth day we came down out of the hills toward the river valley, and the road turned wrong.

I felt it before I saw anything — a wrongness in the air pressure, the way the sky goes flat and attentive before weather breaks. Then Corren reined up at the top of the rise, and we all saw it together.

Smoke. Not chimney smoke. Too much, too black, too widely spread across the lowland ahead, where the map promised a town called Cinderhollow.

"Fire?" Lena asked.

"Started days ago, by the look of it," Corren said. His jaw had gone tight. "Or fires."

We came down cautious, Tamsin Vael and her drover and her bad hip, past fields gone strange with ash drifts like dirty snow. The nearer we got, the fewer people we met on the road — and the ones we did meet had a look I recognized from flood country, from the year the Brassen broke its banks. The hollowed-out walk of people who had lost the argument with the world.

At the town's edge, a woman sat on a milestone with a child wrapped in a blanket, and I did what ferry-folk do, what you learn to do when your livelihood is strangers: I slowed, and I listened.

"—came in the night," she was saying to another woman, voice scraped raw. "Grey iron, dozens of them. Said a gifted child was hiding here. Said the town was harboring. They gave the reeve till dawn to hand the child over, and when he couldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "They burned the east quarter. Just that. Just enough. Like a lesson written in fire."

"Did they find the child?" the second woman asked.

"No one knows. Some say the child set the fire itself, when they came for it. Some say it sang the flames to sleep and saved half the town." The woman laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in weeks. "A child, saving us. That's where we are now."

Beside me, Lena had gone very still. I knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic of her own — not ten crowns against a neighbor, but something larger, something with a child's weight in it.

"We should go around," Corren said quietly. "Ash means disease, and ash means hunters circling back to admire their work. Every instinct says around."

"Yes," Lena said.

She didn't move.

"Lena."

"There might be survivors," she said. "There might be a child who sang fire to sleep, alone, in a burned town, with every soul in it either dead or too frightened to feed it." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes had that storm-light in them, the thing I'd seen only once before, on the docks in Brassen, right before the river stood up. "You ran a ferry. You've pulled people out of water. Tell me — when you hear someone drowning, do you calculate the current first? Or do you already be swimming?"

I thought about old Pieter's grandson, going under, and how I hadn't calculated a single thing.

"Oh, you're going to be a problem," I told her. "You understand that. You are going to be a problem for the entire length of this journey."

"Probably," she agreed.

Corren sighed the sigh of a man whose bad decision had already been made for him, somewhere around the moment he'd first laid eyes on her. "Tamsin Vael," he said grimly, gathering the reins, "does not trade in Cinderhollow. But a wool merchant might detour through looking for salvage. Cheap goods, after a fire."

"Now that," I said, climbing up beside him, "is the most dishonest honest thing you've ever said. I'm almost proud."

The smoke thickened as we rolled down toward the town, and somewhere in it — faint, threading through the ash-smell like a note through noise — I swear I heard something that might have been singing.

Low. Small. Keeping time with the embers.

Lena heard it too. I saw her head come up like a deer's.

"Hold on," she said, and we drove into the ashes.
