## 7. The Ash Choir

Cinderhollow smelled like a hearth that had eaten its own family.

That was the first thought I had standing at the edge of it, and I hated myself for the poetry of it, because poetry is what you do when you can't do anything else. The town had burned three weeks past, Corren said — maybe four. The rain since then had soaked the ash into a grey paste that clung to our boots and turned every step into a small accusation. Here is where they lived. Here is where they stopped.

"Nobody rebuilds this fast or this slow," Corren said behind me. "They just leave."

He had that way of saying true things like they were directions to somewhere else. He did it a lot. I'd known him all of nine days and he'd already taught me how to sleep in a tree, how to spot crown criers by their shoes, and how to hate my own mother's name a little less, which was somehow the hardest lesson of the three.

"Why are we here?" Maren asked. She asked that about everything now — why the long road, why the cold camp, why the gods, why us. It wasn't really a question. It was a way of keeping her hands busy while her heart sulked. "There's nothing in a burned town but ghosts and looters."

"Looters," Corren said. "And sometimes things looters leave."

I didn't ask what he meant. I was learning not to ask. In the Hollow, questions were safe because answers were boring — when will the roof need mending, whose turn is it for the well. Out here every answer cost something. I'd paid for two already: *your mother was an oath-bearer* and *the covenant is dying and you are the cure*. I was running low on coin.

We walked into Cinderhollow single file, down a lane where doorways stood open like mouths mid-scream. A church had half-collapsed, its bell still hanging, blackened, silent. Somebody had chalked a symbol on the well-head — a circle crossed by three lines. The Veiled Court's mark, Corren said, though he'd only heard rumors himself. Hidden folk, leaving signs for each other the way travelers scratch arrows into bark.

The sign said something else too, if you read it right. *Gifted lived here.* Which meant the fire hadn't been an accident, and which meant the hunters had come through with their lists and their grey iron and their tidy little word *seize*, which everyone understood meant whatever made it easiest on the men doing the seizing.

"I don't like this," Maren said, quieter now. Her gift sat close under her skin; I could feel it the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the ears. When she was frightened, the puddles trembled. "Towns like this, after towns like this — there's usually someone left. Someone who didn't run fast enough. Or someone who ran and couldn't stop."

"Then we'll be quick," Corren said.

We were not quick.

* * *

We found him in the granary, or what the granary had become, which was a cave of charred beams with one corner swept clean as a kitchen floor. He was small. That's the thing nobody warns you about children in ruins — they're always smaller than the story you'd built around them. Maybe seven years old, maybe eight, sitting cross-legged on a blanket with his back straight like he'd been told good posture would save his life, and maybe someone had.

In front of him, laid out with terrible care, were eleven objects. A tin cup. A wooden horse missing a leg. Three buttons. A hair ribbon. And so on. Eleven small dead things, arranged in a row.

He was singing.

Not loudly. Not a child's nonsense song either. This was low and steady and old-sounding, a tune that went up where you expected it to go down, and as he sang, the air around him shimmered faintly, like heat off a summer road, except it was autumn and there was no sun.

The embers. That's what he was singing to. There was a scatter of charcoal near the wall that should have been stone cold, and around that boy it glowed. Gently. Like coals being sung a lullaby instead of fanned.

Maren grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. Corren had gone very still, the way he did when his broken oath-mark itched, which I was beginning to understand was often.

"That's not fire-raising," he murmured. "That's fire-*tending*. Old blood. Deep blood." He swallowed. "Sleeper's line. I thought it was gone."

The boy stopped singing. He'd seen us. Children see you before you decide you've been seen. He looked at the three of us — the big scarred man, the river-woman with her jaw set, the girl with storm in her wrists who was trying very hard to look harmless — and he did the bravest thing I've ever watched anyone do.

He put his body between us and the embers.

"You can't have them," he said. His voice cracked in the middle, which made it worse, which made it braver. "They're sleeping. If you wake them up wrong they'll hurt somebody again."

"Wake them—" Maren started.

"The fire." He lifted his chin. "It's sleeping. I sang it to sleep. Mama said if I sang it right it would stay asleep until the rain came, but the rain came and it didn't go all the way out, so I keep singing." He looked down at his eleven treasures, then back up, and his chin wobbled once before it set again. "Are you from the list? The grey-iron men have a list."

"No," I said, before I knew I was going to say anything at all. My voice came out strange and soft. "No, we're not from the list."

"They took everybody who was on it." He recited it like a lesson, flat, the way children recite terrible things when feeling them whole would break them. "The miller's wife. The tanner. Old Sef who talked to crows. My mama. They made her stand in the square and they asked her questions and she said her gift was gone, it dried up, and they wrote something down anyway, and then—" He stopped. He looked at the embers. "Then the fire started. They said she started it. She didn't. She was trying to sing it asleep too, but she never sang as good as me."

The world went very narrow and very bright, the way it does right before a storm breaks. I felt my hands start their old trembling, the tremor I'd spent my whole life hiding in the Hollow, mending roofs, passing cups of water, being ordinary, being safe, being *nobody* — and for the first time in my life I didn't want to hide it. I wanted it. I wanted lightning with teeth.

Corren's hand landed on my shoulder. Heavy. Deliberate. An anchor thrown exactly where an anchor was needed.

"Breathe," he said quietly. "Sky-child. Breathe."

I breathed. The boy watched me do it with sharp, wet eyes.

"What's your name?" I asked him.

A pause. The kind of pause that means the name has been sitting alone in a burned building for weeks, unsaid, and saying it aloud feels like opening a door to the dark.

"Tam," he said.

"Tam," I repeated, so the name would be real again, would exist outside that room, would have witnesses. "My name is Lena. This is Corren. This is Maren. We're not going to wake your fire, Tam. But we're not leaving you to sleep next to it either."

He considered that with the grave thoroughness of a magistrate. Then he gathered up his eleven treasures, wrapped them in the blanket corner by corner — the cup first, then the horse, then the buttons, an order I suspected mattered enormously — and stood up.

"Mama said if anyone ever came who wasn't from the list," he said, "I should ask them one question first." His voice shook now, finally, all the way through. "She said to ask if they know any songs."

* * *

He came with us. Of course he came with us. Leaving him was a thought I tried on for exactly four seconds, the way you try on a coat that fits terribly and costs too much, and then I threw it away forever.

But taking him was its own catastrophe, and we all knew it, and we rode out of Cinderhollow's ash-paste streets with a fire-tending child on Corren's saddle bow and the knowledge between us like a stone none of us would pick up. Hunters tracked gifted blood. What did a child who sang to embers track like, on their instruments, in their ledgers? A beacon. A bonfire of a beacon. Every mile we traveled with Tam was a mile we traveled lit up like a festival.

So we moved at night, and we camped cold, and Tam sang the campfire down to almost-nothing every morning before we doused it, and I lay awake under the stars listening to that impossible lullaby and thinking about my mother, whom I had spent twenty years remembering as a smell and a letter and a shape of grief, and who was turning out, piece by piece, to be a woman who had hidden her daughter in a valley where magic couldn't reach — and who had taught her son, apparently, somewhere else, some other life, a song to keep fire gentle.

Two children. Two hiding places. One dying covenant.

"She sent him away too," I said to Corren on the third night. We were on watch together. Tam slept against Maren's side, and Maren pretended to be asleep and was not, because her hand rested on the boy's hair and stayed there. "Different places. Different people. She scattered us like seed."

"That's what oath-bearers did," Corren said. "When the rot started. You don't keep the last of something precious all in one basket. You spread it out where the fire can't find it all at once." He rubbed the dead mark on his forearm absently, the habit of a man scratching a wound that had stopped hurting years ago and started meaning more. "Your mother wasn't hiding from the covenant, Lena. She was preserving it."

"Well," I said, "she should have written better instructions."

That got a huff out of him that might have been a laugh. "Yes. Well. Mothers are notorious for dying without appendices."

And then, because the night was quiet and the boy was breathing slow and I had been carrying the question since the sky-gate sliver, since Veyl's dimming light, since my own hands had shaken over a burned town — I asked.

"Teach me something. From the liturgy. You said you'd teach me when I was ready. When is ready?"

Corren was quiet a long time. Long enough for the fire's ghost to settle, for an owl to make its one complaint somewhere upslope.

"Now," he said finally. "Apparently now. Because you're angry, and anger's the door most of us come in through, and I'd rather you learn the words before you walk through it barefoot."

He sat up straighter, and something changed in him — the disgraced drunkard-of-an-oath-keeper posture fell away, and underneath was the man he'd been trained to be, decades ago, in halls I'd never seen. He spoke the way priests must speak when the god is listening.

"The liturgy isn't magic," he said. "Understand that first. The words don't compel anything. They're a courtesy. A knocking. You're asking the storm to remember that it once had a name for you, and you a name for it. Say the words wrong and nothing happens, which is embarrassing. Say them right and—" He shrugged. "Say them right, and something old turns over in its sleep and looks at you."

"Comforting."

"Nothing worth doing is." He held up his hand, palm out, the way you'd calm a horse. "First words. Only these, tonight. Repeat after me, and mean nothing yet. Just carry them."

And he said:

*"Wind that wore my grandmother's name,*

*rain that fell before the walls,*

*I am the daughter of the promise,*

*small and mortal, and I call."*

Four lines. Childish-simple, like Tam's lullaby. I repeated them, and they tasted like nothing, like a foreign coin in my mouth.

"Again," Corren said. "Slower. Don't perform them. Just say them like they're true even though you don't believe it yet."

I said them again. Wind that wore my grandmother's name. Rain that fell before the walls.

On the third repetition, the world tilted.

There's no other way to describe it. Nothing dramatic happened — no thunderclap, no wind — and that was precisely what made it terrifying. Instead the night simply *acknowledged* me. Every blade of grass within a hundred yards leaned a fraction toward where I sat, as if the whole hillside had turned its head. The air pressure shifted, warm then cool then warm, like vast lungs drawing breath far above the clouds. And inside my chest, in the place where the Hollow had always pressed my gift down flat like a leaf in a book, something unfolded. Stretched. Opened one eye.

*Oh,* said my own blood, in a voice that was mine and older than mine. *There you are.*

The trembling started in my hands and I let it, let it climb my arms and settle into my ribs, and I laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound — because it didn't hurt. It had never hurt. All those years gripping cups in the Hollow, hiding tremors, ashamed of a weakness that was never weakness at all. It was homesickness. It had been homesick for me.

"It answered," I whispered. "Outside the valley. Corren, it *answered*."

"I know." He was staring at the grass. At the sky. At me, with an expression I couldn't parse — awe and grief fighting for the same chair. "Lena. I've said those words to a hundred hollow-eyed heirs across ten years and gotten silence every time. The liturgy's been a dead letter since your mother died." He passed a hand over his face. "It's been waiting for *you*."

Behind us, a small voice said, sleepily:

"That was pretty. Sing it again?"

Tam had propped himself up on one elbow, eyes half-lidded, hair a disaster, watching us with the unembarrassed appetite of a child who has decided these strangers belong to him now. Beside him, Maren had given up pretending to sleep entirely.

"Maybe tomorrow," I said.

"Promise?"

"I promise."

He settled back down, and as he drifted off he began, barely audible, his own song — the fire lullaby, sung now to nothing, to sleeping coals a hundred miles behind us, to a burned town, to a mother. Up where you expected it to go down. Old blood, deep blood, singing in the dark because singing was how he kept the world gentle.

And I sat there under the leaning grass with new words in my mouth and an old thing awake in my chest, and I understood that tenderness and purpose are not different feelings. They're the same feeling, pointed. You love something small and breakable in a world full of grey iron and lists, and love stops being a softness and becomes a direction.

Vess had her list. The crown had its iron.

We had a child who sang fire to sleep, a ferry-woman who hadn't forgiven herself yet, an oath-keeper teaching a dead liturgy back to life line by line —

— and now, apparently, we had the storm itself, turning over in its sleep, looking at me, and whispering, in my mother's voice, in *my* voice, in a voice older than both:

*Small and mortal. And calling.*

Three days later, on the ridge above the river-road, Tam stopped mid-step and grabbed my hand and said, "Lena. There's men coming. Grey men. Lots of them. And they're following *us*."
