## 8. Vess

The prisoner sang.

That was the first thing I noticed about him, before the shackles, before the blood on his split lip, before the way he sat in my interrogation chair as though it were a pew and I were a priest he'd decided not to believe in. He sang. Some river-song from Brassen, low and tuneless, about a girl who drowns her sorrows and then her husband. He had been singing when my soldiers dragged him in, and he was singing now, and I let him finish the verse before I spoke.

"You're wasting your voice," I told him. "No one upriver will hear you."

"Then I'll waste it louder." He grinned at me through the ruin of his mouth. His name was Doss—Doss Ferrow, ferryman's apprentice, twenty-two years old, and according to the papers we'd pulled off him, carrier of a water-mark on his left wrist that glowed faintly when it rained. The mark was dark now. Iron does that. We had learned early in the hunt that cold iron pressed to an oath-sign starves it quiet, and so we kept our prisoners cuffed in it, and they came to us diminished, and most of them broke like dry kindling.

This one would not break like kindling. I could see that already, and I confess it pleased me. Kindling bores me.

"Do you know who I am?" I asked.

"Inquisitor Vess." He said it the way men say the names of storms. "The grey iron herself. They tell stories about you downriver. None of them flattering."

"Good. Flattery implies you want something from me."

"And you don't want anything from me?"

I set the file down on the table between us—his file, thin, because gifted folk who live honest lives make thin files—and I watched him read the gesture correctly, which told me he was smarter than his mouth pretended.

"Everyone wants something from everyone," I said. "The difference is that I admit it."

He laughed. It cost him; I saw the wince behind it where my sergeant had worked him over on the road. "All right, Inquisitor. Ask your questions. I've been rehearsing my lies since Brassen."

* * *

Let me tell you what the stories get wrong, since you are clearly wondering.

They say I hate the gifted. That is true, but hatred is such a blunt word, and I have never owned a blunt tool in my life except my sister's old kitchen mallet, which I keep for sentimental reasons and which has never once been used on a skull, whatever the rumors claim.

What I actually feel is closer to arithmetic. Every gift is a debt someone else pays. Every wonder has a bill attached, and the crown has spent four hundred years pretending the bill would never come due. I am not a hater. I am a collector of accounts.

My name before Vess was Vess Alenne Carrow, and I grew up in a town called Millbrook, which no longer appears on maps because there is nothing left of it worth mapping. You will find it in the histories, if the histories bother: the Night of the Rising Water, they call it, when a covenant-surge went through the river-oath lines and a handful of water-gifted townsfolk—good people, beloved people, people who taught choir and mended nets—found themselves holding more power than their oaths could govern. The river rose eleven feet in a night. Two hundred and six dead. My parents among them, drowned in their own cellar, and my sister Wren—

Wren was fourteen. She was on the roof, waving a lantern, screaming for help, and a water-bearer named Aldous Crane stood in the flood below her and could have lifted her down with a gesture. A gesture. Instead he looked up at her, and later—at the inquiry—he swore under oath that he simply could not spare the strength, that the surge had taken everything he had, that he was sorry, so sorry, and every gifted man in that courtroom nodded along as though *sorry* were a currency anyone should accept.

Wren fell. Or she jumped. At fourteen you do not always know the difference, and neither did she, and I have had nineteen years to decide which answer hurts less, and I still haven't found it.

So no. I do not hate them. I *count* them. And the count always comes out the same: two hundred and six, plus one girl on a roof, minus zero ever held accountable, until the crown finally issued the proclamation and put me at the head of the grey iron and said, *Vess, go collect.*

People ask why I hunt the heirs specifically, the oath-bearers' blood, when the mob that killed my sister was mostly ordinary gifted folk swept up in a surge. Because the mob is the symptom, and the oaths are the disease. The covenant is a pipe running between worlds, and every generation the pipe corrodes a little more, and the pressure builds behind the weak places, and then some ferryman's apprentice floods a dock, or some child burns a village in his sleep, or some storm-blooded girl in a hidden valley makes the sky itself into a weapon. Cut the pipe. End magic at its source. That is not vengeance. That is plumbing.

I am aware this makes me sound mad. Most of the truly necessary things do.

* * *

"Here is how this goes," I told Doss Ferrow, somewhere past the second hour, after the pleasant part of the conversation had exhausted itself. "You know where they're going. The Amberdale girl, the disgraced keeper, the ferrywoman of Brassen—I know you carried messages for Maren Hale's family before she ran. Somewhere in your head is a road."

"There's a lot of roads in my head," he said. "Most of them end badly."

"They all do, eventually. Mine included." I turned my chair to face him fully, and I took off my gloves—one of the small theatrics I permit myself, because fear is a lever and I am not too proud to pull it. My hands beneath the gloves are unremarkable. That is rather the point of them. "But only one of them ends at the Stormseat, and I want it."

He studied me for a long moment. Under the bravado he was doing sums of his own; I could nearly hear the abacus clicking. "They say you burned the Ashford cell," he said. "Eleven people. Some of them kids."

"I burned a barn that eleven people had fortified, two of whom were children being used as sentries by adults who should have known better. The children died of adult decisions, not of fire." I said it flatly, because I have said it a hundred times and it has never once stopped being true, and because the alternative—letting it wound me—is how interrogators lose. "Do you think that story frightens me into gentleness? Or are you buying time while you decide whether to sell?"

"Both," he admitted, and I respected the honesty enough to almost smile.

"Good. Then let's talk price."

"I'm not going to give them up."

"No. But you'll give me the road."

He blinked. For the first time all evening, the song went out of him entirely. "Those aren't different things."

"Everything is different things," I said. "Consider. If you give me the Amberdale girl, you're a traitor to her, and to the ferrywoman who fed you, and you'll carry that like a stone in your chest for the rest of your life, however long I allow it to be. But if you give me a *road*—a direction, a pass, a waystation—you're giving me geography. Geography isn't loyalty. Maps belong to everyone." I leaned forward. "And here is what geography buys you, Doss Ferrow: it buys you a cell instead of a rope. It buys you a trial with a magistrate who owes me a favor. It buys you spring. Maybe even autumn, if the war goes slowly, and I intend for it to go slowly."

"That's it? A cell?"

"A warm one."

He laughed again, but there was no music in it now, just exhaustion wearing a costume. "You know what's funny, Inquisitor? You're not what I expected. The stories have you spitting fire."

"The stories have everyone wrong. That's half of why the realms are coming apart—everyone believes the stories." I let the silence stretch, because silence is the other lever. "The road, Doss."

He looked at his shackled wrists for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone quiet and very young. "If I give you the Oathspine road. The high pass through the remembering-stones. Will you promise me—" He stopped. Started over. "There's a boy traveling with them. Small, dark hair, sings when he's scared. Tam. He can't be more than eight. Whatever you take from them, Inquisitor—the girl, the keeper, all of it—leave the boy out of it. Promise me that, and you can have the whole map."

Ah.

There it is. There is always an *it*, with these people. A sister on a roof, a boy who sings. Everyone's treachery is really just love wearing a mask it stole from fear, and I recognized the mask because I wear its twin.

"You have my word," I said. "The child is not my quarry. Children are not my quarry. They become my quarry when adults hand them knives and call it inheritance, and I will do everything in my power to make sure this boy never reaches that day." Every word true. I have built a career on the architecture of true sentences arranged to bear loads they were never meant to carry.

He gave me the road.

The Oathspine pass, the high route through the mountains where the covenant-stones still hum, the waystation at Sky Gate ford, and—this was the part that made my hands go cold inside my warm gloves—a timetable. They meant to reach the Stormseat before the first snows. Which meant I had perhaps three weeks to move a full company through country that hated us, ahead of a fugitive party that knew the land and had a storm-caller growing stronger with every league.

"Sergeant Ilva will draw up the march order," I said, rising. "You'll be moved to Greywater gaol tonight. Warm cell. Decent bread. Try not to sing; the walls carry, and the other prisoners are snitches."

"Inquisitor." He said it as I reached the door, and I turned. He looked smaller without the song, younger, the way defeated things do. "Your sister. Wren. The stories mention her too. Downriver, they say you do all this for her."

I stood very still. Somewhere in the building, a door slammed; the sound arrived late, like thunder after lightning.

"The stories get her wrong too," I said. "She wasn't brave, or tragic, or any of the words they use. She was fourteen, and she was cold, and she wanted someone to help her down." I opened the door. "Sleep well, Doss. One of us should."

* * *

Ilva was waiting in the map room, as she is always waiting in the map room, standing at parade rest beside the great table with her lantern jaw and her unshakeable calm, the finest soldier I have ever commanded and the only person alive whose company does not tire me.

"Well?" she asked.

"He gave us the Oathspine road. High pass, Sky Gate ford, Stormseat approach before first snows." I spread the survey charts across the table and weighted the corners. "We move within the day. Double-time through the lowlands, then slow once the ground starts climbing—they'll hear us coming in those valleys otherwise."

Ilva bent over the maps. She is the only officer I have whose silences mean concentration rather than insubordination, and I have come to rely on both. After a moment she straightened.

"You gave him terms," she said. Not a question. She had heard it through the door, or guessed it, or simply knows me, which amounts to the same thing.

"A gaol instead of a rope."

"For a road."

"For a road," I agreed. "Cheap, Sergeant. Cheaper than the two hundred and six."

Her eyes flickered—that micro-movement, there and gone, that she probably believes I never notice. Ilva thinks I do not see her flinch at certain of my sentences. She thinks her doubts are well-hidden. She is wrong, and I let her be wrong, because an officer with doubts who believes they are hidden is infinitely more useful than an officer whose doubts have been dragged into the light and forced to choose sides. Doubts dragged into light grow teeth. Doubts left in shadow stay soft.

Besides. I remember what it was to doubt in secret. I did it for years after Millbrook—sat through the inquiries, listened to the gifted witnesses swear their sorrow, felt the little traitor voice in my own chest whisper *but they didn't mean it, Vess, they didn't mean any of it.* Meaning is not the question. Meaning has never been the question. The river did not care whether Aldous Crane meant it. Wren did not fall gently enough for meaning to matter.

"Provisioning for a mountain march in autumn," Ilva said, already writing. "Cold-weather kit, extra iron stores, farrier. Three weeks is tight."

"Tight is generous. Make it two and a half."

"Two and a half." She wrote it without argument, which is why I keep her. Then, carefully, the way soldiers handle things they suspect might explode: "The reports from the east. The sky over the Oathspine. The color of it."

I knew what she was asking. Everyone was asking. For a month now the eastern horizon had worn a bruise that no weather explained—a deep, wrong violet, like a wound seen through skin—and the priests were calling it omens and the scholars were calling it refraction and the gifted, when we caught them, called it *Veyl dying*, and crossed themselves with gestures that predated the church.

"It means the clock is shorter than we thought," I said. "Whatever the covenant is doing as it fails, it's failing faster. Which means either we cut the pipe before it bursts"—I tapped the map, the thin ink line of the Oathspine road—"or we drown in whatever comes out of it. Again."

"And if the girl reaches the Stormseat first? Reforges it, like her mother meant to?"

"Then we spend another four hundred years paying interest on a loan we never agreed to, and someday—maybe not my lifetime, maybe yours, maybe some child's in a town not yet founded—the surge comes again, and the water rises, and there is another roof and another lantern and another girl screaming for a hand that doesn't arrive." I rolled the charts closed and met Ilva's eyes. "I have seen how that story ends, Sergeant. I decline to watch the sequel."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, far to the east, the bruised sky went on quietly dying, patient as rot.

"As you order, Inquisitor," she said at last.

"One more thing. The boy—Tam, the fire-child traveling with them. If the action goes against us, if there's a rout, a melee—he is to be taken alive and unharmed. Any soldier who harms that child answers to me personally. Put it in the orders. Read them aloud to the company."

Something moved behind Ilva's eyes—not the flinch this time, something else, something almost like surprise. "Mercy, Inquisitor?"

"Policy," I said. "Children are not my quarry. They only become my quarry when someone teaches them to inherit a war." I pulled on my gloves. "Someone taught the Amberdale girl. Someone taught all of them. That teaching is what I'm hunting, Sergeant. Everything else is just weather."

I left her with the maps and went to stand at the east window, where the violet stain was spreading across the dark like ink dropped in water, beautiful the way all catastrophes are beautiful from a distance.

Three weeks. Perhaps fewer.

Hold on, Wren, I thought, to the sky, to no one, to the girl on the roof who would be thirty-three now if the world had been governed by better plumbers. Hold on. I'm coming to fix it.
