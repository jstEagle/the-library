## 9. The Mountain That Remembers

The Oathspine did not welcome us. It tolerated us, the way an old man tolerates children running through his house — aware of every footstep, mildly disapproving, and entirely unwilling to be moved.

We had climbed for two days out of the green country below, and with every mile the world grew quieter in a way that wasn't quiet at all. The trees thinned. The birds stopped. And underneath everything — under the wind, under the crunch of our boots on scree — there was a hum. Low and patient, like a hymn someone had started singing a thousand years ago and simply never finished.

Maren heard it first. She stopped dead on the trail, one hand splayed against a boulder, and said, "It's warm."

"It's rock," I said. "Rock is famously indifferent to temperature. It's part of its charm."

"It's *warm*, Corren." She pulled her hand back like she'd touched a sleeping animal. "And it's — saying something. Not words. More like..." She searched for it, frowning at her own fingers. "Like when you stand outside a door and there's people talking behind it, and you can't make out what they're saying but you know they're talking about you."

Tam, perched on my shoulders where he'd ridden since noon because his legs were five years old and made of stubbornness rather than stamina, pressed both palms flat against the stone beside my head.

"They're singing," he said. Then, with the terrible certainty of children: "They know Lena's song."

I felt Lena go still behind me. I didn't turn around. There are moments you can feel through the back of your neck like weather coming, and hers was one of them — that particular silence she made when the past reached up and grabbed her ankle.

"Keep walking," I said, which was cowardice dressed as leadership, and we kept walking.

* * *

We made camp that night in the mouth of a ruined waystation — half shelter, half tomb, the old oath-road architecture that dotted the Spine like knuckles on a hand. The builders of the covenant had raised these stations so their bearers could travel between realms without dying of exposure or worse, and the stonework had outlasted the covenant itself by centuries, which tells you something about how much better stone keeps promises than people do.

I built the fire because nobody else would, and because building fires is the only useful skill I've ever had that doesn't require explaining myself. Maren rationed the hard bread with the grim precision of a woman who'd grown up managing a ferry schedule and had never once forgiven the river for being late. Tam fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew, which is a talent I envy more than any gift the covenant ever handed down.

Lena sat apart, on the low wall where the roof used to be, looking east toward the high pass. Toward the Stormseat road. The storm-sign in her blood had been answering me for days now — little flickers, static in the air between us when I taught her the liturgy's first words — and out here, in the mountains, those flickers came easier. The stones remembered what she was before she'd learned to say it herself.

I should have told her then. I'd been telling myself I would tell her since Brassen, since Cinderhollow, since every night around the fire where she asked me careful questions about her mother and I answered them carefully and lied by omission with every careful word. An oath-keeper's habit. We are trained to hold things, not to give them away. Nobody trains you for the day holding becomes the same as stealing.

She came down from the wall eventually and sat across the fire from me, knees drawn up, and said, "You knew her."

Not a question. That was the worst of it.

"Lena—"

"You knew my mother." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which with Lena is far more dangerous. "You knew the liturgy too well to have learned it from a book. You knew the sign-gestures before I showed you the ones from her letter fragments. You looked at the mountain today like you'd been here before." She leaned forward. Firelight did its usual dishonest work on her face, making her look older and harder than twenty-two winters. "So say it. Say whatever it is you've been not-saying since the valley."

Maren had gone very still by the packs. Even asleep, some animal sense made Tam shift and frown.

I am, by trade and temperament, a man who has survived many bad nights by knowing exactly how much truth a moment can carry. That night I looked into the fire and understood that this moment could carry all of it, or nothing, and that choosing nothing would cost me the only people who had let me walk beside them in eleven years.

"I was sworn to her," I said. "Your mother. Sworn as shield and keeper, covenant-bound, mark to mark." I turned my wrist so the firelight caught the faded brand there — the broken circle, the oath-mark that had gone dark and cold the year Lena was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel. "I was the last of the nine keepers still living when the hunt closed in. The others were dead or scattered or had sold their oaths for pardons. Your mother sent for me. I rode."

"And?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the mountain hummed its unfinished hymn.

"And I was three days late," I said. "Three days. A flooded ford, a bribed ferryman who took my coin and reported me instead, and then a fever that put me in a ditch for two days while hunters walked past close enough that I could hear them laughing. When I finally reached the meeting place, she was gone. Taken. And the child she'd hidden — you — was beyond even my finding, sealed in a valley I couldn't enter because magic dies at the tree line, and I was nothing *but* magic by then. My mark went dark that same week. The oath broke. Or I broke it. In the end it hardly matters which."

Silence. The kind with weight and edges.

Lena stood up slowly, and I watched eleven days of trust reorganize themselves into something with a blade's profile.

"Three days," she said.

"Yes."

"She called for help and you—" Her breath caught, and for a moment I saw not the woman who'd faced down Inquisitor Vess's iron in the valley, but a girl who had spent her whole childhood wondering why her mother never came back, and had built a life on the assumption that no answer would ever come, and had just been handed one, and it was the worst one. "You were *late*. She died waiting for you and then you found me and you didn't — you watched me grieve her. You listened to me ask about her. You *taught me her liturgy* and let me think you were learning it alongside me."

"Yes," I said again, because anything else would have been decoration, and she deserved the bare ugly frame of it.

"Do you know what I thought?" Her voice cracked finally, and I almost preferred the anger. "All those nights by the fire, I thought — here's a man who lost his oath and lost his god and still gets up every morning and helps a stranger he owes nothing to. I thought that was decency. I thought that was *kindness*." She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard since Cinderhollow burned. "You were paying a debt. That's all I've been. Eleven days of installments."

"Lena, that isn't—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and the air around it prickled, static crawling over my arms, the storm-blood rising with her temper the way it never had before the mountains woke it. "Don't tell me what it is. Don't follow me."

And she walked out into the dark, up the trail toward the pass, alone, in country where the stones sang and the crown's criers had offered forty crowns for her head, and I sat by the fire like the failure I apparently always had been, and Maren looked at me with an expression I can only describe as judicial.

"Well," she said. "That went well."

"Maren—"

"No. You had your chance to talk." She banked the fire with unnecessary force. "Eleven days I watched you two do this dance. Every time she asked about her mother, you'd get that look — like a man checking his pockets to see if the lie was still there. I figured it was grief. Turns out it was bookkeeping." She pulled her blanket around her shoulders. "Go after her."

"She told me not to."

"She also told me she hated ferries and then spent four hours helping me patch the hull at Brassen. Go *after her*."

But I didn't move, because pride is just fear wearing armor, and mine was very well fitted. "She needs space."

"She needs to scream at somebody, and you're the somebody who earned it." Maren lay down and turned her back to me. "If she walks off a cliff in the dark because you were busy feeling sorry for yourself, that's not a debt anymore, Corren. That's just you."

I want it recorded, somewhere in whatever ledger the mountains keep, that I lasted nearly an hour before shame beat prudence. It felt like discipline at the time. It looks like what it was from here.

* * *

I found her a mile up the switchbacks, standing on a shelf of bare granite with her face tipped toward a sky full of stars and no moon, and the mountain humming around her like a struck bell that wouldn't stop.

She heard me coming — of course she did; the stones probably announced me personally — and didn't turn.

"I said don't follow me."

"I'm famously bad at following instructions," I said. "Ask anyone whose instructions I've followed."

Nothing. Wind moved through the pass and carried the hum with it, and I realized, standing there, that the sound had changed since sundown. Deeper. Faster. Like the hymn behind the door had noticed us arguing and leaned closer to listen.

"The stones remember her," Lena said quietly. She still hadn't turned around. "Don't they. This road. She walked it."

"She did. Nine times that I know of, and I walked it with her twice." I came to stand a few paces behind her — close enough to catch her if the ground betrayed either of us, far enough to respect the lightning distance. "She liked the third switchback. Said the view reminded her that the world was bigger than the people trying to end it. She used to put her hand on the stone there and laugh, and I never once asked her what the stones said to her, because I assumed I'd have time later." I exhaled. "I assumed a lot of things about later, Lena. It's the family disease of oath-keepers. Later, later, later — until later arrives early and you're standing in an empty safehouse reading a letter addressed to a daughter you'll never find."

"Stop."

"I won't. You deserve the whole ugly inventory. Here it is: I failed her. I have spent eleven years failing her, every morning, because staying alive and doing penance felt like work and dying felt like quitting, and I've never known which one she'd have chosen for me. And then I found you — by accident, by the grace of whatever gods the covenant left behind — and I told myself the story you already know. Debt. Installments. It was easier than the truth, which is that watching you was the closest thing to seeing her I would ever get, and I was selfish with it. I hoarded you. I hoarded her liturgy in your mouth. And every night you thanked me for teaching you, and every night I let you thank me, and that is the sin I'll actually burn for. Not the lateness. The *letting you thank me*."

The hum swelled. On the granite under our feet, faint lines of light woke along seams I'd taken for cracks — old covenant-script, the road remembering its purpose — and Lena finally turned around, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit from beneath by the stone-light like she was standing on the surface of her own inheritance.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because tomorrow we climb to the Stormseat," I said, "and the reforging will ask each of us what we're carrying, and I will not carry a lie into that place. And because—" My voice did something humiliating. I let it. "Because forgiveness isn't owed to me, Lena. But you deserve to know exactly what you'd be forgiving. All of it. The choice is yours, and it was always supposed to be yours. That's why she hid you in the Hollow. So that no one — not the crown, not the covenant, and not a disgraced keeper with good intentions — could take the choice from you."

For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the mountain held its breath, and I thought: *this is where she leaves me.* And I found, examining the thought honestly, that I couldn't blame her, and that some stubborn splinter of me would go on following her anyway, at a respectful distance, until one of us fell off the world. That's what an oath is, in the end. Not a mark on the arm. Just the decision to keep showing up after the mark goes dark.

Then Tam screamed.

* * *

We ran.

His nightmare-fire had flared once before — outside Cinderhollow, a candleflame of orange crawling off his sleeping hands while he dreamed of the night the hunters came, and Maren had smothered it with a wet blanket and we'd agreed, silently, never to speak of how close it had come. This was not a candleflame. This was the whole campsite lit orange, flames standing up off the boy's small body like grass in wind, and the blanket Maren threw over him caught and burned, and she had to roll clear with her sleeves smoking.

"He won't wake!" she shouted. "Corren — I can't — water's a mile down the—"

"Water won't fix this. It's not real fire. It's memory burning its way out." I skidded into the camp on my knees, reaching for him, and the flames licked my palms and didn't burn — they *grieved*, they poured grief into me like a cup overflowing, images of smoke and grey iron and a door breaking, and I recoiled. "Lena! His dream is a fire and fire answers storm — rain puts it out, but it has to be *her* rain, it has to mean it!"

Lena dropped beside him. Her hands hovered over the flames, shaking. "I don't know how. I've called static. I've called sparks. I've never—"

"You don't call it!" I grabbed her wrists, and the storm between our skins jumped and crackled, and the liturgy rose in me unbidden — the first words, the ones I'd been feeding her by the fire for weeks, except now I understood why her mother had taught them to me in exactly this order, on exactly this road, on the third switchback with the wide view. "*What the sky forgets, the blood remembers. What the blood remembers, the storm returns.*" My mark, dark eleven years, ached like a scar in a change of weather. "Say it with me. Mean it. Mean it for *him*."

"*What the sky forgets,*" she said, and her voice broke and steadied, "*the blood remembers.*"

Above us, impossibly, through a starry sky with no cloud within fifty miles, thunder rolled.

Tam's fire bent. The flames all leaned toward Lena like grass before wind, like children turning toward a voice, and the second line came out of her not in words anymore but in pressure — the air dropping, my ears popping, the hair on my arms lifting — and then the rain came. Warm. Gentle. Falling upward slightly, falling *sideways*, falling in defiance of every law I'd ever pretended to respect, a small soft rain contained to six feet of mountaintop, raining only on a burning child.

The flames sighed out. Tam's breathing eased. The nightmare loosened its grip — I could see it let go of him the way a fist lets go — and he murmured something, turned over, and slept the deep untroubled sleep of a boy whose fire had finally met something gentler than it was.

The rain stopped. The stars came back out. The mountain's hum settled into something that sounded, to my ear, suspiciously like satisfaction.

Maren stared at Lena. Lena stared at her own hands. Tam snored.

"Well," Maren said at last, wringing rainwater out of her sleeves. "That's going to make hiding from the crown significantly harder, isn't it."

"Significantly," I agreed.

Lena looked up at me across the sleeping boy, her face still wet — from rain, from crying, from both, the distinction having stopped mattering somewhere in the last minute. Her expression was not forgiveness. I want to be honest about that, because this story deserves honesty: what was in her face was exhaustion, and fury banked rather than extinguished, and something new — the look of a woman who has just discovered that the man she wants to hate is the only other person alive who knows how to do what she just did.

"I'm not done being angry at you," she said.

"I'd be disappointed if you were."

"But we leave at dawn." She gathered Tam into her arms, and he curled into her without waking, and she stood with the weight of him like it was the easiest thing she'd carried all journey. "And Corren? Next time you decide to confess something — try doing it before I have to drag it out of you by the collar."

"Noted," I said. "I'll pencil it in for later."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was, given the circumstances, the most I'd earned in eleven years.

But as we banked the fire and settled the watches, I saw her glance east toward the high pass, where the sky had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to flicker. Not lightning. Not stars. A dimming, like a lamp running out of oil, in a place where no lamp should be.

The mountain hummed its unfinished hymn, and for the first time since we'd entered the Oathspine, I thought I could hear what the stones were actually singing.

They were counting down.
