## 10. The Sky Gate

The waystation had been a grand thing once, if you squinted sideways and forgave it a great deal.

It sat on the shoulder of the Oathspine like an old dog that had lain down one winter and simply decided not to get up again. Broken arches. A courtyard gone to moss and thistle. A tower with its top sheared off clean, as though some enormous hand had found it wanting and corrected it. Corren said it had been built by the oath-bearers themselves, back when the roads between realms were busy and the world hadn't yet learned to be afraid of its own sky.

I said it looked like a place where roofs went to die.

"Careful," he said, hauling his pack off one shoulder as we picked our way through the gate arch. "That's practically a professional opinion. You mended two roofs in your life."

"Three," I said. "And one of them stayed mended."

Maren snorted from behind us. Tam didn't say anything at all — he'd been quiet since the mountain started humming, which was saying something, because Tam could usually find words even for things that had no business having them, like the taste of thunder or the exact color of a nightmare.

We made camp in what had been the courtyard, under the eye of the broken tower. Ilva built a fire with the economy of someone who'd been doing fieldwork her whole life — three stones, one flint, no wasted motion — while Maren saw to the horses and I pretended not to watch the sky.

The sky was wrong. That was the honest truth of it, and I'd been chewing on it for two days without saying it aloud, because everything about my life had been wrong lately and I'd run out of ways to announce it that didn't make me sound like a woman losing her mind.

But it *was* wrong. The clouds over the Oathspine didn't move the way clouds should. They hung there, grey and heavy, going nowhere, like laundry forgotten on a line by someone who wasn't coming home. And every so often — not often enough to prove, just often enough to notice — they flickered. Not lightning. Flickered. Like a candle guttering in a draft.

Like something dying.

"Lena." Corren's voice, low. "You're doing the staring thing."

"I always do the staring thing."

"You do. But this time your hands are doing it too."

I looked down. My fingers were trembling, the old tremor, the one I'd spent twenty years blaming on cold weather in a valley that never got properly cold. Storms passed overhead and my hands knew before I did. It had been a curse in the Hollow. Out here, past the tree line, past the line where magic remembered how to exist, it was starting to feel more like a language.

"When?" was all I asked.

He glanced up at the tower, then at the sun, sinking red behind the western peaks. "Tonight. Moonrise, if the liturgy holds true. Which it may not. It's been thirty years since anyone read it aloud."

"You've been practicing."

"I've been *not sleeping*," he said. "Practicing is what you do when you have confidence."

* * *

Moonrise came up pale and thin over the eastern ridge, and Corren stood in the center of the courtyard with the broken tower looming over him, and he looked — I'll give him this — almost like the man his oath-mark claimed he used to be. He'd scrubbed the road dust off. He wore his grey coat buttoned straight. And on the back of his right hand, where the mark had been dark as a dead coal since before I met him, something faint was stirring. Not light, exactly. More like heat shimmer over stone.

"The waystation is a hinge," he said. We'd heard the lecture twice already; he gave it again anyway, because Corren believed repetition was the mother of competence and also because his voice shook slightly when he talked about what we were about to do, and lecturing steadied it. "Not a door. Doors are for places that want visitors. This is a hinge — a point where the covenant pressed thinnest between the realms. The first oath-bearers built these along the whole spine of the mountains. Most of them have crumbled. This one held."

"Why this one?" Maren asked. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed, water-gift restless in her the way it always was now, droplets rising off her sleeves when she forgot to breathe slow.

"Because of her," Corren said, and nodded at me.

Wonderful. No pressure.

"The gate answers storm-blood. Always has. Your mother came through here — I know because she told me, once, the only time she ever spoke of the Hollow without grief in her mouth." He paused, and I watched him decide not to apologize again. He'd apologized on the mountain for arriving too late to save her; I'd forgiven him for it, mostly, in the way you forgive a wound that's stopped bleeding but still aches when it rains. "Lena. When you feel the pull, don't fight it. But don't chase it either. The gate opens a sliver. A window. You look. You learn. You do *not* reach through."

"What happens if I reach through?"

"I genuinely don't know," he said. "That's rather the point of asking nicely instead."

Tam sat cross-legged near the fire, hugging his knees, watching all of us with those too-old eyes of his. "Is my mama on the other side?" he asked.

The courtyard went very quiet.

"No, love," Maren said, crouching beside him. "The other side isn't — it's not where people go. It's another country. Storms and light and —"

"My mama liked storms," Tam said. "She said they were just the sky having feelings."

Ilva, who had been standing sentry at the arch with her hand on her sword out of habit rather than need, turned around slowly. "Sky having feelings," she repeated.

"That's what she said."

Ilva looked at me. I looked back. Neither of us said anything, because neither of us wanted to be the one who pointed out that a child's dead mother had described the theology of Veyl more accurately than anything in Corren's liturgy books.

"All right," I said, before the silence could curdle. "Let's wake it up."

* * *

Corren taught me the first words of the covenant-liturgy back in Cinderhollow, in a burned town, with a child asleep against my side: *We who stand beneath, we call to those who ride above.* Nine words. Simple as bread. But when I'd spoken them, my blood had answered — a hum in my wrists, a pressure behind my teeth, the taste of rain on a day with no clouds — and I'd understood, in a way I couldn't explain, that the words weren't a spell. They were a greeting. Old as old. Something you said to a neighbor across a fence.

Now, under the broken tower, with the moon climbing and the wrong grey clouds hanging overhead like held breath, I said all of it. The whole liturgy. Corren fed me the lines in a low voice and I gave them back, and somewhere around the fourth verse my trembling hands went still — perfectly, eerily still — and I knew the storm-blood wasn't shaking anymore. It was listening.

*We who stand beneath, we call to those who ride above. Keep faith with us as we keep faith with you. Let the rain fall clean and the light fall kind.*

The last word left my mouth and the world changed.

Not dramatically. That's what I'll never be able to make anyone understand later, when they ask. There was no thunderclap, no pillar of fire. The air simply *opened*, high above the courtyard, above the sheared-off top of the tower — a seam of darkness splitting to show something brighter behind it, widening into a ragged oval maybe ten feet across. Through it I could see stars, but not our stars. These burned in colors stars shouldn't come in: green-white, violet, a deep bruised gold. And beneath them —

Beneath them was Veyl.

Oh, it was beautiful. Even starving, even dying, it was beautiful, and I think that's what broke my heart about it. Rolling country made of cloud-stuff and light, rivers of luminous vapor running silver toward a horizon that glowed like the inside of a pearl. Mountains, if you could call them that — vast slow shapes of storm, shouldering up against the sky, trailing veils of rain that fell upward. Wind moved through everything visibly, a current you could see the way you see water, carrying leaves of light.

And it was dimming. As I watched, a whole valley of that glowing country guttered — the same flicker I'd seen in our clouds for days — and went grey. Just for a heartbeat. Then it brightened again, but not all the way. Not quite all the way. Like a hearth burning down with no one to feed it.

"It's real," Maren whispered. Her arms had uncrossed. One hand was pressed flat over her heart, and I realized water was streaming gently off her hair, rising, drifting toward the seam in the sky like it wanted to go home too. "Gods above, it's real."

"Below gods," Corren said softly. His dark mark was glowing now, faint but unmistakable, and his voice had gone strange. "Above oaths. My grandmother used to say — I never believed her — that you could see the covenant itself, from this side. Look. Between the mountains. Do you see it?"

I saw it.

A line. A thread of light running across the whole visible breadth of Veyl, from horizon to horizon — no, not running. *Anchored.* Pinned at intervals by points of brightness, seven of them, like stitches through fabric. The covenant. The binding that held the storm-realm to ours, that let rain fall clean and kept the wild sky from pouring down unbounded onto human heads. And four of the seven stitches were nearly dark. Two more were fading as I watched, their light pulsing slower, slower, a heartbeat winding down.

One stitch still burned steady and strong.

"Seven signatories," Corren murmured. "Seven heirs. Six lines failed or scattered or hunted down over the generations. Only one bloodline kept faith all the way to the end." He turned and looked at me, and I felt his gaze land on me like a physical weight. "Hers. Yours."

The seam in the sky rippled.

At first I thought it was wind on the far side. Then the ripple organized itself, gathered, became a shape — tall and robed in moving light, standing at the edge of the gate as though it had been waiting there, perhaps for years, perhaps for decades, patient as weather. I couldn't see a face. I couldn't see anything that a face would attach to. But the whole shape bent toward me, attentive, hungry in a way I recognized because I'd been hungry like that myself — hungry for a door, a voice, a reason.

And it spoke.

"*Lena.*"

My mother's voice.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left at that moment. It wasn't an imitation. It wasn't close. It was her voice exactly — the low warm grain of it, the slight lift at the end of my name like my name was a question she was delighted to keep asking. A voice I'd heard last when I was four years old, that I'd carried half-remembered through twenty years of a valley that forgot magic and nearly made me forget her. I would have known it drowned. I would have known it burned to ash.

"Mama?" Tam said, tiny, beside me — and then Maren's arm came around him fast, because of course he'd hope, of course a child hears that voice and hopes, and my chest cracked open along a seam I didn't know I had.

"It's not her," I said. My own voice sounded foreign. "Tam, listen to me, it's not —"

"*Lena.* Come and see. Come home. I kept your place."

The shape raised what might have been an arm. Behind it, the dimming country of Veyl stretched away, gorgeous and guttering, and I swear to you — I swear on every crooked oath ever sworn — that the light bent toward the gate the way flowers bend toward the sun. The whole realm leaning. Listening. Waiting on one word from a girl who mended roofs in a valley that never loved her back.

My foot moved. Half an inch. Gravel shifted under my boot.

Corren's hand closed on my shoulder — hard, steadying, terrified. "Don't reach through," he breathed. "Lena. *Don't reach.*"

"I'm not reaching," I whispered. Except I was. Every part of me was reaching — my blood, my bones, the storm-sign humming in me like a plucked string, singing back to that voice, to that light, to the place where my mother had gone and my name was known and I had a place kept for me. Twenty-two years of exile pulled taut between my heels and that shining seam, and the seam was winning.

Then the flicker came again.

Right there at the gate, right in front of the shape wearing my mother's voice, a swath of Veyl's light guttered and died — and this time it didn't come back. A valley of that luminous country went grey and stayed grey, and I watched frost crawl across it in patterns no frost should make, watched the beautiful rivers of vapor slow and stiffen like breath fogging glass. Dying. Not metaphorically. Starving, Corren had called it, and I finally understood what he meant: a realm being starved of the one thing keeping it alive, which was us. The faith below. The keeping of promises. Everything my mother had died trying to protect.

And the shape — the thing in my mother's voice — didn't turn to look at the dying light. Didn't grieve it. Didn't even notice.

That was how I knew. Whatever stood at the gate, it wasn't her. Her letter had burned in a hearth in the Hollow, but I'd carried its fragments in my head all my life, and every fragment had been about *us*. About the valley. About keeping me safe. About the weight of a promise and the cost of keeping it. Whatever loved me enough to hide me in a place where magic couldn't follow, it would have looked at that dying valley and wept.

This thing only smiled. I felt the smile without seeing it.

"*Come home,*" it said again, sweeter now, urgent. "*Before the door closes. Before it's only silence. You don't want silence, Lena. Silence forever, for everyone. Come home and none of it needs to hurt.*"

Silence forever, for everyone.

Somewhere in the back of my skull, cold and clear as well-water, a thought formed: *that's not an invitation. That's a threat with sugar on it.*

I stepped back. One full step. The gravel crunched loud as a breaking bone.

"No," I said — and my voice came out doubled, my own voice and underneath it the low rolling undertone of distant thunder, the storm-speaking I'd only touched once before, in desperation, at a ford where Corren bled. The shape recoiled. The whole realm behind it shivered. "Whatever you are, you don't use her voice. You don't use *anyone's* voice. And I'm not coming home through a hole in the sky. If Veyl wants me—"

I lifted my chin at the starving, beautiful, dying country above me, at the four dark stitches and the three failing ones and the one that still burned because my mother's blood had refused, all these years, to stop burning.

"—it knows where to find me. I'm the daughter of an oath-bearer. I keep my promises. Starting now."

For a long moment the shape hung there, and the hunger coming off it was so vast I felt it like a change in air pressure. Then, softly — in my mother's voice, one last time, and somehow that was the cruelest cut of all — it said:

"*Too late, little seal. The stitching is already undone.*"

The seam snapped shut. The stars of Veyl vanished. Our own ordinary, faithful, wrong-grey clouds rolled back overhead, and in the sudden dark of the courtyard, everyone was looking at me.

Tam spoke first, small and fierce, his fists knotted in Maren's skirt.

"It said the stitching was undone," he said. "What does that mean? What's a seal?"

Corren had gone the color of the dead coals of his old mark. He was staring at me — at my hands, which had stopped trembling entirely, and at the faint new light rising along the backs of them, stitch-shaped, seven-pointed, like a promise being written on my skin in a script older than kingdoms.

"Lena," he said hoarsely. "Show me your hands."

I held them out to the firelight.

And on both palms, delicate and unmistakable and growing brighter by the heartbeat, the mark of the seal was opening like a flower that had waited twenty-two years to bloom.
