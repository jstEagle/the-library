## 11. The Traitor's Bargain

I've always been good at reading water. You have to be, running a ferry. The river tells you everything if you listen — where the sandbars have shifted in the night, where the current runs mean under a calm surface, where a body might be snagged and waiting for someone foolish enough to swim after it.

So maybe that's why I saw it coming. Or maybe I just didn't want to look, which is its own kind of skill, and one I'd been practicing my whole life.

We were three days out of Cinderhollow, camped in a fold of hills where a stream cut through on its way to the lowlands, when Tam came to me at dusk with his hands cupped like he was carrying something precious and terrible, which, knowing Tam, he was.

"Maren," he whispered. "There's smoke. Not cookfire smoke."

He was right. It was coming up from the valley below us — thin, disciplined threads of it, the kind of smoke an army makes when it's trying not to be seen. And moving wrong. Not toward the pass we'd been aiming for. Toward *us*.

I should have woken Corren right then. That's what I keep coming back to, in the long dark hours since. I should have shaken him awake and said, *hunters, three miles, maybe less,* and we'd have run for the high ground and none of what followed would ever have happened.

Instead I stood there with a child's hand in mine and did arithmetic.

Corren was half-healed from Cinderhollow still, favoring his left side. Lena hadn't slept properly in days; her storm-blood had been restless since the burned town, twitching under her skin like a cat hearing dogs. We were slow, and tired, and the hunters were fast and fresh. If we ran, they'd run us down in open country, and open country is where gifted folk die.

And there were four of us now. Four, counting a boy of eight who sang fire to sleep and whose parents were ash because people like me had let themselves be found.

The smoke kept rising. I kept doing arithmetic. And somewhere in the middle of all that summing, the answer came out wrong, and I let it.

* * *

They took me an hour past midnight.

I'd told the others I was taking first watch, which wasn't even a lie — I wanted first watch, wanted to be alone with the sound of the stream and the weight of what I was already deciding. I walked downstream a quarter mile, far enough that our fire wouldn't show, and I sat on a stone and waited, because some part of me — the part that reads water, the part that knows exactly how a river will break around a hull — understood that if trouble was coming, it would come by the water.

They came quiet. Grey iron doesn't glint in moonlight; it drinks it. Six of them, fanning out through the reeds with the practiced silence of men who've done this to other sleeping camps before, and behind them, unhurried as a woman walking to market, came Inquisitor Vess.

She stopped ten feet away and looked at me the way you look at a knot you're deciding whether to cut or work loose.

"The ferry-woman," she said. "Maren of Brassen. Heir of the deep oath." She said it flatly, no wonder in it, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Your friends are asleep by a fire I could put out with their own blood. Your boy is dreaming. I want to know where you're taking him."

"And if I don't tell you?"

"Then I take all four of you to the capital in wagons, and the boy goes into the question-rooms, and I promise you, ferry-woman, the question-rooms do not care how old a prisoner is."

My mouth went dry. My hands, traitors that they apparently were in every sense, stayed perfectly steady. Ferry-hands. They know how to hold steady while the flood comes through.

"Tam," I said. "You leave the boy out of it."

"He's on the list same as the rest."

"Then here's my price." I heard myself say it, clear and cold, a stranger's voice wearing my throat. "You get your ambush. You get Lena Amberdale trussed and delivered, and the disgraced oath-keeper too. In exchange, the child walks free. No wagons. No question-rooms. You give him to a village family, unmarked, and your hunters never speak his name again."

Vess studied me for a long moment. She had eyes like well-water — dark, still, and colder the deeper you went.

"You'd sell them," she said. Not accusing. Almost curious. "For the child."

"I'd buy his life," I said. "With whatever coin I've got."

Something moved behind her face — not softness, nothing so kind, but recognition, maybe. A woman who'd lost someone once, weighing another woman's arithmetic and finding it familiar.

"Dawn," she said. "The ford at Marlow's Crossing, two days east. Tell them you've found a safer route north. When they're mid-crossing, my company takes them. The boy walks away untouched. Break faith, and I burn the child first, so you can watch what your loyalty bought."

* * *

I walked back to camp and lay down and did not sleep, which surprised no one, least of all the stars.

Here's the thing about betrayal that nobody warns you: it doesn't feel like a door slamming. It feels like a ferry rope paying out slowly through your hands, one loop at a time, each one reasonable, each one survivable, until you look down and realize the far bank is gone and you're adrift and the rope was never yours to give.

I told myself stories all night. *It's not really a betrayal if it saves a child. Corren's an oath-keeper; he'll understand bargaining. Lena's strong — she'll fight free.* Lies, every one, and I stacked them like sandbags against the rising water of what I actually knew: that I'd traded two people who'd pulled me off a dock and called me family, and I'd done it in a reed bed at midnight without giving them the courtesy of asking.

Because that was the worst part, if I'm honest. Not the selling. The *silence*. All day, as we packed camp, as Tam rode on my shoulders and pointed at hawks, as Lena laughed at something Corren said — actually laughed, that rusty unused laugh of hers — I smiled back and said nothing, and every hour of saying nothing was another loop of rope through my hands.

At noon we crested a ridge and I saw it: Marlow's Crossing, the river running wide and brown and quick between willowed banks, the ford a pale scar of stones just breaking the surface. Safe-looking. Rivers always look safe right up until they aren't.

"Found a better route," I said, casual as commenting on weather. "North road's crawling with criers. The ford's quieter."

Lena glanced at me — just a flicker, just a beat too long — and I felt the rope slip another loop.

* * *

We hit the water at midafternoon. Corren went first, testing the stones with his staff, Tam clinging to his back. Lena followed. I went last, because I couldn't bear to walk beside any of them, and because some drowned little piece of me hoped the river would simply take me and settle the account.

Halfway across, the willows stood up and became soldiers.

They came out of both banks at once, grey iron drinking the daylight, crossbows leveled, and Vess walked down to the waterline with her arms folded like a woman watching laundry dry.

"Hold!" Corren roared, and shoved Tam toward the far bank. "Lena — the boy — *go!*"

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way drowning does. Crossbow bolts hissed into the water. Lena screamed — not fear, fury, the sky above us going bruise-dark in seconds, thunder rolling in from a cloudless horizon. But Vess had chosen her ground well; the hunters' iron was warded, and the storm broke ragged around them, splitting like a wave on pilings.

And Corren turned around.

He turned *around*, away from safety, and planted himself in the middle of the ford with his broken old staff and his dark dead covenant-mark, and he held the crossing. I watched him do it. Watched him catch the first hunter's blade on his staff and drive the man into the current, watched him take a bolt through the shoulder and not fall, watched him stand in the flood with his grey head thrown back and make himself a wall made of nothing but stubbornness and shame, buying Lena time to carry Tam up the far bank.

He was still standing when they finally brought him down. Three of them, blades and boots, and the river went pink around him, and I stood waist-deep in my own element — *my* river, *my* crossing, water I could have bent into a hammer or a shield or a drowning fist with a word — and I did nothing.

That was the bargain. That was the price I'd agreed to pay in someone else's blood.

A hand closed on my arm. Vess, at the waterline, her voice low and private beneath the battle-noise.

"The boy reached the trees," she said. "He's alive. A bargain kept." Her eyes went past me to Corren's body in the shallows, and something crossed her face that was not triumph. "This is what the covenant costs, ferry-woman. Remember it kindly."

Then she turned to her soldiers and gave the order to take Lena, and Lena — cornered on the bank with Tam hidden behind her — did something with the storm that I'll be hearing in my dreams for the rest of my life. The sky *tore*. Rain came down like judgment. Half the hunters fled outright, and in that chaos of white water and black cloud, Lena got Corren over her shoulder — bleeding, broken, breathing, barely — and dragged him into the trees, and I stumbled after them because I no longer had anywhere else in the world to go.

* * *

Night, in a hollow under a rock shelf, rain still falling soft and endless. Corren lay grey-faced on my rolled cloak, breath whistling thin through a cracked rib-cage, and Lena knelt over him with her hands pressed to his wounds and tears cutting clean lines through the mud on her face.

Tam was asleep against my side. Alive. Unmarked. Bought and paid for.

Lena looked up at me across the fire she wouldn't let herself light, and her eyes were the color of the sky before hail.

"You knew," she said. Quietly. That was the worst of it — no screaming, no storm. Just quiet. "You knew they'd be at the ford. You picked the crossing."

"I—"

"Don't." She looked back down at Corren. "Just tell me why. Tell me anything true."

And I opened my mouth, and every story I'd built in the night — the noble ones, the selfish ones, all the sandbags — washed away at once, and what was left was just the plain ugly truth of it.

"Vess promised me Tam's life," I said. "If I gave her yours."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere out in the dark, a hunter's horn sounded, far off, patient.

Lena stared at me for a long, long moment. Then she went back to pressing Corren's wounds, and she said, in a voice I'll hear until I die:

"He'd have given himself for the boy anyway. That's who he is. So you sold him for nothing, Maren. You sold him for something he'd have paid freely."

She was right. That was the knife of it, twisted clean. I hadn't saved Tam from a cruel fate. I'd just stolen the choice from the people who loved him enough to make it themselves.

I sat there in the rain with a sleeping child against my side and a dying man's blood drying on my cloak, and I understood that I had become the thing I'd spent my whole life avoiding: the calm stretch of river that looks safe and drowns you.

Out in the darkness, the horn sounded again. Closer now.

And Lena lifted her head, listening, and I saw something change in her face — grief hardening into something older and hotter, the storm-blood rising in her eyes like weather.

"They're tracking us," she said. "Which means tomorrow, we run again. And Maren—" she didn't look at me, and that was mercy and punishment in one, "—you walk at the rear. Where I can see you."

I nodded, because there was nothing else to do. The rope had paid out to its end. Now I just had to find out whether there was any way to climb back up it — or whether I'd dragged everyone I loved down into the flood with me.
