## 12. Salt and Ashes

Corren was dying the way he did everything else — stubbornly, badly, and with commentary.

"Stop hovering," he said from the trampled reeds where Maren and I had dragged him after the ford. "You hover like your mother. She hovered. Drove me mad."

"You're not dying," I said, because I had decided it, and my decisions were binding now. That was apparently how this worked. You lost everything familiar, you picked up strangers like burrs, and then you simply *declared* things and the universe was expected to comply.

"Course I'm not," Corren agreed, and coughed wetly into his fist. The cough had a sound in it that I didn't like — a sound like water going somewhere it shouldn't. The bolt had gone in under his collarbone, and though Maren had pulled it out with hands that shook only a little, the wound had opinions of its own. "I'm too mean to die. Ask anyone."

"Everyone who could answer is dead or hunting us," I said.

"Then ask Tam."

Tam was sitting ten feet away with his knees pulled up to his chin, watching the treeline across the river the way small children watch doors at night. He'd stopped crying an hour ago, which frightened me more than the crying would have. When Tam went quiet, fire was usually thinking about something.

The ford was behind us. That was what we'd won, if you wanted to call it winning. Corren had held the crossing alone while we got the horses and the boy across, and he'd held it the way old oath-keepers hold anything: with his body, until his body was used up. Three hunters dead in the shallows. A dozen more on the far bank when the light failed, and Vess's grey banners among them, patient as winter.

Now night had come down over the salt marshes south of the river, and we were crouched in a hollow of dune-grass with our dying man breathing in that wrong, wet rhythm, and I could hear the hunters moving on the other side of the black water. Not crossing yet. Waiting for dawn, probably. Vess struck me as someone who liked her atrocities conducted in good lighting.

* * *

Maren found me at midnight, down by the waterline where the marsh met the sea and the air tasted of salt and rot and cold. I was doing the thing I'd been doing all evening instead of useful things: holding my hands out in front of me and watching them tremble, waiting for them to tell me something true.

"They're not coming tonight," she said.

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been staring at that river like you can drown it through sheer resentment, and honestly, Amberdale, if anyone could—"

"Maren." My voice came out flat and strange. "Why did they find us at the ford?"

She went still. Maren was never still — she talked with her whole body, hands and shoulders and eyebrows, even when she was being insulting, which was always. So the stillness told me before any words did.

"The road forks three ways past the hills," I said. "We took no fires for two days. We moved at night. And they were standing on that bank like they'd been invited." I turned around. "How did they know, Maren?"

The tide breathed in and out. Somewhere behind us, Corren coughed.

"I want to say something first," she said finally. "Before you decide what I am. I need you to hear the whole shape of it, because the whole shape is the only honest part."

"Then say it fast."

Her jaw worked. "At Cinderhollow. After we left the ash town, there was a checkpoint on the coast road. Hunters. They had a net strung across the whole valley mouth, and they had Tam's description, and yours, and—" Her breath hitched. "And they had a child with them, Lena. A little girl, maybe six, gifted, already collared. They said she'd go to the interrogation tents unless the net caught bigger fish. And I thought — Tam. They'll take Tam someday, some checkpoint, some night when we sleep, and there will be nothing I can do. I've drowned docks. I've flooded towns. I can't be everywhere, and I am so *tired* of being able to move rivers and not save one child."

"So you made a bargain," I said.

"I made a bargain." She said it like spitting. "I sent word through a fishwife who owes me. One message. *They travel toward the Stormseat by the low road.* Nothing about faces, nothing about numbers. Just enough to buy the girl's freedom — they released her to a village elder, I checked, I swear on every drowned god I checked — and enough to keep them off our scent for a week. It should have bought us a week. Instead Vess read between my lines, because of course she did, she reads everyone like weather, and she set her ambush at the ford because the low road funnels there and she knew I knew it." Her eyes were bright and furious and fixed on mine. "Corren bled because of me. So don't forgive me. Don't you dare forgive me quickly. But know that I did it for Tam, and know that I would do it again, and know that I hate myself in a way that doesn't have a bottom."

I looked at her for a long time. The moon came out from behind cloud and showed me her face — the face of a woman who ran a ferry, who fed strangers, who had flooded a dock for two people she'd known for a day.

"You should have told us," I said.

"Yes."

"You should have let us choose."

"Yes." Her voice cracked on it. "That's the lesson, isn't it. That's the whole crooked lesson of this stupid covenant. Nobody gets to carry the choice alone." She laughed, wet and awful. "Your mother tried it. Look how that ended."

"Don't," I said quietly, "talk about my mother."

"No," she agreed. "Fair."

I walked back up the beach alone. I didn't forgive her. I want to be clear about that, because stories like to make forgiveness arrive on schedule, neat as a ferry. It didn't. What I felt walking away from her was a wound with her name on it, and underneath the wound, grudging as gravel, the understanding that she had done exactly what I might have done for Tam, and hated herself better than I ever could. Understanding isn't forgiveness. But it shares a fence line.

* * *

Toward the grey hour before dawn, Corren called me to him.

He'd gone the color of the salt crust on the marsh stones, and his lips had a bluish tinge that Maren kept glancing at and looking away from. She'd done everything she knew — packed the wound, boiled willow bark, kept him warm — and her competence had run out against arithmetic. Too much blood. Too deep. Too long.

"Come here, storm-girl," he rasped. "Sit. Stop looming, you loom like a gallows."

I sat in the grass beside him. His hand found mine, and his grip surprised me — still strong, still calloused, the grip of a man who had carried me over a ridge with hunters' arrows singing past us.

"I owe you a confession," he said.

"If it's that you arrived too late for my mother, I've known since the Oathspine. We settled that."

"Not that one." He smiled, and it cost him; I watched it cost him. "A new one. Fresh sin, hot from the oven." His breath whistled. "When I took the oath-mark, the old keeper who gave it to me told me what the mark really means. Everyone thinks it means *I will protect you.* It doesn't. It means *when I fail you, I will spend what's left of me making it right.* That's why mine went dark, Lena. Not because I broke faith. Because the mark spends itself on failure. It paid everything I had the day your mother died, and there was nothing left to burn." His eyes found mine, fever-bright. "It's been dark for eleven years. And yesterday, on that ford, holding the crossing for you —"

He turned his forearm toward the dying moonlight.

The mark was glowing. Faint, guttering, like a coal remembered rather than alive — but glowing, blue-white, the old liturgy-script crawling along his skin in lines I almost recognized.

"It's waking up," he whispered. "Because you're close, Lena. Because whatever's in your blood is starting to remember what it's for." His hand tightened. "Which means you have to stop being afraid of it. Do you hear me? There are four heirs left and one dying man, and the hunters cross that river at dawn, and I cannot walk out of here. So you're going to have to do the thing you've been tiptoeing around since the sky-gate. You're going to have to call the storm."

"I don't know how."

"You do. You've known since you were a child shaking under thunder. Your mother wrote you a letter and your foster father burned it, but nobody can burn what's in the blood." He pulled me closer, and his voice dropped to the register he used for liturgy, low and worn smooth as river stone. "The first words of the covenant. The ones I taught you in Cinderhollow. Say them with me. *What was sundered, let it turn.*"

"What was sundered, let it turn," I repeated.

"*What was scattered, let it gather.*"

"What was scattered, let it gather."

"And the last part — the part no oath-bearer ever says lightly." His smile was terrible and kind. "*Let the sky remember its own.*"

Behind us, faint across the river, came the ring of iron — hunters moving early, or moving confident, torchlight blooming among the far trees like rotten fruit.

"Now," Corren said, "go stand where they can see you, and give them something to write home about."

* * *

I walked down to the waterline. The tide was turning; the river ran slow and heavy toward the sea, and beyond it the grey banners hung motionless in the windless dark. Torches. Maybe forty men, maybe more, massed at the crossing with the patience of people who believed dawn belonged to them.

Inquisitor Vess stood at the head of the ford. Even across the water I knew her silhouette — straight-backed, still, the iron gorget catching torchlight. She raised one hand, and forty hunters raised their crossbows.

And here is the thing I will try to tell truly, though true telling of it is like trying to hold rain.

I wasn't brave. I want that recorded. I stood on that salt-flat with death aimed at me across black water, and what I felt was not courage but a kind of homesickness — a sudden, piercing ache for a sound I hadn't heard since childhood. Thunder. Real thunder, overhead, the kind that used to make my hands tremble in the Hollow while storms passed over the tree line that magic couldn't cross, and I'd press my palms flat and feel the sky trying to reach me and failing, failing, always failing, because the valley forgot.

But I wasn't in the valley anymore.

*What was sundered,* I thought, *let it turn.*

I reached up — there's no other way to say it — I reached *up*, the way you reach for a rope thrown to a drowning woman, and the sky took my hand.

The first thing was pressure. The whole weight of the atmosphere leaning down on the marsh, pressing the salt-grass flat, pressing my ears until the world went thick. Then a smell like coins and cut stone. Then every hair on my arms lifting, and Tam's voice behind me, small and awed: *"Lena, the clouds—"*

There were no clouds. Then there were. They came from nowhere and everywhere, boiling up over the stars, and lightning moved inside them the way blood moves inside skin — not flashing but *circulating*, vast slow veins of white fire, and I understood with a clarity that has never left me that a storm is not weather. A storm is a decision the sky makes. And it had decided to make it with me.

*What was scattered, let it gather.*

The wind arrived all at once, screaming off the sea, flattening the hunters' torches to blue coals. The river stood up. I mean that literally — the ford rose in a ridge of black water, waves marching upstream against the current, and the first crossbow bolts that leapt the gap were swatted from the air like flies.

*Let the sky remember its own.*

Lightning came down.

Not once. Not as a strike. As a *presence* — a pillar of white fire walking across the far bank, and where it walked, grey iron scattered. I heard shouting, horses screaming, the crack of a banner-pole splitting. I watched men who had hunted children across half a kingdom drop their weapons and run, and I watched Inquisitor Vess stand in the middle of it, unmoving, her face turned up toward me across the burning water, and I saw — I will swear this to my grave — I saw her *understand*. She looked at me the way you look at a door closing on a room where someone you loved is trapped. Grief recognizing grief.

Then the wind took her banners and the dark took her soldiers, and I was alone with the storm, and the storm was alone with me, and oh, it was *magnificent*. That's the shameful truth at the center of this chapter, the thing I'll carry longer than the fear: it felt magnificent. All that power answering me, all that sky bending to my small shaking hands, after a lifetime of trembling in a valley that muted me. For one blazing moment I understood why the gifted of old had grown proud, and why the proud had grown cruel, and why the crown feared us enough to build a hunt.

Power doesn't corrupt. Power *invites*. And I stood in the doorway of that invitation with my arms full of lightning and my enemies fleeing, and I wanted —

I wanted to keep it.

That wanting lasted three heartbeats. Then I heard Corren cough, wet and failing, behind me, and I heard Tam whimper at the light, and I remembered what power was *for*: not the sky remembering itself, but the sky remembering *us*. I closed my fists. I let the storm go — reluctantly, grumbling, trailing sparks across the marsh like a cat denied the door — and the silence afterward rang louder than the thunder had.

Every spy on every road saw it. Every crier would be telling it by noon. The storm-caller of the Hollow, revealed at last, lit up against the dark for half a kingdom to witness. Whatever thin shadow of anonymity we'd clutched on the roads was burned to salt and ashes on that riverbank.

I stood in the aftermath, soaked to the bone, smoking faintly, and discovered I didn't care.

Let them come. Let them all come.

* * *

Corren died at sunrise.

No — he didn't. I keep writing that sentence in my head and having to cross it out, because for one long grey hour, with the storm spent and the wound winning, he did die, or stood close enough to death's door to read the knocker. Maren held his head. Tam pressed both small hands to the wound and sang to it, that fire-lullaby turned to something softer, warmth without flame, and I held his marked forearm against my cheek and begged the liturgy into his skin — *what was scattered, let it gather* — over and over, gathering what, I didn't know, gathering anything, gathering breath.

And the mark answered. The guttering coal flared. Blue-white script crawled up his arm and across his chest, and beneath Maren's hands the bleeding slowed, then stopped, then — impossibly — reversed, the torn flesh knitting with the slow reluctance of a man doing chores he resents. The covenant-mark, spending itself again. Paying out failure into mending. By full morning he was asleep instead of dying, which in our company counted as triumph.

I sat back in the salt-grass and laughed, and the laugh came out closer to sobbing, and Maren put her arm around my shoulders, and I let it stay there, and I did not forgive her, and I did not pull away. Both those things were true. I am learning that most true things come in pairs like that.

"We can't hide anymore," Maren said quietly, nodding at the eastern sky, where news of last night was already traveling faster than any horse. "Every spy, every crier, every bounty-hunter with a grudge. They'll come at us in hundreds now."

"Then we stop hiding," I said.

She looked at me. Tam looked at me. Even sleeping Corren seemed, in some moral sense, to look at me.

"Vess wants the Stormseat destroyed," I said. "Ilva's proof, the plans, all of it. And the covenant dies a little more every day we spend running. So we stop running. We walk the rest of the road in the open, in daylight, with the storm over our heads — and we see who else is tired of hiding." I stood, and my legs shook, and I let them. "There's a hidden court somewhere out there. Bakers and midwives and soldiers, Veiled Court folk, generations of them crouching in cellars. Hiding is how the covenant died. I intend to knock on their doors and offer them something better than a cellar."

Maren studied me for a long moment. Then, wryly, tiredly, with the ghost of her old insolence: "You're going to start a war with a storm and a speech, aren't you."

"No," I said, watching the sun climb clean out of the sea, gilding the marsh, touching the face of a sleeping man whose oath had burned bright again. "I'm going to finish one."

Far to the north, above the Oathspine mountains, the sky had begun to darken. Not weather-dark. *Hunger*-dark. Veyl was dying faster than any of us knew — and something in that darkness, something wearing a voice I loved, had already begun to whisper my name.
