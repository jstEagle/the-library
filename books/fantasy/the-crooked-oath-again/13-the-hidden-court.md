## 13. The Hidden Court

The door was in a well.

I want that written down properly, because nobody ever believes me when I say it. Not a door near a well, not a door behind a well. *In* the well. We climbed down the inside of it like beetles, and halfway down there was a hole in the stones, and Corren knocked on the hole four times — two slow, two fast — and a voice said "who taught you that knocking," and Corren said "a dead woman," and the voice said "come in then, she always had terrible timing."

That was how we got into the Hidden Court.

Tam, I should say. That's me. Eight years old, nearly nine, which is old enough to know that most grown-ups lie about important things and young enough that they still tell me true things by accident. I sing fire to sleep. That's my gift. It sounds nice until you learn that the reason I'm good at it is because I set one on fire once, a house, a whole house, and everybody who was sleeping in it stayed asleep, which is the polite way of saying what happened. So now I put fires to bed like they're grumpy toddlers, and I don't sleep much myself, and I count doors, because doors are either ways out or ways in and knowing which is which keeps you alive.

The well-door was both. I liked it immediately.

* * *

Down underneath, past the hole in the well-stones, there were tunnels, and the tunnels opened up into a cavern so big my breath went funny. There were houses down there. Actual houses, with chimneys that didn't go anywhere — the smoke just curled up and vanished against the ceiling like it was embarrassed — and lanterns that burned green instead of orange, and washing strung between rooftops under the earth like the sky was something you could hang sheets from.

People came out to look at us. A baker with flour to her elbows. Two midwives, arguing about something even as they walked. An old soldier missing an arm, leaning in a doorway with his boots crossed at the ankle like he had all the time in the world and half the legs for it.

"They're staring," Maren whispered.

"Course they are," said Corren. "We're the most interesting thing that's fallen down their well in years."

"That is not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort's earned. Curiosity comes free."

Lena didn't say anything. Lena hadn't said much since the ford, since Corren got carried through these last miles wrapped in sailcloth and grey-faced, since Maren told us all what she'd done. The whole walk down the mountain Lena had been quiet in a way that wasn't like being quiet at all. It was like being a storm cloud that hadn't decided yet.

A woman came toward us through the crowd, and everyone made room for her the way water makes room for a big stone.

She was small. Smaller than Maren, and Maren's not big. She had white hair cut short and hands like a laundress's and eyes that did the thing where they looked at you and finished looking before you'd noticed they'd started. She wore an apron over a soldier's coat, which I thought was the most honest outfit I'd ever seen.

"The oath-keeper," she said, looking at Corren on his makeshift stretcher. "Corren Vael. You look terrible."

"Thank you, Sefa. I worked hard on it."

"Get him to the healers' row." She snapped her fingers and people moved, and then her eyes came to Lena, and stayed, and I watched something pass between them that I was too short to see over. "And you'll be Amberdale's girl."

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew her name. Down here, that's mostly what we have of each other." Sefa turned, coat and apron swinging. "Come. All of you. Even the little one. Especially the little one — children hear everything, might as well let them hear it straight."

I decided I liked her. Grown-ups who say children should hear things straight are usually lying about something else, but she said it like she meant it, and besides, she had flour on her apron over the soldier's coat, and anybody who bakes *and* fights has figured out what life is actually for.

* * *

They fed us first. This is the Veiled Court's great secret, if you ask me, more than the tunnels or the green lanterns or the fact that they've been hiding under half the kingdoms for three hundred years: they feed you before they talk to you. Bread still warm, real butter, soup with actual meat in it. Tam's ruling on the Veiled Court: any secret society that can produce warm bread underground has its priorities right.

We ate at a long table in a hall carved out of the rock, and the walls had names on them. Thousands of names, chiseled small, going up past where the lamplight reached.

"What's that?" I asked, mouth full.

"Our ledger," said Sefa. "Every gifted soul the Court ever sheltered. Every one who was born hidden, lived hidden, died hidden. And every one we failed." She tapped a section lower down, where the chiseling got hurried, the letters crooked. "Those are the ones taken in the purges. We remember them by carving them twice — once for the life, once for the loss."

Maren stopped eating. I noticed because Maren never stops eating; she says ferry-folk don't trust food that waits around.

"How many?" Lena asked quietly.

"On this wall? Eleven thousand and some. In the world?" Sefa sat down across from us, folding her hands. "More than the crown would like, fewer than there should be. We survive, Miss Amberdale. That's all we've ever done. Survive. And we've gotten very good at it. Three hundred years good. Bakers who charm their ovens at midnight. Midwives who can feel a gifted babe in the womb and warn the mother before anyone else knows. Soldiers who misplace orders. Ferry-women—" she glanced at Maren "—who lose certain passengers' names from their books. We are everywhere, and we are no one, and that is why we are still here."

Corren, propped up on his stretcher with bandages all up one side, made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. "You're describing exactly what her mother tried to do."

"Yes," said Sefa. "And I'm going to describe it again, louder, in a moment. Eat first."

* * *

Here is the argument, as best as I can write it down. I was sitting next to Lena, close enough to feel the storm-hum coming off her skin like heat off a road in summer. When Lena gets upset, the air gets thick. My gift doesn't like it much. Fire hates storms, the way cats hate baths.

"You brought the covenant-mark here," Sefa said. "Or rather, it brought itself. The old signs have been waking up all over, these last months. Our people feel it in their sleep. Something is dying up in the high places, and everything tied to it is rattling in its chains." She leaned forward. "So let me tell you what happens next, Miss Amberdale, because it has happened before, and we have the graves to prove it. You will be tempted to fix it. To stand up. To gather your five heirs and march on some mountain and do something loud and bright and doomed. And the hunters will follow the noise straight to every tunnel, every kitchen, every hidden cradle we have spent three centuries protecting. Loud and bright is how we die."

"So what do I do instead?"

"Hide. Properly. Stay here. We can shield you better than any valley — our concealments are woven, not geographic. The crown can't seize what it can't find. Live, grow strong, and maybe in twenty years, thirty, when the hunt forgets—"

"My mother hid me for nineteen years." Lena's voice didn't go up when she said it. It went *down*, which was worse. "In a valley where magic couldn't touch me. And do you know what it bought? It bought the covenant dying anyway. It bought witch-hunters crossing a border that was supposed to be impossible. Hiding didn't save anything, Sefa. Hiding is *how* it died. Everyone keeping their head down, generation after generation, letting the oath-bearers scatter until there weren't enough left to matter. The silence was supposed to protect us. Instead it just meant nobody was holding the line when the line needed holding."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Around the hall, the other hidden folk had gone very still. The baker. The midwives. The one-armed soldier by the door. All of them listening with the faces of people who had heard this argument before, from inside themselves, and lost.

"Child," Sefa said at last, gently, "you are asking me to spend eleven thousand names on your war."

"I'm asking you to stop adding to the wall."

"And if you fail? If the Stormseat falls, if the hunters take you? What happens to the boy sitting next to you? To the fire-singer?" Her eyes flicked to me, and I made myself not flinch, because flinching tells grown-ups things. "He is eight years old. He has already been hunted out of one home. Do you offer him a battlefield instead of a bunker and call it kindness?"

"He's sitting *right here,*" I said.

Both of them looked at me. I hadn't planned on saying anything. But my mouth does that sometimes, especially when grown-ups start talking about me like I'm a sack of flour someone might drop.

"I slept in a bunker," I said. "Sort of. The house in Cinderhollow — Aunt Rill kept the shutters shut and the stove cold and we never sang, not even hymns, in case somebody heard something. And the hunters came anyway. Because hiding isn't safe. Hiding is just being scared somewhere specific." I looked at Lena, then at Sefa, and my voice did the wobble but I pushed through it, because Corren says pushing through the wobble is the entire job. "She sings to me. Lena. When my fire gets nightmares. Nobody ever did that before. So wherever she goes, that's the safe place. Not the tunnels. *Her.*"

Silence. The green lanterns buzzed. Somewhere far off in the tunnels, a baby started crying and was hushed by a midwife, and I thought: that's the whole thing, right there. That's what Sefa built. Babies, hushed, safe, underground. And that's what was dying above us, in the realm where the light was going out.

Sefa looked at me for a long time. Then at Lena.

"You've collected quite a court yourself," she said dryly. "An oath-keeper with a broken mark, a ferry-heir who betrayed you and confessed it, a stone-heir wearing hunter's grey, and a child who argues doctrine at dinner." She sighed, and it was the sigh of a woman losing an argument she'd already known the shape of. "Your mother stood in this hall, you know. Nineteen years ago. Pregnant with you, heading for the Hollow, and I gave her this same speech. Hide. Stay. Let the world forget you."

Lena went very still. "What did she say?"

"She said—" Sefa smiled, and it was a tired, crooked smile, the kind you find on old roads. "She said, 'Forgetting is a door that swings both ways.' And she walked out, and I carved her name on the living side of the wall, and I have regretted the argument ever since. Not because she was wrong." She stood, and pressed her laundress's hands flat on the table. "Because she was right, and right has such a terrible cost, and I am too old to pay it again. So I won't march with you, Miss Amberdale. But the Court will shelter you tonight, and mend your oath-keeper, and fill your packs. And some of the younger ones—" her eyes went sideways, to the one-armed soldier, to a couple of the midwives, to a boy about Maren's age with river-mud eyes "—have been making their own arguments lately. Quietly. I expect they'll make them to you by morning."

* * *

They gave us a room off the healers' row, all of us together, which felt strange and good, like the houses in the cavern with their fake chimneys. Corren slept like a dropped sack. Maren didn't sleep at all — she sat by the lamp turning her own oath-mark ring around and around on her finger, and once she looked at Lena across the room like she wanted to say something, and Lena looked back, and neither of them said it, and I pulled my blanket over my ears because some silences you're not supposed to overhear.

I dreamed about the fire in Cinderhollow. In the dream it wasn't angry, though. It was cold, blue-white, and it was burning *upward*, in the sky, over a black causeway, and it was singing my name in a voice that wasn't fire's voice at all. It sounded hungry. It sounded like something enormous trying to fit through a keyhole.

I woke up gasping and the lamp had gone out and every candle stub in the room was lit and standing at attention like little soldiers, because my gift panics when I panic.

Lena was awake instantly. Of course she was. She crossed the room and gathered the flames off the candles onto her palm, one by one, gentle as picking up kittens, and held them there glowing while I got my breath back.

"Tell me," she said.

So I told her. About the fire burning upward. About the black causeway. About the hungry voice.

Lena's face did something complicated. "Causeway," she repeated. "Was there a fortress at the end of it? High, with towers like teeth?"

"Towers like teeth," I whispered. "It was cold there. Colder than Cinderhollow."

She looked at the little flame trembling on her hand, and I saw the storm-hum come up in her skin, brighter than I'd ever felt it, and she said, almost to herself:

"That's the Stormseat. That's where it's calling from." She closed her fist and the light went out, kindly. "Sleep, Tam. Tomorrow we're going to need every dream you've got."

Outside our door, soft as flour falling, I heard footsteps — the one-armed soldier's uneven tread, and others with him, several, stopping just past the threshold. Waiting. The way people wait when they've already decided and are working up the courage to say so.

Lena heard them too. I saw her hear them.

She got up, straightened her shoulders like she was putting on armor one piece at a time, and went to open the door.
