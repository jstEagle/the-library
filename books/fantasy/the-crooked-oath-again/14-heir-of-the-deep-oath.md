## 14. Heir of the Deep Oath

I have been stabbed before. Twice, if you count the time in Kell's Ferry that was technically a pitchfork, and I do count it, because it hurt worse than either knife. But lying on a pallet of straw in a smuggler's cellar under a burned town, watching a woman named Sela stitch my side closed by lamplight while Tam watched over her shoulder with the rapt attention of a boy who had decided surgery was his new favorite thing — this was the first time I'd been stabbed and not been able to walk away from it after.

"Hold still," Sela said.

"I'm holding very still."

"You're talking. Talking is not holding still."

"Talking," I said, "is how I know I'm alive."

She tied off the thread and gave me a look that would have curdled milk. "Then find another way to know."

The wound was from the ford. Vess's ambush, Maren's bargain, all of it a bad night I kept replaying like a man picking at a scab because he wants to confirm it's still there. The hunter's blade had gone in under my ribs and out again, which the old soldiers say is better than in-and-stay-in, but old soldiers also say a lot of things to keep younger men from crying.

Lena came down the ladder twice a day. She never said much. She'd sit on an upturned crate, check the bandage with hands that were careful in a way her words weren't, and once — only once — she pressed two fingers flat against the skin beside the wound, and I felt something move through me like distant thunder deciding whether or not to bother with our valley.

"You're doing that on purpose," I said.

"Doing what?"

"Practicing."

Her jaw set. "If I'm going to be a seal, Corren, I should know what I'm sealing."

That was Lena. Even grief, even terror, she turned into homework. I admired it the way you admire weather — from a respectful distance.

* * *

On the fourth day, Ilva came.

I heard her boots before I saw her. That's the thing about grey iron: it announces itself. Every soldier of the hunt walks the same way, heel-to-toe, measured, like the ground owes them something they intend to collect. Sela's smugglers had a back door and three bolt holes, and any sensible fugitive would have used them. Instead I lay there and listened to the boots come down the stairs, and I thought, well. At least I'll die horizontal for once.

Sergeant Ilva filled the doorway the way a doorframe fills a doorway — completely, without apology. Grey uniform, no helm, a sword at her hip worn the way working people wear tools rather than the way officers wear jewelry. Her eyes went around the room: me, the straw, the lamp, Tam frozen halfway up the ladder rungs with his fists already smoking faintly.

"Don't," she said to Tam. Not harshly. Wearily. "I'm not here for the boy."

"Funny," I said. "That's usually exactly who you're here for."

"Corren Vaile." She read my name off me like a charge sheet. "Oath-keeper, third covenant of the Spine. Struck from the rolls eight years ago for dereliction." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I wrote part of your file."

"Flattered. Did you write the part where I'm devastatingly handsome? Because that's recent."

Something flickered across her face — annoyance, yes, but underneath it, something rawer. She crossed the room in four strides, and before I could decide whether to reach for the knife under my pallet, she pulled a gauntlet from inside her coat and dropped it on my chest.

Grey iron. Officer's make. And etched into the inner wrist-plate, so small you'd miss it unless you knew to look, a mark I hadn't seen since I was a young man kneeling on cold stone: a deep spiral, turning inward, like a stone rolled into the earth.

The deep oath. The stone-oath. The mark of the fourth heir.

"Where did you get this?" My voice came out wrong. Careful. The way it goes when hope shows up wearing armor.

"It's mine," Ilva said. "It's been mine since I was born. It doesn't burn. It hasn't ever burned. But when I stand on bare rock—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When I stand on bare rock, I can hear it. Underneath. Like something humming through the floor of the world. I thought everyone could. Until I was twelve, and I mentioned it, and my mother went white as ash and told me never to speak of it again. Then she sent me to the hunt's academy, because she figured nowhere was safer for a girl like me than inside the thing hunting girls like me."

Tam had come down the ladder without anyone noticing, the way children do. He was staring at the gauntlet. At Ilva. At the space between them, maybe, where a whole life of not-knowing hung.

"She was right, mostly," Ilva said. "Until Inquisitor Vess."

* * *

She sat then — uninvited, unbothered — on Sela's stitching stool, and told us about the last month of her service, and every word of it made my ribs hurt worse than the blade had.

Vess had changed after Cinderhollow. Everyone on the road had felt it; the hunt moved faster, hit harder, stopped bothering with proclamations and tribunals. Ilva had assumed it was grief. Vess's sister, the mob, all the old story every soldier knew and nobody spoke aloud.

But then came the orders that didn't fit any tribunal. Survey teams at the Oathspine. Engineers. Blasting powder requisitioned by the wagonload, logged as "roadwork." A sealed dispatch Ilva, as ranking sergeant, was meant to countersign — and did, because refusing meant questions, and questions meant the block — but not before reading it.

"They're not marching to capture the Stormseat," Ilva said. "They're marching to collapse it. The dispatch calls it 'permanent resolution of the gifted question.' Bring the mountain down on the covenant-stones, crack the seal-line all along the Spine. Vess has convinced herself the covenant isn't failing on its own. She thinks it's rotting the world, and that cutting it out will end the storms, the gifted, the hunts — all of it. One big amputation." Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, not quite. "She showed me the survey maps herself. Asked me if I understood what we were doing. I said yes, sir. I've been saying yes, sir for twenty years."

"And now?" Lena asked from the top of the ladder. I hadn't heard her come down. None of us had.

Ilva looked up at her for a long moment. "Now I've read your mother's name in Vess's files enough times to know what happens to people who say no. So I'm saying it here instead." She stood, and it was like watching a wall decide to become a person. "I brought the dispatch. The maps. The requisition ledgers. Everything you need to know where she'll be and when. And I brought myself, which I suspect is worth less, but there it is."

"The proof matters," Lena said slowly. "But you matter more. You know why?"

Ilva's chin came up, braced for accusation.

"Because the covenant needs five heirs," Lena said, "and we only have four." She counted us off with one hand, storm-grey eyes bright. "Me. Maren. Tam. And Corren keeps insisting his broken mark counts, though he's never once let me test it—"

"It counts," I said. "Probably. Mostly."

"—and the fifth seat has been empty since before any of us were born. The deep oath. The stone-oath." Lena came down the last rungs and stood in front of Ilva, this enormous armed woman who could have ended all of us with a whistle to her old comrades, and looked at her like Lena looked at everyone — straight on, no flinch, as if fear were a language she'd refused to learn. "It didn't go empty, did it? It just went quiet. It hid. It joined the hunt and marched under their banners for twenty years, keeping the bloodline safe the only way it could."

Ilva's breath left her in a rush. Twenty years of yes-sir cracking down the middle. "My grandmother's letters. After she died I found letters she'd hidden in a floorboard. About an oath. About a line that had to survive even if it survived wrong." Her voice dropped. "I burned half of them. I thought they were madness. I thought—" She laughed, short and terrible. "I thought I was the sane one."

"You're heir of the deep oath," Tam said solemnly, from his spot by the lamp. "That means you're like dirt. Good dirt, though. Strong dirt."

"Tamsin," Sela began.

"No, he's right," Maren called down from above, where she'd apparently been eavesdropping the way ferry-people eavesdrop on everything. "We've got storm, river, fire, and whatever Corren is — a complaint, mostly. We needed dirt."

And Ilva — grey, iron, twenty years of the hunt — put both hands over her face and cried like the soldier she'd never been allowed to be, and none of us pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is how the Veiled Court stayed veiled, and that era was ending whether the crown liked it or not.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up and the lamp was low, Ilva sat beside my pallet and cleaned her sword with the mechanical calm of someone putting her old life away piece by piece.

"Your file says you failed an oath," she said, without looking at me.

"My file's accurate."

"Lena's mother."

"Lena's mother." I watched the whetstone move. "Sworn to get her to safety. Got her to the Hollow eleven days too late to save anything worth saving. She died making a hiding place for a baby, and I stood outside the valley feeling the magic die at the tree line and telling myself there was nothing past it I could reach." I shrugged, and immediately regretted the shrug. "There wasn't. That's the joke. I genuinely couldn't have done more. And I've spent eight years failing to believe it."

Ilva set the whetstone down. "Twenty years I enforced the thing hunting my own blood. I signed seizure orders for children who hummed the same hum I do." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were dry now, and harder for it. "So don't tell me about failure, oath-keeper. But I'll tell you what my grandmother's letters said, the ones I didn't burn. She wrote: *the deep oath does not hold the mountain up. It holds the mountain together.* Cracks and all. Especially the cracks."

I lay there a while after she climbed the ladder, doing sums I wasn't good at: five heirs, one dying god-country overhead, an army with blasting powder marching on the Stormseat, and my own dark mark lying quiet against my forearm like a debt I'd stopped paying.

Then, somewhere between one heartbeat and the next, it wasn't quiet.

A warmth, deep down, under the scar tissue of eight years — a slow turn of something vast and patient, like bedrock remembering it was once fire. The deep oath, answering the deep oath. Or answering her.

I pressed my palm flat against the floorboards, against the stone beneath, and listened.

Underneath us — under the burned town, under the roads, under the whole crooked spine of the world — something turned over in its sleep and began, unmistakably, to wake.

Above me, Lena's voice came sharp through the floor: "Corren! Get up here — there are lights on the ridge road. Too many, moving too fast."

I got up. It hurt. I went anyway.
