## 15. The March on the Stormseat

### Lena

There is a particular sound a crowd makes when it stops being a crowd and becomes something else. I heard it for the first time outside the walls of Dunmarrow, when the Veiled Court came out of the dark in their hundreds — bakers and midwives and one extremely opinionated farrier — and did not scatter at the sight of us.

They cheered.

I want to be honest about this: my first thought was *please don't*. My second thought was *oh no, they're going to follow us*, and my third was the one that stayed, which was that all my life I had wanted to belong somewhere, and now I belonged everywhere, loudly, inconveniently, with baggage trains.

"They're singing," Tam said beside me. He was riding on the cart because his legs were short and his dignity was negotiable. "Is that ours? Did we get a song?"

"We got a song," Maren said grimly from the driver's bench. "That's how you know you're doomed. Nobody writes songs about people who survive."

The song, as far as I could make out through the dialect, involved a ferry, a flood, and a great deal of artistic license regarding my hair. I had stopped correcting the details around the third village. A woman who can call lightning learns quickly which battles are worth her breath.

We marched openly now. That had been my decision, made in the dark under the Veiled Court's city, against every sensible voice in the room — including Corren's, including mine, if I'm being fair, because there is a version of me that still lives in the Hollow and flinches at raised voices. But hiding was how the covenant died. Hiding was how my mother died alone with a letter she couldn't send. The gifted had spent generations making themselves small, and small things get stepped on.

So we walked down the middle of the road with our marks showing, and the hidden came up out of their cellars and their kitchens and their garrisons, and they fell in behind us like rain filling a dry riverbed.

By Dunmarrow we were two hundred. By the ford at Callow's Bridge we were six hundred. By the time the Oathspine rose ahead of us — grey and humming, the stones remembering what the world had tried to forget — I stopped counting, because numbers started to feel like responsibility, and I needed my hands free for other kinds.

Because the sky was wrong.

I noticed it first as a taste, copper at the back of the throat, the way air goes before a storm that never arrives. Then the light began to fail. Not cloud-fail — this was a bruised, underwater dimness pressing down from a sky that should have been bright autumn blue, and it followed us like a lid following a pot.

"Veyl," Corren said quietly, when I asked him. He rode at my left, always my left, some old oath-habit he'd never explained. "It's dimming faster. What we saw through the sky-gate was a realm starving slowly. This is a realm starving quickly."

"Since when?"

"Since someone started killing oath-bearers." He didn't look at me when he said it. He never did, when the subject was Vess. "Every heir hunted, every mark put out, every gifted child driven into a cellar — the covenant feels all of it. It's not just failing, Lena. It's being murdered."

Ahead of us, the column stretched out along the mountain road, six hundred people who had decided I knew what I was doing. Behind us, at the very edge of sight, dust hung over the lowlands where no wind should raise dust.

Vess was coming. She wasn't hurrying. She didn't need to. She had an army, a road, and a dying sky doing half her work for her — every day the light failed, more of our followers looked up and wondered what exactly they were marching toward.

Tam tugged my sleeve. "Lena. If the storm-realm dies before we get there—"

"It won't."

"But if—"

"Then we'll be there already," I said, "and it will have to die in front of us, and I don't think it wants an audience." He considered this with the gravity of an eight-year-old who sings fire to sleep, and apparently found it acceptable, because he went back to counting crows.

I looked up at the bruised sky, and I swear — I swear on my mother's burned letter — something up there looked back.

### Corren

An army of the hidden is a contradiction, and I have spent three weeks learning all the ways it fails.

Hidden folk do not march. They scurry, they drift, they stop at hedgerows and check over their shoulders with the reflexes of rabbits. Half of them won't cook in the open. A quarter of them flinch at uniformed men even when the uniforms are ours — and we'd had to invent uniforms, which is how the Oathspine March came to be wearing grey sashes dyed in a wash-tub by a retired laundress named Bess who took command of logistics without asking anyone's permission and has ruled our supply line ever since with an iron spoon.

But here is what the hidden also are: practiced. Every soul in that column has spent a lifetime moving unseen, lying well, reading a room for danger. When we camp, we vanish. When Vess's outriders probe our flank, six hundred people melt into terrain and leave the riders chasing ghosts and cold fires. I served in armies, before my fall. This one frightens me more than any of them.

And behind us, always, the grey iron.

Vess does not attack. I keep waiting for her to, and she doesn't, and that is worse. She shadows us at a day's distance, patient as winter, letting the darkening sky do her recruiting. Every night a few more of our followers slip away — not many, but some — and every night I stand watch and do the arithmetic of dread.

Tonight Lena found me at the fire line.

"She's herding us," I said, before she could ask.

"I know."

"Do you?" I turned to look at her. Firelight doesn't soften her anymore; it sharpens her, the way it sharpens a blade. "There's one road up the Oathspine to the Stormseat, and it narrows at the Grey Stair. If she lets us commit to the climb and then takes the stair's mouth behind us—"

"Then we fight her in a place where six hundred of ours are worth sixty of hers," Lena said. "Ilva worked the same sums. So did Maren. We're not walking into it blind, Corren."

"And if the sums are wrong?"

She was quiet a moment. Above us the sky pressed down, that terrible bruised dimness, and I watched her watch it — watched her feel it, the way I once felt my own mark, back when it lived.

"Then the sums were wrong," she said, "and we improvise. That's been the whole plan since the valley, if you recall. You wrote most of it."

"I wrote the parts where we run away."

"You've mentioned." Almost a smile. "Corren. Why doesn't she attack?"

I had been turning that question over for days, and I gave her the truest answer I had. "Because she doesn't want to destroy us. She wants us to lead her to the Stormseat. Ilva's proof said destroy — but I've been thinking about what destroy means to a woman like Vess. She doesn't want to burn it from a distance. She wants to stand inside it. She wants to put her hands on the thing that killed her sister and end it herself, personally, with witnesses."

Lena absorbed that. "Then we're not fleeing her. We're inviting her."

"That's one way to say it."

"It's the way that keeps me up at night," she said, "so let's both say it and go to sleep." She paused at the edge of the firelight. "Your mark's burning again. I can see it through your sleeve."

It was. For the first time in nine years, the broken oath-sign on my forearm glowed like a coal banked for morning, and every mile closer to the Stormseat it burned brighter, and I had not told anyone what else it was doing.

It was pulling. Like a compass needle. Like a dog straining toward home.

Some nights I was afraid to find out what waited there for a man who broke his word to the last signatory's daughter. Tonight I only said, "Goodnight, Lena," and watched her walk back through the sleeping camp, past six hundred hidden people who had decided, absurdly, miraculously, to be found.

### Maren

Let me tell you what nobody warns you about leading a revolution: the shoes.

Six hundred people eat. Six hundred people blister. Six hundred people have opinions about the route, the pace, the soup, and the theological implications of my cousin's flood at Brassen docks, which has somehow become scripture. There is a woman in the third hundred who believes I am a prophet. I have corrected her four times. She now believes I am a modest prophet, which is worse.

I was a ferry-keeper. I ran a boat. I had exactly one skill — water answers me, when it feels like it, mostly when I'm scared — and now I'm apparently the Admiral of a Walking Ocean, which consists of me, a cart, a child, and Bess's supply wagons.

Still. Still.

This morning a soldier named Doss — ex-crown infantry, defected at Callow's Bridge with his whole squad because his sister was Veiled Court — walked beside my cart for an hour teaching Tam parade drill, badly, both of them laughing. Last week a midwife stitched a stranger's hand and the stranger wept because no one had touched her gently since the proclamation went up. Small kindnesses, all down the column, passed hand to hand like bread. The hunt taught these people to be invisible. It turns out invisible people see everything, including each other.

I hate that it's working. I hate that I love it. Both things are true and I've stopped apologizing for either.

At noon we reached the foot of the Grey Stair, and everyone stopped pretending the sky was normal.

It hung over the peaks like a closed fist. The light was the color of deep water with no sun in it, and the air tasted of iron and ozone, and the stones under our feet — the Oathspine stones, the ones that hum — had gone quiet. All of them. At once.

"They're listening," Tam said. He'd gone still in a way I'd learned to fear; the last time he went still like that, a barn caught fire. "The mountain. It's listening to the sky."

"The sky isn't saying anything," I said.

"Yes it is." His voice dropped. "It's saying *soon*."

Behind us, far down the valley, the dust of Vess's host had stopped moving too. Even the hunters, it seemed, wanted to hear.

Ilva came up the line at a double march, sergeant-straight even in exile, and reported to Lena with the flat economy I'd come to respect: scouts confirmed the Stair's mouth unheld — for now — Vess's van eight miles back and closing, and the pass above split into two roads, the high one exposed and the low one flooded each spring by snowmelt.

"Flooded," I repeated.

Ilva looked at me. Lena looked at me. Even Corren, arriving late and breathing hard, looked at me, and I threw up my hands.

"Oh, fine. Yes. If it comes to it, I can hold the water back or let it loose. But somebody say it plainly, because I'm not volunteering to drown anybody on a maybe."

Nobody said anything. The fist of sky pressed down. Somewhere above us, in a starving realm none of us had ever seen, something said *soon*.

"Then we climb," Lena said, "and we pray we don't need you, Maren."

"And if we need you," Ilva added quietly, "you'll be the reason the covenant survives. Again."

I looked at the water-stain mark on my own wrist, faint as an old scar, glowing now for the first time since Brassen.

"Prophet," I muttered. "Modest prophet. Unbelievable."

### Ilva

I served Inquisitor Vess for eleven years. I know how she thinks, which is why the silence behind us frightened me more than drums would have.

Drums mean confidence. Silence means she is saving everything for one blow, and she has chosen where. I had stood on the Grey Stair's lower shelf that afternoon and looked down the valley, and I understood: she does not intend to catch us on the climb. She intends to let us climb, take the mouth behind us, and seal us on the mountain with her army below and a starving sky above. Starve us out. Watch the darkening do her work. She has always preferred sieges to battles; a siege lets the enemy defeat themselves while she remains blameless before her own conscience.

Her conscience. Eleven years, and I still cannot decide whether she has one or whether she built one, brick by brick, out of grief, the way you build a wall.

I reported all of this to Lena at dusk, and she listened the way she listens — completely, which is a rarer gift than storm-calling — and then she said the thing that has kept me loyal since the day I defected.

"Then we don't take the bait," she said. "We split the column. Half climbs tonight, fast and light. Half stays visible in camp with our fires lit, so Vess sees an army still asleep below. And when her van moves to seal the mouth—" she glanced at Maren, at me, at Corren standing shadowed at the fire's edge, "—we're not inside the trap. We're on its hinge."

It was a good plan. It was also a plan that required me to lead the decoy camp, which meant staying below, in reach of Vess's iron, among soldiers I used to command.

Lena saw me do the arithmetic. "You can refuse."

"Sergeant Ilva doesn't refuse," I said. "She reassigns herself." And then, because the mark on my wrist — stone-oath blood, the deep inheritance, the thing I carried unknowing through eleven years of hunting my own kin — had begun to hum in time with the mountain, I added: "One more thing. The stones. They've started answering me. When I put my hand on them, I can feel... weight. Load-bearing. Like the mountain is a building and I'm being shown the beams."

Corren spoke from the shadows. "Stone-heirs held the Stormseat's foundations. That's why your family ended up in the hunt, generation after generation — the crown kept its stone-blood close, without ever knowing why."

"Then the mountain knows whose side I'm on," I said, "and if Vess brings her siege engines to the Stair's mouth, I'd like to see what happens when the ground itself takes a position in this war."

For the first time in weeks, someone laughed — Tam, high and startled, and then others, the laugh running down the fire lines like a spark down a fuse. Against that fist of dark sky, six hundred people laughing felt less like hope than like defiance, which lasts longer.

I checked my sword, kissed the stone mark on my wrist out of a superstition I'd have mocked a year ago, and went down to build a camp full of lies and lit fires.

* * *

### Tam

The sky said *soon* again at midnight, and this time Lena heard it too.

I know because I woke up and she was standing at the top of the Grey Stair with her head tipped back, and her hair was lifting even though there wasn't any wind, and the mark on her arm was shining through her sleeve like a lamp behind cloth. I went and stood next to her because that's what we do now, me and her, when the scary things happen. We stand next to each other.

"Did you hear it?" I asked.

"Yeah," she said. "It sounds like my mother."

"It sounds like the storm-realm."

"Those might be the same thing right now." She picked me up so I could see over the rocks, which she pretends is for practical reasons. Down in the dark, torches were moving. Lots of torches. Grey iron torches, coming up the valley fast now, not slow anymore, the way wolves move when they stop stalking and start running.

"They're coming," I said.

"Ilva's decoys bought us the night. By dawn, most of us will be on the Stair." Her arm was warm around me, and humming, actually humming, like the mountain. "Tam. If it gets bad tomorrow — if the water comes, or the fighting — I want you to do the thing we practiced. Sing the fire down and stay small. Can you do that?"

"I'm not small."

"You know what I mean."

"I'm not," I said, "and neither are you. That's the whole point of us."

She was quiet, and then she laughed, one short breath, and hugged me quick the way she does when she doesn't want anyone to see. "When did you get wise?"

"Bess says I was born wise and everyone's been slow."

Above us the fist of sky cracked — just a hairline, just for a second — and through it I saw light. Real light, gold-and-green storm-light, like the realm was showing us its face through a closing door. And in that light, for one heartbeat, I saw fields. Dimming fields, starving fields, but fields, and towers, and something enormous moving through clouds that used to be alive.

Then it shut, and the dark came back, and down the valley the torches came on faster.

"Soon," the sky said.

"Soon," Lena answered, out loud, in the old words Corren taught her, the liturgy words, and the mountain stones all along the Stair lit up beneath our feet like a road of embers climbing into the dark.

Behind us, far below, the first horns of the grey iron sounded off the valley walls.

We started to climb.
