## 16. The Voice in the Storm

The Stormseat did not look like a throne. It looked like a wound.

We crested the last ridge an hour past dawn, and there it was below us — a ring of standing stones on a bald crown of mountain, black against a sky the color of old dishwater. Lightning moved inside the clouds without ever striking, like something pacing. The stones themselves were cracked, half of them fallen, and between them the air shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers over a road in summer, except this shimmer was cold. I could feel it from fifty yards away. A thinning. A place where the world's skin had gone papery.

"That's it?" Tam asked, from atop Corren's shoulders. He'd been riding there for two days, ever since his feet blistered on the scree, and none of us had suggested he walk. "It looks broken."

"It is broken," Corren said. His voice was rough. He'd been rough-voiced since the ford, since the wound that should have killed him and hadn't, though the bandages under his shirt told a different story every morning when Maren changed them and her face went carefully blank. "That's rather the point, lad."

Maren stood apart from us, arms wrapped around herself, watching the stones with the expression of someone looking at a bill she couldn't pay. She'd barely spoken to me in three days. Not since the confession — *I told Vess where we'd cross* — not since I'd looked at her and found nothing in myself generous enough to say. Ilva stood beside her, solid as her own name, and every so often I caught the sergeant glancing at Maren with something that wasn't quite forgiveness either but was at least company in it.

Four heirs. Five, counting me, though I still flinched at the word. And one child who sang fire to sleep, who had appointed himself our standard-bearer by virtue of being small enough to fit through gaps and stubborn enough to be worth ten soldiers in a crisis.

"Well," I said. "Let's go break into a dying god's house."

* * *

The climb down to the stone ring took less time than the climb up the ridge, which felt like a theological statement of some kind. Everything important in my life lately had been downhill and fast.

The shimmer thickened as we approached, and my hands started their old trembling — the storm-tremor I'd spent twenty-two years hiding in a valley where magic went to die. Except here, at the threshold of the Stormseat, the tremor didn't feel like sickness. It felt like recognition. Like my blood had been knocking on a door my whole life and had finally heard footsteps on the other side.

The nearest stone rose twice my height, veined with lines of pale light that pulsed slow and irregular, a heartbeat in atrial fibrillation. Carvings covered its face — spirals, lightning-forks, and rows of names so worn they were more suggestion than letters.

Corren put out his hand and stopped me before I could pass the stone.

"Lena." He said my name the way you say a word you've practiced. He'd been doing that more often since the ford. Since I pulled a storm out of the sky with my bare grief and nearly drowned the mountainside. "Before you go in. There are things about your mother I never told you. Things I told myself I'd tell you after, when it was done, because I'm a coward in addition to being a failure—"

"Corren."

"—and if you don't come back out of there, you deserve to have known them. All of them." His jaw worked. "She wasn't hiding you from the crown. That was part of it, but it wasn't the heart of it. The Hollow — magic can't enter it. Your mother chose it because she was afraid of what would speak to you. What speaks to oath-bearers at thresholds like this. She'd heard it herself, once. Near the end, when the covenant was already failing. And she came back changed. She came back *quiet*."

I stared at him. Rain — or something pretending to be rain — had begun to fall, fine and cold and smelling faintly of iron. "Changed how?"

"She stopped singing," Corren said simply. "For three days. And when she sang again, it wasn't her song."

The words went through me like a splinter seeking bone. My mother, whom I knew as a letter burned in a hearth and a handful of half-memories: hands over mine on a loom, a hummed lullaby with too many notes in it, the smell of rain on her cloak. Someone had worn her voice like a coat. And now, apparently, it was waiting for me behind the next stone.

"Charming family tradition," I managed.

"Lena—" Maren started, and then stopped, because she'd lost the right to my name three days ago and both of us knew it, and the look on her face was the loneliest thing I'd seen in weeks.

"I'll be fine," I lied, cheerfully, because that is a skill the Hollow teaches you whether you want it or not. "Watch Tam. If anything comes out of those stones wearing my face and asking for soup, don't feed it."

* * *

I stepped past the stone, alone.

The shimmer closed behind me like water. Sound changed first — the wind dropped away, and my own footsteps went soft, muffled, as though the mountain had decided my arrival was not worth announcing. Then the light changed. The grey daylight thinned and stretched until it came from everywhere and nowhere, and I understood that I was no longer quite standing in the human world, though I hadn't gone anywhere. I was in the seam. The hem of two garments stitched badly together.

And the storm-realm showed itself to me through the gap.

Not fully. Just glimpses, the way you see a room through a keyhole. But oh — what glimpses. Towers of cloud with foundations of lightning. Rivers of rain falling upward. A country built entirely of weather, vast and strange and beautiful, and dimming. Dimming like a lamp running out of oil. Whole provinces of that sky-country had gone grey and still, storms frozen mid-spiral like insects in amber, and where color remained it had the washed-out, apologetic quality of a dress dyed too many times.

*Starving,* I thought. Corren had said it once, and I'd filed it under poetry. It wasn't poetry. It was famine, at the scale of a sky.

"You came."

The voice came from everywhere, which is how I knew immediately that it wasn't her. My mother had had a particular way of speaking — slightly ahead of her breath, always, as though words were things she was chasing rather than releasing. This voice was measured. Patient. It wore her sound the way the thing in the stones wore the daylight: convincingly, if you didn't know better.

But I did know better, and it knew I knew, and it used her voice anyway. That was the first cruelty. Or perhaps the first honesty.

"You're not her," I said.

"No." No apology. The shimmer in the air gathered, coalesced, and became — almost — a figure. Tall, robed in moving shadow, with a face that kept resolving toward my mother's and then losing its nerve, sliding through a dozen women's faces like a hand shuffling cards. "I am what remains of the covenant's keeping. The part that remembers. The seal fails, Lena Amberdale, and I am failing with it, and I am *tired*, and I would like to show you something before you do whatever it is you've climbed all this way to do."

"Show me, then."

The seam around us rippled, and the glimpse widened.

I saw the human lands. Villages I knew, roads I'd walked. And overlaid on all of it, like a second map drawn in ash, I saw the silence spreading. Places where the covenant's thread had snapped and magic had simply — stopped. Wells that no longer answered midwives' songs. Hearth-fires that wouldn't hold a blessing. Gifted children born already muted, their gifts arriving stillborn, and never knowing what they'd lost because no one remembered there had been anything to lose.

"And when it's finished," the voice said, gentle as a closing lid, "there will be nothing left to hunt. No gifts. No signs. No witch-hunters, because witch-hunters need witches. Inquisitor Vess will grow old chasing smoke. Every child like you — every child like Tam — will live and die *ordinary*. Safe. Unremarkable. Untouched." A pause, tuned perfectly. "No one will ever burn again, Lena. Isn't that what you want? Wasn't that the dream of every night you spent hiding tremors in your hands beneath a leaking roof?"

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I'll carry longer than the scar this day left in me:

It worked.

For a long moment — I don't know how long; time in the seam moves like syrup — I stood in my mother's stolen voice and let the temptation pour over me like warm water. I saw it all. The Hollow again, but this time chosen, not sentenced. Maren running her ferry with no gift straining in her blood, just a woman and a boat and honest work. Tam growing up with nothing stranger about him than a nice singing voice. No lists of names. No grey iron at tree lines. No burning towns. No mother's letter in a fireplace.

Peace. The flat, gray, bloodless peace of a world with nothing left to say.

"All you must do," the voice murmured, "is refuse. Step back from the threshold. Let the covenant die unwitnessed. Walk away, and live. Everyone lives. Everyone forgets. Forgetting has kept you alive your whole life — hasn't it? The valley that forgets raised you. Forgetfulness is *mercy*, little storm."

My hands were shaking. Not the good shaking, not the storm-tremor. The other kind.

And I thought about saying yes. I want to be honest about that, because everyone who writes about moments like this makes themselves nobler than they were, and I refuse. I stood there in the dying seam between worlds, and I wanted it. I wanted the small life. I wanted the roof I mended and the neighbors who never quite learned my name and the safety of being nobody. I wanted it so badly my teeth ached.

What stopped me — what finally, blessedly stopped me — was not heroism. It was arithmetic.

"Forgetting kept me alive," I said slowly. "Yes."

"Then—"

"But forgetting is what killed my mother."

The figure went very still.

"The covenant died because people hid," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Oath-bearer by oath-bearer. Heir by heir. Each one deciding that safety was worth more than the vow, each one passing the debt down instead of paying it, until it landed on a woman with a baby and a bad choice. She hid me in a valley that eats magic. Do you know what that means, in your beautiful arithmetic? It means she hid me so thoroughly that when the covenant needed its signatory's daughter, *no one could find me*. Including me. Her mercy nearly broke the world. Your peace isn't peace. It's exile. Exile made universal. You're not offering to save the gifted. You're offering to make the whole world the Hollow."

The figure's borrowed face flickered — my mother, then a stranger, then my mother again, holding longer now, as though the argument had given it conviction.

"They will not thank you," it said, and the patience had frayed at the edges, showing something older and hungrier underneath. "They will curse your name. They will write ballads about the girl who could have let them sleep and wouldn't. The ones who suffer for your choice — and some will suffer, Lena, that is the price of every living thing — they will suffer *because of you*. Whereas if you refuse—"

"If I refuse, no one suffers because of anyone. They just suffer anyway, causelessly, forever, in a world gone mute." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "You know what I figured out, somewhere on the road with a disgraced oath-keeper and a furious ferrywoman and a child who sings fires to sleep? Belonging isn't the absence of danger. It's having somewhere to stand while the danger happens. You're offering me nowhere. You're offering everyone nowhere. That's not mercy. That's just the exile I grew up in, with the walls taken down."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

Then the figure leaned close — close enough that I could see my mother's eyes in its face, and in them, horribly, something genuine. Something that might even have been love, filtered through eons of failing machinery.

"Your mother refused too," it whispered. "At the end. When I offered her the same bargain. And look how that ended."

The words hit me like a fist. Because it was true, and it meant nothing, and it meant everything, and I could not tell which. My mother had stood exactly here, hearing exactly this, in exactly her voice, and she had said no — and she had died for it, or near enough, exiled and hunted and burning her own history in a hearth to keep it from grey iron.

"Yes," I said. "And look how *this* ended. Look at me. I'm standing at her door, and I'm not afraid of you anymore."

That was a lie. I was terrified. But I've learned something on this road about fear: it doesn't disqualify you. It's just the toll you pay at the door of everything worth doing.

I turned my back on the figure in my mother's shape and faced the broken ring of stones, the failing seal, the heartbeat stuttering in the rock. Behind me the voice rose — not angry, worse, *reasonable* — reciting every cost, every death, every name that would end up on my account. I let it talk. Grief could have its say. Fear could have its say. I had carried them this far; they'd earned their seats at the table.

But the table was mine now.

"Lena!" Corren's shout, from beyond the shimmer, ragged with alarm.

I turned — and the seam around me *tore*.

Not gently. The keyhole view of the storm-realm blew wide like a door kicked open, and through it poured not the starving greyness but the other thing, the thing I'd glimpsed underneath: the fury of a realm dying unjustly, the scream of frozen storms wanting to finish falling. Wind hit me like a wall. The stones blazed with light and cracks raced between them, and above the ring the bruised sky began to turn, slowly, like an eye opening.

And far off, down the mountain, down the winding road we'd climbed for nine days —

horns. Many horns. Grey iron, coming at last, with Vess at its head and murder in the order of march.

The voice in the storm laughed, low and almost fond, as the world came apart around me.

"So be it, little storm," it said, in my mother's voice, in every voice. "Come and seal it, then. Come and see what it costs."

The stones began to sing.
