## 17. The Breaking of the Grey Iron

*Ilva*

I had served Inquisitor Vess for nine years, and in all that time I had never seen her smile. So when I stood at her left shoulder on the ridge above the Stormseat causeway and saw the corner of her mouth twitch — a dry, bloodless thing, like a crack in old paint — I knew we had all of us already lost something.

"There it is," she said.

The Stormseat rose out of the mountain's shoulder like a crown someone had thrown away in anger: five broken spires around a hollow ring of black stone, and above it a sky that was not sky. It was bruised. It churned. Lightning moved inside the clouds in slow, deliberate patterns, like veins under skin, and every flash lit the causeway below us — a mile of stone bridge, narrow as a knife's edge, spanning the gorge that was the only way in.

The Veiled Court was massed on the far side of it. Bakers with pitchforks. Midwives with slings. Two hundred people who had spent their whole lives learning to be invisible, standing in plain sight at last, and looking terrified about it.

"They won't hold," said Lieutenant Brask, beside me. He was chewing something. He was always chewing something. "Two volleys of crossbow and they'll scatter."

"Mm," said Vess.

I should tell you what I did next, but to understand it you need to know what I'd been carrying since the night before. A stone, small enough to close my fist around, warm as a hearthstone no matter how cold the night got. Corren had pressed it into my hand in the dark before dawn, when half my company still thought me loyal, and he'd said only: *When the moment comes, you'll know. The stone will know first.*

It had been humming against my palm all morning like a trapped bee.

"Form ranks," Vess said. "Crossbows on the flanks. Pikes center. We take the causeway by noon and burn whatever's inside."

"And the heirs?" Brask asked.

"Alive, if the storm-child can be managed. The others—" She looked out at that bruised sky with an expression I had mistaken, for nine years, for conviction. I saw now it was hunger. "Dead serves just as well. Dead serves better. Dead things don't argue."

We began to move. And the stone in my fist began to sing.

* * *

*Maren*

Here is a thing nobody tells you about being an heir of the water-oath: rivers gossip. All water is connected — that's not poetry, it's plumbing — so when two thousand boots started down toward the causeway, I felt them through the snowmelt in the gorge like footsteps on a floorboard above my head.

"They're coming," I said. "All of them."

Lena stood at the near end of the causeway with wind already pulling at her hair, though there was no wind anywhere else. Tam clung to her skirt with one hand and held his little fire in the other, cupped like a moth he was afraid to crush. Corren leaned on a spear he could barely stand on, grey as old ash, insisting he was fine in the tone of voice men use when they are visibly dying.

"How long?" Lena asked.

"An hour. Maybe less." I looked down the length of the stone bridge, that beautiful, stupid, narrow spine of rock, and then at the gorge beneath it. Forty feet down, the river ran white and furious with spring melt. I had been listening to it for three days. It had been telling me things. Rivers are terrible gossips but excellent strategists, and this one had been saying, over and over, in its cold rushing voice: *break me loose and I'll do the rest.*

There was a sluice-gate at the head of the gorge, some ancient covenant-work, stone older than any kingdom. If I opened it — really opened it, all at once, with everything I had — the whole meltwater weight of the mountains would come down that channel and take the causeway's footings with it.

And me with it, probably. Water takes what water wants. That's the first lesson of the ferry, and I'd been dodging it my whole life.

"No," Lena said, when I told her. Just like that. No discussion. She had her mother's way of deciding things before other people finished proposing them.

"It wasn't a request."

"It wasn't a good idea." She turned and gripped my shoulders, and up close her eyes had lightning in them now, actual lightning, little white worms of it crawling through the grey. "Maren. You're not doing this alone. Nobody in this family dies alone. That's the whole point of having one."

*I'm not your family,* I almost said. I'd said it in Brassen, on the docks. I'd said it on the road six times. Somewhere past Cinderhollow I'd stopped saying it, and I hadn't noticed myself stopping, which is how you know a lie has died — quietly, without ceremony, while you weren't watching.

"Then here's the plan," I said instead. "You get everyone off the causeway. You buy me ten minutes at the gate. When the water comes, you make sure the storm keeps the hunters too busy to climb."

"And you?"

"I swim," I said, which was either a promise or a prayer, and I honestly didn't know which.

* * *

*Ilva*

The first volley went high. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever book gets written about this day: Sergeant Ilva gave the signal for the first volley and her hand shook, and four hundred crossbows sent bolts screaming into empty sky, and not one soldier asked why.

Vess asked why.

"Sergeant." Her voice carried the way winter carries — no heat in it at all. "Correct your line."

I corrected my line. I marched down the slope with my pike square behind me and the stone in my fist singing louder, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since midnight. Nine hundred soldiers on this ridge. Of those, how many had ever actually seen a gifted person hurt anyone? How many had only heard criers? How many, like me, had spent years hunting people and finding — what? Bakers. Children. An old woman whose crime was making cheese that never spoiled.

Half. I'd have sworn it was half.

The causeway fight began at midday, and it was short, because the Veiled Court didn't fight. They retreated. On purpose, in good order, drawing our pike squares onto that narrow stone spine where four abreast became three became two, packed shoulder to shoulder over forty feet of nothing. Brask laughed and called it cowardice.

Then the sky broke open.

Not rain. Lena Amberdale came walking onto the causeway's far end like she owned the weather's deed and title, and the storm came down with her — not wild, not yet, but *aimed*, forks of white light stepping down out of the clouds to strike the gorge walls on either side of the bridge, again and again, penning us in thunder. Men screamed. Horses — we'd brought horses onto a causeway, Brask's idea — went mad in their traces.

And through it all, I walked forward, because my plan required me to be exactly where I was going.

Brask caught my arm at the causeway's mouth. "Sergeant! Pull back and regroup—"

"Lieutenant," I said, "do you know what the Stormseat actually is?"

"A nest of abominations."

"It's the thing keeping the sky from falling on your mother's farm," I said. "And the Inquisitor means to break it. Permanently. No more gifted folk — and no more rain that isn't poison, no more harvests, no more anything. She told me herself, three nights ago, in her tent. I can show you the orders. Signed."

"You're lying."

"I'm defecting," I said, which had the advantage of being true, "and I brought proof, because unlike you I plan ahead."

He stared at me. Behind him, four hundred men were pinned on a bridge under a living storm, and every face I could see wore the same expression: men discovering that the story they'd enlisted under had chapters in it nobody read aloud.

Then I opened my fist, and let them see the stone — warm, glowing faintly, humming — and I said the words Corren taught me, the old oath-words, the ones every soldier's grandmother supposedly forgot:

*Stone remembers what kings forget. Stone keeps faith with the low and the lost.*

Half my company had grown up in stone-country. Half my company had mothers who sang that as a lullaby.

The pike square behind me stopped advancing. Then, man by man, like frost melting off a window, it stopped being a pike square at all.

* * *

*Maren*

Ten minutes. She'd promised me ten minutes, and Lena Amberdale delivered eleven, because she always overspends on the people she loves.

I ran the gorge path with the river roaring beside me, and when I reached the sluice-gate I put both hands on the stone and understood immediately that I had underestimated it. This wasn't a gate. It was a *seal*, covenant-work from the first age, and it was holding back a mountain's worth of meltwater the way a cork holds back a keg, and it did not want to move, and I loved it for that, and I asked it anyway.

*Please,* I said. Not with words. With the thing under words, the current under the current. *Please. They're going to kill my family.*

The stone groaned. Somewhere in its deep grammar, it considered me.

I thought about my ferry. Eleven years of hauling strangers across the Brassen water, charging fair rates, pretending I wasn't waiting for something. I thought about a frightened kid with fire in his hands falling asleep against my shoulder on the road, trusting me, that stupid, beautiful trust. I thought about Corren bleeding out at a ford because of me — because of a bargain I'd made in the dark, selling a piece of my family to buy another piece back.

Redemption isn't a feeling. Corren had told me that, hoarse, feverish, forgiving me before I'd even properly confessed. *It's a thing you do with your hands.*

So I did it with my hands.

The gate opened like a wound. The river came out of the mountain the way grief comes out of a person who has finally stopped swallowing it — all at once, colossal, beyond anyone's control including mine. I rode the front of it. There was no choice; it took me the way it takes everything. For one long white moment I was inside the water and the water was inside me, and I felt the causeway's footings crack like knuckles, felt the whole mile-long spine of stone shudder, tilt—

—and let go.

The bridge went down in three sections with a sound I will hear until I die. The center span folded into the gorge. Four hundred hunters on the wrong side of a new waterfall, and the waterfall growing.

Me, I went under. Of course I went under. Water takes what water wants.

But I am an heir of the water-oath, and I had finally, finally stopped fighting the terms. Down in the dark and the roar, I stopped swimming against it and *asked*, and the flood — my flood, my ridiculous magnificent flood — gathered me up like a mother picking up a child, and carried me, tumbling, half-drowned, laughing or sobbing or both, and laid me on the shingle downstream like a fisherman returning something too small to keep.

* * *

*Ilva*

I watched the causeway fall from the ridge, and I watched Vess watch it, and in her face was the first thing I'd ever seen there besides hunger: doubt. A hairline crack. The flood had taken her victory and her bridge and half her siege train, and the sky above the Stormseat had gone from bruised to blazing, and her sister's murderers were nowhere in it — just bakers and midwives and a sergeant who used to be hers, standing on the far side of a new waterfall with proof in their hands.

"Inquisitor," I said, loud enough for the nearest ranks to hear. "Your orders?"

She looked at the drowned causeway. At her own soldiers, half of whom had grounded their weapons. At the sky.

"Hold position," she said finally, and her voice was smaller than I had ever heard it. "Send riders back for the reserve. We... regroup."

"Yes, Inquisitor." I saluted, because I am a professional to the end, and then I turned and walked along the ridge, and one by one, quietly, in the language of sergeants everywhere — a glance, a shrug, a hand flat on a shoulder — I told my soldiers to stay exactly where they were and do absolutely nothing at all.

They obeyed. It was the finest discipline they ever showed.

Behind me, above the broken bridge, the storm gathered itself into a single column of white fire and struck the topmost spire of the Stormseat, and the whole mountain rang like a bell, and every remaining soul in Vess's army — every last one of us — heard, clear as a crier's bell, a woman's voice that was not a woman's voice say:

*The seal is failing. Come, or stand aside. There is no third choice.*

Vess stood very still for a long time.

Then she drew her sword, pointed it at the burning sky, and said the worst sentence I ever heard her speak:

"There is always a third choice. Bring me the reserve."

And far to the south, over the hills, I saw dust rising. A lot of it. Whatever was coming, it wasn't just riders.
