## 18. Confrontation at the Seal

The Stormseat was not what I had imagined, which is to say I had imagined nothing like it. I had imagined a throne room, maybe — something with banners and a dais and a convenient place for the villain to monologue. What I got was a hollow mountain, a cathedral of standing stones arranged in a ring wide enough to swallow the whole village I grew up in, and every one of those stones dying.

I could feel it the way you feel a fever in your own blood. The covenant-stones rose around me in a spiral, taller than the Oathspine itself at the center, and they had once sung. Corren told me that on the march up: *They used to sing, Lena. You'll know it when you hear it.* But what came off them now wasn't song. It was the last note of a bell after everything else has gone quiet — a hum so thin it was more absence than sound, and beneath it, a flicker. Stuttering. A heartbeat deciding whether to bother.

Frost crawled up the stones in patterns that weren't frost. Light moved inside them like fish under ice, dimming, flaring, dimming again. And above, through the broken crown of the mountain, the sky over Veyl showed through — not sky exactly, but the near-edge of the storm-realm, grey and guttering, like a lamp left burning in an empty house.

My mother's voice had spoken my name there once, through a sliver of sky-gate in a ruined waystation. I'd spent the whole march telling myself it was a trick, or grief wearing her shape. I still didn't know. That was the trouble with mothers who die when you're four: you'll take almost anything as their voice.

But that was later's problem. Presently, my problem had grey iron boots and was climbing the last stair into the ring.

* * *

I heard Vess before I saw her — not her footsteps, which were quiet as a held breath, but her soldiers' outside the walls, hundreds of them, a tide held back by Ilva's voice and Maren's flood and whatever Tam had done to their siege-lines with a song. The battle was happening without me. That felt right. This part, the stones seemed to want alone.

She came through the southern arch with her sword already drawn and her coat scorched from the causeway, and for a moment we just looked at each other across the dying ring, two women who had been chasing each other across half a kingdom and had never once, until now, been in a room together.

"You're smaller than the reports," she said.

"You burn easier than the rumors," I said, nodding at her sleeve. Wry. That was the register I'd chosen. Somewhere on the road I'd learned that if your hands shake, put the shaking into your mouth instead, and let it come out sideways.

Vess did not smile. I don't think she'd practiced it much. She walked into the ring of stones the way other people walk into churches — careful, unwilling, respectful of something she hated. Her eyes tracked up the central spire and I watched her do the arithmetic: this place mattered. Whatever it was, destroying it would matter.

"So this is the covenant," she said. "The great engine. The thing my sister died under."

"Your sister died in Ashford," I said. "Twelve years ago. A mob of gifted folk burned a quarter of the city because someone told them the crown was coming to take their children."

"And whose covenant taught them they were owed the world?" Vess's voice stayed level. That was the frightening thing about her. Not the cold — cold I could have handled. It was that she was *reasonable*. Every word arrived pre-weighed, pre-measured, like coins counted out by a moneylender who never made mistakes. "Every horror in the last century traces back to this. To gifts. To bloodlines that decide they are more than blood." She turned a slow circle, taking in the stones. "You think I'm here to kill you, Amberdale. I'm here to end the argument forever."

"The argument being whether some people deserve to exist."

"The argument being whether power belongs to anyone who hasn't earned it." She looked back at me. "You've seen what the gifted do when they're afraid. Cinderhollow. Brassen. Half the border towns. Fear plus power equals fire, always, everywhere. Your covenant didn't stop it. It just gave it a theology."

"That's not—" I stopped, because arguing the history with her was a losing game; she knew the atrocities better than I did. Of course she did. Grief is a scholar. It reads everything about the thing that killed you. "That's not why you're here," I said instead. "The history is the excuse. Tell me about your sister."

Something moved behind her eyes — fast, like a fish under ice, like the light in the stones — and then it was gone, and I understood I'd paid for it too cheaply. "You don't get to use her."

"Someone should," I said. "You've been using her for twelve years and look what it's built."

* * *

She came at me then, and I want to be honest about how it went, because the songs — if there ever are songs, and knowing Tam there will be, badly rhymed — will want to make it glorious, and it wasn't. It was ugly and short and mostly me running.

I had called a true storm at the ford three days ago. Wild, terrifying, magnificent — Corren's words, though he'd also spent an hour afterward teaching me how to breathe again. But a storm needs a sky, and the Stormseat had given its sky to Veyl, and the stones drank everything I reached for before it reached my hands. I got lightning twice. Both times she read it — actually *read* it, saw the gathering in my shoulders the way Ilva must have taught her during all those years hunting people like me — and stepped through the gap, and her sword found my ribs on the second pass and opened a line of fire along my side that dropped me to one knee among the failing stones.

The third time I reached for lightning, she kicked my hand out from under it and put her boot on my chest and her blade at my throat, and that was that.

Except it wasn't, because Inquisitor Vess, terror of the gifted, victor of a hundred hunts, looked down at me pinned and helpless among her enemy's holy stones — and hesitated.

Not from mercy. I watched her face while she did it. She was listening. The stones were singing again — thin, stuttering, dying, but singing — and my blood was answering, and where my body touched the floor of the ring, light was moving, and Vess stood in the middle of a miracle she had marched a war to destroy and couldn't quite make herself do the last small necessary thing.

"Why?" I asked. Blood in my mouth made it lisp. "You've won. Finish it."

"Because it's *asking*," she said, and hated herself visibly for saying it.

"It's dying," I corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters."

"Everything matters less than it thinks it does." But she stepped back. Off my chest. Blade still up, but back. "Get up, Amberdale. I won't kill a woman on her knees. My sister would be ashamed of me, and I've spent enough years disappointing her."

So I got up, holding my ribs, and we circled each other among the stones, and somewhere in the circling I understood that the duel was over and had been since the stairway. We hadn't come here to fight. We'd come here to finish an argument, and arguments are fought with whatever's nearest, and swords were simply what we had.

* * *

"Tell me how it ends," Vess said. "If I walk away. If your five heirs touch these stones and work whatever rite you've carried up the mountain. Tell me what I'm supposed to believe happens after."

"I don't know," I said, and her mouth curved at my honesty, so I kept going. "I know what Corren knows, and Corren knows liturgy, not outcomes. I know the storm-realm is starving — I've *seen* it, through the sky-gate, light going out like a village putting lamps down one street at a time. And I know that when it goes out entirely, everything on this side goes with it. Magic first. Then weather. Then—" I gestured vaguely at the concept of crops, rivers, breathable air. "The covenant isn't a leash on the gifted, Vess. It's a bridge. Bridges carry traffic both ways or they carry nothing."

"Lies from a realm that wants feeding."

"Then don't believe me." I turned and pressed my palm against the nearest stone, and my ribs screamed, and I ignored them, because I had decided something in the moment her boot lifted off my chest, and decisions are easier when you're bleeding; there's less of you left to object. "Believe your own eyes. You wanted proof the covenant is rot. Here's your chance at proof it isn't."

"What are you doing?"

"What my mother did," I said. "What she crossed a kingdom and married a mason and bore a daughter in a magic-dead valley to buy me time to be ready for. Showing someone the truth." I closed my eyes. "*Aeth veylora. Aeth veyl-sennet.* Come and see. Come and witness."

Corren had taught me the words in the burned town of Cinderhollow, sitting by a fire with a sleeping child between us, his voice low and patient like a man handling something that bit. *The old oath-bearers used them to show the covenant to the doubtful,* he'd said. *It costs. Everything true does.*

It cost.

The stone took my blood and my breath and whatever-it-is that makes me me, and for a long terrible moment nothing happened, and Vess took a step toward me with her sword rising — and then the ring woke.

Not all of it. Not the song it used to be. But light ran the spiral of stones like dawn running along a mountain ridge, and the broken crown of the Stormseat opened, and the sky-gate tore wide — wider than the sliver at the waystation, wide as a door, wide as a wound — and Veyl poured through.

I heard Vess stop breathing.

You have to understand what she saw. Not glory. Glory would have been easy to dismiss. She saw the storm-realm as it is now: vast, beautiful even in ruin, continents of cloud and towers of living lightning, and all of it dimming, guttering, going grey from the edges inward. She saw fields of storm-grass bowed flat by wind that no longer blew. She saw the creatures of that place — the veylkin, the bright ones, things I had only glimpsed and dreamed — huddled around the last few strong lights like villagers around a winter fire. Starving. Waiting. Dying politely, the way old people die in houses where nobody visits.

And she saw — because I aimed it, deliberately, the way you aim a blow — the small ones. The young ones. Whatever passed among the veylkin for children, clustered at the heart of the brightest remaining light, and one of them looking back through the gate at us with eyes like weather, curious and unafraid, the way children are until they learn.

"My sister was thirty-one," Vess said quietly. It wasn't agreement. It was the sound a wall makes before it decides.

"I was four when my mother hid me," I said. "I don't remember her face. I remember her hands, and a letter I wasn't allowed to read, and a valley where nothing magical could follow. She spent her whole life buying me exile because exile was safer than inheritance." My ribs were screaming again; I talked through them. "You lost a sister and you built a hunt. My mother lost everything and built a hiding place. Neither of us chose well. That's what grief does — it hands you a tool and swears it's the only one."

"And yours handed you a kingdom of starving ghosts and told you to feed them." She was staring at the small one at the gate. It had pressed something like a hand against the far side of the light. "Look at them. They're *children*, Amberdale. If I burn this place—"

"More of them starve. More of us orphaned. You'd become the mob from Ashford, just better organized." I let that sit. Then, because mercy that's only clever isn't mercy: "Vess. I'm not asking you to love them. I'm asking you to stop making more people like us. You know exactly what it costs to grow up hunted. Every child you take, every heir you break, you manufacture another version of yourself with a grudge and a gift and nothing to lose. Twelve years of the hunt, and has the world gotten quieter? Has it?"

The gate-light played on her face and for once I could read it, all of it: the sister, the mob, the years of ash and lists and grey iron, and underneath, so far underneath it barely qualified as visible, the girl who had presumably once laughed at something her sister said.

"No," she said finally. "It hasn't gotten quieter."

"That's the argument," I said. "That's the whole argument. You can keep killing the answer and the question keeps surviving. Or—" I spread my hands, ribs and blood and all, offering her the ruins of my certainty. "Or you let us try the other tool. One rite. One chance. If we fail, if the covenant dies anyway, you'll have lost nothing but the hour it takes to watch. And if we succeed—" I nodded upward, at the small one at the glass, at the grey fields, at the whole starving country of the air. "Then nobody's sister burns in a mob. Nobody's daughter hides in a dead valley. Nobody grows up learning that the cure for fear is iron."

Vess looked at me for a long time. Then at the stones, still trembling with the effort of the gate, still spending themselves on my borrowed miracle. Then, last, at her own sword, as if seeing it newly — as a tool, one tool, among others.

"If your rite fails," she said, "I will burn the Stormseat with everyone in it. Including you. Especially you."

"Fair," I said. "Ilva will hold you to it. She's very literal."

That startled something out of her that might, in better lighting, have been a laugh. She sheathed the sword. Turned to the gate. And Sergeant Ilva's voice was already echoing up the stairwell — *she's alive, she's alive, stand down, STAND DOWN* — and below us, out in the dark, the war began forgetting its reasons.

* * *

I sat down hard against the central stone afterward and did the breathing Corren taught me, and let the gate close gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. My side was a mess. My hands were steady. Funny, the order things settle in.

Footsteps on the stair — several sets, uneven, familiar. Maren's voice first, ragged with river-water and relief: "Lena? LENA, if you're dead I'm going to be so angry—" and then Corren, and then small quick feet that could only belong to Tam, and light from five different directions converging on the ring where the covenant-stones waited, humming their thin last note, holding their breath.

Holding *my* name, I realized. They'd been holding it the whole time.

The stones had one song left in them. And I understood, suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've known all along — why the seal had to be living, why my mother hid me from magic until I could choose it, why every road I'd ever walked ended here, in this ring, among these people, bleeding and laughing and afraid.

The stones were waiting for someone to give them a reason to sing.

I looked at my family, arriving.
