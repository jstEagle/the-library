## 19. The Reforging

**CORREN**

The Stormseat did not look like a throne. It looked like a wound.

We came up the last stair at dawn, the five of us and the army of the hidden behind us — bakers and midwives and a sergeant's worth of defected soldiers, all of them breathing hard in the thin air of the Oathspine. And there it was: a ring of standing stones on the crown of the mountain, each one tall as a church spire, each one dark. Not grey. Dark, the way a hearth is dark when the fire has been out so long you've stopped expecting warmth.

In the center of the ring was a basin of black glass, and in the basin, nothing.

"The seal," Ilva said quietly beside me.

"That's not a seal," I said. "That's an empty chair."

I had seen covenant-marks my whole life. I'd worn one myself, once — a small silver spiral on my forearm that burned whenever I kept my word and went cold whenever I broke it. It had gone dark eleven years ago, the night I arrived too late to save Sera Amberdale, and I had carried that cold mark like a stone in my boot ever since. So I knew what I was looking at. A covenant is not a document. It is not a law or a treaty or a piece of parchment with wax on it. A covenant is people who have promised something and keep promising it, day after day, until the promise becomes a place where other things can live.

And this place was dying because everyone who had promised anything was dead or hiding or hunted.

Except five.

Lena walked to the edge of the basin first. Of course she did. She'd been walking toward this since a peddler read her a proclamation in a valley that forgets, though she hadn't known it yet. She stood there with her hands at her sides and the wind pulling at her hair, and she looked into the empty seat like she was looking down a well she'd fallen into as a child.

"Corren," she said. "It's humming."

I put my hand against the nearest stone. Nothing. Then — there. A vibration so faint I might have imagined it, deep under the rock, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"It remembers us," Maren said from behind us. She didn't sound happy about it. She never sounded happy about anything, but lately her unhappiness had developed a certain tenderness underneath, like a thorn wrapped in wool.

Tam pressed his small palms flat against the stone and giggled — actually giggled, the little fool — and said, "It tickles," and for one absurd moment we all just stood there, four adults and a child, listening to a mountain remember how to breathe.

Then Vess's horns sounded below us, and the remembering was over.

* * *

**MAREN**

I want it noted, somewhere, by someone, that I did not want to be here.

I was a ferrywoman. I had a boat, a mooring rope, and a life that made sense: water goes where it goes, people pay their fare, you get them across. Nobody writes songs about ferries. That's the whole appeal. But then a storm-blooded girl and a disgraced oath-keeper walked onto my dock, and now I'm standing in a ring of haunted rocks on top of the world while an inquisitor marches three hundred soldiers up the mountain to kill us, and I'm trying to remember what exactly about that seemed better than the ferry.

"Tell me again what we're doing," I said.

Lena didn't turn around. "Reforging the covenant."

"Right. And reforging requires what, precisely?"

"Everything we have." Her voice was steady. Too steady. The steadiness of someone holding a door shut against weather. "The old liturgy says each heir gives their gift to the seal. All of it. Water, fire, stone, oath, storm. Poured into the seat until it fills."

"And then?"

"Then the seal lives again."

"Living how?" I asked. "Stones don't live. Stones sit there being stones. Someone has to—"

I stopped. Because Corren had gone very still, and Ilva had gone very still, and even Tam had stopped poking the rocks, and I understood, with the particular clarity that always arrives about ten seconds too late, what *living seal* meant.

"No," I said.

"Maren—" Lena began.

"No. Absolutely not. We find another way. We pour the gifts into the stones and we *leave*, and if the seal needs a heartbeat we'll find it a goat, we'll find it a volunteer, we will find it literally anyone who isn't—"

"Maren." She turned around then, and she was smiling. That was the worst part. After everything — the ford, the ambush, the confession I'd made with my throat full of broken glass, the weeks of her not looking at me — after all of that, she smiled at me like I was worth smiling at. "You've been carrying me since Brassen. Let me carry this."

"I haven't forgiven you," I told her, because it was true, and because if I said anything softer I would come apart right there on the mountain.

"I know," she said. "You will or you won't. Both are allowed."

That is the most infuriating sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I think about it still.

Below us, the horns sounded again, closer now. Ilva started shouting orders in a voice that could strip bark off trees, and the hidden army of the Veiled Court — the bakers and midwives and ferry-people and thieves who had spent generations learning to be invisible — began, for the first time in living memory, to be visible.

I took my place at the eastern stone, because the water-oath belongs to the east, and I put my hand on the rock, and I waited for a girl to decide to die.

* * *

**TAM**

The mountain talked to me.

Not with words. Words are for people who are slow. The mountain talked the way fire talks, which is pictures and feelings all mashed together, and mostly what it showed me was *before*. Before meant colors. Before, the stones had been full of light like embers under ash, and there had been voices singing in them — lots of voices, hundreds, all the old oath-bearers when they were alive and young and loud. They sang the same song over and over. It wasn't a pretty song. It was a crooked song, with wrong turns in it, but they sang it anyway, and the wrong turns were the best part.

Mama used to sing fire to sleep. Now the whole mountain was asleep, and nobody was singing to it, and its dreams were getting darker.

The lady with the grey iron was coming up the stairs. I could feel her soldiers like cold spots, like stepping in a creek in winter. Lots of them. More than the hidden people. The hidden people were scared. I could taste it on the wind, sour like milk going off.

But the stones weren't scared. The stones were just hungry.

I put both hands on my stone — mine was the southern one, fire's stone, and it fit my hands like it had been waiting for me specifically, which gave me a funny proud feeling in my chest — and I whispered to it the way Mama taught me. *Hush now. Hush. I'm coming.*

Because that was the plan. Everyone gives their gift to the seal. Even me. Even my fire, which was Mama's before it was mine, which was the only thing of hers I got to keep.

I decided I wasn't scared. I decided it really hard. Deciding things hard almost works if you do it enough.

Lena walked to the middle and stood over the empty black seat, and the wind went quiet all at once, like the whole sky was leaning in to listen.

* * *

**ILVA**

I have given orders in battle that sent good soldiers to die, and slept afterward. I have held a shield line while friends were cut down beside me, and eaten supper that night. This is not a confession of cruelty; it is the job, and I have never pretended otherwise. But when Sergeant Ilva of the Third Grey Iron Company turned her back on the company she had served for nine years and raised her blade against it instead, her hands shook, and I have not forgiven them for it yet.

Vess came up the causeway at first light with everything she had left. Three hundred foot, two companies of crossbows, the ram-carts, and her own cold eyes at the front of it all. I knew every officer in that column. I had drunk with half of them. Dossen on the left flank owed me money. Young Pell, carrying the standard — I'd taught him spear drill when he was seventeen and all elbows.

They thought I was a traitor. Perhaps I was. The word depends entirely on which side of the line you're standing on when the truth arrives, and the truth had arrived for me in a cellar in Cinderhollow: Vess did not mean to save the world from magic. She meant to kill the Stormseat itself and end magic everywhere, forever, and damn whatever starved on the far side of the sky when it died.

"Heirs!" I called, because the moment required ceremony whether or not any of us felt ceremonial. "Take your stations!"

Five stones. Five heirs. East, south, west, north, and center — and the center was the problem, the center was the seat itself, the center needed a heart that was willing, and hearts cannot be ordered anywhere. Hearts arrive or they don't.

I took my place at the western stone and laid my palm against it, and the stone-oath woke in my blood for the first time in my life. Nine years serving the hunt, and the inheritance they were hunting sat sleeping inside me the whole time, patient as bedrock. My father's father's fathers had been oath-bearers of the deep places, and none of them had known, and none of them had passed it on except in the blood, quiet as a coal under ash.

The mountain hummed through my bones. *At last,* it seemed to say. *At last, at last.*

Below, the first arrows went up.

* * *

**CORREN**

The battle at the rim of the ring lasted less time than it takes to tell. Ilva's defectors held the stair; the Veiled Court held the flanks; Maren pulled fog off the morning clouds like drawing a blanket, and the crossbows fired at shapes. Tam set the ram-carts' wheels alight, gently — he has learned *gently*, gods help us all, a child arsonist with a conscience — and the carts rolled backward down the slope trailing smoke, and Vess's host stalled at the tree line of stones like a wave meeting a seawall.

Which left only the center. Which left only Lena.

She stood at the edge of the black glass seat, and I stood behind her, because I had sworn an oath to stand behind her mother and failed it, and some oaths transfer. That's the crooked thing about oaths — the whole reason our book is called what it's called. An oath doesn't die with the person who swore it. It finds the next person foolish enough to pick it up.

"Lena," I said. "Before you do this. There's a thing I never told you."

"You've told me everything, Corren. Twice. Some parts three times."

"Not this part." I made myself look at the empty seat while I said it, because looking at her would have undone me. "Your mother didn't hide you in the Hollow to punish you. Or to protect you from the hunt. That was part of it. But it wasn't the reason."

"Then what was the reason?"

"The Hollow is the one place magic can't reach. Do you understand? Your mother was the living seal's daughter-line. Magic had claimed her before she could walk — claimed her the way it claims all of us, before we're old enough to be asked. She spent her whole life bound to something she never chose." My voice cracked on the next part, and I let it. "She wanted you to grow up *unclaimed*. To grow up whole, and ordinary, and free — so that when the day came, if the day came, you could choose it. Because the old covenant was forged by people who had no choice, and a covenant built without choice was always going to rot. That's why it's failing, Lena. Not because the signatories died. Because it was never freely given. Yours will be the first that is."

For a long moment she said nothing. The wind moved. Somewhere below, steel rang against steel, and above us the sky churned the color of a bruise, the starving realm pressing close.

"My whole life," she said finally, "I thought the valley was my prison."

"The valley was your cradle."

She laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one. "You're crying, Corren."

"Oath-keepers don't cry."

"What does yours say right now?" She nodded at my forearm, where the dead silver mark had been burning brighter every mile of the climb.

I pushed back my sleeve. The spiral was blazing white, brighter than it had been in eleven years, bright enough to throw light. "It says," I managed, "*yes.*"

She stepped down into the seat.

* * *

**LENA**

Here is what no one tells you about giving away everything you are: it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like taking off boots you forgot you were wearing.

I stood in the basin of black glass, and I reached for my gifts — all of them, the storm first, that wild thing in my blood that had terrified me since childhood, the tremor in my hands on windy days, the hum under my skin when lightning crossed the sky. I had hidden it my whole life. I offered it up like a woman laying down a heavy pack at the end of a long road.

And the seat drank it. And waited.

Maren came next, furious and grieving and generous all at once, pouring her river-self into the stone with tears freezing on her cheeks, cursing us the entire time in language that would have gotten her thrown off her own ferry. The eastern stone kindled blue-white. Tam laughed and cried and gave his fire with both hands, singing to the mountain the lullaby his mother must have sung him, and the southern stone bloomed orange like a sunrise. Ilva knelt at the west stone and gave her stone-deep strength with a soldier's economy, no waste, no complaint. Corren gave his oath — the broken one, the dark mark, eleven years of failure and guilt — and the mark flared so bright the shadows fled the whole ring, and I heard him gasp as if a weight he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine had finally been set down.

Four gifts. Four lights burning in four stones. And the seat beneath my feet stayed dark, and hungry, and waiting.

*Living seal.*

I understood then — truly, all the way down — what my mother had done. Every piece of it. She had not hidden me from magic. She had hidden magic from me, long enough for me to become someone who could say yes. In the Hollow I had mended roofs and learned names and loved a valley that never quite loved me back, and none of it had been exile. All of it had been the making of a choice. She had raised a daughter who belonged nowhere so that her daughter might one day choose to belong everywhere — and a chosen belonging, I saw now, is the only kind the covenant had ever lacked.

"Mother," I said aloud, to the mountain, to the sky, to whatever of her remained in the old liturgy Corren had taught me syllable by syllable. "You crooked, clever woman."

I spoke the final words of the liturgy. Not the ones Corren taught me. Those were the frame. These were the door:

*I was not taken. I was not commanded. I am not a sacrifice.*

*I am coming home.*

And I let go — of the storm, of the valley, of the girl named Lena Amberdale who mended roofs — and stepped fully into the seat, and the black glass rose around me like water rising, warm as bathwater, gentle as hands.

The last thing I felt, before the boundaries of *me* went soft and wide as weather, was not fear.

It was my mother's voice — the true one this time, not the counterfeit from the sky-gate — saying, clear across the distance between the living and the kept:

*Welcome home, little storm.*

The five stones ignited. Light went up the Oathspine like dawn arriving early, and the mountain sang the crooked song with all its wrong turns, and far away in a realm made of weather, something starving opened its eyes and remembered light.

And below us on the causeway, Inquisitor Vess stood motionless in the blaze, her sword arm lowering, her face turned up toward a sky that had just changed forever —

— and began, very slowly, to weep.
