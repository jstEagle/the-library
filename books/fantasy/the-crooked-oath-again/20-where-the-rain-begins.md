## 20. Where the Rain Begins

The first thing you should know about being a living seal is that nobody warns you about the paperwork.

There isn't any, technically. But there are *duties* — vast, wordless, tidal duties that arrive not as thoughts but as weather. Every dawn I feel the covenant-stones turn over beneath me like an old man shifting in bed. Every dusk I taste the boundary between realms the way you'd run your tongue along a chipped tooth: checking it's still whole.

I am everywhere in the Stormseat and nowhere in particular. I am rain falling on Brassen's ferry docks and I am light blooming in Veyl's drowned sky, and some mornings — if mornings even mean anything when you live inside two of them at once — I am both before my second cup of tea would have gone cold.

I still think about tea. That surprises people, when Corren visits the shrine and I manage to scrape enough of myself into one place to talk. *Don't you miss food?* he asked once, awkwardly, the way he asks everything. And I said no, mostly, because hunger was always a kind of wanting and wanting is what got me here — but then I caught myself lying, because I do miss tea. The warmth of it in your palms on a cold morning. The way steam curls off a cup like something alive and unhurried.

You can be a cosmic hinge between two realms and still miss tea. Nobody tells you that either.

* * *

Let me back up, because memory works strangely now and I've learned to hold onto things deliberately, like pressing flowers.

The reforging didn't hurt. That was the cruelest part, in a way. I had braced for fire, for tearing, for the kind of pain that makes stories worth telling around fires. Instead there was a moment of perfect quiet — the quiet of the Hollow, the valley that forgot magic, the valley where I spent nineteen years learning to be small — and then every storm I'd ever swallowed came home.

My mother's voice was there. Not the thing wearing her voice — that thing fled screaming into the deep dark between realms when the seal closed around me, and good riddance — but her actual self, or the echo the covenant had kept all these years, folded into the stones like a letter tucked into a book. She didn't say goodbye. She said *there you are*, the way you greet someone coming home late from the fields, and then she let go, and I understood that she had been holding the door open for me her entire life, and after.

The other heirs gave their gifts and stepped back. Maren poured her river-oath into the stones with tears freezing on her cheeks and then swore at herself for crying, which is how I knew she'd be fine. Tam sang the fire-song one last time, his small voice cracking, and the flames in the seal took it gently, like a mother taking a candle from a sleepy child. Ilva pressed her palm flat against the stone and whispered something in a dead dialect, an oath-keeper's farewell in the language of mountains. And Corren —

Corren tried to give his gift too. His mark had burned bright again since the Oathspine, restored by proximity to the covenant, and he stood there with his hand outstretched and his jaw set like a man about to take a flogging, ready to pour whatever was left of him into the seal.

It refused him.

The stones wouldn't take it. I felt them weigh him — his failure, his years of ash-grey silence, the night he arrived too late at my mother's hiding place — and then they weighed everything else. The ridge-road he'd walked with me. The liturgy he'd taught me word by word, patient as rain. The way he'd held the ford with a broken body so the rest of us could cross.

They gave it back. They gave it back *brighter*, and the covenant spoke through me in a voice like distant thunder, and it said: *Keeper.*

He's mine now, in the old sense. Sworn again, this time to the seal itself rather than to any single bearer. He pretends to find this embarrassing. He visits anyway, nearly every week, walking up from the village below the Stormseat with bread and gossip and, once, a tin of dried tea leaves he left at the base of the shrine without saying anything.

I made it last a month.

* * *

Veyl is blooming.

I know every inch of it now, the way you know your own hands. For three hundred years the storm-realm starved while its human signatories died out or hid — a sky-realm running out of light, its storms going grey and mean, its great singing towers dimming one by one until only the Stormseat remained, humming its failing note into the dark. The entity in my mother's voice hadn't been wrong about the dying. It had simply offered the wrong cure.

Now the light comes back like spring after a long cruel winter. The towers remember their songs. There are creatures there — I hesitate to call them storms, though that's what they are, living weather with long slow thoughts — who have begun to gather at the boundary, curious about the warm world beyond, pressing against the veil like cats at a window.

And on our side: rain.

Clean rain, the first in generations. You have to understand what rain meant before the covenant failed. Rain carried grit and static and a low sick hum that made gifted folk flinch and ordinary folk irritable without knowing why. Now it falls soft and sweet, and things grow that hadn't grown in living memory, and in the villages below the mountains they've started leaving barrels out to catch it, and calling it *seal-water* in some places, which embarrasses me more than I can say.

I felt the first rainfall land on ten thousand upturned faces at once. I heard a farmer in the lowlands stop in his field and laugh, just laugh, at nothing, at water. A child in Brassen put out her tongue to catch it and her grandmother told her the story of the girl who became the storm, and got half of it wrong, and I decided I didn't mind. Legends belong to everyone. That's rather the point.

Maren runs the ferries between realms now — real ferries, on a river that flows through the opened sky-gate, carrying traders and pilgrims and the occasional very nervous scholar. She claims she does it for the money. She has refused payment eleven times that I know of. When I mentioned this she threatened to stop visiting me, and we both knew she wouldn't, and that's Maren all over.

Tam goes to school. An actual school, in Cinderhollow, in a town rebuilt from the ashes of itself, where he sings the fire-songs to the children whose houses were lost and teaches them that flame is a neighbor, not a monster. He writes me letters. His handwriting is terrible. I keep every one.

Ilva is rebuilding too — the hunt, or what's left of it, into something else entirely. But that's her story, and she tells it better than I do, and besides, I promised her I'd stop narrating her life from inside the weather. It unsettles her.

* * *

Vess.

I should tell you about Vess, because the day the rain began clean, Inquisitor Serah Vess stood alone on the causeway below the Stormseat among her routed soldiers, and watched the sky do something it hadn't done in three hundred years, and broke.

I saw it from both sides. From within the seal I felt her grief hit the covenant-stones like a wave against a cliff — her sister, the mob, the fire, the fifteen years of iron certainty that magic was rot and rot must be cut out. And from the rain itself, falling on her upturned face, I watched that certainty come apart.

She didn't cry. I want that recorded accurately, because she deserves the dignity of it. She didn't cry. She stood very straight, the way she does everything, and she let the rain fall on her, and something behind her eyes quietly, completely collapsed, the way a wall collapses — all at once, with dust.

Her lieutenant — her former lieutenant; Ilva had peeled half her host away hours earlier with the truth — approached her afterward, cautiously, sword still sheathed, and waited.

"Stand down," Vess said. Her voice was steady. Her voice is always steady; it's the steadiest broken thing I've ever known. "All of it. The hunt is dissolved. Every writ, every bounty, every list." A pause. The rain drummed on her grey iron. "Burn the lists."

And then she did the thing I will never stop marveling at, the thing that taught me more about strength than the reforging did. She climbed the causeway to the Stormseat herself. Alone. Unarmed, because Ilva had seen to it her weapons stayed below. She walked into the hall of covenant-stones where I lived now, into the presence of the woman she'd hunted across half a kingdom, and she knelt.

Not to me. She made that clear, later, in her clipped way. She knelt to the stones — to the oath, to the idea, to the architecture of a peace older than either of us.

"I killed gifted people," she said, to the stones, to the air, to whatever she thought was listening. "In the name of a protection I invented because the truth was unbearable. Tell me what the penance is."

And I — the seal, the storm, the girl from the Hollow who mended roofs and hid her shaking hands — I answered her the only way I knew how.

I showed her Veyl.

I opened the veil, just a sliver, and let her see the storm-realm in its blooming: the singing towers, the living weather, the small bright creatures of lightning chasing each other through clouds like fish through water. I let her see what the hunt had been starving for fifteen years, what the rot had actually been — not magic, but abandonment. A covenant neglected until it nearly died, and thousands of hidden people paying the price of that neglect in blood.

"There's no penance," I told her, with her own steadiness borrowed back. "There's only work. Build instead of burn. It's slower. It's worse. Do it anyway."

She looked at me for a long moment — the first time she'd ever really looked, instead of cataloguing, assessing, hunting — and something in her face shifted, tectonic and permanent.

"The lists," she said again, "will burn tonight," and she rose, and walked out of my shrine into the rain, and began the long slow labor of building.

We're not friends. I don't know if we ever will be. But she sends reports to the Veiled Court now, honest ones, including the names of every gifted person the hunt ever took, so that they can be mourned properly. It takes her clerks months to compile each one. She compiles them anyway.

Grief doesn't vanish. It changes jobs. That's the closest thing to wisdom I've earned.

* * *

Here is what nobody warned me about, the true cost, the fine print written in water:

I gave up the ordinary.

I don't sleep. I don't eat. I don't feel cold, or sunburn, or the specific ache of a long day's physical work followed by a meal and a chair. I don't wake slowly on a grey morning with the smell of bread from below. My body — the shape of me that visitors see at the shrine — is a courtesy the covenant extends, a garment woven from stormlight and habit, and it is very good at looking like Lena Amberdale. It is not Lena Amberdale. Lena Amberdale ended at the moment the seal closed, and something vaster and stranger and, I hope, still recognizably myself began.

Some days that feels like triumph. Some days it feels like the Hollow all over again — a different kind of exile, a valley with no tree line to escape over.

The other night — nights still exist in the human realm, thank goodness, and I keep them faithfully — Corren sat at the shrine until well past dark, not talking, the way he does, and finally he said: "Do you regret it?"

And I thought about lying, because he looked tired and worried and I love him in the fierce sibling-way you love the family you built yourself, and tired worried people deserve kindness.

"No," I said. And then, because honesty deserved better than a tidy answer: "Yes. Both. Ask me tomorrow and the ratio will be different."

He nodded slowly, the way he nods when an answer is true enough to be uncomfortable. Then he said, "When I was sworn to your mother, she told me something once. She said the covenant asks everything except the one thing that matters — it never asks you to stop being a person. Even at the center of it. Especially there."

"She sounds like she'd have liked you," I said.

"She'd have found me tedious," Corren said, and almost smiled, which for Corren is a religious experience, and we sat together in the rain-sweet dark, the disgraced keeper and the living seal, missing tea.

* * *

Which brings me to why I'm telling you all this now, today, on this particular morning of my strange doubled existence.

Because this morning, the covenant stirred beneath me with a feeling I'd learned to recognize during the long march — that faint wrongness in the stones, like a tooth with a chip, like a name mispronounced.

Something is crossing the boundary. Small. Frightened. Gifted — genuinely gifted, wild and untrained, the kind of power that flickers like a struck match in a wind.

And behind it, following the mountain road from the north, moving fast and heavy: riders. Not crown riders — the hunt is dissolved, its lists burned, its writs ash. These carry no insignia I know. Grey cloaks. Iron masks. And they move like people who believe no law applies to them anymore, which is, historically, the most dangerous kind of people there are.

The hunt is dead. But hunts, I'm learning, are like oaths. Someone is always crooked enough to swear one again.

Below the Stormseat, on the road, a figure stumbled out of the tree line into the rain — young, soaked, blazing faintly at the edges like a lantern about to gutter — and looked up at my shrine with the expression I knew too well, the expression I wore for nineteen years in a valley that forgot magic.

The look of someone who belongs nowhere, running toward the only place that might.

I gathered myself — all of myself, storm and seal and girl — and went down to meet them.

Rain begins somewhere. Today, it begins with me.
