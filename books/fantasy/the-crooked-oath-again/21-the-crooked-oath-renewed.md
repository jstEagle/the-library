## 21. The Crooked Oath Renewed

I have kept oaths for most of my life, and I can tell you this: the easy part is swearing them. The hard part is the morning after, and every morning after that, when the words have to be lived instead of spoken.

So it was with the covenant. The reforging took one night. The living with it has taken a season, and counting.

* * *

The Hollow opened its roads on the first day of autumn, and I was there to watch it happen, mostly because Lena asked me to be and partly because I didn't trust anyone else to stand at the tree line and not flinch.

For as long as anyone could remember — longer than memory, really; the forgetting was baked into the place like lime into mortar — the valley had been a held breath. Magic died at the tree line. Storms passed overhead like strangers who wouldn't meet your eye. And the people of the Hollow had built their whole quiet lives on that silence, the way you build a house on a fault line and then agree, all together, never to mention the tremors.

Now the silence was over. The covenant lived again, and where the covenant lived, magic followed, and magic did not respect property lines or old agreements about minding one's own business. The first storm after the reforging came down into the valley itself, walked right past the tree line like it owned the deed, and stood over the Amberdale farmstead and rained.

Just rained. Clean rain, warm rain, the kind that makes farmers weep into their hats. But I watched the Hollowfolk come out of their doors and stare up at it like it was a wolf walking through their kitchen.

"They're frightened," Lena said beside me. She'd been different since the Stormseat — not older exactly, though she carried herself like someone who had been given a very large room and was still learning where the walls were. "They have a right to be."

"They have a right," I agreed. "Doesn't mean they'll use it well."

"No," she said. "It usually doesn't."

That's the thing about my oath-sister — she sees people clear, without cruelty and without mercy getting in the way of each other. It's a rare trick. I've spent my whole life swinging between the two.

The valley council met three times before they worked up the nerve to send word to her shrine. The message, when it finally came, was written in the careful hand of a man who had known Lena since she was small enough to ride on his shoulders, and it said, in essence: *We do not know what you are now, but we know whose daughter you were, and we remember how your mother mended our roofs in the years before she left, and we would speak with you, if speaking is still something you do.*

Lena read it twice and laughed until she cried, which I have learned is simply what grief does now when it has nowhere urgent to be.

"Roofs," she said, wiping her eyes. "Of all the things to be remembered for."

"It's a good thing to be remembered for," I said. "I've been remembered for considerably worse."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about an oath-mark coming back to life: it hurts.

Mine went dark nineteen years ago, on a rain-slick road outside a valley I couldn't enter, when I arrived too late to keep the only vow that ever mattered. For nineteen years it sat under my skin like a coin left in a dead man's pocket — cold, mute, a reminder of exactly what I was worth. Then Lena stepped into the seal, and the covenant woke, and every oath-keeper in the world felt their mark answer whether they wanted it or not.

Mine lit up like a brand. I screamed. Tam thought it was hilarious.

These days it burns steady, a pale gold thread along my forearm, warm as a hand on my shoulder. When I'm near the shrine it brightens. When I fail at something — and I still fail, more often than I'd like, at patience, at forgiveness, at keeping my mouth shut while Maren explains sailing to me as if I hadn't grown up on rivers myself — it dims a little, like a dog turning its back. Ilva says that's not how covenant-marks work. Ilva says they don't judge. I've decided Ilva doesn't know everything, which took some doing, because for a while there I was fairly sure she did.

I could have gone anywhere, after. That's the strange gift of it. Oath-keepers are needed everywhere now — the Veiled Court alone could use a hundred of us, and half the realm's garrisons want sworn men who answer to something older than a crown. Corren the disgraced, Corren the too-late, suddenly had offers.

I stayed in the Hollow. Near the shrine. Where the mark burns brightest and the work is smallest: carrying shingles, settling disputes between farmers whose fences have opinions about each other, sitting with Lena on the long evenings when being a living seal means being a woman who cannot quite sit inside her own skin.

"You could be a general," she told me once. "Or a kingmaker. People would follow you."

"People followed me once," I said. "Off a cliff, more or less."

"That was Vess's doing."

"Some of it was mine." I turned my cup on the table. "Besides. Someone has to mend the roofs."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed, and the storm-light in her eyes — which is real now, a faint grey flicker behind the brown, like weather deciding — softened into something that was just Lena, just my friend, just a woman who grew up hammering nails because her hands shook and hammering made them steady.

"Roofs," she said. "You and my mother both."

Best company I've ever kept, if I'm honest. Dead woman and living seal. Between the two of them I'm learning what an oath actually is. It isn't the words. It's showing up. Every morning after.

* * *

Maren runs ferries now. Real ones.

When the sky-gate stabilized — Ilva's engineers would say *anchored*, Maren says *tamed*, and Maren should know, because she's the one who talked the river-side of it into behaving — someone had to carry traffic between the realms. The storm-realm of Veyl, no longer starving, has turned out to hold more than storms: islands of cloud-stone, rivers that run silver and uphill, folk of wind and thunderlight who had nearly forgotten there was ever a world next door. They're curious about us. We're curious about them. Somebody has to pole the boat.

Maren volunteered before anyone asked, which surprised exactly no one who knew her, because Maren's whole life is a ferry: standing between two shores, hauling people across, pretending she'd rather be anywhere else.

"I hate this job," she told me last week, poling us through the gate while the clouds parted around the hull like a crowd making room. A thunderling child leaned over the rail of her ferry and tried to eat a compass. "I hate the weather. I hate the passengers. I hate that the water up here tastes like lightning."

"You love it," I said.

"I will deny that under oath."

"You'd swear it falsely, too. I've seen your oath-face."

She jabbed the pole at me hard enough to rock the boat, and the thunderling shrieked with delight, and Maren — sour, furious, frightened Maren, who flooded a dock to save two strangers and hated herself for one bad night and chose sacrifice anyway when the causeway needed holding — grinned like a girl, quick and crooked, before she could stop herself.

She still carries the guilt. She'll tell you she doesn't. She told Lena she was sorry eleven times before Lena stopped counting, and the twelfth time Lena said, "If you apologize again I'm going to make you fix the shrine roof," and Maren fixed the shrine roof, unasked, twice a year, ever since. That's her penance and her love language and she'd gut me for writing this down. Fortunately I'm writing it in a book nobody in this valley can read.

Tam can read it. That's the problem with teaching children letters. They get ideas.

* * *

Tam goes to school.

Let me say that again, because I still can't quite believe it: the boy who sang fire to sleep in the ashes of Cinderhollow, who slept in my coat for a month and flinched from every grey cloak on the road, sits in a schoolhouse in Brassen with forty other children, learning his sums and his letters and — this part the schoolmaster doesn't advertise — how to sing to the hearth so it burns even and sweet all winter.

The Veiled Court arranged it. After the hunt dissolved, the hidden gifted had to decide what hiding had been *for*, and their leader — a midwife named Sorrel with iron-grey braids and a voice like a closing door — decided it had been for this: a generation that wouldn't need to hide. So there are schools now, quiet ones, in cellars and orchards and one converted lighthouse, where children learn to carry their gifts the way you teach a child to carry eggs. Carefully. With both hands. Understanding that some will break anyway.

Tam comes home to the Hollow on feast-days. He's shot up like a weed. His voice is changing, and when it cracks mid-song the candles gutter, which he finds mortifying and everyone else finds wonderful. Last visit he sat with Lena by the shrine for an hour, just talking, and when he left she said, quietly:

"He was the first one who needed me. Before I knew what I was for."

"He still needs you," I said. "He just needs you less loudly. That's what growing up is."

"Did you grow up?"

"I arrived. There's a difference."

* * *

Ilva rebuilds. Of course she does. Give that woman rubble and a deadline and she'll hand you back a city.

Vess's host scattered after the Stormseat — some surrendered, some slunk home, a hard core stood down in the field when Ilva told them the truth about what their Inquisitor meant to do at the seal. Ilva herself took a wound that should have slowed her and didn't, and now she runs the reconstruction of the border towns the war burned, Cinderhollow first among them. She sends reports like other people send letters: precise, unsentimental, and somehow, between the lines, full of feeling.

Last month she wrote: *Cinderhollow walls raised. Orphanage sited on the old chapel ground. Tam's suggestion. He cried at the ceremony and denied it. Recommend we build him a monument now while he's still embarrassed enough to be honest.*

And beneath the official seal, smaller: *My stone-oath blood served the wrong banner for four generations. I intend to spend the rest of mine paying that debt. Tell the seal-shrine I said hello. Formally, if you must.*

Ilva. Enemies discovering shared inheritance, and then discovering friendship, which is harder and better.

* * *

But the valley. I started with the valley, and the valley is where the true work is, because realms can be reforged in a night but a people take longer.

The Hollowfolk came to the shrine in ones and twos, that first autumn. Old Hesta, who taught Lena her letters, brought bread and stood at the edge of the shrine-stones like they might bite. The miller's boys dared each other closer and closer until Lena, exasperated, called out, "If you're going to gawk, at least help me stack these offerings," and after that they were underfoot every week. The councilman who wrote the careful letter came in person, hat in his hands, and said, "Your mother mended my roof the winter my wife died. I never thanked her. I'm thanking you."

And slowly — the way thaw works, invisible and then all at once — the valley decided who it was.

Not the valley that forgets. Not the valley that hides. Something new: a valley that remembers, and chooses what to do with the remembering. They keep the festival of the Quiet Years now, once a year, not to mourn the silence but to honor the people who survived inside it. And then they open the gates and let the storms walk in, and the children run out under the lightning like it's a parade, because to them it always was going to be.

There's a plaque on the shrine now. Lena let them put it up only after they agreed to wording she approved, which took four drafts. It reads:

*Here stands the seal of the covenant, freely chosen. She belongs here. So do you.*

"So do you," I said to her, the evening they set it. "You know that? You actually belong somewhere. All that running, and it ends with a plaque."

"Careful," she said. "Or I'll put you on the next draft."

* * *

Tonight I sit on the shrine steps with my mark burning gold against the dark, and the storm-walkers pass overhead on their rounds — great slow shapes of thunderlight, tending the weather like gardeners — and one of them dips low over the valley, the way they do, checking on the seal.

Lena sits beside me, tired in the good way, the way of a day's honest work. Below us the Hollow glows with lamplight. Somewhere Maren's ferry crosses between worlds. Somewhere Tam misplaces his homework in a house that won't burn it. Somewhere Ilva raises another wall.

"Corren," Lena says. "Do you ever miss it? Being nobody?"

I think about it honestly, which is the only way she accepts answers.

"No," I say. "I was never nobody. I was a somebody who'd failed. This is better."

"And now?"

"Now I keep a small oath, every morning. Show up. Mend what's broken. Stay."

She nods, satisfied, and looks out at her valley — hers, truly, at last — and neither of us speaks for a while, because we've learned that too, from each other. Silence shared is not exile. It's just rest between words.

Then, far off down the opened road, past the tree line where the forgetting used to begin, a lantern bobs toward the shrine through the dusk. Small. Hurried. And beneath the ordinary dark of the night, faint but unmistakable, I feel the mark on my arm shift — brighten — the way it does when someone gifted draws near.

Lena feels it too. She stands, shading her eyes.

"Someone's coming," she says.
