## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Hours

The bells of Saint Vellum's began at dusk, as they always did, and Sebastian Winterbourne flinched, as she always did.

She had learned to disguise it — a small hitch in her step, a hand raised to tuck hair behind her ear that didn't need tucking. Seven years of practice had made the flinch nearly invisible. Nearly. Old Marta across the landing still watched her sometimes with that soft, puzzled look, the look of a woman trying to remember where she'd put something and beginning to suspect someone else had taken it.

*Why?* Sebastian asked herself, for the ten-thousandth time, as the bronze voice rolled down over the crooked rooftops of Vellharrow. *Why bells?*

There was no answer. There was never an answer. There was only the place inside her chest where the answer should have been, smooth and scarred over like a healed wound, itching in cold weather.

She pulled her hood up and went out into the evening.

Vellharrow at dusk was a city pretending to be honest. Lamplighters worked their way down the Serpentine with their long brass poles, lighting wicks that the Crown taxed by the flame. The smell of frying eel from the Bridge Street stalls fought with the river stink and won, mostly. Somewhere a drunk was singing the coronation anthem off-key, and two Watchmen were letting him, because even the law got tired near suppertime.

Sebastian moved through all of it like water through a net — present everywhere, caught by nothing.

Her trade required it. Memory-thieving was not, despite what the penny dreadfuls claimed, a matter of dark alleys and chloroform rags. It was a matter of *permission*, mostly. Of being the kind of person people opened doors for. A widow's granddaughter come to help sort linens. A notary's clerk with ink-stained fingers and a forged seal. Tonight, she was Sister Ameline of the Little Hospice of Saint Corvin, sent to sit with the dying, and the gray habit under her cloak itched abominably because she'd bought it secondhand from a deacon who should have known better than to sell vestments to a woman with her eyes.

The house on Candlemaker's Row was narrow and leaned left, like a man listening hard for bad news. She knocked the way clergy knock — three slow beats, apologetic.

The door was opened by a man in his fifties with the particular hollowness of someone who had been crying in a room where crying wasn't allowed. "Sister. Thank you. Thank you for coming." He stepped back to let her in, and Sebastian noted, professionally, the locket.

It hung at his throat on a leather cord, old silver gone black at the hinges, and it *hummed*. Not audibly — nothing so crude. It hummed against her senses the way a struck tuning fork hums against the teeth, a thin bright thread of stored magic in a city where magic had been contraband for longer than most people had been alive. Enchanted goods were confiscated on sight. Enchanted goods were burned in the square on Feast Days, while crowds cheered and inquisitors in dove-gray robes looked bored.

This man was wearing one around his neck like a relic.

"Your mother," Sebastian said gently, following him up the stairs. "Tell me about her."

He told her everything. They always did. Grief made people generous; they wanted so badly to give away what they couldn't carry. Her name had been Odile. She'd been a lacemaker, then a cook, then, after the fire, a woman who sat by windows. She had raised Tam alone after his father drowned in the harbor works. She had a laugh like a cart going downhill. And forty years ago, on a night neither of them ever spoke of directly, she had paid a hedge-witch — before the outlawry, when such things cost silver instead of your hands — to take her single happiest hour and keep it for her.

"She said she wanted to know it was safe," Tam whispered, outside the sickroom door. "That somewhere, it was safe, even if she couldn't have it back. She used to touch the locket when things were bad. Like checking a purse."

"And now?"

"Now she's been asking for it. All week. Saying she wants to *wear* it, whatever that means, she's wearing it, but she keeps saying it anyway—" His voice cracked. "Sister, I don't know what she wants. I don't know how to give it to her."

*I do,* thought Sebastian Winterbourne, and hated herself a little for the clarity of it.

* * *

Odile lay small beneath the blankets, a bird-boned woman with a face like crumpled paper, and her eyes found Sebastian the moment she entered — found her and *held*, with a sharpness that made Sebastian's professional smile falter.

"You're not a sister," Odile said.

"I'm—"

"Hush." The old woman's hand emerged from the blankets, thin as a bundle of kindling, and beckoned. "Come here where I can see you. Yes. Oh, yes. You've got the look. My grandmother had the look. You're one of them that can *hold* things." Her eyes went to her son, dozing upright in the corner chair, and back. "Don't lie to a dying woman, girl. It's rude, and we haven't time."

Sebastian stood very still. In seven years of careful work, no mark had ever seen through her this fast. "What is it you want, mistress?"

"What I paid for." Odile touched the cord at her own neck — Tam must have moved it to her hours ago, or she'd demanded it back. "Forty years I let that hour sit in the dark like money in a mattress. And now I'm leaving, and I find I'm a coward about it. I want to *go* holding it. Do you understand? Not the memory of having been happy. The happiness itself. One more time, warm, in my own head, where it belongs." Her grip closed on Sebastian's wrist with shocking strength. "Can you open it?"

Opening a memory-vessel without destroying its contents was delicate work. Opening one and pouring it into a dying mind was harder. Doing both while a son dozed six feet away, in a city where the penalty for unsanctioned extraction was the *hollowing* — the public scraping-out of every memory the inquisitors deemed excessive, which was all of them — was the kind of job Sebastian would normally walk away from.

But the old woman's eyes were wet and furious and alive, and Sebastian had always been weak in exactly that one way.

"Yes," she said. "But it will cost you your last coin, and I mean that plainly. Dying women shouldn't pay thieves."

"Dying women," Odile said, "are the only ones who know what anything's worth."

* * *

The work took an hour, and it was the finest hour of craft Sebastian had done in years, though no one would ever know it.

She sat with Odile's hand in hers and sank into the locket the way a diver sinks into green water. The vessel was old work, hedge-craft, crude but honest — a single hour folded and sealed with wax and willow-ash, kept warm all these decades by the body heat of the woman who wore it. Inside, it was June.

Inside, it was a garden behind a lacemaker's shop that no longer existed, and a man with sawdust in his hair — not Tam's father; someone earlier, someone young — was laughing so hard he had to sit down among the beans, and a girl of nineteen with Odile's exact eyes was laughing with him, and the light came through the apple leaves in coins, and the whole hour was soaked through with the specific, unreproducible sweetness of a happiness that does not yet know it will end.

Sebastian had lifted hundreds of memories. She had learned, early, not to feel them — to hold them like hot pans, gloved, at arm's length. But this one got past her gloves. This one got in under her ribs, because it was so *ordinary*, because it was beans and sunlight and a boy laughing, because it was the kind of hour that happens to everyone and is remembered by no one unless someone pays a witch to save it.

*This is what they take from us,* she thought, in the dark behind her own eyes. *Not crowns. This.*

She poured it gently along the thread between the locket and the dying mind, easing it past the scar-tissue of age and morphine and approaching death, and she felt Odile receive it — felt the old woman's whole body soften, felt her breath change, felt the June light bloom somewhere behind those clouded eyes.

"Oh," Odile breathed. Her face, for a moment, was nineteen. "Oh, you *fool*, Aldous, you'll crush the beans—"

Then, softer, to Sebastian, or to no one: "There you are. There you are. I knew you were safe."

She died before midnight, smiling, her thumb resting on the black silver of the locket, and Sebastian sat with her until the end because some part of the trade — the part that never appeared on any price list — required it.

Tam wept in the kitchen afterward and pressed a purse into her hands that weighed far too much, and she took only the coin they'd agreed on and left the rest on the table when he wasn't looking, and slipped out the door into the blue-black cold of the Vellharrow night with the empty locket in her pocket, humming faintly, hollow as a church.

* * *

The night market ran in the flooded foundations of the old Chantry district, which everyone pretended was a coincidence of geography and no one believed for a second.

To reach it you went down. Always down — through a fish-cellar, through a door that was legally bricked over and practically not, down stairs slick with river-damp, into vaults where the Chanters had once sung the binding-litanies before the Crown outlawed singing them, outlawed the litanies, outlawed the *words*, outlawed finally the very idea that words could be load-bearing. The water had never been pumped out. It stood ankle-deep in the corridors, black and mirror-still, and the lamplight of the market doubled itself in it beautifully, so that walking the Night Market was like walking through a city that existed only upside-down.

Sebastian loved it and could not have said why. That was the shape of her whole life now: loves with no roots, griefs with no wounds, a flinch at bells with no story attached. She was a house with the furniture arranged just so and every portrait taken off the wall.

"Winterbourne." Quill's stall was easy to find; it was the one that looked like nothing. A fence of magical goods had to be invisible by law and findable by reputation, and Quill managed both with the serenity of a man who had made peace with contradiction long ago. He was small and round and bald and wore spectacles with plain glass in them, purely, he claimed, for the aesthetic. "You look like a funeral."

"I *came* from a funeral."

"Even better. Those pay best." He held out his palm, and she set the locket in it. His eyebrows climbed above the fake spectacles. "Old work. Pre-Outlawry. Where did you—"

"The owner died happy tonight. That's all anyone needs to know."

"Mm." Quill turned the locket over, and his professional detachment flickered into something almost like reverence. "You know what these are, girl? Properly? Everyone calls them lockets, keepsakes, trinkets. But this is a *vessel*. Someone swore something into this metal, forty-some years ago, in the old tongue, the tongue they burned the books for. An oath of keeping. *Let what is given be held; let what is held be safe.*" He tapped the hinge. "The magic isn't in the silver. It's in the promise. Silver's just where the promise lives."

"Poetic. What'll you give me?"

"For this? Enough to keep you in secondhand vestments till spring." He named a figure. She doubled it. They settled, as always, in the middle, and as always each of them walked away certain they'd won, which was the only kind of deal Quill considered moral.

She was turning to go when the bells rang again — the late bell, Saint Vellum's nine o'clock, and she flinched, full-body this time, before she could dress it up, and Quill saw it.

He had known her seven years. He had never once asked about the flinch, because Quill understood that some merchandise a customer keeps locked in their own cellar. But tonight he tilted his head, and the lamplight caught the plain glass of his spectacles, and he said, quietly:

"You know, there's a man comes round sometimes. Buys vessels like this one. Old ones. Pre-Outlawry oaths, sworn-work, anything with the old tongue in it. Pays stupid money. Doesn't ask questions, which in my experience means he's working for someone who can't afford to have answers lying about." He turned the locket in his fingers one last time. "Been meaning to mention him to you for a while. Never quite seemed relevant."

"Why mention him now?"

Quill shrugged, a small, contained movement, like a man closing a drawer. "Dunno. Feeling in my knees. Weather coming." He pocketed the locket. "Go home, Winterbourne. You've done your good deed and your bad one both today. That's a full ledger for anyone."

* * *

She climbed home through streets gone quiet, past the Watch-post where the lamp burned blue, past the shrine to the King's Accession where fresh ribbon had been tied — seven years this winter, the ribbons said, seven years since Aldric took the crown, and the ribbons were new every month because the Crown paid for new ones, and Sebastian had never once wondered why that detail snagged in her like a fishhook. Some hooks you stop feeling.

Her garret was at the top of a house on Ropewalk Lane, four flights, a room with a bed, a stove, a table, and a window that framed a slice of sky and the top of Saint Vellum's tower, so that even in her own home the bells lived above her like weather.

She lit the stove. She counted the night's coin. She ate bread and hard cheese standing up, the way she always did, because sitting down to eat alone felt too much like a photograph of loneliness and she preferred hers unposed.

And that was when she heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. More a *wrongness* — the sensation of a room where a clock has stopped, noticed not by the ears but by the skin. Sebastian set down her bread. Her hand found the knife she kept in the bread loaf itself, because she was a thief and sentimental about tradition.

Someone was on the stairs.

Four flights, and she heard no footfall — but she heard the *absence* of footfall, the careful pressure of small bare feet placed with a precision no child should possess. The wrongness grew. The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft. And in her pocket, in the little drawstring bag where she carried her tools — her needles, her wax, her three legal charms and two that weren't — she felt something she had never felt in seven years of the trade.

She felt her charms *curdle*.

There was no better word for it. The little woven knots of intent, the charms that warmed her pockets like sleeping mice, went cold and sour and *rotten*, one after another, like milk turning, like fruit collapsing, and Sebastian Winterbourne stood in her own garret with a bread-knife in her fist and terror rising in her throat like floodwater, because nothing in the world did that. Nothing rotted magic. Magic didn't rot. It faded, it frayed, it was confiscated and burned —

Three knocks at her door.

Slow. Apologetic. The way clergy knock. The way *she* knocked.

She crossed the room without deciding to and put her eye to the peephole, and the hallway beyond was empty — no, not empty. Small. A child stood at her threshold, a girl of perhaps eight or nine, barefoot on the freezing boards, in a smock too big for her, with hair the color of river-clay and eyes that caught the candlelight and held it a half-second too long.

The girl could not possibly have known Sebastian was looking. But she raised her face to the door exactly as if she did, and she spoke — and her voice was the strangest thing Sebastian had ever heard, because the words were shaped like no language sold or smuggled in the Night Market, and yet they landed in Sebastian's mind with *meaning*, the way a tuning fork lands in the teeth:

*"The hour is owed,"* said the child, in the tongue that predated every kingdom, in the language of oaths, *"and the keeper is coming to collect."*

Behind her, above her, far across the sleeping city, the bells of Saint Vellum's began to ring the midnight hour — and for the first time in seven years, Sebastian did not flinch.

She only stood there, knife forgotten, blood roaring, understanding suddenly and completely that the smooth scarred-over place inside her chest had just begun, very softly, to itch.
