## 2. A Knock Older Than Doors

The knock came at the hour when Vellharrow forgot itself — when the gaslamps guttered low and the fog off the Sere Canal crept up the stairwells like a debt collector who knew you were home.

Sebastian Winterbourne heard it through a dream of bells.

She woke with her hand already under the pillow, fingers closing on the cold brass of her lockpick charm, and for three heartbeats she lay in the dark of her garret listening to the sound repeat. Not knocking, exactly. Knocking was knuckles or a stick or the flat of a palm, and it demanded. This was softer than that. Patient. The way rain knocks on a window it has no intention of entering through.

Three taps. A pause long enough to count your sins in. Three taps again.

She had lived above the shuttered chandlery on Wren's Cut for six years, and in all that time no one had climbed the ninety-one stairs to her door uninvited. The stairs were the point. She'd chosen the room the way a crab chooses its shell — narrow, high, accessible by one winding spine of rotting wood that groaned a warning at every third step. She knew every voice those stairs could make. Whoever stood below had climbed them without waking a single one.

Sebastian sat up. The floorboards were cold as river stones beneath her bare feet. Through the leaded window, the fog had swallowed the city whole; only the far bell of Saint Ossuary's tower showed, a smudge of darker dark, and even that seemed less a building than a memory of one.

The knock came again. Three taps. Pause. Three.

And then — this was the part she would turn over and over in the days after, the part that would not smooth out no matter how she pressed it flat — the pause between the second set of taps and whatever came next was *familiar*. Not the rhythm. Something underneath the rhythm. It landed in her chest the way a name lands when someone calls it across a crowded market: before thought, before sense, there is simply the turning of your own head.

She did not remember why. That was the shape of her life now, seven years wide and seven years deep: a hundred small turnings toward things she could not name, flinches without teeth, griefs without faces. She had learned to live beside them the way you live beside a locked door in your own house. You stop asking what's behind it. You stop, eventually, seeing the door.

She wrapped her shawl around her shoulders, took up the little lamp, and lit it with a pinch of tallow-scraps she kept in a tin because matches cost coppers and coppers were always elsewhere. The flame caught and steadied, throwing her shadow huge against the slanted ceiling — a hunched giantess with a lamp for a heart.

"Who's there?" she said, and heard how thin her voice sounded against all that dark.

No answer. Of course no answer. But she felt — and she was a professional in the business of feeling things that weren't supposed to be felt; she made her living handling other people's ghosts by the gram — she felt something waiting on the other side of that door. Not menace. Menace she knew the taste of; it was metallic, quick, like biting foil. This was older. Slower. This was the patience of something that had already waited longer than her whole life and was prepared to wait longer still.

She crossed the room. Ninety-one stairs below, the fog pressed against the chandlery's dead windows. Somewhere across the rooftops a night-watchman coughed, and the cough came back twice from the canal walls, diminished each time, until it sounded like a man apologizing.

Sebastian put her eye to the peephole — a knot-hole she'd drilled herself, oiled so it wouldn't squeak — and looked down into the lamplight spilling from her own doorway.

A child stood there.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, perhaps ten — it was hard to say, because she was thin the way winter is thin, all edges and endurance, wearing a coat that had been cut for someone twice her size and rolled at the cuffs four times over. Her hair was dark and cropped close, unevenly, as if done in haste with a knife rather than scissors. Her feet were bare. Bare, in the fog-wet cold of a Vellharrow autumn, on ninety-one stone-and-rot stairs, and they were not blue, not shaking, not even marked with grime. They were simply clean, as if the dirt of the city had considered touching them and thought better of it.

The girl was looking directly up at the peephole.

Not at the door. At the hole. At Sebastian's eye, presumably, though she couldn't have known it was there — except that she tilted her head slightly, the way a bird does when it has decided you are more interesting than frightening, and waited.

Every instinct Sebastian owned — and she owned good ones, bought dearly, paid for in scar tissue and sleepless nights — told her to blow out the lamp, go back to bed, and let the fog have whatever it wanted. Children at doors were cons. Children at doors were traps laid by the Guild, by inquisitors, by creditors, by anyone with a grievance and a spare orphan. She had seen a child used as a delivery system for a needle-thin blade once, in her other life, the life with the hole in it, and the memory of that — not the event, just the *shape* where the event should be, an ache like a tooth pulled wrong — rose in her throat now.

But the girl's feet were clean. And the fog did not touch her. And the knock had been three and three, and somewhere under Sebastian's sternum, in the place where the missing years lived, something had turned over like a body in shallow water.

She unbolted the door.

* * *

Up close, the child smelled of nothing. That was the first wrong thing, and it took Sebastian a moment to understand why it was wrong, because *nothing* is such an ordinary smell. But everything smells. Stone smells of rain and lichen. Wool smells of sheep and smoke. People smell of their kitchens and their fears. This girl smelled of clean air in a city that had not had clean air since the foundries opened two centuries ago — as if she carried a pocket of somewhere else around with her, some high cold place with no chimneys.

"You're lost," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't much of a statement either. "This is Wren's Cut. The alms-house is back down past the tannery, left at the shrine, and don't let them tell you it's full, because it's never full, they just—" She stopped. The girl was watching her mouth move with an attention that had nothing childish in it at all. It was the attention of a scholar reading a difficult text. Or a judge.

"Ives," the girl said.

Just that. One word, in a voice like a small bell rung underwater — clear, and strange, and resonant in a way that seemed too large for the throat producing it. It was not a name, Sebastian understood immediately. It was not any word. The syllables sat in the ear oddly, refusing to mean anything, and yet they meant *something*, the way a chord means something even if you can't read music. The word went into Sebastian and found the locked door inside her and pressed itself against it, gently, the way you press your forehead against a door of a room you used to live in.

Her missing years ached.

It was not pain, exactly. It was the ache of a limb remembered — phantom, insistent, *there*. For half a heartbeat Sebastian stood in her own doorway and was certain, absolutely certain, that she had heard that word before, spoken by someone whose face she could almost — *almost* —

The feeling passed. It left behind the familiar hollow, the seven-year silence, and a fine tremor in her hands that she hid by gripping the doorframe.

"No," she said. "That's not a word. Or if it is, it's not one of mine." She crouched, bringing herself level with the girl, because that was what you did with children, and also because it gave her a better angle on the stairwell in case this was a trap after all. "Where's your mother? Your father? Anyone?"

The girl regarded her. Her eyes were dark — not brown, not black, but dark the way deep water is dark, the color going down further than eyes ought to go. In the lamplight they held no reflection of the flame. Sebastian noticed that and filed it away in the drawer where she kept things she would think about later, or never.

Then the girl spoke again.

The words were long and liquid and utterly foreign, and they should have been nonsense — should have washed over Sebastian like the mutterings of any beggar-mad pilgrim in any market square. But they were not nonsense. They were structured. They had grammar; she could feel the grammar, the way you feel the bones of a fish under the flesh. Each syllable interlocked with the next, consonants dovetailing into vowels, vowels opening onto new consonants, and the whole utterance moved with the terrible confidence of a sentence that has been true for a very long time and expects to remain true.

And the lamp guttered.

Not the way lamps gutter — the ordinary stutter of a drafty room. The flame bent sideways, away from the girl, and shrank to a blue bead no bigger than a peppercorn, and the cold came in. Real cold, cellar cold, grave cold, rolling off the child's small shoulders like water off a wet dog. On the shelf beside the door, Sebastian's stock-in-trade sat in its neat rows: memory-charms, a dozen of them, little vials and locket-seals and wax tablets, each holding a stolen hour or a borrowed afternoon, each humming faintly with the residual warmth of somebody's joy.

They began to curdle.

There was no better word for it. Sebastian watched, frozen in her crouch, as the charm nearest the door — a locket holding a baker's memory of his daughter's wedding, a warm golden thing she'd been meaning to fence on Thursday — clouded over like milk in thunderstorms. The gold went grey. The grey went green. A smell rose from it, sweetish and foul, the smell of a feast gone over, and along the shelf the corruption spread charm to charm like laughter through a congregation, each vessel dimming, souring, rotting from the inside out. Hours dying. Somebody's happiest day turning to compost on Sebastian Winterbourne's shelf.

The girl stopped speaking. The cold stopped. The lamp straightened itself and burned yellow and ordinary again, and the fog outside resumed being merely weather.

On the shelf, twelve charms sat ruined. Twelve little deaths of light. Sebastian had lifted most of them herself, carefully, tenderly even, from people who'd had hours to spare, and she had planned to sell them for enough to eat through the winter, and now they were spoiled meat, and she could not make herself care about the money, which frightened her more than the rest of it.

"What," Sebastian said quietly, "are you?"

The girl looked at the ruined shelf. Something moved across her face — not guilt, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Sorrow's distant cousin. She reached out one small hand, hesitated, then touched the nearest rotted locket with one fingertip.

Under her finger, the green receded. Just a little. Just at the edge, like frost retreating from a warmed pane. Not healed. Apologized to.

Then she looked back up at Sebastian, and her mouth shaped words again — the same language, the same impossible grammar — but slower this time, deliberate, and though Sebastian understood none of it, she understood the *manner* of it perfectly, because she had spent her life reading what people meant instead of what they said. The girl was saying: *I am sorry for your things. I could not help it. I do not belong near them.*

Or possibly she was saying: *Run.*

"Inside," Sebastian heard herself say. Her voice came out rough, scraped. "Before the watch sees you. Before *anything* sees you."

The girl stepped over the threshold the way you step into a church you've been excommunicated from — carefully, and with a kind of grieving respect — and the moment her bare foot touched Sebastian's floorboards, every hair on Sebastian's arms stood up, and the ache behind the locked door in her chest turned over again, and settled, and was quiet, like something finally lying down to sleep after seven years of standing watch.

* * *

She fed the child bread and dripping because feeding people was the oldest trick in the book for making them yours, and because the girl's thinness offended something in her that had nothing to do with strategy. The girl ate neatly, completely, wasting nothing, and watched the room the whole time — the charms (ruined), the picks (oiled), the window (barred), the loose floorboard under the bed (concealed, but perhaps not concealed enough). She catalogued the garret the way Sebastian catalogued a mark's house: thoroughly, and without judgment.

Sebastian sat across from her and tried to think like a professional.

Professionally, this was a disaster. Professionally, she should wake Quill at dawn, or better, never wake Quill at all, and put the girl out into the fog with a heel of bread and directions to the alms-house, and bolt the door, and drink until the ache behind the locked door went back to sleep. Professionally, a child who rotted magic by proximity and spoke in a tongue with no living country was not a guest. She was a corpse with a pulse, waiting to happen, and everyone within fifty feet of her would hang from the gibbets on Magistrate's Row when the Crown finished asking its questions.

The Crown. The word surfaced in her mind unbidden, and with it the image she could not account for: a hall, banners, an anthem, a king who —

Who what? Who smiled? Everyone knew King Aldric smiled. He was famous for it. The Smiling King, the papers called him, seven years beloved. There was nothing under that thought. There was no hall in her head, no anthem, no reason at all for her hands to be trembling around her cup of chicory.

The girl reached across the table and touched Sebastian's wrist.

Her fingers were cool — not cold, cool, like stone in shade — and where they lay, the trembling stopped. Just stopped, the way a struck bell stops when you grip it. Sebastian looked down at the small hand on her arm and felt the strangest sensation of being *held shut*, the way you'd hold a book closed to keep the wind from turning pages.

The girl said something. Softly. Two syllables this time, gentle as a lullaby, and though the language was alien, the tone needed no translation anywhere in the world. It meant: *sleep now. I will keep the door.*

And Sebastian — who had not slept a full night in seven years, who woke at every bell and every footstep and every dream of churches, who had built her whole careful lonely life on the principle that sleep was a vulnerability and trust was a currency she could not afford — believed her.

She slept until the fog turned grey with morning. She dreamed of nothing at all, and it was the kindest gift anyone had given her in living memory, which was, she reflected later, a shorter span than it should have been.

When she woke, the girl was standing at the window, watching the sun burn holes in the fog, and on the table beside the ruined charms she had arranged three objects in a row: a button, a crow's feather, and a scrap of paper covered edge to edge in flowing, interlocking script that hurt to look at, the way a bright sky hurts after a week indoors.

Oathscript, though Sebastian didn't know the word yet. Wouldn't know it until tomorrow, until Quill went white behind his counter and the inquisitors came down on the night market like owls on mice.

For now it was only marks on paper, drawn by a child's steady hand, and one of them — one glyph near the top, a crooked vertical line crossed by three descending strokes, like a ladder broken mid-climb — made the locked door inside Sebastian slam so hard she tasted blood.

She looked at it and knew, with a certainty that had no evidence and no mercy, that she had drawn that exact shape before.

Seven years ago.

With her own hand.

The girl turned from the window. Their eyes met across the small grey room, over the dead charms and the living bread, and the child pointed one clean finger at the glyph, and then at Sebastian, and mouthed a single word that needed no translating at all:

*Remember.*
