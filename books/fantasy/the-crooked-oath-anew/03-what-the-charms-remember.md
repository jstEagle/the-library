## 3. What the Charms Remember

The night market of Vellharrow opened the way a wound opens: slowly, reluctantly, and always in the same place.

Sebastian came down from the garret with Ives's hand locked in hers and a story rehearsed on her tongue — *my sister's daughter, mute since the fever, don't ask questions* — but no one asked questions in the Shambles. That was the whole architecture of the place. The market lived in the ribs of a collapsed basilica whose bell tower had been toppled during the Purging, its bronze bells buried where they fell so that no one would ever be summoned by them again. Vendors had built their stalls into the arches like birds nesting in a skeleton. Lantern-glass glowed amber and green with bottled light-sellers' wares. The air tasted of tallow smoke, wet stone, and the particular sweetness of memory being traded — a smell like overripe plums and candle wax that most people never noticed because they had never learned to notice it.

Ives noticed it. Sebastian felt the girl's small hand tighten around her fingers at the first stall, and when she glanced down, Ives was staring at a jar of bottled hours the way other children stared at caged songbirds. Not with longing. With recognition. And something that looked, horribly, like pity.

*Don't*, Sebastian signed against the girl's palm, the thieves' sign for *be nothing, want nothing*. She'd only invented it two days ago and already used it forty times a day.

They moved through the crowd. Sebastian kept her hood up and her gait unhurried, which was harder than it sounded, because every instinct she owned was screaming at her to run. The charms in her coat pocket — three extraction foci she'd meant to sell tonight, each one a sliver of bone or glass or riverstone wrapped in waxed silk — had been *wrong* since the child arrived. Wrong the way milk goes wrong. She could feel them through the fabric, a faint sour heat against her hip, like carrying a dying thing.

She'd told herself it was coincidence. Charm-rot happened; bad storage, bad wax, a rival's spite-work. But last night she had laid all eleven of her working charms out on the table in a careful row, and Ives had walked past them on her way to the window, and every single one had *curdled* in sequence as the girl passed, like dominoes falling in slow motion, like a row of candles guttering one after another as something invisible walked by.

Eleven years of craft, souring in the space of four footsteps.

So: Quill.

Quill kept a shop in the third arch of the basilica, behind a curtain of hanging keys that didn't open anything anymore. He was a fence of magical goods in a city where magic was contraband, which meant he was also an archivist, an appraiser, a historian of the forbidden, and — though he'd deny it under torture and probably had — a coward of long standing and considerable refinement. He was old the way driftwood is old, gray and bleached and oddly smooth, with spectacles ground from bottle glass that made his eyes enormous and mildly astonished at all times.

He saw them coming and his face did three things in quick succession: professional neutrality, genuine pleasure, and then, landing on Ives, a flicker of naked alarm that he smothered badly.

"Winterbourne." His voice was dry as the ledger paper he loved. "You've brought me something cursed."

"Good evening to you too."

"You brought me something *cursed*," he repeated, "and you're pretending it's a social call. I can smell it from here. It smells like—" He stopped. Took off his spectacles. Polished them on his sleeve with unnecessary thoroughness. "It smells like a church."

Sebastian's spine went cold. "It smells like charm-rot. I need to know if it's salvageable, and I need to know what's causing it, and I need both answers before the night watch changes."

"And the child?"

"Asleep on her feet, mostly. She doesn't talk."

"Mm," said Quill, in the tone of a man who had just been lied to by a professional and wanted her to know he knew.

He lifted the curtain aside. Inside, the shop was a throat of shelves, every surface crowded with the confiscated and the forgotten: prayer wheels stripped of their prayers, a saint's fingerbone in a matchbox, rosaries sold by the pound as beads, grimoires with their pages pressed flat between boards of lead so they couldn't whisper. A single lamp burned low. Quill lit two more before he would let them sit, and Sebastian understood that too — light was a kind of testimony, and whatever he suspected, he wanted witnesses, even if the witnesses were only lamps.

He unwrapped her charms one at a time on a square of black velvet, handling each like a coroner.

The riverstone focus he turned over twice, held to the lamp, sniffed. "Salvageable. The rot hasn't reached the core. Whatever touched these—" he tapped it, "—didn't attack them. Understand that. Rot is an assault. This is more like..." He searched for the word, hating every candidate. "...an argument. As if something spoke to your charms in a voice they were obliged to answer, and answering spoiled them. Like men who go mad from hearing a secret."

"That's not a thing."

"No," Quill agreed pleasantly. "Neither is half of what's in my shop. Next."

The bone sliver made him stop. He set down his lens, took it up again, set it down. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed registers entirely — dropped out of the merchant's patter into something older and quieter, the voice he presumably used for prayers he no longer believed in.

"Where did you get this?"

"Bought it off a dredger two winters ago. Why?"

"Because this is choir-bone. Pre-Purging. Sanctified, then desanctified, then — do you see the marks?" He angled it toward the lamp. Along its length ran a line of characters, fine as scratches from a pin, that Sebastian had always taken for maker's marks or decorative filigree. "These aren't decoration. These are *words*. And they're not written, exactly. They're *sworn*. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire history of the world, roughly speaking."

"Quill."

"I'm arriving, I'm arriving." He removed his spectacles altogether, which Sebastian had seen him do perhaps twice in nine years of trade, both times preceding news she hadn't wanted. "Before the Purging, before the outlawry, magic wasn't cast. Everyone thinks it was cast — waved hands, spoken spells, all that theater. It wasn't. It was *sworn*. Binding-magic especially. You didn't command a power; you contracted with it. And contracts require a language precise enough that neither party can wriggle. Ordinary speech wriggles. So the old orders developed a tongue for it. Formal. Closed. Every word a clause, every clause a chain-link." He looked up, and behind the bottle-glass lenses his eyes were enormous and afraid. "Oathscript."

The word went through Sebastian like a needle finding a nerve she hadn't known she had.

Somewhere behind her eyes, seven blank years shifted in their sleep.

"The tongue binding-magic was sworn in," she said. Her own voice sounded far away. "And my charms are rotting because—"

"Because someone has been *speaking it near them*." Quill's gaze slid past her shoulder to the child, who stood very still by the door, watching them with those grave dark eyes, her silence more articulate than any noise. "Or singing it. Or dreaming it aloud. The words themselves carry weight, Winterbourne. A sworn language doesn't stop being sworn just because the speaker doesn't understand it. Water doesn't stop being wet because the fish is ignorant."

"She's a child. She's mute. She makes sounds sometimes, but—"

"Make her make them."

"I will not interrogate a—"

"*Make her make them,*" Quill said, with a hardness she had never heard from him, "because if what I think is true, there are people in this city who would burn down the Shambles to hear those sounds, and I would prefer to know what we're all dying for before we die for it."

The lamp flames leaned, all three at once, though there was no draft.

And Ives — who had not spoken, who had not made a sound beyond breath and the occasional small humming note that Sebastian had privately decided was the girl thinking out loud — stepped forward into the lamplight and looked at the bone sliver on the velvet, and opened her mouth.

What came out was not a child's voice. It was a child's voice the way a chapel is a room — technically true, structurally misleading. The sound was small and clear and it *stood up* in the air. It had corners. It had weight-bearing walls. Three syllables, maybe four, arranged with a formality that made Sebastian's teeth ache, and as the girl spoke, every curdled charm on the black velvet shivered once, like insects waking, and the lamp flames bent toward her — all three, all together, like courtiers bowing.

Then it was over. The flames straightened. The silence came back in like water filling a footprint.

Quill sat down. Sebastian realized she was gripping the edge of his counter hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that her heart was going like a fist on a door.

"What did she say?" she whispered.

"I don't know." Quill's face had gone the color of his ledger paper. "That's rather the point. Nobody living knows. It's a dead tongue, Winterbourne. Dead for seventy years. We killed everyone who spoke it — that was most of what the Purging *was* — and we burned the grammars, and we salted the archives, and we congratulated ourselves. And now a mute child walks into my shop and conjugates it like she was born mid-sentence, and your charms fall over like fainting ladies, and you're asking me *what did she say* as if that's the interesting question."

"What's the interesting question?"

"Why her." He was rewrapping her charms with hands that weren't quite steady, folding the velvet corners with excessive care, buying time with ritual. "Why now. And why — and I want you to hear how much I hate saying this — why does the presence of an Oathscript speaker make *sanctified objects* respond? Because that's not a parlor trick. Objects respond to sworn language because they were *party to oaths*. Choir-bone answered her. Your charms flinched from her. Do you understand what that implies about what's still listening, after seventy years? What's still *bound*?"

The lamp nearest the door guttered.

And beneath it, beneath everything, Sebastian felt the ache again — the pulled-tooth throb behind her eyes that had lived there for seven years, the shape of the hole where her missing memories should have been. It had woken the night Ives arrived. It woke again now, worse, rhythmic, almost like a second pulse. Almost like something knocking.

*The past is not dead,* her teacher Dree used to say, back when she'd had a teacher and a past worth mentioning. *The past is inventory. Nothing in Vellharrow dies, girl. It just gets priced.*

"Quill," she said slowly. "Seven years ago. Before my missing stretch. Was there anything — any trade, any rumor, anything moving through your shop that involved Oathscript? Anything at all?"

Something crossed his face. Something priced and shelved and carefully dusted.

"You know I keep no records of that kind."

"You keep records of *every* kind. You have ledgers going back to the Purging. You alphabetize your sins."

"And you," Quill said gently, "have spent seven years not asking me that exact question, and I have spent seven years admiring your discipline. Don't break it tonight. Tonight of all nights." He pushed the wrapped charms back across the counter toward her and, with them, a look she couldn't fully read — pity, warning, and underneath both, fear wearing its good coat. "Take your rot and your child and go home, Winterbourne. And tomorrow, take the child somewhere that isn't here. Somewhere that isn't anywhere. Because if she speaks what I think she speaks, then the Crown's mnemonic inquisitors have been waiting seventy years for exactly one thing, and it isn't a person. It's a *word*. And words, unlike people, can't hide."

He was still talking when Sebastian heard it.

At first she took it for weather — a low pressure change, a shift in the market's hum outside, the crowd's murmur changing key. Then the murmur *stopped*, all at once, the way a forest goes silent before something arrives, and beneath the silence came a sound she knew from nightmares and from other people's ruined faces: the measured, many-booted tread of the inquisition walking in cadence. Not hurrying. Never hurrying. They had all the time in the world because everyone else's time belonged to the Crown.

Lanterns bloomed orange between the arches. Voices rose — vendors kicking over braziers, shutters slamming, the night market collapsing back into the wound it had opened from. Someone screamed a name. Glass broke somewhere with a bright, expensive sound.

"Inquisitors," Quill breathed. He was already moving, snuffing lamps, sweeping valuables into lead-lined drawers with the speed of long practice. "Back way. Through the reliquary, mind the floor, the third flagstone lies. Go, go — and Winterbourne—" He caught her arm at the curtain of dead keys, and his enormous frightened eyes were very close. "Whatever she is, whoever sent her — she was sent *to you*. People don't find you by accident. Ask yourself who knew you before you knew yourself."

Boots in the basilica nave now, loud as judgment. A voice, amplified by some sanctioned art, rolling through the stone: *"By order of the Crown, this market is closed for examination. Remain where you are. Memory is contraband. Contraband will be collected."*

Sebastian grabbed Ives's hand and ran.

They went through the reliquary past empty niches where saints used to stand, over the lying flagstone, down a service stair slick with condensation, out through a coal chute into an alley that smelled of ash and river. Behind them the orange light spread through the Shambles like ink through water, and the amplified voice went on reciting its litany of ownership, and somewhere in it — threaded through the official words like wire through rope — Sebastian heard another voice, thinner, human, terrified, and recognized Quill's merchant patter rising into a register she'd never heard him use: the sound of a man selling his innocence and hoping the price was right.

She pressed herself and the child into the shadow of a buttress and held her there, feeling the small heartbeat hammering against her palm, faster than any child's should.

Ives looked up at her. In the distant orange glow her eyes were wide and wet and utterly calm, the way eyes are calm when they have seen the thing coming for a long time. Slowly — deliberately, the way she did everything — the girl raised one small hand and drew on the air, tracing shapes Sebastian now recognized: the same fine scratches that lived on the choir-bone. Oathscript, written in nothing, for no one.

Three glyphs. Then she pointed at Sebastian. Then, with terrible gentleness, she touched her own chest.

*You. Me.* Something sworn between them, or something that had been sworn long ago and was only now coming due.

Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, a lantern swung its light across the cobblestones and stopped.

A figure stood there in inquisitorial gray, and even at this distance, even in the bad light, Sebastian could see that the man was not looking at the shadows where they hid. He was looking at the space *above* them — at the air where a mute child had just finished writing — and he was smiling the way people smile at a debt finally come home.

* * *

The bells of Vellharrow had been melted decades ago, all but the ones buried under the fallen tower, and yet as Sebastian pulled Ives deeper into the dark, running from the light, she heard them anyway — deep underground, drowned in rubble, ringing once, faintly, like something turning over in its sleep.

Her missing seven years knocked again behind her eyes.

And this time, she was almost certain, something knocked back.
