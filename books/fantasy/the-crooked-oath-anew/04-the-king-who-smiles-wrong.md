## 4. The King Who Smiles Wrong

The summons came on cheap paper, which was how Sebastian knew it was official. Anything worth forging in Vellharrow was printed on good stock; the Crown, secure in its monopoly on legitimacy, couldn't be bothered.

*By order of the Lord Chamberlain, all licensed tradesfolk of the Middle Wards are commanded to attend His Majesty's public audience, there to witness the bounty of the realm and the grace of its sovereign.*

Bounty. Grace. The words sat on her tongue like coins from a dead man's pocket — technically currency, but warm in the wrong way.

She went anyway. Not out of loyalty. Out of trade craft.

A memory-thief who ignored a royal audience was a thief with something to hide, and Vellharrow's mnemonic inquisitors had learned, in seven years of diligent bookkeeping, to read absence the way other men read ink. So she pinned on her second-best coat, tucked Ives into the care of the widow Maren three floors down — *one hour, and if she speaks anything but silence, send for me* — and joined the river of bodies flowing toward the Palace Green.

The crowd was thick as wool by the time she reached the barriers. Bakers and bricklayers, chandlers and clerks, all of them pressed shoulder to shoulder behind rope lines gone gray from handling, all of them craning toward the dais where the King of Vellharrow would receive his people's petitions.

Sebastian had worked crowds before. She knew their textures: the greedy ones that smelled of sweat and speculation, the frightened ones that smelled of tallow and prayer. This one smelled of soap. Everyone had scrubbed. Everyone had dressed in their least-worn clothes. Fear, in Vellharrow, now came laundered and pressed.

She found a place near a fruit-seller's stall where she could see the dais through a gap in the banners, and she did what she always did at gatherings: she read the crowd for loose pockets and looser tongues, and she waited.

The trumpets nearly took the roof off the sky.

Then came the anthem — the *Vow of the Realm*, they called it now, though it had been called something else before the outlawry, something older, and no one sang the old verses anymore because no one remembered them. The crowd bellowed the new words with the flat devotion of people who had practiced. And down the long colonnade, flanked by halberdiers whose armor gleamed like fish bellies, came King Aldric the Restorer, the Beloved, the Unbroken.

Sebastian had seen him twice before, both times at distance, both times in the company of the particular numbness she felt toward all authority. He was not an old man. That was the first thing. Seven years into his reign he still moved like a man in his forties, broad through the chest, silver only at the temples, his face kept by cooks and physicians and whatever else kings could afford. He wore the crown lightly, or seemed to — a slim circlet of black iron set with a single pale stone, nothing like the fat gold monstrosities of the old dynasty.

He reached the dais. The anthem swelled toward its final verse.

And Sebastian noticed it because noticing was her profession.

He wasn't blinking.

Not in the way of a man suppressing a blink, or a man staring hard at something. In the way of a thing that had never learned the rhythm. She counted without meaning to — old habit, counting seconds between heartbeats when she lifted a memory, timing her pull to the body's own pulse. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. The anthem ended, the trumpets flared again, the crowd roared itself hoarse, and the king's eyes stayed open through all of it, wide and wet and perfectly steady, two dark coins set in a face arranged around them.

Then, in the space between one trumpet note and the next, he blinked. Once. Twice. Three quick blinks, mechanical, like a clock catching up on hours it had missed.

And then open again, and still.

Sebastian's hands had gone cold inside her gloves.

She told herself what any sensible person would tell themselves: dry eyes, a medical matter, the strain of ceremony. She told herself that kings were strange creatures, raised in glass jars of ritual until they grew into shapes no natural person would recognize. She told herself this while her professional eye did what it could not stop doing — reading him the way she read a mark, cataloguing the tells.

The smile was wrong too. It was a good smile, generously made, deployed at exactly the right moments. But it arrived whole. There was no assembly to it, no gathering at the corners of the mouth first, no flicker in the eyes preceding it. It simply appeared, complete, the way a memory appears when you pour it out of a charm — someone else's joy, worn at second hand.

*The crown is a mouth,* thought some part of her mind, unbidden, in words that felt less like her own than like something she'd once known and paid to forget. *And mouths are for swallowing.*

She pressed the thought down. It had come with a taste attached — candle smoke and cold stone and a woman's voice saying *hold it steady, Seb, hold it steady* — and that taste was the taste of her missing years, and chasing it in public, in front of the throne, was suicide.

The petitions began.

An old woman came forward about a well that had gone bitter since the war. A guildsman about tariffs. A boy of maybe twelve, holding a hat full of grain, petitioning for his father's pardon — the father had been caught with a memory-charm, a wedding day he'd bought back off the black market so he could remember his wife's face, and the law was the law, and the boy's voice cracked on *the law is the law* while the king listened with those unblinking eyes and then smiled, wholly, instantly, and said something gentle that made the crowd sigh.

Sebastian watched the boy leave the dais. Watched the guards escort him not back to the crowd but sideways, through a small door in the colonnade, and watched the door close with the softness of a thing designed to close quietly.

No one else seemed to notice. No one else ever noticed. That was the trick of Vellharrow, she thought — not that the Crown hid its teeth, but that it had taught everyone to look at the smile instead.

* * *

It was during the fourth petition — a land dispute between two farmers, interminable — that she felt the attention settle on her.

Thieves know when they're being watched. It's not magic; it's scar tissue. A certain weight arrives at the back of the neck, a pressure like a hand hovering an inch above the skin, and every thief Sebastian had ever known had learned to honor it without turning around, because turning around confirmed you'd felt it, and feeling it meant you had something to hide.

She kept her eyes on the dais. She let her gaze drift, lazy and bored, across the courtiers ranged along the colonnade — and there.

A man in dove-gray silk, standing slightly apart from the others. Courtier's dress, courtier's post, hands folded at his belt with courtier's ease. Everything about him was correct. That was the wrongness. Every other person on that colonnade leaked something — the fidgeting chamberlain, the bored captain shifting his weight, the young page whose eyes kept sliding to the crowd. This man leaked nothing. He stood the way furniture stands.

And his eyes.

Sebastian had spent seven years handling memories, and she knew what emptied looked like. She'd seen it in clients who'd sold too much and come back hollow-eyed, asking *who am I, do you know who I am*, gripping her wrist with desperate strength while their own faces hung on them like coats on hooks. This man had that look. Worse. The clients still had hooks under the coats. This man's eyes were windows in a house where the lamps had been carried out one by one, long ago, and the curtains drawn, and yet the house stood at its window anyway, watching the street.

He was looking directly at her.

Not scanning. Looking. With the patient, unblinking attention of the king himself, as though the same stillness had been poured into both of them from the same jug.

Sebastian did the hardest thing in her repertoire, harder than any extraction: she did nothing. She yawned. She scratched her jaw. She turned to the fruit-seller beside her and asked, loudly enough to be forgettable, whether the plums were any good this season, and haggled over two of them with the genuine pettiness of a woman thinking about plums.

When she glanced back along the colonnade — casual, a woman checking the weather — the dove-gray man had turned away. But his head had turned a fraction, marking her position against the barrier, the stall, the banner. She'd seen surveyors do the same with landmarks. He hadn't memorized her face.

He'd memorized her coordinates.

* * *

She stayed for the rest of the audience because leaving early was a tell. She stood through six more petitions, through the king's closing blessing — delivered in a voice like warm honey poured over something with too many joints — through the recessional and the trumpets and the crowd's ragged, relieved cheering. Then she let herself be carried out with the tide, bought a meat pie she didn't want from a vendor she didn't look at, and took the long way home, doubling back twice through the tannery lanes where the stink discouraged followers.

Ives was sitting on the garret floor when she came in, cross-legged, drawing in the dust with one small finger. The widow Maren reported, in a whisper, that the child had been silent as stone and eaten everything offered, and had also — here the old woman's face went uncertain — *hummed*. Just once, when the church bells rang noon. A tune Maren didn't know, in a mode that made her teeth ache, and the moment it stopped, the girl had looked at her own hands as if they'd betrayed her.

After Maren left, Sebastian crouched and studied the dust-drawing. Lines and angles, deliberate, repeating — the same glyph over and over, refined with each repetition, the way a child practices letters.

"Who taught you that?" she asked, uselessly.

Ives looked up. Her eyes were dark and enormous and utterly present, and for a moment Sebastian had the vertiginous sense of looking at the opposite of the courtier — a vessel filled past capacity, brimming with something that had no business living in so small a body.

Then the girl pointed at the glyph, and then, slowly, at Sebastian's chest.

"No," Sebastian said. "That's not — I don't know what that is."

Ives pointed again. Insistent. And drew a second glyph beneath the first, and a third, and then pressed her palm flat over all three, and the air in the garret went strange — thick, sweet, like the moment before a storm breaks, like the smell of a charm about to curdle.

Sebastian's head rang. Somewhere behind her eyes, in the sealed vault where seven years should have been, something shifted against the door. Not pain. Recognition. The way a dog knows its name in a stranger's mouth.

"Ives," Sebastian said — the name arriving from nowhere, or from somewhere she couldn't reach, feeling less chosen than remembered. "Your name is Ives. Say it."

The child's lips moved. No sound came. But her finger traced one last glyph in the dust, slow and careful, and Sebastian stared at it and felt the pulled-tooth ache of her missing years throb in time.

The glyph looked, God help her, like the pale stone in the king's black-iron crown.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the palace bells began to ring the hour — and somewhere in the Middle Wards, a man in dove-gray silk was writing down an address.
