## 5. Ives

The child did not sleep so much as hover at the edge of it, like a moth deciding whether a flame was worth the cost.

Sebastian learned this the first night, when she gave up her own bed out of a pragmatism she refused to call kindness — the girl had wedged herself into the corner where the wall met the window, knees to chest, and watched Sebastian with eyes that reflected lamplight the way an animal's do, all mirror and no mercy of expression. By the third hour Sebastian surrendered, blew out the candle, and lay on the floorboards beneath her coat, listening to the city's small night noises: the groan of the garret settling, rain beginning somewhere over the Tanner's Quarter, the far bell of Saint Vessory counting hours she always flinched from without knowing why.

The child slept eventually. But even sleeping, her lips moved — shaping those syllables in that dead language, soft as ash sifting down.

In the morning, Sebastian made tea for two before she could stop herself, then drank both cups out of spite.

* * *

Naming her was a matter of necessity. Sebastian could hardly keep calling her *the girl*, or worse, *it*, though there were moments — when the memory-charms hanging by her door curdled another shade toward rot, their little woven spells souring like milk left in sun — that *it* felt more honest. Whatever the child carried, it ate magic the way rust eats iron: slowly, thoroughly, and with no malice whatsoever.

Sebastian tried questions first. She was a thief of memories; she knew how information lived in people, where it hid, what pressure coaxed it loose. But this child's mind was a locked room with no seams. When Sebastian signed — the quick thieves' cant of the night market, *who sent you* — the girl only tilted her head, birdlike, and repeated one of those impossible syllables, and the teacup on the shelf cracked clean down its glaze.

So: a name.

"I can't call you nothing," Sebastian said, crouching to the girl's level on the fourth morning, keeping her voice flat and transactional because transactional was safe. "People who are called nothing get collected by someone who wants to fill the space."

The girl watched her mouth move. She had begun doing that — tracking words as if they were coins passing hand to hand, checking each for counterfeit.

Sebastian looked around her garret for inspiration and found none. The room held a bed, a cold hearth, three locks on the door (two of which no longer worked properly, thanks to the child's presence), and a window with a view of slate roofs and, beyond them, the crooked spire of the Hall of Records where she sold what she stole. Nothing in it suggested anything worth naming a child after. She'd chosen her own name the same way, at fourteen — *Sebastian*, plucked from a gravestone in the Potter's Field because the stone hadn't fallen over yet and therefore implied durability.

Her gaze landed on the ivy forcing itself through a gap in the window frame. It had been there since before she rented the room. She'd cut it back twice; it returned thicker, patient, indifferent to being unwanted. It grew toward the light through solid brick if brick was what stood between.

"Ives," she said.

The girl went very still.

Not the stillness of recognition — Sebastian knew that look, had seen it on faces from which she'd lifted whole childhoods, the sudden wet brightness of something returning. This was different. This was the stillness of a word landing on a thing that had been waiting, however unknowing, to be called.

"Ives," Sebastian said again, and pointed.

The child touched her own collarbone. Then, deliberately, she took up the stub of charcoal Sebastian used for sketching escape routes, knelt on the floorboards, and drew.

Sebastian had seen Oathscript glyphs before — everyone in her trade had, pressed into old wax seals on contraband charms, inked into the margins of books too dangerous to burn publicly. They were supposed to look like nothing: loops and bars and interlocking angles, geometric nonsense. Quill had a drawer full of them he sold to collectors who liked the frisson of forbidden geometry.

What the child drew was not nonsense.

It was a single glyph — but drawn the way a musician plays a note rather than copies it. The lines moved. Not visibly, not with any trick of light, but Sebastian's eye kept sliding off them and returning, unable to settle, the way one cannot stare directly at one's own pulse. And beneath her breastbone, in the place where seven years should have been, something *ached*. A deep, molar ache, the ache of a tooth that knows the pull is coming, throbbing in time with the charcoal's strokes.

"Stop," Sebastian said, and heard her own voice come out wrong.

The child stopped. Looked up. And — this was the part Sebastian would turn over for many nights after — the child *apologized*. Not in gesture, not in common tongue. In that language, two soft syllables directed at Sebastian like water offered to a burned hand, and though Sebastian understood not one word, she understood entirely.

*It hurts you. I'm sorry.*

She put the charcoal down and did not draw again that day.

* * *

Teaching her thieves' signs was, Sebastian told herself, pure practicality. A mute child in Vellharrow was a liability; a mute child who couldn't be warned away from a watchman's whistle was a dead one. That was all. The tightness in her chest when the girl's fingers first fumbled through *danger* and *hide* and *run* was indigestion, or the damp, or seven years of bad sleep finding their interest.

They worked at the table by the window, in the grey hour between the market's closing and the lamps coming on, because that hour belonged to no one.

"*Run.*" Sebastian flicked two fingers off her palm, sharp.

Ives copied it. Her hands were small and ink-stained and terribly earnest, and she made the sign like a question, fingers hovering: *like this?*

"Again."

Again. Better. Again. The third time, the sign came clean and quick, and something in Sebastian's chest did a thing she chose not to examine — a small warm collapse, like a lock giving way under the right pick.

"*Hide.*" Flat hand sliding behind the other. "*Watch.*" Two fingers, angled eyes. "*Money.*" Thumb rubbing fingers, contemptuous. "*Liar.*" The most important word in the trade: tongue against cheek, a smile that didn't reach.

Ives learned the way water learns a channel — without apparent effort, without resistance, simply flowing into whatever shape the moment required. Within three days she could hold a silent conversation of survival. Within five, she'd begun improvising: a new sign, invented and offered shyly, tapping her chest then pointing at Sebastian — *you* — then pressing both palms flat together over her heart.

"What's that one?" Sebastian asked, wary.

Ives pointed at the sign, then at Sebastian, then made the sign again. *You. This. You.*

"That's not thieves' cant."

A shrug, eloquent in its smallness. *It is now.*

Sebastian let it stand, because refusing seemed somehow crueler than allowing a nonsense sign to exist. She used it exactly once, that same evening, signing *stay here* before slipping out to fence a locket's contents — and caught herself adding, unbidden, the pressed palms. *Stay here. You. Heart.*

She scowled at her own hands the entire walk down the stairwell.

* * *

But the girl taught back. That was the trouble. That was the wound in all of it.

She drew in the margins of everything: flour dusted on the tabletop, breath fogged on the winter glass, charcoal on the backs of Quill's wrapping papers. Small glyphs at first — Sebastian recognized the ache they raised, duller than the first one, like distant weather. Then, on the ninth day, a sequence: three glyphs in a row, arranged with the care of someone laying stones across a stream, and Ives tapped the middle one and looked at Sebastian with an expression of such grave importance that Sebastian's mouth went dry before her mind supplied a reason.

The ache arrived like a pulled tooth finally letting go — a bright, sick lurch behind her sternum, and with it, half a second, no more, of *something*: snow falling through torchlight, a knife handle worn smooth by someone else's grip, her own voice saying words she could not afterward remember having said—

Gone. Severed at the edge, as always, the missing years sealed shut like a door bricked from the inside.

Sebastian found herself gripping the table's edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles, breathing like a woman surfaced too fast from deep water.

Ives had gone pale too. She scrubbed at the glyph with her palm, smearing it into grey ghost, and signed rapidly — the pressed palms, *heart*, then hands spread helplessly — *sorry, sorry, sorry* — and then, hesitantly, a new sign Sebastian hadn't taught: one finger touching the center of her own forehead, then extended toward Sebastian. A question.

*Does it live in there? The hurt?*

"Don't draw that one again," Sebastian managed. "Whatever it says. Don't."

Ives nodded slowly. Then, with the terrible patience of children and tides, she waited until Sebastian turned away to bank the fire, and drew it once more in the frost on the window — small, quickly, watching Sebastian's back the whole time — and studied it herself with narrowed eyes, memorizing its shape, its weight, its *meaning*, filing it wherever she filed things, in that locked room behind her eyes.

Testing it. Keeping it. For later. For Sebastian.

* * *

The nights were worst. Or best. Sebastian declined to decide.

The child slept in the bed now — Sebastian had stopped pretending she'd reclaim it — and Sebastian had resumed her floor, and some nights the girl talked in her sleep. Not the ash-soft syllables. Other nights, rarer, she dreamed badly: small sounds, a whimper pitched below hearing, hands opening and closing on nothing. The first time, Sebastian lay in the dark listening and felt the flinch come — the same flinch the church bells provoked, the flinch with no history attached — and understood suddenly that she and the child were alike in at least one respect: both carrying wounds whose origins had been taken from them.

She crossed the room before she decided to. Pulled the blanket higher over small shoulders. Ives, half-waking, caught her wrist — quick as any cutpurse, and Sebastian felt a stab of absurd pride — and held it, just held it, pressing the pressed-palms sign weakly against Sebastian's skin before sinking back under.

*Heart. You. Heart.*

Sebastian sat on the edge of the bed for longer than dignity allowed, looking at the frost-feathered window, at the city beyond it with its thousand lit windows and its king who never blinked, and admitted it the way one admits a theft to oneself: quietly, privately, with an accurate accounting.

She was getting attached. Found family, the fences called it, when they bothered to sentimentalize at all — the networks of obligation that grew up between people the world had broken in matching places. Sebastian had spent seven years cultivating careful loneliness the way other women cultivated gardens, pruning every attachment before it could take root, because attachments were leverage and leverage got people emptied into ledgers. She had a system. The system worked.

And now there was a child asleep in her bed who spoke the language bindings were sworn in, whose drawings made her missing years throb like a bruise pressed from the inside, who had invented a sign for *you-heart* and given it away freely, expecting nothing back, which was the most alarming magical act Sebastian had witnessed all year.

"Inconvenient," she told the sleeping girl, softly. "You are profoundly inconvenient."

Ives smiled in her dream. It was, Sebastian noted with resignation, the wrong kind of smile — too knowing for sleep, too wide for a child, the corners pulling just slightly too far, and for one vertiginous moment the face wore an expression Sebastian had seen only once before, across a crowded square, on a king greeting petitioners.

Then the child sighed, and rolled over, and was only a child again — small, ink-fingered, breathing slow — and Sebastian told herself she'd imagined it, the way she told herself the bells meant nothing, the way she told herself the ache in her chest was only the ache in her chest.

On the frosted window, faint in the moonlight, the glyph the girl had redrawn still lingered where her breath had written it. Three shapes in a row. A sentence in a language older than any kingdom.

Sebastian didn't know what it said.

But somewhere beneath seven years of brick and mortar, something in her did — and it had begun, patiently as ivy, to grow toward the light of that knowledge. Three days later, when Quill's runner scratched at her door with a message about a buyer asking unusual questions about Oathscript wax seals, Sebastian read the note twice, looked at the child eating bread at her table, and reached for her coat.

Some doors, once drawn on frost, insist on being opened.
