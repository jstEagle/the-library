## 6. The Mnemonist's Ledger

The interrogation rooms beneath the Hall of Records had no windows, which Serane Corvane considered a professional courtesy to the work. Sunlight made people sentimental. Lamplight made them honest — or at least it made them tired enough that honesty and exhaustion became indistinguishable, and in her experience the distinction rarely mattered.

She had been an inquisitor of the Mnemonic Office for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that memory was the only honest currency left in Vellharrow. Coin could be clipped. Deeds could be forged. Bloodlines could be invented by a sufficiently patient genealogist. But a memory, once taken true and held under glass, did not lie. It might grieve you. It might ruin you. But it did not lie.

Which was why she disliked Quill on principle before she ever laid eyes on him.

The fence of magical goods kept his shop in the Tanners' Reach, wedged between a chandler and a man who sold songbirds by the ounce, and it smelled the way all his kind's establishments smelled: of beeswax and cold iron and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked drawer. Preserved memories. They always soured eventually, no matter how well they were stoppered. Everything stolen did.

"Serane Corvane," she said, setting her warrant on his counter with two fingers, the way one sets down a dead bird. "Office of Mnemonic Integrity. You'll come with me."

Quill was a small man built like a coiled rope, all knots and tension, with spectacles so thick his eyes looked like coins at the bottom of a fountain. He looked at the warrant, then at her, then at the two gray-cloaked wardens flanking his door, and Serane watched him perform the arithmetic every smuggler performed: the cost of running against the cost of cooperating, weighted by the odds of each ending in blood.

"I'll bring my coat," he said. "It's cold in your cells."

"It is," Serane agreed. "That's not an accident."

* * *

The cell they used for first interviews was not a cell at all but a room furnished like a poor clerk's study: a table, three chairs, a lamp turned low, a pitcher of water gone warm. The cruelty of it was deliberate. Men expected pain from the Crown's inquisitors and braced themselves accordingly; what undid them was the suggestion that this was merely paperwork, that their ruin was a formality already filed.

Quill sat with his hands flat on the table, which told her he'd been interrogated before and knew where his hands were safest. Good. She hated starting from nothing.

"Do you know why you're here?" she asked.

"Because someone talked."

"Someone always talks. That's not a reason. That's weather." She opened the leather folio she'd carried down from the Hall and let him see the thickness of it without letting him see its contents. "Three weeks ago, a woman died in the Saltrow almshouse. Widow Anselm, sixty-one years. In the last hour of her life, someone took her happiest memory out of a locket charm and left her with nothing but the shape of having been happy once. The almshouse matron says the widow spent her final minutes asking everyone around her why she couldn't stop smiling when she couldn't remember what there was to smile about." Serane let that sit. "Her locket was traced through four hands to a stall in the night market off Cordwainer's Lane. A stall that pays rent to a man named Quill."

"I don't control my tenants' inventory."

"You don't control anything. That's your genius. You're a man who has arranged his whole life so that nothing can be pinned to him. And yet." She slid a single sheet across the table. It bore a charcoal rubbing of a maker's mark — a quill inside a keyhole — lifted from the locket's clasp. "Your mark. On the housing. Which means either you crafted the charm yourself or you certified it, and both are unsanctioned trade in mnemonic goods under Statute Nine."

Quill looked at the rubbing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone carefully pleasant, the way voices do when they're being carried across ice. "Statute Nine has more exceptions than rules, Inquisitor. Half the relics in the Crown repository were bought from men like me."

"And half the men like me started by asking exactly that question." She leaned back. "Here is what happens now. You give me names. Sellers, buyers, routes. You give me the woman who emptied that locket, and you give me whatever else you know about her, and in exchange your name appears nowhere in my report except as *cooperating witness, identity withheld*. Refuse, and I take this shop apart board by board, and everything I find becomes yours in law as surely as if you'd signed it."

He was quiet long enough that she heard the water dripping somewhere in the building's bones, the slow tick of the old Hall settling into the earth.

"She's careful," he said at last. "Whoever it is. Careful in a way that isn't fear. Fear makes people sloppy in patterns. This one's sloppy in ways that look like grief."

"Grief."

"She leaves things. Little things. A charm returned to a doorstep with the extraction fee tucked inside, when she didn't have to return anything. Once she took a memory out of a dying man's head and left him a different one in its place — a good one, fabricated, seamless work — so his family would have something to bury him with." Quill's coin-eyed gaze came up to hers. "You've been doing this eleven years, Inquisitor. How many thieves fabricate replacements?"

Serane did not answer, because the honest answer was *one*, and she had been thinking about that one for three days.

"The night market keeps no ledgers," Quill went on. "But I keep accounts. Not of names — of debts. Everyone who trades in stolen hours owes somebody, and I'm owed by nearly all of them." He wet his lips. "There's a page you want. Seven years back. Autumn. There was a run of extractions that season, high-quality work, too clean for the usual gutter-cutters. My ledger records the certification fees. If your office ever audited me properly, you'd have found it years ago."

"Why didn't we?"

"Because seven years ago, someone in your office decided that page wasn't worth reading." Something moved behind the thick lenses — not defiance. Wariness. The wariness of a man who had survived a long time by knowing precisely which doors stayed shut. "I'll show you the page. That's my cooperation. Names after, if we're still friendly."

* * *

They brought the ledger up to her own office, because Serane wanted witnesses for the handling and none for the reading. Her office sat on the fourth floor of the Hall of Records, a narrow room lined floor to ceiling with drawers of glass-stoppered vials, each labeled in her own hand: *deposition, verified; deposition, partial; deposition, sealed by order.* At night, with the lamps lit, the whole wall glowed faintly amber, like the inside of a hive. She had always found it beautiful. Lately she had begun to find it suffocating, though she could not have said why, and the inability to say why bothered her more than the feeling itself.

Quill's ledger was a fat thing bound in green calf, its spine cracked white with use. He opened it to a ribbon marker near the middle and turned it toward her with one finger, not touching the pages beyond what was necessary, a scrupulousness she noted and distrusted.

"Autumn. Seven years past," he said. "Certification fees, paid in advance, per item. See the hand? Same hand all the way down. Steady. Professional. Someone who'd been trained."

Serane read.

The entries ran down the page in a neat, upright script, each line a small transaction rendered banal by bookkeeping: *two hours, sourced private, cert. fee 3 crowns. One hour, sourced private, cert. fee 2 crowns. Fragmentary set, six items, sourced private, cert. fee 12 crowns.*

Fragmentary set, six items.

She had read ten thousand ledgers in her career and most of them meant nothing, but something in that phrase snagged her the way a nail snags silk. Fragmentary sets were rare. Extraction took whole contiguous memories; a fragmented set meant a mind deliberately broken into pieces before removal, either by trauma or by design. Nobody fragmented their own memory by accident.

And the dates. She counted backward without meaning to, an old habit, the inquisitor's reflex of placing every fact against the calendar of state. The entries clustered in the third week of the autumn month — the same week, she realized with a slow cold spreading under her sternum, as the Affair of the King's Vigil. The week the young King Aldric had canceled three public audiences in a row and the court had explained it as a fever, and the city had believed it because believing was cheaper than the alternative.

Coincidence. She wrote the word in her mind firmly, the way one writes a label on a drawer. The city produced coincidences by the thousand weekly.

Then she saw the name.

It was not the seller's name. The seller was recorded, as sellers always were in Quill's private book, by mark rather than by name — a small drawn sigil, a winter tree bare of leaves, struck through with a single diagonal line. She copied it into her notebook without knowing why, her pencil moving with a steadiness she was grateful for.

It was the witness name. Every certification fee in Quill's world required a countersignature — some licensed party willing to swear the goods were what they claimed, a formality, a legal fig leaf. Most fences used the same bored notary for years. Quill's countersignature for that entire autumn, for every entry on that page, was a name she knew.

*Corvane, S., Office of Mnemonic Integrity.*

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the Hall's clocks changed the quarter-hour, and the sound came up through the floor like a heartbeat felt through a wall.

"That's not possible," she said, and heard how thin her voice came out.

"No," Quill agreed mildly. "It isn't. And yet."

"I never—" She stopped. Rebuilt. "I joined the Office nine years ago. This predates my service by—"

"By two years. Yes. I know." Quill folded his hands. "I also know that signature. I watched it written. A woman, dark-haired, gray cloak, ink on her fingers, who looked at me the way you're looking at me now — like she was trying to remember whether she'd met me before and couldn't make the answer come." He tilted his head. "Inquisitor. Have you ever lost time? Not sleep. Time. Woken with your boots muddy and your ledgers up to date and no memory of the day between?"

Serane did not answer. There was a hole in her memory — she had always known there was a hole, the way you know about a missing tooth, by the shape of the gap — a fortnight, early in her career, that the Office's physicians had attributed to marsh fever contracted during a posting in the Fens. She had accepted the explanation because the Office provided it, and because the alternative was the kind of question that ended careers, or lives, or both.

"Seven years ago," Quill said softly, "someone walked into my shop wearing your face and your seal and countersigned a series of extractions that should never have been certified. Six fragments, pulled from a single source, broken apart on purpose. And then that person vanished from my life until you walked in tonight wearing the same face and asking about a locket." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried, a man buying himself time to watch her. "I've wondered about it for years. What was in those six pieces. Who broke them. Why the Office buried the page." The spectacles went back on. "I wonder if you wonder too."

"Give me the seller's mark," Serane said. "The winter tree. Whose is it?"

"Now that," said Quill, "is the part that costs extra."

* * *

She released him at dusk with the promised paper — *cooperating witness, identity withheld* — and stood at her office window afterward watching the lamplighters work their way along the Processional, flame by flame, the city lighting itself against the dark the way it did every night, obedient and unthinking.

The amber glow of the vial-wall lay across her desk. She had pulled the old file while Quill waited downstairs: her own service record, the marsh fever, the physician's certificate, the fortnight of missing days stamped *excused, medical* in an archivist's hand. Two years before her recorded certification of Quill's autumn ledger. Two years that the ledger said she had spent standing in a fence's shop with ink on her fingers.

Either the ledger lied, or the record did. Or — and here was the thought she kept setting down and picking up again, unable to leave it alone — or both were true, and the woman in the ledger was her, and the woman in the record was also her, and between the two of them was a fortnight that someone had taken out and stoppered and shelved, the way the Office shelved everything, in glass, in the dark, labeled by someone who wanted it forgotten.

*Deposition, sealed by order.*

Whose order?

She looked at the charcoal rubbing of the winter-tree mark in her notebook. Bare branches, struck through. A tree in winter crossed out — erased, denied, hidden. Somewhere in the Tanners' Reach and the night markets and the crooked lanes above the harbor, someone wore that mark, someone who had sold six broken pieces of a memory seven years ago during the very week the king had taken to his rooms and come out smiling wrong.

Serane Corvane had spent eleven years believing that memory was the only honest currency, that her drawers of amber glass were a treasury of truth, that the Office served the truth the way priests were said to serve gods who had stopped answering.

Now she sat alone with a ledger that proved her own hand had touched the lie, and she understood, with the particular horror of the dutiful, that she had been not the treasurer of truth but one of its thieves — that somewhere in her own sealed drawers, or in someone else's, lay a fortnight of her life that had been confiscated by the very institution she served.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the vial-wall glowed on, patient as a jury.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would request the certification logs for the Affair of the King's Vigil. Tomorrow she would find the winter tree.

Tonight she allowed herself, just once, to say aloud in the empty office the sentence she had been carrying since sundown:

"What did I sign?"

The amber drawers gave no answer. But far across the city, in a garret above the Tanner's Reach, a lamp burned late over a child's drawings of branching glyphs — and Serane Corvane, who could not have known it, had already begun walking toward them.
