## 7. The Man Who Sold Himself Twice

The dead were easier to find than the living. Sebastian had learned that in her first year of the trade: grief kept addresses alive longer than love did. People wrote to their dead. They paid rent on empty rooms. They left lamps burning in windows for men who had been drowned, hanged, or — in one particular case in the Saltrow district — officially cremated seven years ago at a funeral attended by two hundred mourners and not one true tear.

Dree Halloway was dead the way old debts are dead: on paper, and nowhere else.

Sebastian had spent three days finding him, and she had done it the way she did everything now — carefully, with Ives tucked against her side like a knife she hadn't decided whether to carry or throw into the river. The trail ran through a pawnbroker who owed Dree money from before the funeral, through a widow who still lit a candle for him every Duskday because he had once pulled her son's nightmares out like rotten teeth, and finally through a fishwife at the Brackish Gate who took Sebastian's coin and said only: *The house with the gull on the roof. Knock like rain, not like law.*

So they went, at the grey end of an afternoon when Vellharrow smelled of coal smoke and low tide, down streets that narrowed as they descended until the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators sharing a secret. The house with the gull on the roof stood at the bottom of Cutlass Lane, a crooked thing of salt-black timber with a wooden gull nailed to its ridgepole, wings spread in permanent flight toward a sea it would never reach.

Ives stopped at the threshold and would not cross it.

Sebastian looked down at her. The child's face had gone strange — not frightened, exactly. *Attentive.* The way a dog goes attentive before an earthquake, ears forward, eyes fixed on nothing visible.

"What is it?" Sebastian asked, though she had learned by now that questions aimed at Ives mostly came back as silence, or as glyphs scratched in dirt that made Sebastian's skull ache behind the eyes.

Ives raised one small hand and pointed — not at the house, but at the air above it. At something in the texture of the light, or the smoke, or whatever else children who spoke dead languages could see that honest thieves could not.

Then she stepped over the threshold first, pulling Sebastian after her by two fingers, which was how Ives held hands: as if letting go were a possibility she refused to entertain.

* * *

The door opened before Sebastian knocked. That should have frightened her more than it did. Instead it settled something in her chest, cold and familiar, like a coin dropped into an old pocket. Of course he'd know she was coming. Dree Halloway had always known things slightly before they happened, the way a good thief knows where the watchman's lantern will swing next.

The man in the doorway was a ruin wearing her teacher's posture.

He had been broad once, Dree — barrel-chested, loud, a man who filled rooms the way spilled wine fills floorboards. What remained of him was folded and diminished, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin gone stiff with salt, his beard white and untrimmed, his hands — those hands, those famous hands that could lift a memory out of a living mind as gently as lifting a moth from water — cracked and knuckle-swollen from decades of nets and rope. Only the eyes were unchanged. Grey as ledger paper. Still doing arithmetic on everything they saw.

They went through her face line by line. Her jaw. Her scars. The new calluses on her fingers. Then past her, down to the child clutching her coat hem.

Sebastian watched the arithmetic stop. Watched the answer arrive. Watched Dree Halloway go white in a way she had never seen a man go white — not like fear, not like illness, but like a page being wiped clean. All the blood left his face at once, as if his body understood something terrible before his mind consented to it.

"No," he said. His voice was rust and rope-fiber. "No, no. You get her off my step. You take her back wherever she came from and you lose her in the crowd, girl, do you hear me—"

"Hello to you too, Dree." Sebastian heard her own voice come out flat and cold and was glad of it. Seven years of rehearsing this conversation in the dark had at least bought her a steady voice. "Lovely funeral, by the way. Very well attended. I wept."

"You didn't." He said it with certainty, and hated himself for the certainty, and she saw him hate it.

"No," she agreed. "I didn't. Because I couldn't remember why I was supposed to. Because someone sold my grief along with everything else, and I've spent seven years flinching at church bells without knowing why." She stepped forward, into the doorway, forcing him back. "We're coming in, teacher. Unless you want this conversation on your step, where the whole of Cutlass Lane can enjoy it."

For a moment she thought he might actually shut the door in her face. His hand rose toward it — then dropped. He turned and shuffled inside, and the set of his shoulders was so defeated, so *old*, that some treacherous part of her wanted to comfort him, and she strangled that part where it stood.

*You owe me,* she reminded herself, following Ives into the dark. *Whatever else you are, whatever you did — you owe me.*

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of tallow and dried kelp and, beneath it, faintly — so faintly she might have imagined it — of the old workroom on Candlemaker's Row where she had apprenticed. Chalk dust and hot metal and the particular ozone bite of freshly handled memory. Her stomach clenched around the smell like a fist.

The single room was a fisherman's room: nets coiled on pegs, a stove, a cot, mended oilskins. But there were additions that belonged to no fisherman. A shelf of blank lockets, dozens of them, each one sealed with wax. A mortar and pestle stained with the blue residue of dream-salts. And on the wall above the hearth, hung where a pious family would hang a saint's card, a strip of parchment covered in dense, angular script that made the backs of Sebastian's eyes itch to look at.

She didn't ask about it. Not yet. She sat instead on the one stool, pulled Ives onto her knee — the child came willingly, watching Dree with that same attentive stillness — and waited.

Dree put water on to boil he didn't intend to drink. A stalling ritual. She let him have it. Outside, the tide groaned against the pilings below Cutlass Lane, and somewhere a gull screamed its one ugly note, and the light through the salt-fogged window went from grey to copper as evening came on.

Finally he spoke, facing the stove. "How did you find me?"

"A pawnbroker, a widow, and a fishwife. In that order. Your death was very thorough, Dree, but you left debts everywhere, and debts remember."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. "That's the first thing I ever taught you, isn't it. Debts remember."

"It's the second. The first was never sell what you can't afford to lose." She let that sit between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "Funny thing, that lesson. I've been trying to work out lately whether I ever learned it. Or whether someone took the learning away before I could."

"I don't know anything about that."

"Liar."

"I don't know anything about that," he repeated, "that I'm free to tell you." He turned at last and looked at her, and the honesty in his ruined face was worse than any lie. "There's a difference, girl. There's a difference, and it's the whole of my life now, that difference."

"Then tell me what you *are* free to tell me." She reached into her coat and drew out the scrap of birch bark she'd been carrying for three days, wrapped in silk like the relic it apparently was. On it, in Ives's careful childish hand, the glyphs she'd scratched in the ash outside Quill's shop. The ones that had made Quill — a man who fenced cursed mirrors without blinking — go pale and refuse payment. "Start with this."

Dree wouldn't touch the bark. He looked at it from across the room, from all of four feet away, as if it lay at the bottom of a well.

"Where did she write this?"

"She writes it everywhere. In ash, in flour, in the condensation on windows. She doesn't sleep unless she's written it somewhere near. It's the only word she has." Sebastian kept her voice level with effort. "Quill called it Oathscript. He said it was the tongue bindings used to be sworn in, back when binding was legal, back before the Purges. He said nobody living speaks it. And yet here's a mute child who does nothing else." She leaned forward. "And here's you, Dree, looking at it like it's your own headstone. So. Read it to me."

"I can't."

"You taught me the recognition drills. Old Oathscript, defensive study, 'a thief must know the shape of the chains,' you said. You made me copy the glyph-tables fifty times each. You—" She stopped. Something moved in her memory, or tried to, and hit the wall where her missing years lived, and the ache of it was sudden and physical, a tooth pulled long ago still throbbing in a jaw that remembered. "You made me learn them. Why would a memory-thief teach his apprentice the language of binding magic? Why would a *dead man* keep Oathscript hanging over his hearth?"

Dree lowered himself onto the cot across from her. It took him a long time, joints protesting, and Sebastian understood suddenly that the diminishment wasn't age alone. Guilt had been working on him for seven years the way water works on stone. It had hollowed him.

"That's not a word," he said quietly. "What she wrote. It's not a word the way you mean a word. It's half of one."

"Half of what?"

He looked at Ives then — really looked, the way he'd once looked at difficult extractions, with total attention, total respect, and total dread. And Ives looked back at him, unblinking, and lifted one small hand, and traced a shape in the air between them. Just air. Nothing visible. But Dree flinched as if she'd drawn a knife.

"The counter-word," he said. "To a royal binding."

The stove ticked. The tide groaned. Somewhere above them the wooden gull creaked on its nail, turning in a wind Sebastian couldn't feel.

"Say that again," she said, "slowly."

"There are two halves to every great binding, girl. The oath and the counter-oath. The lock and the key cut from the same iron. When the great bindings were sworn — the old ones, the ones the histories were burned for — the counter-word was never spoken aloud. It was *carried*. Given to a vessel. Kept apart from the binding itself, because if the two ever met..." He made a gesture, loose and terrible, like something unfolding. "The binding comes undone. Whatever was bound comes *out*."

"And what's bound under King Aldric's crown?"

Dree's hands, those beautiful ruined hands, closed into fists on his knees. "I don't know its name. Names are part of what binds it. I know it's old — older than Aldric, older than the Winterbourne kings, maybe older than Vellharrow. I know it's worn eleven crowns before this one, and eaten nine of its wearers down to the skin and kept the rest as coats." His voice dropped. "And I know that seven years ago, in a cellar under the palace, a group of people swore a crooked oath to cage it — a rushed, imperfect, bleeding binding, done in desperation with half the proper rite missing — and that it held. Barely. Like a dam holds. With groaning."

The room had gone very cold, or Sebastian had. "Seven years ago," she said.

"Yes."

"The same seven years that are missing from my head."

"Yes."

"Dree." Her voice came out very quiet, which she knew, from experience, frightened people more than shouting. "Look at me and tell me I wasn't there."

He looked at her. He had taught her everything — how to hold a stranger's memory like a bird, how to lie with her pulse, how to walk away from a job that smelled wrong. He had taught her that a thief's best tool was a face that gave nothing away. And now she watched him try to use that face on her, his own student, and fail utterly, because guilt had eaten through it like rot through a hull.

"You were there," he said.

Ives made a sound. Small, high, involuntary — the first noise Sebastian had ever heard her make that wasn't Oathscript, just a child's frightened breath given voice. Her small hand found Sebastian's and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

Sebastian didn't feel it. She was standing, though she didn't remember standing. The room tilted around the fixed point of Dree's grey eyes.

"What was my part?" she asked. "Tell me my part."

"No."

"*My part*, Dree. Did I hold a door? Did I stand watch? Did I—" The words came faster now, spilling, seven years of flinching at bells and waking at midnight with her hands aching as if they'd gripped something. "Why can't I remember? Was it taken from me, or did I give it? Whose idea was the selling? *Was it mine?*"

"Stop." He was up off the cot now too, and for one moment the old breadth of him reasserted itself, the teacher's authority, the voice that had commanded workrooms. "You listen to me, Sebastian Winterbourne, and you listen well, because I'll say this once and then I'm done. Yes. You were there. Yes, it was your choice — every part of it, including the forgetting, was your choice, made freely, for reasons that were *good reasons*. And if I told you those reasons tonight, in this room, with that child listening—" his eyes flicked to Ives, and something in his face cracked further, "—then everything you paid would be spent for nothing, and worse than nothing, and I will not be the man who did that to you twice."

"Twice," she whispered. "You said twice. You've already done it to me once."

Dree closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the teacher was gone out of them, and what remained was just an old criminal in a dead man's house, drowning slowly in a truth he'd carried alone for seven years.

"Girl," he said gently, "you have no idea what you've already survived. That's the mercy of it. That's the *whole* mercy."

* * *

Later — much later, after the stove had burned down and Ives had finally, exhaustedly, slept against Sebastian's side on the cot, one hand still curled around Sebastian's finger even in sleep — Sebastian stood at the salt-fogged window and watched the lights of Vellharrow climb the hills above the harbor. Ten thousand windows. Ten thousand lives being remembered, hour by hour, by the people living them. Such a simple miracle. She had stolen thousands of hours of it and never once thought to envy it until it was taken from her.

Behind her, Dree sat by the embers, awake, watching the sleeping child with an expression Sebastian couldn't read — except that it wasn't wonder, and it wasn't tenderness. It was recognition. The look of a man seeing a bill come due.

"She's the counter-word," Sebastian said softly, not turning. "Not just carrying it. She *is* it, somehow. Aren't she. That's why the charms rot near her. That's why Quill's wards curdled. She's not a child who speaks Oathscript, Dree. She's Oathscript that learned to walk."

Dree said nothing, which was its own confession.

"And the renewal rite," she went on, her breath fogging the glass. "Seven years. Everyone says the king renews his coronation oath every year on Founding's Eve, private ceremony, no witnesses. Binding magic needs renewing, doesn't it, teacher? Locks wear. Damns groan." Now she turned. "It's in three weeks. And I've got half a key asleep on this cot, and a teacher who'd rather die twice than tell me what I did, and inquisitors hunting us through every market in this city. So here's what happens next, Dree. Either you start telling me things — the things you're *free* to tell me, all of them, starting with who else was in that cellar — or I walk out that door and take my chances with the Crown's mnemonic men, who at least ask questions worth answering."

Dree Halloway looked at her for a long time across the dying fire. Then he got up, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and went to the wall above the hearth, and took down the strip of parchment covered in the script that made her eyes itch.

He held it out to her with both hands, the way one returns a borrowed weapon.

"Sit down, girl," he said. "It's a long cellar, and everyone who was in it is either dead, damned, or hunting you. We'll need the whole night."

He hesitated, and added, almost too quietly to hear:

"And pray the thing under the crown hasn't already felt her walking around the city. Because if it has — if it knows its key is loose — then it won't send courtiers after you anymore."

Outside, far up the hill, in the direction of the palace, a bell began to toll. Sebastian flinched. She always flinched.

But this time, watching Dree's face, she understood the flinch had never been hers at all. It had been left in her, planted, the way a man leaves a mark on a door he means to come back to.

Something had been coming back for seven years.

And now it knew where to knock.
