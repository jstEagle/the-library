## 8. Seven Years Missing

Dree Halloway's safehouse was a coffin with a chimney.

It sat at the bottom of a drowned lane in the Sump, where the river had given up its claim a century ago and left behind a street of leaning houses like drunkards propping one another upright. Sebastian had walked past it twice before she found the door — a door painted the exact grey of every other door, marked with nothing, known to no one. But her fingers knew. They rose on their own and tapped the rhythm she hadn't consciously remembered learning: two, pause, three, pause, one. *Thief's greeting. Friend at the threshold.*

The door opened before the last knock fell.

Dree Halloway looked like a man who had been embalmed while still ambulatory. He was thinner than the ghost of him she'd carried — though how could she carry his ghost when she couldn't remember ever meeting him? She knew him only from Quill's terrified whispering and from the glyphs in Ives's drawings, and yet her body recognized him with a horrible intimacy: the way her eye went first to his hands, the way her feet had already mapped the room's exits before he finished closing the door.

"Seven years," Dree said. His voice was ash and gravel, a voice that hadn't been used for much besides muttering. "Seven years, and you knock like you never left."

"I don't know you." The words came out flat, rehearsed, as if she'd said them before. Perhaps she had. Perhaps this conversation had happened in some other room, in some other life, and been stolen along with everything else.

"No," Dree agreed. He turned away, busying himself at the stove where something bitter stewed. "That's rather the point."

Ives stood pressed against Sebastian's leg, one small hand fisted in the hem of her coat. The girl stared at Dree with an expression Sebastian had never seen on her face before — not fear exactly, but recognition of a colder kind. The look of someone meeting the author of their story and finding him smaller than expected.

"She shouldn't be here," Dree said without turning around.

"She has nowhere else."

"Everyone has somewhere else. That's what makes them people." He ladled something grey into three bowls anyway, and set them on a table scarred with old burns. Old burns in the shape of glyphs. Sebastian's stomach turned over slowly, like something waking in deep water.

They ate in silence. The stew tasted of nothing much — turnips and regret — but Ives ate hers with the desperate efficiency of a child who had learned never to trust a full bowl to stay full. Sebastian watched her over the rim of her own bowl and felt the ache in her missing years throb again, that pulled-tooth pain that had lived behind her eyes since the night the child arrived.

"Dree," she said. "Quill told me your name. My mouth said it like it knew it. My hands know your door. There is a table in my garret with a burn shaped like the glyph Ives drew on my ceiling. I want it back. All of it. Whatever you took—"

"I didn't take anything." He set down his spoon with exaggerated care, as though even small violence might shatter something. "That's the thing you have to understand, girl. No one took anything from you."

"Then explain the hole."

"You dug the hole yourself." He looked at her then, fully, and Sebastian saw that his eyes were wet-rimmed and exhausted, the eyes of a man who had been waiting seven years for this conversation and dreading every hour of the wait. "You sold your own memories. Willingly. Freely. With witnesses and a contract and a price paid in full. You are not a victim of theft, Sebastian. You are the thief. The job you did on yourself was the cleanest work I ever taught anyone — and I taught you, so it would be."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Sebastian heard them, watched the ripples spread, and could not make sense of the shape beneath.

"That's impossible."

"It's the most possible thing in Vellharrow. People pay me—" he caught himself, "—people used to pay memory-thieves to forget all kinds of things. Dead children. Betrayals. Crimes they couldn't live beside. You didn't hire it done. You did it yourself, which tells you something about how badly you needed it gone. A person who hires out the work keeps a little distance from the blade. You wanted no distance. You wanted it *gone*, root and branch, and you were good enough to make it stick."

"When?" Her voice sounded far away to herself. "When did I do this?"

"Nine days after Midwinter. Seven years ago." He recited it like a date carved into a cell wall. "You came to this room. You were crying. You laid out your kit on that table — the same kit you're carrying now, don't think I haven't noticed — and you worked on yourself for six hours. I sat in that corner and watched, because you asked me to watch, because you were afraid you'd lose your nerve halfway through and leave yourself a ruin. And at the end you stood up, and you looked at me, and you said—"

He stopped.

"*What did I say?*"

"You said: 'Don't tell me. Not ever. If I come back asking, don't tell me.'" Dree's hands were trembling around his bowl. "And I've kept that oath for seven years, which is more than can be said for most of mine."

Sebastian stood up so fast the bench screeched. Fury arrived all at once, whole and blinding, filling every hollow space the missing years had left vacant. It was a gift, almost — seven years of careful loneliness burning off in a single instant, replaced by something hot and alive.

"You watched?" she said. "You sat there and watched me cut myself open and you just — you *obeyed*? Some oathbreaker you are. You break every vow except the ones that hurt!"

"Yes," Dree said simply.

"Don't *yes* me! What did I hide? What was worth seven years of my life? What could possibly be so terrible that I would rather not know my own name?"

"I can't tell you that."

"You mean you won't."

"I mean both, and they are different things, and the difference matters." He rose too, slowly, joints cracking like green firewood, and faced her across the burnt table. Up close, Sebastian could see how badly age had treated him since whatever life they'd shared — or perhaps age hadn't done it at all. Perhaps guilt had. "There are prices in this trade, girl, and you know them better than most. Every extraction takes a little of the taker. You've felt it. The flinch at bells. The dreams you can't hold onto. Now imagine the price of *telling*. Not the telling itself — the knowing. Some truths aren't information. They're weights. And if I put that weight on you before you're strong enough to carry it, it will crush you, and everything we're trying to protect dies with you."

"We?" She seized on it. "*We*? So there's a plan. There's something I was supposed to do, someday, after the hole. You left instructions in my own head and didn't tell me?"

"I left nothing in your head. I swear it by every crooked god." Something moved behind his face — grief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Whatever comes next has to come free, or it doesn't count. That was the condition. Yours, not mine."

"What condition? *Whose* condition?"

"The child's."

The word dropped into the room like ice into water. Ives looked up from her bowl, spoon stilled halfway to her mouth, and her dark eyes went from Sebastian to Dree and back again with the grave attention of someone following a conversation conducted mostly underneath its words.

"She can't have given conditions," Sebastian said. "She wasn't— she's a child. She's ten years old, maybe. Where was she seven years ago?"

"Nowhere," Dree said. "And everywhere. In the way that a promise exists before it's kept." He passed a hand over his face, and for a moment he looked every year of his life and several he hadn't reached yet. "I've said too much already. I can feel it going wrong, the way it always goes wrong. Ask me anything except that. Ask me about the trade. Ask me about your training, your first lift, the night market before the purges. I'll give you all of it, hours of it, every boring beautiful day we—"

"Don't." The fury was shifting now, curdling into something worse: vertigo. The room seemed to tilt. She was standing in a stranger's kitchen being grieved at by a stranger who knew her better than she knew herself, and the child beside her — the child she had fed and sheltered and named, the child whose hand fit inside hers like a key returning to its lock — had somehow been present at the excavation of her own mind before either of them had ever met. "Don't offer me crumbs from my own loaf. You don't get to ration me my life."

"Someone has to." Dree's voice hardened, and beneath the hardness Sebastian heard the teacher she must once have loved, or hated, or both. "You want to know what I remember? I remember you, nine days after Midwinter, kneeling on this floor, sobbing, saying *make sure I don't go looking*. Do you understand what that means? It means the woman standing in front of me right now — the one demanding answers, the one furious at the cage — built the cage. Bar by bar. Paid for it with the best years of her life. And if I knock it down today because she's angry at me, I betray not the woman in front of me. I betray the woman who begged me. And they're the same woman, and they want opposite things, and that—" his voice cracked, "—that is the cruelest work I've ever done, and I once helped bind a king."

Silence. The stew went cold in its bowls. Outside, somewhere above the drowned lane, a church bell began to ring the hour, and Sebastian flinched — that same old flinch, the one with no story attached — and saw Dree see it, and saw something in his face close like a door.

"Why do I flinch at bells?" she asked quietly.

"I can't tell you that."

"Is it part of it? Part of what I hid?"

"Girl—"

"*Is it?*"

"Yes," Dree said, and closed his eyes. "It's the hinge. Everything turns on the bells."

Ives made a sound. Small, urgent, a note in that older-than-kingdoms language, and she slid off the bench and crossed to Sebastian and took her hand — took it in a specific grip, thumb to thumb, fingers interlaced, a grip that meant something, a grip that Sebastian's body understood before her mind rebelled against understanding.

*Steady,* said the grip. *Hold.*

"How does she know that?" Sebastian whispered. "How does she know thief-sign? How does she know *me*?"

"I can't tell you that," Dree said, but his voice had gone rough, and he wouldn't look at the joined hands. "But I'll tell you what I *can* tell you, since you'll only tear my house down otherwise. Three things. First: your memories weren't destroyed. Nothing true is ever destroyed in this trade; it's only moved. They exist, somewhere, whole. Second: they can be recovered. There is a road back, and you've already found its first mile without knowing it — the child, the glyph, the ache. Third." He opened his eyes, and they were terrible and clear. "Third: when you find them, you will have to choose whether to keep them. And I am begging you, as the man who taught you everything, as the only friend your past self trusted enough to ask — when that choice comes, remember that the woman who made it wanted you to have the choice. Not the answer. The choice. Can you carry that much?"

Sebastian's fury had not gone. It sat in her chest like swallowed coals, and it would sit there for a long time, she suspected, perhaps forever. But beneath it, vertigo was giving way to a strange, cold clarity. She had done this. She had stood in this room with her own kit spread on the table and unmade herself, and she had asked the man across the table to guard the ruins. Whatever she had hidden, she had judged it worth her entire self — and some buried part of her, the part that knocked on doors in rhythms she didn't remember learning, had judged it correctly.

Or had been made to judge it correctly. The thought arrived uninvited and refused to leave.

"One question," she said. "Answer it or I walk out that door and start digging on my own, and I'm good enough that I'll find it, and I won't have you watching my nerve."

Dree's jaw tightened. "Ask."

"The contract. When I sold the memories. Extraction always leaves a receipt — buyer, seller, date, price. Where is mine?"

For a long moment Dree said nothing. The fire ticked in the stove. Ives's hand stayed wrapped around hers, steady as a mooring line.

"There is no receipt," Dree said finally. "Because there was no sale. Not in any ledger, not in any market. You didn't sell your memories, Sebastian." He drew a breath that shook on the way in. "You *hid* them. Sealed them, warded them, locked them behind your best work — behind a door that can only be opened from a place no fence will name and no inquisitor will ever find. You told me the location once. I let myself forget it. Deliberately. Best extraction I ever did was on myself, forgetting where you put yourself."

The floor seemed to drop away. Sebastian gripped the table's edge. Hidden, then — not sold. Buried treasure instead of traded goods. Which meant somewhere in Vellharrow — or beneath it, or beyond it — there was a sealed vault containing seven years of Sebastian Winterbourne, intact, waiting, warded by her own hand against her own curiosity.

"And the key?" she asked. Her voice was almost steady. "Every seal has a key. Even mine."

Dree looked at her. Then, slowly, terribly, he looked at Ives.

The child looked back at him, and then up at Sebastian, and very carefully — with the gravity of someone performing a rite rather than answering a question — she raised her free hand and drew a single glyph in the cold air between them. The air held it for a heartbeat, faintly luminous, a shape like a door seen edge-on.

Then she folded Sebastian's fingers closed around nothing at all, and pressed her small forehead against their joined knuckles.

"You see the problem," Dree said softly.

Outside, far across the sleeping city, toward the palace on its hill, a bell began to toll. Not the hour-bell. The other one — the deep, slow bell Sebastian had heard only twice before, the one the criers called the King's Peace and everyone else called nothing at all, because calling it anything invited questions.

Tolling once. Pausing. Tolling again.

Counting down to something.

Dree was already moving, snuffing lamps, pulling a travel-stained cloak from behind the stove. "They've started early," he said. "I have eleven days, maybe less, and I swore an oath seven years ago that I'd spend them getting you ready — whether you hate me or not." He threw her a second cloak, which she caught without thinking, her hands remembering the weight. "Eat the rest of the stew. We leave before dawn. And Sebastian—"

She looked up.

"When the choice comes — and it's coming faster than either of us planned — don't ask me what you hid." His eyes flicked once, briefly, to Ives. "Ask yourself why the child is the key. And then ask whether some doors should open."

* * *

They left the drowned lane in the blue-black hour before dawn, three shadows moving through streets still slick with night fog. Dree led them down ways Sebastian's feet knew and her mind didn't — through a tannery yard, under an aqueduct, along a canal path where the water lay still as poured glass. At every turning he paused and listened, and at the third turning Sebastian heard what he heard: boots. Rhythmic. Many. Moving in the pattern the Crown's mnemonic inquisitors used when they swept a district — not hunting, yet. *Herding.*

"They know you came to me," Dree murmured. "Serane Corvane pulled Quill apart two nights ago. Gentle, by her standards. But Quill sells everything eventually. It's what fences are."

Ahead, the canal path emerged onto a wider street, and beyond it the Sump gave way to the Warrens, and beyond those, pale in the pre-dawn grey, the palace hill rose with the renewal rite's scaffolding already climbing its flank like ivy of iron. Eleven days to the coronation renewal. Eleven days for Sebastian to decide whether she was the woman who built the cage or the woman who would break it.

Ives tugged her hand. When Sebastian looked down, the child was pointing — not at the palace, but down, at the ground, at the canal path itself. At a spot where the frost had formed overnight in a pattern too regular for weather: lines, angles, a shape.

A door, seen edge-on.

The same glyph she had drawn in Dree's kitchen.

And beneath it, traced in the frost in a child's careful hand, in letters older than any kingdom, words that made Sebastian's missing years ache like a pulled tooth and her heart clench like a fist:

*Not sold. Not lost. Below.*
