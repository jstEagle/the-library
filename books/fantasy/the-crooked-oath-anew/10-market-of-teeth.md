## 10. Market of Teeth

The coin Dree gave them lasted four days.

Sebastian counted it out on the garret floor by candlelight, Ives cross-legged beside her with her chin in her hands, watching the little stack of silver shrink as Sebastian named each thing it had to buy: passage papers, a week of bread and oil, bribes at the river gate. The arithmetic was ugly. Flight was expensive. Being hunted was more so.

"We need work," she said, mostly to herself.

Ives tapped the floor twice — the thieves' sign they'd built together that meant *I'm listening* — then pointed one small finger at the window, toward the night market's distant glow, and made a shape with both hands that Sebastian didn't know yet but understood anyway: *money*.

"Not stealing." Sebastian swept the coins into her palm. "Stealing is how we got here."

The girl looked at her with an expression of devastating patience, the kind adults spend decades learning to fake and children are born with, and drew one slow circle in the air: *you steal for a living.*

"I take," Sebastian corrected. "Taking has rules."

---

There were three rules to extraction, and Dree Halloway had taught her all of them back when he still used his real name, before he died on paper and went on living anyway.

One: never enter deeper than you can walk out of. Two: always leave a thread — a sensory anchor, the taste of your own mouth, a sound from the room where your body sits. Three: know what the memory costs its owner, because you pay that cost too, whether you mean to or not.

She broke rule three within the hour.

Guildmaster Oskar Vetch ran the Coopers' and Binders' Guild out of a counting-house on Tallow Row, and he had a problem money couldn't fix and memory could: somewhere in his head was the combination to a strongbox, and someone had poisoned him with a slow tincture that was eating his recollections from the edges inward. He could remember his mother's face but not his own wedding day; the guild's debts but not its assets. He wanted the strongbox opened before the poison finished him. He did not want to know why Sebastian Winterbourne needed two hundred crowns by week's end, and she did not tell him.

"A mind-extraction," he said, turning his heavy rings. "My nephew says there's a woman in Saltmarket who does it clean. No bleeding, no drooling. Just a gap after, like waking too fast."

"Your nephew overpays," Sebastian said. "And underestimates the risk."

"What risk? You go in, you look around, you come out."

She almost smiled. Almost. "It isn't a house, Guildmaster. It's more like weather. And sometimes the weather doesn't want to let you go."

He paid half up front regardless, because dying men bargain badly, and that evening she sat across from him in his lamp-lit office while Ives waited in the outer room with strict instructions — signed, drawn, rehearsed twice — to knock if anyone came, and to do nothing else, no matter what she heard.

The last instruction Sebastian gave her was with her hands, the new sign they'd invented only days ago, the one that meant *wait for me.* Ives returned it, then spoiled the gravity of it entirely by adding a flourish: a little wiggle of the fingers that meant nothing except *hurry.*

"I will," Sebastian said, and touched the extraction charm to Vetch's temple, and fell.

---

A strongbox is not a place. It is a weight. That was the first thing she understood inside him: his memory of the combination wasn't stored like a fact but carried like a stone, sunk somewhere deep in the sediment of him, and the poison had raised the waterline until the stone was fathoms down.

So she went down.

Vetch's inner country was a city of warehouses — minds organized by trade often were, all shelving and inventory and locked rooms — and every warehouse stood ankle-deep in gray water that rose as she watched. She waded past doors marked with years. Behind one, a wedding party frozen mid-dance, faces already blurred like wet paint. Behind another, a boy on a dock holding a fish too big for him, grinning into a light that flickered. The poison moved through the water in threads, pale as eel-skin, tasting everything it passed and taking the flavor with it.

She found the stone on the third level down, wedged beneath a memory of his father teaching him to plane wood, curls of shaving gold in lamplight. The numbers were carved into the stone in a child's hand — he'd chosen the combination at nine years old, something about his birthday reversed, and had never once thought of it since, which is why the poison hadn't found it yet. Poison eats what you use. It starves on what you bury.

Sebastian wrapped her hands around the stone and lifted.

It came free easier than it should have. That should have warned her.

The moment it left its resting place, the water changed. Not rose — *changed*, going from gray to black between one heartbeat and the next, and the black had texture, and the texture had intent. The poison wasn't a disease. In here, in this deep country, it was a tenant, and she had just broken into its cellar and stolen its landlord's treasure.

The threads came for her all at once.

Rule one said never enter deeper than you can walk out of. Rule two said always leave a thread of your own. She had left hers — the smell of lamp oil and Vetch's ringed fingers drumming, the creak of his chair — and she grabbed for it now the way a drowning woman grabs for a rope, and the rope was gone. Not frayed. *Gone*, as if the room above had never existed, as if there had never been a body sitting in it waiting for her, as if Sebastian Winterbourne had always lived at the bottom of this black water, carrying stones.

Panic is a luxury. She knew that. Knowing it didn't stop her lungs from trying to breathe water that wasn't water, didn't stop her legs from running through warehouse doors that now opened onto nothing — no memories behind them, just more dark, more rising cold. The poison wrapped a thread of itself around her wrist, gentle as a nurse, and began to *read* her.

That was the true danger of extraction, the thing she'd never told a client: a mind defends itself, and a dying mind defends itself by digestion. The threads weren't attacking her. They were *unraveling* her, pulling loose the seams of who she was to see what she was made of, and each seam they pulled took something with it. A name. Her mother's kitchen. The specific blue of a dress someone had worn —

Someone had worn a blue dress. Who. *Who—*

The flinch at church bells. Seven years of flinching, and even now, being eaten alive in a stranger's drowning memory, she reached for the reason and found the same smooth absence, the same wall of priced silence she had sold herself. The absence was worse than any thread. The absence was a door in her own skull standing open onto nothing, and the black water poured in—

—and stopped.

Stopped the way a crowd stops. The way a held breath stops. Every thread in the water froze mid-curl, and through the sudden absolute stillness Sebastian heard, impossibly, faintly, from very far above:

A knock.

Two knocks. Then a third, off-rhythm, wrong, *deliberately* wrong — not the sign they'd rehearsed but a new one, a sequence Ives had never been taught, a rhythm that sounded less like knuckles on wood than like language. Like syllables. Like the oldest syllables there were.

And the water *recognized* it.

She felt it happen — felt the black threads recoil from her wrist, felt the whole drowned city shudder as though a bell had tolled through its foundations, felt the poison do the one thing poisons never do: retreat. The threads pulled back and back and back, coiling into the far corners of Vetch's sinking country, leaving a corridor of clear gray water between her and the surface, and along that corridor drifted a single word in a language with no living speakers, spoken in a child's voice, and though Sebastian could not have translated it to save her life, she understood it completely, the way you understand falling.

It meant, approximately: *she is mine to lose, not yours.*

Then she hit the surface of herself like a diver breaking into air.

---

She came back into her body gasping, hands clawing at the arms of the chair, and the first thing she saw was Ives.

The girl stood directly behind Vetch's chair with both hands pressed flat against Sebastian's temples — no, against the air beside them, tracing glyphs so fast they blurred, her lips moving around shapes that bent the lamplight. The flame itself had leaned toward her, every flame in the room leaning like grass in wind, and when the last glyph closed, all the candles snapped upright at once and went ordinary again.

Ives lowered her hands. Looked at Sebastian. Wiggled her fingers: *hurry.*

"How long?" Sebastian rasped. Her voice came out sanded raw.

Ives held up four fingers. Four minutes. It had felt like a season. It had felt like the rest of her life, ending.

"Guildmaster." She turned to Vetch, who sat slack and blinking, tears tracking through the stubble of his cheeks without his notice. "Oskar. Nine digits, your ninth birthday backwards. Say them."

He said them. His voice cracked on the last number, and he wept openly then, laughing, clutching her hand in both of his ringed fists and calling her a miracle, a marvel, asking what he owed for the other half, the rest of the salvage, surely there was more down there worth—

"Nothing," Sebastian said, and found she couldn't make her hands stop shaking, so she put them in her pockets where he wouldn't see. "You've paid enough. Don't open the box tonight. Sleep on it. Sleep a lot."

In the street outside Tallow Row, the cold hit her like honesty, and she stopped under a guttering lantern and let it, because walking required pretending and she could not pretend for one more step.

Ives watched her come apart with the same terrible patience as always. Then she stepped close and did something new: she took Sebastian's shaking hand and pressed it flat against her own small chest, over her heart, and held it there. Steady. Warm. Beating.

*I'm real,* the gesture said. *You're real. Here.*

"You saved me." Sebastian's voice did something embarrassing. She let it. There was no one on Tallow Row at midnight but a guttering lantern and a mute child, and neither of them would ever repeat what they heard. "Down there. Whatever that was — whatever you said — it listened to you. The poison listened to you, Ives. Things don't listen to children. They especially don't listen to children in *that tongue.*"

The girl's face did something complicated — pride and fear sharing the same small space, elbow to elbow — and she shrugged in a way that said, as clearly as speech: *it was nothing.*

"It wasn't nothing." Sebastian knelt so they were eye to eye. Her heart was still slamming. Her wrist, where the thread had held her, bore a faint pale mark like a bracelet of frost, already fading. "I've done extractions for eleven years. I have walked out of places that would have kept me. Tonight I didn't walk out. I was *let go.* Because of you." She swallowed. "I don't know how to repay that. I don't even know what it was."

Ives considered this with great seriousness. Then she reached out and, with one finger, drew on Sebastian's frost-marked wrist a single glyph — the first glyph Sebastian had ever seen her write, the one that made her missing years ache like a pulled tooth — and below it, in the thieves' signs, slowly and deliberately:

*Later. Together.*

Together. As if repayment were not a debt between strangers but a road shared by travelers.

Sebastian looked at the glyph burning faintly against her skin, at the girl who had spoken a dead language to living darkness and been obeyed, and felt the vertigo rise again — the sense that she was standing at the edge of something enormous, some architecture seven years wide, and that this child was not near the secret but *was* the secret, wearing scraped knees and borrowed boots.

But that was tomorrow's terror. Tonight there were two hundred crowns in a strongbox on Tallow Row, and a boat to bribe, and a flight to fund, and a small warm hand still pressed over her hammering heart as if to say: *here. Here. Here.*

They were halfway home when Sebastian saw it.

Across Saltmarket Square, past the shuttered stalls and the sleeping fountain, a figure stood beneath the clock tower where the light couldn't reach — a courtier's silhouette, narrow shoulders, a hat held perfectly still in hands that did not shift their weight, did not sway, did not breathe in any rhythm she could see. Watching the garret district. Watching, she understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the night, the exact window where a lamp had burned until four minutes ago.

The figure raised one hand. Not a wave. A courtesy. The gesture of a servant announcing a guest.

Then it turned, unhurried, and walked away into the dark, and Sebastian stood in the square holding a child's hand and understanding that the flight had just begun without them.
