## 11. Hollow Eyes

The safehouse was a cooper's loft above a shuttered chandlery in Saltwrick, where the air tasted of tallow and old brine and the river fog came through the walls like a guest who would not leave. Sebastian had chosen it for its exits — the stair, the window over the drying yard, the loose plank in the floor that dropped into the chandlery's cellar — but she had stayed three nights because of the light. It came slantwise through a cracked skylight each morning, laying a long gold bar across the floorboards, and Ives liked to sit in it with her knees drawn up, drawing glyphs in the dust with one finger.

Sebastian watched her from the doorway with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand.

The girl drew the same spiral she always drew first — the one that made Sebastian's missing years ache like a tooth with the nerve exposed — and then, this morning, something new beneath it: a lattice of small hooked strokes, precise as stitching.

"What's that one?" Sebastian asked, though she knew Ives could not answer in any tongue the city spoke.

Ives looked up. Her eyes were the grey of rain on slate, and they held that terrible patience children sometimes have, as if they had been waiting for the adults to catch up to something obvious. She tapped the lattice twice. Then she pointed — deliberately, gravely — at Sebastian's chest.

"My heart," Sebastian said flatly. "It's a drawing of my heart."

Ives shook her head once, slow. She pointed at her own chest, then at Sebastian's, then traced the lattice again, and made a small motion with both hands like a door being closed.

Sebastian set down the cup. "No," she said. "Whatever you're saying, no. I'm not locked. I'm just — empty. There's a difference."

But even as she said it, she thought of the way memory-charms curdled in Ives's presence, the way Quill's finest warded locket had gone black and stinking within an hour of the girl touching it. Things rotted near Ives. Things *unmade*. And if the child was right — if there were locks on Sebastian that no charm could find because they weren't charms at all —

She crouched, took Ives's chalk-stained hand, and signed, in the thieves' cant they'd been building between them for days: *Later. Food first.*

Ives signed back *later* with the exaggerated patience of the very young, and Sebastian went down to the street to buy bread and eels from the pie cart, and that was how she happened to be outside when the courtier found them.

* * *

He did not come up the street like a hunter. That was what unsettled her most, afterward. He came up the street like a man keeping an appointment — unhurried, gloved hands folded behind his back, his boots finding the cobbles between the puddles without looking at them. The crowd of Saltwrick parted around him the way water parts around a keel, not because he pushed but because some animal ledger in each passerby tallied him as *wrong* and stepped aside before the conscious mind could say why.

Sebastian saw him across the market square and her body knew him before her mind finished the arithmetic: the dove-grey coat, the silver chain of office, the face from the palace audience — the man who had stood two rows behind the throne while King Aldric greeted petitioners without blinking through the anthem. The man whose eyes had found hers in the crowd and marked her, patient as a debt collector.

Hollow eyes. She'd thought it a figure of speech when Dree used it, back when Dree still talked in riddles instead of confessions. Now she watched the man cross the square toward her and understood it was a description, exact as a surveyor's note.

There was nowhere to run that wouldn't lead him to the loft. So Sebastian did what she did best: she became nobody. She turned to the pie cart, haggled over an eel pie with the fervor of a woman who cared about nothing else in the world, paid too much, and drifted toward the mouth of Cooper's Lane with her purchases and her heartbeat hammering somewhere up behind her teeth.

He fell into step beside her at the lane's mouth.

"Madam Winterbourne," he said. His voice was pleasant. It was the pleasantness that frightened her — a voice with no weather in it, no season, the same warm level tone whether he said *good day* or *kneel or burn*. "You attended the public audience on the Feast of Chains. You stood near the fountain. You left during the anthem."

"I left because the anthem's dull," Sebastian said. "Everyone leaves during the anthem."

"The anthem is seven years old," he said. "Composed for the king's coronation. Most people weep during it." He tilted his head, and the motion had a faint wrongness to it, like a puppeteer testing a string. "You flinched. At the bells. Do you know why you flinch at bells, madam?"

The question went through her like a fishhook finding old scar tissue. Seven years of flinching at church bells and never once knowing why; seven years of a routine built so carefully around a hole shaped like herself. "Bad hearing," she said.

"Yes," he agreed mildly. "That is one explanation." He stopped walking. The lane was narrow here, walled with timber-framed houses leaning together overhead like conspirators, and the fog had thickened until the far end was only a suggestion. "Inquisitor Corvane has your name in a ledger. The fence called Quill has described your lodgings. I have been given the task of retrieving you and the child. I would prefer," and here the pleasant voice did something almost wistful, almost human, a hairline crack in porcelain, "that you come quietly. It is easier. For everyone. It is easier for me."

"And why would it be easier for you?" Sebastian asked, edging half a step back, calculating distances, the knife at her hip, the roofline above.

"Because resistance is remembered," he said, "and remembering hurts me."

Then he moved.

* * *

He was fast in a way that was worse than skillful. Skilled fighters committed to feints and recoveries; this man simply *arrived*, his hand closing on her wrist with a grip like a dock winch, and Sebastian did the ugly pragmatic thing her trade had taught her — drove her thumb toward his eye, stamped his instep, twisted free leaving a strip of sleeve in his fingers. He didn't stagger. He absorbed the blows the way a wall absorbs thrown stones, and his face never changed, and that was when she got her first good look at his eyes at close range.

They were brown. Warm brown, ordinary brown, the kind of eyes a fiddler or a baker might have. And they were *empty* — not vacant the way a drunkard's are vacant, not cruel the way a torturer's are cruel, but empty the way a house is empty after the family has moved out and strangers hang their coats in the rooms. Something looked out of them. Something wore them. But nothing lived behind them, and the longer she looked, the more certain she became that whatever looked out was looking through borrowed glass, squinting, adjusting, trying to remember how a face was supposed to move.

"You're not him," she breathed. "Whoever you were — you're not him anymore."

Something flickered across the stolen face. Not pain. The *memory* of pain, worn smooth by handling, like a coin rubbed featureless. "I am Aldric's servant," he said. "I am the king's faithful—"

"No." She circled left, toward the drying yard, buying seconds. "Aldric's been dead seven years. Whatever sits on that throne wears him the way you're wearing—" she gestured at all of him, the dove-grey coat, the pleasant face, "—that. How long? How long have you been a coat someone else puts on?"

"Stop," he said, and for the first time the voice cracked, split into two registers a half-tone apart, like a bell with a flaw in it. "*Stop talking.*"

Behind him, at the mouth of the lane, Ives appeared.

She should have been upstairs. She should have been anywhere else. But she stood there in the fog with her grey eyes wide and her small fists clenched, and Sebastian saw the man see her, and saw the whole architecture of him reorient around the child like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone.

"The vessel speaks Oathscript," he said, and now the two voices braided together, the pleasantness fraying. "The residue must be returned. The oath must be renewed. These are the terms. These have always been the terms."

He lunged past Sebastian — not around her, *through*, shoulder-checking her into the wall hard enough to crack a rib or convince her ribs they'd cracked — and reached for Ives with those gloved hands, and Sebastian did the second ugly pragmatic thing of her day: she threw the eel pie at his head.

It was undignified. It bought exactly the half-second she needed. She came off the wall and put her knife against the side of his neck, and Ives — quick as a pickpocket, terrible as an omen — darted forward and pressed her bare palm flat against the man's cheek.

The effect was immediate and obscene.

The glove smoked. Under the skin of his face, something *moved* — a darkness shifting like silt stirred in still water, coiling away from the child's touch, pressing outward against the limits of the flesh that held it. And the man screamed. Not with the pleasant voice. With another voice underneath it, higher, younger, a voice that had not been used in years, a voice that said words in no order and then, horribly, in perfect order:

"*It's dark in here. Please. It's been so long, please, is anyone—*"

Sebastian's stomach turned over. She had robbed dying women and sold grief by the hour; she had thought herself past revulsion, past pity, fluent in every currency of human misery. But this — a person reduced to a room, a room with someone still inside it, knocking on the walls of his own skull while something else answered the door —

*Ives.* "Let go," she said hoarsely. "Ives. Let him go."

The child released him and stumbled back, grey-eyed, shaking, and the darkness sank beneath the surface of the man's skin like sediment settling, and the pleasant face reassembled itself piece by piece — mouth first, then brow, then the hollow brown eyes — and he knelt there in the mud of Cooper's Lane breathing like a man surfacing from deep water.

For a moment — one moment — the emptiness in his eyes filled with something raw and drowning and *his*.

"He kept me," the man whispered. His real voice, rusted. A young man's voice. A clerk's voice, maybe, or a choirboy's. "Seven years. I can hear the anthems from inside. I can hear everything. I *clapped*—" and then the darkness rose in him like a tide, and his face smoothed, and the pleasant voice returned, wet and level: "You will come to the palace. All of you. Before the renewal. It is kinder."

Sebastian drove her knee into his temple and ran.

* * *

They went out through the drying yard and over three roofs and down a chimney that cost Sebastian most of the skin on her palms, and did not stop until the fog of Saltwrick had given way to the coal-smoke labyrinth of the Tanneries, where the stink was thick enough to drown pursuit and the alleys turned back on themselves like intestines.

Only then did Sebastian let herself lean against a wall and shake.

Ives stood in front of her, pale, her palm still faintly smoking at the edges where the glove had burned it. Sebastian took the small hand and turned it over. The burn was clean, oddly clean, as if the glove had scalded rather than struck.

"You touched him," Sebastian said. "Why? You didn't know what he was. You couldn't have known."

Ives met her eyes. Then, slowly, deliberately, she raised her burned hand and signed — clumsy, newly learned, but unmistakable — the sign they had built together for *hurt*: fist to heart, then open.

*He hurt.*

Not *he threatens*. Not *he hunts*. *He hurt.* The child had put her hand on a monster's face and felt the prisoner inside it, and her first instinct had been the same instinct that had carried her mute through the streets of Vellharrow to a stranger's garret door: to reach toward the wound.

Sebastian laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and unsteady, and slid down the wall until she was sitting in the muck, and pulled Ives against her side. The girl came willingly, smelling of chalk dust and river fog, and Sebastian held her and stared at the dripping eaves and thought about the man in the dove-grey coat clapping from inside his own skull, seven years of anthems heard from a locked room, and thought about a woman named Sebastian Winterbourne who had sold her own memories and could not remember why she flinched at bells.

"We're all wearing stolen faces," she said quietly, to no one, to the fog. "Some of us just paid for ours."

Ives tilted her head up. Then she pointed at Sebastian's chest — the same grave gesture as that morning, the lattice of hooks, the closing door — and this time she added a new glyph beside it in the air with her finger, quick and sure, and though Sebastian could not read Oathscript she could read *emphasis*, and the emphasis said: *soon. It opens soon.*

And that was when the bells began.

All across Vellharrow, from the Cathedral of the Chained Saint to the meanest riverside chapel, the bells started ringing the seven-year anthem — hours early, off schedule, urgent — and under their bronze clamor Sebastian heard, or dreamed she heard, threaded through the peals like a voice through a wall:

Dree Halloway, screaming her name.
