## 12. Dree's Confession

The safehouse smelled of tallow and old lies, which was to say it smelled of Dree Halloway, who had been both fuel and falsehood for seven years.

He sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood the way he used to make Sebastian sit when she was young enough to still flinch — palms open, fingers spread, nothing hidden in the fists. It was a thief's posture of surrender, and a teacher's posture of patience, and tonight it was neither. Tonight his hands were just an old man's hands, spotted and scarred, resting on wood he'd bought with money earned from selling false relics to true believers.

Across from him, Ives slept. Not in a bed — there was no bed, only the pallet Sebastian had claimed and the blanket the girl refused to share — but curled in the corner chair like a cat who had decided the chair was hers by right of occupation. Her breathing made the small sound of someone dreaming without language. Even asleep, her fingers moved sometimes, tracing glyphs against the armrest, writing letters in a tongue that had no business surviving its own funeral.

Dree watched those fingers and drank tea he did not taste.

Sebastian was on the roof. He could hear her up there — not her footsteps; she was too good for that — but the particular quality of silence a person makes when they are watching streets. She had learned it from him. Everything dangerous about her, she had learned from him, and everything kind she had learned despite him, and he had spent seven years telling himself this was a fair trade.

The knock came at midnight. Three short, one long. The rhythm of a mnemonic inquisitor's summons, because inquisitors were bureaucrats before they were anything else, and even their terror arrived with paperwork.

Dree set down the cup. He looked at Ives, sleeping in her chair, and at the ceiling, where Sebastian crouched above the chimney smoke like an answer to a question no one had asked aloud yet.

Then he opened the door.

* * *

There were three of them, which meant they thought little of him, or much of what he knew. Inquisitors traveled in pairs when they expected resistance and threes when they expected confession — the third always carried the ledger, and the ledger was the real weapon. Blades killed bodies. Ledgers killed lives, retroactively, thoroughly, with citations.

The woman in front was tall and gray-eyed and wore her authority like a garment tailored by someone who hated her. Serane Corvane. Dree knew the name the way a fisherman knows the name of a storm: with respect, with resentment, and with the fatalistic understanding that eventually they would meet.

"You're dead," she said, by way of greeting. "According to the registry, Dree Halloway died of lung-rot six years ago in a charity ward in Lower Vellharrow. There's a death certificate. There's a grave."

"There's a body," Dree agreed. "I paid for it. It was a very cooperative corpse."

"Master Halloway." The third inquisitor, the one with the ledger, spoke without looking up from it. Young. Nervous. The kind of zealot who hadn't yet learned that certainty was a debt that collected interest. "We have records of unsanctioned extraction work spanning nineteen years. We have witness testimony placing you at the Nightingale Market two nights ago. And we have—" he turned the ledger around, and Dree saw his own name written in a clerk's careful hand, dated, witnessed, filed— "enough to hang you four times over, if the Crown still hanged oathbreakers instead of rendering them."

Rendered them. Boiled down into chalice-water for a king who smiled wrong. Dree had heard rumors of the renewal rite from Quill, who heard rumors of everything, and he had told himself the rumors were the usual market-mush — half rot, half wish. But Corvane's face, when the young man said it, did something small and terrible. A tightening at the mouth. A flicker behind the eyes that was not loyalty and not fear but recognition, the look of someone who has seen the machinery and cannot unsee it.

Interesting, Dree thought. And then: dangerous. And then, with the weariness of a man picking up a weight he had set down years ago: mine, now. Everything interesting became dangerous became his. That was the shape his whole life had made.

"You want something," he said. "Inquisitors don't knock politely at midnight over paperwork. Paperwork gets kicked in doors. You knocked. So you want something you can't take."

Corvane stepped past him into the room. She did this with the ease of a person accustomed to rooms yielding, and Dree let her, because the room was arranged to yield. The false wall behind the cupboard held nothing but dried herbs and a decoy ledger full of invented crimes. The real treasures were sewn into the hem of the coat hanging by the door, and the coat was currently on Sebastian's back, walking the rooftops away from here, if she had any sense.

She didn't, usually. Sense had never been the part of her he'd sharpened.

Corvane stopped at the corner chair. Ives slept on, small fingers twitching their silent alphabet, and the gray-eyed inquisitor looked down at the child for a long moment with an expression Dree had last seen on the faces of people reading their own eulogies.

"She was at the market," Corvane said quietly. "With Sebastian Winterbourne. My men tried to take the child. The charms they carried rotted in their pouches. One of them wept for an hour afterward and couldn't say why." She turned. "What is she?"

And there it was. The question under every question, the one Dree had spent seven years not answering, first to others and finally, expertly, to himself.

"I'll trade you," he said.

* * *

They sat at the table. The young inquisitor — Ferren, his name was, ink-stained and frightened beneath the uniform — kept the ledger between himself and Dree like a shield of paper. Corvane dismissed him to watch the door, which was either trust or theater, and Dree suspected Corvane trusted nothing, including herself, and had simply wanted him gone so the conversation could become what conversations between damaged people become: a negotiation of wounds.

"Before you speak," Corvane said, "understand what I can offer. Not freedom. If you walk out of here free, my superiors will ask why, and I don't have answers I'm willing to give. What I can offer is time. Hours. Perhaps a day. Long enough to run properly, if running is what you intend."

"And what do you want in exchange? The child?"

"No." She said it fast, too fast, and Dree marked it. "I want to know what I serve. Seven years I've confiscated magic and rendered memory and filed reports, and lately the reports go somewhere that isn't an archive. Lately the king smiles at petitioners and doesn't blink through the anthem, and the courtier Vess looks at crowds the way a butcher looks at stock, and I am done pretending the ledger balances." Her hands, folded on the table, were perfectly steady, which is how Dree knew they wanted very badly not to be. "Tell me what happened seven years ago."

Dree poured tea for both of them. His hand shook on the second cup, and he let her see it shake, because a confession delivered steadily sounded like performance, and he needed her to believe this was the hardest thing he had ever said out loud.

It was not the hardest thing. The hardest thing he would save for last, the way he saved all knives.

"Seven years ago," he began, "the King of Vellharrow was a man named Aldric Merren, and he was dying. Slowly, privately, in ways the court hid behind cosmetics and ceremony. And there was a thing that came to him. Or was already in him — the accounts differ, and I've had seven years to stop caring which version flatters anyone. Call it a parasite. Call it a tenant. Call it what the old texts call it: a holder. Something that wears a life the way a thief wears gloves."

Corvane's breath went shallow. She knew. Some part of her had known before she climbed these stairs, and had climbed them anyway.

"We found it in the Oathscript," Dree went on. "Not the language as it survives — the corrupted fragments, the market-charms, the gutter-runes thieves like me bastardized into trade-craft. The original tongue. The tongue in which binding magic was first sworn, back when magic wasn't outlawed because it was evil, but because binding it was easier than understanding it." He laughed, briefly, and hated the sound. "They outlawed it to protect us, do you understand? Like putting a lock on a door and forgetting there was ever a house."

"The binding," Corvane said. "On the king. You helped perform it."

"I designed it." The words came out of him like teeth being pulled, each one leaving a socket that ached. "I was the best oath-smith alive, and I was arrogant, and a delegation came to me in secret — loyalists, physicians, a priest whose name I'll carry to whatever grave actually gets me — and they asked me to cage the thing inside their king while keeping their king alive inside it. Because the alternative was regicide, and they were good men, and good men will always choose the crooked oath over the straight knife."

"And it worked."

"It worked the way a dam works." Dree stared into his tea. "For a while. Against weather it was built for. The binding holds — mostly — because bindings do; that's their nature. They hold until something speaks against them. And the counter-word to a binding, inquisitor, is spoken in Oathscript, and requires a speaker who carries the binding's residue." He lifted his eyes to hers. "You've met her. She's asleep in that chair, and she is not a child the way you mean children, and I did not know she existed until eleven days ago, and I have been sick with knowing ever since."

Corvane looked at Ives again. The sleeping girl's fingers wrote their ghost-letters against the chair. When Corvane spoke next, her voice had dropped into something almost private.

"The ledger," she said. "The extraction records. Nineteen years of your work — and Winterbourne's name appears in a seven-year-old volume. Unsolicited self-extraction. Total memory sale. Dated the same week the king's public health improved." She unfolded her hands. "That's not coincidence. That's architecture."

"You're smarter than your file suggests. Your file suggests you're a functionary with a conscience problem."

"My file is written by people who smile wrong." Flat. Cold. Then, with effort: "Winterbourne. Tell me about her."

And here it was. The tooth he'd been saving.

Dree Halloway had trained eleven apprentices in his long disreputable career. Ten of them he remembered with the mild fondness a smith feels for blades he's hammered — pride, ownership, a certain relief at their departure. The eleventh he remembered the way a drowning man remembers the rope he cut.

"Sebastian came to me at fourteen," he said. "Feral, brilliant, half-starved, with a memory like a colander and a gift like a loaded crossbow. She could lift an hour out of a stranger's mind so gently they never knew it was gone. I taught her everything because she made teaching feel like theft — like I was stealing credit for talent that predated me." He turned the cup slowly on the table. "And seven years ago, when I built the binding on Aldric Merren, she was my apprentice, and the binding required a knife — a memory-knife, forged to cut along the seams of a soul — and it required a carrier. Someone young enough, clean enough, unremarkable enough to walk the thing into the king's presence without the court looking twice."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, cart wheels ground through the street. Above, on the roof, the watching-silence shifted — Sebastian had moved, closer to the chimney, listening. Good. Let her listen. Let her hate him with her whole attention.

"She carried the knife," Dree said. "Into the undercroft. Past the guard. To the foot of the throne. And she stood there while I swore the oath, and she held the blade, and afterward—" his voice cracked on it, and he let it, because Corvane needed to hear the crack to believe the weight— "afterward she came to me with her hands shaking and asked me one question. She said: *Dree. What did we just do.* And I lied to her. I said we'd saved a king. I said history would thank us."

"What did you actually do?"

"We caged something we didn't understand, using words we only half-translated, and we left the door of the cage keyed to a single voice — the voice of the person who carried the knife. That was my design flaw. That was my arrogance. The binding needed a living anchor, a human hinge, and I chose her, because she was the steadiest hand I'd ever trained and I didn't ask her permission because asking permission invites refusal and I was building a miracle on schedule." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "So I took the choice away from her twice. Once with the knife. Once with the lie. And then, when the binding started to slip — three years ago, five years ago, the dreams, the slippage in the rite — I realized the hinge would be sought. Found. Used. Every enemy the crown has would crawl across broken glass to find the one person whose voice could open the cage."

"So she sold her memories." Corvane's voice was barely audible. "To hide herself. From everyone. Including the parasite."

"Including me." Dree dropped his hands. "Especially me. She walked into a memory-broker's shop seven years ago and sold every hour that connected her to that night — and to me, and to the craft, and to the version of herself who could be leveraged — and she walked out a stranger with excellent skills and no idea why church bells made her flinch." He swallowed. "I have watched her for seven years, Corvane. From across markets. Through other people's eyes. I hired the woman who sold her the locket-hour. I have followed my own apprentice around a city like a ghost at her own funeral, and I told myself it was protection, and some nights I almost believed it."

"Why tell me this?" Corvane asked. "Why now? You could lie. You've clearly survived on lies."

"Because time is what I'm buying, and truth is the only currency you'll accept — I've read your record too, inquisitor; I read everyone's — and because there's a piece I haven't given you yet, and it's the piece that should terrify you most."

He leaned forward. Behind him, faintly, Ives murmured in her sleep — a single syllable in a language older than kingdoms, and both of them pretended not to hear it, and both of them heard it.

"The unbinding word," Dree said. "The counter-oath. It exists — it's written into the binding's own grammar, the way every lock contains the shape of its key. But a word like that isn't just spoken. It's *carried*. Half of it lives in the flesh of whoever the binding anchored to. The hinge. The knife-carrier." He held Corvane's gaze. "Which means the other half of the unbinding — the half that isn't in the child, the half no one can teach or translate — is sitting in a vial in the Crown repository, filed under *unsanctioned self-extraction, S. Winterbourne*, in a ledger very much like the one your friend Ferren is clutching."

Corvane went still. Not the stillness of shock — the stillness of a woman rearranging seven years of filings in a single breath, pulling threads, finding that every loose end in her career tied off somewhere beneath the palace.

"The Vault of Given Things," she said.

"You know it."

"I know the requisition forms." She rose, and for a moment her composure slipped entirely, and Dree saw underneath: not a bureaucrat, not a traitor-in-waiting, but a tired woman standing at the edge of the only decision that had ever really been hers. "If I let you walk out of here, and this is true, I'm committing treason. If I report it, and it's true, I'm delivering the last key to the thing wearing the king."

"Yes," Dree said gently. "That's generally how conscience works. It presents you with exactly two doors and sets fire to the hallway."

At the door, she paused. Ferren waited in the street beyond, ledger hugged to his chest like a child's toy. The fog was coming in off the river, swallowing the lamps one by one, and in that gray dissolving light Corvane Corvane looked back at the old oathbreaker and said, quietly:

"Winterbourne is on your roof. She's been there for twenty minutes. I counted her heartbeat in the floorboards on my way in — extraction work leaves tremor-signature in the joists, and hers is distinctive." A pause. "I'm choosing not to have heard it. See that she chooses, soon, what she's going to do about the vault. Before the coronation rite. Before the ledger finds her name again in worse company than mine."

Then she was gone, and the fog closed, and Dree sat alone in the tallow-smelling dark with a sleeping god-child and the ashes of a confession seven years cold.

Above him, softly, the roof-scrape of a boot. A hatch lifting. And Sebastian Winterbourne dropped down into the room — pale, silent, her stolen face gone white as paper — holding the memory-coat against her chest like armor that no longer fit.

"Say it again," she whispered. "The part about the knife."
