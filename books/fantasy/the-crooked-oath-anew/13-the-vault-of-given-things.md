## 13. The Vault of Given Things

Serane Corvane had signed the service ledger at the palace's bone-gate four hundred and eleven times in seven years, and she had never once been asked what she carried down.

That was the arrangement the Mnemonic Office called *clean hands*. An inquisitor did not carry confiscated magic; an inquisitor carried paper, and paper was not contraband. Paper was duty. The distinction had satisfied her for most of those seven years — a smooth stone she turned over in her mind on sleepless nights until it wore down to nothing and she could finally rest. Tonight the stone would not turn. Tonight it sat in her chest like a swallowed key.

The stair beneath the Hall of Petition descended in spirals so tight that her left shoulder brushed the wall with every revolution. The wall was warm. That was the first thing that had ever frightened her about the repository, back when she was new and still capable of being frightened: the walls were warm, all the way down, as though the palace were a body and this its fevered blood. The masons had built with grey granite from the northern quarries. Granite did not hold heat. Something below did.

Her lamp guttered once at the third landing, where the air began to taste faintly sweet — rendered memory always did, like honey left too long in a copper pot — and she steadied the flame with a cupped hand out of habit before remembering that habit itself was now evidence. Inquisitors did not shield their lamps. Inquisitors let the dark do its work.

She let the flame stand.

*Four hundred and eleven signatures,* she thought. *And not one question.*

At the bottom, the door was iron banded with lead, and set into it at eye height was the seal she had sworn her oaths upon: the closed eye within the open crown. She pressed her palm to the wax impression — renewed monthly by clerks who came down in pairs and never spoke on the stairs — and felt the faint tremor of the ward acknowledging her. Not welcoming her. Acknowledging her, the way a jailer is acknowledged by the door he locks.

The Vault of Given Things.

That was the old name, pre-Aldric, carved above the arch in lettering the Crown had never bothered to chisel out because chiseling would mean admitting anyone remembered. Everything below had been *given*, once. Confessed, surrendered, laid down willingly by magicians who believed — gods help them — that the law was a covenant and not a net. Now the word *confiscated* lived in the ledgers instead, and given things became taken things, and taken things became inventory.

Serane gave her countersign to the warden at the inner gate — a hollow-eyed man named Pell who had held his post longer than she'd held her commission, and who looked through her rather than at her, which she had always assumed was boredom — and passed into the long gallery where the Crown kept what it could not burn.

* * *

The gallery was a street underground, if a street could be built by grief. Niches opened off both sides like shopfronts, each one sealed behind glass thick as a hymnal, each one labeled in clerk-hand. And in each niche, something glowed or whispered or wept.

Serane walked slowly, as she always did, past the inventory of a broken world.

Here: a jar of storms, small ones, fist-sized, turning inside cloudy glass with the patience of trapped weather. Here: a wedding veil that moved against no wind, stitched from vows, still trying to keep someone faithful. Here — and she did not slow for this one, she refused to slow — a shelf of bells, silent, each one struck dumb by decree, though if you stood close enough you could feel them humming in your teeth like a word swallowed mid-syllable.

And here, in the middle galleries, the memory-vials.

Thousands of them. Racked floor to ceiling in cedar frames gone dark with handling, each vial no longer than her thumb, each one stoppered with lead and tagged with the name of the person it had been taken from — or, more often, the name of the person who had sold it, because the Crown did not only confiscate. The Crown purchased. Grief made people generous; hunger made them honest. There were whole racks bought from thieves, from debtors, from mothers. There was a rack — she knew the rack number by heart, Rack 9-C, because she had catalogued it herself in her first year — devoted entirely to the extractions of one Dree Halloway, oathbreaker, presumed dead.

She stopped at 9-C anyway. It was a defensible thing to do. An inquisitor might review any rack at any time; diligence was her armor, and tonight she meant to wear every piece of it.

Halloway's vials were duller than most. Sold memories lost their color when they changed owners twice — the first sale bruised them, the second starved them. She read the tags without touching: *a harbor at dawn.* *the smell of bread.* *my mother's voice, singing.* Small things. A man dismantling himself into moments and trading them away one at a time, and no one in the Office had thought to ask why, because asking why was not the work. The work was the ledger. The work was the count.

Except that the count was wrong.

She had known it was wrong for eleven days, since the night she pulled the seven-year-old extraction ledger from the deep files and found, under the week of the king's renewal rite, a name she had seen three days earlier on a warrant draft: *S. Winterbourne. Extraction, self-directed, unsanctioned. Contents unlisted. Purchased by the Crown, price waived.*

Price waived. In seven years of reading the ledgers she had seen price waived exactly once. You did not waive the price of a memory unless the memory was worth more than coin, and you did not record *contents unlisted* unless someone very high up had ordered the contents forgotten — and there was only one will in Vellharrow high enough to order forgetting, and it did not blink during the anthem.

Serane had gone home that night and sat upright in her chair until dawn with her boots on, the way soldiers sit, and by morning she had done something no inquisitor in the history of the Office had done.

She had begun lying in her reports.

Small lies. Professional lies. She reported that the Winterbourne woman's lodgings showed signs of long vacancy. She reported that the fence called Quill, questioned twice, had yielded nothing but profanity and arithmetic. She misdated a sighting by two days and a district by three, each lie small enough to survive audit and large enough to buy time, and every morning she woke expecting the stone in her chest to have dissolved overnight into simple duty again, and every morning it had grown heavier instead.

Because here was the truth she could not file away: she had watched the king's shadow drink.

Eleven days ago, at the private renewal rite, standing witness in her formal capacity with her formal ledger and her formal silence, she had seen the shadow lean from the king's body like a second mouth and lower itself to the chalice of rendered memory, and she had seen King Aldric's face while it fed — the terrible slackness of it, the face of a man dreaming someone else's dream — and when the shadow settled back into him and the king smiled at the court and the court smiled back, Serane had written in her report: *Rite conducted in accordance with statute.*

She had committed treason with a pen, and the pen had not even hesitated.

*Doors open both ways,* her mother used to say, back in the fishing village that no longer appeared on maps, back before the Office took her at fourteen and made her into paper. *A door you lock against the wind will lock against you someday too.* Serane had not understood it then. She understood it now, standing in the cold gallery among ten thousand stolen mornings, understanding arriving in her body before her mind: every door she had locked for the Crown, she had locked from the inside.

She checked the gallery clock — a water-clock, because clocks with gears remembered too much — and saw that she had twenty minutes of sanctioned inspection remaining. Twenty minutes to be exactly what she was.

Then she heard footsteps on the stair.

* * *

They were not Pell's footsteps. Pell moved like furniture being rearranged; these steps were quick, light, unevenly spaced — someone descending fast and then forcing themselves to slow, fast then slow, the rhythm of a person arguing with their own courage.

Serane killed her lamp and stepped into the niche-shadow between Rack 11 and Rack 12, hand on the hilt of her mnemonic pike, and hated herself for the reflex even as her fingers closed around it.

A figure came down the last spiral into the gallery's dim glow. Small. Hooded. Moving with a thief's economy, weight rolling heel-to-toe — and then the figure paused at the arch, beneath the old carved name of the place, and pulled back her hood, and Serane felt the stone in her chest crack clean through.

Sebastian Winterbourne looked worse than her warrant sketch and better than her reputation: sharp-faced, gray-eyed, a fresh cut at her hairline already going brown. Her eyes went to the racks the way a drowning woman's go to the surface. And pressed close at her side, half-hidden in the folds of an adult coat cut down badly, stood a child.

The mute girl. Ives. The name had come through Quill's interrogation, extracted by other inquisitors, filed and forgotten — except that Serane had not forgotten it, had in fact written it on a scrap of paper and then burned the scrap and found she could remember it perfectly anyway, as though some part of her had decided in advance what deserved keeping.

The child was looking at the vials. Not the way the clerks looked at them, or the way Serane had trained herself to look — as inventory, as weight, as tax. The child looked at them the way you look at sleeping birds. With recognition. With something dangerously near tenderness. And as her gaze traveled the racks, the nearest vials began, very softly, to sing — a sound like a wet finger on glass-rim, thin and rising — and Sebastian Winterbourne put a hand on the girl's shoulder and the singing stopped.

They were looking for 9-C. Serane realized it the way one realizes rain has started: not by seeing it but by feeling it. Winterbourne's search had a direction, and the direction was Halloway, and Halloway's rack held everything the Crown had stripped from him except the one thing the ledgers said he'd never sold —

— except that the ledgers said *contents unlisted* for one S. Winterbourne, price waived, same week, same rite.

*Oh,* Serane thought, with a clarity that felt almost like falling. *She's looking for herself.*

Every rule she had ever sworn held its shape in her mind at once: seize, report, record, forget. Every one of them arrived wearing its uniform, standing at attention, waiting for orders. And beside them, ununiformed, unarmed, stood the image of the king's slack dreaming face, and the shadow drinking, and ten thousand tags reading *my mother's voice, singing,* and the child's hand resting flat against the glass of a stranger's memory as though comforting it.

An inquisitor would step out now. Announce herself. Do her duty.

*A door you lock against the wind will lock against you someday too.*

Serane Corvane, who had signed the service ledger four hundred and eleven times, who had lied in triplicate for eleven days, who had watched a shadow drink and written *in accordance with statute* — Serane stepped backward, deeper into the niche, deeper into the dark, and did not step out.

She watched Winterbourne reach Rack 9-C and run her fingers along the tags, quick and desperate, and then stop — stop dead — at the empty slot at the rack's end. Slot nine. Tag present: *S. Winterbourne. Self-extraction. Contents unlisted.* Vial absent. Already claimed, already drawn, already signed out — and Serane knew whose signature would be on that withdrawal slip, because she had forged the authorization herself, three days ago, routing a single unlisted vial to the private examination rooms above, buying it out of general circulation before anyone else could touch it. She had told herself it was containment. Evidence management. Diligence.

It had been the first door she'd unlocked.

Winterbourne's shoulders dropped. For a moment the thief simply stood there in the gallery of given things, and Serane, watching from the dark, saw her press her forehead gently against the empty slot's frame — a small, private gesture of grief, the kind people think no one sees — and the child reached up and took her hand.

Then footsteps above. Real ones this time, booted and paired, the night-shift wardens making their rounds early, and Winterbourne heard them too, and melted backward into the spiral stair with the girl, gone as cleanly as smoke returning to a doused candle.

Serane stayed in the niche until the wardens passed, until their lamps swung away toward the storm-jars, until the gallery was only the gallery again — humming, warm-walled, patient. Then she walked out past Pell, gave him a nod he did not return, and climbed the four hundred steps with the stone in her chest transformed entirely: no longer a weight but a blade, and she the hand that held it.

In her office she wrote the night's inspection report. *Rack 9-C verified intact. No anomalies.* She signed it, sealed it, and set it aside for morning collection.

Then she unlocked the bottom drawer of her desk, took out the withdrawal slip for one unlisted vial, and read the location line she herself had entered: *Subvault annex, east corridor, examination room four.* And beneath it, in the margin, in her own small clerk-hand, a note she did not remember writing and could not explain and did not cross out:

*The lock sticks. Lift the handle as you turn.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she took her cloak, and her keys — all of them, the full ring, the ones that opened the service entrance beneath the Hall of Petition — and she went back out into the Vellharrow night, walking not toward her lodgings but toward the palace's bone-gate, where she would sign the ledger a four hundred and twelfth time, and where, some hours after she passed, a thief and a mute child would find a door standing unlatched in the dark, and no record anywhere of who had left it so.
