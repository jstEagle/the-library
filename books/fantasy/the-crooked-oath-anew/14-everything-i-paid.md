## 14. Everything I Paid

The repository beneath the palace did not feel like a prison. It felt like a throat.

Sebastian had expected chains and iron, inquisitors' seals stamped in red wax, the architecture of punishment. Instead she walked down a corridor of pale stone that curved inward like an esophagus, and the air grew warmer with every step — warm the way breath is warm, the way a mouth is warm. The lamps along the wall burned without flame, each one a small glass sphere holding a curl of grey light that moved like something dreaming.

"Rendered memory," Dree had told her, three nights ago, his voice frayed down to wire. "They burn what they confiscate for fuel. Light, heat, the warding on the palace gates. Every stolen hour some thief ever lifted from a grieving widow or a frightened child ends up keeping the king's feet warm." He had laughed then, a terrible sound. "You'll fit right in."

Now Sebastian moved through that warmth with Ives's small hand locked in hers and Serane Corvane walking three paces ahead with an inquisitor's stride she no longer had any right to. The unlocked service door behind them was already a story they would never be able to tell. Ahead of them stretched the Vault of Given Things, and Sebastian's hands would not stop shaking.

She had stolen from hundreds of minds. She had never once gone looking for her own.

* * *

The vault proper opened like the inside of a bell — vast, domed, resonant even in silence, as though the whole room were listening. Shelves rose out of the dimness in concentric rings, and every shelf held vials. Thousands upon thousands of them, racked in wooden cradles like eggs in a rookery, each one glowing faintly with its own color: amber for joy, rust-red for grief, a bruised violet Sebastian didn't have a name for and decided she didn't want one. Some vials were nearly dark. Some were so bright they cast shadows.

Serane stopped at the threshold and stood very straight, the way she did when she was deciding whether to be a traitor again. "Extractions are catalogued by date and district," she said. Her voice echoed strangely; the room took it and passed it around. "Unsanctioned extractions go to the deep shelves. Yours will be seven years old." A pause. "There will be more than one."

"I know how many there are," Sebastian said. "Dree told me. Eleven."

"Eleven vials," Serane said quietly, "and you only came for one?"

"One is enough to hang me. Let's not be greedy."

But that wasn't the truth and all three of them knew it wasn't the truth. The truth was that Sebastian could not carry eleven years of herself at once, not here, not with the walls listening. The truth was that she had spent seven years learning exactly how much emptiness a person could walk with, and she knew to the ounce.

Ives tugged her hand. The girl looked up, and in the vial-light her eyes held colors that shifted when the shelves shifted, as though she reflected the room rather than the light. She signed, quick and precise — the thieves' signs Sebastian had taught her, repurposed now for questions Sebastian wished she wouldn't ask:

*Which one? How will you know?*

Sebastian knelt. This close, she could smell the Oathscript on the girl's skin — not a smell exactly, but an impression, old parchment and struck flint, the way magic announced itself when it was trying to be polite about it.

"How will I know?" she repeated aloud, because some things needed saying in the open air. "Because I've been missing it for seven years. You don't forget the shape of a hole."

* * *

The deep shelves ran cold. Past a certain ring the warmth of rendered memory gave out entirely, and the air turned the temperature of a cellar where nothing has lived for a long time. Here the vials sat darker, most of them, confiscated things whose owners had died or been emptied or simply never come looking. There was no index Sebastian could read. There was only the ledger system Serane described in a low, clipped voice — dates, districts, extraction marks — and Sebastian's own memory of a single night seven years ago that began with rain and ended with her waking in her garret with a headache like a cracked skull and no idea who she had been before.

District of Saltrow. Late autumn. That much Dree had given her, wrung out of him like the last water from a cloth. *You sold them yourself, girl. You carried them to the fence yourself. I watched you do it and I let you.* And then he had refused to say anything else, and Sebastian had screamed at him until her voice broke, and Dree had taken the screaming the way stone takes weather.

Saltrow. Late autumn. Seven years back.

The vials in this row were mostly dull. Sebastian walked them slowly, trailing two fingers along the wooden cradles, feeling for — what? She didn't know. A resonance. A recognition. The way you know your own footsteps on a stair.

Halfway down the row, her fingers went numb.

Not cold-numb. *Gone*-numb, the way her hand sometimes went when a charm curdled near Ives, except Ives was standing well behind her. The sensation climbed her wrist, her forearm, pooled in her chest. Sebastian looked at the vial her fingers rested against and understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that she hadn't found it by searching at all.

It had called her.

It was smaller than the others. Amber, but a strange amber, shot through with veins of black like rot spreading through honey. It sat in its cradle humming — not audibly, but in her teeth, in the roots of her hair. The label was old Crown script, faded, and Serane had to hold her lamp close to read it aloud.

"*Winterbourne, S. Extraction, self-administered. Quantity: one year, concentrated.*" Serane's voice caught. "Self-administered. You did this to yourself."

"I've been told." Sebastian picked up the vial.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it, she thought — the trade's oldest lesson and still somehow always new. A year of a human life, and it weighed less than a coin. All those hours, the taste of bread, someone's laughter, whatever face she had loved enough to weep over — sealed in glass lighter than a sparrow's bone.

"Why one year?" Serane asked. "If you sold your memories to hide something, why not sell all of it?"

And Ives, who did not speak, answered for her. The girl stepped forward and drew one glyph in the dust on the shelf edge with a fingertip — quick, sure, a spiral broken by a single stroke — and Sebastian knew this one now; Ives had drawn it often enough. *A door left ajar,* the sign meant. *Enough opening to come back through.*

"She kept a key," Sebastian said. Her own voice sounded far away. "One vial, hidden somewhere, so the rest could be found if—" If what? If the thing being hidden ever needed unearthing. If the crooked oath ever needed paying off. Her stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like something rolling in its sleep. "Dree said the price of telling me was worse than the price of ignorance. So he hid the telling inside my own head instead. Coward. Clever, clever coward."

Serane said nothing. But she unhooked a reader-lens from her belt — inquisitor equipment, contraband twice over in Sebastian's hands — and held it out, handle first, her hollow-eyed courtier composure cracking just slightly at the edges.

"You should see it before we run," Serane said. "Whatever it is. You shouldn't learn what you paid on a gallows."

* * *

Memory-reading was a thief's art and Sebastian had practiced it on other people for a living, which meant she knew precisely what she was doing to herself and could not make herself stop.

She sat down on the cold stone floor between the shelves. Ives folded herself into Sebastian's lap without asking, the way she always did, a warm weight like a cat that had decided ownership. Serane stood watch at the row's end, lamp raised, her shadow long and thin against the vials.

Sebastian pressed the vial's seam with her thumb, the old trick, the first thing Dree had ever taught her — *break the seal slow, girl, a memory opened fast burns going down* — and felt the glass part along its hairline with a sigh, releasing a scent that hit her like a fist: woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath it, faintly, blood.

Then the world folded up like a map and put her down somewhere else.

* * *

Rain. The binding night began with rain, and the rain was falling on the palace undercroft — the same undercroft, she realized, the same flagstones under her knees, seven years older now and none the wiser. Memory-Sebastian knelt on those stones and they were cold through her skirts, and she was crying, and the crying was not delicate. It was the ugly crying, the kind that wrecks the face, and she could feel her own past grief in her present body like a fever taking hold.

Across the undercroft, King Aldric lay on a slab of black marble, and he was screaming without sound. His mouth opened wide enough to show the root of his tongue and no noise came out, because Dree had already taken his voice — Sebastian remembered that, the theft, the extraction Dree had performed with the king's terrified consent while the court physician held the man's shoulders down. The parasite had been in him four days. Four days since the old king's death rite had gone wrong, since something ancient and patient that had been waiting in the crown regalia for longer than the kingdom had existed had slipped up through the anointing oil and settled into the young king's spine like a hand closing around a sword hilt.

Four days, and Aldric was already losing. His eyes, when they found Sebastian across the room, were still his own. That was the detail that undid her — that they were still his own, blue and human and begging.

*Do it,* the king's mouth shaped, voiceless. *Please. Before I'm not here anymore.*

And Dree Halloway stood over him with a knife, and Sebastian — memory-Sebastian, apprentice-Sebastian, twenty-six years old and certain of nothing except her teacher's authority — held the whetstone.

No. Not the whetstone.

Her hands, in the memory, were wrapped around the knife's haft alongside Dree's. Four hands on one blade. Because the oath required two swearers, an elder and a bearer, and the bearer had to draw blood from both — from the bound and from the binder — and the knife could not be wielded by the elder alone. The crooked oath demanded shared guilt. That was its design. That was why it was crooked.

*I can't,* her younger self gasped, and Dree's voice came rough above her: *"You can. Look at me, girl. Whatever happens tonight, whatever you have to do after — you can carry it. That's what I made you for."*

Lightning lit the undercroft through the high grates. In the flash, Sebastian saw the parasite.

It rose out of the king like smoke poured upward, a shape that was almost a man, almost a crowned figure, its edges wrong the way reflections in disturbed water are wrong. It wore Aldric's face the way a glove wears a hand. And it looked at her — at memory-Sebastian — and it *smiled*, and the smile said, with perfect patience: *I have waited nine hundred years. I can wait out two fools with a knife.*

*"Swear it!"* Dree roared, and the words he swore were Oathscript, and Sebastian's present-day body flinched because she knew those sounds now — she heard them nightly from a mute child's sleeping lips, fragments, echoes, the residue of this exact night. Dree's oath wound around the parasite like chain, like rope, like a snare pulled tight by desperate hands —

— and it did not hold. Even in the memory she could feel it fail, feel the binding take with a flaw in it the way a bell takes with a crack: it rang, it worked, it caged the thing down into the king's marrow, but the cage had a gap in it, a seam, a syllable mispronounced or a truth withheld, and something small and bright and furious squeezed out through that seam and fled into the world unborn.

Neither of them saw it leave. Neither of them ever knew.

*"Again,"* Dree gasped. *"We swear it again, properly—"*

*"No."* That was her own voice, younger, wrecked, final. *"No — look at him — we can't cut him again, we'll kill him, and if we kill him mid-oath it gets loose forever. It holds. It has to hold. We make it hold."*

And then the memory-Sebastian did the thing that explained everything, that answered seven years of flinching church bells and empty mornings and a name that felt borrowed. She turned the knife on herself — a shallow cut, ritual, the bearer's tithe — and spoke her half of the oath in a voice that broke on every word: that she would keep the secret, that she would bear the knowledge alone, that if the binding ever threatened to fail she would return and finish it, and that the price of her silence would be her remembrance of everything she loved, surrendered willingly, hidden in glass, until such time as the debt came due.

She sold herself to buy the kingdom's ignorance. She made herself the lock and threw away the key — except for one vial. One door left ajar.

The last image was Dree's face, tears cutting through the ash on his cheeks, whispering: *"Forgive me. Forgive me. I couldn't say the third line. If I'd said it truly it would have held, and I couldn't—"* And then the memory folded shut like a book, and the undercroft was gone, and the rain was gone, and the king's begging eyes were gone—

* * *

—and Sebastian was on the cold floor of the Vault of Given Things with an empty vial in her hand and blood on her lip where she'd bitten through it, and Ives was shaking her, both small hands pressed flat against Sebastian's cheeks, signing one sign over and over with terrible urgency.

*Stay. Stay. Stay.*

"I'm here." Her voice came out in pieces. "I'm here, little one. I'm here."

But she wasn't, quite. Part of her was still kneeling on those flagstones seven years ago, watching her own hands hold the knife, hearing her own voice choose. *Devastating* was too gentle a word. What she felt was the specific vertigo of meeting yourself at the scene of a crime and recognizing the handwriting. She had hated Dree for days for hiding this. She had hated the unknown thief who had taken her life. And all along the thief had been a woman kneeling in the rain, weeping, doing arithmetic with her own soul: one year of remembering, traded for a kingdom's survival, the remainder buried in a fence's inventory to wait for a debt she prayed would never come due.

The debt had come due. The binding was failing. She could feel it now — had maybe always felt it, in the ache the glyphs gave her, in the way Ives's presence made charms curdle. The oath she had helped swear was unraveling, and the third line Dree could never speak, the flaw in the bell, the gap in the cage —

Something had escaped through it.

Sebastian looked down at the child in her lap. At the shifting eyes, the old-parchment smell, the glyphs drawn with a certainty no living teacher had given her. At the girl who had appeared at a garret door speaking a dead tongue, who had pulled her out of a guildmaster's mind, who signed *stay* like a prayer.

The realization arrived not as a thought but as a coldness, rising through her like water filling a boot.

"Ives," she whispered. "What came out of the binding? What squeezed through the seam?"

The child went utterly still. Then, slowly, she lifted one finger and pointed — not at the shelves, not at the door, not at Serane.

At her own chest.

And in the vial-light of the Vault of Given Things, the glow of ten thousand stolen hours bent toward her, the way candles lean toward an open window.
