## 15. Why the Child Came

The vial was still warm.

Sebastian could not stop thinking about that — how a memory, once it left the skull, kept something of the body's heat, like a stone pulled from a hearth. She had held thousands of them over the years and never thought twice. But this one had been hers. This one had known her hands before she did.

She sat on the floor of Dree Halloway's back room with her spine against the cold brick of the chimney, watching the last grey light fail through the salt-fogged window, and tried to make peace with the shape of what she now knew: that seven years ago she had stood in an undercroft beneath the palace with a binding knife in her fist, weeping, while her teacher swore an oath crooked enough to hold a monster and broken enough to let it breathe. That she had sold the knowing of it to herself. That the price had been exact, and she had paid it gladly, because a thief understands better than anyone that some things are too heavy to carry and too dangerous to leave lying about.

Ives sat across from her, cross-legged on the bare boards, drawing glyphs in the dust with one small finger.

She had been doing it for an hour. Not idly — Sebastian had learned by now to tell the difference between the child's idle marks and her deliberate ones. The idle ones wandered, looped, became little ships or birds or a cat with too many legs. The deliberate ones were Oathscript, and they came out of her the way water comes out of a spring: without effort, without origin, without permission.

This evening's glyph was one Sebastian recognized from the vial-night. It was the second half of the unbinding word — the half that lived in her own stolen memories, the half Dree had refused to speak aloud even with inquisitors hammering at his door, because *the price of telling is worse than the price of ignorance*. It sat in the dust between them like a wound drawn in chalk.

"You know what it says," Sebastian said quietly. "Don't you."

Ives looked up. Her eyes were the brown of river water over stones, and they held the particular patience of someone who has been waiting a very long time for a question she already knows the answer to.

Then the child opened her mouth, and spoke.

* * *

Sebastian would remember afterward that the room went strange first — that the candle flames leaned all the same direction though there was no draft, that the fog on the window ran upward instead of down, that somewhere below them in Vellharrow's throat every church bell in the city shivered on its hook without ringing. Her memory-charms, the little protective curdles of craft she wore braided into her hair, went soft as wet paper. And then Ives said, in a voice like a bell struck once in an empty cathedral:

"I am what would not stay bound."

The common tongue. After weeks of silence and Oathscript and thieves' signs scratched in candle soot, the child's first words in any language Sebastian could answer were six syllables long, and they rearranged the furniture of the world.

Sebastian did not move. Some animal part of her understood that movement might break whatever fragile channel had opened, and that if it closed now it might never open again. So she sat very still, with her heart going like a trapped bird, and listened.

"The oath was sworn crooked," Ives said. Her voice was a child's voice — high, clear, unpracticed — but the cadence underneath it was older, the rhythm of liturgy, of law read aloud in a court where the judges are dead. "Dree Halloway meant it well. That is the tragedy of it. He meant it well, and he swore it tired, and tired oaths have gaps." She touched the glyph in the dust, and Sebastian saw the girl's fingertip leave a faint silver burnish on the brick-dust, as though the word itself were metallic. "A binding is a sentence. Every sentence has a remainder. The part that does not fit inside the cage. The part that refuses."

"And you're the remainder," Sebastian said. Her voice came out rough.

"I am the refusal." Ives smiled — a small, grave smile, far too old for the face wearing it. "When the parasite took the king, it took him whole. When Dree bound it, the binding caught everything except the piece of it that had already begun to love him. Do you understand? The thing under the crown loved its host. Not kindly. Not the way you love. But it had worn Aldric for two years by then, and something in it had grown roots, and roots do not consent to be torn up. So when the oath closed around it, that rooted piece slipped free. It fell through the cracks of the spell like water through a floor."

"Into what?"

"Into waiting," Ives said simply. "Remainders wait. That is most of what a remainder is." She drew another glyph — the first half of the word, the half that lived in the child herself, the half Dree had identified with hands that shook. "Seven years, Sebastian Winterbourne. Seven years of being nothing but a splinter of a monster's affection, drifting in the spaces between the wards of the palace, looking for a door. And then the binding began to fail — because crooked things fail, always, slowly, the way a crooked house settles — and the failure made a gap, and the gap made a door, and I walked through it into flesh." She spread her small hands, palms up, as if presenting the evidence. "Flesh is expensive. I am still learning the price of having knees."

* * *

Sebastian laughed. It was not a kind laugh; it was the laugh of a woman whose whole architecture of sense had just been kicked out from under her, and it cracked in the middle and turned into something else entirely.

"You're telling me," she said, "that the family I've spent these weeks — the meals, the signs, teaching you to pick a pocket, you drawing pictures of cats on my table — that all of it—"

"—is a spell with legs," Ives finished. "Yes."

And there it was. The thought Sebastian had been circling for days without landing on, the suspicion she'd fed and starved and fed again, spoken aloud in a voice that had only learned common speech ten seconds ago. A spell with legs. A fragment of the very thing that wore the king, sitting at her table eating bread she stole for it, sleeping curled against her back in the narrow bed like any foundling child, learning to sign *stay* with fingers that belonged, by every law of magic Sebastian knew, to the enemy.

She should have felt horror. She catalogued the feeling dutifully, the way she catalogued everything — *horror, here, expected, aisle four* — and found that what actually rose in her was grief, vast and specific and utterly unreasonable.

Because it didn't matter. That was the terrible discovery of it. She had watched this child sleep. She had taught her the nine-fingered sign for *quiet* and the two-fingered sign for *run*, and Ives had invented her own sign — thumb to sternum, then opened outward — for something none of Sebastian's lexicon had a word for, and Sebastian had understood it instantly and never asked her to translate. You did not do those things with a parasite. You did not do them with a weapon. You did them with a person.

"You could have told me sooner," Sebastian said, and heard how small it sounded, and hated it.

"I could not speak sooner." Ives's composure flickered — the first crack in the liturgical calm, and underneath it Sebastian glimpsed something achingly young. "The words needed the memories near. Yours came back tonight, even one vial of them, and the binding-night filled the room with its shape, and shapes are doors for me. Everything I am is a door, Sebastian. That is what remainders are *for*." She looked down at her hands. "I wanted to tell you with signs. I practiced. I made the sign for *monster* and the sign for *me* together, three nights ago, while you slept. You signed back *no* in your dream and rolled over."

Sebastian's throat closed.

"I'm not the monster," Ives said. "But I'm not not the monster either. I am the part of it that didn't want to be what it is. Does that make me innocent? I don't know. I don't think innocence is a thing that applies to pieces." She lifted her chin, and her river-water eyes were wet and furious and brave. "Here is what I know. The unbinding word has two halves. One lives in your sold memories — you've held it now, you've *felt* it, you know I'm not lying. The other half lives in me. It has always lived in me. I am not merely the residue of the binding, Sebastian. I am the key that the lock grew around. The oath refused to hold me because I am the part of it that was always meant to come undone."

* * *

The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere outside, Vellharrow was putting on its night noises — cart wheels, a drunk singing three streets over, the eternal drip of the cistern towers. Ordinary sounds. Sebastian clung to them like handholds.

"Say the rest," she said. "There's a rest. There's always a rest with you."

Ives almost smiled. "You are learning to read me."

"I'm a professional."

"The rest is this." The child folded her hands in her lap, and her voice dropped into the register she used for the true things, the ones that cost. "The word must be spoken truly. Not recited — *spoken*, meant, carried on a breath that has nothing left hidden in it. Any concealment in the speaker and the word comes out crooked, and a crooked unbinding is worse than no unbinding at all. It would tear the king apart and leave the parasite loose with nowhere to wear." She paused. "And the speaker must stand inside the binding when she speaks it. Which means the palace. Which means the renewal rite. Which means—"

"—walking into the mouth of the thing that has been hunting us since the night market," Sebastian finished flatly. "Of course. Why would it ever be a cellar and a quiet afternoon."

"There is more." Ives hesitated, and for the first time since she had found her voice, she seemed afraid to use it. "The word takes payment. All true words do. It requires a speaker with nothing left to lose — nothing *held back*, nothing hoarded, nothing kept behind a locked door in the skull. Whatever the speaker carries that she will not surrender, the word will find it, and the word will take it anyway, and it will take it *loudly*." Her eyes came up. "You have recovered one vial of seven, Sebastian. You have a head full of returned past and a heart full of people you have decided are yours. Dree. Serane Corvane, who left a door unlocked and pretends she doesn't know why. Me."

"Don't," Sebastian said.

"You would have to give up all of it," Ives went on, gentle and merciless as snowfall. "Not lose — *give*. There is a difference, and the word knows the difference, and it will test the difference. To speak me truly, you would have to stand in the undercroft holding my hand and mean, completely, that you are willing to forget me. To forget all of us. To empty yourself so thoroughly that the word passes through you clean." Her voice finally broke, just slightly, on the last of it. "That is why I waited to tell you. Not because I couldn't speak. Because I hoped I would be wrong."

The room was very quiet. The candle guttered and steadied.

Sebastian looked at the child — at the small body housing a splinter of a king-eater's reluctant love, at the fingers smudged with brick dust and candle soot, at the face she had memorized without meaning to, the way you memorize the face of someone you expect to grieve — and felt the full weight of the arithmetic settle onto her chest.

Every road led through the same tollgate. Renew the oath, and Ives was erased — Dree's safe crooked fix, the binding re-sworn tight, the remainder dissolved back into the cage. Speak the word, and the parasite died but so did everything Sebastian had clawed back of herself: Dree's face, Serane's bread, the weight of a sleeping child against her spine. Keep her memories, and the word came out crooked, and a crooked unbinding loosed the thing upon a city of a hundred thousand throats.

There was no version where she got to keep anything. There was only the choosing of what to pay.

"When," Sebastian said. Her voice was steady. She was distantly proud of that, the way you're proud of a corpse for looking peaceful. "When is the renewal rite."

"Nineteen days," Ives said. "The winter solstice. The crown renews its grip at the year's darkest hinge." She reached across the space between them and laid her small hand over Sebastian's scarred knuckles, and her palm was warm, humanly warm, expensively warm. "I am sorry. You should know that I am sorry. I did not choose to be born of a monster's mercy, but I chose to come to your door, out of everyone in this city. Of every door in Vellharrow, I chose the thief who sells other people's happiness because she cannot remember her own." A pause, and then, softer, the old sign rising instinctively to accompany the words — thumb to sternum, opening outward: "I chose you. Whatever else I am, that was mine to choose, and I chose it."

Sebastian turned her hand over and held on.

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a single bell began to ring — not the hour, not the quarter, but the urgent triple-stroke of the Crown's alarm, rolling down through the fog-bound streets like a stone dropped down a well. Then a second bell answered it. Then a third.

They both knew what it meant. Someone had talked. Someone always talked — Quill, or a witness from the market, or Dree's false-death unravelling at last. The mnemonic inquisitors were moving, and moving fast, and somewhere in the dark Serane Corvane was about to discover that the ledger she served had her name written in it too.

Ives stood, quick and light, and blew out the candle.

"Nineteen days," she whispered in the dark, in her new voice, in the language of the living. "We had better run."
