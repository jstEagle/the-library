## 16. Corvane's Choice

The summons came at the hour when Vellharrow's bells went wrong.

Serane Corvane had learned to mark time by that wrongness. The dawn bells rang from the Cathedral of the Ordained Word, six strokes, clean and true. But there was a seventh bell in the old tower above the Mnemonic Court, a bell no one had rung since the Proscriptions, and every morning at half-light it hummed anyway — not sound so much as pressure, a note felt behind the teeth, in the wet dark of the ears. The other inquisitors had stopped hearing it years ago. Serane heard it every day of her service, and every day she told herself it was nothing: old metal remembering an old duty, the way all things in this city remembered too much.

She was dressing when the courier knocked — a boy with the grey sash of the Inner Office, holding a folded paper sealed in black tallow rather than wax.

*Black tallow.* Her hands went very still over the laces of her boot.

There were three seals in the Mnemonic Inquisition. White wax for routine business, red for matters of state, and black tallow for questions that were not, officially, being asked. She had issued two such summonses herself in eleven years of service. Both times, the people who received them had been quietly erased from the rolls by week's end — their names scraped from ledgers, their lodgings let to strangers, their existence reduced to a marginal note in her own hand: *resolved.*

Now the black tallow was on her table, and her name was under it.

*Inquisitor S. Corvane. The Undercroft of Records. Before the seventh bell.*

She looked out the narrow window of her quarters above the Court. Below, the morning market was assembling itself in the frost — fishwives and charm-sellers, a knife-grinder singing his one song, the ordinary commerce of a city that had agreed, collectively and without ever saying so, not to remember what magic had cost them. Somewhere in that crowd, if she was right about the shape of the last fortnight, a woman named Sebastian Winterbourne was moving stolen memories through the night's last shadows, and a mute child was carrying a word older than the crown itself.

And somewhere beneath the palace, Serane thought, I left a door unlocked.

She finished lacing her boot. She pinned the silver quill of her office to her collar — the emblem of the Mnemonics, the bird that writes what cannot be spoken. Then she took down her coat, and after a moment's consideration she took something else as well: the slim iron stylus she used for burning entries out of vellum, which was technically an instrument of record and practically a weapon, and which she slid into her sleeve the way she imagined thieves slid knives.

The seventh bell hummed its wrong note. Serane Corvane went down to answer a question that was not being asked.

* * *

The Undercroft of Records sat beneath the eastern wing of the Mnemonic Court, and it smelled the way all the deep places of Vellharrow smelled: cold stone, lamp oil, and memory gone stale. Confiscated recollections did not rot like fruit; they curdled like milk, sweetening first and then turning, until what remained was thick and faintly sour, a smell that clung to the robes of anyone who worked down here and could never be washed entirely away. The junior archivists called it *the inheritance.* They joked about it in the way of people whistling past a grave.

Three lamps burned in the long reading room. At the far end, beneath the largest of them, sat First Inquisitor Halden Voss.

Serane had served under Voss for nine years. He had administered her final oath himself, his dry hand heavy on her crown, his voice reciting the words every inquisitor swore: *to guard the city against the unsanctioned remembrance; to hold the ledger above all persons; to forget, when ordered, and to remember, when ordered, and to know the difference.* He was a spare man, grey as ledger-paper, with the patient stillness of someone who had spent decades watching other people's pasts and had long ago stopped needing one of his own.

On the table before him lay a book.

Serane knew the book. Every inquisitor knew it, the way sailors know a particular star. It was the Ledger of Extractions, the master record of every unsanctioned taking since the Proscriptions — seven hundred pages of names, dates, and descriptions of things taken that should never have been taken. And she could see, even across the room, even upside down, which page lay open beneath Voss's pale fingers.

A page dated seven years ago. A page bearing a single entry in her own younger handwriting:

*Winterbourne, S. — extraction, self-administered, unsanctioned. Subject unknown. Contents unknown. Witnessed by D. Halloway (see cross-ref.). Status: OPEN.*

"Sit down, Corvane." Voss did not look up.

She sat. The chair was cold through her coat. Above them, muffled by four floors of stone, the Court was waking — footsteps, doors, the ordinary industry of an institution built on the premise that the past was dangerous and must be kept in cages.

"Seven years," Voss said, "you have carried an open file. Do you know how unusual that is? Most files resolve within the year. The city forgets; we help it." He turned the page with one finger, delicately, as though the paper might bruise. "Yours stayed open because you filed it honestly. *Contents unknown.* Most inquisitors would have invented contents. Filled the blank with something tidy. You wrote the truth: you didn't know what she took out of her own head, or why." Now he looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were the color of old tallow themselves. "I have always considered that honesty your finest quality."

"Thank you, First Inquisitor."

"It has also become your most damning one." He smoothed the page flat. "Because now the contents are known. Or will be. Winterbourne has resurfaced. She is harboring the child. And the child, we believe, carries Oathscript — the original tongue of binding." He said it flatly, without awe, the way he reported weather. "Which means that whatever Winterbourne cut out of her own head seven years ago was connected to the royal binding. Which means the open file is no longer open, Corvane. It is the most important document in this building."

Serane kept her breathing level. Beneath the table, where he could not see, her thumb found the edge of the iron stylus in her sleeve and pressed against it until the pain gave her something solid.

"And the question," she said carefully, "that is not being asked?"

Voss almost smiled. It was the closest he came to humor, and it was worse than anger. "You visited the repository four nights ago. The night log shows your seal at the service door of the Crown vault, between the second and third bells, for a duration of nine minutes. Nine minutes, Corvane. Long enough to unlock something. Not long enough to inventory anything." He folded his hands. "So the question is this. When Winterbourne comes for her memories — and she will come, they always come — do we take her at the vault door, or do we let her walk in and take everything, and follow her out?"

The lamp flame bent. Somewhere in the stacks, a confiscated memory ticked in its vellum wrapping, the small restless sound recollections made when they dreamed.

"I don't understand the question," Serane said.

"No," Voss agreed mildly. "You understand it perfectly. That is why you haven't answered it." He rose, unhurried, and came around the table, and stood looking down at her with nine years of shared oaths in his face. "Here is what happens next, whichever answer is true. If you are loyal, you will lead the detail to the vault tonight. We will take the woman, the child, and the oathbreaker Halloway — yes, we know he lives; we have known for some time, we were simply waiting to see who sought him out. All three go to the rendering vats. Their memories are decanted for the Crown. The child goes to the king's own chalice. It is a great honor, in its way. Very few citizens are selected."

The words were delivered in the tone of a supply requisition. That was the horror of Voss; Serane had known it for years and only now felt it in her stomach. He spoke of children going into chalices the way clerks spoke of grain.

"And if I am not loyal?" she asked.

"Then you will be processed alongside them, and the ledger will record that Inquisitor S. Corvane was resolved, with thanks for her service." He straightened his cuffs. "But I confess I don't believe that. Nine years, Corvane. I have read your reports. You are the most dutiful officer I have trained." His voice dropped half a tone, and for one strange moment he sounded almost kind, almost tired, almost human. "That is precisely why I am giving you the choice instead of the order. Dutiful people break, when they finally break. I want to watch which way."

He gathered the Ledger under his arm and walked toward the stair, and paused at the foot of it without turning.

"The vault. Tonight. Second bell. Bring your seal." And then, softly, as though to himself: "Bring whatever else you intend to bring. I already know what it is. I have always known what my officers carry in their sleeves. It is part of the training."

His footsteps climbed away into the dark. Serane sat alone in the undercroft with three lamps and the smell of other people's pasts, and pressed the stylus hard enough against her forearm to leave a bruise, and listened to the seventh bell humming its wrong, forbidden note overhead, and understood at last what the note had been trying to tell her for eleven years.

It was not old metal remembering an old duty.

It was the sound a thing makes when it is held in place by an oath that no longer fits — and it had been ringing for her all along.

* * *

She spent the daylight hours doing the arithmetic of her life, because that was how Serane met any crisis: columned, itemized, totaled.

Assets: one office, one rank, one reputation for incorruptibility, eleven years of service, a pension she would never collect, a set of rooms with a view of the market she loved. One seal of office, which opened more doors than any lockpick in Vellharrow. One iron stylus. One working knowledge of the vault's rotations, the guards' habits, the blind corridor behind the rendering chambers where the pipes carried rendered memory up to the palace like blood to a heart.

Liabilities: a name in a ledger that had been open seven years. An unlocked door, four nights ago, that she had told herself was an oversight and knew was not. And somewhere across the city, in a rented garret or a smuggler's loft, a thief, a liar, and a child who did not know that by the second bell tonight the full weight of the Mnemonic Inquisition would come down on them like the cathedral roof.

She tried the columns both ways.

If she stayed loyal: the woman taken, the oathbreaker taken, the child poured into a chalice for a thing wearing a king. The city safe. The ledger balanced. Herself promoted, perhaps — Voss was old; the Inner Office had been grooming her for years, everyone said so. And then every morning for the rest of her life, the seventh bell would hum, and she would hear it, and she would get up and pin the silver quill to her collar and go to work guarding the cages of the past while the present rotted at the top of the state.

She had watched the renewal rite. She had seen the king's shadow drink.

Knowing is the first treason, she had written once, in a private margin she believed no one would ever read. Everything after knowledge is only choosing sides.

In the afternoon she did one more thing, the thing that settled it more than any argument could. She went to the Hall of Petitions, where citizens queued to have grievances heard and forgotten, and she stood at the back and watched the ordinary people of Vellharrow — a widow petitioning about a wall, a boy about a debt, an old man who only wanted someone official to confirm that his wife had existed, because the records had lost her. The old man wept. The clerk stamped his form and told him, gently, that confirmation was not among the services provided.

Serane watched him shamble out into the light, and she thought: *this is what I have been guarding. Not the city. The cage.*

The oath she had sworn was to guard the city against unsanctioned remembrance. But there was a clause beneath the clause, a meaning beneath the meaning, the way there is in every oath ever sworn crooked or straight: the city was the people. The people were their pasts. To cage the past was to cage them, and she had spent eleven years perfecting the lock.

An oath kept by breaking it. The thought arrived whole, unbidden, in a cadence not her own — and she wondered, with a chill that had nothing to do with the undercroft's cold, whether somewhere a mute child had just signed something.

* * *

The Vault of Given Things received her at the second bell.

Two guards at the service door saluted her seal. Inside, the corridors ran white-lamped and silent between shelves of vellum-wrapped vials, thousands upon thousands, the confiscated centuries of Vellharrow sleeping in their paper shrouds. Serane walked the route she had walked four nights ago, past the rendering chamber where the pipes breathed their slow sweet-sour breath, and she counted her escort the way she counted everything: two ahead, one behind, Voss waiting at the junction to the inner vault with the Ledger under his arm and four more men in the shadow of the doorway behind him.

Nine, she counted. Plus the rotation outside. Fourteen. Against a thief, an old man, and a child.

They waited in the dark of the inner antechamber, lamps shuttered, for the better part of an hour. Serane stood beside Voss and felt her own heartbeat with a strange, detached interest, the way one watches another person's pulse. Once, Voss said quietly, without turning his head: "Whatever you decide, decide it soon. The door won't stay locked forever, and neither will I."

Then — a sound. Soft, at the far end of the service corridor. Not footsteps. Something smaller: the whisper of cloth, the click of a latch lifted with a professional's patience, the particular silence of people who have practiced silence.

Lamps bloomed.

"Sebastian Winterbourne," Voss said into the sudden glare, his voice filling the stone like water filling a cup, "in the name of the Ordained Word and the Crown of Aldric, you are taken for unsanctioned—"

He got no further, because Serane moved.

Later, she would never be able to reconstruct the decision — whether it lived in those daylight columns, or in the old man weeping over his wife's erasure, or in the moment the girl stepped into the lamplight with eyes too old for her face and a word coiled in her throat like a held breath. Perhaps the decision had been made years ago, in a marginal note, in an honest line written in a young clerk's hand: *contents unknown,* the only truthful sentence in a building full of lies.

What the record shows — and Serane, of all people, would insist on the record — is this: she drew the stylus from her sleeve, and before the nearest guard had finished turning she drove it into the lamp manifold on the wall, and the oil line split, and fire went up the wall of the antechamber like a sentence being written in the only language that cannot be confiscated.

"Burn it!" she shouted — to the thief, to the old man, to anyone — "the vault shelf, the western rack, burn it all—" and she put her body between Voss and the aisle, and took the truncheon blow meant for her skull on her shoulder instead, and heard bone grind, and did not fall.

Chaos is generous to the determined. Winterbourne moved through it like smoke given purpose; the old man Halloway, whom Serane had only known as a name in a dead file, fought with a ferocity that suggested his false death had been good practice; and the child — the child stood in the middle of the burning aisle with her hand raised, and where her fingers pointed, the flames leaned, obedient, as though the fire remembered being sworn to something.

The western rack caught. Seven hundred years of caged remembrance went up in a bloom of light that was not entirely light — every vial bursting released what it held, and for a handful of seconds the antechamber was full of other people's moments: a wedding, a drowning, a first snow, a betrayal, a lullaby in a language with no living speakers. The guards staggered under it, men suddenly drowning in borrowed pasts. Even Voss reeled, and in the flare Serane saw his face stripped of its ledger-grey calm, saw him young, saw him afraid.

She wrenched the Ledger from under his arm.

It was heavier than a book had any right to be — seven hundred pages of names, each one a person resolved. She held it over the fire, and for one instant Voss looked at her across the flames and did not shout, did not command, only watched her with that terrible patience, as if this too were a report he had predicted.

"You'll be nothing," he said. "No rank. No name. No record. Nothing."

"Yes," Serane said, and found she was smiling, and that the smile did not feel like ruin at all. "Finally."

She dropped the Ledger into the fire, and the names went up one page at a time, and it was the finest thing she had ever burned and the truest oath she had ever kept.

They ran — she ran with them, shoulder screaming, silver quill torn from her collar and left gleaming on the stones among the ash — out through the service vein of the palace, past the rendering pipes, into the frozen dark of the streets, where the snow was beginning again and the seventh bell, somewhere high above the waking city, hummed its long wrong note like a door finally opening.

At the mouth of the alley Winterbourne stopped, breathing hard, and looked at her with the flat assessing gaze of a woman pricing goods, and said: "Why?"

Serane looked back the way they had come, at the glow above the rooftops where the vault burned, at the smoke of seven hundred years of stolen things rising free into the night sky over Vellharrow.

"Because I read your file," she said. "*Contents unknown.* Seven years, and it was the only honest line in the building." She gripped her ruined shoulder and grimaced. "I'd like to know the contents now. And I suspect you're about to go somewhere I can help you get into."

Behind them, faint but rising, came the horns of the palace alarm — and ahead, deeper in the dark, the old man Halloway had already begun to walk, as if he knew exactly where the night was taking them, and did not like it.
