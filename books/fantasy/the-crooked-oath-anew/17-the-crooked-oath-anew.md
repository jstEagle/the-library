## 17. The Crooked Oath Anew

The undercroft beneath the ruined chantry smelled of wet stone and old incense, the ghost of a god nobody had been allowed to name in seven years. Sebastian set the last of the candles on the altar slab — a plain block of granite now, its saints chiseled away by Crown order — and watched the light crawl up the walls like something cautious.

Ives sat cross-legged in the corner with her slate balanced on her knees, drawing glyphs over and over until the chalk wore down to a gray nub. She did not look up. She had not looked up at Dree since the confession. That was the thing about a child who could not speak: her silences were louder than anyone else's accusations.

Dree Halloway stood apart from them all, near the broken stair, working his ruined hands together the way he always did when he was rehearsing something. Sebastian knew that tell. He had taught it to her — *never let them see you compose, Seb, composition is where liars live* — and here he was, composing.

"Say it," she said.

He flinched. Good. Let him.

"You've been circling the room for an hour like a moth that's decided the candle's family." She straightened, rolled the ache out of her shoulders. The extraction at the guildhall had left bruises inside her skull she could feel when she turned too fast, a deep soreness like a tooth knocked loose but still rooted. "Whatever you're going to propose, propose it while I'm still tired enough to listen."

Serane Corvane sat near the far wall, armor stripped down to her shirt-sleeves, her ledger — the burned one, or what remained of it, a leather spine and a fistful of half-charred pages — laid across her knees like evidence she couldn't stop carrying. She looked up sharply at Sebastian's tone but said nothing. She was learning, slowly, when to be a wall and when to be a door.

Dree came into the candlelight at last.

"There are two ways this ends," he said. "You know that."

"I know one way." Sebastian kept her voice flat. "We get to the rite. I speak the word. Ives speaks it with me. The thing comes off the throne like a tick off a dog, and Aldric wakes up seven years older and seven years robbed, and we run before anyone decides we're traitors instead of surgeons."

"That's one way," Dree agreed. "It's also the way that kills everyone in this room if you're wrong by a syllable." He crouched, slow, joints cracking, and drew in the dust with one finger: two interlocking circles, broken at the seam. "Oathscript isn't language, Seb. Not really. It's load-bearing. Every word is a beam. The original binding was sworn crooked — I told you that much, and I'll not retell it tonight because my shame keeps well enough without stirring. But do you understand *how* it went crooked?"

"The knife," Sebastian said. Her mouth had gone dry. Somewhere behind her eyes, in the vial-shaped dark where her recovered memories still lived, she could see it: her own hand, her own tears, the blade that had carried the oath into the king's flesh. "The binding was cut through a vessel already half-worn. You swore the oath around a moving thing. It caught wrong."

"It caught *crooked*," Dree said. "Like a stitch through the edge of a wound instead of the middle. It holds. Gods know it holds — it's held seven years, and Aldric has smiled his way through every festival and famine the kingdom's thrown at him. But it holds the way a rotten rope holds. And every year at the renewal rite, they feed it, and the stitch pulls tighter, and one day soon—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound cracked off the vaulted dark "—the rope parts, and everything it was holding comes down at once. The parasite free. The king dead, probably. Half the court worn within the month, hollow-eyed and smiling. A city full of vessels, Seb. A kingdom of them."

Ives had stopped drawing. Sebastian felt it before she saw it — the small, total attention of the child turning toward them like a compass needle finding north. The memory-charms sewn into Sebastian's coat collar gave their familiar sour twinge, milk curdling in a hidden glass.

"So there's another way," Sebastian said. Not a question. She'd watched Dree's face for an hour; she already hated whatever lived there.

"There's another way." He wiped the broken circles away and drew new ones. Whole this time. Unbroken. "The original oath was meant for an unworn host. Sworn clean, at the moment of first possession, it would have caged the thing perfectly — no seams, no slack, no residue." His voice caught on that last word, and Sebastian watched him force himself past it. "We can't go back seven years. But the renewal rite re-opens the binding every year. For the length of the ceremony, the oath is *loose*. Malleable. If someone swears the crooked oath anew — properly, completely, in Oathscript, with the right anchor and the right blood-price — the binding seals true. Permanent. Seamless. The parasite caged for as long as the crown exists. Maybe longer."

"And the cost," Serane said quietly, from the wall. It wasn't a question either. Inquisitors were trained to hear the shape of a price before it was spoken. "There's always a cost."

Sebastian didn't look at Dree. She looked at the whole circles in the dust. She looked at the space inside them, which was empty, and understood, with the slow cold slide of ice water down her spine, why it was empty.

She looked at Ives.

"No," she said.

"Seb—"

"*No.*" The word came out of her with more force than she'd meant, and the nearest candle guttered, and Ives flinched — actually flinched, shoulders drawing in, chalk clutched white-knuckled — and Sebastian made herself lower her voice, made herself unclench her hands. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me the seal doesn't require the residue. Tell me the part of the oath that refused to hold — the part standing in this room holding a piece of chalk — doesn't have to be *returned* to make the whole complete."

Dree's face did the thing she had seen dying men's faces do: the honest collapse, the moment the performance ends because there's no audience left worth lying to.

"She wouldn't suffer," he said, which was the worst possible answer, because it was the answer of a man who had rehearsed even his mercy. "It wouldn't be death, Seb, it's not like that. She'd never have existed. The oath takes back what leaked out of it. There's no pain. There's no—"

"There's no *her*," Sebastian said. "That's what there isn't."

"She's not real!" It burst out of him, and immediately he looked stricken, but the words were out, hanging in the incense-dark like smoke. "She's a leak, Seb. She's the slop that spilled over the edge of a botched spell and grew legs. Seven years of spilled magic wearing a child. She doesn't eat like a person eats, she doesn't sleep like a person sleeps, she's made of *leftovers*—"

Serane was on her feet. Sebastian hadn't heard her move. "Get out of arm's reach of me," the inquisitor said, very quietly, "before I remember I used to burn people for less."

Dree raised both hands. "I'm trying to save lives. All of yours. Hers is the only one that isn't—"

"Don't." Sebastian crossed the undercroft in four strides and put herself between Dree and Ives, though he hadn't moved toward the girl, though he likely never would. It didn't matter. Some lines you drew with your body or not at all. "Finish the sentence. Say *real* again. Say the only life in this room that isn't worth saving is the one that learned my signs in three days, that pulled me out of a guildmaster's head when I was drowning in it, that walked through a city hunting us with nothing but a slate and a language older than the crown it's trying to break." She was shaking. She noticed distantly, the way you notice weather. "Say it, Dree. You taught me extraction. Teach me this. Convince me."

He couldn't. She watched him try and fail — watched the old rhetoric assemble itself behind his eyes, *the greater good, the arithmetic of souls, one against thousands* — and watched it fall apart, because he had taught her that rhetoric too, years ago, in a garret much like hers, and then he had taught her, later, with his own ruined hands, what it looked like when a man spent other people's currency to cover his own debts.

"I carried the knife," he said at last, barely audible. "Seven years ago. I helped bind it crooked, and I let *you* carry the knife, and I let you sell the memory so there'd be no witness left, not even yourself. Everything since — every hour of your missing years, every night you woke reaching for people whose names you didn't know — that's mine. Mine and the parasite's, split down the middle." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "And now I find the one clean way to fix it, and it costs the child, and you want me to say she matters more than a kingdom. Seb. I am *asking* you. I don't trust my own judgment anymore. I haven't for seven years. Tell me I'm a monster for even suggesting it."

"You're not a monster," Sebastian said. "Monsters steal because they're hungry. This is worse. This is theft with better manners."

She turned her back on him. Behind her, she heard him sit down heavily on the broken stair, and heard nothing after that, which was somehow louder.

* * *

Ives had drawn something new on the slate.

Sebastian lowered herself to the floor beside the child, knees popping, and studied it. Two figures, rendered in the fierce economical strokes Ives favored: a tall one with a slash of chalk for a knife-hand, and a small one holding up a circle. Above them, in Oathscript, three glyphs. Sebastian had been learning them by feel — the way they ached in her teeth, the way some of them made her missing years throb like a pulled tooth, which was how she'd come to think of the alphabet of her own ruin.

"This one means 'whole,'" she said, tapping the first glyph carefully, testing the sound-shape of it without voicing it. Ives nodded. "This one..." The second glyph made her tongue heavy just tracing it. "'Broken.'" Another nod. Then Ives took Sebastian's hand — her fingers were cool, always cool, like a person carved from the quiet hour before dawn — and pressed Sebastian's fingertip to the third glyph, and shook her head.

Not *whole*. Not *broken*. Something else.

"Not yet?" Sebastian guessed.

Ives considered this with grave seriousness, then tilted her hand side to side. *Close.*

"Neither," Sebastian tried. "In-between. Becoming."

The child's whole face lit, and it was such a human thing, such an unbearably ordinary human thing, that Sebastian had to look away for a moment at the candle flame until her throat agreed to behave.

*I am neither,* Ives signed then, quick and certain, the signs flowing one into the next the way water finds its level. *I am not the spill. I am the refusal. The part that said no.* Her hands slowed. *If they seal the circle, there is no place where a refusal can stand. That is why I must be taken back. That is why it will work.*

Sebastian stared at her. "How long have you known?"

Since the beginning, Ives signed simply. Since Quill's shop. Before.

"And you didn't say."

Ives looked at her then, and her eyes were the gray of old silver, and in them Sebastian saw the same terrible patience she had once seen in mirrors during her worst years: the look of someone who has already done all their grieving in private and is now simply waiting for the world to catch up.

*You would have chosen differently,* Ives signed. *You would have chosen me. And the circle stays crooked, and everyone dies smiling.*

"That's not your choice to carry."

*Whose, then?* The signs were sharp now. *Yours? You sold your own memories rather than carry what you knew. His? He hides from his own name. Hers?* — a tilt of the chin toward Serane — *She burned her ledger and calls it conscience. Everyone in this room is an expert at choosing what to forget. Only I cannot forget. Only I am the thing that remembers being refused.*

The candles burned. Somewhere above them, through the chantry's shattered roof, the bells of Vellharrow began to ring the evening hour — and Sebastian flinched, the old flinch, the one with no memory attached to it, the body keeping score the mind had paid to forget. Ives reached up and touched her wrist. Just that. Just contact, steady and cool.

*Bells rang the night of the binding,* Ives signed softly. *You know that now. From the vial.*

"I know that now," Sebastian whispered.

*Then you know why I came to your door. Out of everyone alive, you had already paid once. You were the only thief in the city with nothing left to steal.* A pause, and then, with the smallest, most devastating economy of movement: *Do not pay twice for the wrong thing.*

* * *

Later, when Ives slept — curled in the alcove behind the defaced altar, wrapped in Serane's cloak, breathing in the shallow pattern that wasn't quite a person's breathing but was close enough, close enough, gods, it was close enough — Sebastian found Dree sitting where she'd known he'd be, at the base of the broken stair, sharpening a knife he had no earthly use for anymore.

"The renewal rite is in six days," she said.

"I know."

"If I swear the oath anew — the correct version, your perfect seamless cage — what happens to the speaker?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was one mercy left in him. "The oath needs an anchor. A living stake driven through the middle of the binding, to hold it true. Whoever speaks the crooked oath becomes the stake. They don't die. They... persist. Standing. Watching. For as long as the crown lasts." He met her eyes at last. "Your friend the courtier with the hollow eyes. The vessels aren't all failures, Seb. Some of them are anchors. Old ones. Worn smooth."

Something turned over in Sebastian's chest, cold and enormous. Seven years of vessels. Seven years of smiling courtiers who never blinked during the anthem.

"How many anchors does the current binding have?"

"One," Dree said. "That's why it's crooked. The original design called for three."

Three. Three stakes driven through a parasite, and one man had carried all of them for seven years, smiling without blinking, drinking rendered memory from a chalice, while the kingdom called him loyal. Sebastian thought of the hollow-eyed courtier marking her face in the crowd, and wondered suddenly, horribly, whether the thing on the throne had ever looked at *her* — at the apprentice who'd carried the knife — with recognition. With hope. With hunger.

"Dree," she said slowly. "When the parasite offered to give me my memories back if I renewed the oath. When it showed me the family I forgot." She made herself keep going. "Was it tempting me? Or was it *recruiting* me?"

The whetstone stopped.

"Six days," Dree said, and his voice had gone thin as paper. "Seb, if it wants you for the third anchor — if it's been waiting seven years for the knife-bearer to walk back into reach — then the rite isn't just our chance to end it." He swallowed. "It's *its* chance to finish itself."

Above them, faint through the broken roof, the bells finished their hour and fell silent. And in the silence, from the alcove behind the altar, came the small clear scrape of chalk on slate — Ives, awake after all, writing something she did not show them.

Yet.
