## 18. Palace of Worn Faces

The palace did not sleep. It *smiled*.

That was the thing no ballad mentioned, Sebastian thought, pressed into the shadow of a buttress while the moon slid behind cloud: the Crown's seat never truly darkened. Windows burned gold on every floor, and behind the glass moved figures — servants, guards, ladies-in-waiting, all of them going about their duties with a smoothness that had stopped looking like grace somewhere around year three of Aldric's reign and started looking like *rehearsal*. The same rehearsed grace. Over and over. A city of clocks wound by one hand.

"Left," Ives signed, small fingers quick in the darkness between them. *Left, then down, then the door that smells of tallow.*

Sebastian almost laughed. Three months ago the girl hadn't known a single thief's sign; now she corrected Sebastian's route through the servant veins of Vellharrow's heart as if she'd been born in its walls. Perhaps she had been born in them, in a manner of speaking. Born of an oath, bled out of a binding, given legs and silence and eyes too old for her face.

*Left,* Sebastian signed back. *Stay behind me. Serane second. Dree last.*

Dree Halloway brought up the rear because he'd insisted on it, and because none of them had the strength left to argue with a dying man about where he preferred to stand when the knives came out. He looked terrible in the good way — the way soldiers look terrible before a battle they've decided to win. His false beard was gone; his real face, gaunt and grey-stubbled, was stranger to her than any disguise would have been.

"You're staring," he murmured as they crossed a yard reeking of ash and lye water.

"I'm memorizing," Sebastian said. "In case."

"In case of what?"

She didn't answer, and after a moment he huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "In case," he agreed quietly. "Aye. Memorize away, then. It's the only inheritance I've got worth leaving you."

Serane Corvane moved ahead of them both, and it still unsettled Sebastian to see her there — mnemonic inquisitor, ledger-keeper, seven years of dutiful horror rendered in grey wool and a blade shaped like a stylus. She wore no insignia tonight. She'd burned them in Quill's hearth along with the ledger, and the fire had gone briefly blue, as if even flames kept records and were shocked to be asked to destroy one.

"The laundry vein," Serane whispered now, crouching at a grate that breathed warm, wet air up from below. "It runs beneath the west wing to the undercroft stairs. There will be workers. They won't question us if we don't stop moving. Stopping is what gets people questioned."

"And if they do question us?" Dree asked.

Serane's jaw tightened. "Then we learn whether my resignation letter reached anyone's hands before I did."

---

The grate gave onto a corridor of wet stone and hanging linens, sheets ghosting past them in the steam-drafts like the shrouds of drowned things. The air tasted of lye and tallow and, underneath, something sweeter and far worse — the smell Sebastian had first caught outside the king's audience hall. Rendered memory. Someone was boiling recollection somewhere in this building by the gallon, and the whole palace drank it in through its skin.

They passed the first of them in a junction where three corridors met.

A scullery maid stood wringing out a cloth, though the cloth had been dry for some time. She was perhaps forty. Her hands worked. Her mouth smiled. And her eyes —

Her eyes did nothing at all. Not blinked, not darted, not *acknowledged*. They sat in her face like river stones, and when Sebastian slowed despite herself, the woman turned her head with a smoothness no spine should own and said, pleasantly:

"Mind the wet floor, mistress. Wouldn't want you slipping."

The voice was warm. The voice was kind. The voice came out of a face that had nothing behind it but a smile stretched over an absence, and Sebastian felt Ives's small hand seize two of her fingers and grip hard enough to grind bone.

*I know,* Sebastian signed against the girl's shoulder, one-handed, clumsy. *I know. Keep walking.*

They kept walking. Behind them the wringing resumed. The smile, Sebastian was certain, remained.

"They're not all vessels," Serane murmured once they'd rounded the corner. "Some are just tired. You can't tell anymore — that's rather the point, isn't it? A court where everyone smiles identically is a court where the parasite can wear any face it likes and hide among ten thousand volunteers."

"How many?" Sebastian asked. "How many of them are empty?"

Serane was quiet long enough that three more smiling shapes drifted past them in the steam, carrying candles, carrying linens, carrying nothing at all behind their pleasant faces. "I filed reports on eleven confirmed hollowings in my first year of service," she said finally. "I stopped filing in my fourth. Do the arithmetic on that however you can bear to."

Ives made a sound — not a word, never a word, not yet, not until the word that mattered — but a small, wounded exhale through the nose, and she signed rapidly, angrily: *They took their hours. Like you take hours. But they didn't give them anywhere. They just — poured them out.*

"Yes," Sebastian said softly. "That's what it does. That's what it's always done."

And that was the arithmetic that mattered, she thought grimly, following Serane down a stair that spiraled into warmer dark. Seven years ago she had carried a knife into an oath-chamber to cage this thing, and she had paid for the privilege with her own past, and she had thought — she had *believed*, with the arrogant mercy of the young — that a crooked oath held badly was better than no oath at all.

Seven years of poured-out people later, she knew better. Some cages are just slower mouths.

---

The undercroft stairs let out beside the rite-chamber itself, and here Serane stopped them with a raised fist and pressed her ear to the door.

Beyond it: chanting. Low, rhythmic, wrong — a liturgy in Oathscript, which meant the words themselves were doing work, dragging at the air the way a net drags at water. Beneath the chanting, a sound like a great slow heartbeat, or like wine being poured very patiently into a cup that could hold no more.

"Renewal rehearsal," Serane breathed. "Full court. Tomorrow night they'll do it true — renew the oath, feed the thing another seven years, seal Aldric to it forever." Her voice frayed at the edges. "Tonight is the dress rehearsal. Which means tonight the chamber is guarded by ceremony instead of soldiers. Ceremony we can pass. Soldiers we couldn't."

"So we go through the rehearsal," Sebastian said.

"We go through the rehearsal," Serane agreed. "Past the throne, past the chalice, into the reliquary gallery where the vault entrance lies. Serane — *I* — mapped it years ago during inventory. Forty feet of open floor with the entire assembled court watching."

"Watching," Dree said, "but not seeing. That's the gift of a room full of worn faces, girl. They smile at whatever walks in front of them."

"Not all of them." Sebastian touched Ives's shoulder. "Not him."

The chanting swelled. Somewhere beyond the door, a bell tolled once — deep, resonant, the coronation bell itself — and Sebastian flinched, hard, before she could stop herself. The old wound. The flinch with no memory attached to it. Except now she knew what lived on the other side of it: a night, a knife, an oath sworn crooked, herself weeping in the corner of a room while Dree spoke words he only half understood.

Bells meant beginnings. Bells meant oaths. Bells meant her, standing in a circle of power with blood on her hands and calling it duty.

"Sebastian." Ives tugged her sleeve. Signed, gentle: *Now is not then.*

Sebastian exhaled. *No,* she signed back. *Now is ours.* She drew the thin extraction needle from her sleeve — not to use, just to feel its weight, the weight of her trade, her old competence, the self she'd been before all of this. "Right. Dree takes the gallery side. Serane walks us straight down the center aisle if anyone challenges — she still has the face for it. Ives stays glued to me. We reach the vault, we get what we came for, we walk out the same way."

"And if the king looks at you?" Serane asked quietly.

"Then I'll smile," Sebastian said. "I'm told it's traditional."

---

The doors opened on light and incense and a hundred unblinking smiles.

The rite-chamber of Vellharrow's palace had been built to make mortals feel small before their kings, and it succeeded even now, even knowing what sat on the throne. Candles in their thousands. Gold leaf catching firelight until the walls themselves seemed to breathe. And there, on the dais beneath the cracked-and-repaired crown — repaired, Sebastian noted, *recently*, a hairline fracture filled with new gold like a scar — sat King Aldric, robed in white, hands folded, watching his court with an expression of mild, patient benevolence.

He did not blink during the chant. He did not blink when the chalice was borne up the aisle, black iron and steaming, its contents that sweet-rot smell concentrated into something that made Sebastian's teeth ache and Ives tremble against her side. He did not blink when the cup touched his lips and he drank, throat working, seven years of someone's stolen childhood sliding down into the thing that wore him.

But he *did* blink — once, slowly, deliberately — when Sebastian walked into the room.

Forty feet of marble. Serane on her left in borrowed livery, chin high, every inch the officer. Ives's hand in hers, small and burning-hot. Dree peeling away toward the gallery shadows as planned, unhurried, a grey man among grey men. The court watched them pass with those river-stone eyes, and the chanting never faltered, and Sebastian counted her footsteps the way she'd once counted seconds in a mark's mind during an extraction — *one, two, three* — each one a small theft of time from a room that owned all of it.

Twenty feet. The king's head tracked her. Only his head. The body stayed folded and serene while the neck rotated a degree past comfortable, past possible, and the eyes — Aldric's eyes, a good man's eyes, buried so deep under the wearing that only a glint of the original owner showed through like a coin at the bottom of a well — those eyes fixed on her and *recognized* her.

His mouth curved. He did not speak. He didn't need to. The curve said: *There you are. I kept your seat.*

Ten feet. Five. The reliquary gallery arch loomed to their right, and Serane angled them toward it, casual as a woman steering children to their pew—

—and then the bell rang again, unprompted, and everything happened at once.

Later, Sebastian could never assemble the sequence properly. There was shouting — the first genuine, cracked, *human* shouting she'd heard in the palace, and it came from the gallery, from Dree, roaring Oathscript at the top of a voice he'd spent seven years pretending was dead. There was light, sickly amber, pouring off the dais like spilled honey. There were guards — real ones, awake ones — converging, and Serane turning to meet them with her stylus-blade drawn and her face set in an expression Sebastian would remember longer than any memory she'd ever sold.

And there was Dree, standing alone on the gallery steps between them and the thing on the throne, arms spread wide, buying distance with his body the way he'd once taught her to buy silence with a lie.

*"GO,"* he bellowed — common tongue now, for her, for the last lesson — *"the word needs a speaker, not a corpse! GO, girl, and be better at finishing than I ever was at starting!"*

The king rose. The court, all hundred of them, rose with him in perfect unison, a field of smiles tilting upward like flowers toward a poisoned sun. Something vast moved in the shadows behind the throne — something that had been sitting there all along, patient as debt — and Dree Halloway, oathbreaker, liar, teacher, debt-with-a-face, met it without stepping back.

Sebastian ran. She hated herself for running and ran anyway, hauling Ives through the gallery arch as behind them the amber light went white and Dree's voice cut off mid-word, cleanly, like a page torn out.

---

The vault corridor swallowed them, and the sounds of the rite-chamber faded to a muffled roar, and still Sebastian ran, Ives's hand in hers, past shelves of confiscated charms sleeping under glass, past jars that whispered, until Serane caught up, bleeding from the scalp, breathing hard, and slammed the gallery gate behind them.

"He's alive," Serane said. It wasn't hope in her voice. It was arithmetic. "The thing doesn't waste vessels that still have debts outstanding. He'll be kept. Questioned. Worn, maybe, if it learns how to fit him."

"Then we finish this tomorrow," Sebastian said, "while it's busy fitting itself to him instead of guarding its crown."

Ives tugged her sleeve. Signed, urgent, trembling: *Tomorrow the oath renews. Tomorrow it becomes forever.*

"Then tomorrow," Sebastian said, and knelt to bring herself level with the child, and found her own hands were shaking, and made them stop, because grief was a luxury with a schedule and theirs was dawn. "Tomorrow we say the word. All of it. Whatever it costs."

Ives looked at her — looked *into* her, the way she did, the way that made Sebastian feel like a house with all its windows lit — and then the girl lifted her small hands and signed something slow and deliberate, the way you sign a vow:

*You'll lose the remembering of me.*

"I know," Sebastian said aloud, and her voice didn't break, which she counted as the day's single victory. "I know, little wren. But a debt you pay forward isn't lost. It's just... waiting on the other side of the door."

Beyond the vault gate, faint through the stone, the coronation bell began to toll the hour — and beneath it, threaded through it like a worm through fruit, came a voice in Oathscript, vast and amused and patient, speaking in a cadence she almost recognized.

It was practicing her name.
