## 19. The Thing Wearing the Crown

They came into the throne room the way water comes into a drowning lung — from below, through passages never meant for light.

The servant veins of the palace had delivered them, at the last, into a gallery above the great hall: a musicians' balcony hung with dusty banners of dead campaigns, where Sebastian crouched now between Serane and Ives and looked down upon a court assembled like flowers pressed in a book — beautiful, arranged, and utterly without breath of their own.

Below, the renewal rite was already beginning.

Candles by the hundred. A chalice of rendered memory glinting on the altar steps like a cup of liquid dusk. And on the throne, King Aldric — or the thing that had worn him for seven years — sat smiling down at his nobles with the patient contentment of a spider in a well-fed web.

He was not blinking. None of them were, quite. Sebastian counted the courtiers nearest the dais: forty seconds without a blink, fifty, a full minute, the whole shining assembly gazing up at their king with eyes like polished river stones. The only movement in the hall was small and human and terrible — a young maid near the far wall, blinking rapidly, wiping her hands on her apron over and over, the one person in the room still entirely herself and knowing it.

*That's what it looks like,* Sebastian thought, *to be awake in here.* Her stomach turned over slowly, like something settling at the bottom of a deep well.

Beside her, Serane had gone very still in the way she went still before violence — not fear, but arithmetic. "The rite renews at moonrise," she breathed. "The chalice must touch his lips before the last bell. After that—"

"After that it's sealed for another seven years." Dree's voice, low, from the stair behind them. He looked older than he had that morning, which Sebastian would have thought impossible; oathbreakers age in decades when they stand near the oaths they broke. "And we are out of clever options, girl. So we do the stupid one."

"The stupid one," Sebastian agreed.

Ives tugged her sleeve.

The child signed quickly, urgently — *wait, listen, wrong* — and then pressed her small palm flat against Sebastian's breastbone, over the heart, and held it there. Through the contact Sebastian felt it again, that curdling she'd first known at her own doorstep: her memory-charms rotting in their pouches, her thoughts going strange and syrup-slow, and underneath it all, faint as a pulse under ice, *the word*. Ives's word. The unbinding, living in the girl's throat like a swallowed coal.

*Soon,* Sebastian signed back. *When it's time.*

Ives did not look convinced. Seven years old and she had already learned that adults' promises were just lies with better posture. She kept hold of Sebastian's hand anyway, and together they watched the king rise from his throne.

* * *

Aldric descended the dais steps alone. The heralds did not announce him. The court did not kneel. They simply watched, stone-eyed, as the man who was not a man crossed to the altar and lifted the chalice of rendered memory in both hands.

Up close — even from the balcony, Sebastian could see it now, could see what she had refused to see at the public audience — the seams. The way the king's smile sat slightly too high on his face, like a mask hung a finger's width above the skull it covered. The way his shadow fell wrong in the candlelight: too long, too many-jointed, pooling around his boots like spilled ink rather than cast by his body. When he raised the chalice, the shadow raised something else entirely, something with too many fingers, and drank first.

The king drank after. His throat moved. His eyes closed — and for one unguarded moment, one flicker, Sebastian saw what was underneath the smile: a man. Old, exhausted, drowned-deep, looking out through his own eyes from somewhere very far away, mouthing something against the inside of his stolen mouth.

*Help me,* maybe. Or *run.* Or simply his own name, said over and over, the way a man says his name so he doesn't forget he ever had one.

Sebastian's hand found Ives's shoulder and gripped it harder than she meant to.

Then the moment passed. The king set down the chalice. Turned.

And looked directly up at the balcony where they hid.

* * *

"He can't—" Serane began.

The hall's great doors boomed shut. Every candle flame bent sideways at once, as if the room had inhaled, and the temperature dropped so fast that Sebastian's breath came out silver. Below, the court turned as one body — one smooth, coordinated, horrible body — until four hundred faces were aimed upward at the gallery, four hundred pairs of unblinking eyes, and among them Sebastian found the hollow-eyed courtier from the audience, the vessel whose face she had split open in a warehouse fight two nights ago, standing now with fresh skin over the wound and an expression of mild, waiting interest.

"Sebastian Winterbourne."

The king's voice. But it wasn't — she understood that immediately, the way you understand a counterfeit coin the moment it touches your palm. The sound came out of Aldric's mouth but it did not come from Aldric; it came from *around* him, from the walls, from beneath the flagstones, from everywhere at once, a voice with no throat and no limit. It filled the gallery the way water fills a hull.

"You have walked very far to bring me such a small thing," the voice said, gentle, almost fond. "Come down. There is no need for all this climbing and creeping. I have been expecting you since before you knew you were coming."

Dree moved first — of course Dree moved first, moving toward danger the way other men moved toward drink — and put himself bodily between the king and the children at the rail. "She doesn't take audience with parasites," he called down, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word *parasites*, because some part of him, Sebastian realized with a cold drop in her stomach, was speaking to himself as much as to the thing below.

"No," the voice agreed pleasantly. "You would know about parasites, wouldn't you, Dree Halloway. You helped sew me in. Come down too. All debts keep."

Serane's hand was on her blade. Ives had gone rigid, both fists pressed over her own mouth, as though she could hold the word inside her body by force, keep it from being taken or tempted out.

And Sebastian stood at the rail with her heart hammering like a thief at a door, and made the only decision available, which was the stupid one.

"I'm coming down," she called, and stepped off the balcony stair into the open, into four hundred unblinking faces, walking down the aisle toward the king with Ives's hand clutched in hers and Dree's hissed curse following her like a stray dog.

* * *

Up close, the wrongness had texture. The king smelled of cold candlewax and old honey and, beneath that, faintly, of rendered memory — that copper-and-rain scent Sebastian knew from her trade, from lockets and vials and the night market stalls where hours of other people's lives changed hands. The parasite wore its host the way a bad fence wears stolen goods: carelessly, certain no one would check the markings.

"There," the voice said through the king's smiling mouth. "Was that so difficult? You steal memories, little thief. I wear them. We are colleagues, you and I. I have admired your work."

It gestured — Aldric's hand, graceful, ringed — and the air beside it unfolded.

Not with light. With *remembering.*

Sebastian had spent seven years inside the shape of her own absence. She knew every wall of it: the flinch at church bells with no reason attached; the empty shelf in her mind where a name should have been; the way she woke some mornings with tears already drying and no memory of dreaming. She had built her whole careful life around that hole, mortared her loneliness into it brick by brick.

Now the hole filled in.

Not returned — *shown*, projected into the air between them like smoke given outline, and Sebastian stood paralyzed while her own forgotten life played itself out in the candlelight:

A garret — this garret, her garret — crowded with people. Quill laughing, actually laughing, head thrown back, a bottle of plum wine passing hand to hand. A woman with grey braid and ink-stained fingers teaching Ives-sized children card tricks on the floor — no, older children, three of them, and one of them calling Sebastian *auntie* in a voice that cracked her open like an egg. Herself, younger, dancing badly at a wedding in the night market while Dree played a fiddle and pretended not to enjoy it. Winter feasts. Summer roofs. Someone — a big man, gentle-handed, a locksmith maybe — carrying her home drunk over his shoulder while she sang. Dozens of evenings. Hundreds. Years of them. A family, assembled from strays and thieves and broken people, loving her with the specific ferocity of people who had chosen each other.

*I had this,* Sebastian thought, and the thought went through her like a knife through wet paper. *I had all of this, and I sold it, and I don't even remember grieving it.*

"And there is more," the parasite murmured, and the vision shifted.

It showed her another version. A variation. The same garret, the same laughter — but no child at the edges of it. No mute girl drawing glyphs in candle-soot. In this version of her life, Sebastian saw herself happy, whole, surrounded by her found family, and nowhere — nowhere in any frame of it — any trace of Ives. No small cold hand in hers. No Oathscript aching in her teeth. No one to walk into the undercroft beside her. Just the family, warm and complete, and a gap shaped like nothing at all.

"You could have this back," the king's mouth said softly. "All of it. Every hour I hold of yours, returned intact — and your choice besides. Keep the version with the child, or the version without. I am generous. I have worn kings; I know how generosity is done. All I ask—" and here the smile widened, and Aldric's eyes, Aldric's poor drowned eyes, rolled up briefly white before the thing behind them settled back into place, "—is the small service you performed once already. The knife. The words. Renew my oath, Sebastian Winterbourne, and go home to your family. Refuse, and the girl finishes her word, and everything I have kept of you burns with me. Every feast. Every laugh. Every auntie. Gone forever, and you will spend the rest of your life unable even to mourn them properly."

Silence. Four hundred stone faces watching. Somewhere high above, the bells began to move toward midnight.

Sebastian became aware, distantly, that she was crying — that tears had been running down her face for some time, unnoticed, the way rain starts while you're indoors. She looked at the vision of her family hanging in the air, at the woman with the grey braid, at the big gentle locksmith, at the child calling her auntie. She looked at the second version, the clean one, the tidy one, the one without Ives.

And she understood, finally, completely, what kind of thief she was dealing with.

Because the parasite had made a mistake. It had shown her the version without Ives thinking it would tempt her — a life uninterrupted, uncomplicated, safe. But Sebastian had spent seven years living precisely that kind of life: careful, curated, every dangerous and beloved thing excised. She knew exactly what that version cost. She was standing in it. It was a room with all the furniture removed and called a palace.

"They weren't yours to offer," she said. Her voice came out rough and steady. "None of it. That's the whole of my trade, creature — I know stolen goods when I see them. And you're holding a warehouse full."

The king's smile did not waver. But the shadows in the hall drew in, all of them, toward the dais, like a tide reversing.

"Then let us see," the thing wearing the crown said, "what the girl chooses, when she learns what speaking her word will truly cost."

Behind her, Sebastian felt Ives go stiff as a drawn wire — and heard, for the first time in all their weeks together, the child make a sound. Not a word. Not yet. Just a small, terrified intake of breath, the sound of someone standing at the edge of a very long fall.

The last bell began to toll.
