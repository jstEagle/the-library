## 20. The Unbinding Word

The thing wearing King Aldric's face held out its hand, and in that hand was a vial of light.

It was small — no longer than Sebastian's thumb, no wider than a sparrow's egg — and it glowed the way a hearth glows at dusk when you are coming home cold: gold and amber and the particular rose-color of a kitchen where someone is laughing. She knew that light. She had carried its twin beneath her own ribs for seven years without knowing what the ache was called.

"Everything," said the king's mouth. "Every hour you sold. Every hour they took from you in payment. The woman who sang in the stairwell of the Drowned Lantern while Dree Halloway pretended not to listen. The boy who taught you to pick a lock with a wishbone. The winter Quill kept your coat behind his counter for free because you'd caught fever and he was afraid you'd die in it. All of it, whole, unspoiled, yours again." The smile spread slowly, the way rot spreads through fruit, beautiful until you understand what you're seeing. "You need only speak the crooked oath anew. Renewal, not ruin. A binding well-made instead of a binding broken."

The throne room had gone very quiet. Courtiers lined the walls like candles, all of them lit from within by something that was not themselves, all of them smiling, none of them blinking. Serane stood three steps below the dais with her ledger-burned hands hidden in her skirts. Somewhere behind the great doors, muffled by oak and iron, she could hear Dree being dragged — or not being dragged; there had been a sound, a single wet sound, and then nothing, and Sebastian had made herself stop listening because listening would unmake her before the work was done.

*Some debts can only be paid forward.* Dree's voice, or her memory of it, which was the same thing now and would soon be neither.

Ives's hand was in hers. Small, warm, calloused at the fingertips from weeks of signing. The girl was trembling, but she did not pull away. Her other hand was raised, fingers shaping the first glyph of the unbinding — the one that looked like a door opening onto a door opening onto nothing — and around her wrist the air itself seemed to thin, as though reality were a cloth someone had begun unpicking at the seam.

"You hesitate," the parasite observed. It tilted Aldric's head, and the crown — black iron worked into the shape of thorns, older than any kingdom, older than any word still living — drank the torchlight and gave none back. "That is wisdom. I have worn kings who hesitated. They lasted longest."

"I'm not hesitating," Sebastian said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. That was the thief's gift, the one true inheritance Dree had given her that could never be confiscated: the ability to keep your hands quiet while everything under them screams.

"I'm counting."

She had been counting since the moment the vial appeared. Counting what she held. That was the first law of extraction, taught on the first night of her apprenticeship with Dree's breath sour with wine against her ear: *you cannot lift what you have not weighed, girl, and you cannot weigh what you refuse to look at.*

So she looked.

She weighed the memories the parasite offered — the found family, the years before the missing ones, the warm rooms and the borrowed hours and the names of people who had loved her badly and well. And beneath them, recovered these last desperate weeks from vaults and ledgers and the vial she had drunk in the dark beneath the palace, she weighed the rest: the binding night, the knife in her fist, her own weeping, Dree's oath gone crooked in his mouth. Seven years of herself. Weeks of clawing it back, piece by piece, each fragment costing blood or coin or someone else's safety.

And then she weighed the word.

Because here was the truth she had been circling for days, the truth that had been sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone ever since Ives first drew the glyphs in candle-soot on the garret floor: Oathscript was not a language you learned. It was a language you *paid*. Every syllable cost the speaker something true. That was why bindings held — because the ones who swore them gave up pieces of themselves to make them real. That was why Dree's oath had gone crooked — he had sworn it holding something back, some small reserve of self he could not bear to surrender, and the parasite had lived in that reserve ever since, grown fat in it, made a kingdom of it.

And an unbinding —

An unbinding was a debt called due. To speak *no*, truly, absolutely, in the tongue that made things so, she could hold nothing back. Not a memory. Not a fear. Not one grain of hope. A speaker with nothing left to lose — Ives had signed it to her a hundred times, patient as water, and Sebastian had understood the words without understanding their weight.

Nothing left to lose meant nothing left at all.

"You're offering me a trade," Sebastian said to the parasite. "My past for my silence. My family for my obedience. That's your whole craft, isn't it? You don't take. You never take. You make people *give*." She looked out across the court, at all those smiling, unblinking faces. "How many of them handed you their lives and called it loyalty?"

The king's face flickered. For half a heartbeat the smile slipped, and underneath it was something vast and hungry and — this surprised her — *tired*. Ancient beyond sense, worn down to its appetite, a thing that had been eating for so long it had forgotten there was any other way to exist.

"They gave gladly," it said. "They always give gladly. That is the mercy of it."

"No," Sebastian said. "That's the theft of it. I should know. I've been a thief my whole life, and I know the difference between taking and trading, and I know the third thing too — the thing nobody talks about." She lifted Ives's hand higher, so the court could see it, so the parasite could see it. "Giving. When nobody owes you. When you get nothing back. When you empty yourself out on purpose and call it yours anyway."

The parasite's eyes narrowed. In Aldric's face it must once have looked kingly. Now it only looked cornered.

"You would be nothing," it said softly. "You would stand in this room tomorrow and know no one. Not the child. Not the inquisitor. Not the man who died buying you ten minutes. You would be a hollowed vessel with a thief's hands and no reason left to use them. Is that the trade you imagine you're making?"

"Yes," said Sebastian.

And then, because she was who she was, and because Dree had taught her one final lesson even in dying — *always leave the mark smiling* — she smiled.

"But you've made an accounting error."

* * *

Here is what the parasite could not see, because parasites live inside their host's hunger and mistake it for the world:

It thought she was choosing between keeping her memories and keeping Ives safe. It thought the sacrifice was the memories. It thought grief was the price and love was the coin.

But Sebastian had spent seven years with holes in her head, and she had learned the thing that only the robbed learn: that memory is not possession. Possession can be stolen; that was her trade, her proof, her whole miserable expertise. But the shape a person leaves in you — the way your hands remember a lock, the way your feet turn toward a door — that is not storage. That is authorship. You can burn every page of a book and the story stays told somewhere, in the way the ash falls, in the reader who walked away changed.

She could not keep her memories and speak the word. Fine. Then she would do what she had done her entire life, the only thing she had ever been good at, the thing they were going to hang her for someday if the parasite didn't get her first:

She would give them away first. On purpose. Freely.

Not lost. Given.

There was a difference. She was staking everything — the world, the child, the ancient starving thing on the throne — on the difference.

"Ives." She knelt, careless of the court watching, careless of Serane's sharp intake of breath, careless of everything except the girl's face, solemn and frightened and ferocious all at once. "Listen to me. After this, I won't know you. I need you to understand that going in, because you're the residue of an oath and you feel things the rest of us only trip over, and I will not have you standing there tomorrow thinking you failed her. You won't fail her. This *is* her. This moment is the most me I have ever been. Everything after is just... a new page."

Ives's eyes were bright and terrible. Her hands moved fast, almost angrily: *You will forget the door. You will forget the signs. You will forget—*

"The bread you burned," Sebastian agreed. "The night you pulled me out of the guildmaster's head. The way you hum when you think no one's listening, which is a lie, by the way, you hum constantly." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I'll forget all of it. But Ives — I'll have *given* it. There's a difference. Remember that part for both of us."

Then she reached into herself, into the place where the recovered memories lived now, all of them gathered home over these frantic weeks — and she began, deliberately, methodically, with the skill of twenty years' practice, to open every latch she owned.

* * *

It did not hurt the way she expected. It hurt worse, and quieter.

Extraction, done to you, feels like drowning — pressure, panic, the wild animal refusal. But giving, done by choice, done with care, felt like this: like standing in her garret on a winter morning and opening the window to let the birds go. Like unbarring the door of the Drowned Lantern and waving goodbye to the singing woman. Like setting each warm room alight, gently, tenderly, watching the smoke rise and thinking: *that was mine, and I loved it, and now it is weather.*

She gave up the stairwell song. She gave up the wishbone lockpick. She gave up fever and Quill's free coat and Dree's terrible laugh and the knife, oh God, the knife, the binding night, her own young face wet with tears in the reflection of the blade — she gave up the guilt too, gave it up clean, gave it up like a stone set down at last after seven years of carrying it, and discovered in the final instant before it went that forgiveness had been living inside it all along, folded small, waiting.

She gave up Ives. The burned bread. The humming. The small hot weight of the girl asleep against her shoulder on the flight from Hollow Eyes's corpse. The thieves' signs traded back and forth across a table like secrets. *Stay,* signed in a doorway. *Stay,* answered.

She gave up Serane — the ledger burning, the unlocked service door, treachery as conscience, the inquisitor's face when she chose. She gave up the plan for tomorrow, the regency council, the pardons, the future she had allowed herself, for one reckless week, to imagine.

She emptied herself the way you empty a house before a flood: carrying nothing, regretting nothing, saving nothing.

And when she stood again at the foot of the dais, she was light — terrifyingly light, transparent almost, a woman made of present tense. She could not have told you her mother's name. She could not have told you why church bells made her flinch. She could not have told you anything at all about Sebastian Winterbourne except what remained when everything removable was removed:

Her hands knew how to steal. Her heart knew how to choose.

That was enough. That was, it turned out, the whole of her.

*Now,* Ives signed, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, and raised both hands, and the air above the throne room began to fill with light — glyphs hanging in rows like a sky that had finally remembered its stars.

* * *

The parasite understood one second too late.

"Stop her—" it said, in Aldric's voice, in the courtiers' voices, in a hundred throats at once, and the vessels lurched forward — but Serane was already moving, ledger-trained and lethal, putting her body between Sebastian and the tide of smiling dead-eyed things, and her sword was out and her voice was ringing off the rafters with words Sebastian didn't recognize but the vessels clearly did, old words, ward-words, words an inquisitor was never supposed to use — *"In the name of the oath I am breaking, HALT"—*

Sebastian took Ives's hand.

And together they spoke.

She did not know the word. That was the miracle and the horror of it: the knowledge went with the memories, and yet her mouth opened and Oathscript came out anyway, syllable after syllable, because the language had never lived in her memory. It had lived in her *giving*. It had waited, all this time, in the empty space she had just made — the unbinding word required a speaker with nothing left to lose, and so the word itself came flooding into the vacancy like water into a lifted lock, filling her exactly to the brim, no more, no less.

Ives's voice joined hers — the girl's first true voice, the voice she'd been born carrying, the voice of the part of the oath that refused to hold — and where the two voices crossed, the air turned white.

The glyphs above them closed like a door closing. Or rather: like a door *opening*, all the way, onto the outside of the cage.

The crown screamed.

There was no other word for it. Black iron thorns split with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and light poured out of the cracks — not the amber hearth-light of stolen memory but something colder and cleaner, silver-white, the color of the moment before dawn. The parasite came out of the king the way smoke comes out of a house fire: reluctantly, hugely, a shape with too many angles to describe, wearing seven years of Aldric and seventy years of whoever came before him and centuries beneath that, all of it peeling away, all of it unraveling in the white light like thread from a cut hem.

It looked at Sebastian once, at the end. With what served it for eyes.

"*Given,*" it said, tasting the word, bewildered. "You gave it. No one gives."

"Now somebody has," Sebastian said — and her voice sounded strange to her, thin and new and unburdened, a stranger's voice, and she did not know why that sentence mattered, only that it did, only that saying it felt like setting down the last of the luggage. "*Remember that part for both of us.*"

The parasite came apart. Not violently — that was somehow worst of all. It simply unraveled, quietly, thoroughly, the way a bad oath unravels when the last condition of it fails, and the last condition had always been consent, freely given, and no one — not Dree, not the court, not the kingdom — had ever given it freely. Only she had. Only a thief would know that theft requires a taker, and she had declined to be taken from, and so the ancient hungry thing, older than kingdoms, starved at last of what it needed most — a willing host — dissolved into the white light and was gone.

King Aldric fell.

Serane caught him — dropped her sword, crossed the dais in three strides, caught the ancient body before it struck the iron throne. And up close, with the parasite gone, everyone could see what seven years of wearing does: the man was old. Old beyond his portraits, old beyond his reign — sixty years stolen from him, spent by something else, his hair white as frost, his hands translucent as paper. His eyes opened. They were his own. That was the unbearable thing. They had been his own all along, locked behind the smile, watching.

"Th-the bells," the king whispered. His voice was rust and dust. "I could never make them stop ringing. Did anyone else hear the bells—"

"Hush, Your Majesty," said Serane Corvane, inquisitor, traitor, the steadiest person in the room. "You're free. Sleep."

He slept. The court stood in the ruins of its enchantment, blinking — actually blinking, wetly, painfully, faces waking like limbs coming back from numbness — and Sebastian stood among them holding a child's hand, and she did not know the child's name.

She looked down. The girl was looking up at her, wrecked and radiant, and Sebastian saw her the way one sees a kind stranger: *someone important to me, clearly; someone I must have loved; someone whose face makes my chest hurt for reasons I can no longer name.*

"Did we win?" Sebastian asked. Her voice was hoarse. "I'm sorry — I don't — I seem to have lost..."

"You gave it away," the girl said. First common-tongue words, and she spent them on that. Her hands rose, trembling, and shaped two signs, slow, deliberate, pressing them into the air between them like sealing a letter: *stay.*

"I don't know what that means," Sebastian said honestly.

"It means stay," the girl said, and began, horribly, wonderfully, to cry. "It means please stay. It means I'll teach you again. It means — it means everything you gave away, I'm going to give back. Not the memories. Never the memories, you bought the world with those. But the *rest*. The new pages. Starting now."

Sebastian looked at her empty hands — thief's hands, skilled and steady, holding nothing, having given everything — and found, in the wreckage of all her missing history, one thing remaining, small and load-bearing and hers:

She wanted to stay.

"All right," she said to the crying stranger-child. "All right. Stay where, exactly? Because I have the strangest feeling I own a garret."

Behind them, Serane laughed — cracked, exhausted, alive — and outside, far across the city, dawn hit the cathedral bells, and they rang, and rang, and rang, and for the first time in seven years the sound was only bells.
