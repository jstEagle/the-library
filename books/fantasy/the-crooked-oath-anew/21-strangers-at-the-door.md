## 21. Strangers at the Door

Sebastian Winterbourne woke to the smell of rain on old slate and the particular silence of a room she did not recognize.

That was the first wrongness. The second was that her body knew the room anyway — knew that the third floorboard from the window creaked, knew the latch stuck unless you lifted before you turned, knew the exact height of the table where her hand found a cold cup of tea she had no memory of pouring. Her body had been living here for weeks. Her mind had been somewhere else entirely, and had come home to find the furniture rearranged by a stranger who was herself.

She sat up. Her joints ached in ways that told stories: a bruise blooming yellow-green along her ribs, a scab on her palm shaped like a crescent moon, a soreness in her throat as though she had screamed for a very long time, or sung, or spoken words too large for a human mouth. She pressed her fingers to her collarbone and felt, beneath the skin, a faint warmth — like a coal banked under ash — that faded when she touched it, the way a dream fades when you look at it directly.

The garret was small and clean. A bed. A stove with ash in it. A shelf holding three cups, one chipped. On the wall above the bed, someone had pinned a child's drawing: a crooked house, four stick figures standing in front of it, and above them a sun drawn not in yellow but in black ink, its rays rendered as careful, deliberate spirals.

Sebastian looked at that sun for a long time.

She could not remember drawing it. She could not remember pinning it up. But something in her chest turned over when she looked at it, slow and heavy, like a boat coming about — and she understood, with the flat certainty of grief, that she had lost something. Not misplaced it. Lost it, the way you lose a tooth: completely, permanently, with blood.

The last thing she remembered clearly was autumn. Chestnut sellers in Lantern Row. A job lifting a dying woman's happiest hour out of a locket. Church bells — she flinched even now, remembering the bells, though she could not have said why; the flinch arrived ahead of any reason, an echo of a wound whose cause had been sold or spent or taken. After autumn there was only fog, and then this morning, this room, this ache.

Seven years ago she had woken the same way. She remembered that waking too — the vertigo of it, the ledger of herself gone blank between one line and the next. She had learned to live inside the gap. She had made a trade of gaps, God help her; she stole other people's missing hours because her own had been stolen first, and there was a symmetry in that which she had once mistaken for comfort.

But this gap was new. This gap was *weeks* wide. And someone had been living in it with her.

The knock came just after dawn broke grey through the shutters.

Three soft raps. Then nothing. Then, after a pause long enough for politeness to die and hope to take its place, three more.

Sebastian took the knife from under the mattress — her hand went there without instruction, another thing her body knew that she did not — and crossed the floor on the boards that didn't creak. She lifted the latch, turned the handle, and opened the door onto the landing and the rain-smell and a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, thin as a winter branch, with dark hair cut unevenly as if by someone in a hurry and eyes the color of deep water over stone. She wore a coat too big for her, sleeves rolled and rolled again. In her hands she held nothing at all. She looked up at Sebastian with an expression that stopped the breath in Sebastian's chest — not the wariness of a stranger, not the pleading of a beggar, but something worse. Recognition. The look of someone coming home to a house that has burned down and finding the walls still standing anyway.

"Hello," Sebastian said carefully.

The girl's mouth opened. Closed. Her hands rose — quick, fluid, certain — and made a shape: two fingers walking across an open palm, then folding inward, then pressing flat against her own heart.

Sebastian did not know the sign. Had never learned thieves' cant signs beyond the market basics — *coin*, *watchman*, *run* — and this was none of those. And yet her hands answered.

It was the strangest sensation of her strange life: watching her own fingers rise without her permission, mirroring the girl's shapes back — the walking fingers, the fold, the press to the heart — smooth and practiced, a conversation her muscles had memorized while the rest of her slept. *Stay,* the shape said. She didn't know how she knew that. She knew it the way she knew the creaking floorboard. *Stay.*

The girl's face crumpled — not into tears, though tears came, silent ones, sliding down and down — but into relief so vast it looked like pain. She stepped forward and Sebastian stepped back, and the girl crossed the threshold into the garret as if crossing back into a room she'd left five minutes ago, setting her wet coat on the peg by the door, which was exactly the right height for a child's coat to hang from, which meant the peg had been put there for her.

"I don't—" Sebastian's voice cracked. She steadied it. "I don't know you."

The girl wiped her face with her sleeve. She tried to speak — Sebastian saw her try, saw her throat work around sounds that wouldn't come — and what emerged instead was a single word in a language Sebastian had never heard and yet felt in her teeth, in the banked coal beneath her collarbone, in the pulled-tooth ache behind her eyes:

"*Ives.*"

The girl pointed at herself. Said it again, softer. "*Ives.*"

Then she pointed at Sebastian, and her small hands moved once more, and this time Sebastian caught only the last shape — the press of knuckles together, held, like two people gripping wrists across a gap — before her own hands completed the rest without her.

Whatever they said, it made Ives smile through her tears. It made Sebastian want to sit down on the floor and weep for reasons she would never be permitted to know.

* * *

They managed, that first hour, the way people manage in ruins: badly, and then somehow.

Ives could not speak — or would not, or the speaking had been spent along with everything else; Sebastian recognized the economy of sacrifice when she saw it, having practiced it her whole life. But the girl could draw. She found the stub of charcoal by the cold stove and knelt at the floorboards and drew with swift, sure strokes: a tower. A crown with a crack running through it. Four figures — always four, always the same four, one tall and sharp-elbowed, one broad and stooped, one straight-backed with a sword-line of a mouth, one small — standing in a circle, holding hands.

And every time she drew the fourth figure, the smallest one, she looked up at Sebastian with that terrible patient hope, as if to say: *you knew me. You chose me. Find your way back.*

Sebastian knelt across from her, charcoal dust on her fingers, and felt the shape of the missing weeks pressing against the inside of her skull like faces against frosted glass. There were moments — fragments, really, less than fragments, more like the heat left where a hand has rested — when something almost surfaced. The smell of tallow and old paper. A man's voice saying *mentors are just debts with faces*. The sensation of running down palace stairs with a child's hand locked in hers. A door, unlocked from the other side, by someone who should not have opened it.

Each fragment brought the ache. Each ache passed. She was learning, already, the rhythm of this new grief: it came in waves, and you breathed through them, and between waves you put water on to boil because a child was shivering and the body knows what to do even when the heart has been robbed.

She was warming milk on the stove when the second knock came.

This one was different. Confident. Two raps and a pause and one more, the knock of someone who had stood on this landing many times and expected the door to open.

Ives went rigid. Her charcoal hand froze mid-stroke. She looked at Sebastian and made a rapid series of signs — too fast, urgent — and though Sebastian couldn't parse them all, the tone needed no translation. *Careful. Careful. Careful.*

Sebastian picked up the knife again and opened the door.

A woman stood on the landing, tall, straight-backed, with dark hair going silver at the temples and a sword-line of a mouth that Sebastian had seen somewhere — in a fragment? in a fever? — and could not place. She wore a plain wool cloak over what had once, by the set of her shoulders and the way she stood, been a uniform. Under one arm she carried a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth, still steaming faintly against the cold.

"Winterbourne," the woman said. Not a question. Her eyes went past Sebastian's shoulder to Ives, and something in her face loosened — briefly, painfully, like ice cracking — before it set again. "You're awake, then. Good. That's— good."

"Who are you?" Sebastian asked.

The woman's jaw tightened. She had prepared for this; Sebastian could see the preparation, see the sentence rehearsed on the stairs, and she watched the woman choose to say it anyway. "Serane. Serane Corvane." A pause measured to the width of a lie. "We're old friends."

"We're not," Sebastian said quietly. "I'd remember."

"You'd be surprised," Serane said, "what you don't remember," and there was such bitterness in it, such specific and personal bitterness, that Sebastian's skin prickled. The woman thrust the bread at her. "From Marrow Lane. The good baker, not the one who stretches his flour with chalk. You used to complain about it. Constantly."

"I don't remember complaining about it."

"No," Serane said. "You wouldn't." She looked at the loaf in Sebastian's hands, at the steam rising from it, and for a moment her composure slipped sideways and Sebastian saw underneath: exhaustion, guilt, and grief worn smooth by repetition, the look of someone who had carried a heavy thing a long way and intended to keep carrying it without complaint. "Eat it while it's warm. Both of you. I'll— I can come tomorrow. If that's wanted."

"Why?" Sebastian asked. "Why bread? Why tomorrow? Why any of this? What happened to me?"

Serane looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Behind Sebastian, Ives had come to stand close — not touching, but near, near enough that Sebastian could feel the small warmth of her, and noticed that she had positioned herself slightly in front of the child without deciding to, the way her hand had known the floorboards, the way her fingers had known the signs.

"That," Serane said finally, "is a longer story than either of us can afford today. Ask me again when you've eaten. Ask me every day, if you like. I'll keep answering until the answers fit." She stepped back toward the stairs, then paused, one hand on the rail, and said — not looking at Sebastian, looking at the middle distance, at some private horizon — "For what it's worth: whatever you paid, it was worth it. Whatever it cost you — it bought something that mattered. Don't let the empty hours tell you otherwise. Empty hours lie. They're the worst gossips in Vellharrow."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, the outer door below opening and shutting against the rain.

* * *

The bread was still warm. They ate it sitting on the floor, cross-legged, the loaf between them, tearing pieces in a silence that slowly stopped being awkward and became something else — companionable, almost, the silence of people who have done this before and are doing it again without needing to discuss it.

Ives ate the way children eat when they've known hunger: thoroughly, without waste, guarding the heel of the loaf with her elbow until Sebastian pushed it toward her and signed — her hands moving again without consultation, fluent in a language she could not name — *yours, eat, stay.*

At *stay*, the girl's whole body eased, like a knot untying.

Afterward, Ives retrieved her charcoal and drew one more picture. She worked slowly now, deliberately, tongue between her teeth. When she finished she turned it around and held it up.

It was the four figures again. But this time each one was labeled in a careful, childish hand — except the labels weren't names. They were single words, common-tongue, spelled with the charming imperfection of someone who had only recently learned to write it:

The tall sharp-elbowed figure: *THIEF.*

The broad stooped one: *LIAR (GOOD).*

The straight-backed one: *BROKEN OATH (KEPT).*

And the smallest figure, the one with the uneven hair and the deep-water eyes, labeled simply: *YOURS.*

Sebastian stared at it until her vision blurred.

"I don't remember you," she said, and her voice came out wrecked and small. "I don't remember any of it. Whatever I was to you — whatever you were to me — it's gone. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I would give anything—"

Ives shook her head. She set down the charcoal, took Sebastian's hand — her grip surprisingly strong, her palm warm — and pressed it flat against her own small chest, over her heart. Then she moved Sebastian's hand and pressed it, gently, against Sebastian's own.

*I know,* the gesture said. Or perhaps: *it's in there. All of it. It's in there.*

And beneath Sebastian's collarbone, the banked coal stirred — one pulse, deep and unmistakable, like a second heartbeat answering the first — and faded again.

Outside, the rain thinned. Somewhere across the rooftops, distantly, the cathedral bells began to ring the hour, and Sebastian braced for the flinch —

— and it didn't come.

Instead, something else: a memory-heat, brief as a struck match. A bell tower. A rope in her hands. A child on her shoulders, ringing the noon bell wildly, wrongly, gloriously, both of them laughing as the whole city looked up to wonder what mad thing had gotten into the bells of Saint Verity's — and a tall woman in the square below, arms crossed, trying very hard not to smile and failing.

Then it was gone. Just heat. Just the shape of where a memory had been, the way snow keeps the shape of the foot that stepped in it.

But Ives was watching her face, and Ives saw it land — saw whatever moved through Sebastian's expression — and the girl smiled. Not the terrible patient hope from the doorway. Something lighter. Something that said: *there. See? One came back. They do that, sometimes. They come back.*

Sebastian looked at the drawing on the floor. At the four figures holding hands. At the word *YOURS* in a child's careful hand.

She thought about the woman called Serane, who would come tomorrow with more bread and more answers measured out like medicine. She thought about the broad stooped liar and the broken oath kept, whoever they were, and whether they were alive, and whether they would also come knocking. She thought about the crack in the crown Ives kept drawing, and the black spiral sun, and the coal beneath her collarbone that pulsed when the girl said her name.

She thought: *whatever I paid, it was worth it. Empty hours lie.*

"All right," she said aloud, to the child, to the room, to the stranger she had apparently been. "Tell me everything. Start wherever you like. We have time."

Ives picked up the charcoal.

And on the floorboards of the garret, in strokes growing surer by the day, she began at the beginning: a tower, a crown, a door left unlocked from the other side — and, at the bottom of the page, small and deliberate, the first glyph of a language older than any kingdom, drawn not as warning this time, but as invitation.
