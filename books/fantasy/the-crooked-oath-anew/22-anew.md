## 22. Anew

The bells of Vellharrow rang for the regency council on a morning so clear the whole city seemed rinsed in it, and Sebastian Winterbourne stood at her window and did not flinch.

She noticed the not-flinching the way one notices a missing tooth — by the strange smoothness where the ache used to be. Quill had told her, weeks ago now, that she used to flinch at church bells. That she would go still as a snared rabbit whenever they began, her hand drifting to her collarbone as if checking whether something had been cut away there. Quill had told her a great many things since, most of them in the low, careful voice of a man describing weather to someone who has never felt rain: *You had an apprentice once, of sorts. You had friends. You had a life with people in it, girl, and you paid it all away for reasons I'm not going to pretend I understand.*

She had not flinched at the bells today. She wondered if that meant she was healing, or only that the wound had finished forgetting itself.

Below, the Street of Nine Lanterns was hung with bunting that no one had officially sanctioned and no one had taken down. The proclamation had gone up on the palace gates eleven days after the coronation renewal rite failed to renew anything: *In the absence of His Majesty King Aldric, governance shall pass to a Regency Council until such time as the succession is resolved.* Beneath it, in smaller letters that had clearly been added by a different and more embarrassed hand: *A general pardon is extended to certain irregular trades, per council discretion.* The word *memory* appeared nowhere. It didn't need to. Everyone in Vellharrow knew which thieves were being forgiven, and everyone knew better than to say so out loud, because saying things out loud was how this city had gotten into trouble in the first place.

Sebastian pulled her coat from its hook — the same coat, though she'd found coins in its lining she had no memory of hiding, and a scrap of paper folded into eight squares that said, in her own handwriting, *you chose this, don't let anyone tell you different* — and went down into the morning.

* * *

The night market still ran, but it ran differently now. There was a lightness to it, a loosening at the shoulders. The mnemonic inquisitors still walked their rounds in grey coats, but Serane Corvane had burned seven years of ledgers in a brazier outside the Chapterhouse and let the ash go wherever the wind pleased, and without ledgers, inquisitors were just people in expensive coats who had suddenly become very interested in paperwork reform.

Sebastian kept her stall anyway. Not to sell — she hadn't lifted a memory in two months, and the not-doing of it sat strangely in her hands, like a tool she'd forgotten how to put down. She kept the stall because it was hers, because the awning still bore the faded sigil she'd painted years ago and couldn't remember choosing, and because people came to it now for other things.

An old lamplighter came on Thursdays to trade stories he'd rather keep than sell — memories he wanted witnessed, spoken aloud to someone who would hold them for an hour and then give them back. A widow brought her husband's last hour every fortnight, not to be rid of it but to sit with Sebastian while it played behind her eyes, so that someone else in the world would know what his laugh had sounded like. Word had spread through the lantern-lit lanes of a memory-thief who had stopped stealing and started keeping, and the desperate and the grieving came to her the way they'd once come to priests, back when the city still trusted churches.

"You're doing it again," said the widow, whose name was Marta and who brought honey cakes every visit as if payment were still required. "That look. Like you're trying to remember something on purpose."

"I do that," Sebastian admitted.

"It doesn't work, does it."

"No." She smiled, and it was mostly true. "It's like reaching for a stair that isn't there. My foot knows the shape of it."

Marta pressed the honey cakes across the counter and covered Sebastian's hand with her own, brief and warm. "Whatever you lost," she said, "you must have loved it very much. It shows. People who've lost nothing hold their hands differently."

After she'd gone, Sebastian sat a long while under her awning, turning that over. Her hands. She looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger who had done remarkable things and neglected to leave notes. The right palm carried a scar she couldn't account for — thin, diagonal, healed long ago. Sometimes, when she turned her hand in certain light, the scar seemed almost like a letter. Almost like script.

Almost like a word she'd known once, and given away.

* * *

Ives came on the last day of the month, the way she always came, which was to say: without warning, through the door that Sebastian had stopped locking.

The child had grown. Or perhaps not grown — changed, the way weather changes, imperceptibly day to day and then suddenly undeniable. She was taller. Her silence had deepened rather than broken; she spoke common-tongue rarely, in short careful sentences like someone handling borrowed glass, but her hands were fluent now, quick and certain in the thieves' signs Sebastian had taught her in another life. *Stay*, she could sign. *Home*. *Enough*. *Again*. Words Sebastian had no memory of teaching her, and yet the girl signed them with the particular inflections Sebastian herself used, the little flourishes, the shorthand — proof written in motion that some part of their history had survived inside Ives even when it hadn't survived in Sebastian.

Today Ives signed: *Bread. Serane. Coming.*

And then, because Serane Corvane was already on the stairs, signing over her shoulder with the exaggerated patience of a woman who had learned the language in six weeks and resented every hour of it: *She says that about me. Rude child.*

Serane filled the garret doorway the way she filled every doorway — grey coat, hollow-eyed composure, arms full of bread and a jar of pickled something-or-other, because the former Inquisitor Corvane had apparently decided that the correct way to defect was to show up weekly with food and refuse to discuss it. She had resigned formally, or been dismissed informally; the story changed depending on how much wine was involved. What remained constant was that she came every week, and every week she told Sebastian a lie.

"Old friend," she said now, setting the bread down, in the tone of a woman reciting lines she'd rehearsed and hated. "We've known each other ages. You helped me once with a matter of... records."

"I know," Sebastian said gently. "You tell me every time."

"And does it work?"

"No. But I appreciate the craftsmanship. You've gotten better at the pause before 'records.'"

Serane's mouth twitched — the closest thing to a smile the woman permitted herself, though it came easier these days than it once had. She unpacked the bread. Ives climbed onto the stool by the window, her stool, the one nobody had discussed assigning to her, and began methodically slicing with the seriousness of a surgeon.

This was the ritual of it. This was the strange liturgy they had built: Serane arriving with food and fiction, Ives arriving with silence and signs, and Sebastian in the middle, holding a family she could not remember owning, being held by it anyway.

They grieved her loss alongside her. That was the thing Quill had tried to explain once, drunk on plum brandy, weeping freely and embarrassed about neither. *You think we come here for your sake, girl? We come because we lost you too. You're sitting right there and we lost you, and the only way any of us can stand it is to keep showing up until some part of you grows back around the shape of us.* He'd cried harder then and demanded more brandy, and Sebastian had sat with him in the wreckage of a friendship she had no access to, and found — this was the unbearable part, the part she turned over at night like a stone with something warm underneath — that she believed him. That she felt the loss too, vast and sourceless, grief with no photograph, mourning without a grave. She missed people she couldn't name. She woke some mornings with her arm curled around empty air, shaped for holding a small sleeping weight, and lay there aching toward a memory that had been sold out from under her.

She had chosen it. The note in her pocket said so. *You chose this.* And on the good days she could feel the truth of it like a bone set straight — that whatever she had bought with those memories had been worth the price, that the woman who made that bargain had been brave, that bravery was inheritable even when the estate itself was gone.

On the bad days she just missed them, whoever they were, whoever she had been.

* * *

The king lived.

That was the strangest thread in the new weave of the city, stranger even than the pardons. Aldric — freed, ancient, emptied of seven stolen years — had not died when the crown cracked. He had woken, they said, in the undercroft of his own palace, an old man with white hair and hands that shook, weeping for a wife who had been dead a decade and asking, over and over, what year it was. The regency council ruled in his name while he sat in a sunny room in the eastern tower learning, slowly, to be a person again. Petitioners were sometimes brought to him. He greeted them kindly, blinked often, and remembered none of them afterward, and the city found this so familiar a condition in a ruler that some wits said he'd finally become honest.

Sebastian had visited him once. She wasn't sure why — some pull in the scar-palm, some instinct that the visit mattered. They had sat together by the window, two amnesiacs of different magnitudes, and he had studied her face for a long time with wet, wondering eyes.

"I know you," he'd said finally. "Not your name. Not how. But there's a — a debt-shape. When I look at you, I feel paid."

"I feel the same," she'd lied, or half-lied, because it was true in the way dreams are true. "Your Majesty."

"Aldric," he'd corrected. "The crown and I have come to an arrangement. It doesn't wear me anymore, and I don't wear it." He'd smiled then, and it had been — for the first time in seven years, according to everyone who'd seen both versions — a smile that reached all the way up. "Whoever you were," he said, taking her scarred hand in both of his trembling ones, "thank you. Whatever it cost. Thank you."

She had walked home through the palace gardens because no one stopped her anymore, past beds where confiscated magic had once been catalogued like contraband seed, and she had stood a long time in the place where the Vault of Given Things had stood before the council ordered it unsealed and its contents returned, vial by vial, to whoever could be found to claim them. Somewhere in those returned thousands there had been a shelf, and on the shelf a space, and in the space nothing — because her memories had not been returned. They had been spent. Spoken. Given entirely, the way you give a breath, the way you give a life, the way you give a word.

She pressed her palm flat against the warm stone and thought: *whoever I was, I hope she knew what she was doing.*

Then she walked home, because home was where the bread was.

* * *

Autumn deepened. The regency council proved, to universal astonishment, competent — three magistrates and a merchant and a stonemason's widow who terrified the other four into honesty. Magic remained outlawed; some laws outlive the reasons for them, and Vellharrow was not ready to unclench that particular fist. But the quiet trades breathed easier. Quill reopened his fence-shop openly, hung a sign reading *CURIOSITIES & APPRAISALS* with a wink implicit in the ampersand, and took to telling anyone who'd listen that he'd known Sebastian Winterbourne back when she was nobody, which was a lie, since by his own account she'd been somebody considerable, but Quill's lies had always been a form of loyalty.

And Sebastian stole nothing, and kept everything.

She learned her own house. That was the work of it, the daily labor no ballad would ever mention: discovering which floorboard sang, which mug was hers by the wear of her own grip, which drawer held needles sized for charmwork she no longer performed but might someday need. She found a second chair tucked in a corner, worn at the arms, positioned — she measured it one idle evening — exactly where a small person sitting beside her could see out the window. She found chalk marks inside the pantry door, glyphs in a flowing angular script that made her scar ache sweetly, like a song heard through a wall. She copied them into a notebook without understanding them, and sometimes at night she traced them by candlelight and felt, unmistakably, accompanied.

Ives stayed. Nobody asked and nobody answered; the question had dissolved somewhere along the way, the way questions do when the answer keeps eating your bread and sleeping in the second chair's shadow and standing at your shoulder at the market stall, learning the trade of witnessing. Because that was Ives's inheritance now, Sebastian decided — not Oathscript, not the terrible old words, but the newer craft, the gentler one: how to hold a stranger's happiest hour without dropping it. How to keep what isn't yours. How to give it back undiminished.

Some evenings they sat together on the garret steps and watched the lanterns come alight down the crooked streets, and Ives would sign little things — *cold*, *hungry*, *look, that cat again* — and Sebastian would answer, and between them they built a conversation the way masons build a wall, one stone at a time, without any plan beyond the next stone.

One such evening, late in the season, Ives went still mid-sign. Then, slowly, deliberately, she raised her hands and gave Sebastian a sign she had never taught and had never seen: both palms open, pressed together, then parted — like a book opening, like a door, like a locket.

*What's that one?* Sebastian signed.

Ives considered her for a long moment. Whatever lived behind the child's eyes — residue, remnant, the piece of an oath that refused to hold, given flesh — it looked out at her with an expression older than kingdoms and younger than bread.

*I remember you,* Ives signed. *Even if you don't. Both are real. Both count.*

Then she leaned her head against Sebastian's shoulder, small and warm and impossibly heavy, the way children are heavy with more than weight, and Sebastian put her arm around her and looked out at the city — the crooked, outlawed, pardoned, breathing city — and understood, at last, in the place below memory where understanding lives:

She had thought she gave everything away. But you cannot give away what has already given itself to you. The memories were gone, yes — the binding night, the knife, the found family, the years. Gone like breath, like coin spent, like a word spoken truly. And what remained was this: a chair worn by a child, chalk glyphs behind a pantry door, a scar like a letter, a woman who arrived weekly with bread and beautiful lies, a fence who wept over plum brandy, a king learning to blink.

What we choose twice is truly ours. She had chosen them once, whoever she had been, and paid everything for the choice. Now she got to choose again — blind, beginning, free — and this time she would get to keep it. This time the choosing was the having.

"Stay," she said aloud, to the weight against her shoulder, to the darkening street, to the year turning over like a page. "Both of you. All of you. Stay."

Ives signed it back against her ribs, lazy and certain, half-asleep: *stay, stay, stay.*

And far off across the rooftops, the church bells began to ring the hour, and Sebastian Winterbourne — thief of nothing, keeper of everything, author at last of her own unwritten days — did not flinch.

She only listened, and smiled, and went inside to light the lamps.
