## 1. The Charm Seller's Morning

The bell above the door of Blackwood Charms had not rung in nine days, and Yara Blackwood had spent most of them pretending that was a blessing.

Quiet was the currency of Vell. Quiet kept you fed, kept your name off lists, kept your mother's fate from repeating itself in your own skin. Yara had learned to love quiet the way a drowning man learns to love the surface — not because it was beautiful, but because it was what stood between him and everything below.

He swept the shop floor at dawn, as he did every morning, though there was nothing left to sweep. Dust motes turned in the gray light leaking through shutters painted with protective sigils that were, of course, only paint. The Order of the Iron Seal permitted charms of *craft* — knots for sore joints, sachets for moths, braided cords said to steady a trembling hand. What they hanged you for were charms of *power*, and so Yara sold the first kind with his whole heart and kept the second kind folded small behind his ribs.

The cat had been on his windowsill since before sunrise.

It was a lean gray thing with one ear notched like a torn page, and it sat watching him through the gap in the shutters with eyes the color of old brass. It had been sitting there three mornings now. Most strays grew bored of Yara Blackwood within an hour; animals always did, once they realized he would not answer them.

*I know what you are,* the cat said. Its voice arrived inside his skull the way rain arrives inside a house with a bad roof — through every crack at once, impossible to stop up. *You pretend very well. But pretense has a smell.*

Yara set down the broom and did not look at the window. Looking was the first sin. Looking was how his mother had died, she who had once knelt in a market square and spoken aloud to a sparrow with a broken wing while half of Copperlane watched, and the Iron Seal had watched too, and that had been the end of Sera Blackwood in all but name.

*Ignore me,* said the cat, washing one paw with insulting calm. *Everyone does.*

"I'm not talking to you," Yara murmured to the empty shop, which was technically true and therefore safe. "I'm talking to myself. Charm-sellers do that. It's a known affliction."

*Then talk to yourself about why your hands shake when I speak.*

His hands were not shaking. His hands were steady as millstones, because he had trained them to be, because steadiness was the whole architecture of his life. He hung a fresh cord of knot-charms by the door — joint-ease, sleep-ease, grief-ease, all of them honest work and none of them magic — and told himself the morning would be ordinary.

For two hours, it almost was.

* * *

She came in just past the ninth bell, when the fog off the Vellwater had burned down to a thin gray gauze over the rooftops. Yara heard her before he saw her: the particular hesitation of someone standing outside a shop they're ashamed to need. A long pause. A breath taken the way you take one before stepping into cold water. Then the bell rang.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed in widow's black gone shiny at the elbows, with the raw-knuckled hands of a woman who had washed other people's linens her whole life and now washed no one's at all. Her eyes moved over the shelves the way a starving woman's move over a baker's window — wanting, distrusting, wanting anyway.

"You're the charm man," she said. Not a question. Accusation, almost.

"Charms of craft only," Yara said, with the smoothness of long practice. "Knots and herbs and good thread. Nothing the Seal would frown at. Can I help you, mistress?"

Her name, it emerged, was Odile Ferran, and her husband had been dead four months, drowned in the Vellwater when his fisher's skiff went over in the spring surge, and everything since then had gone wrong in a manner she could only describe as *wrong*. The net tore. The rent rose. Her son's cough wouldn't clear. And last week, she said, lowering her voice as though the walls might report her, she had dreamed of her husband three nights running, and in the dreams he was trying to tell her something, and she couldn't hear it.

"They say luck turns," she said. "They say a body can carry ill-fortune like a stone in the pocket and never know it fell in. I want it out. Whatever it costs."

"Costs," Yara repeated gently, "are negotiable. Sit, please. There's tea."

There was no tea. He put the kettle on anyway, because the ritual mattered more than the drink, and while it heated he studied her the way he studied all his customers: not their words but their weight. Grief has a gravity. Fear does too, and under Odile Ferran's grief ran a bright wire of fear that had nothing to do with drowned husbands or rising rents.

"You've seen something," he said quietly. "Haven't you. Something you can't explain."

Her hands closed around each other like a trap snapping shut. "No."

"Mistress Ferran—"

"At the dead shrine." The words came out of her as if pulled. "On Tanner's Walk. The little one, the cracked one, the one the Order sealed seven years back. I go there because it's quiet, because no one goes, and I talk to him. To my Aldous. Just talking, mind, just a widow talking to air." Her voice thinned. "Yesterday I asked him why he left me. Just that. Why. And the stone—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "The stone was warm. Under my hand. Warm as a living thing, warm as a cheek, and this morning there's a crack in it that wasn't there before, and out of the crack comes light, faint, like a candle behind a curtain, and I don't know what it means and I'm afraid to ask anyone because asking is how people disappear."

The kettle began to sing. Yara did not move toward it.

Every instinct he owned was screaming at him in his mother's voice: *Don't ask. Don't look. Sell the woman a knot-charm and forget her.* Seven years he had built his life on those instructions, brick by silent brick, and they had held through hunger and fear and the long gray loneliness of being the only creature in Vell who could hear what the pigeons thought of the bread crumbs and what the rats thought of everything.

But Odile Ferran was looking at him with the face of a drowning woman, and Yara Blackwood had never in his life managed to walk past one of those.

"The shrine on Tanner's Walk," he said. "Dedicated to whom?"

"No one remembers. That's the thing — no one remembers any of them anymore. They scraped the names off when the Seal came." She laughed, a dry broken sound. "Praying to a god with no name. Like posting a letter with no address. And yet."

"And yet it answered."

"*Something* answered." She leaned forward. In the flat morning light he saw how much weight she'd lost, how the black dress hung on her like a flag on a surrendered ship. "Charm-man. You sell luck. So tell me true: is my luck turning, or is something turning *me*?"

Outside the window, the gray cat stood up on the sill, stretched, and pressed its notched face against the shutter-gap. Brass eyes found Yara's across the room with unerring aim.

*Tell her,* said the cat. *You know what it is. You've felt it for a week, in the teeth of every rat in this city. Something is waking up, little liar, and you can smell it as well as I.*

"Shut *up*," Yara said — aloud, before he could stop himself, and then covered it with a cough that fooled neither of them.

Odile blinked. "Beg pardon?"

"A draft," Yara said. "This shop breathes like a lung." He crossed to the shelf where the luck-charms lived — the legal ones, oak apples and iron nails and river-stones drilled and threaded on red cord, each one sold with the same careful disclaimer he'd given ten thousand times: *a charm focuses hope, it doesn't command fortune.* He chose the best of them without looking, the way his mother had taught him, feeling for the one whose grain sat right in the palm.

Then, because Odile Ferran deserved better than a lie wrapped around a lie, he did something he had done only twice in seven years.

He listened.

Not to her. To the city. He let the shutters of his gift crack open — just a finger's width, just enough — and the world poured in the way it always did, the vast chattering dark beneath Vell's silence: sparrows quarreling over eaves-rights, the cart-horse on Copperlane muttering its endless grievance against cobblestones, dogs dreaming, mice scheming, and underneath it all the rats, the rats who knew every secret this city buried, going about their gray business with the grim competence of tax collectors.

And threaded through all of it, faint as the light behind the curtain-crack Odile had described, a note he had never heard before. Low. Patient. Coming up from below, from everywhere below, like heat from deep earth. The animals felt it. The pigeons wouldn't roost near the temple district. The rats had abandoned the catacomb warrens in the old quarter entirely — he'd noticed that days ago and told himself it was flooding, or poison, or anything at all besides what it was.

*Warm stones,* the rats had grumbled among themselves, in their flat gray way. *All the stones getting warm. Wrong season for warm stones.*

Yara closed the shutters of himself, hard, and his hands were shaking now after all.

"Mistress Ferran." He set the luck-charm in her palm and folded her fingers over it. Her hands were cold as the river that took her husband. "I'll tell you true, as you asked. Luck isn't a wheel. It's weather. Sometimes it sours for no reason anyone can name, and sometimes—" he chose each word like a man crossing rotten boards "—sometimes the ground itself shifts under a town, and everything built on it trembles. Your husband didn't curse you. Your luck hasn't soured. Something older than luck is stirring, and the charm will keep *you* steady while it passes. Steady is what matters. Steady, and quiet, and not asking questions in places where questions can be overheard."

"Is that a charm-man's wisdom or a warning?"

"It's both. We're a cheap trade; we bundle."

Almost, she smiled. Then her face crumpled and she pressed the charm to her lips like something holy, and Yara looked away to give her privacy with her grief, and hated himself for the warmth in his chest, because it wasn't kindness he was feeling. It was relief. Someone else knew. Someone else had touched a warm stone and seen light behind a curtain and been afraid, and for one merciful moment Yara Blackwood was not alone with the thing scratching at the underside of his quiet.

She paid him in coppers counted out one by one, and he charged her half, and lied that the discount was seasonal. At the door she paused, black dress and raw hands and terrible grateful eyes.

"You're a kind man, charm-seller."

"No," Yara said honestly. "Just a careful one. Go home the long way. Past no shrines."

The bell rang her out into the gray morning, and the shop was quiet again, and the quiet had never felt less like a blessing.

* * *

He meant to close early. He got as far as sweeping — again, pointlessly, again — before the cat came in through the gap in the shutters as if doors were opinions it had declined to hold.

It landed on the counter between the knot-charms and the tea tin and sat, tail wrapped, regarding him with the patience of something far older than its lean gray body suggested.

"You're not staying," Yara told it. "I don't feed strays. Feeding strays is how you end up responsible for strays."

*You've been responsible for me for three days,* the cat observed. *Since the night you pulled the wire out of my leg and never once spoke to me while you did it. Do you know how insulting that is? I thanked you. Twice. You hummed.*

"I hum because humming keeps neighbors from wondering why a charm-seller is kneeling in an alley holding a bleeding cat."

*I know why you hum.* The cat stepped closer, delicately, across the counter, and its brass eyes held his with a weight no animal's gaze should carry. *You hum because your mother sang to the birds, and when they took her, you swore you'd never open your mouth to anything with fur or feathers again. A crooked oath. Half-sworn, badly kept, patched with humming. I have watched many speakers, Yara Blackwood. You are the loneliest.*

The broom handle creaked in his grip. "Get out of my shop."

*The stones are waking,* the cat said, ignoring him, and its voice had changed — lower now, layered, the way thunder sounds layered when it's still distant. *The godless stones, the scraped-blind stones, all across this city. One opened its eye yesterday for a washerwoman on Tanner's Walk. Tomorrow it will be two. Then ten. Then the ones underneath, the old ones, the hungry ones. And when they wake fully, they will look for a voice. For* the *voice. There is one beast-speaker left in Vell, and he sells knot-charms and hums at cats, and he cannot hide forever behind a broom.*

Yara's mouth had gone dry. "What are you?"

The cat looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the fog was burning off, and somewhere across the rooftops the Order's noon bells began to toll — nine strokes, then a tenth, then an eleventh, though the noon bells had only ever rung nine, all the years Yara had lived in Vell, and he stood listening to the extra strokes with the cold spreading through him like water through cloth.

Down in the street, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then every dog in Copperlane at once, a ragged chorus rising toward the gray sky, and the pigeons went up off the eaves in a single clattering sheet, and from the walls behind his own shop came the sound Yara had heard only once before in his life, the day his mother died: rats, thousands of them, all squeaking the same word at the same time.

*Wake,* said the rats. *Wake, wake, wake.*

The cat smiled — there was no other word for the slow parting of its whiskers — and stepped off the counter, and padded toward the door, pausing there to glance back at him with eyes like banked fire.

*Come find out,* it said, and slipped into the morning.
