## 2. Prayer Answered

The dead shrine at the corner of Tallow Street and the Vellwater Steps had been dying longer than Yara had been alive. Its stone god — some forgotten river-lord with a crown of carved reeds and hands cupped to hold water that no longer flowed — had been worn faceless by two centuries of rain. The Order of the Iron Seal had chiseled the sigil from its brow three generations ago, back when they still bothered with small gods, and now the statue stood in its mossy alcove like a beggar too proud to leave.

Yara passed it every morning on his way to the market, and every morning he nodded to it out of habit rather than respect. A charm-seller who ignored shrines drew less attention than one who prayed at them.

This morning he was late. The widow Merta had kept him past dawn with her grief and her coin, pressing a silver bit into his palm for a luck-charm she could not afford while a cat screamed in his skull about cold and hunger and *the tall one owes me fish*. He'd fed the stray on his own breakfast because silence was cheaper than argument, and now the morning bells of the Iron Basilica were already ringing their second hour as he came down the Vellwater Steps with his satchel of charms bumping against his hip.

The fog off the canal was thick enough to drink. It muffled footsteps and faces alike, which was why Yara noticed the beggar at all — the man was kneeling at the dead shrine's feet, and no one knelt anymore.

He was old, or made old by weather; his coat had been fine once, before moths and winters got at it. His hands were pressed together in the way children pray when no one has taught them how, fingers laced crooked, knuckles white. And he was speaking. Not muttering — Yara heard muttering all day, in queues and doorways, the low grumble of people talking themselves through misery. This was different. This had shape.

"—I know You are not there. I know what they taught us. But she is burning up, Master of Reeds, my girl is burning and the physickers want six marks for the fever-tincture and I have—" The voice cracked like river ice. "I have nothing. I have nothing but this."

Yara slowed. He told himself it was professional interest. Charm-sellers lived off desperate men, and there was always the chance of a sale — a luck-charm, a ward against ill fortune, anything to put in the beggar's hands instead of hope.

But the words coming out of the old man weren't for sale. They came from somewhere below the ribs, from the place where people keep the things they cannot afford to say aloud in a city like Vell.

"I know You are stone," the beggar whispered. "I know You are dead. But if there is anything left of You — anything at all — she is seven years old and her name is Ilse and I will give You whatever a dead god wants. Please. *Please.*"

The fog went very still.

Yara felt it before he understood it — a pressure change, like the moment before a summer storm, like the hush when every bird leaves a field at once. Every animal within earshot of the shrine had stopped making noise. That alone should have sent him running. When pigeons go silent, something is hunting. When rats go silent, something is worse than hunting.

The stone god opened one eye.

Not both. One — the left, a seam splitting the weathered granite of the faceless face, and behind the seam was not stone but light, a deep amber glow like lamplight seen through honey, ancient and patient and *awake*.

Yara's breath left him in a thin white thread. His body knew what his mind refused: *run*. But his feet had gone root-stupid, and the gift his mother had died for — the gift he had sworn on her grave never to use again — flared open without his permission, the way an old wound opens in cold weather. He heard the city. Not the way men hear it. The other way.

*Warm,* said something vast and slow beneath the street, a voice like groundwater moving through limestone. *Warm hands. Warm grief. Seven years.*

*Seven years,* echoed the beggar's prayer, and Yara realized with a lurch that the god was answering — not in thunder, not in fire, but in the only language a starving thing remembers: by taking what was offered.

Light moved. It came up out of the statue's cupped hands like water finding its level, spilled down the mossy steps of the alcove, and pooled around the beggar's knees. The old man made a sound Yara had never heard from a human throat — half sob, half hymn — and did not pull away. The light climbed him gently, wrapped his clasped hands, sank into his chest, and was gone.

For a heartbeat the world held its breath.

Then, from somewhere across the district — from beyond the tanneries, past the Shambles, from the direction of the tenement rows where coal smoke sat permanent as weather — a child began to cry. Not the crying of fever. The crying of a child waking from a nightmare to find her father gone and her forehead cool.

The beggar staggered upright. "*Ilse—*" and then he was running, coat tails flying, down the Vellwater Steps and into the fog, weeping openly, loudly, without shame, and no one stopped him because no one had seen what Yara had seen.

The eye closed. The seam sealed. The stone god stood faceless again in its alcove, wearing its crown of reeds, holding water that would never flow, and looked exactly as dead as it had for two hundred years.

Yara's legs finally obeyed him. He walked — fast, controlled, the walk of a man with honest business — until the shrine was out of sight, and then he ran.

* * *

He took the long way home, the way you do when you're trying to outrun your own thoughts. Through Fishwife Row where the gutters ran silver with scale-water. Across Coppersmith Bridge, where the canal barges knocked together in the fog like blind cattle. Up through the Weavers' Quarter, where laundry lines crossed overhead in gray webs and every window had its little iron seal-plate bolted beside the lintel — the Order's mark, the promise that magic was death and the Seal was watching.

Vell was proud of its quiet. That was the thing outsiders never understood. Other cities had outlawed magic in blood and fire and then spent decades looking over their shoulders. Vell had done it with paperwork and patience. The Purges under Magistrate Corven had taken eleven years, methodical as bookkeeping, until the last hedge-witch was ash and the last speaker's tongue was evidence in a locked archive. Now magic was something that happened elsewhere, in barbarian lands, in stories. In Vell, the greatest miracle of an ordinary day was that the drains worked.

And yet a stone god had just opened its eye on Tallow Street and healed a child for the price of a beggar's prayer.

Yara let himself into his rooms above the dye-shop on Ropewalk Lane — two rooms, a stove, a window that looked out on a wall — and put his back against the door and slid down it until he was sitting on the floorboards with his knees up, breathing the way Serah Voss's textbooks probably recommended for panic. Not that he'd ever read one. But everyone in Vell knew what the Order's manuals said, the way everyone knew the shape of the gallows.

*Miracles are sedition. Wonder is the first symptom. Report all manifestations to your district warden.*

His mother had said something once, when he was small enough to sit on her knee and she still spoke freely, before she started whispering, before the whispering wasn't enough. She'd said: *The gods don't die, Yara. They starve. And starving things wake angry, or they wake hungry, or they wake grateful — and you can't know which until it's too late.*

He'd thought it was a story. He'd thought a lot of things were stories.

A scratch at the window.

Yara looked up. On the outside sill, balanced on brick barely four inches wide above a drop to the alley, sat the gray stray from this morning — the one he'd bought silence from with half his breakfast. Its fur was damp with fog. Its eyes, in the dim room, caught what little light there was and held it, green-gold, unblinking.

"No," Yara said. "Go on. Nothing here."

The cat lifted one paw and tapped the glass. Once. Twice. Deliberate, the way a person knocks.

Yara stared at it for a long moment. Then, because he was apparently determined to make this the worst day of his life in ascending order, he got up and opened the window, and the cold walked in along with the cat, and the cat walked in along with the cold, and settled itself on his table among his charms like a magistrate taking a bench.

"Out," Yara said. "I've fed you once. That makes us square, not family."

The cat looked at him. And then it spoke.

Not the way cats speak — the yowl-and-hunger grammar that Yara had spent twenty years pretending not to understand, the simple loud needs of beast-minds. This was language. Structured, layered, old as the moss on the shrine god, and it poured into his head with the weight of something reading him even as it addressed him:

***You saw.***

Yara's whole body went cold. Twenty years of discipline collapsed around him like a burned roof. He had rules. He had walls. He had an oath sworn over his mother's grave with her funeral candle still warm in his fist — *never again, never speak, never listen, be nothing, be nobody, be safe* — and he had kept it so long that the silence had grown over him like bark. And now a stray cat on his table was speaking in a tongue that predated the Iron Seal, predated Corven, predated maybe everything, and saying:

***You saw the Eye open, little liar. You heard it answer. And you have been pretending not to hear me since sunrise.***

"I don't—" Yara's voice came out wrong, scraped. He cleared his throat and tried for the flat charm-seller tone that had talked wardens out of searching his satchel twice. "I don't know what you're talking about. Cats yowl. I'm a tradesman. Get off my table."

***Your table smells of luck-charms and fear and a woman's grave-soil,*** the cat said, washing one paw with terrible calm. ***You sell paper blessings to frightened people and whisper to their dogs when no one watches, because a dog's love is free and yours is not, and you think no one notices. I noticed. I have been noticing for three years, Yara Blackwood.***

His name. His *name*, spoken inside his skull in a voice older than the city.

Yara grabbed the nearest object — a luck-charm, iron and red thread, worth two coppers — and threw it. The cat didn't flinch. The charm passed straight through the space it occupied, or the cat simply wasn't quite where it appeared to be, and either answer was worse than the other, and the charm hit the wall and clattered to the floor between them.

"Who are you?" he whispered. "What are you?"

The cat finished washing its paw. Set it down. Looked at him with those green-gold eyes, and for a moment — just a moment — the eyes were too large, and too deep, and somewhere far behind them Yara glimpsed darkness like the underside of the sea, and a great slow turning, like something wheeling in the deep water of the world.

***I am the question you have been avoiding since you watched a dead god spend itself on a beggar's prayer,*** the cat said. ***And you will want to sit down for what comes next, but you are already sitting, so instead you will want to run. Don't. Running only ever interested Me when you were young.***

"You're mad," Yara said. "I'm mad. Fever. Fog. Bad charmed wine—"

***The shrines are waking, Yara. All of them. The one on Tallow Street was the fourth this week, and the smallest. There is a god under the temple district that has been turning in its sleep for a year, and it will wake first, and it will wake hungry, and the men with iron seals on their doors have forgotten every word of how to feed a god.*** The cat rose, stretched, arching its spine in a curve too fluid for any ordinary animal. ***You heard them tonight. The rats under the Basilica steps have been singing to each other for a month — songs in the old tongue, songs your mother knew, songs you swore an oath never to sing.***

"Don't say her name." It came out of him like a blade leaving a wound. "Don't you dare—"

***Someone has to,*** the cat said softly, and the softness was worse than the vastness. ***Because the Order has found the first waking shrine. Because a captain named Voss keeps a list, and your street is on it. And because, little liar — little oath-keeper, little last-of-your-kind — the gods did not come back by accident.***

The fog pressed against the window. Somewhere across the sleeping city, beneath the temple district, Yara felt — for the first time in twenty years, unmistakably, horribly — something enormous roll over in its sleep.

***They were called,*** the cat said. ***By name. By rite. By someone who knew exactly what she was doing.***

"*She?*" Yara said.

The cat jumped down from the table, landed without sound, and sat in the doorway between him and the stairs — sitting squarely in his path, tail curled around its feet, the oldest posture of authority in the world.

***Come,*** it said. ***We have nine days, and you have a great deal of remembering to do.***
