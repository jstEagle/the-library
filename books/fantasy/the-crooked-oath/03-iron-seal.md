## 3. Iron Seal

The rain had not stopped for three days, and Captain Serah Voss considered it a small mercy. Rain kept people indoors, kept markets thin, kept the charm-sellers huddled behind their stalls where she could count them like sheep.

She stood in the doorway of the Order's district office in Copperlane and watched the water run silver down the iron sign above her: *THE ORDER OF THE SEAL — REPORT ALL UNLICENSED PRACTICE*. The letters were going green with age. Someone would have to re-etch them before winter. Someone always did. The city rotted gently at its edges, and the Order patched it the way one patches a roof — shingle by shingle, never asking why the storm always came from the same quarter of sky.

"Captain." Corporal Denny hovered at her shoulder, holding an umbrella that did more to shelter his own nerves than hers. "The sellers are assembled. Eleven of them. One tried to leave through the back — we've got him in the second room."

"Which one?"

"The one with the bird charms. Ossler's old stall."

Voss turned up her collar. "Keep him waiting longer, then. Fear softens them."

"And if it doesn't?"

"It always does," Voss said. "People are only brave when they believe no one is looking."

* * *

The interrogation room smelled of wet wool and lamp oil. Voss had arranged the eleven charm-sellers along the wall in the order they'd been brought in — not alphabetically, not by seniority, but by how nervous they'd looked crossing the threshold. It was a private taxonomy of hers. Guilt, she had learned over nine years with the Iron Seal, announced itself in the hands long before the mouth caught up.

The room was deliberately uncomfortable: hard benches, one flickering lamp, a table bolted to the floor so that nothing in it could become a weapon or a barricade. On the table lay the object that had brought them all here.

It was a luck-charm. Cheap tin, stamped into the shape of a four-leaf clover, the kind sold three for a copper in any market row in Vell. Except this one had been fished out of the Ashen Canal two nights ago, clutched in the hand of a drowned man who should not have been alive — whose lungs, according to the physiker's report, had been full of canal water right up until the moment he'd walked out of the morgue under his own power, coughing, weeping, and swearing to anyone who would listen that a saint had pulled him back.

The physiker had since revised his report twice. The Order did not tolerate miracles; neither did it tolerate sloppy paperwork.

"You all know why you're here," Voss said. She didn't raise her voice. She never needed to. "Rumors. That is what we're calling them, because rumors are what they remain until someone in this room makes them something worse. Three dead shrines reported warm. A beggar on Tanner's Row claims a stone god opened its eye. A drowned man walking. In seven years, Vell has been quiet. I intend to keep it quiet."

Silence. Good silence — the kind that meant they understood the arithmetic. Magic was outlawed. The gods were magic's oldest and most dangerous face. A waking shrine was not a curiosity; it was a crack in a dam, and the Iron Seal existed to press its palm against every crack until the water forgot it wanted through.

A woman near the end of the line raised her hand as though she were in school. Mid-forties, ink-stained fingers, a seller's license pinned crookedly to her shawl. Voss recognized her — Marda Quill, charms and blessings, licensed tier two, no violations.

"Captain," Quill said carefully, "with respect — most of us sell trinkets. Bits of tin and thread and dried lavender. There's no working in them. You know there isn't."

"I know what's in them now," Voss said. "I want to know what's been said over them. Words are cheaper than spells, Mistress Quill, and they rot slower." She let that sit. Then: "Who has sold a charm in the last ten days to someone frightened? Frightened of anything — debt, illness, the dark. Think."

Hands went up, reluctantly, around the room. Voss watched them rise like weeds after rain.

"Frightened people pray," she said. "And lately, it seems, something has begun to answer. So I will ask each of you the same question, and I want you to think very hard about your answer, because I will remember it word for word, and I compare notes." She picked up the tin clover and held it where the lamplight could find it. "Has anyone bought from you a charm like this one — and come back changed? Come back talking about answered prayers?"

One by one they shook their heads. Voss took their names anyway, and the names of their customers, and the streets those customers lived on. Procedure was a net, and nets only worked if every knot was tied.

Then she reached the seventh name on her list.

* * *

Yara Blackwood came in last, at Voss's own instruction. She liked to watch a suspect wait. Waiting stripped people down to their bones.

He sat across the table from her in the smaller room — the one without an audience — and she studied him while she pretended to study her ledger. Early thirties. Hands calloused in a way that suggested actual craft rather than assembly-line trinkets. Eyes that were doing something she found immediately irritating: they were calm. Not the frozen calm of a guilty man performing innocence, but the settled, unhurried calm of someone who had decided some time ago exactly who he was and saw no reason to renegotiate.

"Yara Blackwood," she read. "Charm-seller, licensed tier two. Stall on Weavers' Lane, corner of the tannery drainage. No violations in five years of licensing."

"Lucky me," Blackwood said mildly.

"Do you consider yourself lucky, Master Blackwood?"

"I consider myself careful."

Voss smiled without warmth. "There's a difference between the two. Luck finds you. Care is something you build. Which brings me to my question." She set the tin clover on the table between them and slid it forward with one finger. "Have you ever sold one of these?"

Blackwood picked it up. He turned it over in his fingers the way a smith might test a stranger's metal — thumb finding the seam, nail testing the stamp. Voss marked that too. Most people held a charm like a wish. This man held it like a fact.

"Bought a batch like it from a factor in Saltmarket two seasons back," he said. "Sold maybe thirty. Tin doesn't hold an edge worth stamping, but people like the shape. Clovers mean things turn around. That's half of what I sell — things turning around."

"And do they?" Voss asked. "Turn around?"

"Sometimes. Usually what turns is the person buying." He set the clover down, precisely centered between them, and folded his hands. "You're asking whether I've noticed anything strange. Customers coming back glowing. Shrines gone warm. Gods blinking awake." His tone stayed level, almost bored. "No, Captain. My customers come back wanting refunds."

Voss leaned back. Around them, the lamp hissed, and somewhere below the floorboards the building's pipes groaned with the rain.

"You're lying," she said pleasantly.

"I'm answering you."

"You're answering the question you wish I'd asked. I haven't asked you yet what you've noticed." She opened the ledger. "Here's what I have. Two nights ago, a beggar named Col Fen prayed aloud at the shrine of the Shepherd on Tanner's Row — a dead shrine, sealed since the Purification. Witnesses say he begged for bread, for warmth, for his daughter's fever to break. And then the shrine's stone face — which has been blind for seven years — opened one eye. His daughter's fever broke by morning. The bread appeared. Warm bread, Master Blackwood, in a sealed alcove that hasn't been opened since the Order poured lead into its locks."

Blackwood's expression did not change. But Voss had spent nine years reading faces the way other people read ledgers, and she saw the flicker — small, fast, quickly buried. Not guilt. Something closer to recognition. As if she'd described a room he'd already walked through.

"Terrible thing," Blackwood said. "Stone shouldn't do that."

"No. It shouldn't. Stone obeys physics, and physics obeys the Seal." She turned a page. "Col Fen is in our custody. He's told us everything he remembers, which is nearly nothing, except one detail he keeps repeating. He says that just before the shrine woke, he heard animals. Rats in the walls, pigeons under the eaves — hundreds of them, all silent at once. Then all crying out at once. Like a congregation." She looked up. "Does that mean anything to you, Master Blackwood?"

"Why would it? I sell charms. Animals don't buy much."

"No," Voss agreed. "They don't. But there was a woman once who claimed they talked to her."

The change was immediate and total. It lasted less than a heartbeat — a stillness in him, a door closing softly in a house that had seemed empty — and then it was gone, smoothed over so cleanly that a lesser interrogator would have missed it entirely. But Serah Voss had built her career on doors closing softly. Everyone lied about the loud things. It was the quiet ones that mattered.

"That was before my time," Blackwood said. His voice hadn't moved, but it had gone careful, the way ice goes careful before it cracks.

"Before your time, yes. Seven years ago. The Purification." Voss closed the ledger and laced her fingers on top of it. "Do you know why the Order burned the speakers first, Master Blackwood? Not the hedge-witches, not the potion-brewers — the beast-speakers. Because a witch can hex a neighbor. A speaker can raise a kingdom. Every rat in the sewers, every dog at every gate, every pigeon on every rooftop — an army that eats garbage and sees everything and cannot be bribed because it cannot be spoken to by anyone else. The old gods used them as heralds. Where a speaker walks, divinity follows. That is not superstition. That is doctrine, written in the blood of the last war."

"You've given this speech before," Blackwood observed.

"Every week. To rooms full of tin-and-thread merchants who think the Seal is a tax collector." She rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at Copperlane dissolving in the rain. "I'm going to tell you something now, Blackwood, because I believe in symmetry. You'll lie to me again — I can see you deciding to as we speak — so I'll give you a truth to balance the scales against your soul. I don't enjoy this work."

He said nothing. She continued anyway.

"People imagine the Iron Seal enjoys cruelty. We don't. Seven years ago this city was a slaughterhouse — gods fighting gods through human mouths, whole districts kneeling to stones that demanded blood, children given away to shrines by parents who genuinely believed it was holy. The Seal ended it. Not with cruelty. With boredom. With procedure. We made magic illegal, then we made it tedious, then we made it embarrassing, and now a child in Vell can grow up without ever learning that the world was once hungry for her. That is the peace I keep. It is fragile, it is unglamorous, and it is the only peace this city has ever had that didn't cost a tithe of blood." She turned from the window. "So when stone gods start opening their eyes, I don't feel wonder. I feel the first cold drop before a flood. And I will wade through every market, every cellar, every licensed stall in this city, and I will find whatever woke them — and I will put it back to sleep. Permanently. That is not a threat, Blackwood. It's weather forecasting."

For a long moment the charm-seller simply looked at her. And Voss saw — clearly, unmistakably — that beneath the calm there was grief. Old grief, worn smooth like a river stone, the kind a man carries so long he mistakes it for his own skeleton.

"My mother sold charms," he said quietly. "In Saltmarket. Before the Purification."

"Was she licensed?"

"She believed in being harmless. It didn't save her."

Voss held his gaze. "No. Harmless has never saved anyone. That's rather the point of the Seal — we don't wait to see who the wolf eats. We count teeth." She gathered her ledger and the tin clover and moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the frame. "You're free to go, Master Blackwood. All of you. No charges today. But understand what today was. Today was me memorizing your faces."

"And tomorrow?" he asked.

"Tomorrow I walk Weavers' Lane," Voss said. "I hear you have a cat. Gray, one ear torn. It sits on your stall like it owns the deed." She smiled thinly. "Animals like you, Master Blackwood. Your neighbors say so. They think it's charming."

"They think it's good for business."

"Mm." She opened the door. "Perhaps it's good for something else."

* * *

That night Voss worked late in her office above the district hall, and the rain finally broke, and the city exhaled steam through ten thousand chimneys.

On her desk she laid out the day's harvest like a general laying out maps: the eleven statements, the customer lists, the seizure records from the Purification pulled at her request. Her corporal thought she was chasing ghosts. Let him. Ghosts were all the Order ever chased — that was its genius. The living learned to hide from men. They never learned to hide from paperwork.

She read Blackwood's statement a fourth time. *My mother sold charms in Saltmarket.* She checked the records. There it was, in faded iron-gall ink: **BLACKWOOD, SARA — speaker (bestial), executed Year 1 of the Peace, Saltmarket district. Surviving kin: one son.**

Speaker. Bestial class. The highest priority classification of the Purification, hunted before witches, before warlocks, before kings who'd bargained with shrines. And her son was sitting in Copperlane selling four-leaf clovers to frightened widows, calm as Sunday, with a stray cat camped on his stall like a sentry.

Coincidence. Probably. Nine years of this work had taught Voss that probably was the most dangerous word in the language.

She took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote a single name at the top of it. Below the name, she drew the small square symbol that meant *under observation* in Order cipher — and beside it, after a moment's thought, a second mark. Not the routine mark. The red one. The one she had used only six times in her career, for the six people who had turned out to be exactly what she feared they were.

Yara Blackwood. Marked.

Outside her window, far off across the rooftops of Copperlane, something yowled — a cat's cry, ragged and strange, almost like language. Voss glanced up, listened, and dismissed it.

But she left the lamp burning, and she slept at her desk, and in the morning she gave orders for two things: a full audit of every surviving relative of every executed speaker in Vell — and an unmarked surveillance detail on a charm-stall at the corner of Weavers' Lane.

By then, Yara Blackwood was already awake, standing barefoot in his kitchen, listening to rats whisper beneath his floorboards about a god stirring in the dark beneath the temple district.

The hunter and the hunted had both begun to move. Neither yet knew they were walking toward the same door.
