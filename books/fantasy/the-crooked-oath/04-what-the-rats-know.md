## 4. What the Rats Know

Sleep did not come to Yara Blackwood that night. It sat at the foot of his bed like a creditor he owed and waited for him to acknowledge it.

The cat — the gray one with the torn ear who had followed him home from the dead shrine on Tanner's Row — had not left either. It lay curled on the windowsill of his rented room above the chandler's shop, watching him with eyes like struck flint, and when he finally blew out his candle it made a sound. Not a meow. Words.

*You saw,* it said, in a tongue older than Vell, older than the Iron Seal, older than the stones the city was built from. *You saw, and you ran.*

"I didn't run," Yara said to the ceiling. "I walked away with purpose."

*Liar,* said the cat, comfortably.

He pulled the blanket over his head. This was the trouble with his gift, the thing no charm-seller's license could cover: beasts did not lie to him, and they did not let him lie to himself. His mother used to say that speaking with animals was like holding up a mirror polished by ten thousand years of honest tongues. He had spent seven years trying to forget what his own reflection looked like.

The cat said nothing more. It simply stayed, a warm gray weight against the window glass, and somewhere below in the streets of Vell the night watch called the hour — two bells, all quiet, the Seal be praised — and Yara lay awake listening to the sound he had spent half his life learning to ignore.

Because it wasn't silence anymore. That was the thing. For seven years the city's beasts had been just beasts: pigeons grumbling about crumbs, dogs complaining about chains, rats saying rat things in their quick, sly voices. Background noise. A tide he'd taught himself not to hear.

But tonight, beneath the ordinary chatter, something else was moving through the animal-mind of Vell like a current under ice. The rats were talking about stone.

* * *

He went down before dawn, when even the Order's patrols grew lazy and the lamplighters had gone home to their beds. He wore his plainest coat, the brown one that made him look like exactly what his papers claimed — a licensed vendor of trinkets, luck-knots, and household blessings, harmless as bread. In his pocket he carried a heel of yesterday's loaf and, wrapped in wax cloth where no honest charm-seller would ever keep it, a sliver of dried liver bought from the butcher on Coppersmith Lane with coins he told himself were for a customer.

The alley behind the tannery was where the rats held court. Every city has such places; Yara had known them since he was small enough to crawl under porches. Rats are the historians of the poor. They see everything, forget nothing, and can be bought for less than a prayer costs at an Iron Seal chapel — which is to say, for food, freely given.

He crouched by the drainage grate, broke the bread, and laid the liver on a flat stone. Then he did the thing he had sworn never to do again.

He opened the door in his mind.

It came back the way it always came back — not gradually but all at once, like plunging into cold water. The world roared alive. The tannery dogs two streets over dreaming of rabbits. The gulls on the temple roofs arguing over a fish head. The horses in the watch stables muttering their slow, patient grievances. And underneath it all, pouring out of the dark of the grate like water from a cracked jug, the rats.

*Meat-giver,* said the first one, a big scarred female with a kinked tail whom he'd known for three years. She emerged with the caution of her kind and the confidence of long acquaintance. *Meat-giver comes at a strange hour.*

"I need to ask you things," Yara said. His voice sounded strange to himself, rusty at the edges. Seven years of disuse, and still the words came in the old way — half sound, half meaning, the grammar of the gift his mother had bled out of her ears to teach him.

*Rats sell nothing for free.*

"The liver is free. The questions are separate." He pushed the wax cloth forward. The scarred female sniffed it, then looked at him with black bead eyes that held, as rat eyes always did, a kind of ancient amusement at the expense of larger creatures.

*Ask, then. The night is short and the cats grow bold.*

Yara hesitated. Once the question left his mouth, there was no taking it back. Asking was a kind of oath. But he had seen a stone god open its eye, and he had heard a beggar's prayer answered by something that should have been deaf for a hundred years, and the cat had followed him home speaking a dead language. The secret was already too heavy. He could feel it bending his spine.

"The shrines," he said. "The old ones. The dead ones. Have the rats noticed anything... waking?"

The scarred female went very still. Around her, in the shadows of the grate, other shapes paused — he felt them pause, dozens of them, a hundred, the whole listening congregation of the alley. For a moment the only sound was the drip of tannery water.

Then, softly, in the voice rats use for things that frighten even rats:

*You felt it too.*

* * *

They told him in the way rats tell stories — overlapping, interrupting, each one contributing its fragment while the others corrected or contradicted, so that the truth assembled itself in Yara's mind like a mosaic being fitted together by a hundred small hands.

It had started, they thought, in the temple district. Of course it had. The great shrine of Ashen-Vey had stood sealed there for seven years — since the Reckoning, since the last of the old priests had gone to the pyres and the Order of the Iron Seal had chained the doors and poured its doctrine into the vacancy like mortar into a crack. The rats knew those catacombs better than any living human; they had nested in the bones of the old faith for generations, and they reported, with the flat certainty of creatures reporting weather, that the deep places had grown warm.

*Warm like a body,* said a young male with white-tipped ears. *Warm like something sleeping under a blanket that isn't a blanket.*

*The stone sweats,* added the scarred female. *In the old crypts, the walls weep at night. My mother's mother said the stone only sweats when the god inside turns over in its sleep.*

And it wasn't only the temple district. That was the part that made Yara's hands go cold around the bread crust. The rats ranged the whole city — through sewers and walls and granaries and the foundations of every noble house — and from their scattered perches they had watched the dead shrines of Vell begin, one by one, to dream.

The shrine of the Twin Sisters in the Weavers' Quarter, bricked shut for a decade: pigeons now refused to roost on its roof, claiming the stones were *occupied*. The drowned fountain-shrine of Maelis down by the fishmarket, dry since the aqueducts were rerouted: rats swore they heard water moving in pipes that had been empty for years, moving *upward*. The little wayside altar in Tanner's Row — the one Yara had fled from two nights past — had become a place the street dogs avoided at a wide, respectful distance, tails low, hackles raised at nothing visible.

*The prayers go down,* said the scarred female, and this she said alone, in the hushed tone of one delivering the sum of the whole testimony. *That is what my people say. For seven years the humans prayed anyway, even though the Order told them the gods were dead. Little prayers, secret prayers, whispered into pillows and behind locked doors. All that time the prayers went nowhere. Now—* she flicked her kinked tail *—now they land. Something is catching them. Something is drinking. And a thing that drinks grows strong, meat-giver, and a thing that grows strong eventually opens its eyes.*

"You've seen it," Yara said quietly. "Haven't you. Under the temple district. You've seen what's down there."

The rats looked at one another. It was the young male who answered, and his voice had lost its cockiness entirely.

*We don't go below the third crypt anymore. The ones who did come back wrong. Sitting in the dark, staring at the wall, not eating. And when you touch them they say one word, over and over.* He paused, whiskers trembling. *They say: soon.*

A cart rattled past the mouth of the alley. Yara froze, heart hammering, suddenly aware of how it would look — a charm-seller crouched at a drain at dawn, murmuring to thin air. If Captain Serah Voss or any of her iron-eyed investigators had seen him, no license in Vell would save him. The Order burned speakers. He knew that down to the marrow his mother had left him.

When the cart passed, the scarred female was waiting with one last thing, delivered in the matter-of-fact tone rats reserve for their most terrible news.

*One more thing, meat-giver. The warm place under the temple district — it is not warming alone anymore. Three nights ago the warmth began to move. Outward. Along the old god-roads under the streets, the tunnels your kind forgot. Toward the other shrines. Like roots.* Her black eyes glittered. *Like a hand reaching for a hundred cups at once. Whatever sleeps down there, it is not merely waking. It is calling its family home.*

* * *

He climbed back to his room as the sky went the color of pewter, and the cat was waiting on the windowsill exactly where he'd left it, as if it had never moved, as if it had been listening to everything through the soles of his feet.

"Well?" he said, before he could stop himself. "You're clearly not an ordinary cat. Tell me what's happening. You know, don't you? You know what's waking."

The cat regarded him for a long moment. When it spoke, its voice was different than before — less mocking, almost gentle, which frightened him more than mockery would have.

*I know that you asked the rats instead of asking me.*

"Because I don't know what you are."

*No,* the cat agreed. *You don't. And yet you fed the rats your fear and your bread, and they gave you truth, because that is what beasts do. Remember how that felt, Yara Blackwood. Remember that the gift is not the curse. The curse is being alone with it.*

He wanted to shout at it. He wanted to throw it out the window. Instead he sat down heavily on the edge of his bed, among his jars of luck-charms and his bundles of knotted thread, the whole modest inventory of his respectable lie, and put his face in his hands.

Seven years. Seven years of selling hollow charms and hearing nothing louder than sparrows, and in three nights the whole buried architecture of his life had cracked open like river ice. Shrines waking across the city. Something vast turning over beneath the temple district, reaching along forgotten tunnels toward a hundred altars, gathering the desperate prayers of a frightened population the Order had forbidden to hope. And above it all, the Iron Seal tightening its grip, hunting rumors of miracles, burning whatever it found.

If the gods were returning, Vell would tear itself apart. He had seen what fear of magic did — he had watched it take his mother. And if the Order learned that a beast-speaker was asking questions, asking *rats*, then the fire would come for him too, and everyone foolish enough to stand near him.

He couldn't carry this alone. That was the truth the rats had handed him along with everything else, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He needed someone who knew the old rites, the old names, the shape of what was rising. Someone who might know why a gray cat with a torn ear spoke the language of dead gods.

"There's a woman," he said slowly, half to himself, half to the cat. "Maren. She keeps the candle shop off Pilgrim's Steps. She sold my mother tallow once, years ago, and when my mother died she was the only one who came to the house. Everyone else was too afraid." He lifted his head. "I always thought she was just being kind. But I saw her once, at the funeral, mouthing something over the grave. Old words. I told myself I imagined it."

The cat began, very softly, to purr.

*Then stop telling yourself stories,* it said, *and go knock on her door.*

Outside, the sun was coming up over Vell, gilding the sealed doors of a hundred sleeping shrines, and beneath the temple district, in the warm dark, something turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count the days.
