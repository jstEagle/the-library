## 5. The God Beneath

The rats had spoken true, and Yara wished to every silent god that they hadn't.

He stood at the mouth of the sealed well behind the tannery on Corvus Lane, where the smell of rendered fat and lye hung thick enough to chew. The grate was iron, older than the Order, older than the peace — its bars furred with rust, its lock swollen shut by decades of neglect. Someone had cut it anyway. Recently. The fresh metal gleamed like a wound in all that brown decay.

*Down,* the rats had said, in their chittering, image-bled way that was less language than a flood of pictures: *down, down, warm-stone, singing-stone, the-thing-that-wakes.* They'd shown him a stair. They'd shown him light where no light should be. And they'd shown him, with the blunt honesty of creatures who have never learned to lie, that they were afraid of it.

Yara crouched at the grate's edge and listened the way he hadn't let himself listen in seven years. Not with his ears. With the other thing, the buried thing, the crooked gift his mother had died for.

The city spoke to him from every direction. Pigeons muttering their grey gossip from the eaves. A dog three streets over dreaming aloud about a bone. Mice in the tannery walls, quick bright sparks of hunger and fear. And beneath it all — beneath everything, down where the stone began — a hum. Low. Patient. Like a hymn remembered by someone who has forgotten the words but not the tune.

It was stronger here than anywhere. Stronger than at the dead shrine where a stone god had opened one eye while a beggar wept into his hands. Stronger even than in his own rooms, where a cat with mismatched eyes sat on his windowsill most nights and watched him with an attention no animal should possess.

"You could just leave," Yara said quietly, to himself, to the city, to whatever was listening. "You could go home. Sell charms. Grow old. Let the world stay quiet."

The hum did not answer. It never answered directly. That was somehow worse.

He worked the grate with a charm — not one of his honest ones, the luck-knots and fever-laces he sold openly for copper, but a real one, whispered soft, that made the iron forget it was locked. The metal sighed. The gate swung inward on hinges that screamed once and then went silent, as if ashamed.

Below, darkness. And stairs, just as the rats had promised, cut into living rock, descending past the reach of the grey afternoon light.

Yara Blackwood, charm-seller, coward, keeper of a seven-year silence, went down into the earth.

* * *

The catacombs of Vell were supposed to be empty.

Everyone knew this. The Order knew it; they'd sealed the temple district's undercrofts themselves in the first year of the Iron Peace, bricking up the old shrines, salting the prayer-wells, chiseling the faces off every god that could be reached without collapsing half the city. The merchants knew it; they'd bought the salvaged marble cheap and sold it dearer. The poor knew it best of all, because the poor always know what's been taken from them, even when they've learned not to say so.

But the stairs went down farther than any catacomb Yara had ever heard described. Farther than bones should need room for. He counted two hundred steps and stopped counting, because the number had begun to feel like a message in itself.

The dark changed around him as he descended. It thickened, then softened. By the time he reached the bottom — a landing where the stair turned and opened into a corridor — he could see. Not by torchlight. There were no torches. The walls themselves gave off a faint warmth-colored glow, like hearthstone long after the fire has died, and Yara understood with a lurch that the stone was *remembering* light. Remembering what it had been like, once, to house something holy.

His gift rose in him unbidden, the way a scar aches before rain. The mice in the walls above were far away now, their voices thin as thread. But other voices filled the deep places. Bats, somewhere, singing their stitched-together maps of the dark. Things with many feet moving through cracks too small for names. And underneath, everywhere, the hum — closer now, textured, almost words.

Almost.

"Seven years," said a voice behind him, human, female, and calm as still water. "I was told seven years. I confess I'd stopped believing it."

Yara spun, heart slamming, one hand already dipping into his coat for a charm he knew would do nothing against a person. She stood at the corridor's far end, framed in that faint stone-glow: a woman of perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair braided close to her skull, dressed in plain wool gone soft with age and wear. No weapon. No lantern. Her hands were folded in front of her like a woman waiting at a well.

She didn't look surprised to see him. That frightened him more than a blade would have.

"You're the Order," he said. It came out flat, an accusation and a plea at once.

"I'm the opposite of the Order." She tilted her head, studying him the way he'd seen scholars study texts — patiently, hungrily. "You came down the Corvus Lane stair. You followed the animals." Not a question. "I felt them talking about you for three days. The rats first, then the gulls, then last night the cats — and the cats are always the last to share anything. A speaker, they said. A speaker walks in Vell again."

"I don't know what you mean." The lie was smooth. Seven years of practice had made it smooth. "I sell charms. Luck-charms. Fever-laces. Nothing—"

"Your left hand is shaking," she said gently, "and you haven't blinked since you saw me, because you're listening to the bats behind me telling you whether I'm alone. I am alone, by the way. They can confirm it." She unfolded her hands. "My name is Maren. I was a priestess of the Undivided Flame, back when there was such a thing, back when saying so didn't get your name written in a seizure ledger. And I have spent seven years preparing for you, though until tonight I wasn't certain you existed."

The hum swelled. Yara felt it in his teeth now, in the roots of them, and behind the hum — or inside it, threaded through it like gold wire through dark cloth — he heard something else. A word. Or the shape a word leaves when it's been spoken so long it's worn through the air itself.

"*Come,*" it might have been saying. "*Come. Come.*"

"Why seven years?" he asked, and hated himself for asking, because asking meant staying, and staying meant the silence was already broken.

Maren's composure flickered — just once, just at the edges, like a candle in a draft. "Because seven years ago," she said, "they burned a beast-speaker in the Saltmarket square. A woman. They said she'd corrupted the minds of the city's animals, turned dogs against their masters, taught pigeons to carry messages for traitors. It was a lie — mostly. What she actually did was refuse to tell them how the gift works. Whether it can be given. Whether it can be *taken*." Her eyes held his, steady and sorrowing. "They burned her to make an example. And every creature within a mile of that square went silent for three days. Grief, if you want my belief. The whole city's beasts sat shiva for her."

The floor did not move. The walls did not move. Yara stood perfectly still on ground that had become ocean.

"That woman," he said, and his voice sounded like it belonged to someone much younger, "was named Sefa Blackwood."

Something moved through Maren's face then — recognition, and beneath it, carefully banked triumph, and beneath *that*, something rawer that she was clearly too disciplined to show. "I know," she said softly. "I was there. I laid flowers on the ash for a week until the Order stopped me. And I swore, standing in that square, that if her gift survived her — if it passed to blood, as the old stories say — I would find whoever carried it. And I would not let them die the way she did. Alone. Afraid. Believing the world had no place for what they were."

The hum was a roar now, though his ears heard nothing. Every animal voice in the deep had gone quiet, the way a forest goes quiet, the way the world goes quiet when something enormous is paying attention.

"She told me to be quiet," Yara said. His mother's face, the night before the arrest: tired, fierce, her hand cupped around his ear as if she could pour the warning directly into his skull. *"Never speak to them where anyone can see. Never let them hear you answer. Promise me, little wren. Promise me the crooked way — live crooked, stay small, stay silent, and live."* He had promised. He had kept it for seven years, keeping it the way a man keeps a wound clean, painfully, daily, with both hands. "She told me silence was survival."

"And look what the silence bought her." Maren's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "Forgive me. That was cruel. But Yara—" and she said his name as if she'd earned it, as if she'd carried it seven years like a stone in her shoe, "—the shrines are waking. All across Vell. The dead gods are opening their eyes, and the Order is frightened, and frightened men with seal-blades do not ask gentle questions. You heard them calling. That's why you're here. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise."

Above them, muffled by two hundred steps and a hundred years of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The *other* one — the alarm, the one Yara had heard only twice in his life, the one that meant the Iron Seal was moving through the streets in force.

Maren went very still. Then, unhurried, she turned and lifted a small hooded lamp from a niche in the wall — it had been there all along, unlit — and struck flint to it with steady hands.

"They found the tannery grate," she said. "Or they will within the hour. I'd hoped for more time. I'd hoped to bring you gently." She looked back at him, and in the lamplight her face was suddenly very old and very tired and absolutely unyielding. "So. Choose quickly, son of Sefa. Up the stairs to the seals and their questions — and I promise you, they have ways of making speakers sing. Or down. Down with me, past the sleeping one, to the sanctuary the Order never found because the animals have guarded it for seven years. Down to the god beneath."

"The god beneath," Yara repeated. The hum pressed against him like a tide against a seawall. "*Come,*" it sang. "*Come. Come.*"

"It's been waiting for you longer than I have," Maren said. "It dreamed of a speaker before I ever did. Come and ask it why."

The bell above rang on. Somewhere up in the world of lye and lamplight, boots were finding the Corvus Lane stair.

Yara thought of his mother's voice. He thought of the crooked oath. He thought — and this was the thought that moved his feet — of a beggar kneeling at a dead shrine, praying to nothing, and nothing answering, and the look on the man's face when the stone opened its eye: terror first, yes, but after the terror, something Yara had never once seen in the grey streets of Vell.

Hope. Ugly, dangerous, impossible hope.

"Down," he said.

Maren nodded once, shouldered her lamp, and led him into the dark — and behind them, far above, the first iron boot touched the bottom step.
