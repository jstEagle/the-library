## 6. Seven Years of Silence

The dream always began with bread.

Not the burning — never the burning, not at first — but the smell of dark rye rising in the little oven behind their cottage in the Fenmarsh quarter, and his mother's hands dusted white to the wrist, and her voice humming an old tune that had no name in any language Yara had learned since. In the dream he was nine years old again, barefoot on cool flagstones, and the world was still full of voices.

Because in those days, everything spoke.

* * *

He had been born listening, though no one had taught him. His mother, Sefa Blackwood, said it came from her side — from a grandmother in the hill country who could call hawks down out of a clear sky — but she said it quietly, in the way people say dangerous things, with her back to the door.

"Never let them hear you," she told him when he was five, after he'd answered a question a sparrow asked through the kitchen window and a neighbor's face had gone strange and thoughtful. She knelt then, flour still on her hands, and took his chin so he could not look away. "Yara. Look at me. The world before the Seal was loud — beasts talking, gods talking, men thinking they were gods. The Iron Seal burned the loudness out of Vell, and they are proud of it. If they hear you, they will come for you the way they came for your grandmother. Do you understand?"

He understood the fear in her voice if not the reason for it. Children understand fear first; reasons come later, and too late, usually.

So he learned crookedness the way other children learned letters. He learned to move his lips without sound, to answer a dog's greeting with a tilt of the head that looked like nothing. He learned which neighbors watched too closely and which market guards were bored and which were ambitious. His mother sold charms — luck-knots, fever-sachets, bride's-thread — all of them honest nonsense, dried herbs and knotted string, because honest nonsense was legal. And at night, when the shutters were closed and the candle was hooded, she would let the silence off its leash.

Those nights were the truest of his life. A moth would land on her knuckle and tell her what the wind was doing three streets away. The house cat — a scarred grey tom named Bracken who thought himself a philosopher — would deliver long opinions on the moral failures of dogs while Sefa laughed until she had to sit down. And Yara, curled against her side, would listen to the rats in the walls gossip about dropped crumbs and the ambitions of the rat who lived under the bakery, and feel something he had no word for yet: that the world was a conversation, and he had been invited into it.

"The old days were like this," his mother murmured once, half-asleep, the cat a warm weight across both their feet. "Everything talking to everything. That's why they're afraid of it. Not because magic is power. Because it's *company*."

* * *

The Order of the Iron Seal did not burn witches anymore. That was the official history, recited in every schoolroom: the Purges ended a generation ago, the Seal brought peace, magic was a sickness cured by law rather than fire. What the Order did now was quieter. They registered. They investigated. They took people away in unmarked wagons on gray mornings, and the people did not come back, and their houses were sold within the month, and everyone agreed afterward that they had always seemed a bit odd.

Sefa Blackwood had seemed a bit odd for seven years. She sold charms. She kept to herself. She was careful.

But there were things carelessness could not fix. There was the winter a fever swept the Fenmarsh and half the street's children lived who should have died, and someone remembered that Sefa had gone door to door with her sachets, muttering. There was the day a cart horse reared on Cordwainer's Row and Yara — fourteen, tired, frightened — snapped at it aloud, one sharp word in the beast-tongue, and the horse stilled as if a hand had touched its brow, and a man in a gray coat turned around very slowly in the crowd.

They did not come that day. That was the worst part, Yara understood later. They waited. The Order was patient the way traps are patient. For six weeks the gray-coated man appeared in different places — buying bread, inspecting chimneys, asking the charm-sellers of the Fenmarsh cheerful questions about their suppliers — and each time, Sefa grew a little thinner, a little quieter, humming less.

"We should run," Yara said. He was fifteen by then. "To the hill country. Where your grandmother—"

"There is nowhere the Seal doesn't reach," she said. She was packing the good charms into a box she'd hidden under the floorboards — not the legal ones, the others, the ones with real knots in them, knots tied while speaking to the wool itself. "And if we run, we confirm everything. Listen to me." She took his face in her hands, and her fingers were cold. "If they ever come, you know nothing. You are a boy who sells charms and hates his mother's superstitions. You have never heard anything speak that shouldn't. Swear it to me now, so it's already true in your mouth when you need it."

"I swear," he said, and hated how easily it came.

She smiled, and it broke his heart even then, even before. "Good lad. Crooked oaths are the only kind that hold in Vell. Straight oaths snap under pressure. Crooked ones bend."

* * *

They came on a Tuesday, three hours past midnight, and they did not knock.

Yara woke to the sound of the back door coming off its hinges and his mother's voice — not screaming, worse than screaming, *low*, urgent, speaking fast in the beast-tongue to something in the dark yard. He was out of bed and into the main room in time to see the lanterns swing in through the broken door, four of them, five, gray coats and iron badges stamped with the Seal, and Captain Dren Halloway — he would learn the name later, from the trial records he stole and read until the paper went soft — standing in the doorway with the particular calm of a man doing paperwork with swords.

"Sefa Blackwood," Halloway said. "By writ of the Order of the Iron Seal, on suspicion of proscribed practice. You'll come quietly."

Bracken the cat was on the table, fur risen along his spine like a ridge of knives, and he was saying — yowling it, making it plain enough that even a deaf man might have caught the shape of it — *run, boy, run, out the window, RUN* —

And his mother looked at Yara across the room, lantern-light jumping on her face, and she did not say run. She said, in the beast-tongue, in front of all of them, deliberately, spending her last coin in one throw:

"*Window. Now. Say nothing. Never say anything.*"

Then she picked up the iron poker and swung it at the nearest lantern, and the room went dark, and Yara went through the window with glass in his hair and Bracken's teeth in his shoulder, hauling him back from the door he was trying to reach.

He heard it all from the drainage ditch behind the tannery two streets over, pressed into black water with the cat's heart hammering against his ribs faster than his own. He heard the crackle of the fire they set — the Order always set a fire; accidents explained everything — and he heard his mother's voice rise over it once, not begging, not screaming. Speaking. To the horses in the Order's wagon, to the dogs on their ropes, to the rats in the walls and the moths battering themselves against the lanterns. Calling every beast in the Fenmarsh to witness, in the old tongue, that she died for the crime of being listened to.

And the beasts answered. That was the thing no record ever captured, the thing Yara carried alone for seven years: the whole night *answered*. Dogs howling from a dozen yards, horses screaming and breaking their traces, rats pouring out of the gutters in rivers, moths and crows and one enormous pale owl circling the burning cottage like a ring of ash given wings. The Order's own animals turned on them in the smoke. It bought her nothing. But the city knew, for one hour, that the loudness was not dead — and the Order spent the next week shooting dogs and clearing rookeries and calling it plague.

Yara lay in the ditch until dawn with water in his ears, and the only voice left in the world was Bracken's, saying his name over and over in the dark, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

*Boy. Boy. Boy.*

He never answered it again.

* * *

The oath happened three days later, in the ruins, before anyone could stop him.

They had left the cottage standing — burned to its bones, but standing, because the Order preferred its warnings visible. Yara went back in daylight, past the char-marked threshold seal the investigators had stamped on the doorframe, and stood in the black rafters where the roof used to be, and found, in the ashes of the hearth, the iron trivet his mother had cooked on every morning of his life. It had not burned. Iron does not burn. He held it and it was cold, colder than the water in the ditch, cold as the inside of a locked door.

Bracken sat in the doorway, thin now, watching him with eyes like struck flint.

"You want me to talk to you," Yara said aloud. His voice sounded wrong in the ruin, too large, human-sized, ugly. "You've been trying for three days. Go on, then. Say something. I'm listening."

The cat opened his mouth. Yara saw the shape of the words come out — *boy, come home, boy, I'm sorry, boy* — and heard nothing. Nothing at all. Because hearing was not only a gift; he understood that now, kneeling in his mother's ashes. Hearing was a *consent*. The world had been talking to him all his life because he had been agreeing to listen, and a man could simply — stop. Close the door from the inside. Refuse the invitation.

It hurt like setting a bone. He felt the voices of the ruin — the pigeons in the rafters, the rats already returning to the walls, a spider going about its patient work above the blackened stove — press against the place where the door had been, felt them realize, one by one, that the boy was no longer home to them. The pigeons left. The rats went quiet. The spider kept working, indifferent, and somehow that was the worst.

"I swear it," Yara said to the trivet, to the ashes, to the woman who was not in them anywhere except everywhere. "I will never speak with beasts again. I will never listen. I will be deaf to the whole loud world, and I will sell honest nonsense until I die, and no one will ever burn because of me. This is my oath, and I make it crooked on purpose, so it bends and doesn't break. Mother. *Mother.*" His voice cracked on it. "You told me straight oaths snap. So this one is bent. This one is crooked as a broken finger. And it will hold until I do."

Bracken cried all night outside the ruin. Yara lay on the floor of a rented room above a chandler's shop and stared at the ceiling and did not sleep, and did not answer, and learned that silence has a texture, and the texture is swallowing glass.

* * *

Seven years later, in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the city of Vell, Yara Blackwood woke with salt on his face and the taste of smoke that was not in the room.

Morning light came gray through the shutters. Below, the street was waking — carts, shutters banging open, the milk cart's horse blowing through its nose. All of it mute to him. All of it, for seven years, mute. He had made a life in the muteness the way a man makes a life in exile: small, orderly, defended. He sold charms. He was pleasant. He was forgettable. Last week he had sold a luck-charm to a frightened widow and smiled at her and meant none of it and all of it at once.

And yesterday, at a dead shrine in Tanner's Walk, he had watched a stone god open its eye.

And last night a stray cat — not Bracken, couldn't be Bracken, Bracken was seventeen years dead — had followed him home through the dark, and spoken to him in a tongue older than the beast-speech, older than the Seal, older than the stones of Vell. And Yara had lain awake until dawn with his hands over his ears, which did nothing, because the voice was not coming through his ears and never had been.

He sat up. The cat was on the windowsill, sitting in the gray light with its tail wrapped around its feet, amber eyes fixed on him with the patience of something that had waited seven years and could wait seven more.

"Go away," Yara said hoarsely. His voice cracked on it, the way it had cracked in the ruin. "Whatever you are. Whatever this is. I swore an oath."

The cat blinked slowly. And when it spoke, the old tongue poured through the shuttered window like water through a floorboard, soft and certain and utterly indifferent to his grief:

*"Crooked oaths bend, little speaker. Yours is bending now. Your mother didn't die so you could be safe. She died so someone would be left — and here you are."*

Yara pressed his back against the wall, heart slamming, and the cat began, gently, horribly, to wash one paw — and somewhere beneath the temple district, deeper than the rats had ever dared to go, something old rolled over in its sleep and began, at last, to wake.
