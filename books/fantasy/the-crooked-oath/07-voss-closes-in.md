## 7. Voss Closes In

Captain Serah Voss had a theory about liars: they were most honest when they thought no one was listening.

She stood in the archive beneath the Iron Seal's chapterhouse, three floors of iron shelving sunk into the city's bones, and listened to the dead. The seizure records of the Purge years lay stacked in leather-bound ledgers, their spines furred with dust, their pages stiff as dried skin. Seven years ago the Order had swept through Vell and torn out its magic root and branch — shrines desecrated, relics melted, practitioners dragged to the sealing blocks. Voss had been a young blade then, too junior to understand what was being done in her name. Now she understood too well, and it sat in her stomach like a swallowed nail.

The rumors had started six weeks ago. A luck-charm that worked too well. A candle that burned without consuming itself. A beggar in the Saltrow who swore the old stone god at the corner of Tanner's Lane had opened its eye and wept. The Order's official position was that miracles were impossible; the Order's unofficial position, the one Voss held privately and would never speak aloud, was that impossible things were merely things that hadn't yet been properly investigated.

She had spent those six weeks doing what she did best: following paper the way other hunters followed blood.

The shrines were waking in a pattern. That was the first thing she'd seen, and once she'd seen it she could not unsee it. Tanner's Lane first, then the drowned shrine under Gullmarket, then two more in the Weaver's Quarter within a single night. Not random. A line drawn across the city, district by district, moving inward toward the temple ruins like a tide coming home.

Someone was walking that line. Someone was waking them on purpose.

Voss turned the ledger open to the seventh year of the Purge, the year the Order had hit the old faith hardest, and ran her finger down the column of names. Most meant nothing to her. Seized, sealed, released without charge. Seized, sealed, transported to the coast. Dead in custody. Dead resisting. Dead.

Then her finger stopped.

*Maren Solenne, priestess, sect of the Undivided Dawn. Apprehended at the shrine of Ashen-Vey, temple district. Relics seized: one (1) votive stone, pre-Imperial. Subject released after oath-sealing per magistrate's discretion.*

Released. After the Order had gutted every other priestess in Vell, this one had walked free. Voss read the entry again, slower, tasting the wrongness of it. Magistrate's discretion. She knew that phrase. It was what clerks wrote when someone important had leaned on someone less important, or when a case smelled of something the Order preferred not to examine.

And the shrine where Maren Solenne had been taken seven years ago — the shrine of Ashen-Vey, deep in the sealed catacombs beneath the ruined temple district — was the exact point toward which the waking shrines were converging.

Voss closed the ledger. In the lamplight her reflection ghosted across its cracked leather cover, hollow-eyed and grim.

"Corporal Dain," she said, not turning around. The young man waiting by the archive door straightened. "Pull every record touching the Solenne woman. Where she lived before the Purge. Where she went after. And find me who signed her release."

"Yes, Captain." He hesitated. "And if she's the one waking the shrines?"

Voss picked up her seal-blade from the table, sliding it home into its scabbard with a click that sounded, in the dead quiet of the archive, like a lock turning.

"Then she's been patient for seven years," Voss said. "I intend to know why patience like that finally broke."

* * *

Yara knew something was wrong before the rats told him.

It came as a pressure behind his eyes, a wrongness in the way the pigeons wheeled over Saltrow — not their usual lazy circles but tight, alarmed spirals, the kind they flew when a hawk was overhead or when men with nets were in the streets below. Then a rat he'd befriended behind the tannery found him at his stall, bold as you please in daylight, and stood up on its haunches right where the widow customers couldn't see.

*Iron smell,* it said, in the chittering way of its kind that arrived in his mind as images braided with feeling. *Iron and boot-leather and fire-oil. They are asking questions at the charm-stalls. Asking about a woman with gray in her braid. Asking about a man who talks soft to animals.*

Yara's hands went still over the luck-charms he'd been sorting. Brass and bone and knotted thread, all of them harmless, all of them lies — except the ones that weren't, lately, and that was the whole problem.

*When?* he asked, shaping the question carefully. Speaking with beasts took effort even now, even with the world gone strange and the old god's covenant humming in his chest like a second heartbeat. Each conversation was a small betrayal of the oath carved into him at fifteen, and each one cost him something he couldn't name.

*Last night,* the rat said. *They searched the tannery row. They have drawings.*

Drawings. Of course they had drawings. The Order of the Iron Seal didn't chase rumors; it chased faces. Yara glanced up the street, casual as a man checking the weather, and saw what he'd been dreading: two figures in slate-gray cloaks working their way down the market row, unhurried, showing a folded paper to stall-keepers. One of them carried herself like water finding its level — Captain Voss. He'd only met her once, but some people you remembered the way you remembered scars.

He packed his stall in ninety seconds flat, a lifetime's practice at vanishing, and slipped into the alley behind the tannery with his satchel over his shoulder and his heart going like a drum in a procession.

The cat was waiting for him there, of course. The cat was always waiting.

*They will go to her next,* the cat said, tail flicking. Its voice had changed in recent weeks — deeper somehow, older, threaded with harmonics that no ordinary cat should carry. Since the shrine-waking began, since the covenant, since Yara had started carrying Ashen-Vey's word from shrine to shrine, the cat had become less animal and more... announcement. *Maren does not run well anymore. Her knees. Her heart. You must reach her before the iron does.*

"I know," Yara said aloud, because saying it out loud made it real, and he needed it real. He needed to be afraid properly, cleanly, instead of in the shapeless way that made mistakes. "How long do we have?"

*Less than you want. More than nothing.* The cat rose and flowed ahead of him down the alley, glancing back with lamp-yellow eyes. *Run, beast-speaker. The night is shorter than it looks.*

They went down through the city's skin — through the culverts behind the dye-works where the water ran green and stank of alum, through the smuggler's gap in the old seawall that the rats had shown him years ago and he'd sworn never to use again, down, always down, toward the catacombs beneath the temple district where the oldest god slept with one eye open and waited for him like an unpaid debt.

Behind him, above him, spreading through the streets of Vell like oil through cloth, the Order was closing its hand.

* * *

Serah Voss found Maren Solenne's name written in four places, and the fourth place was a lie.

The release order bore the signature of Magistrate Ellery Crane, retired these five years, and when Voss pulled Crane's docket from the same period she found the magistrate's hand was wrong — the flourishes too careful, the ink a shade too black. Forged, and forged by someone good enough that it had survived seven years of routine audits. Someone inside the Order, or close enough to wear its clothes.

That changed everything. A heretic priestess walking free was a failure of vigilance. A heretic priestess *sprung* by a forged writ was a conspiracy, and conspiracies were the Order's favorite meal.

By dusk she had the rest. Maren Solenne had surfaced twice since her release — once in a charity ledger at the Almshouse of Saint Verren, listed as a healer of the poor; once in a complaint filed by a landlord in the Weaver's Quarter about "strange chanting, animals gathering, candles burning at odd hours." Animals gathering. Voss read that phrase three times. In a city where magic had been stamped out, where the very word was contraband, a woman whose house attracted beasts the way sugar attracted flies was either mad or guilty, and Voss had never once met a madwoman who could forge a magistrate's signature.

"They're in the old temple district," Corporal Dain reported, breathless, as the squad mustered in the courtyard. His face was pale in the torchlight. "A lamplighter swears he saw a light down the sealed stair near the Ashen-Vey ruin. And Captain — the birds. There were crows sitting on every gargoyle along the temple wall. Dozens of them. Just watching."

Voss buckled her gauntlets. Around her, twenty blades of the Iron Seal checked flintlocks and lit lanterns, their faces set in the expressions men wore when they believed in what they were doing. She envied them that, briefly, and then put the envy away where she kept everything else she couldn't afford.

"Crows watch fires," she said. "Nothing more. Form up. Torch-bearers center. Nobody breaks formation, nobody touches the relic-stones, and nobody — *nobody* — goes past the third landing without my word. We take the priestess alive. If there is anyone else down there—" she paused, thinking of charm-sellers and soft voices and a market stall that had emptied itself with suspicious speed, "—we take everyone alive. The Seal judges. It doesn't murder."

Dain nodded. Somewhere beyond the wall, the evening bell rang, and beneath it — so faint Voss might have imagined it, so faint she chose to imagine it — came another sound, low and resonant, like a great stone throat clearing itself after a long sleep.

"Move out," said Voss.

* * *

They came at moonrise, and the dark told Yara first.

He felt it as a tremor in the stone under his knees, a hundred small feet stampeding upward through the warrens — rats flooding out of the lower galleries in a silver river, streaming past him where he knelt beside Maren at the shrine of Ashen-Vey. The old priestess looked up from her preparations, votive oil gilding her wrinkled hands, and read his face the way she read everything: instantly, mercilessly.

"The Seal," she said. Not a question.

"The Seal." Yara was already moving, snuffing lamps, sweeping the covenant-scrolls into his satchel. "Rats say twenty, maybe more. Torches. They'll reach the second gallery within the quarter hour."

Maren did not panic. That was the thing about her that frightened Yara most, more than the gods or the Order or the growing certainty that everything happening in Vell passed through her hands like thread through a loom. She simply rose, unhurried, and laid her palm against the votive stone of Ashen-Vey.

"Wake fully, then, lord of ash," she murmured. "Your faithful require sheltering."

The stone's carved eye, which had been half-open for weeks now, opened the rest of the way. Heat rolled off it like a hearth-door swinging wide. Yara felt the god's attention settle over the chamber, vast and slow and pleased in a way that made his skin crawl, and the god spoke — not in words, not exactly, but in a pressure of meaning that pressed against the inside of his skull:

*THEY COME WITH IRON AND DOUBT. LET THEM. THE FAITHFUL ARE MINE TO KEEP.*

"We're leaving," Yara said aloud, to Maren, to the god, to himself. "All of us. Now."

"There is a way out through the ossuary galleries," Maren said. "Older than the catacombs. The rats know it, I think, if you ask them sweetly."

He asked them sweetly. They answered in a chorus of whiskers and urgency — *this way, this way, follow the water-mark, hurry, hurry, iron comes* — and they were halfway to the ossuary arch when the light came down the main stair.

Torchlight, then lantern-light, then the flat gray gleam of seal-blades, and boots, many boots, and a voice like cold water: "Order of the Iron Seal! By authority of the Magistracy, remain where you stand!"

Yara grabbed Maren's arm and pulled her into the dark.

What followed was not a chase so much as a descent — a stumbling flight through galleries floored with centuries, past alcoves stacked with the anonymous dead, the rats shrieking directions while behind them the torches multiplied and split and hunted. Twice the Order cut them off and twice the darkness itself seemed to conspire, lamps guttering, a shelf of bones toppling across a passage with a crash like judgment, a swarm of bats boiling up out of nowhere to claw at lantern-glass. Yara did not ask whether the animals were helping him on their own account or on the god's. He was afraid the answers were the same, and more afraid of what that meant.

At the second cutoff, it stopped being clean.

A crossbow bolt out of the dark — not aimed to kill, aimed to stop, but aim was a courtesy chaos didn't extend. It took Maren high in the side as she turned to shield him, punching through her robe, and she made a sound Yara had never heard from her: small, surprised, human. He caught her as she folded, hauled her arm over his shoulders, and ran, her blood hot through his sleeve, her breath sawing against his ear.

"This way," the cat said, materializing out of black air atop a funerary urn, eyes twin coals. "Down. Through the crack. Hurry, speaker, HURRY—"

The crack was barely wide enough. Yara turned sideways with Maren half-lifted against his chest and forced them both through a seam in the living rock, stone scraping his ribs raw, and on the far side the passage dropped away into a crawl of wet gravel and old water. Behind them, in the torchlit gallery, he heard Voss's voice — furious, controlled, closer than it should have been.

"Seal the galleries! She's wounded, she can't have gone far—"

Then gravel, and dark, and distance.

* * *

They surfaced an hour later through a collapsed cistern mouth in the hillside above the tanneries, and by then Maren's blood had soaked through two layers of bandage and Yara's fear had curdled into something colder.

He knelt over her in the wet grass, pressing the wound, and the old woman gripped his wrist with a strength that shamed her condition. Her eyes were bright and feverish and utterly certain.

"You cannot let them take me," she whispered. "Everything dies with me, Yara. The rites. The names. The *door*. Without me the covenant is just words in your mouth."

"Stop talking. Save your strength."

"They will come for you next. You know that. The captain with the cold eyes — she has already marked you." Maren smiled, and there was blood on her teeth. "You should have asked me sooner, boy. About the release. About who signed it. About why a priestess walks free for seven years and no one asks."

Yara went very still. The night pressed close. Somewhere downslope, in the city, bells were ringing alarm, and the crows were lifting off the temple wall in a black ragged sheet, and far beneath his feet he felt Ashen-Vey's eye close slowly, satisfied, patient, waiting.

"What aren't you telling me?" he asked quietly.

But Maren's eyes had rolled back, and the cat leapt onto her chest, ears flat, and yowled once into the dark — a sound with harmonics in it that no cat should own, a sound that carried for miles, a summons.

In the rookery on Tam Ossler's roof, an old crow cocked its head, remembered a debt, and spread its wings.

* * *

Serah Voss stood alone in the violated shrine of Ashen-Vey, surrounded by scattered scrolls and cooling lamps and the empty votive stone whose carved eye had, by every report of her own soldiers, been open when they breached the chamber.

She picked up a scrap of parchment from the floor. On it, in a hand she recognized from the market surveillance reports — the same careful, cramped script as the luck-charm receipts — was a line of verse in Old Imperial. Beside it, in different ink, newer: nine names of shrines, eight struck through.

One remaining.

Voss closed her fist around the parchment and looked up at the stone god's blind, sleeping face, and for the first time in seven years of service, Captain Serah Voss prayed to something she did not believe in.

*Don't let me be wrong about this.*

The stone said nothing. But somewhere above, faint through miles of rock, a cat was calling — and every crow in Vell answered.
