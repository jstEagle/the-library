## 8. A Debt in Feathers

The crow found them before the Order did.

Yara heard it first as a scratch of thought at the edge of his mind — *wingbeat, wingbeat, black-wing, come* — while he was still hauling Maren through the drainage cut behind the tanneries, her arm slung over his shoulders, her blood leaving a trail any competent tracker could follow to the end of the world. She had stopped apologizing for the weight somewhere around the third alley. Now she just breathed, wet and ragged, and kept her feet moving because he told her to.

*Black-wing, come. Black-wing, come.*

He knew that voice. He had known it three winters ago, when a crow with a shattered wing had fallen from the eaves of Cooper's Row into a puddle of tannery filth, and a younger, stupider Yara Blackwood had knelt in the mud and set the bone with his thumbs and whispered nonsense to it until dawn — nonsense that was not nonsense at all, but speech, the old speech, the one his mother had died for.

"Tell me you have somewhere," Maren gasped. "Tell me you're not just running."

"I'm running *toward* something," Yara said, which was technically true, though what he was running toward was a bird.

The crow dropped out of the grey morning like a thrown stone and landed on a rusted drainpipe two paces ahead, head cocked, one bright eye fixed on him. Up close Yara could see the old scar along its breast where the bone had knotted wrong — a featherless seam, pale as candle wax. It had never flown quite right since. It had never needed to. Crows remembered debts the way rivers remembered rain.

"You followed me," Yara said under his breath. "All this time."

*I follow,* the crow said, in the flat, certain way crows had, as if stating the shape of the sky. *You mended. I remember. Men forget. I do not.*

"Well," Yara muttered, "I hope your memory includes a place to hide a bleeding priestess."

The crow lifted off without answering, which was answer enough.

* * *

It led them up. That was the strange part. Vell's fugitives went down — into cellars, sewers, the buried bones of the city — and Yara had spent the last hour expecting exactly that. Instead the crow flew a crooked line across the rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter, past drying frames hung with hides like flayed giants, toward the leaning hulk of an old counting-house whose upper floors had burned in some fire no one talked about anymore. The lower windows were bricked. The roof was a forest of broken chimneys and rotted beams, and perched on every one of them, silent as a jury, were crows.

Dozens. Then scores. They lined the gutters and the weathervanes and the ribs of the collapsed bell-tower next door, and not one of them made a sound as Yara half-carried Maren up the outside stair — a staircase nailed on by someone with more confidence than carpentry — to a door in the burnt shell of the roof.

The door opened before he knocked.

The man standing in it was perhaps fifty, built like a barrel someone had tried to make elegant, with a beard going to iron-grey and sleeves rolled over forearms scrawled with ink-stains that were very deliberately *not* sigils. He looked at Yara. He looked at Maren, grey-faced and dripping red. He looked at the crow on Yara's shoulder, where it had landed as if it owned the perch.

"Seven years I've been in this attic," the man said, "and in all that time, not one soul has climbed my stairs who wasn't a bird or a bill collector." He stepped aside. "Come in before the whole quarter sees you. And mind the third floorboard; she bites."

* * *

The inside of the ruined counting-house was nothing like its outside. The burnt floors had been shored with salvaged timber; the broken roof was patched with mismatched skylights of green glass, so the whole space swam in underwater light. Shelves climbed every wall, crammed with jars, brass instruments, bundles of dried herbs, books with their spines defaced so no Iron Seal clerk could catalog them. A kettle muttered on a stove. Somewhere below, doves murmured — the rookery, Yara realized, extended down through the building, floor after floor of birds, which meant eyes, which meant the entire Tanner's Quarter reported to this room.

"Tam Ossler," the man said, pressing a cup of willow-bark tea into Maren's hands and a stronger-smelling one into Yara's. "Formerly of the Collegium. Currently of nowhere, officially."

"Maren," the priestess managed. "Of the—"

"I know who you are." Tam's eyes flicked to Yara, and there was something in the look that made the back of Yara's neck prickle. "Maren of the catacombs. Your name's been in the seizure records since the Purging. Which means, little priestess, that whoever pulled you out of an Order raid this morning has either a death wish or a talent." He turned fully to Yara. "So. Which are you?"

"A charm-seller," Yara said. It came out smooth. Seven years of practice, smooth as river stone. "Luck-knots, fever-wards, the usual. Nothing worth a raid."

Tam smiled. It was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of a man watching a card trick he'd already seen through. "That's a lovely lie. Truly. You've polished it." He nodded at Yara's shoulder, where the crow sat preening. "But I've spent seven years learning to listen to birds, friend, because they're the only witnesses in Vell nobody interrogates. And this morning, forty of my crows started screaming about a man in the tannery alleys who *talked back*."

The room went very quiet. The kettle muttered. Green light swam across the shelves.

"They say," Tam went on softly, "that you knelt in the mud and spoke to a dying crow like it was your brother. They say the rats have been meeting in unusual numbers lately. They say the pigeons around the shrine district have gone strange — circling, watching, carrying word." He leaned closer. "They say a speaker walks in Vell again."

Yara's heart was a fist against his ribs. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to deny it, to laugh, to sell the man a luck-charm and walk out into the grey morning and vanish. That was the oath. That was the whole architecture of his life: *be small, be dull, be nothing the Seal would ever look at twice.* His mother had been loud with magic, luminous with it, and loudness had killed her.

But Maren was watching him too, her tea untouched, her wounded arm bound in Tam's clean linen. And the crow watched him with one eye like a held coin.

"The cat talks to me," Yara heard himself say. "And the rats. And apparently your crows." His voice cracked on the last word, seven years of silence splitting down one hairline seam. "I don't advertise it."

Tam exhaled slowly, and Yara realized the man had been holding his breath.

"Do you know," Tam said, "what you are? Not a curiosity. Not a trick." He crossed to a shelf, pulled down a book with its spine scraped blank, and laid it open on the table between them. The page showed an engraving: a figure ringed by beasts of every kind, wolf and wren and serpent, all facing inward, all listening. "The old faith didn't run on priests alone. It ran on speakers. The gods didn't speak to crowds — they couldn't, not directly, not without burning the air. They spoke through beasts, and the beasts spoke to speakers, and speakers carried the covenant." He tapped the page. "Every waking god needs a mouth. And if the shrines are stirring — and my birds say they are, all across this city — then something is looking for exactly what you are."

"I didn't ask to be looked for," Yara said.

"No one ever does." Tam closed the book gently. "My exile cost me the Collegium, my name, and the use of half my craft — they sealed the words into my hands, did you know that? I can read a spell and I cannot cast it. Like a man who remembers music but has no throat." He flexed his ink-stained fingers. "So believe me when I tell you I understand wanting to be small. But small men don't get chosen by gods, friend. Only useful ones."

* * *

They worked on Maren through the afternoon. Tam's hands were surgeon-steady; whatever else the Collegium had taken from him, it hadn't taken that. The wound was a seal-blade cut, deep along the forearm, and he cleaned it and stitched it while Maren bit down on a leather strap and refused, with visible effort, to cry out.

"The Order took the catacombs?" Tam asked her.

"What they could reach. I collapsed the far galleries behind us." Her voice was hoarse. "The relics are scattered now. Some saved. Ashen-Vey's heartstone is—" She stopped, glanced at Yara.

"Go on," Tam said dryly. "He's already in it past his knees."

"The heartstone is safe," Maren finished. "And the god wakes regardless. Waking doesn't need stone. Stone only focuses it." She looked at Yara, and there was something in her gaze he couldn't parse — gratitude braided with something heavier, something almost like guilt, though he told himself he was imagining it. "You saw the shrine open its eye. You heard it. There's no walking away from that, Yara. Not for you."

"There's always walking away," Yara said. "There's a whole city to walk away into."

"And how long," Maren asked quietly, "before the Order finds the charm-seller who was seen fleeing a raid with a condemned priestess? How long before they trace the crow? The rats? How long before they work out why the animals of this quarter keep clearing the streets ahead of their patrols?"

Yara had no answer. The silence stretched, and in it he heard the cat — his cat, the stray who had followed him home from the dead shrine days ago and simply stayed, speaking in that old tongue he half-understood — scratching at the roof hatch where it had arrived an hour behind them, uninvited and unsurprised.

*Small house,* the cat observed through the boards. *Many secrets. Good.*

"He stays here," Yara said. "With me. That's not negotiable."

Tam raised his eyebrows. "A beast-speaker with a familiar and he hasn't worked out what the familiar is yet. Wonderful. Yes, the cat stays. Everything stays. That's rather the point of this place." He began banking the stove, businesslike again. "You'll both sleep here tonight. Tomorrow we'll see about moving Maren somewhere safer and deciding what a man does when gods start asking for him by name."

"And if I decide I want none of it?"

Tam looked at him across the green-lit room, and for a moment the exiled mage looked very tired and very old and very kind.

"Then in a year you'll be selling charms in some other city, and you'll hear a bird say something it shouldn't, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering," Tam said. "Believe me. Wondering is worse than hanging."

* * *

Night came down through the green glass in pieces. Tam spread pallets in the loft among the rafters, where the doves' murmuring rose through the floorboards like slow breathing, and Yara lay awake listening to the city he had spent seven years hiding inside.

It sounded different tonight. He had always heard Vell as a held breath — ten thousand people swallowing their prayers, the Order's boots keeping time. But beneath it, faint as a pulse under bandages, he could feel the other city now. Rats running messages along the aqueduct walls. Dogs lifting their heads at midnight in yards all across the Tanner's Quarter, scenting something old on the wind. And everywhere, everywhere, crows — settled on chimney pots in their hundreds, silent, patient, waiting the way his crow waited, the way debts wait.

*You mended,* the crow had said. *I remember.*

He understood, lying in the dark, that the debt ran both directions and had always run both directions. He had spent seven years believing his gift was a wound he kept hidden. But the animals had never treated it as a wound. To them it was a hand reaching across the oldest wall in the world — and they had been waiting, all this time, for someone to reach back.

The cat appeared on the rafter above him, a darker shape against the dark, and flowed down to curl against his side, warm as a coal.

*You are afraid,* the cat said. Not a question.

"Yes."

*Good. Fear means you understand the size of it.* The cat's purr vibrated through his ribs like a second heartbeat. *Sleep, speaker. Tomorrow the god will want to talk to you properly. And you will want to know what it offers before you decide what you owe.*

"That's not ominous at all," Yara whispered.

The cat purred louder, which was somehow worse.

Downstairs, he heard Tam moving among his shelves, and Maren's low voice, and once — briefly, sharply — Tam saying something he couldn't quite catch, ending in words that drifted up through the floor like smoke:

"—too convenient, Yara. Gods don't wake on schedule. Someone *woke* them. Find out who, before you give them your voice."

Yara stared up into the dark rafters, the cat warm against his side, the crows settling on the roof above like a held breath, and for the first time in seven years he let himself wonder — really wonder — what his mother would have done.

The answer scared him more than the Order ever had.
