## 9. The Gods' Bargain

The candle Maren lit did not behave like a candle.

Yara noticed it at once — the way its flame stood perfectly still in a chamber where the air moved, the way the light fell *toward* things instead of away from them, pooling in the hollows of the stone like water finding low ground. He had sold a hundred candles in his time, tallow and beeswax and one memorable block of whale-fat that had made a magistrate's wife sneeze for an hour. None of them had ever looked like they were listening.

"That's not ordinary wax," he said.

"Nothing down here is ordinary." Maren knelt at the center of the shrine floor, drawing lines in the dust with a bone stylus worn smooth by seven years of use. "Sit where I've marked. And Yara—" She glanced up, and in the strange light her face looked older than it had an hour ago, carved rather than lived-in. "Whatever you hear, whatever you see, do not speak until it has finished speaking first. That is not ritual courtesy. That is survival."

He wanted to ask what she meant. He didn't. Seven years of silence had taught him that some questions were better carried than opened.

The chamber beneath the temple district was smaller than he'd expected after the long descent — a low-vaulted room no larger than his shop above, its walls furred with mineral bloom, its ceiling lost in dark. But the shrine itself filled the space the way a drowned man fills water. It was not a statue. Statues were shaped by hands; this thing had been *revealed*, as if the stone around it had been peeled back to show what had always been sleeping inside. A torso without a face. Arms crossed over a chest like a closed door. Ash-grey granite shot through with veins of something darker that might have been metal or might have been old blood.

The rats had called it *the deep-warm*, and they had been afraid of it, and rats were afraid of very little except owls and hunger.

Yara sat on the chalk mark. The cat settled beside him, tail wrapped over its paws, green eyes fixed on the god with an expression he could only describe as recognition.

Maren began to sing.

It was not a song he knew, and yet parts of it snagged on something buried in him — a lullaby cadence, a rising phrase that felt like his mother's voice before he could remember why. The words were in no living tongue. They were older than Vell, older than the Iron Seal, older probably than the idea of outlawing anything. As she sang, she traced the dust-lines with the stylus, and the lines began to glow — faintly, then less faintly, then with a light that had nothing to do with the candle.

The candle went out. Nobody had touched it.

"Ashen-Vey," Maren said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable like ice under a boot. "Ashen-Vey, who was first among the quiet gods. Ashen-Vey, who kept faith when faith was forbidden. We bring you a speaker."

The veins in the stone brightened.

And the god spoke.

* * *

Yara had heard voices all his life that no one else heard — sparrows arguing about gutter rights, horses complaining about their riders' weight, a dog once who had delivered a forty-minute monologue on the moral bankruptcy of cats. He had thought he understood what a voice was: breath and throat and intent, shaped small enough for ears.

Ashen-Vey's voice was none of that. It came up through the floor, through the soles of his boots, through the bones of his pelvis and spine, resonating in his chest cavity like a bell struck inside him. It did not travel through air. It traveled through *stone*, and everything touching stone, which meant everything, which meant Yara, sitting on a chalk mark above a vein of waking divinity, was being spoken through like a pipe.

**A SPEAKER.**

The word alone nearly knocked him flat. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Maren pressed her palms flat to the floor and bowed her head, and even the cat flattened its ears.

"Yes," Maren said. "As promised. As foretold. A beast-speaker of the old line, blood of the—"

**I KNOW WHOSE BLOOD.**

The pressure of those three words made Yara's nose sting. He clamped his jaw shut, remembering Maren's warning, and stared at the crossed arms of the god and tried to breathe.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for him to count his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Then:

**SMALL ONE. THE ONE WHO TALKS TO RATS AND FEEDS CROWS AND LIES TO IRON WOMEN WITH A STRAIGHT FACE.**

Heat crawled up Yara's neck. The god had been watching. Of course the god had been watching. Everything in this city that wasn't human had been reporting to something, and he had spent seven years believing his little kindnesses — the crow's splinted wing, the stray cat fed off his own thin margins — were invisible.

"I'm here," he said. His voice sounded absurdly thin, a mouse squeak in a cathedral.

**YOU ARE AFRAID.**

"I'm sensible. There's a difference."

A sound rolled through the stone that took him a moment to identify as laughter — deep, grinding, geological, the sound a mountain would make if it found something funny. Maren flinched at it. The cat did not.

**GOOD. FEAR IS HONEST. THE FAITHFUL WHO ARE NOT AFRAID OF US HAVE ALREADY MADE US INTO FURNITURE.** The voice shifted, and Yara felt it turn toward Maren like a searchlight. **PRIESTESS. YOU HAVE KEPT MY SHRINES SWEEPED AND MY NAME WHISPERED FOR SEVEN YEARS IN A CITY THAT FORGOT ME ON PURPOSE. SPEAK YOUR PETITION.**

Maren lifted her head. Her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady — the steadiness of someone who had rehearsed this moment every day for seven years and was determined not to waste a syllable of it.

"Lord Ashen-Vey. The shrines are waking. Nethra's fragment walks. The people pray again, in cellars and behind shut doors, and the Order burns whoever they catch. We are few, and hunted, and the Seal grows stronger every year it goes unchallenged." She drew a breath. "We ask for your protection. Shield your faithful. Let them worship without the pyre."

**AND WHAT WOULD YOU GIVE?**

"Everything," Maren said simply. "What we have always given. Ourselves."

**NO.** The word landed like a dropped beam. **THAT IS THE OLD COVENANT, AND THE OLD COVENANT IS WHY THEY BURNED YOU. A GOD WHO TAKES EVERYTHING MAKES HIS PEOPLE INTO NOTHING, AND NOTHING CANNOT DEFEND HIM. I HAVE HAD SEVEN YEARS UNDER THIS CITY TO THINK ABOUT WHAT WENT WRONG.** Another seismic rumble, not laughter this time. **I WILL SHIELD THE FAITHFUL. THAT IS NOT THE QUESTION. THE QUESTION IS DELIVERY.**

"Delivery," Yara repeated, before he could stop himself.

**A COVENANT IS A THREAD, SMALL ONE. IT MUST BE TIED AT BOTH ENDS. MY END IS HERE, IN THE STONE. THE OTHER END MUST BE TIED AT EVERY SHRINE IN VELL — EACH ONE WOKEN, EACH ONE HEARING MY TERMS FROM A LIVING MOUTH, EACH ONE SEALED BY A VOICE THAT CAN SPEAK FOR MORE THAN MEN.** The veins pulsed, slow as a heartbeat. **THERE ARE NINE SHRINES STILL TRUE IN THIS CITY. NINE SLEEPING EYES. IF ALL NINE ACCEPT THE COVENANT WITHIN NINE DAYS, MY PROTECTION FALLS OVER EVERY SOUL WHO KNEELS, AND NO SEAL-BLADE IN VELL CAN TOUCH THEM. THE ORDER CAN RAID CELLARS FOREVER AND FIND ONLY EMPTY ROOMS.**

Yara's mouth had gone dry. "And if they don't all accept?"

The pause was answer enough, but the god gave it words anyway, and the words were worse.

**THEN THE COVENANT FAILS, AND I DO WHAT SLEEPING GODS HAVE ALWAYS DONE WHEN THEIR PATIENCE RUNS OUT.** The temperature in the room seemed to drop. **I STOP BEING PATIENT.**

* * *

Afterward — after the glow had faded and the candle had relit itself, which Yara decided firmly not to think about — the three of them sat in the ringing quiet and looked at each other.

"Nine shrines in nine days," Maren said. She was crying now, quietly, without wiping her face, as though tears were just another part of the liturgy. "Seven years I prayed for this. Do you understand what he's offering? Protection. Real protection. Not hiding — *sanctuary*. Every faithful soul in Vell under a god's own shield."

"And every shrine woken is a bonfire lit," Yara said. "You know what happens when a shrine wakes. Chapter house gets rumors, rumors get Captain Voss, and Voss gets a warrant. You want me to walk through all nine districts lighting beacons with my name attached."

"Yes," Maren said. "That is exactly what I want."

"You're not the one whose name is attached."

"No." She reached out and took his hand, and her grip was fierce, and her eyes were wetter than ever. "I'm the one who's been waiting seven years for someone who *could*. Yara. Please."

He pulled his hand back gently and stood, because if he stayed kneeling he was going to say yes just to make her stop looking at him like that, and he needed to think, and thinking required walking, and walking required not being touched.

The cat followed him to the edge of the chamber and sat down directly in his path, which was a thing it had started doing recently and which he resented deeply.

*You're going to say yes,* the cat said.

"You don't know that."

*I have known you for four years. You fed me when you couldn't afford to feed yourself. When the widow Brenna couldn't pay for her charm last winter, you told her the luck was free that week. You are constitutionally incapable of walking away from something that needs you.* The cat licked one paw with terrible calm. *Also, you already said yes in your head about a minute ago. You just haven't told your mouth.*

"That's not how consent works."

*With gods, it's exactly how consent works.* The cat's eyes lifted to the grey shape of Ashen-Vey, and something passed between them — some current Yara couldn't hear but could feel, like standing near a struck tuning fork. *Ask it the question you're actually afraid of.*

He turned back to the god. The crossed arms hadn't moved. The veins had dimmed to embers, patient as banked coals.

"If I do this," Yara said slowly, "what happens to me? At the end of the nine days. When the covenant's tied."

**YOU WILL BE ITS HERALD.**

"That's a title. What does it mean?"

**IT MEANS WHEN THE FAITHFUL LOOK FOR THE FACE OF THEIR DELIVERANCE, THEY WILL FIND YOURS. IT MEANS THE ORDER WILL PUT YOUR FACE ON EVERY WALL IN VELL WITH A PRICE UNDERNEATH IT. IT MEANS YOU WILL NEVER AGAIN BE A CHARM-SELLER IN A QUIET SHOP, AND YOU WILL NEVER AGAIN BE UNSEEN, AND YOU WILL NEVER AGAIN BE SAFE.** The voice softened, which was somehow more frightening. **I WILL NOT LIE TO YOU, SMALL ONE. LYING IS HOW THE NEW GODS LOST THEIR CITIES. YOU WILL BE KNOWN. THAT IS THE PRICE OF THE THREAD.**

Known. The word sat in Yara's chest like a swallowed coal. For seven years his entire life had been built on not being known — the plain stall, the modest prices, the smile that promised nothing magical whatsoever. His mother had been known. His mother had been *seen*, in the town square at Aldermoor, with the whole village watching and the Seal's men making sure nobody looked away, and the last thing she had said — the only thing she'd had time to say — had been his name.

And underneath the terror, treacherous and warm as a fever, was the other thing. The thing Maren's wet eyes were doing to him. Nine shrines, and every one of them a room full of frightened people who prayed to dead stone because it was the only prayer they had left — and he could make the stone answer. He could be the reason it answered.

Duty never arrived wearing chains, he thought. It arrived wearing a crown, and let you put it on yourself.

"The widow Brenna," he heard himself say. "The cellar congregations. The ones who get caught in between. If I carry your covenant — your shield covers them whether they kneel or not?"

**MY SHIELD COVERS ANY WHO SHELTER THE FAITHFUL OR ARE SHELTERED BY THEM. THE COVENANT IS A ROOF, NOT A MEMBERSHIP.**

"And the Order? The soldiers, the investigators — most of them aren't evil, they're just—" He groped for the word. "*Convinced.*"

**CONVICTION IS A KIND OF FAITH, AND MY COVENANT DOES NOT ASK ME TO SMOTHER FAITH. ONLY TO SHELTER IT. WHAT THE ORDER DOES WHEN THE ROOF GOES UP OVER THEIR HEADS — THAT IS BETWEEN THEM AND THEIR OWN GODS, IF THEY HAVE ANY LEFT.**

It was a good answer. That was the problem. Every answer the god gave was good — measured, generous-sounding, wise in the way of something that had had seven years of darkness to polish its wisdom. Yara kept waiting for the trap to show its teeth, and the trap just kept offering him tea.

"Then I'll carry it," he said. "Nine shrines. Nine days. On three conditions."

Maren made a small strangled sound. Negotiating with a god was either blasphemy or the bravest thing anyone had done in Vell in a decade, and from her expression she genuinely could not tell which.

**SPEAK THEM.**

"One: I choose the route and the order. I know this city's streets and its patrols better than any priest does, no offense."

**NONE TAKEN. AGREED.**

"Two: when a shrine wakes, the people there hear the terms from *me*, plainly, including the price. No fire-and-brimstone recruiting. If they kneel, they kneel knowing what it costs."

Something in the stone seemed to warm. **THE HERALD WHO TELLS THE TRUTH ABOUT THE BARGAIN. YES. THE OLD COVENANT DIED OF PRETTY WORDS. AGREED.**

"Three." Yara hesitated. This was the one he'd been circling since the god first spoke, the question with teeth. "If it goes wrong — if the ninth shrine doesn't take, if the Order catches me, if the nine days run out — you tell me what 'not patient' means. Before it starts. Not after. I don't carry a threat I can't see."

The silence stretched. The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

**FAIRLY ASKED,** Ashen-Vey said at last, and the fairness in its tone was the most terrifying thing it had said yet. **IF THE COVENANT FAILS, I WILL RISE. FULLY. NOT THROUGH SHRINES AND SPEAKERS BUT THROUGH THE STONE OF THIS CITY ITSELF, WHICH REMEMBERS ME, WHETHER THE SEAL'S CHARTERS DO OR NOT. WALLS WILL OPEN. FOUNDATIONS WILL ANSWER. AND EVERY PERSON IN VELL WHO HAS EVER PRAYED TO ME WILL KNOW IT WAS BECAUSE THE HERALD FAILED — AND SOME OF THEM WILL THANK ME, AND SOME OF THEM WILL BLAME YOU, AND BOTH WILL BE CORRECT.** The veins flared once, briefly, like a held breath released. **THAT IS WHAT NOT-PATIENT MEANS, SMALL ONE. NOW YOU HAVE SEEN IT. WILL YOU STILL CARRY THE THREAD?**

Every sane part of Yara screamed the obvious answer. Nine days to wake nine shrines across a city crawling with Iron Seal investigators, one of whom had already marked him, carrying a covenant whose failure meant a god rising through the streets like floodwater — and the alternative to trying was the same floodwater, just without anyone having tried.

His mother's face rose in his memory, unbidden: not the square, not the fire, but earlier — her crouched in the garden, hand cupped to her ear, smiling at something in the hedge he couldn't hear anymore because he'd been too young to have the gift yet. *Listen, Yara. The world is talking all the time. Someday you'll hear it too.*

She'd been right. And they'd killed her for it.

"Yes," Yara said. "I'll carry it."

**THEN IT IS SPOKEN,** the god said, and the words sank into the floor, into the walls, into Yara's own bones, settling there with the weight of a brand. **NINE SHRINES. NINE NIGHTS. THE FIRST IS CLOSEST, AND THE FIRST IS ALWAYS THE HARDEST — THE WATCHERS ARE FRESHEST AND THE FAITH MOST AFRAID. GO UP, HERALD. THE CLOCK OF YOUR LIFE JUST STARTED.**

The light died. The candle guttered out properly this time, and in the sudden true dark, Yara heard Maren begin to laugh — soft, disbelieving, half-hysterical with seven years of hope finally given somewhere to go.

"It's real," she whispered. "Oh, Yara. It's *real*."

He didn't answer. He was staring at the cat, which had risen to its feet and was looking toward the passage up with its ears pricked, its whole body gone rigid as a drawn wire.

*What?* he asked silently. *What is it?*

The cat's eyes reflected the last ember-glow of the dying veins, and when it spoke, its voice was stripped of all its usual dry amusement.

*Someone else knows,* it said. *There are boots on the stairs. Iron-shod. Coming down.*

* * *

They snuffed the last light and pressed into the recess behind the shrine, Maren's hand iron-tight on Yara's wrist, the four of them — man, priestess, cat, and god — holding one collective breath while the footsteps descended. There were many of them. They moved in the disciplined cadence Yara had heard only once before, through the shuttered window of his shop, the day Captain Serah Voss had walked her patrol past his stall and paused, just for a moment, to look at him.

The footsteps stopped at the mouth of the lower passage. A lantern hissed alight. And a woman's voice, cold and clear as a seal-blade being drawn, echoed down into the dark:

"Sweep the chamber. And if there's anyone down there — the captain wants them breathing. For now."

Above them, faint through seven feet of stone, the first shrine of nine waited. Below them, the god held its silence. And somewhere in the black, Yara realized with a lurch that the only way out was the way the Order had come in.
