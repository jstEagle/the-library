## 10. Tam's Heresy

Tam Ossler had spent eleven years in exile learning one lesson above all others: nothing that arrives easily is trustworthy.

He had been a mage of the Azure College once — before the Iron Seal burned the College's towers and scattered its students like sparks on a wind. He had watched masters he loved dragged into the sealing-vans with their hands bound in cold-iron wire, watched the Order's clerks inventory miracles like contraband: a levitation stone here, a scrying mirror there, each tagged and catalogued and carried off to be melted or buried or worse. He had learned that power in Vell was not destroyed. It was *managed*. Hoarded by the few who claimed the right.

So when the shrines began to wake — nine days after a beggar's prayer cracked open a stone god's eye, nine days before some divine deadline that no one had voted on — Tam did not feel wonder.

He felt the particular chill of a man who has seen this trick performed before, and knows how it ends.

* * *

The rookery smelled of pigeon droppings, tallow smoke, and the sharp mineral tang of the wards he'd layered into every beam and rafter. From the outside it was a derelict dovecote clinging to the cliffside above Tanner's Row, invisible behind a glamour of rot. From the inside it was the last library of forbidden things in Vell: three hundred books under canvas, a wall of confiscated sigils, and a kettle that boiled water without fire because Tam had never quite managed to give up his small heresies.

Yara Blackwood sat at Tam's worktable with the covenant-scroll unrolled before him, tracing its lines with one finger as though reading music. The cat — that gray beast with the too-old eyes — lay curled at his elbow, watching Tam with an attention no ordinary cat bothered to spend.

"You're staring," Yara said without looking up.

"I'm thinking." Tam poured two cups of willow-bark tea and set one down. "There's a difference."

"Mm." Yara turned the scroll a quarter-turn. "It's beautiful, isn't it? The script. Maren says it predates the Seal by a thousand years. It's not written so much as *grown* — look, the letters change when you're not looking directly at them. Ashen-Vey says the covenant remembers every hand that ever held it."

"Ashen-Vey says a great many things," Tam said.

Yara looked up then, and Tam saw it — saw it plainly, the way you see frost forming on a window, gradual and then suddenly everywhere. There was a light in the man's face that hadn't been there eight days ago, when Maren had dragged him bleeding out of the catacombs and Tam had hidden them both among his pigeons. A light like purpose. Like belonging.

It frightened Tam more than any patrol ever had.

"Say what you've been circling for three days," Yara said quietly. "You think I haven't noticed? You polish your spectacles every time I mention the god's name. You've got a tell, old man."

"Everyone has tells. That's how I knew you were lying to Captain Voss in your shop, back before any of this." Tam lowered himself into the chair opposite. His knees crackled; he was fifty-six years old and felt every year of the fall from grace. "And yes. I'll say it. Because if I don't, no one will, and because you're walking toward something with your eyes shut and calling it faith."

The cat's tail flicked once. Tam ignored it. He had long ago stopped pretending the animal wasn't listening.

"The timing is wrong," Tam said. "Sit still and let me finish. For seven years — seven years since the last purge, since your mother, since the shrines were sealed with iron and salt — nothing. Not a whisper. The old gods were dead, or sleeping, or gone, and Vell learned to live in the quiet. And then, in the space of a single season, they all wake at once? Every district, every shrine, coordinated like a garrison answering a muster horn?" He tapped the table. "Dead things don't coordinate, Yara. Sleeping things don't keep schedules. Only *managed* things do."

Yara's jaw tightened. "Maren explained—"

"Maren explains everything. That's what worries me." Tam pulled a ledger across the table — his own, kept in cipher, the record of a lifetime of noticing. "I've been checking. The first shrine woke in Lowbridge, near where the beggar prayed. Then Saltmarket, three days later. Then the tannery shrine, the gate shrine, the one under the aqueduct. Do you know what those locations have in common?"

"They're old. They're forgotten. They're—"

"They're *wells*." Tam opened the ledger to a page dense with his cramped cipher. "Every single waking shrine sits above a natural confluence — places where, in the old days, mages drew power for the city's foundations. I know because I helped survey three of them before the fall of the College. Someone didn't just find these shrines, Yara. Someone *chose* them. Chose them years ago, maybe. Prepared them. And then lit the fuses in order."

The rookery pigeons murmured on their perches. Somewhere below, a cart rattled through Tanner's Row, and Tam heard the Order's evening bell toll from the Sealhouse — six notes, flat and final.

Yara was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle in the way that meant he thought Tam was grieving something. "Tam. I know what was taken from you. I know what the Seal did. But listen to what you're saying. You're telling me that the gods coming back — the first hope this city has had in seven years — is a *trick*. That people praying at shrines and getting answers is a conspiracy."

"I'm telling you that convenient miracles are the most dangerous kind." Tam leaned forward. "When I was at the College, my master Corvane used to say: *the devil doesn't knock, he invites.* Whoever engineered this — and someone did — they built it to be irresistible. A deadline. A bargain. A humble hero chosen by destiny. Nine days to carry the covenant to nine shrines, or the faithful go unprotected. Does that not strike you as theatrical? Gods don't set deadlines, Yara. Salesmen do."

"And what would you have me do?" Yara spread his hands. "Refuse? Let Ashen-Vey's protection fail and let the Order sweep through the low districts arresting anyone who knelt at a waking shrine? You weren't in Saltmarket, Tam. You didn't see the widow who touched the altar and had her lungs heal after twenty years of the wet cough. She wept. Half the street wept. Are you going to tell me that woman's tears were manufactured too?"

"No," Tam said. "Her healing was real. That's the cruelest part of the design." He took off his spectacles and polished them — caught himself doing it, and laughed, short and bitter. "You see? You've given me a tell now too. Look. The miracle is real. The mercy is real. And because it's real, no one asks who paid for it, or why, or what's owed when the bill comes due. That's how the Seal itself operated, in the early days. Real protection. Real order. Real bread in the granaries. And by the time people asked what it cost, the asking had been made illegal."

"That's not the same."

"It never is. That's what makes it work."

* * *

They argued until the tallow candles burned down to stubs.

Tam laid out everything: the survey records, the sequence of wakings, the fact that Maren had been waiting in the catacombs for seven years — seven years! — with rites prepared and a prophecy about a beast-speaker, as though she'd had a shopping list and had simply been waiting for the item to come into stock. He showed Yara the old seizure records he'd bribed a records-clerk to copy: Maren's name appearing not once but four times over the years, always released, always unpunished, as though someone in the Order's depths found her useful. Or protected her. Or *cultivated* her.

"She should have been sealed years ago," Tam said. "A practicing priestess of the old faith, in Vell, during the purges? They hanged people for less. They hanged your mother for less. Why is Maren alive, Yara? Who decided she was worth keeping?"

Yara stood abruptly, and the chair scraped, and the cat rose to its feet in one fluid motion, ears forward.

"Don't," Yara said. His voice had gone low and dangerous. "Don't you dare use my mother."

"I'm using the only argument that reaches you."

"My mother died because she was kind. Because she healed a fevered child in an alley and someone reported her. There was no design in that. No grand scheme. Just cruelty, the ordinary kind, the kind the Seal breeds in ordinary men." Yara's hands were shaking. "And now you want me to believe the gods' return is the same kind of machine. That Maren — who bled for me in the catacombs, who took a seal-blade through the shoulder getting me out — is some clerk's instrument."

"I'm saying I don't know," Tam said, and made his voice soft. "That's my whole point, boy. I don't know, and neither do you, and neither does she, perhaps. But I know this: you've stopped asking questions. Eight days ago you flinched at your own gift. Now you walk into shrines and speak to crowds and call it duty, and when I raise a doubt you look at me like I've spat on your mother's grave. That's not conviction, Yara. That's what being *used* feels like from the inside. It feels exactly like purpose."

The silence stretched. In it, Tam watched the light in Yara's face gutter — just for a heartbeat — and then steady again, and that steadying frightened him most of all. A man could be argued out of a mistake. But a man in love with his own usefulness would burn the world rather than feel useless.

"The covenant needs a speaker," Yara said finally. "That's all this is. The gods are weak, waking, half-blind — they need mortal hands and mortal voices, and mine happen to fit. If that makes me a tool, fine. A hammer doesn't ask the carpenter's motives. It just builds the house."

"And if the house is a cage?"

"Then I'll break it from inside." Yara rolled the covenant-scroll with careful, reverent fingers. "Two shrines left, Tam. Two nights. After tomorrow, the covenant is complete and the faithful are shielded, and whatever you suspect, whatever you've dug up — the city will be safer than it was. Can your heresy wait until then?"

Tam looked at him for a long time. At the gray cat watching from the table's edge with those ancient, patient eyes. At the scroll grown from a thousand-year-old script that changed when unobserved.

"It can wait," Tam said at last. "Doubt keeps. That's the thing about doubt — unlike faith, it doesn't spoil."

Yara almost smiled. He gathered the scroll, whistled softly to the cat, and climbed the ladder toward the trapdoor and the night beyond — toward the eighth shrine, and the crowds, and the patrols that grew thicker every dusk.

At the top of the ladder he paused. "Thank you," he said, not turning around. "For saying it. Even if you're wrong. Especially if you're wrong. Someone should."

Then he was gone, and the trapdoor thumped, and the rookery settled into its nighttime murmur of wings and small dreams.

* * *

Tam sat alone a while, then took down his ledger and wrote by candlelight, ciphering quickly:

*Ninth day. He won't hear it. Love has closed his ears — love of purpose, love of the priestess, love of the gray beast that chose him. Note: the cat. It was there before the shrines woke. It found him years ago. Ask: who sent it, and when, and why a fragment of divinity would waste itself on a charm-seller unless the charm-seller was already part of the plan.*

He paused, quill hovering.

Then he added:

*If I'm wrong, may I be forgiven for poisoning a good man's peace. If I'm right — then someone must be standing outside the temple when it falls, taking names.*

He blotted the page, and as he did, one of the rookery pigeons — a ragged bird he'd nursed through a broken wing two winters past — dropped from its perch and landed on the table, head cocked, regarding him with one orange eye.

"Well?" Tam said dryly. "Out with it. You lot hear everything in this city. Did you see who lit the fuses?"

The pigeon cooed. It stepped once, twice across the ledger, and pecked — precisely, deliberately — at the word he had just written.

*Cat.*

Tam went very still.

The bird looked at him, and in its small orange eye was something old and sorry, and it said — in the rusted, whistling voice of a creature speaking human words for the first and perhaps only time in its life:

"*She was here first.*"

Then it ruffled its feathers, hopped to the windowsill, and flew out into the dark over Tanner's Row, leaving Tam alone with his candle, his cipher, and the sudden, terrible certainty that his heresy was not heresy at all —

— and that somewhere in the city, on the road to the eighth shrine, Yara Blackwood was walking straight into the arms of whatever had been waiting for him all along.
