## 11. Serah's Wound

The report reached Captain Serah Voss at the fourth bell of the morning, folded twice and sealed with the wax of a junior clerk who had clearly been crying when he wrote it.

*Incident at the Shrine of the Twin Rivers, Saltrow Ward. Child: Pellin Marsh, age six. Condition on arrival: grey fever, final stage. Attending physician had withdrawn care. Family gathered for the death-watch. At the third bell past midnight, witnesses report light from within the sealed shrine. The child walked out at dawn.*

Voss read it three times. Then she read it a fourth time, slowly, the way she read everything, because reading twice was for people who trusted their eyes and she did not trust anything anymore, least of all her own.

The paper trembled. Not in her hand — in itself, a fine shiver through the fibers, as though the words were cold. She set it flat on her desk and pressed her palm against it until it stilled.

"Captain?" Corporal Denny Hask stood in her doorway, helmet under his arm, his round face arranged in the careful blankness the Order taught its recruits and never quite managed to stamp into them. "You've seen the Saltrow report, then."

"I've seen it."

"The men are asking questions."

"The men should be sweeping the rookery district like I ordered yesterday." She stood, took her coat from the peg — iron-grey wool, the Seal embroidered at the collar in thread that caught no light — and settled it across her shoulders like armor, which was what it was. "Tell them questions are a luxury. We have a job."

"Yes, Captain." He didn't move. He was twenty-three and he had joined the Order because his father had, and he believed in it the way you believe in weather — without examination, without choice. It made him honest in ways that were sometimes unbearable. "It's just — my sister knew that family. The Marshes. The boy was dying, Captain. Everyone said so. The physician said so. And now he's—"

"Walking around. I know what the report says, Corporal."

"And the shrine's been sealed since the Purification. Seven years. There's no one in there. There couldn't be anyone in there."

"No," Voss agreed quietly. "There couldn't be."

She took the report off her desk and folded it into her coat, against her heart, where she kept the things she hadn't decided how to hate yet.

* * *

Saltrow Ward smelled of fish brine and tar and the particular sourness of poverty that no amount of sea wind could scour away. The streets narrowed as they descended toward the water, houses leaning together overhead like conspirators, and by the time they reached the Shrine of the Twin Rivers the sun had gone behind cloud and the whole ward seemed to hold its breath.

The shrine had once been beautiful. Voss remembered it from before — a low dome of white stone, twin channels cut into the floor so that river water ran through the nave and out to the harbor, carved gods standing hip-deep in the flow. After the Purification the Order had bricked the doors, poured the channels full of mortar, and left the building to the gulls. Seven years of salt air had streaked the white stone grey. The seal-stamp on the brickwork was still legible: *BY DECREE OF THE IRON SEAL, THIS PLACE IS CLOSED TO PRAYER.*

A crowd had gathered outside it anyway. That was the first wrong thing.

They weren't rioting. They weren't chanting or burning effigies or doing any of the loud, punishable things that would have let her disperse them with a clear conscience. They were simply *standing*, forty or fifty of them, fishermen and net-menders and widows, faces turned up toward the bricked door with an expression Voss recognized and had spent her career training herself not to see.

Hope. Raw, unlicensed hope.

"Back up," Hask said beside her, and his voice cracked on the second word. "Give the captain room."

They backed up. They even looked ashamed of it, hoods coming down, shoulders curving, the crowd folding inward like something trying not to be noticed. And still they didn't leave.

Voss walked to the brickwork. Up close she could see the mortar had not been touched — no chisel marks, no powder, no crack. Sealed from the inside was impossible; sealed from the outside was impossible too, because her own sappers had inspected every closed shrine in Saltrow only last month. The wall was exactly as the Iron Seal had built it.

Except.

There, at the base of the door, where the old threshold stone met the brick — the mortar had softened. Not broken. Softened, the way candle wax softens, slumping outward in slow grey drips that had hardened again mid-fall. As if something inside had breathed heat against the wall for a long time. As if the wall had begun, very gently, to melt.

"Captain," said a voice behind her. "Captain Voss."

The child's mother. Voss knew her distantly — Ada Marsh, mended nets two piers down, husband lost to a trawler accident four years back. She came forward with her hands wringing each other and her chin up, which took courage, because approaching an Order officer was an act most people in Saltrow performed only under compulsion.

"You're here about my Pellin."

"I'm here about the shrine."

"He's all right." Ada Marsh said it like a woman defending a fortress with her body. "He's weak yet, but he ate bread this morning. Real bread, and keeped it down. The fever broke between midnight and dawn, Captain, and I watched it break. I sat by him and I watched the sweat come, and I watched the grey go out of his lips, and I—" Her voice snagged. "I know what your books say. I know magic's outlawed and shrines are dead and prayers go nowhere. But I was *there*. Whatever your books say happened, it isn't what happened."

"What did happen?"

"I prayed." Simple. Terrible. "I haven't prayed since they closed the shrines. Seven years I kept my mouth shut like a good citizen. But my boy was dying and there was nowhere else to put it, so I put it in the wall. I put my hands on the bricks and I asked — I didn't ask any god in particular, Captain, I don't remember their names, nobody remembers their names — I just asked whoever used to live here. Please. Please. And the bricks went warm under my hands. Warm as skin." Her hands had stopped wringing. They hung open now at her sides, palms out, as if they still expected warmth. "And then the light came."

"Describe the light."

Ada Marsh frowned, searching. "Amber," she said finally. "Like — like lamplight through honey. Like the color of the river at evening when the sun goes low. And it wasn't frightening. That's the thing I can't make fit, Captain. Everything the Order ever told us said magic is a knife in the dark. But this wasn't a knife. This was somebody *opening a door*."

Behind her, the crowd murmured agreement — a low sound, many-voiced, like the sea deciding something.

Voss looked at the melted mortar. She thought: *I could arrest this woman.* Disturbing the peace, unlawful assembly, remembrance of proscribed faiths — she could find a charge; finding charges was the one miracle the Iron Seal performed reliably. She thought: *and tomorrow there will be another shrine, and another mother, and I cannot arrest the tide.*

"Go home," she said instead. "All of you. If anyone asks, the Order investigated and found nothing."

"Did you?" Ada Marsh asked. "Find nothing?"

Voss held the woman's gaze and discovered she could not perform the lie. "Investigate the shrine," she said, and turned away.

* * *

They entered through the roof — a maintenance hatch the Purification crews had overlooked, rusted but willing — and dropped into the dark of the nave on ropes, lanterns swinging.

Inside, the Shrine of the Twin Rivers was a tomb that had forgotten it was a tomb. Silt had drifted in through broken vents and lay in pale dunes across the floor. The mortared channels stood like scars. The statues of the twin river gods — Voss dredged the names up from childhood catechism, before the catechisms burned: *Ashen-Vey of the deep current, Nethra of the shallow light* — loomed over the flooded dark, their features worn smooth by salt wind, their faces neither kind nor cruel. Just old. Old the way mountains are old, indifferent as stone is supposed to be indifferent.

Except the dunes of silt had been disturbed.

Hask saw it at the same moment she did. "Captain — look."

Footprints. Small ones. A child's bare feet, leading from the base of Ashen-Vey's statue — from the exact spot where Ada Marsh must have pressed her hands to the outer wall — across the nave, through silt that should have swallowed and held such prints for years in this sealed air. The prints were crisp. Fresh. And they led directly to the melted doorway, where they stopped, because beyond it there was nothing but brick.

"There's no way in," Hask said. His lantern shook. "Captain, there's no way *in*. How does a sick child get in here to walk out again? How does anyone—"

"Corporal."

"Yes, Captain."

"Breathe."

He breathed. She made him breathe twice more, because his breathing was the only sound in the shrine besides the drip of water, and because if she stopped listening to it she would hear the other sound — the one underneath the dripping, beneath the range of ordinary hearing, a hum she felt in her back teeth more than heard. A hum like a held note. Like something enormous keeping very still.

She raised her lantern to Ashen-Vey's face.

The statue's eye was open.

Statues do not close their eyes. Statues are carved with eyes open or shut and remain so forever; every initiate learns this in their first year, along with the composition of seal-ink and the proper way to burn a grimoire. She had inspected this statue herself, eleven months ago, during the routine survey of closed sites. Its eyes had been blank carved ovals, pupils uncut, the smooth sightlessness of all sanctioned iconography.

Now the left eye was open. Cut clean, pupil and iris and lid, in stone that showed no chisel work, no dust of carving, no debris at its feet. As if the eye had simply *decided*, sometime in the night, to exist.

And it was looking at her.

Not in her direction. At her. With the patient, bottomless attention of a thing that has waited seven years in the dark and knows precisely who has come to stand in its light.

"Captain." Hask's voice had gone thin. "Captain, we should—"

"We should document it." Her own voice came out level, clipped, professional — a small miracle of engineering performed over a fault line. "Photographs, sketches, silt samples. You'll take the measurements, I'll write the narrative. Standard procedure for site irregularities."

"It's a *statue*, Captain. Statues don't—"

"Hask." She turned and looked at him fully, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop. "In five minutes you're going to want to tell yourself you imagined this. In an hour you're going to succeed. By tonight you'll have half-convinced yourself it was shadow and lantern-oil and nerves, and in a month you'll believe it completely, and you'll sleep fine, and someday you'll tell your children the story of the strange thing you almost saw once." She held his gaze. "Don't. Whatever this is — and I don't yet know what this is — it is real, and pretending otherwise is how good soldiers end up on the wrong side of history. Do you understand me?"

He stared at her. Then, slowly, he nodded, and lifted his lantern higher, and made himself look at the open eye, and wrote down what he saw.

That, Voss thought, was the bravest thing she had witnessed in the Saltrow Ward in seven years of service. Braver than the mother. Braver than the child walking out of a tomb. Because it was done knowing the cost.

She understood, standing in the drowned nave with the hum rising through the soles of her boots, that the cost would come due for her too. Soon.

* * *

The Order of the Iron Seal kept its archives in the Undercroft of the Bastion Hall: nine floors of iron shelving descending below the waterline, holding the confiscated memory of a nation. Grimoires in lead-lined cases. Prayer wheels with their spokes filed off. God-names reduced to catalog numbers. Serah Voss had spent the first three years of her command here, cross-indexing seizures, and she knew its geography the way other officers knew taverns.

She came alone, after midnight, with a lamp and a key and the Saltrow photographs in her coat.

*ASHEN-VEY,* said the catalog card, in her own handwriting, dated four years back. *River-god, deep current. Cult suppressed pre-Purification. Iconography: sealed sites 12 through 19. Threat assessment: dormant/inert.* She pulled the file. Inside were the standard forms, the seizure inventories, the survey reports — hers among them, eleven months old, confirming inert status.

But paperclipped to the back, misfiled where only someone looking for misfilings would find it, was a single sheet she had never seen before. Older paper. An older hand.

*A Note on the Culling, circa Year One of the Seal.*

*It is the judgment of this office that the suppression of the god-cults cannot succeed by statute alone. Gods do not require believers; believers require gods, and the channel between runs through certain bloodlines — the speakers, the beast-talkers, those born able to hear what the Seal forbids. Where the shrines are bricked, the speakers remain. Kill the speakers and the channel silences for a generation. Let them breed and every sealed door in Vell becomes a door indeed.*

*Recommendation: quiet cull. No proclamations. Families scattered, records burned, survivors unaware of why. The work will be deniable by design.*

Below it, in the margin, in different ink: *Approved. — First Seal.*

Voss stood in the cold of the Undercroft with the page trembling in her hand — her hand, this time, unmistakably her own.

Seven years ago, a woman named Sefa Blackwood had died in the Lantern District. Voss remembered the case because it had been her first assignment out of training: a charm-seller, a beast-speaker, executed under emergency provisions with paperwork so irregular that Voss had queried it and been told, by a captain since promoted out of existence, to lose the query. She had lost it. She had been twenty-four and obedient and she had lost it, and she had told herself for seven years that law was law and orders were orders and the irregularities meant nothing.

The cull had meant something. The cull had a name, and the name was written in her own institution's hand, approved at the top, and it had murdered a charm-seller's mother while her son watched.

And now the shrines were waking. Now the sealed doors were becoming doors indeed. Now a child walked out of a tomb and a god opened its eye and looked at *her*, specifically, deliberately, as though it knew exactly whose signature sat at the bottom of seven years of quiet murders.

She thought of the interrogations — the charm-sellers lined up in the Bastion yard, the young man with the careful hands and the luck-charms priced too honestly, Blackwood, Yara Blackwood, who had lied to her so smoothly and whose lie she had marked and followed and hunted through three districts while the city's dead gods rose around her like water through floorboards. She had been so certain of him. So certain the rot came from outside the walls, from smuggled charms and criminal tongues, from people like him.

The rot had come from inside. From the first seal down. From her.

Voss sat down on the archive steps, in the cold, under the iron shelves, and did something she had not done since she was a child: she prayed. Not to any god — she didn't remember their names either. She prayed the way Ada Marsh had prayed, into the dark, to whoever used to listen.

*If you can hear me,* she thought, *tell me what I'm supposed to do now.*

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Far above, in the waking city, a bell began to ring — not the Order's alarm, not any sanctioned bell. It came from the direction of the temple district, thin and silver and impossibly old, a sound the Undercroft should never have heard, ringing from a tower that had been silent seven years. One bell. Then a second, answering from the river wards. Then a third.

Across Vell, the shrines were calling to one another.

Serah Voss stood up, folded the confession into her coat against her heart, beside the Saltrow report, and went to find the one man in the city who might understand what was happening — the liar with the careful hands, whose mother's murder she carried proof of in her pocket.

By dawn, she told herself, she would know whether she was going to arrest Yara Blackwood or beg his forgiveness.
