## 12. Nine Shrines, Nine Nights

The first shrine woke in the Saltmarket on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning half the district had forgotten how to be afraid.

Yara stood in the crowd outside the old brine-god's alcove — a squat stone face set into the wall of a shuttered tannery, its mouth crusted with seven years of soot and pigeon filth — and watched a fishwife press her palm to its cheek and weep without shame. The stone was warm. He could feel it from ten feet away, a low hum like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall: *awake, awake, awake.*

"You did this," Maren murmured beside him, hood up, hands folded in the posture of any pious widow at prayer. Only her eyes moved, cataloguing the crowd, the rooftops, the two Order outriders already cantering down Brine Street with their seal-blades drawn.

"I carried it," Yara said. "It woke itself."

"That distinction will not save you." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse.

The cat sat on his shoulder, tail curled around his neck like a scarf of smoke, and said in the old tongue, *They are coming. Walk, don't run. Running is a confession.*

He walked. It was the hardest thing he'd done since his mother's funeral — turning his back on the fishwife's tears, on the alcove where the stone god's eyes had opened for the first time in living memory, on the crowd beginning to kneel in the mud of the Saltmarket — and strolled away at a market-day amble while iron-shod hooves rang closer behind him. A rat darted along the gutter at his heel, and he heard it clearly, painfully clearly: *the tall ones in grey are asking names, asking names, asking names.* He kept walking. He did not answer. Answering was also a confession.

That night, in Tam Ossler's rookery above the dye-works, he counted on his fingers like a child learning sums. One shrine. Eight nights left.

*Seven shrines,* the cat corrected from the windowsill, washing one black paw. *Ashen-Vey's covenant asks nine. But two have already begun to wake on their own — the god under the Granary Steps stirred three days ago, and the drowned one in the canal district has been dreaming loudly enough that the eels have fled the eastern locks.*

"Then I'm ahead," Yara said.

*You are behind,* the cat said, and its green eyes caught the lamplight flat and ancient. *Waking is not the same as carrying. A shrine may stir for a century and never open its eye. You must speak the covenant into each one before the ninth night dawns, or everything Ashen-Vey has promised — the shielding, the waking, all of it — curdles. And what curdles does not go quietly.*

Tam looked up from his workbench, where he was grinding something that smelled like burnt sugar and lightning. "Cheerful guest you've got."

"She's honest," Yara said.

"Honest things usually are cheerful about other people's funerals." Tam blew a strand of white hair out of his face. "Get some sleep, charm-seller. Tomorrow you start being a prophet, and prophets need their beauty rest more than most."

* * *

The second shrine was in the Weavers' Row, hidden behind a loom-shop's false wall, and the third stood in plain sight in Copperlane Square disguised as a fountain — or rather, the fountain had been built over it, and when Yara spoke the covenant's first words into the drain, the water rose in a slow spiral and every coin at the bottom turned face-up, and the crowd that gathered took less than an hour to become a congregation.

By the fourth night, the Order knew his pattern.

He felt them before he saw them — the horses told him, tossing their heads and stamping, *grey riders, grey riders, they circle the square like hawks* — and he slipped out through a cooper's yard while Captain Voss's patrol sealed the streets around Copperlane's shrine. From a rooftop he watched her stand before the newly woken god, her lantern raised, her face lit gold and hard as a coin. She didn't smash it. That surprised him. She stood there a long time, reading the covenant's words as they glowed in the stone, and then she ordered her soldiers to seal the square rather than deface it, and Yara thought: *she's studying us now, not hunting us,* and he couldn't decide if that was better or infinitely worse.

"The captain is the dangerous kind," Maren said later, binding a cut on Yara's palm where a shard of waking stone had nicked him. "The kind who thinks."

"Most people think."

"No. Most people repeat. Thinking is rare, and it always ends in either conversion or obsession." She tied off the bandage. "Pray she converts."

The cat, draped across the rafters like a shadow that had learned to purr, said nothing. It had been saying nothing all day, and its silences were starting to have texture — a weight to them, a direction. When Yara finally climbed up to ask what was wrong, it looked at him with those flat green eyes and said only:

*You spoke your mother's name to the third shrine.*

"It seemed right. Ashen-Vey asked for a true word to anchor each covenant—"

*I know what it asked.* The cat's tail flicked once, precisely, like a metronome winding down. *I am asking why that name. Why hers, of all names, comes to your tongue when the old words want blood or breath or memory.*

"Because she died for this." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Because whatever's waking in this city, she paid for it before any of us knew there was anything to pay for. If the gods are listening again, then let them hear who listened first."

The cat held his gaze for a long moment. Something moved behind the green — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Grief wearing recognition's coat.

*Yes,* it said at last. *Let them hear.* And it leapt down into the dark and was gone until dawn.

* * *

The fifth shrine nearly killed him.

It lay in the Undercroft beneath the Chandlers' Guildhall, a place the rats had warned him about in shrieking fragments — *cold, cold, the tall stone drinks the light, do not go, do not go* — and the rats were right. The god down there was an old one, older than Vell's walls, carved in a style no living mason could name, and when Yara spoke the covenant to it, it did not glow. It *inhaled*. The whole chamber drew breath, torches bending toward the stone, dust streaming off the floor in silver threads, and for one vertiginous moment Yara felt himself being pulled after the air, felt the god considering him the way a man considers whether to swallow or spit.

Then the cat landed between him and the altar, arched its back, and made a sound no housecat should ever make — a sound like a temple bell cracking, like the first syllable of a word too large for any throat.

The god exhaled. The torches straightened. And in the sudden ordinary dark, the cat sat down and began washing its paw as though nothing had happened, though its ears stayed flat a moment longer than casual.

"What," Yara said, when he could breathe again, "was that."

*A dialect,* the cat said. *Of a language neither of us should speak here. Come. The guild guards change shift at the bell, and you still smell like a man who has seen something he shouldn't have. Guards can smell that too.*

But on the ladder up, gripping rungs slick with candle-grease, Yara heard the cat say something else, very quietly, almost to itself — in the old tongue, but a register of it he'd never heard, deep and formal, the way his mother used to sing when she thought he was asleep:

*...as it was before the sealing, so shall it be after...*

And when he asked what it meant, the cat said, *Nothing. A fragment. Old prayers stick in the teeth.*

* * *

Sixth night: the Ferryman's shrine at the river stairs, where the covenant woke in the pilings themselves and the whole river ran briefly upstream, and a hundred boatmen fell silent as the water stood on end like a held breath. Seventh: the glass-blower's quarter, where a god embedded in a kiln-wall opened its eye in a flood of orange light and every window in the street sang one clear note, and the Order arrived in force — four wagons, twenty soldiers, Voss herself — and found not a riot but a street full of kneeling artisans who would not move, would not speak, would only look at her with the terrible patient faces of people who have finally been answered after years of silence.

Yara watched from the rookery's far edge, crow-shadowed, and heard the crows reporting in their clipped, gossiping way: *the grey captain stands, the grey captain reads, the grey captain does not draw, the grey captain looks at the sky, why does the grey captain look at the sky.*

"Why does she look at the sky?" Yara asked aloud.

"Because miracles don't fit in ledgers," Tam said, coming up behind him with two cups of something steaming and illegal. "I've been thinking about that."

"You're always thinking about something."

"This one matters." Tam handed him a cup. "Nine shrines, nine nights, one speaker, right when the Order's grip is tightest, right when the city's thirst is deepest. Doesn't that strike you as convenient? Gods are weather, boy. Weather doesn't keep appointments."

"Ashen-Vey gave terms. Terms mean negotiation. Negotiation means someone's home."

"Or someone wants you to believe someone's home." Tam's eyes, sharp and kind, held his. "I'm not saying stop. I'm saying keep one eye on the door even while you pray."

That night the cat came back smelling of rain and somewhere deeper than rain — stone dust, candle smoke, the mineral ghost of the catacombs — and when Yara asked where it had been, it said, *Visiting,* and would say nothing more. But it slept that night not at the foot of his bed as it always had, but near his head, facing the door, and once, in the small hours, he woke to find it watching him with an expression he could only call *guilty*, which was absurd, because cats cannot be guilty, and because what did it possibly have to be guilty of?

* * *

The eighth shrine stood in the Poor Quarter, in a burial ground so old the graves had become geography — soft hills, leaning stones, children using dead men's markers for hopscotch. Yara spoke the covenant at moonrise, and the whole graveyard breathed, and the dead did not rise but the grass did, silver-green, growing in visible ripples outward from the central stone like the rings of a dropped coin, and the Poor Quarter came out of its tenements in hundreds, barefoot in the frost, and no one spoke, and no one needed to.

Maren wept openly. Yara had never seen her weep. It frightened him more than the Undercroft god had.

"One more," she whispered. "One more and it's done. Nine days, Yara. Seven years I waited, and you've done it in eight nights."

"Almost," he said, and the word tasted strange, because some part of him — the part that had grown up memorizing which floorboards creaked, which neighbors reported, which smiles concealed denunciations — that part had noticed something. The crowds were too ready. The shrines woke too completely. Every district, every night, exactly where he was meant to be, exactly when the faithful were gathered to see.

Weather doesn't keep appointments.

He pushed the thought down where he kept his mother's last scream, and smiled at Maren, and said, "Where's the ninth?"

She told him. And the cat, sitting in the shadow of a leaning gravestone, went very still — stiller than stillness, stiller than stone — and its green eyes fixed on Yara with an intensity that was almost pain.

*Not there,* the cat said softly. *Anywhere but there.*

"Why not?"

The cat looked away. For the first time in all the nights of the covenant, in all the years of their strange companionship, it looked away from him.

*Because the last shrine is the great one,* it said. *The shattered one. Your mother's shrine. And what waits inside it has been waiting to meet you since before you were born.*

Above the Poor Quarter, past the rooftops and the frost and the sleeping city, the distant silhouette of the broken great shrine stood against the stars like a wound in the skyline — and Yara felt, for the first time, that instead of carrying the covenant to it, he had been carried, all nine nights, along a road someone else had drawn.

The cat would not meet his eyes. And tomorrow was the ninth night.
