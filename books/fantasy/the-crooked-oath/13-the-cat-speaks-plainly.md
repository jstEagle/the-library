## 13. The Cat Speaks Plainly

The rookery smelled of feathers and old smoke and Tam Ossler's terrible cooking. Yara sat on a crate beneath the eaves of the exiled mage's attic, watching the fourth shrine of his nine burn low in its brazier of coals, and the cat watched him watch it.

It had been three days since Ashen-Vey's voice first poured through him like molten iron. Three days since he'd carried the covenant to the shrine in the Tanner's Quarter while a crowd wept and an Order patrol turned the corner too late. Two more shrines since. Five down. Four to go. His hands still trembled when he thought about it — not from fear anymore, but from something worse. From how *good* it had felt.

"You're staring," he said to the cat.

The cat sat on the windowsill where it always sat, black as spilled ink with one white paw, amber eyes catching the coal-light. It had followed him for eleven years. Through his mother's funeral that no one attended because everyone was afraid to attend it. Through the lean years when charm-selling barely bought bread. Through every night he'd lain awake listening to the city's animals murmur in their sleep and hated himself for understanding them.

"I said you're staring."

*I heard you,* said the cat.

Yara went very still.

In eleven years, the cat had never spoken to him. Not once. Not in the loose animal-speech of hunger and warmth and *move over, this sill is mine* that all beasts used. The cat had only ever looked at him, and purred at him, and followed him, and once — the night they buried his mother — pressed its small warm weight against his throat like a hand holding shut a wound.

Now it spoke, and its voice was not a cat's voice at all. It was layered, the way deep water is layered — a cat's purr on top, and beneath it something vast and slow and feminine, something that made the coals gutter and the rooks on the roofline fall silent all at once.

"Say that again," Yara whispered.

*You heard me the first time.* The cat stepped down from the sill, unhurried, and crossed the floorboards toward him. *You have always heard me, Yara Blackwood. You simply never asked.*

"Asked what?"

*Anything.* It sat before him, tail curled around its feet, and looked up with eyes that were suddenly, terribly old. *You never asked why I stayed. You never asked why I found you. You never asked what I am. Humans are strange that way. You will accept a miracle sleeping on your windowsill for a decade rather than risk asking it a question.*

His mouth had gone dry. "What are you?"

The cat closed its eyes. When it opened them, the amber had gone pale as moonstone, and the light in the attic bent slightly toward it, the way water bends around a stone.

*I am Nethra.*

The name landed in Yara's chest like a dropped anvil. Nethra — the Veiled Mother, the goddess of thresholds and small mercies, whose shrine stood crumbling behind the fish market, whose face had been chiseled off her statues by the Order of the Iron Seal before Yara was born. One of the dead gods. One of the gods who were waking.

"That's not possible," he said. "Nethra is — the shrines are just now stirring. Maren said—"

*Maren knows fragments,* the cat said, with something like contempt. *Maren dug up bones and read old rites and called it knowledge. I am not a fragment of ritual, child. I am a fragment of a goddess. There is a difference, and it matters very much to you, though you do not yet know why.*

"Then tell me why."

The cat began to wash one paw, slowly, deliberately — and the gesture was so ordinary, so utterly feline, that it was somehow more frightening than the voice. As if the goddess wore the cat the way he wore his charm-seller's shawl: as a disguise.

*Do you remember the night your mother died?*

"Don't." The word came out cracked.

*You were fourteen. You came home late because you'd been talking to the miller's dogs about a fox that was raiding his hens — you wanted to broker peace between them, because even then you could not bear to see creatures fight when you might stand between. And you came home to find the Iron Seal already there. Four of them. And your mother standing in the doorway of your little house with her arms out, keeping them from going inside, from finding you, because she knew what you were and they knew what she was.*

"Stop."

*They burned her in the street, Yara. In front of you. And do you remember what you did?*

He did. He remembered it the way you remembered a bone you'd broken — always there, aching when the weather changed. He had hidden in the neighbor's woodpile with the cat pressed against his chest, and he had listened to his mother scream, and he had done nothing. He had held a cat and done nothing.

And afterward, half-mad with grief, he had sworn his crooked oath — kneeling in the ash-stink of the street, whispering into the dark: *I will never speak with beasts again. Never. Let me be deaf to them. Let me be safe. Let me be small.* An oath spoken to no god, because the gods were dead. An oath that had lasted exactly as long as it took a beggar to pray at a dead shrine and a stone eye to open.

*You swore your oath to the darkness,* the cat said softly, *and the darkness gave you back to me. Because I was the one listening.*

Yara stood up so fast the crate tipped over. "No. No, you don't get to — you don't get to make my mother's death about *you*. She died because the Order—"

*She died because the Order was hunting speakers,* the cat said, and its voice lost all softness. *And the Order was hunting speakers because someone told them where to look. Sit down, Yara.*

"I won't—"

*Sit down.* The word was not loud but it went through him like a hook through the gills, and his knees folded, and he was sitting on the floor among the feathers without any memory of choosing to sit. The rooks shifted uneasily on the roofline. Somewhere below, Tam dropped a pot and cursed.

The cat padded closer. Close enough to touch. Yara didn't touch it. He wasn't sure he ever would again.

*Eleven years ago,* it said, *I was dying. What remains of me after the Severance — after your Order's founders cut the gods out of the world — is small. A splinter of the Veiled Mother, wearing fur because fur is easy to hide inside. I should have faded. Instead I felt something I had not felt in two hundred years. I felt a child in Vell speaking to dogs, and the dogs answering, and the speech between them clean and strong and old — the old tongue, the tongue the world spoke before men built walls against it.*

"The miller's dogs," Yara said numbly. "That was — I was nine."

*Nine years old,* the cat agreed, *and already stronger than any speaker born in a century. So I came to you. I let you heal my broken leg — do you remember? You set the bone with splints from a broom handle and told no one, because you already understood that some things must be secret. And I stayed. I stayed because I needed a vessel, Yara. I stayed because the gods are waking, and when they wake fully, they will need a speaker to carry their covenants — and I wanted that speaker bound to me before any other power claimed him.*

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well. Yara listened to them hit bottom, one after another.

"A vessel," he repeated.

*Yes.*

"You chose me. Eleven years ago. Before the shrines woke. Before Maren. Before any of it. You chose me the way — the way a farmer chooses a field."

The cat's tail flicked, once. *I chose you the way a drowning woman chooses a rope. Is that so different?*

"It's different if the rope gets a say!"

For a long moment neither of them moved. The coals ticked. Above them, through the slanted skylight, the first stars were coming out over Vell, and Yara thought absurdly of the four shrines still waiting, of the crowds gathering, of Ashen-Vey's covenant burning in his chest like a swallowed coal, and of how every step of his journey here had been walked beside this creature. This companion. This *handler*.

"Tell me the rest," he said quietly. "Tell me about my mother. All of it. If you've decided I'm your vessel, then I'm owed the truth, and you know it."

Something moved across the goddess-fragment's face — or perhaps it was only the coal-light. But when it spoke again, the vastness in its voice had thinned, and underneath it Yara heard something almost like shame.

*Your mother was a speaker too. Weaker than you — most are. She could calm frightened animals, that was all. She hid it her whole life, as you hid yours. But seven years ago, the Order began a new campaign. Quietly. Not the public burnings — those breed sympathy. This was a culling. Lists. Names. They had learned — the Seal's founders had always known — that speakers matter. That when the gods stir, it is a speaker who hears them first, and a speaker the gods reach for. Your Order calls you abominations. I promise you, they fear you for a better reason than piety.*

"They had lists," Yara said. "Of speakers."

*Of every speaker in Vell. And your mother's name was on the list long before she died. She lived three years longer than she should have, because she was careful, and because — * the cat paused, and the pause was the worst thing yet — *because I was watching over her house. I kept the rats from carrying rumors to the Order's informants. I kept the crows from repeating what they'd seen. Small mercies. Threshold-work. My oldest craft.*

"But they came anyway."

*They came anyway. Because the culling was accelerating, and because someone in the Order had begun to understand what was coming — what is coming now — and wanted every speaker in Vell either dead or registered before the shrines woke. Your mother was not random, Yara. She was not unlucky. She was harvested. They killed her the way a man picks fruit before the frost.*

Yara's hands had curled into fists without his permission, nails biting into palms hard enough to sting. He looked down at them and saw, with strange clarity, that they were shaking — and that under the shaking, under the grief and the fury, there was a colder thing taking shape. A question.

"Who," he said. "Who gave the Order its list? Speakers hide. We're careful. My mother was careful for forty years. Someone *found* her. Someone found all of them."

The cat looked at him for a long time. Outside, a rook croaked and another answered, and somewhere in the streets below a dog began to bark — and Yara realized distantly that he could hear the dog's words now, plain as speech, *strangers, strangers, strangers at the corner*, and that his gift had grown so loud since the shrines woke that he couldn't have silenced it if he tried.

*There are records,* the cat said at last. *Seizure records. The Order writes everything down; it is their religion, paper and seals and names. The name of the officer who compiled the speaker-lists seven years ago exists. I have known it for some time. I did not tell you because—*

"Because you were managing me," Yara said. "Because a vessel that's angry makes mistakes."

*Because a vessel that is angry walks into Vell's heart alone and dies,* the cat snapped, and for one instant the whole attic flared with light that cast no shadows, and every rook on the roofline screamed at once. Then it was gone, and the cat was small again, and tired-seeming, and old. *Forgive me. I have worn this shape so long I forget how much of the rest of me remains. Yes, Yara. I managed you. I guided you. I let Maren find you because she had the rites I lacked and the faith I have lost, and I let Ashen-Vey speak through you because the covenant must be carried and you are the only carrier left alive. I have spent eleven years arranging your life the way a gardener arranges a trellis. And I will not apologize for it, because everything I arranged kept you breathing. But I am telling you now — all of it — because you asked, and because you deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else.*

"From anyone else," Yara echoed. "Meaning there's more. Meaning Maren—"

*Maren believes she woke the gods,* the cat said. *Maren believes this was her design, her great work, seven years in the making. And she is not entirely wrong. But ask yourself, Yara — ask yourself what a priestess needs to wake a sleeping god. Rites? Bones? Candles? No. She needs a listener. She needs ears in the dark. She needed a beast-speaker, and there was only one in Vell, and I led her to you as surely as I led myself.*

The floor seemed to tilt. Yara gripped the edge of the overturned crate.

"Maren didn't find me by accident."

*Nothing about you has been an accident since you were nine years old,* said Nethra, in the voice of a cat, on the floor of a mage's rookery, in a city that had outlawed miracles and was drowning in them. *I am sorry. That is the truth of it, entire. Now decide what you will do with it — because the fifth shrine waits tomorrow night, and Ashen-Vey grows impatient, and I can smell the Iron Seal on the wind from here. Captain Voss has found Maren's name. She is coming, Yara. Perhaps sooner than any of us planned for.*

Above the skylight, the rooks had gone silent — all of them, every bird on every rooftop in sight, facing the same direction, toward the temple district, toward the river, toward the sound — very faint, very far away — of iron boots on cobblestones.

Yara looked at the cat that was a goddess that had been his only friend for eleven years, and felt his whole life rearrange itself around the shape of a lie he had loved.

"Then we run," he said. "And along the way, you're going to give me that name. The one who wrote the lists."

The cat's moonstone eyes held his.

*You may not thank me for it,* Nethra said. *It belongs to someone you trust.*
