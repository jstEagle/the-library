## 14. Maren's Confession

Maren had known this moment would come for seven years. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the catacombs, in the years when the silence of the shrines was the loudest sound in Vell — rehearsed it the way soldiers rehearse dying: thoroughly, uselessly, and with no true belief it would ever arrive.

Now Tam Ossler stood in the doorway of the rookery with a handful of paper, and the rehearsal meant nothing at all.

"Sit down," Tam said.

The rookery smelled of feathers and lamp oil and the sour tang of Tam's alchemical experiments, and through the slatted windows the evening light came in bars, laying stripes across the floor like a cage built out of nothing but brightness. Yara was not there. He had gone to the third shrine on his covenant-walk with the cat riding his shoulder like a small god of judgment, and Maren had stayed behind because someone had to tend Ashen-Vey's altar-fire, and because — she understood now, watching Tam's face — she had wanted one more hour before the end.

"I'm standing," Maren said.

"Sit down, or I'll say this standing, and you'll want to be sitting."

She sat. Her knees decided for her, which was answer enough.

Tam laid the papers on the table between them, one by one, with the deliberation of a dealer laying out a losing hand. Seven years of seizure records copied from the Order's archive — Tam had friends in the paper-rooms, friends who owed favors that could not be asked twice. Seven years of names. And among them, written in a hand Maren had not used since she buried her old name beneath her new one:

*Maren Solane, acolyte of the Rite of Waking. Assisted in the rite of first-kindling, shrine of the Twin Brothers. Status: fled.*

"You were never just hiding," Tam said. "You were working."

* * *

Seven years ago, the gods had not been dead. That was the lie everyone believed, priests and Order-men alike — that the Iron Seal had killed them, that sealing the shrines and burning the rites and outlawing every whispered prayer had starved the old powers into silence. Dead things stayed dead. The Seal had made a graveyard of Vell and called it peace.

But Maren had been an acolyte in the last living temple, and she knew what the elders had known and never said aloud: the gods had not died. They had *withdrawn*. Pulled back behind the skin of the world like a hand withdrawing from a flame, waiting for the fire to pass. Waiting for the age of iron to exhaust itself. They could sleep a hundred years or a thousand; divinity did not measure time the way flesh did.

And so the question that had eaten Maren alive, night after night in the sealed catacombs, was not *how do we bring them back*. It was *when will they come back on their own* — and whether "on their own" meant in her lifetime, or her grandchildren's, or never, because withdrawn gods needed something to wake *to*. A reason. A door. A voice.

The rites of first-kindling were forbidden even in the old days. Not because they were evil — because they were *rude*. They knocked where the gods had chosen not to answer. They took the sleeping divine by the shoulder and shook it. The Twin Brothers' shrine had been the experiment: a circle of nine faithful, a night of chanting, blood let into the altar-cracks, and Maren at the center holding the words like a knife held correctly for the first time.

It had half-worked. One eye of one stone brother had opened, and looked at her, and closed again — and the look in it had not been gratitude. It had been *hunger*, the hunger of something that had dreamed of waking and found the world still iron, still sealed, still full of people who would kneel to it only in secret, in cellars, with their hands over their mouths.

That was the night Maren learned the truth that shaped everything after: a god woken too soon wakes *starving*. And a starving god does not free magic. It consumes its way toward freedom, and calls the consuming holy.

So she had stopped. She had run. She had spent seven years telling herself she had repented — while all the while, in the cellar of her heart, she kept the rite folded up like a letter she could not stop rereading. Because there was another truth underneath the first one, the one she had confessed to no shrine and no soul:

The Order was getting *better* at killing hope. Every year the Seal tightened. Every year fewer children learned the old words. Magic was not going to survive in Vell long enough for the gods to wake naturally. Patience was not piety. Patience was extinction with good manners.

What was needed was not a knock. What was needed was a *key* — a voice that could carry the covenant from shrine to shrine, a speaker whose gift was itself a kind of divine grammar, someone the waking gods would recognize as a herald rather than a meal.

Beast-speakers were almost extinct. The Order had culled them first and hardest, because a beast-speaker could hear what the rats heard, what the crows saw, what the stones murmured in their slow stone dreams. A speaker in Vell was a walking witness to everything the Seal wanted buried.

And then, three years ago, in a market square, Maren had watched a charm-seller calm a spavined cart-horse with nothing but a murmur — and the horse had *bowed its head* — and her whole body had gone cold and then hot, the way it goes when you find the thing you buried and discover it has been growing all along.

Yara Blackwood. The key. Walking around selling luck-charms and pretending to be small.

She had not approached him then. She had watched him for two more years instead, ashamed of how easily shame came to her, until the cat found him — Nethra's fragment, the goddess's own stray — and began steering him toward the dead shrine where a beggar prayed and a stone god opened its eye. Whether the cat acted on Nethra's will or its own, Maren never knew. It hadn't mattered. The door had opened. All that remained was for someone to be standing in it when the god looked through — someone who could speak the covenant into its ears.

She had made sure she was standing there. Seven years of waiting, and she had made sure.

* * *

"That's not evidence," Maren said now, looking at the papers. "That's an accusation with ink on it."

"It's your handwriting." Tam tapped the page. "I've seen you write the litany of the waking a hundred times since I met you. Same slant on the S. Same little hook on the E, like it's trying to climb off the page." Tam's voice was flat, and that was worst of all — Tam, who argued about everything, who planted doubts the way other people planted flowers, who had spent weeks insisting the gods' return felt manufactured — Tam wasn't arguing now. Tam had already finished arguing, alone, in the nights, and arrived here with proof instead of theory.

"The rite of first-kindling," Tam went on, "requires nine voices. Nine faithful in a circle. You told me there were four of you left in all Vell who remembered the old words."

"There were," Maren said. "Once."

"So either the records are forged—"

"They're not forged."

"—or you've been recruiting. Quietly. For years. Cellars and back rooms and funeral vigils, gathering nine voices while you smiled at us over soup." Tam's hand trembled slightly as it pressed flat against the table. "And the second thing. Tell me the second thing isn't true, Maren. Tell me you didn't know what Yara was before he ever walked into those catacombs."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, in the eaves, the exiled rooks shifted and muttered in their sleep, and Maren envied them — creatures who could hold a grudge cleanly, who never had to weigh a betrayal against a salvation and call the weighing love.

"I knew," she said.

The words came out of her the way a tooth comes out — with resistance, with a small terrible sound, and then with relief, and then with the ache of the space left behind.

"I knew for three years before the shrine woke. I watched him sell charms and swallow his own gift like it was a disease. I watched him flinch when horses spoke near him, pretending he couldn't hear. Do you know what that is, Tam? Watching the answer to seven years of prayer walk around buying bread?" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "He was the key. He was always the key. His mother was a speaker — the Order burned her for it, they burned the whole line, they thought they'd finished the job — and he survived, and he hid, and he *hid so well*—"

"And so you arranged for a god to open its eye in front of him." Tam's voice rose at last, finally, breaking its flatness. "You arranged for the thing he swore an oath against — the thing that got his mother killed — to come looking for *him*. And then you gave him a covenant and a deadline and called it duty, and he's out there right now, tonight, walking shrine to shrine with your god's leash in his mouth—"

"Ashen-Vey is not my god."

"Whose is it? Because it isn't *his*. He didn't choose any of this. You chose it *for* him, and dressed the choosing up as prophecy!" Tam swept the papers off the table and they scattered like frightened birds. "I warned him. I told him the timing was too clean, the miracles too convenient — and you stood there and let me plant doubt in him knowing it was *your* harvest I was describing—"

"Yes," Maren said.

The word fell between them and neither moved to pick it up.

"Yes," she said again, quieter, because once you have confessed the crime, confessing it twice costs nothing, and because some part of her — the acolyte part, the part that had knelt in the Twin Brothers' shrine with blood on her palms — believed that truth spoken aloud had weight in whatever court the old gods still kept. "Yes. I helped wake them. Not just Ashen-Vey. I have been walking Vell for six years lighting fuses under shrines, teaching the rite to the desperate, building the circle one grieving widow and one hunted hedge-priest at a time. Nine voices. Then eighteen. The Seal thinks faith died with the temples. Faith went underground and *bred*, Tam."

"Why?"

"Because patience was killing us." She was on her feet now, though she had no memory of standing, and the striped light lay across her arms like bonds. "You think I wanted this? You think I wanted to lie to him, to *use* him — he trusts me, he looks at me like I'm the first person who ever told him his gift was holy instead of criminal, and every time he does it I die a little more, and I did it *anyway*—" She pressed her fist against her mouth, then lowered it. "Because I did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic doesn't care about our feelings. Another decade of the Seal and there are no speakers left. No rites. No words. The gods withdraw forever and Vell becomes exactly what the Order wants: a city of people who have forgotten they were ever promised anything. War is the only door left. War wakes the world. And the gods — starving, furious, half-mad gods — need a herald they cannot devour, a voice that speaks the oldest language there is. Beast-speech. The tongue the world was made in. Yara isn't collateral in my plan, Tam. He's the entire point. Without him the covenant dies at the first shrine and Ashen-Vey eats whoever's kneeling closest."

"So you fed him to them anyway."

"I *offered* him to them," Maren said, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back. Taking it back would have been the final cruelty.

* * *

They stood in silence a long time. The rooks muttered overhead. Down in the street a dog barked twice at nothing — or at something — and Maren thought: *the city hears everything now. The city has been hearing everything since the first shrine woke.* Even her confession, perhaps, was being carried somewhere on four feet and reported to someone with better hearing than hers.

"He'll come back," Tam said at last. "From the third shrine. In an hour, maybe less. And you're going to tell him. Not me. Him. Everything — the kindling, the years, the watching, all of it. If I have to drag it out of you sentence by sentence in front of him, I will, but you're going to choose to say it yourself, because if he hears it from me first, you lose the only thing you have left to give him, which is the truth from your own mouth."

"He'll hate me."

"Yes," Tam said, without cruelty and without comfort. "Probably for the rest of his life. But he'll hate you *knowing*, and knowing is the only mercy we have left to offer him. He has nine shrines and five nights left, Maren. Five nights carrying a covenant you built like a trap around his shoulders. He deserves to know what he's carrying and who tied the knots."

Maren looked at her hands. There was no blood on them. There had been blood on them once, seven years ago, in the shrine of the Twin Brothers, and she had washed it away and told herself the washing meant something.

"When he asks why," she said slowly, "and he will ask why — what do I tell him? That I loved the cause more than I loved him? That's true and it's monstrous. That I thought using him was the only way to save everyone including him? That's also true and it's worse."

"You tell him the truth," Tam said. "All of it, crooked and unforgivable. He's the one man in Vell who understands crooked oaths. Maybe he'll understand a crooked love." Tam gathered the scattered pages, squared them, and set them on the table like a verdict. "But understand this too. If he walks out that door and turns toward the Order instead of the fourth shrine — if your god's war costs him anything more than it already has — I won't protect you from him. And I won't protect him from himself, either. Whatever he chooses when he knows, that choice is real. That's the price of confession. You don't get to arrange his heartbreak the way you arranged everything else."

Footsteps on the stair. Light ones. And with them, faintly, the soft tick of claws on wood, and a low animal sound that was not quite a purr.

Maren's heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door.

"He's early," she whispered.

"No," Tam said, moving to bar the stair out of some old soldier's instinct, useless and loyal. "Something else is early."

Because the sound coming up the steps was wrong — Yara's tread was uneven, stumbling, and beneath it the cat was making a noise Maren had never heard it make, a rising yowl like an alarm, like a bell, like a small goddess screaming a warning through the only mouth she had —

—and through the window slats, across the rooftops of Vell, the sky above the temple district had begun, hours before its time, to glow.

Ashen-Vey was not waiting for the ninth shrine.
