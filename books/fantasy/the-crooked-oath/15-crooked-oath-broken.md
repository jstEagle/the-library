## 15. Crooked Oath Broken

The rookery smelled of ash and old feathers and the sour tang of Tam's cheap wine, and Yara sat in the middle of it with Maren's confession still ringing in his skull like a cracked bell.

*She used you as the vessel.*

Seven years of silence. Seven years of keeping his head down and his mouth shut and his gift buried so deep he'd almost convinced himself it was dead — all of it spent like coin in a game Maren had been playing since before he ever met her. She had known what he was from the first night she'd found him feeding pigeons behind the tannery. She had smiled at him with those warm, patient eyes and called him *friend*, and the whole time she'd been waiting for him to ripen.

"You could have asked," he said again. His voice came out flat, scraped hollow. "You could have told me what you wanted and let me choose."

"And you'd have said no," Maren said. She stood by the window with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest, and she didn't flinch, and that was somehow worse than if she had wept or begged. "You'd have said no because your mother taught you that saying yes gets people burned. I know. I was there, Yara. I held her hand while the Seal took her."

The words landed like a slap. Yara stood up so fast his stool went over behind him. The cat — Nethra, the cat, the goddess who had slept on his pillow for three years and never once told him — lifted her head from the windowsill and watched him with those green-gold eyes that were suddenly, unbearably ancient.

"Don't," he said. "Don't you dare say her name like it gives you the right."

"It gives me the truth." Maren turned to face him fully. "Your mother died because speakers were being culled, one by one, quiet as rats in a wall. Because the Order was winning. Because silence was killing us slower than fire would have. I swore after her funeral that I would never let it happen again — that when the chance came to bring the gods back, I would take it, whatever it cost." Her voice softened, and that softness cut deeper than any blade. "I'm sorry it cost you. But I am not sorry I did it."

Yara's hands were shaking. He looked down at them — the hands of a charm-seller, a liar, a coward who had spent seven years selling luck to frightened people while real magic starved in the dark.

"Get out," he said.

"Yara—"

"*Get out.*"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, slowly, like a judge closing a case. She gathered her cloak, wincing where the Order's seal-blade had grazed her shoulder two nights ago, and paused at the door.

"When you're done being angry," she said quietly, "remember this: Ashen-Vey is waking whether you carry its covenant or not. Nine shrines, nine nights. You're already four nights in. If you stop now, everything you've done — every crowd, every waking, every prayer answered — happens anyway, but without a speaker to guide it. Think about what that means before you throw away the only leash anyone has."

Then she was gone, and the door banged shut, and Yara stood alone in the feather-strewn dark with his heart hammering and his gift roaring in his ears louder than it had in seven years.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat on the floor of the rookery with his back against the coop wall and the cat curled in his lap — because despite everything, despite the betrayal coiled inside her purr, his hands needed something warm to hold — and he thought about Voss.

Captain Serah Voss of the Order of the Iron Seal. Cold eyes, colder certainty. She had marked him in that first interrogation; he'd felt it like a hook set under his skin. And now there were seizure records with Maren's name, raids on catacombs, patrols doubling through the shrine districts every night. It wouldn't take her long. A day, maybe two, and she'd trace the thread from Maren to the rookery to him.

And wasn't that the answer? Wasn't that the clean way out?

He could walk to the Ironhold tomorrow morning. He could put himself in front of Voss and tell her everything — the waking gods, the covenant, Maren's engineered resurrection, Nethra sleeping in a cat's body, the whole rotten beautiful conspiracy of it. Let the Order do what the Order did best. Seal the shrines. Break the faithful. Drag Maren away in chains. Burn it all down and salt the earth, and Vell could go back to its suffocating peace, and Yara Blackwood could go back to being nobody.

It would end it. All of it.

His mother's voice rose in his memory — not the screaming from that last day, but older, softer, from before. *A gift unused isn't a gift, little sparrow. It's just a weight.* She'd said it laughing, with a sparrow perched on her finger telling her about the worms behind the baker's house.

They had killed her for that laugh.

Yara pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars. "If I give it to Voss," he said aloud, to no one, to the dark, "it's over. Everyone's safe. Nobody else burns."

The cat shifted in his lap. When he lowered his hands, she was looking up at him, and her voice slid into his mind like water into a crack — that old tongue that had become, over nine days, almost familiar.

*Is that what you believe? That if you kneel, they will stop?*

"They stopped for seven years."

*They stopped because there was nothing left to burn. And now there is. Me. Maren. Tam. Three hundred faithful in the undercrofts. And you.* Her tail curled slowly around his wrist. *Voss is not a monster, Yara. That is what makes her dangerous. She will burn us all with tears in her eyes and call it mercy.*

"I don't care anymore." His voice cracked on it. "I never asked for any of this. I broke my oath for *her*—" he jerked his chin toward the door Maren had left through, "—for a lie. For a war somebody else decided I should fight. I'm done. Done being the vessel, done being the key, done being everyone's—"

He stopped. Because somewhere below the rookery, faint as breath, he heard it.

Not a voice. Not words. Just — attention. Dozens of small attentions turning toward him through the walls of the city, the way a field of flowers turns toward a break in the clouds. Rats in the eaves. Pigeons on the roof. Dogs sleeping in yards three streets over. They had been listening to him for nine days, drawn along the covenant-thread like moths, and they were listening now.

*They heard you,* Nethra said softly. *All of them. Every word since the first shrine woke. You are not alone in this, Yara. You only keep choosing to be.*

He looked down at her. At the goddess who had lied to him for three years by saying nothing. Fury rose in him again — hot, clean, better than despair — and with it something else. Something that had been building since the widow's shop, since the stone god opened its eye, since the first time a rat had spoken to him of shrines waking beneath the temple district.

Anger was a kind of clarity. And clarity said: *Maren used you. Ashen-Vey wants to crown you. The Order wants to erase you. Everyone wants something from you, and every one of them plans to decide your life without asking.*

Fine. Then he would ask someone. He would ask the ones who had never lied to him, never used him, never wanted anything from him except worms and warmth and shelter from the rain.

The animals.

His oath rose up in him like a wall — seven years of brick and mortar, sworn over his mother's grave with blood on his knuckles and ash in his hair. *Never speak with beasts again. Never draw the ear of the Order. Never be the thing that got her killed.* He had built his whole life inside that wall. He had sold charms and kept his eyes down and called it living.

But walls, he thought, are also cages. And his mother hadn't died because she spoke to sparrows. She had died because she did it *alone*, in a city designed to make sure she was alone.

Yara stood. The cat dropped from his lap and landed silent on the boards, watching him with an expression he couldn't read — hope, maybe, or fear, or both braided together.

"What are you doing?" Tam's voice, groggy, from the loft above. The old mage leaned over the rail, gray hair wild, a lamp sputtering to life in his hand. "Yara? It's the black hour. What in the deep—"

"Tam." Yara's own voice sounded strange to him. Steady. "How far can a voice carry, if it isn't really a voice?"

Tam went very still. "That depends," he said carefully, "on who's listening."

* * *

He climbed to the roof.

The rookery's rooftop was a chaos of coops and weather-vanes and Tam's rusted scrying dishes, and beyond it Vell spread out under a bruised pre-dawn sky — ten thousand rooftops, a hundred thousand sleepers, the great dead shrine of the temple district looming black against the stars. Somewhere out there, Voss was awake too. He could feel it, the way you feel a storm front. Somewhere out there, Ashen-Vey was stirring in its ninth shrine, growing, hungry, patient. Somewhere out there, Maren was walking the streets with her confession on her lips and her faith intact, believing she'd saved the world by breaking him.

Let them all watch, then.

Yara walked to the roof's edge and stood where the whole city could have seen him if any of its human eyes had bothered to look up. The wind off the Graywater tugged at his coat. Below, the streets were empty except for the things that lived in the margins — and those things were gathering. He could hear them now without trying. Rats pouring along gutter-pipes. Cats flowing over fences like spilled ink. Crows lifting from chimneys in ragged spirals. Dogs standing at the ends of their chains, ears pricked, eyes bright in the dark.

They had heard him. They had come.

His oath stood in front of him one last time — his mother's face on it, her ashes, her scream. And underneath the oath, quieter, her other voice. The laughing one. *A gift unused isn't a gift, little sparrow.*

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "I'm sorry it took me this long. I'm sorry I let them make me ashamed of you."

Then Yara Blackwood opened his mouth, and spoke aloud — not in charm-seller's patter, not in whispers, but in the true tongue, the beast-speech, the language that had gotten his mother burned — and he did not whisper it. He *threw* it.

**"HEAR ME."**

The word went out of him like a shockwave. He felt it leave — felt his gift tear loose from seven years of binding and pour through the night air of Vell, touching every ear that was shaped to hear it. On the rooftops, ten thousand heads turned. In the gutters, ten thousand bodies went still.

**"My name is Yara Blackwood. My mother was Sera Blackwood, and she was a speaker, and your city burned her for it. I swore an oath of silence over her ashes, and I have kept it for seven years, and tonight I break it."**

The words hurt coming out, the way a broken bone hurts being set. Liberation and terror in the same breath, exactly as promised. His voice shook. He let it shake.

**"There are powers waking in this city. Old gods, returned — some of them mean you well, and some of them mean to wear this city like a crown. There is an Order that means to seal every shrine and burn every believer, and it does not know yet how much it has already lost. And there are men and women who planned all of this before any of us were given a choice."**

A crow landed on the ridgepole beside him. Then another. A dog sat down in the street below, looking up. The windows of the tenements across the lane began to light, one by one — sleepers waking, sensing something they couldn't name, pressing faces to the glass.

**"I cannot fight the Order. I cannot unmake what Maren has woken. I am one man, and I have been one man alone for seven years, and alone is how they want us."** His voice hardened. **"So I am asking. Not commanding — asking. The god Ashen-Vey wants a herald to march through this city. The Order wants a criminal to hunt. I want something else. I want eyes. I want wings. I want every rat in every wall and every pigeon on every sill and every dog behind every gate to see what is coming and tell each other, the way you always have, the way they could never stop you from doing."**

He spread his arms. The wind pulled at him. Far to the east, over the temple district, the sky had begun to glow — a faint amber pulse, like a heartbeat under skin. The ninth shrine, waking early. Or being woken.

**"Three nights remain. Help me finish what was started — honestly, this time, in the open, with no one used and no one burned. Walk with me. Fly with me. And if the Order comes for me—"** his throat closed, and he forced the words through it, **"—then remember my mother's name. Sera Blackwood. Say it in your own tongues, in your own ways, wherever they take me. Let this city know what it did, and let it never be able to say it didn't know."**

Silence. One full breath of it, vast as the sky.

Then the crows screamed — all of them at once, a sound like tearing cloth, and it rolled across the rooftops of Vell in a wave. Dogs began to howl, street by street, district by district, a rising chorus that no Order decree had ever managed to silence. Rats poured from the drains in silver rivers, not fleeing but *gathering*, flowing toward the rookery, toward him. Somewhere a stable of carriage-horses stamped and whinnied in unison. Somewhere the gulls off the Graywater wheeled up in a white spiral against the dark.

And beneath it all, threaded through every cry, he heard it passed from beak to ear to paw: *Sera Blackwood. Sera Blackwood. Sera Blackwood.*

Yara stood on the rooftop with his broken oath lying in pieces around his feet, and he laughed — a raw, cracked, astonished sound — because it didn't feel like ruin. It felt like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Beside him, Nethra sat with her tail wrapped around her paws, and for the first time since he'd known her, the goddess in the cat's body looked afraid.

"You knew I'd do this eventually," he said. "Didn't you? That's why you chose me."

*I hoped,* she said. *Hoping is all any of us have left, now.*

"Why?"

She looked east, at the pulsing amber glow strengthening over the temple district, and her ears flattened.

*Because Ashen-Vey felt you speak,* she said. *And it has stopped waiting.*

Across the city, every bell in Vell began to ring — and none of them had been touched by human hands.
