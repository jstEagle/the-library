## 17. Ashen-Vey's True Face

The last shrine stood at the top of the world, or close enough to it.

The Hollow Crown — that was what the old maps called it, and what no living map dared call it now — rose from the highest terrace of the temple district like a broken tooth. Once it had been a ring of nine spires around an open sky, a crown worn by nothing but the wind. The Order had sealed its gates with iron and salt forty years ago, and the city below had learned not to look up at it.

Yara climbed the pilgrim stair alone.

Behind him, Vell spread out in the grey light before dawn: ten thousand rooftops furred with frost, the harbor a dull pewter coin, the smoke of a hundred thousand hearths rising straight into windless air. Somewhere down there, patrols were moving. He had felt them all night through the soles of his feet — dogs straining against leashes, horses made restless by riders who smelled of fear. The Order knew where he would end. They had always known. There was only ever one shrine left after eight.

His knees ached. His throat was raw from speaking words no human tongue was built for. Nine days of walking, nine nights of waking stone, and every shrine he opened had sung the same song: *Come. Come. Come.* Now, at the threshold of the last one, the song had stopped.

That silence frightened him more than any patrol could have.

The cat sat on the third step above him, tail curled around her feet, waiting the way she always waited — as if time were something that happened to other people.

"You could tell me what's up there," Yara said.

"I could," said the cat, in her voice like dry leaves over stone.

"But you won't."

"I won't." Her green eyes caught the first light. "Not because I don't know. Because knowing has never once stopped you from climbing."

He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You know, for a fragment of a goddess, you're remarkably bad at comfort."

"For a fragment of a goddess," the cat replied, "I am remarkably honest. There is a difference, and you will understand it soon."

She said nothing else. She turned and flowed upward through the ruined gate, past seals that should have burned her and didn't, and Yara, because he had never in his life done the sensible thing when it mattered most, followed her into the Hollow Crown.

* * *

Inside, the shrine had not decayed the way the others had.

The eight shrines he'd woken had been ruins wearing their ruin like old clothes — dust and pigeon filth and the patient rot of things abandoned. This place had been *kept*. Someone had swept these floors. Someone had oiled these hinges. The nine spires still stood whole around the open circle of sky, and at their base, arranged in the old crescent of welcome, sat candles. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Their combined light made dawn unnecessary.

And they were full. The faithful knelt in the crescent — two hundred souls at least, maybe three — in homespun and merchant silk and, here and there, the unmistakable cut of Order uniforms with the insignia torn away. Maren stood at the center of them, arms raised, her grey hair loose down her back, and when she saw Yara at the gate she smiled with such naked relief that his heart broke a little before he understood why.

"He came," she breathed, and the word went through the crowd like fire through dry grass. "He came. Nine shrines. Nine nights. As promised."

Tam Ossler was there too, near the edge, arms crossed, and he did not smile. He watched Yara the way he'd watched him for three days now, since the rookery, since he'd laid out his heresy in the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant: *too convenient, too timed, too clean.* Their eyes met across the candlelight, and Tam gave him a single slow shake of the head.

Yara had ignored it. Yara had been too drunk on purpose to listen.

Now, standing in this swept and candlelit hollow, watching two hundred people rise to their knees as he passed, he felt the first cold drop of Tam's doubt slide down his spine.

"Maren." His voice sounded small in all that space. "What is this?"

"The ninth shrine." She took his hands. Hers were warm; his were not. "The heart of it. You've carried Ashen-Vey's covenant across the whole city, Yara. Every shrine singing to every other shrine. Do you feel it?"

He did feel it. He had felt it building for days — a low note beneath the stones of Vell, growing rounder each night, each woken shrine adding its voice. Here, at the center, the note was a chord, and the chord was almost a word.

"What I feel," he said carefully, "is a god about to speak. And gods only speak when they want something."

"They want to save us." Maren's eyes shone. "Seven years, Yara. Seven years since the last Purge, since they dragged speakers into the square and—" She stopped herself. She always stopped herself there; they both knew what lived in that pause. "Magic is dying in this city by inches. The Order sees to that. But tonight, tonight the covenant completes, and Ashen-Vey will shield every soul who kneels. No more hiding. No more burning. That is what we waited for. That is what *you* are."

"Is it?" Yara asked softly. "Or am I just the key? Because keys get turned, Maren. Keys get used."

Her hands tightened on his. For half a heartbeat — less than a breath — something flickered behind her certainty. Something that looked terribly like guilt wearing hope as a mask. Then the candles guttered, all of them, all at once, though there was no draft.

And the sky inside the crown began to speak.

* * *

It did not come as a voice at first. It came as pressure — a hand closing gently around the entire shrine, around everyone in it, tender as a mother and vast as weather. The candle flames bent inward toward the center of the circle. Frost bloomed up the nine spires in feathered patterns. And in the air above the kneeling faithful, ash began to fall: fine grey flakes that melted before they touched the ground, that smelled of hearth-smoke and burnt libraries and the inside of a closed coffin.

Then the voice. It used the sound of many voices braided together — Maren's cadence, the widow's from his first morning, the beggar's prayer at the dead shrine, even, horribly, a thread of his own mother's tone, lifted from somewhere deep in him without permission.

**MY VOICE-BEARER. NINE SHRINES. NINE NIGHTS. YOU HAVE DONE WHAT NO SPEAKER HAS DONE IN FOUR HUNDRED YEARS.**

Every animal within a mile of the crown went silent. Yara felt it happen — the pigeons freezing on their ledges, the rats pausing mid-route, the dogs in the yards below lifting their heads as one. The whole listening city holding its breath.

"I did what was asked," Yara said aloud, because Ashen-Vey seemed to expect speech, and because his mouth was very dry. "The covenant is delivered. The faithful are gathered. Shield them, as you swore."

**I WILL.**

The word landed like a dropped anvil. Two hundred people wept with relief.

**I WILL SHIELD THEM FROM THE ORDER. FROM THE SEAL. FROM THE MEN AND WOMEN WHO HAVE MADE CRUELTY INTO LAW. THIS I SWEAR UPON MY STONE NAME.**

"Good," Yara said. "Good. Then it's done. I'll go—"

**IT IS NOT DONE.**

The pressure closed a little tighter. Not painful. Not yet. The tenderness of it was the worst part — the way a fist can be gentle right up until it isn't.

**MY VOICE-BEARER. LOOK AT ME.**

The falling ash gathered. Above the circle it spun, thickened, took shape — a figure thirty feet tall, seated upon a throne of cinders that had not existed a moment before. It had a body like a man's grown immense and then worn smooth, features eroded the way river stones are eroded, faceless except where the ash occasionally gathered into eyes. When those eyes formed, they looked directly down at Yara, and Yara felt them the way a rabbit feels a hawk: as weight, as mathematics, as the simple arithmetic of being prey.

"You're showing yourself," Yara whispered. "Why show yourself?"

**BECAUSE THE TIME FOR MASKS IS ENDED, AND BECAUSE YOU DESERVE THE TRUTH THAT YOUR COMPANIONS COULD NOT GIVE YOU.**

Beside him, he heard Maren go very still. Heard Tam say, low and bitter, "*There* it is."

**ASK ME WHAT I INTEND, VOICE-BEARER.**

The crowd murmured. Maren's grip found his arm. "Yara," she said quickly, "don't — it doesn't matter, the shielding—"

"Ask me," the god said again, gentler now, in a voice that was suddenly, exactly, his mother's, "what I intend."

The shrine went silent enough to hear candlewicks drowning.

"What," Yara said, "do you intend?"

**AT DAWN, WHEN THE COVENANT SEALS, EVERY SHRINE IN VELL WAKES FULLY. AND A WOKEN GOD DOES NOT MERELY SHELTER ITS FAITHFUL. A WOKEN GOD ACTS.** The ash-eyes did not blink, because they were not truly eyes. **THE ORDER OF THE IRON SEAL HAS KILLED OUR SPEAKERS, BURIED OUR NAMES, AND STARVED OUR PEOPLE OF THEIR BIRTHRIGHT FOR FORTY YEARS. THEY WILL NOT BE NEGOTIATED WITH. THEY WILL BE PURGED.**

The word fell into the crowd like a lit lamp into oil. Yara heard it catch — a ripple of hunger moving through the kneeling faithful, some nodding, some going white, a boy of perhaps fifteen whispering *yes* under his breath with a fervor that made Yara's stomach turn.

"And the rest of the city?" Yara asked. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which surprised him. "The ones who aren't faithful? The ones who are just afraid?"

**AFRAID PEOPLE CHOOSE SIDES QUICKLY WHEN GIVEN A CLEAR ONE.**

"That's not an answer."

**IT IS THE ONLY ANSWER CITIES HAVE EVER UNDERSTOOD.** The god leaned forward on its throne of cinders, and the heat of it touched Yara's face like an open oven door. **BUT YOU, VOICE-BEARER — YOU ARE NOT ASKED TO SWING ANY BLADE. YOUR PART IS FINER THAN THAT.**

"Don't," Tam said loudly, stepping forward into the candlelight. "Don't say it. Whatever it is, don't let him—"

**YOU WILL STAND AT MY SIDE WHEN THE PURGING COMES. YOU WILL SPEAK TO THE BEASTS OF THIS CITY AND TELL THEM WHOM TO OBSTRUCT AND WHOM TO SPARE, SO THAT THE FAITHFUL WALK FREE THROUGH A CITY BLINDED AND KNEELING. AND WHEN IT IS DONE, WHEN THE SEAL IS BROKEN AND MAGIC RUNS FREE IN VELL AGAIN, YOU WILL BE CROWNED.**

"Crowned," Yara repeated. The word tasted like iron filings.

**VOICE-KING. FIRST SPEAKER OF THE NEW AGE. INTERMEDIARY BETWEEN GODS AND THE CITY THAT FORGOT THEM.** The throne of ash spread wider, grander, sprouting steps that led up to a seat sized for someone slightly larger than a man. **THE FAITHFUL WILL FOLLOW YOU. THE FEARSOME WILL FEAR YOU. NO BLACKWOOD WILL EVER DIE IN A SQUARE AGAIN.**

And there it was. There was the knife slid in under the ribs, angled perfectly, twisted with perfect knowledge. His mother's name spoken by a thing made of ash, wrapped around an offer no grieving son could easily refuse.

Yara thought of her. Of her hands cupped around a sparrow's body, of her laughing at something a mule had said, of the rope, of the square, of the crowd that had watched. He let himself think of it, fully, honestly — let himself imagine the world where none of it had happened because a god had been awake and willing to burn the people responsible.

It was a beautiful world. That was the trap of it. It was beautiful the way a snare is beautiful, silver-wrapped and soft-lined.

"No," Yara said.

The single syllable dropped into the shrine and rang there.

Maren made a small sound. Tam exhaled like a man surfacing. The crowd stirred, confused, frightened, angry — he could feel their attention swinging between him and the towering shape of ash like a compass needle hunting north.

**NO?** said Ashen-Vey. And for the first time, something moved beneath the tenderness of its voice. Something old. Something with teeth.

"No." Yara stepped forward, away from Maren's hand, away from the offered steps. "I've carried your covenant because people needed shelter, and I'll not apologize for that. But I didn't spend nine nights waking shrines so a god could do to this city what the Order's been doing for forty years. Purges are purges. It doesn't matter whose hands hold the torch."

**YOU MISUNDERSTAND YOUR POSITION, VOICE-BEARER.**

"No," Yara said quietly. "I really don't. That's the problem. See — I finally understand it completely."

He raised his voice, and he spoke to the whole shrine, to the two hundred kneeling faithful, to Maren and Tam, to the ash-throned thing that wore his mother's voice like a stolen coat.

"You didn't wake by accident. You didn't return by chance. Somebody woke you — deliberately, carefully, seven years in the planning — and somebody found me, groomed me, aimed me at these nine shrines like an arrow. I've spent nine days telling myself the timing didn't matter, that the outcome was good enough to excuse the questions." He looked at Maren, and watched her face come apart by degrees. "But a god that plans a purge before it plans a rescue isn't a savior. It's a conqueror with better manners than the last one."

**AND YET,** said Ashen-Vey, and the tenderness drained out of its voice entirely, leaving something geological, **YOU WILL DO IT REGARDLESS.**

"Will I?"

**THE COVENANT IS SPOKEN THROUGH YOUR MOUTH. SEALED BY YOUR GIFT. YOU ARE ITS VESSEL, VOICE-BEARER, AND VESSELS DO NOT VETO WHAT THEY CONTAIN. AT DAWN THE LAST SHRINE WAKES — WITH YOUR CONSENT OR WITHOUT IT. YOU MAY STAND AT MY RIGHT HAND OR KNEEL AMONG THE PURGED. I PREFER THE FORMER. I HAVE GROWN... FOND OF YOU.**

The pressure closed around Yara like a fist finally making up its mind. Not crushing — not yet — but inescapable, patient, certain. He felt his gift stir inside him, the beast-speech that had cost his mother everything, and felt how thoroughly it was laced through with Ashen-Vey's covenant now, root and branch, every woken shrine a knot tied in him.

Around the circle, the faithful were rising to their feet. Some wept with joy. Some looked at Yara with dawning horror. The boy who'd whispered *yes* was smiling.

Maren reached for him. "Yara, please, if we resist now, everything—"

"Everything burns either way," Tam said flatly, planting himself at Yara's other side. "Question was always just *who holds the torch*. I told you. Three days ago I told you."

"You told me," Yara said.

The cat appeared at his feet, materializing out of shadow as cats do, and looked up at the throne of ash with an expression of ancient, unimpressed contempt. But she said nothing. For the first time in nine days, she said absolutely nothing — and her silence was the loudest warning she had ever given him.

Dawn was perhaps an hour away. The sky beyond the nine spires had gone the color of a healing bruise. Down in the city, Yara felt through the soles of his feet the Order mobilizing — hundreds of boots, hundreds of drawn blades, Captain Serah Voss among them like a struck bell — all converging on the temple district, all marching toward a battle that hadn't started yet and already couldn't be stopped.

Ashen-Vey settled onto its throne of cinders and waited, the way stone waits, the way winter waits.

**CHOOSE QUICKLY, VOICE-BEARER,** it said, almost kindly. **DAWN DOES NOT WAIT WITH US.**

And in the silence that followed, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Yara felt something else stir at the edge of his awareness — faint, cool, familiar as a hand on the back of his neck. Not the god's pressure. Not the covenant's pull. Something older, quieter, coming up through the stones themselves, through the roots of the mountain under the city, through every rat-hole and roost and den in Vell.

A second voice. A goddess-shaped absence, gathering itself in the dark below.

The cat finally spoke, one word, barely a breath:

"*Listen.*"
