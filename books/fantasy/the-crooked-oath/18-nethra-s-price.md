## 18. Nethra's Price

The rookery smelled of ash and old feathers, and Yara could not sleep.

He sat in the corner Tam had cleared for him, beneath the broken skylight where the first grey of pre-dawn would eventually show itself, and he listened to the city. That was the trouble with his gift — he could not stop listening. Even now, even after everything, the night poured its voices into him: rats quarreling over a crust in a wall of Copperlane, dogs dreaming in their yards, the rooks above him muttering half-formed grievances in their sleep. And beneath it all, deeper than any of them, the slow grinding attention of Ashen-Vey turning beneath the temple district like a great wheel beginning to spin.

*Voice-king,* it had said. *Whether you consent or not.*

Yara pulled his knees up and pressed his forehead against them.

"You should sleep." Maren's voice, soft from across the loft. She sat near the cold stove with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest, her face half-lit by the single candle she'd refused to let him snuff. "Tomorrow — today — will ask more of you than any day before it."

"Will it?" His laugh came out cracked. "I can't imagine how."

She flinched at that. He watched her flinch and felt nothing but a dull satisfaction that disgusted him a moment later. Seven days ago he'd have crossed the room for her. Seven days ago he hadn't known she'd built him like a lock builds a key.

"The cat's been gone since midnight," Maren said.

"I know."

"It'll come back."

"I know that too." He lifted his head. "That's what frightens me."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, Tam was arguing with his crows again, his voice rising and falling in the rhythm of a man who had long ago stopped caring whether anyone human heard him. The sound should have been comforting. It only reminded Yara how few people were left who knew what he was, and how many of them had lied to him.

Then the silence came.

It did not arrive gradually. One heartbeat the rookery was full of small voices, and the next they were simply — gone. The rats hushed. The dogs hushed. Even the wind against the shutters seemed to hold its breath. And in the middle of that vast, ringing quiet, the candle flame stood perfectly still, like a painting of a flame.

Yara was on his feet before he understood why.

"She's here," he said.

Maren rose too, her good hand going to the amulet at her throat. "Nethra?"

The shadows along the far wall deepened, gathered, and became a shape. Not a woman. Not a cat. Something between — a silhouette with too many edges, eyes like two slivers of moon caught in dark water. When it spoke, the voice came from everywhere at once: from the walls, from the floorboards, from inside Yara's own skull where no one else could ever follow.

*You have heard what my brother intends.*

"Ashen-Vey." Yara kept his voice low, though he suspected volume meant nothing here. "You were listening."

*I am always listening. I was listening the night your mother died. I have been listening since before this city had a name.* The shape drifted closer without moving. *And I have heard enough to know that if dawn comes and you do nothing, Vell burns. If dawn comes and you carry my brother's covenant willingly, Vell burns slower, and you burn with it — crowned.*

"Then tell me there's another way," Yara said. "Tell me you didn't come all this way — seven years in a cat's skin, following me through the gutters of this city — just to describe my death to me in prettier language."

Something that might have been amusement moved through those moon-slit eyes.

*There is another way. There is always another way. But none of them are free, little speaker, and I will not lie to you about the price as others have done.* A pause, deliberate as a blade being drawn. *Ask me. Ask me what it costs, and then decide whether you wish to hear the rest.*

Yara looked at Maren. She had gone very still, her lips moving in some silent prayer, and he realized with a strange distant clarity that she was afraid — truly afraid — of the goddess she had spent seven years serving. That frightened him more than anything else in the room.

"What does it cost?" he asked.

*Everything you are,* Nethra said. *Your gift. All of it. Not silenced — silenced things wake again. Not hidden — hidden things are found. Given. Poured out entire, the way water is poured into wine until there is no water left to find. You will walk out of the covenant a whole man and an empty one. No whispers. No voices. No beast will ever speak to you again, nor you to it. You will be deaf in a world that is finally singing.*

The words landed one by one, each with its own weight.

"And that buys peace?" Yara managed. "How?"

*Because the gift is the hinge.* The shadow-shape spread, filling the corner of the room, and for a moment Yara saw what wore the cat: something vast and patient and very old, coiled around the roots of the world. *The old gods cannot bind themselves. That is the flaw in our nature, the crookedness we were made with — we can only be bound from outside, by a will that is not divine. Your mother's line carried that will. Speakers always have. It is why we loved you, and it is why we feared you, and it is why the Order hunted you without ever knowing what they were hunting for.*

"Speak plainly," Yara said. "I've had a week of gods and priestesses talking in circles."

A ripple went through the darkness — laughter, he thought, or grief; with Nethra they sounded the same.

*Ashen-Vey means to make you its crown. Through your gift it will speak through every mouth in this city, beast and faithful alike, and what speaks through every mouth cannot be argued with. War follows. Purge follows. Your friend Serah Voss will die on the steps of a shrine believing she failed her Order, and the Order will die after her, and then the god will be alone with its worshippers forever, which is all it has ever wanted.*

"But if I pour the gift out—"

*—there is no vessel left to crown. No hinge for conquest. And the act of pouring, freely given by a speaker of your mother's blood, becomes the binding itself. Your surrender becomes the chain. All of us — my brother, the waking shrines, the drowned god under the river, every name the old faith remembers — bound into one covenant of stillness. Peace, Yara. Real peace. Bought with the only coin that matters.*

The candle flame trembled. Maren made a small sound, and when Yara glanced at her, tears were running down her face without her permission.

"That's why you chose me," she whispered. "Not as Ashen-Vey's vessel. As the binding. Oh, gods — I thought I was waking them for war, but you—" She pressed her hand over her mouth.

*I chose the bloodline,* Nethra said, and her gentleness was worse than cruelty. *I chose a boy who fed a dying crow because he could not bear its pain. I did not choose the manner of his mother's dying, and I did not stop it, and I will carry that failure longer than your city carries its stones. Do not mistake me for something kind, priestess. I am old, and I am hungry, and I have wanted my brother leashed since the world was young. I am telling him the truth only because lies have brought us all to this room.*

Silence again. Yara found that he was shaking.

"And if I refuse?" he asked. "If I keep my gift, and I fight him my own way?"

*Then you fight a god with a man's hands, and you lose, and the losing takes everything anyway. Or you win, and become the thing I just described. There is no road from tonight that keeps you whole, little speaker. I am sorry. Truly. It is the only sentence I have spoken in a thousand years that I did not plan.*

Yara turned away from her. He walked to the window — the broken skylight, the slice of sky above the rookery — and looked out at Vell.

The city lay dark under a thin scum of clouds, ten thousand rooftops, a hundred thousand sleeping people. Down in Copperlane the widow he'd sold a luck-charm to would be waking soon, checking her door-lock twice because fear had become habit in this city. Somewhere a patrol of Iron Seal men was stamping cold feet at a checkpoint, young men doing their jobs, believing in their seals. Somewhere a child was feverish, and a mother was praying to a dead god, and — for the first time in seven years — the god might actually answer.

*Whether you consent or not.*

And on a windowsill in a shop that didn't exist yet, in a life he couldn't imagine, a cat might someday sit. Just a cat.

"How long?" he asked quietly. "Do I have to decide?"

*Before dawn.* The shadow drew back from the walls, gathering itself smaller, more contained — becoming, once more, almost a cat. *When the sun touches the great shrine, the choice makes itself. Ashen-Vey moves at first light. If you stand in its path carrying my covenant, you are bound. If you stand in its path carrying only yourself, you are taken. There is no third standing.*

"And Maren? And Tam? And the faithful?"

*Live or die according to what you choose. As they always have. As we all do.* A pause. *One thing more, Yara Blackwood, and then I leave you to your grief. What you give, you give speaking your mother's name. That is the old law. Her blood opened this road; her name must close it. Say it at the last, at the heart of the binding, and mean it — forgiveness or fury, it does not matter which, so long as it is true.*

His throat closed. He could not answer. He nodded instead, once, and the shadow accepted it, folding down and down and down until it was only a cat-shaped absence in the corner, and then not even that. Sound came flooding back into the world — the rooks, the wind, the far-off bark of a dog — ordinary sounds, suddenly unbearable, each one a voice he was already mourning.

The candle steadied. Dawn was perhaps an hour off.

Maren crossed the room slowly. She stopped an arm's length away, and for once in all the time he'd known her she did not reach for him, did not touch him, did not try to hold together what she had helped break.

"Yara," she said. "Don't do this for me. Whatever you choose — don't do it for me. I've spent seven years deciding things for you. I won't add my guilt to your sacrifice."

"I'm not doing it for you." He heard his own voice as if from far away, calm and terribly clear. "I'm doing it for a widow in Copperlane who checks her locks twice. For a kid with a fever whose prayer deserves an honest answer or no answer at all. For Voss, maybe — for all of them." He laughed softly. "For the cat, even. Whoever the cat turns out to be when I can't hear her anymore."

Maren's face crumpled. He let her cry and did not comfort her, and hated himself a little for that, and let it stand.

Below, Tam's voice had stopped mid-argument. Footsteps on the ladder, fast ones, and then the old mage's head appeared through the hatch, wild-eyed, a crow perched on each shoulder like a pair of black epaulettes.

"They're moving," Tam said. "Crows from the temple quarter — hundreds of them, all flying one direction, and underneath them—" He swallowed. "Underneath them the ground's glowing, lad. The streets around the great shrine are lit from below like coals. Ashen-Vey isn't waiting for first light."

Yara looked at the window. Far to the east, beyond the rooftops, the sky had begun — very faintly, treacherously early — to pale.

He picked up his coat.

"Maren," he said, "tell me the fastest way to the great shrine. And someone get me that damned cat. I want to say goodbye while I can still hear her."

* * *

They went down through the city like water finding its level — Tam's crows scouting ahead, Maren leading through alleys she had memorized in seven years of secret worship, Yara running with the covenant warm against his chest and his decision sitting in him like a swallowed stone. Behind them, the glow from the temple district climbed higher, staining the undersides of the clouds red, and ahead of them, somewhere past the last row of houses, the sun waited at the edge of the world to make his choice final.

On a rooftop three streets from the shrine, the cat was waiting for him.

She sat perfectly still against the reddening sky, tail curled, eyes catching the fire-light from below, and when Yara stopped in front of her, breath ragged, she spoke in the old tongue one last time.

*Well, little speaker. Have you decided?*

Yara knelt, so that they were eye to eye, and reached out — not with his hands.

"Yes," he said.
