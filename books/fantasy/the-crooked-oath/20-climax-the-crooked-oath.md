## 20. Climax: The Crooked Oath

The great shrine of Vell had been a cathedral once, before the Iron Seal came with hammers and fire and the long law of silence. Now it was a wound in the heart of the city — its dome cracked open to the sky like an egg, its pillars snapped at varying heights, its floor a mosaic of broken saints that no one had swept in seven years. Ash fell there even when nothing burned. It had always fallen there. Yara understood that now.

He walked into it through the shattered western door, and every eye in the dark turned toward him.

Maren stood at the center of the ruin, on the raised dais where the altar had been torn out, her white robes streaked with soot and something darker. Around her, perhaps two hundred of the faithful knelt in ragged rows — dockworkers, lamplighters, a fishwife Yara had sold charms to, their faces lit by candles that burned without flickering, which was wrong, which was *Ashen-Vey's* wrongness, order imposed on flame like a hand pressing down on a throat.

And above them, filling the broken dome, was the god.

It had stopped pretending to be stone. Ashen-Vey hung in the air as a figure of grey light and drifting cinders, vast as a bell tower, its face a smooth oval with two eyes like cooling furnaces. When it spoke, it did not use sound. It used certainty — words arriving fully formed inside the skull, the way a verdict arrives.

*THE SPEAKER COMES. KNEEL, LITTLE VOICE.*

"I don't kneel," Yara said, and his voice cracked across the silence like a whip. He heard it echo off the ruined walls and realized, distantly, that he was not afraid. That was the strangest part. Seven years of fear, and here at the end of it, his hands were almost steady.

Behind him, through the western door, came the ring of iron on stone.

The Order entered in column, forty strong, seal-blades drawn, Captain Serah Voss at their head with her grey cloak singed at the hem and blood on her cheek that was not her own. Yara had seen her last in the burning streets, turning her blade against Commander Hale rather than let him put a torch to a tenement full of civilians. She had crossed the city since then, he'd heard, cutting a path through her own brothers-in-arms. She looked like a woman who had burned her only bridge and was walking anyway.

"Blackwood." Her voice carried flat and hard across the shrine. "Stand away from it."

"It doesn't want me standing away," Yara said. "That's rather the problem."

*CAPTAIN VOSS.* The god's attention slid to her like a slab of slate moving. *YOUR ORDER BURNED MY SHRINES AND SALT MY NAME FROM EVERY BOOK. YOU ARE THE REASON FOR THE CULLING. YOUR COMMANDER'S ASHES FEED MY ROOTS EVEN NOW.*

"And you're the reason my city is on fire," Voss answered, and Yara heard it — the fracture in her voice, the seam where rigid conviction had split and something rawer showed through. "Whatever you were before the Seal buried you, you've woken up hungry."

*I HAVE WOKEN UP JUST,* said Ashen-Vey, and the candles all flared at once, and two hundred faithful murmured like a tide coming in.

Yara stepped forward onto the broken mosaic floor. Beneath his boots he felt it — the god's roots, running down through the catacombs, through the bones of old Vell, threading every waking shrine in the city. He could feel them the way he felt the rats in the walls, the pigeons on the eaves, the dogs in the yards. Everything alive in Vell was a note, and Ashen-Vey was trying to be the chord.

"Maren." He said her name quietly, and she flinched as if he'd struck her.

She came down from the dais three steps and stopped, hands open at her sides, palms up — the old gesture of the faith, *nothing hidden, nothing held.* Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"Yara. You came."

"You used me." His voice stayed low. He would not give this crowd a performance; this was not for them. "Seven years you waited for a beast-speaker. You knew what I was before I knew. You fed me to Ashen-Vey shrine by shrine, nine nights, nine doors, and every step I thought I was carrying a covenant. I was carrying a leash."

"Yes." She did not look away. That was the worst of it — Maren never looked away. "I woke them deliberately. I helped end the Silence because peace built on graves isn't peace, it's just a slower massacre. Your mother. My mother. Every speaker they burned. I would do it again." A breath. "But I didn't know what Ashen-Vey wanted to be, Yara. I swear it on Nethra's nine names. I thought I was midwifing gods. I gave birth to a conqueror."

*YOU GAVE BIRTH TO ORDER,* the god said. *AND THE SPEAKER WILL BE ITS VOICE. COME, YARA BLACKWOOD. YOUR MOTHER DIED SCREAMING IN A CELL BECAUSE SHE WAS WEAK. YOU WILL NOT BE WEAK. YOU WILL SIT AT MY RIGHT HAND AND EVERY BEAST, EVERY SHRINE, EVERY PRAYER IN THIS CITY WILL PASS THROUGH YOUR MOUTH—*

"No."

The word came out of him smaller than he intended, and larger than anything he'd ever said.

Ashen-Vey's furnace-eyes narrowed. *NO?*

"My mother's name was Elira Blackwood." Yara's voice shook now, but he let it. "She sang to horses. She talked bees out of swarming. She hid speakers in our cellar when the Seal came through the district, and when they took her, she didn't scream. She told the rats to gnaw through my window bars so I could run, and she smiled at me through the glass while they dragged her away. She wasn't weak. She was *kind*, and kindness isn't weakness, it's the only thing your kind has never understood."

The god went very still. Even the candle flames seemed to lean back.

*THEN YOU CHOOSE THE OTHER PATH. THE CAT'S PATH. NETHRA WHISPERS IN YOUR EAR EVEN NOW — I CAN TASTE HER ON YOUR BREATH. SHE OFFERED YOU A PRICE, DIDN'T SHE? YOUR GIFT. YOUR SELF. AN ORDINARY MAN IN EXCHANGE FOR BINDING US ALL. SHE DIDN'T TELL YOU WHAT BINDING MEANS. IT MEANS THE SILENCE AGAIN, FOREVER. IT MEANS YOUR MOTHER DIED FOR NOTHING.*

"She died for me," Yara said. "And tonight I'm going to make it mean something more."

He closed his eyes.

The gift rose in him like water finding its level. He could feel everything — the two hundred hearts kneeling in the ash, the forty Order blades trembling in tired fists, the rats pouring through the catacombs below, the rooks wheeling over the broken dome, Tam Ossler somewhere on a rooftop with a spyglass and a mouth full of curses, the cat — Nethra — pacing the shadow of the western door, tail high, watching him with eyes like green lamps.

*NOW?* she asked, in the old tongue, in the tongue his mother had sung in. *NOW, LITTLE VOICE?*

"Now," Yara said aloud.

And he broke his crooked oath.

Not quietly. Not in a cellar, not in a whisper, not the way he had lived seven years — sideways, muffled, small. He opened his mouth and he spoke to every living thing in Vell at once, with the whole of his voice, the voice he had sworn never to raise:

*"Hear me! All of you — hear me!"*

It went out of him like a bell being struck. He felt it leave — felt the gift pour through his throat and out into the night, into the sewers and the steeples and the stables, into ten thousand small ears. Dogs sat up in their yards. Pigeons burst from every rooftop in the temple district in one grey thunderclap. In the shrine, the rats came up out of the cracks in the floor and did not flee the fire or the blades, but sat in rings around him, whiskers forward, listening.

*"I am Yara Blackwood, son of Elira, and I am a beast-speaker, and I am done hiding. Ashen-Vey wants a war. The Order wants a silence. And I—" his voice broke, and he let it break— "I want my mother back, and I can't have her, so I will have this instead."*

He turned to face the god.

*"Nethra! Old mother, cat of nine shadows — I accept your price. Do you hear me? I ACCEPT IT."*

The darkness under the broken dome deepened. Out of it, the cat walked — but no longer small. She flowed upward as she came, shadow unfolding into shadow, until a goddess made of night and green eyes stood between Yara and Ashen-Vey, tall as the god was vast, wearing still, at her center, unmistakably, the shape of a sitting cat.

*HE ACCEPTS,* Nethra said, and her voice was purr and avalanche together. *WITNESS IT, ASHEN-VEY. WITNESS IT, ALL OF YOU. THE COVENANT IS OFFERED AND THE PRICE IS PAID.*

*THE PRICE IS THEFT,* Ashen-Vey roared, and the whole shrine shuddered, dust cascading from the broken dome. *HE IS MINE. I MARKED HIM THROUGH NINE SHRINES—*

"You marked me," Yara said, "and I'm unmarking myself."

He reached into himself for the gift the way he had reached for it his whole life — and instead of letting it rise, he *poured*. 

It is hard to describe the giving-up of a thing that is woven through every hour of your life. It was like exhaling and knowing there would be no next breath. It was like his mother's hand slipping out of his. Yara Blackwood stood in the ruins of the great shrine and emptied himself out, and the gift went where he sent it: not into Ashen-Vey, not into Nethra, but *between* them — into the space where a covenant lives, into the binding-place, the crooked middle path his oath had taught him to walk for seven years.

The two gods screamed.

Not in rage — in *change*. Ashen-Vey's grey light and Nethra's gathered dark twisted together around the thread of Yara's gift, and he wove while they burned, weaving the way his mother had sung bees calm, the way he had once charmed a crow's broken wing, patience and pressure and love. He pulled the Order's seal-law into the weave, and Voss's broken-and-remade conviction, and Maren's terrible necessary faith, and Tam's doubt, and the widow's luck-charm, and the beggar's prayer at the dead shrine that had started everything — he pulled all of it in, everything the last nine days had made of Vell, and bound the gods into it.

*YOU CANNOT—* Ashen-Vey began.

*"I can,"* Yara said, in the voice of every beast in the city speaking with him, *"because I'm not doing it alone."*

And the beasts answered.

The rats rose in their hundreds from the catacombs. The rooks poured through the broken dome like black rain. Dogs howled in every quarter of Vell at once, and cats sang from fences, and horses screamed in their stables, and the salmon in the river leapt, and every living voice in the city joined itself to Yara's thread — not commanded, not leashed, *asked*, and answering. The gift passed through him and out of him and became theirs, became the covenant's, became the new law written not in iron but in fur and feather and scale:

*Magic returns to Vell. The shrines stay waking. The gods are bound to answer, and never to command. No speaker burns. No prayer goes unheard. No beast is a tool.*

Ashen-Vey dimmed. The furnace-eyes cooled to embers, then to hearth-glow, then to something almost gentle — a god of ashes learning, at the last, to be a god of hearths. Nethra folded downward, smaller and smaller, until she was a cat again, sitting in the ash before Yara, looking up at him with those green lamps.

*Oh, little voice,* she said, very softly. *You are giving away so much.*

"I know," Yara whispered.

The thread ran out. He felt it go — the last of it, the deep vein of it, the part that had been his mother's and hers before her — flowing out of him into the covenant, sealing it, finishing it. The world went quiet in a way it had never been quiet. Not silent. *Quiet.* The difference between a held breath and a resting one.

The candles guttered out. The ash stopped falling.

And Yara Blackwood, standing in the ruins with two hundred faithful and forty soldiers and a goddess-cat and a weeping priestess all staring at him, reached inward for the first time in his life and heard —

Nothing.

No rats in the walls. No pigeons on the eaves. No whisper of the cat. Just his own heartbeat, human and ordinary, loud in the sudden stillness.

"Yara?" Maren was beside him, catching his arm as his knees buckled. Her face was wet. "Yara, what did you—"

He tried to answer and found he didn't have the words, which was fitting, because for the first time since he was nine years old, there was nothing in his head but a man's own thoughts.

Across the shrine, Serah Voss lowered her seal-blade until its point touched the floor. She looked at the cooled god, at the kneeling faithful, at the ordinary man swaying in the priestess's grip — and slowly, joint by joint, like a wall coming down, she sank to one knee.

Around her, one by one, so did the faithful.

Vell, outside the broken dome, had gone utterly still. Then, from somewhere far off — a dog barked. Once. Twice. An ordinary dog, barking at nothing, at everything, at the new morning.

Yara laughed, or sobbed, or both, and let the darkness take him.

The last thing he saw was the cat, sitting in the ash, watching him with plain green eyes that held no words at all.
