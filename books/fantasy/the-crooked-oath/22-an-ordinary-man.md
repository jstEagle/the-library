## 22. An Ordinary Man

The bell above the door of Blackwood Charms rang at half past eight, the way it always did when the morning wind came off the canal, and Yara looked up from his ledger with the small, practiced smile of a man who had learned to be glad of interruptions.

It was only the wind. He went back to his figures.

The shop was narrow and warm-smelling — beeswax and dried lavender and the faint iron tang of river clay. Shelves ran floor to ceiling on both sides, crowded with jars of salt blessed by no one in particular, bundles of rowan twigs, candles poured in his own back room, and charms that were, for the first time in his life, exactly what they claimed to be. A knot of red thread against headaches. A hagstone on a leather cord for restful sleep. A little clay cat with a copper whisker, sold to mothers of teething children, which worked no magic whatsoever but which children loved, and which Yara made badly on purpose so that no two were ever alike.

Under the new covenant, he was required to post a license in the window. *Practitioner of Minor Rites, Third Class.* The parchment had cost him two days' earnings and a queue outside a government office that smelled of ink and fear slowly evaporating. He had hung it crooked, deliberately, and Tam had laughed until he cried when he saw it.

*You could have gotten First Class,* Tam had said. *They'd have given you anything. You bound six gods into a covenant with your bare soul.*

*I'd like to see the examination for that,* Yara had said, and that had been the end of it.

Third Class suited him. Third Class meant luck-charms and sleep-charms and sitting with the dying if their families asked, holding their hands and saying nothing magical at all, just saying things. Third Class meant being ordinary, and he had paid more for ordinariness than any man in Vell had ever paid for power, and he had never once regretted the price.

Mostly never. There were mornings. This was one of them.

He set down the ledger and flexed his right hand — the one that had burned brightest on the last night, the one the healers said should have scarred but hadn't, as if the fire had gone somewhere instead of into him. It ached sometimes in cold weather, a deep bone-ache like an old break, and on those mornings he caught himself listening. Not with his ears. With the place behind his ears where the listening used to live, the quiet room in him where ten thousand small voices had once crowded — sparrows arguing about gutters, dogs reporting the dreams they'd had, rats reciting the city's secrets in their dry, patient chorus, and above them all, threaded through everything, her voice: sardonic, ancient, amused, *well, fool, what have you broken today?*

The room behind his ears was silent now. It had been silent for a year. He had built other rooms there instead — memories mostly, and the ledger, and the recipe for cough syrup Maren had left him before they took her to judgment.

The bell rang again. This time it was a customer.

* * *

She was young, maybe nineteen, with a shawl pulled up over her hair and the particular hunched walk of someone carrying something heavy that wasn't visible. She stood in the doorway a moment, taking in the shelves, the jars, the crooked license, as if she'd expected something else entirely and didn't know what to do with what she'd found.

"Are you—" She stopped. Started again. "They say you're the one who did it. The covenant."

"I'm the one who sells charms," Yara said mildly. "The covenant was a group effort. Six gods, several thousand beasts, one very stubborn priestess, and a captain of the Iron Seal who committed treason in front of four hundred witnesses. I mostly talked."

Her mouth twitched. Then it wobbled, and then she was crying, quietly, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as though tears were a personal failing.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I just — my brother's been sick since winter. Fever that comes and goes. The physicians can't name it. And my grandmother used to say that before the Seal, you could take a sick child to a shrine and *ask*, and something would listen, and I thought — everyone says the gods are real now, everyone says it's legal, so I thought—"

"Bring him," Yara said.

She stared at him.

"Bring your brother. Tomorrow morning, before the shop opens. There's a rite — Third Class, nothing dramatic, it's in the licensed book, page forty." He was already moving along the shelves, pulling down a jar of willow bark and a twist of paper. "It's not a miracle. It's medicine with better manners. Sometimes the fever listens to it. Sometimes it doesn't, and then we go back to the physicians, and we keep going, because that's what people do now. That's the whole point of what we built. Nobody gets turned away, and nobody gets promised more than can be kept."

"You sound like a pamphlet," she said, wet-eyed and suddenly fierce.

"I've been reading a lot of pamphlets. They make me want to be better than they are." He wrapped the willow bark, added a sprig of dried yarrow from the jar Tam swore by and Yara trusted anyway, and slid both across the counter. "No charge today. Pay me when he's well. In bread, if you like. My baking is a crime against the city and I need professional help."

She laughed — a startled, cracked-open laugh — and took the parcel like it was made of glass, and thanked him three times on her way out the door, and the bell rang behind her, and Yara stood in the sudden quiet with his heart doing something complicated in his chest.

That was the part nobody had warned him about: that losing the gift would leave this behind. Not silence exactly. Something more like *reach*. For thirty years he had heard the city's every creature and told himself it made him safe, hidden, small. It had actually made him alone — one man sealed inside a private cathedral of voices, hoarding what he heard because sharing it would kill him. Now the cathedral was gone and the doors were open and people walked in out of the rain and asked him for help, and he could give it, plainly, legally, with his whole ordinary self.

His mother would have liked the shop. He thought that most days. She'd have rearranged all the shelves within an hour and argued with him about pricing and charmed the widow downstairs into bringing them pies. He kept her mortar and pestle on the top shelf, too high for customers to reach, where the light came through the window in the late afternoon and turned the old stone gold.

He reached up to straighten it — a habit, a small daily devotion — and that was when he saw the cat.

* * *

It sat on the windowsill outside, on the street side, in the exact spot where the sun hit first in the morning. Grey, with a torn ear and white socks and eyes the color of green river glass. It was not a large cat. It was not a remarkable cat. It was, by any measure the city could take, an ordinary stray — thin at the haunches, dignified about it, watching the street with the flat patience of a creature that has decided a location is its own and is merely waiting for the world to agree.

Yara's hand stopped on the mortar.

A year ago — a year and some weeks — he would have known its name. Not the name humans gave it; its own name, the taste-and-sound of it, the way cats named themselves after the thing they hunted or feared or loved. He would have known whether it had eaten, whether it had fought, what it thought of the new constable on Cordwainer Street, what the gulls were claiming about the harbor. He would have known all of it without wanting to, the way you know the smell of rain.

Now he knew nothing. It was a cat. It was weather and fur and appetite, opaque as a stone, and looking at it was like looking at a locked door in a house he used to own.

But it was *this* windowsill. It had been on this windowsill eleven days.

He had counted. He hated that he had counted.

The first day he'd assumed coincidence. Strays find warm ledges; that's what strays do. The third day it had watched him through the glass while he served customers, unblinking, and he'd told himself firmly that cats watch things, it's practically their profession. On the fifth day it had brought him something — a dead mouse, laid precisely on the sill's outer corner with the gravity of a formal presentation — and he had laughed out loud in the middle of weighing chamomile, and the customer had given him a strange look, and he hadn't been able to explain.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he had told no one, not Tam, not the widow downstairs, not the blank pages of his own journal: the gifts Nethra had taken, she had taken *all*. He could not hear the pigeons. He could not hear the horses in the livery yard or the dogs in the yards or the rats under the streets, and he missed even the rats, God help him, he missed their dry gossip like a man misses a language. The covenant had closed every door. Whatever looked out through a beast's eyes now looked out alone.

So if this cat stayed — if it chose this sill, day after day, out of all the sills in Vell —

It couldn't be her. He knew that. He had held her while the binding burned through her, felt the vast slow presence gutter down and down to something small and mortal, and the last thing she'd shown him — not said, *shown*, mind to mind, the only time in thirteen years she'd ever let him see the whole of her — had been a doorway closing gently, like a hand drawing a curtain, like a mother tucking a child in. *Sleep now, fool. You were never a burden. You were the point.*

Cats don't come back from that. Goddesses least of all.

He opened the window.

The cold came in with the smell of the canal, and the cat regarded him with its green glass eyes, and Yara Blackwood — binder of gods, breaker of oaths, the man whose name was carved on the restored great shrine and spoken in pamphlets he refused to read — crouched down on his own shop floor so that his face was level with a stray's, and waited.

"Hello," he said.

The cat blinked. Slowly. The way cats do, which is either nothing at all or everything, depending on the day and the depth of your loneliness.

"I can't hear you," he told it. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I want you to know that. Whatever you are — if you're her, if you're some piece of her that stuck, if you're just a cat who likes warm stone — I can't hear a word you say. I gave it up. All of it. That was the price, and I'd pay it again, I'd pay it twice, so don't sit there looking at me like—"

The cat stepped forward across the sill and touched its nose to his fingers.

Once. Briefly. Cold little nose, rough breath, the plain animal fact of it.

Then it settled back on its haunches, curled its torn-eared head toward the sun, and began, unmistakably, to purr.

Yara stayed crouched there a long moment, hand out, throat tight, feeling the purr come through the air between them — not through the old listening, not through any gift, just through the ordinary miracle of sound crossing a small distance on a Tuesday morning. Anyone could hear it. That was the unbearable beauty of it. Any person walking past on the street could have heard that cat purring, because it was a cat, purring, at a man, and none of it required magic at all.

"All right," he said finally, and his voice broke on the second word and he let it. "All right. Stay, then. But I'm naming you myself, and I warn you, I'm terrible at it."

* * *

He named her Sorrel, because of a patch of reddish fur behind one ear that he chose to believe was deliberate. She approved of the name by ignoring it completely, which he understood to be the highest honor her kind could confer.

She moved into the shop the way water moves into low ground — gradually, then totally. She had opinions about which chair was hers (the good one), which shelf was hers (the sunny one, currently occupied by the yarrow, which she evicted without discussion), and which hours of the day required her to be asleep on the ledger (most of them). She caught mice with theatrical excellence and ate perhaps one in five, leaving the rest as tribute in the same outer corner of the windowsill, an offering Yara eventually learned to clear away before customers arrived.

Tam came by on Thursdays with bread and gossip. Tam was rebuilding the rookery into a school now — small, careful, licensed, the first of its kind — teaching hedge-magic to farm kids and dock kids and one terrifyingly gifted child of an Iron Seal clerk. "Voss sent another donation," Tam reported, dropping a coin purse on the counter. "Anonymous. Very anonymous. She seals the envelope with wax and then wraps it in brown paper and then doesn't look at me the entire time she hands it over."

"How is she?"

"Better. Still sharp as broken glass. She walks the shrine circuit every evening, checks the wards herself. The faithful have started leaving her flowers, which she pretends to hate." Tam scratched Sorrel under the chin; she permitted it, regally. "And Maren?"

"Sentence commuted to service. Five years restoring shrines in the eastern districts." Yara turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. "She wrote to me. Long letter. Half apology, half argument — she still thinks she was right about the war, she just thinks she was wrong about the method. She asks about the shop. She asked—" he paused, "—she asked if I still had the mortar."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it's on the top shelf, where the light comes in." He looked up. "I told her when her service is done, there's work here if she wants it. Someone has to know the old rites properly, and I only know the ones that fit on page forty."

Tam studied him for a moment with those quick crow-bright eyes. "You forgive easily."

"No," Yara said. "I forgive *specifically*. There's a difference. She used me, and I'll carry that till I die, and she knows it, and she carries it too — I could hear it in every line of that letter, even without—" He smiled, wry, at the old reflex. "Even without anything. But she also spent seven years keeping a candle lit in the dark for people the city had thrown away, and my mother was one of them, and I think my mother would want the candle kept and the debt collected in the same breath. That's how she'd have done it. Crooked, but honest."

"Crooked but honest," Tam repeated, and raised his tea in salute. "Put it on the sign."

* * *

Closing time came at sundown, as it did in the shortening days of autumn. Yara swept the floor, banked the stove, checked the willow-bark order for tomorrow, and lit the lamp in the window — the little blue-glassed one that meant, in the new grammar of Vell, *a practitioner is here; knock anytime; no one is turned away.* Some nights it was a frightened kid with a burn. Some nights it was an old man who just wanted to sit where the gods could supposedly see him and talk aloud for a while. Once it had been a young Iron Seal trooper, off duty, out of uniform, who stood in the doorway shaking and said, "I was there. At the temple district. I held the line while civilians burned," and Yara had made him tea and listened, with the only ears he had left, for three hours.

Sorrel supervised all of this from her chair, contributing nothing, which was her contribution.

Last of all, as he always did, Yara climbed the stairs to the little apartment above the shop and paused at the window that overlooked the canal. The city lay out there in the lamplight and moonglow — the rebuilt shrine district, scaffolding still on the great dome; the canal barges; the distant glow of the rookery school; everywhere, woven through it, the small bright windows of people who no longer had to whisper their prayers. Somewhere out there, six gods slept the easy sleep of a covenant kept, dreaming together instead of apart. Somewhere out there, a rat was telling another rat about a crumpet, and neither of them needed him to understand it, and that was fine. That was more than fine. That was the world working as designed.

On the windowsill below — the shop sill, the sunny one, long past sunny at this hour — Sorrel had already taken up her night post, curled into a grey comma, one torn ear cocked toward the street.

Yara watched her for a while. He found, these days, that he could almost imagine the shape of what she might be saying — not hear it, never hear it, but feel the outline of it the way you feel the shape of a key through cloth. Something patient. Something that had chosen a spot and was waiting for the world to agree.

"Goodnight, your holiness," he murmured, and meant it as a joke, and it landed somewhere deeper than jokes go.

He blew out the lamp. Below him, in the dark, the cat purred — a small, stubborn, mortal sound, audible to anyone, needing no gift at all — and Yara Blackwood, ordinary man, went to bed in an extraordinary world, and slept the whole night through.
