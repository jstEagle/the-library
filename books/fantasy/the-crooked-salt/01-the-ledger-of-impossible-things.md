## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There were three rules at the Crooked Salt, and Emrys Fairweather had written two of them down so often they had worn grooves into their own handwriting.

Rule the first: *We trade in impossible things, therefore nothing that enters this house may be treated as ordinary.* Rule the second: *What is sold is sold truly; what is kept is kept honestly.* The third rule was older than the guild itself, older than the guildhouse, older, some said, than the guild's founder's regrettable taste in hats. It was not written anywhere. It lived instead in a scar across Emrys's throat and in twenty years of careful, deliberate, exhausting silence.

*Swordspeakers do not speak.*

Emrys had gotten very good at not speaking. They had, in fact, made an art of it, the way some people make an art of bread or calligraphy. Their silence had corners and textures. There was the polite silence they offered customers who haggled badly. There was the pointed silence for Maren when she suggested, yet again, that the guildhouse roof could wait one more winter. And there was the deep silence, the true one, the vow-shaped silence that sat behind their sternum like a stone dropped down a well — the one that never quite stopped echoing.

Tonight, all three silences were in residence, and Emrys was cataloguing.

The counting room of the Crooked Salt smelled of cedar oil, candlewax, and the faint metallic tang of things that remembered being dangerous. Shelves climbed the walls in crooked ranks — the building itself had settled over the centuries into a lean, like an old drunk leaning on a younger friend — and every shelf held its impossible inventory in careful order.

Emrys dipped a pen and opened the ledger to a fresh page.

*Item forty-one,* they wrote, in the small, severe hand that Maren called "funeral-ready" and Emrys considered simply *efficient*. *One storm, bottled. Provenance: the Widow Anselm of Harrow Fen, who caught it herself off the eaves during the equinox gale and would not be talked out of the price she asked, which was fair, though she also tried to include a chicken.*

Emrys glanced at the shelf where the storm sat. It was a squat bottle of green glass, corked with wax the color of a bruise, and inside it the weather turned. Clouds no bigger than a thumbnail rolled and grumbled against the glass. Occasionally a needle of lightning stitched itself from cloud to cloud, and the whole bottle flickered like a lamp deciding whether to be a lamp.

It was, Emrys reflected, a very good storm. Compact. Well-tempered. It had the sullen patience of weather that knew it would get out eventually and saw no reason to hurry.

*Condition: restless but contained,* Emrys wrote. *Note: do not shelve beside the bottled spring thaw. Last time they argued through the cork and we lost three days of sunshine and half the herb garden.*

They set down the pen, lifted the storm bottle with both hands — you handled weather respectfully or you learned why — and carried it to the far shelf, past the jars of last light from summer evenings (sold by the spoonful to people who needed one more hour), past the drawer of unspent apologies, past the locked case where the guild kept names that had not been earned yet, each one folded into paper like a moth waiting for its season.

On the way back, Emrys paused at the window.

Snow was coming. Not the polite first dusting of autumn, but the real thing — the long winter's opening argument, fat flakes falling straight and silent out of a sky the color of dishwater. Beyond the guildhouse wall, the town of Lowmark was shuttering itself for the season. Lamplighters hurried. A dog barked once at the snow and thought better of it.

Emrys watched the flakes come down and felt the particular contentment that they had spent twenty years teaching themselves to feel. Quiet house. Warm fire. Work done well. No one looking for them.

*Invisibility is safety,* they reminded themselves, as they did every night, the way another person might say a prayer. *No name on any list. No voice on any wind. The war is over because the ones who could start it again chose to be no one.*

It was almost true, if you said it enough times.

* * *

Maren found them an hour later, which meant Maren had finished her third cup of cider and was now operating at the precise level of honesty she liked best.

She filled the doorway of the counting room the way she filled every doorway — broadly, comfortably, with the air of a woman who owned the door and several doors like it. She was broad-shouldered and gray-streaked and had hands like a shipwright's, and she had been quartermaster of the Crooked Salt longer than Emrys had been its ledger-keeper, a fact she mentioned whenever she wanted to win an argument, which was always.

"You've alphabetized the regrets again," she said.

"They were out of order."

"Regrets are *supposed* to be out of order, Emrys. That's what makes them regrets." She came in, stole an apple from the bowl Emrys kept for exactly this purpose, and settled onto the counting stool with a creak that the stool had long since accepted as fate. "Storm's shelved?"

"Shelved."

"Courage vial?"

Emrys nodded toward the desk, where a stoppered phial of pale gold liquid sat in its velvet cradle, glowing faintly, the way courage does. *Item forty-two: borrowed courage, one measure. Lent by a knight who no longer needed it and will want it back someday, gods help us all.* Borrowed courage was tricky stock. Unlike most of the guild's wares, it had to be returned — courage, once given away, left a hollow in the giver shaped exactly like whatever they'd been afraid of. The knight in question had sent it south with a note reading *I have decided to be afraid of nothing, which I am told is a symptom.* Emrys had filed the note under *Medical.*

"Maren," Emrys wrote on their slate, because that was how they spoke — chalk and slate, quick and dry, a voice made of schoolroom dust. *You only visit the counting room when you want something.*

"I want nothing." She ate the apple with tremendous innocence. "I'm checking on my friend."

Emrys raised an eyebrow and wrote: *Friend.*

"My associate."

Emrys wrote nothing, which in slate-language was a devastating rejoinder, and Maren snorted.

"Fine. I came to say the roof leaked over the east storeroom and I've put a bucket under it, and before you write *the roof can be mended in spring*, I want you to know that I have heard that sentence four winters running and I am keeping a ledger of my own."

Emrys wrote: *A ledger?*

"A ledger of your excuses. It's very thorough. You'd be proud." But her eyes had gone soft in the way they did when she was about to say something kind and hated doing it. She looked around the counting room — at the shelves, the storm muttering in its bottle, the small figure of Emrys bent over the great book with their candle — and something in her face went quiet. "Twenty years," she said. "You've kept our books twenty years, and in twenty years I don't think I've heard your voice above a whisper, and never once the — " she waved a hand vaguely, " — the other thing. The old thing."

Emrys's chalk hovered.

They did not talk about the old tongue. That was rather the point of it. The old tongue was the language that had once commanded blades to sing — really sing, with intent and memory and appetite — and it had sung so many blades, in the Sundering War, that the fields of three kingdoms had gone black and stayed black. When the war ended, the surviving swordspeakers had done the arithmetic of grief and arrived at a single answer: *never again*. They had bound themselves, each one, oath by scar and scar by oath. Silence, sworn in the deepest part of the voice, where even whispers could not dig it out.

Emrys set down the chalk and wrote, slowly: *Some doors are locked for a reason.*

"And some locks," Maren said, standing and brushing apple crumbs from her apron, "rust shut on the people inside them." She paused at the door, and her voice gentled further, which for Maren was nearly a confession. "There's stew on the hearth. Come eat while it's hot. Even ghosts should eat."

*I am not a ghost,* Emrys wrote.

"No," Maren agreed, and left. "Ghosts have more fun."

* * *

Later — much later, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the storm on its shelf had worked itself into a sulky thunder-sleep — Emrys finished the page, blotted the ink, and closed the ledger.

They sat for a moment in the warm dark, listening to the house. The Crooked Salt talked at night; all old houses do, but this one had opinions. Timbers ticked. The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs, Tess the hedge-witch muttered in her sleep about tariffs. The bottled storm dreamed its small dreams of ruin.

This was the hour Emrys loved best and admitted to loving least. The honest hour. In the daytime they could pretend that the silence was a preference, that the ledgers were a calling, that a life spent recording other people's wonders was a life entire. But at this hour the truth stood next to the desk like a patient customer: that Emrys Fairweather had once spoken a language that made steel remember joy, and had chosen — rightly, *rightly* — to never speak it again, and that the choosing had taken everything else with it. Every road home. Every name they might have been called by. Twenty years of chalk dust where a voice used to be.

*Invisibility is safety,* they told the dark.

The dark, politely, said nothing.

Emrys banked the fire, took up the candle, and was halfway to the stairs when they heard it.

At first they thought it was the storm bottle. Then they thought it was the wind. Then they understood it was neither, because it was coming from the front door, and it was small, and it was rhythmic, and it was a knock.

Three knocks. A pause, as if the knocker had lost their nerve. Three more, weaker.

Emrys stood very still. Snow fell against the windows in long silver strokes. Nobody knocked at the Crooked Salt after midnight unless they were desperate, and nobody was desperate in *this* particular way — out of the falling long winter, out of the dark, alone — unless they had nowhere else in the world to be.

Emrys crossed the hall, set their hand on the latch, and hesitated, because twenty years of caution is not a coat you take off quickly.

Then they opened the door, and the cold poured in, and the snow, and something else — something that made every blade in the guildhouse, from the kitchen knives to the rusted ceremonial sword over the mantel, turn in its sleep.

Standing in the drifts was a child. Small, starved, snow-crusted, wrapped in a blanket that had been fine cloth once. Lips blue. Eyes enormous.

And when the child looked up at Emrys and spoke — one word, a word like a key turning in a lock older than kingdoms — Emrys's blood went cold in a way the weather had nothing to do with.

Because Emrys knew that language.

Emrys had sworn, in blood and scar, never to speak it again.

And here it was, standing on the doorstep, shivering.
