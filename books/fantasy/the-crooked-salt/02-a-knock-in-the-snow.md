## 2. A Knock in the Snow

The first hard snow of the falling winter came at dusk, the way bad news always does—quietly, then all at once.

Emrys Fairweather heard it before they saw it: a change in the sound of the world outside the Crooked Salt, a softening, as though someone had laid wool over the whole city. They stood at the tall window of their rooms above the guildhouse and watched the flakes come down fat and deliberate, each one taking its time, as if the sky had decided to be thorough about it. By morning there would be a hand's depth on the sills. By the night after, the gutters would groan and the city's beggars would begin to die in the usual numbers, and the ledgers would need a new column for *coffins, pine, unadorned*.

Emrys did not write that column yet. Instead they lit the lamp, warmed the ink bottle against the ceramic tile near the hearth—in winter the ink went thick as regret—and went down to the counting room to finish the evening's entries.

The Crooked Salt occupied a narrow wedge of building between a chandler's and a house that had been condemned so many times it had developed opinions about it. From the street it looked like nothing: shuttered windows, a door the color of old dishwater, a sign with no words on it at all, just the guild's mark—a salt spoon bent into an S, crooked as its name. Inside, however, the counting room ran long and low and warm, shelved to the ceiling with ledgers bound in leather the color of every kind of patience. Each ledger held its own impossible inventory.

*Bottled storms*, shelf nine. *Borrowed years*, shelf two, kept apart from *stolen years*, which were a different trade entirely and a moral quagmire besides. *Names not yet earned*, shelf fourteen, and Emrys handled those with cotton gloves because an unearned name could bite.

Tonight's work was pleasant, as such work went. A bottled storm had come back from a client in the hill country who had bought it to frighten off a rival's wedding procession and found, upon uncorking it, that the storm had spent thirty years in glass developing *opinions*. It had flattened his barn, his henhouse, and his dignity, in that order, and he wanted a refund.

Emrys held the bottle up to the lamplight. The storm inside was small now, sulky, a fistful of gray cloud turning in slow circles and throwing off the occasional spiteful spark. On the label, in the original cataloger's hand: *One (1) thunderstorm, temperamental. Do not store near hope.*

Emrys made a note in the margin: *Returned. Client error. Storm functioning as designed.* Then they logged the vial of borrowed courage that had come in from a knight who had used it to propose marriage, succeeded, and immediately felt so fraudulent about the whole affair that he'd demanded his courage back. The vial sat on the counter glowing a faint, embarrassed gold.

*Courage, borrowed, one dose, partially used. Note: returns of this item are more common than sales. Consider revising trade terms.*

It was good work. It was quiet work. It was, Emrys told themselves for the four-thousandth time, exactly the life they had chosen. Invisibility was safety. Silence was the shape safety took when you were the last of your kind. A swordspeaker who never spoke could pass through the world like a draft under a door—felt, perhaps, but never seen, never named, never dragged into a square and made an example of while the crowd remembered what blades could do when words got into them.

Twenty years since the last of the order had died. Twenty years since Emrys had stood over that deathbed and sworn the oath in the old tongue itself, which was either very pious or very rude, depending on how you looked at it: *I will keep your words. I will not speak them.* The oath lived in Emrys's chest like a swallowed coal. Some nights it was only warm. Most nights it was only there, the way a scar is only there.

They were sealing the day's ledger with a drop of wax when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they have learned that large knocks get doors opened faster, but also get children shouted at.

Emrys went still, quill hovering. Nobody knocked on the Crooked Salt's door by accident. The street did not know the door existed; the street had been persuaded of this, gently and expensively, some years ago. Clients came by introduction, at appointed hours, having first stood across the road for a full minute convincing themselves. Children did not come at all.

The knock came again. Three more. Smaller, somehow. More determined.

Emrys crossed the counting room, lifted the little brass viewing plate, and looked out into a world gone white and blue with snowfall.

A child stood on the step. Small—a head shorter than the door's iron latch, which put her somewhere around eight or ten, though hunger edits age downward. She was wrapped in what had once been a man's coat and was now merely a tent with sleeves, crusted gray with road salt and older filth. Her hair was black as wet slate, plastered to her skull. Snow had built up around her feet in a little drift, which meant she had been standing there for some time, working up the nerve, or waiting for whoever watched the door to be fooled.

She was not shivering. That was the first wrong thing. In that cold, everything shivers—the dead things most of all, in a manner of speaking. But the girl stood still as a fencepost, looking directly at the viewing plate as though she knew precisely where it was, and her eyes were the second wrong thing: pale, pale gray, almost white, the color of the underside of ice.

Emrys let the plate fall shut and stood in the dark of their own hallway with their heart going like a drum in a parade.

*Turn her away,* said twenty years of careful living. *You don't know her. You don't owe her. The guild owes nothing to anyone who hasn't been introduced, and she has not been introduced, and the snow is deep, and the Crown has ears in every gutter, and you are the last swordspeaker alive, and the last thing—*

The knock came a third time, and this time it was followed by a voice, thin and cracked from cold, saying one word through the door.

Not a word Emrys knew. Not a word in any of the eleven languages Emrys read and the six they spoke and the three they pretended not to. And yet Emrys understood it completely, the way you understand a blow: *open.*

Because it wasn't any language Emrys had learned. It was the language underneath them all. The pre-kingdom tongue. The tongue that had once been spoken in the halls where blades hung on walls and *sang*—that had commanded steel like a shepherd commands a dog, that had turned farm scythes into armies and armies into ash, that had started the Sundering War and written its name in fire across half a continent before the kings of the new age had done the only wise thing anyone had ever done about it.

They had killed everyone who spoke it. All but the order of swordspeakers, who were permitted to keep the tongue on the strict condition that they never, ever use it—an oath sealed in blood and law and, in Emrys's case, in something considerably more personal.

And now it was standing on the doorstep in a dead man's coat, saying *open* in a voice like a cold bell.

Emrys's hand was on the bolt before they had decided anything. This is the treachery of hands: they act on old kindnesses the mind has formally renounced. The bolt slid back. The door swung in on its oiled hinges, letting in a blade of freezing air and the girl along with it, and Emrys stood aside as she walked past them into the warmth with the unhurried certainty of a cat entering a house it has already chosen.

She stopped in the middle of the counting room floor. She looked at the shelves. She looked at the bottled storm sulking on the counter, and tilted her head at it, and the storm—Emrys would swear this later, under questioning, to Maren, who would laugh—went quiet. Settled. Like a dog that has decided the stranger is all right after all.

Then the girl turned and looked up at Emrys with those ice-pale eyes and said a string of words in that old, old tongue, and Emrys understood none of the grammar and all of the meaning:

*I was sent to find the one who keeps words.*

Every hair on Emrys's arms rose. The coal of the oath in their chest flared hot enough to hurt.

"No," Emrys said aloud, in plain, honest, safe Lowmarket speech. "No. Whatever you were told, whatever sent you—I'm not—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, because shouting at a starving child was the sort of thing the person Emrys had sworn to be simply did not do. "There's food," they said instead, hating themselves. "Kitchen's through there. Sit down. Don't touch anything."

She ate like winter itself had hired her as an instrument: bread and dripping, the heel of a cheese, three cups of milk warmed on the stove, a jar of pickled plums that Emrys had been saving for no reason worth admitting. She ate neatly, though, without greed, pausing between dishes to look around the kitchen as though memorizing it. When she finished, she folded her napkin into a small precise square, set down her spoon parallel to her bowl, and said, in the old tongue, a word Emrys did know—not from study, God knew, but from the oldest place, the place where the oath lived:

*Sable.*

Her name. Or the only word of herself she had left. Emrys wrote it down without meaning to, on the back of a receipt, in the shorthand they used for inventory: *one (1) child, arrived unscheduled, condition: starving, contents: impossible.*

Then Emrys sat down across from her, put their face in their hands, and understood, with the slow sick clarity of a man watching the first snowflake of an avalanche, what they had done.

They had opened the door. They had fed her. They had *written her down*. Somewhere in the guild's books there was now a line in Emrys's own hand connecting the Crooked Salt to a child who spoke the tongue of the Sundering, and the Crown paid good fresh coin for exactly that kind of line, and somebody—somebody—had already sold information out of this guild once this year, or Emrys was a bishop.

The girl—Sable—watched them across the table. Then she reached out one small chapped hand and touched the back of Emrys's wrist, lightly, the way you touch a horse you don't yet trust, and said something soft in the old tongue. Emrys didn't need grammar for it. It meant, roughly: *I know you're afraid. I'm afraid too. The soldiers are coming behind me.*

Outside, the snow fell harder, erasing the road, the footprints, the whole white world—everything except, somewhere far off in the dark, the sound of hooves.

Emrys closed their eyes. *Invisibility is safety,* they told themselves one last time, without conviction, like a prayer said by someone who has stopped believing in the god but likes the architecture.

Then they got up, banked the fire, and went to wake Maren, because quartermasters, whatever else they were, were the people you woke when the inventory started talking back.
