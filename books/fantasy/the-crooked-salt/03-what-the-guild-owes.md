## 3. What the Guild Owes

Maren Voss had kept the ledgers of the Crooked Salt for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one iron law of the trade: the stranger the merchandise, the more paperwork it required. A bottled storm needed three signatures and a humidity clause. A borrowed year needed a notarized apology to whoever you took it from. So when she came down the stairs on the second morning of the snowfall and found a half-frozen child sitting at her counting table, eating porridge out of the inventory bowl, her first instinct was not alarm.

Her first instinct was to ask whether anyone had filled out the intake form.

Nobody had, of course. Emrys stood in the corner like a man guarding a door that had already been kicked open, arms crossed, radiating the particular misery of someone who had done a kind thing and wanted credit for none of it. The child—Sable, they said, though Maren suspected that was less a name than a placeholder, the way you'd label an unlabeled crate *contents unknown*—looked up at her with eyes too old for the face above them and kept eating.

"Well," Maren said, setting down her lamp. "Someone explain to me why my good porridge bowl is now guild property."

"She was starving," Emrys said. Their voice, as always, was quiet and precise, a voice used to being rationed. "She knocked. I opened."

"You *opened*." Maren pulled out a chair and sat, because this was going to take a while. "Emrys Fairweather, you have not opened that door after dark in eleven years. When the tinker's boy got caught in the whiteout two winters back, you made him sleep in the woodshed and passed him blankets through a window crack like we were feeding a bear."

"The tinker's boy," Emrys said, "was not speaking a dead language."

Maren looked at the child. The child looked at Maren, and said something—a short phrase, liquid and strange, syllables that seemed to arrive a half-second before her mouth moved. The candle flames all leaned toward her at once, very slightly, like flowers toward sun, and then straightened up again as if embarrassed.

Maren had been quartermaster of the Crooked Salt long enough to be unimpressed by most things. Bottled thunderstorms, fine. A jar containing somebody's grandmother's last breath, seen it twice. But candle flames leaning was new, and new things in her guildhouse were either money or trouble, and her experience said they were usually both.

"That," she said, "is not a dead language. Dead languages don't do that."

"I know what it is," Emrys said, in the tone of someone who wished they didn't.

"Then say it plainly, because I'm not playing riddles before breakfast."

Emrys was silent long enough that Sable stopped eating and looked between them, sensing, the way children and cats always did, that she was the subject under discussion. Finally Emrys said: "It's the tongue from before the kingdoms. The one the swordspeakers spoke. The one that started the Sundering War."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked its opinion.

"And you let her in anyway," Maren said.

"Yes."

"And fed her."

"Yes."

"Out of the good bowl."

"*Yes*, Maren."

Maren nodded slowly, and reached over, and turned the ledger on the table so it faced Emrys, and tapped the open page with one blunt finger. "Then write her in. Intake column. Date, description, provenance unknown, classification—" she thought about it— "*impossible thing, living.* We've sheltered bottled storms, Emrys. We've sheltered a well that grants wishes but only insults you. We are currently storing, in cellar three, a door that opens onto next Tuesday. A child who speaks the language that ended the world is frankly more housebroken than the door."

"You don't understand what she is."

"I understand exactly what she is. She's cold, she's hungry, and she's mine now—that's how it works when they eat out of the good bowl, it's an ancient and binding contract." Maren folded her hands. "What I don't understand is why you're looking at her like she's a debt come due."

* * *

The sealed records lived in the vault beneath the guildhouse, behind a door that required three keys, a spoken passphrase, and, according to guild legend, a small bribe for whoever had built the lock. Maren held two of the keys. Emrys held the third. Neither of them had opened the vault together since the incident with the wishing well, which Maren maintained had been handled responsibly and Emrys maintained had been handled at all.

They went down now, the child between them, because Emrys had gone pale over breakfast and said, "I need to check something," in the voice of a person about to confirm a fear, and Maren had said, "Then check it where I can watch you do it," because that was friendship, in her accounting: being present for other people's worst ideas so you could bill them for candles later.

The vault was cold and smelled of dust and older decisions. Emrys lit the lamps with hands that were not quite steady, and Maren pretended not to notice, and Sable wandered along the shelves reading spines she couldn't possibly read, trailing one finger across the leather like she was greeting animals.

"The sealed correspondence," Emrys said. "Pre-Sundering acquisitions. There were swordspeakers who traded here, before the war. Before the silencing. If any of their letters survive—"

"They're in the black chest. Third shelf. The one that bites."

"The one that *what*?"

"It bit Aldous in '61. It has opinions about who touches it." Maren fetched it herself, wrapped her apron over her hand, and set it on the table. "Open it, then. Let's see your dead language."

Emrys opened the chest. Inside lay letters bound in faded ribbon, and a single slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, and Emrys unwrapped it the way other people disarmed traps—which, Maren reflected, was probably accurate. The book was old past sense, its pages soft as cloth, covered in a flowing script that hurt to look at sideways, the way bright snow hurts.

"Now," Emrys said, and knelt to bring the page level with the child's eyes. "Sable. Can you read this?"

Sable bent over the page. Her lips moved. And then she began, softly, to speak—not translating, Maren realized, not rendering it into some common tongue, but simply *reading aloud*, the way a literate person reads, easily, the way you'd read a letter from home.

Every lamp in the vault flared. The biting chest snapped its lid once, respectfully, like applause. And from the shadows at the back of the vault came a sound that raised every hair on Maren's arms: a low, sweet, metallic hum, rising and falling, unmistakably a tune. She turned. On the wall hung a display of confiscated weapons, guild policy, nothing sharp left lying around—and among them an old rusted sword, junk, unsellable, hanging there for thirty years.

It was singing. Quietly. Happily. Like a dog hearing its name.

"Emrys," Maren said carefully. "Your book."

Emrys wasn't listening. Emrys had gone absolutely still, staring at the page, at the child, at the sword, and Maren watched twenty years of careful silence rearrange themselves behind those grey eyes like furniture in a flooded house.

"It matches," Emrys said at last, barely audible. "The script. The grammar. It's—it's the same tongue. She reads pre-Sundering script like I read trade contracts." They closed the book with exaggerated gentleness, as if it might wake up. "This is impossible."

"We're the Crooked Salt," Maren said. "Impossible things are our entire inventory. Write it down."

But Emrys wasn't writing anything down. Emrys was looking at Sable with an expression Maren had catalogued before, usually on customers about to make catastrophic purchases—the expression of a person who has already decided and is merely waiting for the courage to admit it.

"You care about her already," Maren said.

"I am *assessing* her."

"You've known her one day and you've fed her, warmed her, and brought her into the vault, which you wouldn't do for the Crown's own mother. You care."

"Maren—"

"Don't 'Maren' me. I've watched you refuse to care about things for eleven years. You're very bad at it. You care the way a roof leaks—slowly, and then all at once, and then there's water in the pantry." She jabbed a finger at the humming sword, which had begun, if anything, to harmonize with itself. "And more to the point: that blade hasn't made a sound since we hung it up. Whatever she is, whatever that tongue does, it's already started. The question isn't whether you care about the child. The question is whether you're going to help her or just stand there feeling guilty while the world notices her."

"The world noticing her," Emrys said quietly, "is exactly what I'm afraid of. That language ended a war by starting ten others. If the Crown learns what she is—if anyone learns—"

The vault door, three floors up, slammed open hard enough to shake dust from the rafters.

Both of them froze. Footsteps crossed the main floor above—heavy, unhurried, more than one pair, and beneath them the particular clink of coin purses and the particular creak of armor that tries to pretend it isn't armor. Then a voice, smooth as fresh-minted silver, calling down through the floor:

"Honorable keepers of the Crooked Salt! A customer! I've come about a rather unusual item—I'm told you recently acquired a child who speaks wrong."

Maren looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at Maren. Above them, politely, the biting chest began to growl.
