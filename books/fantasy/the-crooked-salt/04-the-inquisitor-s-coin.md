## 4. The Inquisitor's Coin

The coin was wrong before the man wearing it was wrong.

Emrys noticed it first, because Emrys noticed everything through the ledgers, and the ledgers did not lie. That was the whole point of them. The Crooked Salt traded in impossible things, but its accounts were ruthlessly honest: what came in, what went out, who owed whom, and what had been paid in storm-bottles, borrowed years, or the occasional unearned name. Every entry sat in Emrys's neat hand, because Emrys could not say the things other people said, so they wrote them instead. Twenty years of ink. Twenty years of silence, kept like a garden.

So when the stranger set a single silver crown on the counter to pay for a jar of pickled fog—a novelty item, truly, Maren stocked it mostly to amuse children—and Emrys turned the coin over in their palm, they felt it at once.

Too bright. Too new. Minted silver darkens; it wants to darken, the way all metal remembers being ore. This coin gleamed like the day it left the die, which meant either someone had polished it with obsessive devotion, or it had come straight from the Crown's mint in Vaelharren, four hundred miles south, without passing through a single pocket on the way.

Nobody paid for pickled fog with treasury-fresh silver.

Emrys looked up.

The man across the counter was unremarkable by design—gray traveling cloak, gray eyes, a face arranged into the particular blankness of someone who has practiced not having one. He had snowmelt on his shoulders and the smell of long roads about him, horse sweat and cold leather. But under all that, faint as a watermark, was something else. Ink. Lamp oil. And the specific, institutional cleanliness of a person whose boots were expensive but deliberately scuffed.

"Good evening," he said pleasantly. "I'm told you deal in unusual goods."

"We deal in inventory," Maren called from the back room, where she was doing something loud with a crate of bottled thunderclaps. "Whether it's unusual is between the item and its buyer."

Emrys set the coin down and slid it back across the counter. *Wrong mint,* they wrote on their slate, because even now—even with dread already pooling cold in their stomach—they would not spend bad money. *We take guild scrip, trade barter, or honest coin.*

"Honest coin," the man repeated, as though tasting the word. "Of course." He produced another from his purse, older, properly worn, and laid it down with the air of a man making a small point about patience. "Forgive me. Habit. I handle a great deal of new money."

*Who handles new money?* Emrys wrote.

"Mints," he said. "Treasury offices. Certain offices of inquiry." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners, and Emrys thought: *there it is.* "Inquisitor Dannel Corvane," he added, and did not produce credentials, which was somehow worse than producing them. "But tonight I'm only a customer."

Behind Emrys, the back-room noise stopped.

Maren appeared in the doorway wiping her hands on her apron, a broad woman built like a well-stocked pantry, with the expression of someone who had been interrupted mid-curse and was deciding whether to resume it. She took in the stranger—the cloak, the smile, the second coin—and Emrys watched her arrive at the same conclusion they had, roughly three seconds slower but considerably louder.

"Inquisitor," she said flatly. "We're not in arrears on anything. I check."

"I'm sure you aren't. The Crooked Salt has always been scrupulous." Corvane folded his hands on the counter like a man settling in for tea. "No, this is a private purchase. A small thing. Information, if you sell it. If you don't, then I'll have the fog and be on my way, and no one will remember my visit at all."

Emrys wrote: *Information isn't in the catalog.*

"Everything's in someone's catalog," Corvane said mildly. He reached into his cloak and drew out a folded paper, smoothed it flat on the counter between them, and turned it around.

It was a sketch. Charcoal, competent, done from life by someone who'd been close enough to touch her.

Sable.

Small face, sharp chin, eyes too big for it, hair cropped raggedly as though someone had cut it in a hurry with a knife instead of scissors. The artist had caught the way she held herself—shoulders in, hands hidden—as if the world had recently taught her to make less of herself.

Emrys's blood went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when you hear the floorboard creak upstairs.

"This child passed through the region some days ago," Corvane said. "Or will. Reports are inconsistent. She speaks a language our scholars find... irregular. Wrong, some have called it. Words that don't match any tongue in the Crown's records going back nine hundred years." He tapped the sketch once, gently, as if knocking on a door. "A child who speaks wrong. Have you seen her?"

*No,* Emrys began to write, and stopped.

Because here was the thing about twenty years of oaths: they teach you precision. A swordspeaker's silence was not emptiness—it was discipline, every unsaid word held behind the teeth like a drawn bow. Emrys had spent two decades turning speech into ink, and ink did not tremble, and ink did not hesitate, and above all ink did not pause mid-word while an inquisitor watched.

But Emrys paused.

It lasted perhaps half a heartbeat. Perhaps less. It was enough.

Corvane's gray eyes warmed by exactly one degree. "Ah," he said softly, like a man hearing a lock turn. "You have."

Maren moved. It was a small movement—she simply came to stand beside Emrys at the counter—but she did it the way she did everything, with the full weight of a woman who had quarreled with the guild's rules for thirty years and won most of them. "That child," she said, "is none of your business, inquisitor, and neither are we. You want fog? Fog's a copper. You want trouble? Trouble costs extra, and we don't stock it."

"The Crown pays well for trouble avoided." He slid a small pouch across the counter. It landed with the dense, satisfied clink of real money. "For information already given, actually. Someone was kind enough to write us ahead of your visitor's arrival. We're merely confirming details." His gaze drifted, unhurried, past Emrys's shoulder—to the stairs, to the closed door at the top of them, behind which a small girl was supposedly asleep, and was in fact almost certainly listening at the keyhole, because she was eight years old and had survived soldiers and knew exactly what listening at keyholes was for.

Emrys did not look. It took everything they had.

*A letter,* they wrote, keeping the slate steady by an act of will that cost more than any swordwork ever had. *Someone sent you a letter.*

"A very informative one. Unsigned. Good penmanship." Corvane rose, collected his pickled fog, and tucked the sketch away with the tenderness of a man putting a bird back in a cage. "You'll understand I had to see for myself. Reports exaggerate. Letters exaggerate. Faces don't." He settled his cloak, and at the door he paused, hand on the latch, and turned back with that same pleasant warmth.

"For what it's worth—and it may be worth something, coming from me—the Crown doesn't want to hurt her. The Crown wants to *use* her, which is kinder, in the way that harnesses are kinder than knives. There are people above me, however, who feel differently, and winter makes everyone impatient." He opened the door onto the whirling dark and the falling snow. "Do think about which kind of kindness you'd rather arrange for her."

Then he was gone, and the cold came in after him like a debt collector, and the door shut, and the guildhouse was silent except for the fire and the wind.

Emrys stood at the counter for a long moment. Then they picked up the pouch he'd left, untied it, and poured the coins out across the ledger.

Every one of them was fresh from the mint. Bright as sin. He hadn't even pretended, the second time.

"He knows," Maren said quietly. "He walked in knowing, didn't he. The letter, the questions—that was theater. He came to look at our faces."

Emrys nodded slowly. They looked down at the coins, and the dread in their chest did something strange: it cracked, and underneath it there was heat. Not comfort. Something sharper. For twenty years Emrys Fairweather had told the truth in ink and silence both, had let the oath be the shape of their whole soul, and tonight, when it mattered—

They picked up the slate, wiped it clean with their sleeve, and wrote:

*I lied to him.*

"You hesitated," Maren said. "That's not lying."

*I was about to.* Emrys stared at the words as though someone else had written them. Their heart was slamming against their ribs like a fist on a locked door, and beneath the terror ran something that felt disgracefully like joy, thin and bright as the edge of a blade. Breaking ice, they thought. That's what it felt like. Standing on a frozen river and hearing it give, and discovering you wanted to hear it give again.

Maren read the slate twice. Then she did something rarer than inquisitors' visits: she put her hand briefly on Emrys's shoulder, heavy and warm. "Well," she said gruffly. "About time."

But Emrys wasn't listening anymore. They were looking at the ledger, at the neat columns of honest entries, and thinking about unsigned letters with good penmanship. Someone had written to Vaelharren. Someone who knew Sable was here, knew what she was, knew the guildhouse well enough to describe it. Someone inside these walls.

Their eyes went, unwilling, to the shelf where the sealed guild records lived—the old ones, the ones from before the Sundering War, the ones Emrys had opened two nights ago with shaking hands to compare against a child's sleeping murmurs. Those records were supposed to be known to four people. Only four.

And downstairs, faint through the floorboards, almost lost under the wind, came the sound of Sable singing to herself in the dark—in the old tongue, soft as embers—and every key in the guild ringed gently, like struck glass, like small bells remembering something.

* * *

Upstairs, later, Emrys stood outside Sable's door with a cup of honeyed milk they had no way to explain, and listened to the silence inside, and made a list in their head the way they made lists in ledgers: bolt the doors, burn the letter-sketch, watch the hands of everyone who touched the ledgers, and learn, somehow, before the snows deepened and the roads filled with soldiers, how to protect a child who spoke the language that had once taught swords to sing.

Below, in the common room, someone turned the guild's front sign to CLOSED.

Above the sign, unnoticed, the rusted ceremonial sword that hung as decoration—hung there for sixty years, hung there dead as a nail—shivered once on its pegs.

And hummed.
