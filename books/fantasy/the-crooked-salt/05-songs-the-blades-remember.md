## 5. Songs the Blades Remember

The tall one with the quiet hands did not sleep.

Sable knew this because she did not sleep either, and because the guildhouse at night was a small country of sounds, and she had learned its borders in three days. The fire ticked. The beams above her pallet groaned like old men complaining about weather. Somewhere below, mice argued about a crust of bread with the passion of kings disputing a throne. And in the corner room where they had put her—warm, dry, smelling of cedar and lamp oil—the tall one sat with their ledgers and their lamp and their silence, and did not sleep.

They were afraid of her.

She could not blame them. She was afraid of herself.

Sable pulled the rough blanket to her chin and listened to the house breathe. In her village, before, the nights had sounded different: goats, wind off the fells, her grandmother's low humming through the wall, the old words worn smooth as river stones from use. *Come inside, little ember. The dark is only hungry if you feed it.* She had thought those words would last forever, the way children think everything lasts forever.

Then the soldiers came, and forever turned out to be one afternoon long.

She rolled onto her side. Under her pallet, wrapped in the shirt she'd been wearing when the world ended, was the thing she had carried out of the fire: a sword. Not hers. Her grandmother's—or her grandmother's grandmother's; no one living knew. It was rusted nearly to nothing, more memory than metal, and it was the reason she had walked north through the snow until her feet split, because her grandmother's last words had been pushed into her hands along with the blade:

*Find the one who keeps words. North. Past the salt roads. They will know your tongue even if they cannot answer it.*

*Why can't they answer it?* Sable had asked, and there had been no time for answers, only for shoving, only for smoke, only for *run.*

The tall one kept words. Sable was almost sure of it. When she spoke—even small things, *thank you*, *more bread please*—the tall one went still as a held breath, and their eyes did the thing she had seen once on a man who had put his hand into a trap meant for wolves. Pain, and wonder, and the terrible knowledge that he had done it to himself.

But the tall one never answered. Never a word of the old tongue. Not one.

*Everyone is afraid,* Sable told the ceiling beam. *Everyone is always afraid.* She said it in her own language, the old one, the pre-kingdom one that made lamps flicker and hinges remember being trees. The beam creaked back at her, apologetic.

That was the part nobody understood, she thought. It wasn't a weapon, her language. It wasn't a spell. It was just... talking, but honest. When she spoke to a thing in the old words, the thing remembered what it was. That was all. A door remembered being an oak and swung gratefully, glad to be wood again for a moment. A kettle remembered rain and sang. A loaf of bread, once, had remembered the wheat field so vividly that the whole kitchen smelled of summer, and the cook had cried without knowing why, and Sable had learned that grown people could be undone by the smell of a field.

Things liked being remembered. People were the ones who got frightened.

* * *

In the morning, the round loud one—Maren, keeper of keys and opinions—found her in the cellar.

Sable hadn't meant to go to the cellar. But the cellar was where the Crooked Salt kept its impossible things, and impossible things called to her the way hurt animals call to certain people. She had felt it through the floorboards since the first night: a thin, threadlike keening, like a violin string someone had forgotten to stop playing.

Now she stood among the shelves and found it. A sword. Rusted, pitted, hung on a peg between a jar of bottled storm (it grumbled in its glass like a dog dreaming) and a vial labeled *borrowed courage — do not return late*. The sword was older than the bottled storm by centuries. It had been hanging there so long that dust had made a soft gray pelt of it, and it was singing—not aloud, not really, but in the way a thing sings when it has been forgotten past all decency.

"Oh," Sable said softly, in the old tongue. "Oh, you poor thing."

The sword's song stuttered. Stopped. Then, hesitantly, like a dog that has been kicked for barking, it began again—louder now, hopeful.

Footsteps on the stairs. Maren appeared, arms crossed, flour on one sleeve. "Right," she said. "I don't know what you're doing down here, but Emrys will have my hide if—" She stopped. Frowned. Tilted her head the way people do when they hear a sound they can't place. "Do you hear that?"

Sable pointed at the sword.

"That?" Maren snorted. "That's been on that peg since before I was born. Old stock. Worthless. We keep it because throwing it out felt rude." She squinted. "It doesn't do anything."

"It's singing," Sable said. Then, remembering herself, she said it again in trade-tongue, carefully: "It sings. It is lonely."

Maren looked at the sword. Looked at Sable. Looked at the sword again, longer this time, with the expression of a woman recalculating a ledger entry she'd closed years ago. "Huh," she said finally. "Well. It's been quiet about it."

"It is not quiet," Sable said. "You are deaf."

Maren barked a laugh that echoed off the jarred storms. "Deaf! Did you hear that, you rusty relic? The child says we're deaf." But she came closer, and her voice gentled in spite of itself. "What's it saying? If it's saying."

Sable listened. The sword's song was slow and sad and very old. Songs of a forge-fire and a name it used to have. Songs of a hand that had held it—a woman's hand, broad-palmed, scarred—and of grasslands, of riders, of a dawn battle whose name had been lost but whose fear still lived in the steel like frost in a wall.

"It remembers a woman," Sable said. "Who held it. Who was brave. It does not remember her name anymore, and it is ashamed."

The cellar went very quiet. Even the bottled storm stopped grumbling to listen.

"Emrys should hear this," Maren said at last, and her voice had gone strange.

* * *

They did not make her sing in front of Emrys. Maren wanted to; Sable could see it, the practical hunger of a quartermaster confronted with a new category of inventory. But Emrys appeared in the cellar doorway before anyone could arrange anything—drawn, Sable suspected, by the same thread of sound that had been pulling at them for three days—and stood there with their ledger clutched to their chest like a shield.

So Sable sang alone, in the middle of the cellar floor, feeling foolish and grieving and lit up all at once.

She sang the waking-song first, the one grandmothers sang to sickles and scythes at harvest, telling the metal it had been ore once, and ore had been mountain, and mountains were patient and so the metal should be too. Then, because the rusted sword on the peg leaned toward her song like a plant toward sun, she sang the remembering-song, the deep one, the one her grandmother had only sung twice and both times with tears in her eyes.

And the sword remembered.

Rust flaked away in curls, drifting down like orange snow. The pitted edge smoothed. The steel, gray-brown for a hundred years, woke to a dull silver shine, and the whole blade began—not hummed, *sang*, a clear high note that set every jar on the shelves ringing in sympathy, so that the bottled storm howled with joy and the vial of borrowed courage glowed like a coal, and somewhere overhead a hinge remembered being an oak and creaked out something that was unmistakably gratitude.

Sable stopped singing. The note faded slowly, reluctantly, the way summer fades.

Silence. Three faces: Maren's open-mouthed, Emrys's white and stricken, and the sword's—well, swords don't have faces, but if ever a blade looked embarrassed and proud and hungry for more, it was that one, gleaming on its peg like a cat that has knocked something over on purpose.

"Well," said Maren faintly. "That's coming off the worthless list."

Emrys took one step forward. Their lips parted. Sable saw it—the shape of a word rising in them, an old word, she could tell by the way their throat moved, the way their hand rose half-curled as if around a hilt—

And then Emrys closed their mouth. Pressed it flat. Turned on their heel and climbed the stairs two at a time, and the cellar door shut behind them with a click that sounded, to Sable, exactly like a lock.

* * *

She found them that evening in the counting room, because she was a child and children are owed explanations, and also because she had nowhere else to put her anger, which was mostly grief wearing armor.

"You know the words," she said, in trade-tongue, so there could be no pretending. "When I sang, you almost answered. You know them."

Emrys sat very still behind the great desk, lamplight pooling on the ledger pages, on hands ink-stained and steady. Steady the way ice is steady.

"I know them," Emrys said quietly. It was the most they had ever said to her.

"Then why won't you say them?"

For a long moment the quill scratched nothing. Then Emrys set it down and looked at her, and Sable saw that behind the stillness was a room full of locked doors, and behind every locked door was a fire.

"Because the last time people spoke that language freely," Emrys said, "blades sang on both sides of a war, and the war ate the world down to the bone. Because the kingdoms swore it would never happen again, and they made sure of it the only way they know how." A pause. "They silenced us. All of us. There were hundreds of swordspeakers once, Sable. Now there is one, and she keeps books."

"But the words aren't bad," Sable said, and her voice cracked, and she hated it. "The words made a kettle remember rain. The words made a dead woman's sword happy. My grandmother—" The grief came up like floodwater and she let it, because he should see it, they should all see it. "My grandmother sang to our door every night so it would hold fast against wind. And soldiers burned it anyway. Burned everything. They weren't afraid of our words, they were afraid we *were*, and killing us didn't make the words go away, it just made everyone lonely!" She was shouting now, and the lamp flames stood up straight and tall to listen, and the ledger pages stirred though there was no wind. "Everyone is lonely. You're the loneliest thing in this whole house and it's full of bottled storms."

Emrys didn't move. But something in their face shifted, tectonic and slow, the way a glacier shifts—one crack, deep down, that will take chapters to travel.

"I'm sorry about your grandmother," they said at last, and it was not enough, and it was true, and Sable supposed that was what most true things were like.

* * *

That night she slept better than she had any right to. The rusted sword—no longer rusted, though Maren had refused to let it leave the cellar ("Inventory stays inventoried")—hummed to her through the floorboards, a lullaby it remembered from some other child, in some other century, and Sable slept inside the song like a boat inside a harbor.

She dreamed of her village burning, which was normal now. But in the dream, this time, the flames paused. Leaned toward her. And in the old tongue, the fire asked her a question it had never asked before:

*Little ember, why do you grieve us? We remember what we were too.*

She woke before dawn with her heart hammering, and lay listening to the guildhouse, and heard what she had been too grief-struck to hear before: under the fire's tick and the beams' groan, the whole Crooked Salt was murmuring. Every nail, every hinge, every coin in every strongbox, whispering in the old tongue, awake and restless, turning over in their sleep like people who have remembered something important and cannot get back to bed.

And beneath it, fainter, from far away across the snow—from the direction of the king's road, the direction of cities and crowns—came another sound. Not a murmur. A marching rhythm. Steel keeping time.

Something was coming that had been taught to forget everything except obedience.

Sable sat up in the dark, hugged her knees, and whispered to the floorboards, "Wake up. Please. Wake up and remember."

All over the guildhouse, quietly, things began to shine.
