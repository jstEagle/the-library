## 6. The Traitor's Ledger

Maren Voss had three rules for auditing the Crooked Salt's books, and she'd written them herself on the inside cover of the master ledger in ink so permanent it had outlasted two guildmasters and one small fire.

One: count everything twice.

Two: trust nothing that smells of lavender, because lavender oil is what people use when they want you to think a page has aged honestly.

Three: if the numbers feel too tidy, someone is lying to you.

It was rule three that kept her at the counting table past midnight on the ninth day of the long winter's falling, with a candle guttering, a pot of tea gone bitter beside her elbow, and the quarterly ledgers spread out before her like the entrails of some particularly dishonest animal.

The trouble was not that anything was missing. That would have been simple. Missing things announced themselves. No — the trouble was that everything was *correct*. Too correct. The ledgers balanced to the copper across all four quarters, which never happened. Not once in nineteen years had Maren closed a season without finding at least one entry where someone had paid six crowns and change for a bottled fog and rounded up because they couldn't be bothered with the change.

She turned back to the autumn pages and looked properly at them this time — not at the sums but at the hand that had written them.

She knew that hand.

Of course she knew that hand. She had taught half that hand its flourishes herself, years ago, when Corvin Hale had come to the Crooked Salt with clever fingers and no trade, and she had put a pen in them because he could copy any script set before him after seeing it twice. It was Corvin who kept the sales ledger these days, Corvin who handled the Crown-facing accounts because his manners were smooth and his accent was better than anyone else's under this roof. Corvin, who had been the first to warm to Sable, who carried her on his shoulders through the market, who taught her dice games she was now frighteningly good at.

Corvin's writing sat on these pages, neat as a hymnal.

And every seventh entry or so — Maren counted, because rule one — there was a sale recorded at a price just slightly wrong. A vial of borrowed courage logged at four crowns when the standing price was three and eight. A jar of preserved moonlight entered at eleven when it should have been ten and five. Never large. Never enough to notice unless you were the sort of person who noticed, which, thank the saints and also damn them, Maren very much was.

But here was the thing about skimming: the skim wasn't the crime. The skim was the *habit*. Nobody skimmed from their own guild in a pattern this careful unless the money was going somewhere it couldn't be seen to go.

She went further back. Spring. Winter before. And there, tucked between legitimate sales like a smuggler's coin sewn into a coat lining, were entries she had no record of receiving stock for. *Sold: one memory, minor (childhood, riverside). Paid in full.* Maren kept the vault inventory herself. She had never held a riverside childhood in her life. You couldn't sell what the guild didn't have — except apparently you could, if you sold it to someone who wanted something else entirely and needed the transaction to look ordinary.

Someone was using the Crooked Salt's books as a blind. Someone was selling information dressed up as inventory.

Maren sat back. Her tea had gone cold enough to chew, and she drank it anyway, because sentimentality about tea was for people whose ledgers weren't betraying them.

There was exactly one person in the guildhouse who could write those entries without raising an eyebrow. There was exactly one person who had access to the Crown-facing account. And there was exactly one person who, three days ago, had asked her — casually, oh-so-casually, over breakfast — whether Emrys had said anything more about "the child's funny language," and whether the guild kept sealed records of old tongues, and wouldn't it be interesting to know.

Maren looked at the ledger for a long moment.

"Corvin," she said to the empty room, "you absolute turnip."

* * *

He was in the common room, of course. He was always in the common room in the evenings, playing cards badly on purpose with Doss the bottle-wright and letting Sable stand behind him and whisper advice that made him lose even worse, because it made her laugh, and Corvin had always known the value of being loved.

Maren watched him from the doorway for a moment — the firelight on his gray-streaked hair, Sable's chin hooked over his shoulder to peer at his cards, the whole picture warm as bread. Then she cleared her throat.

"Sable. Bed. If Doss catches you counting his tells again he'll cry, and I'll have to comfort him, and I'm bad at it."

Sable grinned, kissed the top of Corvin's head with the fearless affection of a child who did not yet know what grown people cost, and went. Maren waited until the stairs stopped creaking.

"Walk with me, Corvin."

They walked to the counting room, and she lit the lamp, and she opened the autumn ledger to the page with the riverside childhood on it, and she turned it toward him, and she said nothing at all, because she had learned that silence was Emrys's weapon and it worked nearly as well for her.

Corvin looked at the page. She watched him decide — watched it happen behind his eyes, the little flicker of calculation, the weighing of lies — and then, to his credit, he sighed and sat down.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"Ninety minutes. But I've been suspicious since you asked me about the sealed records, and I've disliked your handwriting since 1811."

"The handwriting is immaculate."

"The handwriting is *too* immaculate. Rule three." She tapped the ledger. "Whoever wrote these entries wanted them to look honest. Honest isn't tidy, Corvin. Honest is messy and full of other people's mistakes."

He rubbed his face with both hands. When they came down, he looked older, and tired, and — this was the worst part — he looked *reasonable*.

"Maren. Before you fetch Emrys and the whole tribunal of it. Hear me."

"I'm hearing."

"The Crown came to me in early autumn. An inquisitor — polite fellow, terrible taste in boots. They already knew we were sheltering a swordspeaker. Do you understand? They *knew*, Maren. They've always known. The only question was whether the Crooked Salt got to keep existing."

"So you gave them the child instead."

"I gave them *nothing*." His voice cracked upward. "Not yet. I've given them weather reports. Which shipments, which buyers, nothing that touches her. I've been slow-walking them for months, feeding them crumbs, keeping them convinced they don't need to come through our door. Every wrong price in that book is me buying time." He leaned forward. "You think I'm a traitor. Fine. Call me one. But ask yourself what happens to Sable, to Emrys, to you, to Doss and his ridiculous bottles, when the inquisitors stop buying my crumbs and start taking the loaf. Because they will. They're patient, but they're not *blind*."

Maren let the quiet sit. Outside, the snow fell with that particular long-winter hush, the kind that seemed to lean its weight against the windows.

"You've decided we're doomed," she said finally. "That's the part I mind most. Not the selling. The deciding. You looked at us — at *her*, at that girl who kisses your stupid head goodnight — and you decided in advance we were dead, and started negotiating with the thing that killed us."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is. Survival requires sacrifice, you're going to say next. I can hear it queuing up behind your teeth."

"Survival *does* require sacrifice!" He stood, chair scraping. "You think the guild survives on warmth and found family? The guild survives because it's useful and because it's small and because nobody important cares. A child speaking the Sundering-tongue makes us *important*. There is no clever ledger line that hides us from that. Somebody pays, Maren. The only question I ever got to answer was whether it was all of us or—" he gestured helplessly, "—or the smallest possible number of us."

"And you appointed yourself the one who does the arithmetic."

"Somebody had to. You were busy believing in everyone."

The words landed harder than he probably intended, which was Corvin's special talent. Maren looked at him — really looked — and understood, with a sick settling feeling, that he wasn't lying. That was the trap of it. He believed every word. He'd built a whole moral architecture out of arithmetic, floor by floor, until he could stand in it and call himself a shield instead of a knife.

"Here's what happens now," she said quietly. "I don't fetch Emrys tonight. Not because you deserve it, but because I need until morning to decide what the guild owes *you* — and I'm not doing that deciding angry, because I make expensive mistakes angry."

Corvin exhaled. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. One condition. You stop writing in the Crown-facing ledger starting now. Tonight. Whatever arrangement you've made, whatever signal you owe them — it waits. If one more entry appears in that book in your hand, I go straight to Emrys and I tell them everything, including the part where I covered for you, and we all drown together. Are we clear?"

"Perfectly." Something in his face loosened — relief, she supposed, though with hindsight she would later reclassify it.

She saw him to the door of the counting room. At the threshold he paused, half-turned, and for one moment he looked like the young man she'd handed a pen to twenty years ago, all clever fingers and no trade.

"You'd choose the people over the guild, then," he said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It sounded almost like envy. "If it came to it. You'd burn the Crooked Salt to save the people in it."

Maren thought of Sable asleep upstairs with a rusted sword she hummed to like a doll. She thought of Emrys, silent as a locked room, standing between the child and the world without ever saying a word.

"Yes," she said. "Without hesitation. That's the difference between us, Corvin. You think the guild is the thing worth saving. The guild is just the roof. I've been counting what's under it for nineteen years."

He nodded slowly, and left, and his footsteps faded up the stairs.

Maren stood alone with the ledgers a while longer. Then, because rule one applied to people too, she counted the silver in the strongbox twice, and checked the seals on the vault, and finally climbed to bed — where she lay awake listening to the snow, turning over the shape of tomorrow's conversation with Emrys.

She had almost talked herself into sleep when she heard it: the front bell. One soft, deliberate ring, deep in the small hours, the ring reserved for customers who did not wish to be announced.

Then voices below — low, courteous, unmistakably official — and Corvin's answering tone, smooth as fresh ink, saying:

"Of course, Inquisitor. We've been expecting you. Come in out of the snow."

Maren was out of bed and reaching for her boots before the second word finished landing, and the only coherent thought in her head, absurdly, was rule three: *if the numbers feel too tidy, someone is lying.*

Corvin hadn't even waited for morning.
