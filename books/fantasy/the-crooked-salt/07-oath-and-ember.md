## 7. Oath and Ember

The storm came back on the ninth night, as if it had only stepped out to stretch its legs.

Emrys knew storms the way other people knew old debts—by the ache they left behind. The bottled tempest on shelf eleven had been restless all day, rattling against its glass like a dog that smelled rain coming, and now the real thing arrived over the rooftops of Lowmark with its teeth out, driving snow sideways past the guildhouse windows.

They were in the counting room when the knock came.

Not a knock, precisely. A fist. Three blows, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned discipline.

Maren looked up from her ledgers. Sable, curled in the corner chair with her feet tucked under her and a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands, went very still—the particular stillness of prey animals and children who have heard soldiers before.

"Storm's bad," Maren said mildly. "Whoever it is can freeze a while."

The fist struck again. And beneath it—faintly, through the oak and iron of the Crooked Salt's famous door—came a voice Emrys knew.

"Quartermaster Maren. By authority of the Crown. Open."

*Corvane.*

Emrys's hand found the edge of the desk. Twenty years ago, an oath had been pressed into them like a brand: *you will keep the silence, you will keep the ledgers, and you will never speak the words that wake the steel.* In twenty years they had kept all three. They had stood in market squares while men insulted their dead masters. They had held their tongue through funerals, through betrayals, through one truly catastrophic tax audit. Silence was not merely their discipline. It was their architecture.

But they could still lie in ink. They always had been able to lie in ink.

Emrys pulled a fresh page toward themselves and wrote, in their neat ledger hand: *Don't answer. He has no warrant I've seen. Make him show papers through the grate.*

Maren read it, nodded once, and went to the door. Sable rose too, silent as spilt milk, and pressed herself against Emrys's side. Her small hand closed on their sleeve.

Through the speaking-grate, muffled by wind, came the ritual exchange: name, purpose, papers. Papers were shown. Maren squinted at them for a long moment.

"Warrant names this house," she said at last. "It doesn't name a child."

"The child is named in the annex," Corvane said pleasantly. "Open the annex, quartermaster."

And then—a third voice, one Emrys had known for thirty years, since he'd been a gangly apprentice shelving bottled fogs:

"Second door on the left! She sleeps near the kitchen!"

* * *

Aldous Vetch. Senior cataloger. Keeper of the sealed records. The man who had taught Emrys how to tell a genuine vial of borrowed courage from the vinegar frauds, who doctored his tea with honey because he claimed sugar was vulgar, who had once sat up three nights straight helping Emrys reorganize the impossible-things wing after the fire of '41.

His voice came through the grate bright and helpful and traitorous, and Emrys felt the words land somewhere under the ribs, in the place where oaths are stored.

Maren turned. Across the room, her eyes met Emrys's, and Emrys saw the same arithmetic happening there: *he knows the layout, he knows where she sleeps, he opened the door before we agreed anything.*

"Vetch," Maren called toward the stairs, her voice carefully level, "come down here a moment."

He did. Of course he did. He came down smoothing his waistcoat, wearing the apologetic expression of a man about to explain why the cat simply *had* to be let out, and he looked at Emrys first—not at Maren, not at Sable—at Emrys—and something in his face faltered.

"You wrote to them," Emrys said. They couldn't say it aloud. They never said anything aloud. But they held up the slate they used for household matters, the chalk letters large enough to be unkind: *YOU TOLD THE CROWN.*

Vetch's chin lifted. "Someone had to. You've been coddling a fuse, Fairweather. That child is the Sundering with a runny nose, and you're teaching her card games." His voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "The guild survives what the Crown permits. It has always survived that way. I'm saving us, even if you hate me for it."

Sable made a sound. Small. Not a word in any kingdom's tongue or any tongue older than kingdoms—just a sound, the kind a person makes when a floor gives way beneath them.

And Emrys, who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of meaning things without saying them, discovered that some meanings require no language at all. They put one hand flat against the child's shoulder blade and stepped between her and the door.

That was the whole of their rebellion: one step. It might as well have been a declaration of war.

* * *

The door burst inward on the second blow rather than the tenth, because Vetch had already drawn the bolts.

Snow and lamplight and six soldiers poured into the entry hall together, and at their center, brushing flakes from his gray coat with the air of a man arriving late to a dinner party, stood Inquisitor Corvane. Up close he had the kind of face that made you want to check your pockets—pleasant, forgettable, and entirely wrong around the eyes.

"There she is," he said warmly, as though Sable were a lost parcel. "You see? No trouble at all. Master Fairweather, isn't it? The mute archivist. I've read your ledgers. Beautiful penmanship." He smiled. "Hands where I can see them, if you would."

Two soldiers moved for Sable.

What happened next, Emrys would spend the rest of their life taking apart piece by piece, and every accounting would come out wrong.

It began with Sable herself. She did not scream. She planted her small boots on the flagstones, filled her lungs, and spoke—one syllable of the old tongue, cracked and terrified and *true*, aimed at nothing, at everything, the way a drowning person strikes water.

And the house remembered.

Emrys felt it happen inside their own chest before they saw it anywhere else: a pressure behind the breastbone, a heat climbing the throat like a swallowed coal rising, twenty years of silence suddenly revealed as not empty but *full*—a cellar packed to the ceiling with every word they had ever refused to say. The oath hadn't been holding the magic in. It had been sitting on the lid.

The hearth-iron poker sang. A low, sweet note, impossible, unmistakable—the note of steel being told what it was.

Then the soldier nearest the door gasped, and Emrys watched his sword half-draw itself, humming, straining toward the child like a plant toward sun. The second soldier's blade rang against its scabbard. The third dropped his entirely; it hit the flagstones ringing like a bell and lay there shivering, and the soldier stared at it the way a man stares at a dog that has begun to recite poetry.

*Every blade in the room remembers being sung to.* Emrys understood that, distantly, from somewhere outside their own body—understood that this was what the Sundering had been made of, this exact feeling, scaled up to kingdoms: steel remembering love, steel remembering rage, and no one left alive who knew which song was which.

Because it wasn't only the soldiers' weapons. It was the letter opener on Maren's desk. It was the bread knife in the kitchen. It was, God help them all, the decorative halberd over the mantel that Vetch had bought at auction and everyone had privately mocked him for.

And underneath all of it, louder than all of it, was Emrys's own voice—caged, clawing, one word from freedom. *Say it,* the magic urged, in no language at all. *Say the word and every sword in Lowmark rises. Say it and she walks out of here untouched. Say it and burn the world down doing it.*

That was the lesson of the Sundering, written in ash across half a continent: the words worked. That was never the question. The question was what they cost.

"No," Emrys mouthed—to the magic, to themselves, to the whole crooked inheritance of the swordspeakers—and clamped both hands over their own mouth like a child keeping a secret.

Which is why they saw none of the blow that took them down.

* * *

Pain arrived as an afterthought, politely late. First came the floor, cold against their cheek; then the taste of blood; then, distantly, the discovery that someone had kicked them hard enough to relocate several ribs. Through the gray roaring in their ears, the world assembled itself in fragments:

Maren, swinging the fire tongs with both hands and the vocabulary of a dockworker, being borne backward by two soldiers who would carry bruises and a lifelong respect for quartermasters.

Vetch, standing by the open door, holding it wide, his face gone the color of tallow—as if he had imagined this scene many times and never once imagined the actual cold air of it.

Sable, kicking, screaming in two languages now, being carried into the snow by a man whose grip was professional and whose eyes kept flicking to his own humming sword with naked fear.

And Corvane, crouching beside Emrys, studying them with genuine curiosity.

"Fascinating," the inquisitor said softly. "You didn't speak. Even now. Do you know, I'd half convinced myself you weren't really one of them?" He reached out—Emrys flinched—and plucked the slate from the floor, turning it over in his gloved fingers. On its face, in Emrys's neat hand, the chalk still read: *YOU TOLD THE CROWN.*

"I'll take good care of the girl," Corvane said, rising. "Better than you did. Words are weapons, Master Fairweather. Weapons belong in armories, not nurseries." At the door he paused, snow swirling in around his gray coat. "Oh—and your colleague will be compensated. The Crown pays well for sense."

The door slammed. The wind took the hoofbeats almost immediately, folding them into the storm until there was nothing.

* * *

Afterward there was the long business of standing up, which Emrys accomplished twice and failed once. Maren, bleeding from a split lip and breathing like a bellows, got an arm under them anyway, and together they stood in the wrecked entry hall among scattered ledgers and one abandoned soldier's cloak.

The blades had gone quiet. All of them. The poker, the knives, the ridiculous halberd—silent again, ordinary again, pretending, as steel does, that it remembers nothing.

Vetch was still there. Emrys didn't know why he was still there. He stood with his back against the stair rail, opening and closing his mouth, and finally produced: "I didn't—they said there would be paperwork. A hearing. There wasn't supposed to be *kicking*."

Emrys crossed the hall—ribs screaming, vision graying at the edges—and stopped in front of him. They looked at him for a long moment, this man who had shared their table for thirty years. Then they picked up the slate, wiped it clean with their sleeve, and wrote four words in letters so deliberate the chalk squeaked:

*GET OUT OF MY HOUSE.*

Vetch left. The storm swallowed him too.

* * *

Later—it may have been an hour, it may have been most of the night—Emrys sat at the counting-room table with their ribs bound, a candle guttering, and before them the ledger of impossible things, open to a blank page. Maren sat opposite, drinking the medicinal brandy that was theoretically reserved for guild emergencies, which this unquestionably was.

Emrys dipped the pen. Their hand shook, so the first line came out crooked, and for once they let it stand.

*Item: one child, Sable. Taken by the Crown. Value beyond reckoning. Debt incurred: total.*

Below it, after a while:

*Item: one silence, kept twenty years. Found to be worth less than nothing. Cost of keeping: one child, one friendship, one family. Recommend immediate disposal.*

Maren read it upside down, the way she read everything, because patience had never been among her virtues. When she reached the bottom line she set down her glass.

"They'll take her to Greyhold," she said. "The fortress above the pass. Everyone the Crown wants forgotten goes to Greyhold." She turned the glass slowly on the wood. "Six days' ride. Longer in this weather. Suicide, in this weather."

Emrys met her eyes.

"I didn't say don't go," Maren said. "I said it's suicide. Those are different conversations, and we're going to have both, but the second one requires more brandy." She pushed herself up, wincing, and limped to the window, where the storm was still worrying the shutters like a creditor. For a while she just watched it. Then, without turning around:

"Twenty years, Emrys. Twenty years I've poured your tea and read your slates and never once asked why a person binds their own tongue. Tomorrow you're going to tell me. All of it—the war, the oath, all of it." She turned then, and her face in the candlelight was terrible and kind at once. "Because I've been thinking, and there's a thing you can do that isn't silence and isn't that word either. I don't know what it is yet. But you're going to help me find it, and then we're going to go get our girl."

Emrys looked down at the ledger, at the crooked lines, and felt something crack open in their chest that was not a broken rib.

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, for the first time in twenty years, the last swordspeaker of the Crooked Salt began—slowly, painfully, in chalk on slate—to plan in the future tense.
