## 8. The Long Winter's Price

*Emrys*

The guildhouse felt different after the soldiers left. Emrys had catalogued that feeling once, years ago, in a ledger entry they still regretted: *Item: one house, emptied of its purpose. Condition: haunted by its own furniture.*

They had not known then how it would feel to be the haunting.

Three days since Sable was taken. Three days since every blade in the common room had sung like a struck bell and gone silent, and Emrys had stood in the wreckage of their own making with blood on their sleeve—not all of it theirs—and watched the door swing shut behind a child who had trusted them.

The ledgers were open on the table, but Emrys could not write in them. The quill hovered. Ink dried on the nib. Somewhere upstairs, floorboards creaked as another member packed another bag.

By morning there would be fewer of them. By spring, perhaps none.

Emrys set down the quill and did the one thing left to do with hands that had failed: they made tea.

* * *

*Maren*

Maren counted heads the way she counted stock—by candlelight, twice, with a growing knot in her chest.

Fourteen had fled since the raid. Old Corvin first, which stung less than she'd expected; he'd been planning his exit for a decade and only needed an excuse. Then the twins, then Hesper with her bottled fogs, then half the cellar staff in a single night, leaving IOUs written on napkins like cowards.

Eleven remained. Eleven, out of a guild that had once filled three floors and a courtyard.

She found Emrys in the kitchen at dawn, boiling water with the grim precision of someone defusing a bomb. They looked terrible. Maren said so.

"You look terrible."

"Thank you," Emrys said. "I've been practicing."

"Don't." Maren sat down across from them and helped herself to the tea uninvited, because if she waited for an invitation she'd die of old age first. "Don't do the thing where you make jokes until I leave you alone. I've watched you do it for nine years. It doesn't work on me anymore."

Emrys's hands went still around their cup. "What thing would that be?"

"The invisibility thing. The 'I am merely a clerk' thing. You've been doing it so long you've forgotten it's a performance." She blew on her tea. "It stopped working about the time a child walked through our door speaking dead words, and you looked at her like the sky was falling. Invisibles don't flinch, Emrys."

Outside, the snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes. The falling long winter, the farmers called it—the kind that buried roads and starved cities and made men desperate. Somewhere under all that white, a fortress held a girl who sang to swords.

"I'm going to ask you something," Maren said, "and I want the truth, because I'm too tired for anything else. What happened twenty years ago? And don't say 'nothing,' because nothing doesn't leave scars shaped like oaths."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Maren had seen them face down debt collectors, inquisitors' clerks, and a man made entirely of borrowed smoke, and never once had they looked afraid.

They looked afraid now.

"All right," Emrys said quietly. "But you'll want more tea."

* * *

*Emrys*

"You have to understand what we were," Emrys began. "Before the kingdoms. Before the Crooked Salt. There was a language—older than any crown, older than writing. We called it the First Tongue, though that's flattering. It was just... speech, from before speech learned manners."

Maren said nothing. She had the rare gift of silence that listened instead of waited.

"Swordspeakers spoke it. That's what we were called. And the things we said—" Emrys's hand drifted toward the knife block on the counter and stopped, deliberately, an inch away. "A blade is a made thing. Made things remember being made. Speak to one in the old tongue and it remembers everything: the forge, the fire, the hand that hammered it, the intent. And intent, Maren, is the dangerous part. Because a sword's intent is not peace."

"The Sundering War."

"The Sundering War." Emrys laughed, and there was no warmth in it. "That's what the histories call it. Two hundred years of blades singing themselves awake across half the world. Not fought, at the end—sung. Whole armies disarmed by words. Cities burned by swords no one was holding. My order didn't start it, whatever the Crown teaches. But we kept it burning. Every swordspeaker who raised their voice added fuel. We thought we were wielding power. We were feeding a fire that ate the world."

"And when it ended?"

"They ended us. Not with executions—that would have been honest. They made us swear. The Oath of Silence: never again speak the old tongue, never teach it, never write it. In exchange, our lives. Our names, even—most of us took new ones. I was not born Emrys Fairweather. That name is a coat I put on so the old one could be buried." They turned the cup slowly on the table. "Twenty-two years I haven't spoken it. Twenty-two years of holding every word behind my teeth like broken glass. Do you know what that does to a person? You stop talking altogether, eventually. Talking reminds you of what you're not saying."

Maren's tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because I broke it." Emrys's voice cracked on the word. "In the fortress—I didn't fight my way to her cell, Maren. I answered her. In the old tongue. One sentence, and the oath shattered like ice on a pond, and I felt it go, and do you know what I felt?" They looked up, eyes wet and furious at themselves. "*Relief.* Twenty-two years of silence and the worst moment of my life was also the first time I could breathe. And now every blade in this house knows my voice, and the Crown knows what I am, and Sable is in a cell because I taught her that doors open for kindness and then let them close behind her."

The kitchen was very quiet. Snow ticked against the window.

"So that's the confession," Emrys finished. "The last swordspeaker, oathless, useless, sitting in your kitchen drinking your tea. You should turn me out. It would be the sensible thing. The guild has bled enough for me."

Maren set down her cup with a click.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No, I shouldn't turn you out. Turning you out would require believing this is your fault, and I've done the accounting, Emrys—it's what I *do*—and the numbers don't support it. A child came to our door. You sheltered her. Soldiers kicked it in. That's not your ledger. That's theirs." She leaned forward. "Here's what I actually think. I think you've spent twenty-two years treating silence like penance when it was just fear wearing a hair shirt. And I think the woman who walked into that fortress alone to answer a frightened child in a dead language is the bravest fool I've ever shared a roof with."

"Maren—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice softened, which was somehow worse. "You want forgiveness. I can hear it. You're waiting for absolution like it's an entry in a ledger, debit and credit, balanced and done. I'm not offering that. Forgiveness implies you wronged *me*, and you didn't. What I'm offering is better." She pulled a folded paper from her apron and spread it on the table between them. "Partnership."

Emrys looked down. It was a list. Names—eleven names, the eleven who'd stayed. Beside each, in Maren's cramped, merciless handwriting: skills, supplies, debts owed and held.

"Hesper can fog a street in four seconds flat. Corvin's nephew owes us his life and owns a cart. Pip can pick any lock in the city and most of the locks outside it. And you—" she tapped the paper, "—are the only person alive who speaks the language that makes weapons forget themselves. So no, I'm not forgiving you. I'm recruiting you. Get it right."

Emrys stared at the list. Then at her. Something in their chest that had been clenched for twenty-two years loosened, not all the way, but enough to hurt in a new way.

"You're insane," they said. "You know that. The Crown will burn this place down around us."

"It's already burning. Question is whether we stand in it or walk through it." Maren stood, collected both cups, and refilled them without asking. "We get her back, Emrys. Not because of prophecy or war or any of that. Because she's ours. She ate at our table. She hummed off-key in my storeroom and stole honey cakes and called the storm-in-a-bottle 'the angry uncle.' She's *ours*, and nobody takes what's ours."

Emrys took the fresh cup. Their hands were steadier than they had any right to be.

"When do we start?"

"Now." Maren was already moving toward the door, and over her shoulder she added, with the ghost of a smile: "First item of business. Pip picked the lock on the inquisitor's courier satchel this morning. There's a letter inside, sealed with the Crown's black wax, and it's addressed to the fortress commander." She paused. "It mentions a transfer date. Three days from now. They're moving her somewhere even we can't reach."

Emrys was already on their feet.

"Then we'd better read it twice," they said, "and steal it once."
