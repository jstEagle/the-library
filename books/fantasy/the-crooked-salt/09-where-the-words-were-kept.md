## 9. Where the Words Were Kept

The fortress of Vael Corrin had been built by men who believed that fear was a foundation stone. It squatted on the cliff above the frozen harbor like a toad that had won an argument with the sea, all black basalt and iron teeth, and every window glowed with the particular yellow light of people who were paid to stay awake.

Emrys stood at the tree line for a long while, letting the cold explain itself.

They had dressed as what they had spent twenty years pretending not to be: nobody. A gray cloak, a courier's satchel, a face arranged into the expression of a person carrying someone else's errands. Under the cloak, against their ribs, they carried two things. A knife Maren had pressed into their hands at the gate—"You'll forget to eat, you always forget to eat, so I put dried plums in the left pocket"—and a page torn from the guild's oldest sealed ledger, the one written in the pre-kingdom tongue, which Emrys could read perfectly and had never once, in twenty years, permitted themselves to say aloud.

Reading was not speaking. That was the loophole they had lived inside for two decades, a loophole shaped exactly like a coffin.

*You are going to get her back,* Maren had said, gripping their shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise he would apologize for later. *And then you are going to come home, because I have already argued with you about this once and I refuse to do it twice.*

Home. When had the Crooked Salt become that? Emrys suspected it had happened somewhere around the second bowl of stew Sable had eaten, or possibly the moment the child had fallen asleep against Maren's side with her boots still on, and neither of them had moved for three hours rather than wake her.

The thought hurt more than the cold did. Emrys filed it away and walked toward the fortress.

* * *

Getting in was insultingly easy, which was how Emrys knew the trap was real.

The postern gate stood open. The guard on duty was warming his hands over a brazier and did not look up from his cup. A clerk waved Emrys through the inner ward with the bored flick of two fingers reserved for couriers, and the courtyard beyond was full of soldiers drilling in the snow—and there, propped on racks along the armory wall, were swords. Dozens of them. Crown steel, plain hilts, unremarkable in every way except one.

Every single blade was pointed at the sky, and every single blade had been wrapped at the crossguard with red cord.

Emrys knew that cord. Every swordspeaker did. It was the binding-knot, the old way of gagging a weapon—of telling a blade *you will remember nothing*. The Crown had not merely imprisoned a child. They had armed an entire garrison against her voice, which meant the Crown understood exactly what her voice was.

Which meant the Crown wanted it.

That was the arithmetic Emrys carried up four flights of stairs, past a guard who yawned, past a door marked in fresh paint—*RECORDS*—and through it, because a courier delivering documents to records was the most invisible thing in any fortress in the world.

The room inside was warm, lamplit, and obscene.

Shelves lined every wall, and the shelves held ledgers—not guild ledgers, but interrogation transcripts, hundreds of them, each labeled with a date and a single word: *SUBJECT.* And on the great table in the center, spread open under weights, lay diagrams. Emrys recognized them the way one recognizes a scar. Circles of binding. Chords of command. The grammar of the old tongue laid out in the Crown's tidy hand, annotated in the margins with words like *yield* and *harness* and—one entry, underlined twice—*the Sundering event, reproducible.*

There was a letter on top, sealed but already broken, and Emrys read it because reading was not speaking and because their hands were shaking too badly to stop them.

It was addressed to the Inquisitor Halvard Vess from someone whose signature was only a crown drawn in ink. It thanked him. It spoke of *the asset.* It spoke of the coming spring campaign, of borders that had been inconvenient for a hundred years, of a war that had ended too soon the first time and left too much of the map undecided. It said, in language so clean it made Emrys's stomach turn:

*The child is not to be harmed. The child is to be taught. What she speaks, our blades will learn to obey. We do not fear the Sundering, Inquisitor. We intend to schedule it.*

Emrys stood very still in the warm room full of stolen grammar, and understood that they had spent twenty years guarding a door the enemy had already picked.

Sable had walked out of a burned village and across a whole winter to find "the one who keeps words." She had been sent as a warning. She had been sent as a key. And instead of keeping her safe, Emrys had kept her *hidden*, which was the same thing to a child and a very different thing to a fortress.

Somewhere below, faint through the floor, Emrys heard singing.

Small. Ragged. In a language older than kingdoms, sung to no one, in a cell, in the dark.

Emrys took the diagram page—the one marked *reproducible*—folded it into their coat, and went down toward the sound.

* * *

The cells were under the armory, which meant they were under the red-corded swords, which meant someone had thought about this very carefully, and Emrys hated them for it.

Two guards at the stair. Emrys had planned for the guards. The plan involved the dried plums—Maren's plums, dosed with tincture of poppy that Maren had absolutely not admitted to adding, because Maren never admitted anything, they simply handed you a paper bag and raised one eyebrow until you understood the world. Emrys offered the bag with a courier's shrug. One guard ate two. The other ate five, being hungry, and hunger was the most reliable soldier in any army.

By the time Emrys reached the bottom of the stair, both men were asleep against the wall, breathing slow, dreaming whatever plum-flavored dreams the Crown allowed its servants.

The cell door was iron, barred, and locked—but locks were just knots, and knots were just words written in metal, and Emrys had spent twenty years learning every knot the guild traded in. It took ninety seconds and a bent hairpin. It felt like the longest work of their life.

Inside, on a cot too short for anyone over ten years old, Sable sat with her knees drawn up. Her wrists were chapped from the cold. Her eyes found Emrys in the dark and went wide, and then—this was the part that broke something loose in Emrys's chest—she did not cry out, did not scramble back. She simply looked, the way you look at a door you had stopped believing in.

Then she began to speak.

The old tongue poured out of her, low and fast, cracked from disuse, and Emrys understood every word and answered none of them, as they had answered none of them since the night she arrived, and her voice climbed with something worse than anger—it climbed with *hope dying*, the specific grief of a child deciding an adult has failed her again—

And Emrys Fairweather, last of the swordspeakers, keeper of the ledgers, breaker of no oaths for twenty years, opened their mouth.

"*Vaelu sereth,*" they said. *I am here.*

The word came out of them like water out of a cracked dam—all of it at once, twenty years of it, the whole buried river. And the fortress heard.

Down the corridor, the red-corded swords shivered on their racks. Upstairs, in the armory, forty blades hummed against their bindings—a low, rising note, the sound steel makes when it remembers being sung to. Emrys felt the magic move through their own chest like a second heartbeat, vast and old and *listening*, waiting to see what shape they would give it.

This was the moment. This was the hinge the whole world swung on. Emrys could feel the old commands rising, the words that had started the Sundering, the words that turned armies' weapons against their own hands—they were right there, ready, and the fortress was full of enemies, and Sable was crying, and duty said *burn it down*.

Instead, Emrys knelt on the freezing stone floor, took the child's chapped hands in theirs, and kept speaking—gently, clumsily, a swordspeaker's accent thick as frost on the old vowels.

"*Sereth imma vaelu. Oath kelth serran.*" *I am yours to keep. My silence ends now.*

Sable stared at them. Then, slowly, wonderingly, she corrected their pronunciation.

They laughed—both of them, absurdly, in a dungeon, while the fortress groaned and woke around them—and Emrys understood, with a clarity that felt like sunlight through ice, that this was why the oath had never fit. An oath of silence kept the old tongue from commanding. But spoken *with* someone—to someone, *for* someone—it was not a command at all. It was a conversation. The magic had never been a weapon. It had been waiting, all these centuries, for someone to use it like a voice instead of a fist.

Boots thundered on the stairs above. Voices shouted in the Crown's language—*the prisoner, the prisoner, seal the armory*—and somewhere overhead the red cords began, one by one, to snap.

Sable stood, wiped her face with her sleeve, and looked at Emrys with the fierce, ancient patience of someone who had crossed a winter to deliver a message.

"*Kelthu,*" she said, and held out her hand. *Together.*

Emrys took it.

Above them, the last cord broke, and every sword in Vael Corrin began to sing.
