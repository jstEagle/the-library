## 10. The New Tongue

The cell smelled of iron and old fear, and Sable had decided she was done being afraid of it.

She had counted the days by the drip of water through the ceiling stone—eleven drips to a day, or maybe twelve when the guards above walked heavy—and on the eleventh-or-twelfth day since they had taken her from the Crooked Salt, the door did not open for a guard with bread and questions.

It opened for Emrys.

They looked terrible. That was Sable's first thought, and her second thought was that she loved them anyway, and her third thought was that their coat was on inside-out, which under the circumstances felt forgivable. There was blood on their sleeve that she hoped belonged to someone else, and their hands were shaking in a way she had never seen, not even the night the soldiers came and every blade in the guildhouse had woken up singing.

"You came," she said, in the old tongue, because it was the only language that had ever felt like hers.

Emrys knelt. Their oath had kept them silent in this language for twenty years—Sable knew that now, knew all of it, the war and the silencing and the ledgers full of impossible things—and what they said next was not a word of it.

"Yes," they said. In the old tongue. Cracked and rusty as a gate left shut through winter, but *yes*, unmistakably yes. "I'm sorry I'm late."

Sable laughed, which surprised her, because her ribs hurt when she laughed, and she laughed anyway. "You're never late. You're the one who keeps the books. Books are just lateness with good handwriting."

"That is," Emrys said gravely, "the most accurate definition of accounting ever spoken, and I intend to steal it for the ledger."

* * *

The fortress around them was waking badly. Sable could feel it the way she felt everything now—the hum of metal remembering itself, the low angry drone of a hundred swords in a hundred scabbards, all of them dreaming of the Sundering the way old men dream of wars they were too young for. The Crown had brought her here to be a key. The inquisitor with the soft voice and the fresh coin had explained it to her twice, patiently, the way you explain things to furniture: sing the blades awake, child, and the kingdom will owe you everything.

The blades didn't want to be sung awake. She'd listened to them for eleven days. They wanted to sleep. They wanted to forget the taste of hands and the work they'd been made for. Every sword in this fortress was tired in its bones, and no one had ever thought to ask them about it.

"Emrys," she said. "I need to tell you something, and I need you not to put it in a ledger."

"I would never." A pause. "Unless it was a very good entry."

"The language isn't broken. It's not a weapon that got locked away. It's a *language*. It does what the speaker means. My grandmother's people sang swords into hating because they meant hatred. The war wasn't the tongue's fault. It was ours." She took both of Emrys's scarred hands. "So if we speak it wrong—if we speak it *new*—"

"It won't ignite," Emrys whispered. "It will unbind."

Somewhere down the corridor, boots. Many boots. And beneath them, rising like a tide, the hum of every blade in the fortress turning toward the sound of the old tongue like flowers toward a sun that had been gone a very long time.

"They're coming," Sable said.

"Then we'd better be fluent," said Emrys Fairweather, last of the swordspeakers, breaker of a twenty-year silence, "very quickly."

* * *

Here is what Sable learned in the next hour, pressed against Emrys in the dark of a cell while the fortress shook: that a language is a garden, not a grave.

They built it word by word. The old tongue had a word for *blade* that meant *the tooth of a promise*; Emrys gave it a second meaning, *the thing that remembers being a plowshare*. The old tongue had a word for *oath* that meant *a rope around the throat*; Sable softened it into *a hand held on purpose*. They took the grammar of command—the terrible imperative that had made steel leap and sing and slaughter—and bent it, gently, the way you bend a sapling, until it asked instead of ordered.

*Remember what you were,* they sang together, in the hybrid tongue, the new one, theirs. *Before you were sharpened. Before you were chosen. Remember the ore sleeping in the mountain, the smith's honest sweat, the shape you might have been.*

And the weapons forgot how to hate.

Not all at once. First the sword in the guard's hand at the end of the corridor went quiet mid-hum, like a dog lying down. Then the pikes in the armory racks sighed and sagged. Then, floor by floor, the whole fortress's arsenal let go of its long dream of war—not breaking, not rusting, just *forgetting*, the way you forget a fever after it passes. Soldiers found themselves holding lengths of metal that felt suddenly, embarrassingly like what they were: tools, and not even very good ones for holding a frightened child captive. Several of them sat down on the stairs without quite knowing why. One began, quietly, to cry, and could not have told anyone it was relief.

When the guards finally reached the cell, their captain looked at his own sword the way a man looks at an old argument he can no longer remember the start of, and then he looked at Sable, small and stubborn and unafraid, and he stepped aside.

The inquisitor did not step aside. He stood at the end of the corridor with his fresh coin and his soft voice, and he said, "Do you know what you've done?"

"Yes," said Sable.

"It's rather rude," said Emrys, "to ask a question when the answer's standing right in front of you."

The Crown's men took him anyway—not because his soldiers still hated, but because their superiors, three kingdoms away and out of reach of the new song, very much did. Sable watched him go and felt nothing so grand as vengeance, only the small cold satisfaction of a ledger balancing. The traitor from the Crooked Salt, whose name Maren had struck through with a line so hard it tore the page, went with him, arguing survival to the last, and nobody listened, which is its own particular punishment.

* * *

They walked home through the falling snow, Sable riding on Emrys's back because her legs were eleven days tired, and the world was quiet in a way it had never been quiet before. Not the silence of held breath. The silence of a room after everyone in it has decided to stay.

"Do you miss it?" she asked near the border of the town. "The oath. Being the person who couldn't speak."

Emrys considered this for a long time. Snow gathered on their shoulders like patient punctuation.

"I spent twenty years thinking my silence was a wall keeping the war out," they said at last. "It turns out it was just a wall. Walls don't keep anything out. They only keep you in." A breath. "No. I don't miss it. I miss who I thought I had to be. That's different, and the difference fits in a ledger, and I intend to record it."

"What will you write?"

"*Item the first: one voice, recovered, slightly used.*" Emrys's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "*Value: beyond entry.*"

* * *

The Crooked Salt reopened on a gray morning with a crooked sign and a door that stuck, because some things should never be fixed.

Maren had repainted the guild motto over the lintel herself, in letters visible from the road: *WE TRADE IN IMPOSSIBLE THINGS.* Beneath it, smaller, in fresher ink, someone had added: *AND IN POSSIBLE ONES, WHICH ARE HARDER TO COME BY.*

Inside, the guildhouse was half-empty and half-full, which Maren declared was the correct proportion for a new beginning. Those who had fled sent apologies wrapped in gifts—a cask of wine, a bolt of cloth, one truly alarming taxidermied owl. Those who stayed swept floors and argued cheerfully about whether a bottled storm counted as inventory or a resident. The rusted sword that hummed when Sable sang hung over the hearth now, retired, and if you listened closely on quiet nights you could hear it humming lullabies instead of war-songs, which everyone agreed was a significant career improvement.

Sable had a room. It was small and the window whistled and she had never loved anything so much in her life. On the first night she lay in the narrow bed listening to the sounds of the house—Maren's ledger closing downstairs, the storm-bottle grumbling on its shelf like a cat—and she practiced the new words under her breath. *Home*, in the old tongue, had meant *the place you are buried*. In the new one it meant *the place that keeps your name warm*. She fell asleep mid-word and finished it in a dream.

Emrys, meanwhile, kept the books. But the ledgers changed. There was a new column now, ruled in careful lines, headed simply: *THINGS SAID ALOUD.* The entries were short.

*Day one: Told Maren the stew needed salt. She said she knew. She did not know.*
*Day four: Sang the kettle to boil. It remembered being rain. Small magic, but honest.*
*Day nine: Read Sable a story in two languages at once. She corrected my grammar. She was right.*

And on the last page of the last ledger, in a hand steadier than it had been in twenty years:

*Final entry, and first: The vow is broken. The voice is kept. The war died unborn, and no one will ever write its history, which is the best fate any war has ever had.*

*We reopen tomorrow. Impossible things only. Well—*

*Mostly.*

Below that, in a different hand, smaller, rounder, learned only weeks ago and already better than Emrys's, someone else had written:

*I checked the math. It balances.*

—S.
