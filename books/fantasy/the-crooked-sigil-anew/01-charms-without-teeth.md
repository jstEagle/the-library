## 1. Charms Without Teeth

The rain came to Vellharrow the way debt came to the poor—slowly, then all at once, and never where it was wanted.

Elodie Yarrow stood behind the counter of her charm shop and watched it streak the warped glass of her front window, blurring the street into watercolors: the grey facades of Tanner's Row, the awnings sagging under their burden, and the figures moving through it all with their collars up and their heads down. She had learned, in ten years, to read a customer before they reached her door. The ones who hurried were only escaping weather. The ones who slowed were looking for something they couldn't name.

The woman slowing now had that look. Middle-aged, hands red from washing other people's linens, eyes that kept darting to the little painted sign above Elodie's door—*Yarrow's Charms & Curios*—as though the words might change if she read them twice.

Elodie arranged her face into something warm and harmless. It was the same expression she wore every day, and like everything else she sold, it was hollow at the center.

The bell over the door gave its cracked little jangle. The woman entered trailing wet wool and river-smell, and stood dripping on the floorboards Elodie had never gotten around to replacing.

"You're open, then," the woman said, as if shops on Tanner's Row were sometimes not.

"Rain or shine." Elodie gestured to the shelves behind her, crowded with the inventory of her small dishonest life. Copper bracelets hammered thin and hung with glass beads. Knots of horsehair braided into rings. Little cloth sachets stuffed with dried lavender and, for the ones who paid more, a pinch of ash from a hearth fire that had once, briefly, held something true. "Looking for anything particular?"

"My boy's going downriver on the barges next week." The woman's fingers moved along the shelf without touching, the way people touched nothing in a shop like this. "His father drowned that way. I'd sleep better knowing he had—" She stopped, embarrassed.

"Protection," Elodie supplied gently.

"I know it's foolish."

"It isn't foolish to want your child safe." Elodie turned to the case beneath the counter and lifted out a bracelet she'd made three nights ago by candlelight, working the copper until her thumbs ached. It was pretty. It was well-made. It was, in every way that mattered, an empty thing—a trinket with no more power than a button, sold to a frightened woman because frightened women needed to buy *something*, and this was all Elodie had left to give them.

She thought, as she always thought, of what she could have made instead. A binding woven into the metal itself, patient as spiderwork, keyed to the wearer's heartbeat so it would warm when danger drew near. Her mentor, Master Aldous Fen, could have taught her to do it in an afternoon. Had tried to teach her, once, in a workroom that smelled of hot iron and juniper smoke, his big scarred hands guiding hers—

She put the memory away the way she put away everything, in the dark place behind her ribs where she kept the things that would otherwise stop her from functioning.

"For the river," she said aloud, "you want this one." She wound the bracelet through her fingers. "Copper for the water's temper. The blue bead is for finding your way home. And—" here she leaned in, lowering her voice to the register of confidence, the closest thing to magic permitted in Vellharrow anymore, "—you tell him to wear it on the left wrist. Left is nearer the heart. That's old craft, that is. Older than the guilds."

It wasn't a lie, exactly. That was the trick of it. Everything she sold was built on truths that had been gutted of their substance, shells of real practice, the way a crab shell on the beach still holds the shape of the creature long after something has eaten out its middle.

The woman bought the bracelet, and a lavender sachet besides, and left two coppers lighter and visibly easier in her mind. Elodie watched her go and felt the familiar taste rise in her throat, coppery and bitter, that was equal parts shame and grief.

*Charms without teeth,* Aldous used to call such things, back when he was alive to call them anything. *A charm without teeth is just jewelry, girl. You might as well sell them buttons.*

He'd said it laughing. He hadn't been laughing the last time she saw him.

* * *

Midmorning brought the inspectors.

They came up Tanner's Row in their pairs, as regulation required, wearing the slate-grey coats of the Magistrate's Office of Covenant Compliance—the letters stitched in silver thread that cost more, Elodie suspected, than most of the Row's residents earned in a season. They walked slowly. That was the point of them. They were meant to be seen, meant to make shopkeepers straighten their displays and lower their voices and remember, collectively, what had happened to the people who forgot.

Elodie knew both of these. The tall one was Inspector Halloway, who had a wife with bad lungs and a habit of buying cough-sachets from Elodie and pretending they were medicine rather than magic, which suited them both. The short one was new, or newish—young, with a jaw like a drawn bolt and the particular eagerness of a man who believed in his work.

Halloway paused at her window. Tapped the glass with one knuckle. When Elodie nodded, he pushed the door open and let the rain in behind him.

"Mistress Yarrow."

"Inspector. Sachets today? I've fresh lavender."

"We're not here for sachets." But he drifted toward the shelf anyway, because he always did, because his wife's cough was worse in autumn and pride was a luxury he couldn't afford. The young one stayed by the door, arms folded, surveying the shop with the flat appraisal of a man taking inventory for a confiscation order.

"Routine walk," Halloway said. "There's been talk. Down by the docks, mostly. Talk of unlicensed workings." He said it the way a man reads a notice aloud, no weight on any word. "You'd know nothing about that, I expect."

"I sell bracelets, Inspector. If copper could work wonders, half of Vellharrow would be immortal."

The young inspector snorted. Halloway didn't smile, but something around his eyes loosened by a degree. He picked up a sachet, weighed it in his palm, set down a coin without being asked.

"Keep your head down, Mistress Yarrow," he said quietly, and there was something underneath the words, some sediment of meaning that Elodie couldn't quite sift. "Times are getting nervous again. Nervous times make for thorough inspections."

"What kind of talk?" she asked. "Of the unlicensed workings."

Halloway paused at the door, rain streaming past him into the grey street. For a moment he looked older than his years, tired in a way that had nothing to do with his lungs or his wife.

"The kind that gets people taken," he said, and left.

Elodie stood a long while with the coin in her hand. It was good silver, too much for a sachet, and she understood it for what it was—not payment but apology, or perhaps warning, delivered in the only currency a man in a grey coat could safely spend.

Behind her ribs, in the dark place where she kept things, something stirred and settled. Like a muscle remembering itself.

* * *

She closed early, as she did every year on this day, and told herself it was for inventory.

The shop shutters went up. The sign flipped. And Elodie climbed the narrow stair to the room above, where she kept the things that were not for sale: a chipped basin, a bundle of juniper, a folding knife with a bone handle, and a wooden box with a false bottom that she had not opened in eleven months.

She opened it now. She always opened it on this day.

Inside lay a guild token—a disc of dark iron, worn smooth at the edges, stamped with the sigil of the Weaver's Guild: two interlocking circles, one open, one closed, the symbol of the veil between worlds and the covenant that bound it. Beneath the token, wrapped in oilcloth, was a scrap of parchment bearing a single line of script in a hand she would have known anywhere, in any century, in any world.

*Aldous Fen, Journeyman-Principal, Third Chair of Weaving.*

Third Chair. There had been seven chairs once, seven masters of the Weaver's Guild, mediators between the human realm and the Otherworld beyond the veil. Not mages—that distinction mattered, though no one in Vellharrow remembered why anymore. Mages took. Mediators *kept*. They kept the balance, kept the terms, kept the slow old bargain that let both realms breathe without suffocating each other. Every charm worth its salt, every ward that held, every blessing that didn't curdle in the mouth—all of it flowed through the covenant, and the covenant flowed through the guild.

Ten years ago, the Magistrate Corvane had dissolved it. One decree, sealed with wax and backed by soldiers. *Unlicensed thaumaturgy is hereby prohibited within the bounds of Vellharrow and its dependencies.* Unlicensed. As if the guild had ever needed a license—as if the covenant itself were a trade requiring permits. The masters had been arrested in their meetinghouse on a night in late autumn. The journeymen scattered. The apprentices were given a choice: renounce or vanish.

Elodie had renounced. She had stood in the magistrate's hall with her head bowed and signed the paper with her right hand while her left hand, hidden in her sleeve, burned with the sigil-marks every guild-trained weaver carried under the skin of their wrists—marks that flared white-hot whenever true craft was worked nearby, marks that had gone quiet and cold for ten years, dormant as seeds in frozen ground.

Aldous had not renounced. Aldous had stood in front of his apprentices, all seven of them, and told Corvane's men that the guild would reopen when the covenant required it, and not one day sooner, and that no decree written by mortal hands could unwrite a bargain struck before kingdoms had names. They took him away for that. They did not bring him back.

She had never learned exactly how he died. She knew only that it was in the winter, and that his body was returned to no one, and that the token in her box had come to her by way of a dockworker who asked no questions and accepted no answers.

Elodie sat on the floor of her room with the token in her palm and let herself feel it, the way she allowed herself once a year: the whole weight of it, undiluted. The ache of hands trained for work they would never do. The talent sitting in her like a swallowed stone. Ten years of selling hollow charms to frightened people, ten years of watching the city's small magics gutter out one by one—hearth-blessings fading, birth-wards thinning, the old prayers losing their grip—and telling herself it was mercy, it was wisdom, it was survival.

Survival. She had gotten very good at it. She had gotten so good at it that some mornings she woke and could not find, for several seconds, any reason to get up.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the Row a cart rattled past, and a dog barked at it, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening closed over the day like water.

Elodie closed the box. She pressed her thumb against the token's worn sigil—one last indulgence, one last touch of the shape her fingers had traced ten thousand times in another life—and felt, as she always felt, nothing. Cold iron. Dead metal. The guild was ash and the covenant was a story and the marks under her skin were scars, nothing more, scars from a life that had ended before it finished.

She was reaching for the lid when the burning started.

It began in her wrist—both wrists at once, the sigil-marks flaring alight beneath the skin like coals breathed upon, sudden and searing, the first heat they had given her in ten years. Elodie gasped and nearly dropped the token. The light of it shone faintly through her flesh, two interlocking circles glowing rose-gold at her pulse points, and the room seemed to lean toward her, the shadows drawing in like a held breath.

Cold iron. Dead metal. Except the token in her palm had begun, very softly, to hum.

Elodie sat perfectly still, heart slamming, and listened—to the hum, to the burn, to the rain hammering the roof, and beneath all of it, faint and far off, threading up through the floorboards from the street below, a sound she had not heard since she was nine years old and the veil still breathed easy over Vellharrow.

Footsteps. Small ones. And a voice, low and rhythmic, speaking words in a language that predated every kingdom, every decree, every wall in the city—

The First Tongue, rising through the rain, coming up her stairs.
