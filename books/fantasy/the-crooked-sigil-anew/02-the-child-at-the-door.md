## 2. The Child at the Door

The rain came to Vellharrow the way debt collectors did—suddenly, and with a grudge.

Elodie Yarrow heard it before she saw it: a hiss against the shutters of her shop, a drumming on the awning that had sagged three years ago and never been mended because mending cost coin and coin was a language the city had stopped speaking to her. She set down the brass charm she'd been polishing—a luck-knot, or what passed for one these days—and watched through the window as Tanner's Row dissolved into grey water and running shadows.

*Hollow trinkets for hollow times.* That was what Bren used to say about the trade they'd both fallen into after the purge, back when he still said anything worth remembering. A luck-knot that held no luck. A warding-bell that rang sweet and protected no one. A love-charm of knotted red thread that worked exactly as well as believing in it, which was to say: sometimes, briefly, the way all lies work when the heart is desperate enough to meet them halfway.

She had gotten good at selling them. That was the shame of it. Ten years since the Weaver's Guild had been broken on Magistrate Corvane's anvil, ten years since the inspectors had come through the Row with their ledgers and their fire, and Elodie had learned to smile the particular smile of a woman selling nothing—warm, apologetic, forgettable. *Just charms, goodmaster. Pretty things. Old habits, nothing more.*

The trick was to be invisible without seeming to hide. The trick was to make them look through you.

She was good at that too. She hated how good.

Outside, a guild-inspector's lantern swung past through the rain, its light the color of old cheese, and Elodie's hands went still on the counter out of pure animal habit before her mind caught up and reminded her: *you sell trinkets now. Trinkets are legal. You are legal.* But her body kept its own ledgers, and somewhere beneath her skin, along the faded lines of sigil-marks she'd burned away with lye and prayer a decade ago—or thought she had—something shifted like a sleeper turning over.

The inspector moved on. The rain thickened.

She should close early. Storms like this emptied the street and filled the gutters, and there was no sense burning lamp-oil for customers who wouldn't come. She was reaching for the shutter-hooks when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when knocking is itself an act of courage.

Elodie stood in the middle of her shop, listening, telling herself it was the wind, the sign swinging on its bracket, a branch from the sickly elm two doors down. Then the knocks came again, more insistent, and beneath them she heard something else—a sound half-drowned by the storm, thin and high and wrong. Not crying. Not quite. More like the sound a kettle makes just before it screams.

She opened the door on the chain, the way she always did after dark, and the night leaned in against her face, cold and smelling of wet stone and tannery run-off.

A girl stood on her step.

Small—seven years old, perhaps eight, though hunger and fear could shave years off a child until guessing was cruelty. Soaked to the skin despite a cloak that had once been fine, its hem embroidered in patterns Elodie's eyes slid off of the way they slid off certain dreams. Dark hair plastered to a narrow face. Eyes too large for that face, and the wrong color—not brown, not green, but the deep shifting amber of resin held to a flame.

And she wasn't shivering. That was the first thing that snagged in Elodie's chest like a fishhook. Any child standing in this rain would be blue-lipped and shaking, teeth chattering hard enough to crack. This girl stood in the downpour as if it were falling around her rather than on her, droplets veering at the last instant, and Elodie told herself she was imagining it, because the alternative was a word she hadn't let herself think in ten years.

"Shop's closed," Elodie said through the gap. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Come back tomorrow if—" 

The girl looked up at her. And spoke.

Not Vellharrow's gutter-tongue. Not the high speech of the magistrate's court, which Elodie had heard exactly twice and never wished to again. The sounds that came out of the child were older than either—older than the city, older than the kingdom, older, some buried part of Elodie whispered, than the idea of kingdoms at all. They moved through the rain like things swimming upstream. They made no sense and perfect sense at once, the way a melody makes sense even when you don't know the words.

And under Elodie's skin, her sigil-marks woke up screaming.

She staggered back from the door, hand pressed flat to her sternum, where the oldest mark—the covenant-knot every guild apprentice received at their binding, scarred over and dead for ten years—had begun to burn. Not ache. *Burn*, live coals laid along lines she'd believed were only scars, heat crawling out along her forearms where the lattice-marks ran, up her throat where the breath-sigil sat. She could see them. In the dark of her shop, lit only by the single guttering lamp, she could see them glowing faintly through her own skin like embers under ash, gold-white, pulsing in time with the child's voice.

The girl stopped speaking. The burning didn't stop—it settled into a deep throbbing hum, a struck bell that would not finish ringing—but the girl simply stood there, dripping on the step, watching Elodie with those resin-colored eyes. And in them Elodie saw not triumph, not menace, but recognition so naked and so vast that it frightened her worse than the burning did.

The child knew her. Or knew *of* her. Knew something about her that Elodie herself had spent a decade burying.

"No," Elodie said aloud, to the girl, to the marks, to the whole rotten arrangement of the world. "No, whatever you think I am, I'm not. I sell buttons and bells. I'm nobody."

The girl tilted her head. Then she reached into her cloak.

Every instinct Elodie owned—the instincts of a woman who had survived a purge by being small and quick and unremarkable—screamed at her to slam the door. Instead she watched, frozen, as the child drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, something she clutched with both hands the way children clutch the one thing they were told above all else to protect.

The girl held it up. Held it *out*.

Against every rule she had lived by, Elodie unlatched the chain and opened the door.

Rain gusted in; she pulled the girl inside and shut it behind them both, and then the shop was very quiet except for the storm battering the walls and the child's soft breathing and the low singing hum of Elodie's own blood. Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and something greener underneath, like crushed leaves, like a forest floor after snowmelt. Like places Vellharrow hadn't contained in a hundred years.

"Let me see," Elodie said, and her hands shook as she took the oilcloth bundle.

Unwrapping it felt like unwrapping a wound.

Inside lay a guild token. Cast bronze, palm-sized, shaped as a stylized hand cradling a thread—the mark of the Weaver's Guild, the hand that holds the thread between worlds. It was old, older than the purge, its edges worn smooth by generations of thumbs. And across its face, where every token bore its owner's personal sigil cut deep into the bronze, ran a mark Elodie would have known blindfolded, drunk, dead, or dreaming.

A spiral broken by three crossbars. The mark of Master Aldous Wren.

Her mentor. Dead eight years—drowned, they said, in the canal behind the Scriptorium, an accident, the inspectors said, though everyone on the Row knew what *accident* meant when it happened to a weaver who'd refused to recant. Dead eight years, and here was his token, warm—*warm*, impossibly, as if it had just left his hand—as if the bronze remembered him better than the city allowed anyone to remember anything.

Elodie sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their contract with her. She landed on the stool behind the counter, the token cradled in her palms, and the child watched her with that terrible patient recognition, water pooling off her cloak onto Elodie's clean floorboards.

"Where," Elodie said, and had to start again. "Where did you get this?"

The girl opened her mouth. What came out was the First Tongue again, that flowing, ancient sound, and the marks along Elodie's arms flared in answer, and this time—because the burning stripped away ten years of careful deafness—Elodie almost understood. Not the words. The *shape* of them. The shape said: *given*. The shape said: *he gave it*. The shape said something like *when the water came* and something like *keep it safe* and something like a name, and the name, spoken in that tongue, sounded like wind through a keyhole, and it was not the girl's name.

It was *her* name. Elodie Yarrow, spoken in a language that predated names.

"I don't understand," she whispered, which was a lie, because she understood perfectly and understanding was the thing she couldn't survive. "You can't be here. There's a reward—they post them every few months, notices about strange speech, about signs, and the inspectors—" She stopped. The child was watching her mouth move, tracking each word with fierce concentration, filing it away somewhere behind those amber eyes. Learning. Gods help them both, the girl was *learning*.

The girl reached out and touched the counter. Deliberately. Then pointed at the luck-knots hanging in their rows, the warding-bells, the knotted threads—all of it, the whole hollow inventory of Elodie's cowardice—and made a sound that was not a word in any language but carried its meaning anyway, the way a look carries meaning, the way grief does.

It was not a kind sound.

Heat rose in Elodie's face. "You don't know anything about me," she said. "You don't know what they did. You weren't *here*." But the girl only kept looking at her, and the marks under Elodie's skin pulsed their slow golden pulse, and the token lay warm in her hands like a living thing, like a hand reaching out of eight years of dark water, and Elodie found she could not meet those eyes anymore.

She looked down at the token instead. And saw, for the first time, what was scratched into the oilcloth wrapping—faint, hurried, gouged with a fingernail or a knife-point rather than written with any instrument. Human letters, Vellharrow letters, in a hand she knew as well as her own:

*RUN. THEY ARE COMING FOR HER. BOTH SIDES. — A.W.*

Eight years dead. And writing warnings in the present tense.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain redoubled, hammering the shutters like fists, and beneath the storm—so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she prayed she had—came another sound. Boots. Many boots, moving up Tanner's Row in the cadence she knew better than her own heartbeat: the patrol-step of the guild-inspectors, unhurried, thorough, the walk of men who knew exactly which doors they intended to open.

The child heard it too. She went utterly still, the way small animals go still, and her eyes found Elodie's, and in them was no plea at all—only a question, calm and terrible: *what will you do?*

Elodie Yarrow had spent ten years learning to be invisible. Ten years selling smoke and calling it safety. Ten years walking past the notice-boards with their rewards for strange speech, ten years saying *not my fight, not anymore, not again* until the words wore grooves in her soul like water wearing stone.

Master Aldous Wren had died calling her his best student. His best student had repaid him with silence and knotted thread.

The boots came closer. Three doors away. Two.

Elodie blew out the lamp.

* * *

In the sudden dark, lit only by the fitful glow of her own traitorous skin, Elodie moved fast and quiet, the way she'd moved once, long ago, in another life. She swept the luck-knots from their hooks with one arm—clatter, muffled under a sack—and hauled up the loose floorboard behind the counter, the one that hid the cache every charm-seller kept and admitted to no one: the true stock. The contraband. Three warding-bells that actually warded, bought dear from a smuggler and never once sold. A vial of salt blessed by no church. A bundle of guild-script pages she should have burned years ago and had never managed to, because burning them would mean admitting they were only paper.

"You'll need to be silent," she breathed. "Can you do silent?"

The girl nodded once, grave as a judge.

"Whatever you hear—whatever you *hear*, do you understand me—no sound. No matter what." Elodie took the girl's shoulders, small and cold as river-stones even through the soaked cloak, and the contact sent a fresh wave of warmth spiraling through her marks, gentle now, almost tender, like a hand closing around hers. The girl looked up at her, and slowly, deliberately, pressed the guild token back into Elodie's palm and folded Elodie's fingers over it.

*Keep it safe,* the shape of the old words said again. *He gave it. Keep it safe.*

Down the Row, the boots stopped. Right next door, at Maren's candle-shop, where Maren sold candles and nothing else and had never trafficked in so much as a whispered blessing. Keys rattled. A door splintered inward with a sound like a bone breaking.

"In," Elodie whispered. "Now."

The girl slipped into the dark beneath the floorboards, folding herself small with an ease that was its own quiet horror, and Elodie lowered the board over her and dragged the rug across it and stood in her pitch-black shop with a dead man's token burning in her fist and her heartbeat loud enough to wake the whole street.

The door next door creaked. Maren's voice, high and frightened. An inspector's voice, flat as slate. Questions. The scrape of furniture.

And under Elodie's skin, along every line of every mark she had spent ten years killing, the old sigils glowed on and on and would not sleep, gold-white through her wrists, her throat, her sternum—bright enough, she realized with a lurch of vertigo, to leak through the gaps in the shutters. Bright enough to be seen from the street.

Bright enough to hang her.

She clenched her fists. She thought of nothing, of blankness, of the gray smile and the hollow trinkets, *I am nobody, I am nobody, I am a woman selling buttons in the rain*—and the glow dimmed, sullen, refusing, banking itself down to embers but not out. Never quite out. As if it had waited ten years and did not intend to wait much longer.

The footsteps crossed Maren's shop toward the wall they shared.

Elodie stood alone in the dark, holding a dead man's warning, listening to the empire of ordinary life come apart one door at a time—and understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman stepping off a ledge, that whichever way tonight ended, the woman who had opened this shop that morning was not going to survive it.
