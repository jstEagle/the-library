## 3. What the Inspectors Burn

The knocking started before the lamps were lit.

Elodie was at her workbench, a half-finished luck-knot of braided horsehair between her fingers, when the sound came down Crooked Sigil Lane like a drumbeat: fists on wood, three hard strikes, a pause, then three more. She knew that rhythm. Every shopkeeper on the lane knew it. It was not the knock of a customer. It was the knock of men who had already decided what they would find.

"Get up," she said, and her voice came out low and strange, as though it belonged to someone braver.

The child looked up from the corner where she sat, cross-legged among the scraps of ribbon Elodie had given her to play with. The girl's dark eyes found hers in the gathering dusk—calm eyes, too calm for a child who had been hunted across the threshold of two worlds—and Elodie felt again the burn of the sigil-marks along her forearms, the old guild-writings inked into her skin at nineteen and dead for ten years. They had been warm since the night the girl arrived. Now they were hot.

Three more strikes. Closer. The chandler's shop next door.

*They're working their way down the lane.*

She crossed the floor in four strides and knelt at the edge of the rug beneath her display counter, hauling it back to reveal the trapdoor she had sworn never to use—the one her mentor, Aldous Vane, had made her cut into her own floorboards a decade ago, back when she still laughed at his paranoia. *Every weaver needs a hole to hide in, Ellie,* he'd said, *because the day will come when the only thing left worth saving fits inside one.* She had thought him mad. She had thought him dramatic. He had died in the purge all the same, dragged from his own shop with his hands bound in wire so his sigil-marks could not answer him, and Elodie had spent ten years selling charms without teeth above a hiding place built for treasures she no longer owned.

She pried the door up. Below lay the crawlspace—a shallow cellar of packed earth, barely deep enough to kneel in, lined along one wall with the contraband she had never found the courage to destroy: three true warding-stones that hummed faintly when touched, a jar of grave-moss, a spool of thread spun during the last hours of the guildhall, before the burning. Real things. Forbidden things. The whole small archive of everything she had pretended not to be.

"In here," she said. "Quickly now."

The girl did not move at first. She looked at the hole, then at Elodie, and something passed across her face—not fear but inquiry, the look of someone weighing whether this was a test. Then, deliberately, she reached down and took hold of the hem of her own skirt, folding it against her knees so it would not drag in the dirt, and climbed down into the dark with the unhurried grace of someone stepping into a bath.

Elodie almost laughed. Almost. Instead she pressed a shuttered lantern into the girl's hands—"Don't light it unless you must"—and laid a warding-stone beside her, watching the child's fingers curl around it with an ease that made the marks on Elodie's arms ache worse.

"You stay quiet," she whispered. "No matter what you hear. No matter what you hear me say." She held the girl's gaze until those dark eyes blinked once, slow, in acknowledgment. Then she pulled the trapdoor shut, dragged the rug over it, and turned to face her own front door just as the knocking arrived.

Three strikes. A pause. Three more.

She opened the door with a shopkeeper's smile fixed on her face and a heart hammering somewhere up behind her teeth.

There were four of them on her step. Two guild-inspectors in their grey-and-crimson livery, faces she half-recognized from the parade grounds—a broad man with a broken nose and a narrow woman whose eyes never stopped moving. Behind them waited two more in plain clothes, the kind of watchers Corvane kept off the rolls, the kind who could deny themselves if it became convenient. The broad one held a paper in one gloved hand.

"Mistress Yarrow," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded like a charge being read. "Charm-wright, licensed, third class."

"That's me," Elodie said pleasantly. "Though the license says second class, if we're keeping accounts."

Something flickered in the narrow woman's eyes. Amusement, maybe. Or appetite. "We're conducting inspections," the broad inspector said. "By order of the Magistrate's Office. There have been... irregularities reported on this lane. Unlicensed practices. We'll need to see your premises."

"Of course." She stepped back and let them in, hating how easily her body performed it, how smoothly her hands gestured them across the room where the girl had sat not five minutes past. There was still a scrap of blue ribbon on the floor by the corner. Elodie watched the narrow woman's gaze travel toward it, hover, and move on.

They were thorough. That was the worst of it—they were good at their work, these grey-and-crimson clerks of ruin, tapping walls, opening jars, holding her harmless trinkets up to the lamplight with expressions of professional disappointment. The broad inspector lifted her best-selling love-charm, a knotted cord sold with a printed verse, and turned it over twice.

"Horsehair," he said.

"Local source."

"No resonance." He set it down with something that might have been contempt or might have been envy. In ten years Elodie had learned to read that expression precisely: inspectors hated finding nothing. Finding nothing meant another empty report, another night writing *no irregularities observed* while their masters in the Spire grew richer and the city grew quieter. Finding something meant advancement. Men did not rise under Corvane by confirming peace. They rose by discovering treason.

It was the narrow woman who lingered. She drifted along the wall where Elodie's sigil-marks lay hidden beneath her sleeves, and stopped, and stood very still, the way a pointer stands before the flush.

"Cold in here," she said.

"It's autumn."

"Mm." She turned. Her eyes went over Elodie once, slowly, top to bottom, the way a butcher looks at a hanging carcass and estimates. Then, softly, almost kindly: "You were guild-trained, weren't you? Before. I can always tell. There's a way you people stand near your own shelves. Like a widow standing near a bed."

Elodie let the smile stay exactly where it was. "I sell ribbons and horsehair, mistress. Whatever I stood near ten years ago is ash."

"Is it?" The woman smiled back, and there was nothing kind in it at all. Then she turned away, mercifully, and the inspection wound down through the inventory and the ledgers and the license, and at last they stood at the door, and the broad inspector produced a fresh paper and held it where the lamplight caught it.

"One more matter. Public notice—you're required to post it where customers can see." He handed it over. "Posted this morning by order of the Magistrate himself. Reward notice."

Elodie took the paper and read it, and was grateful—genuinely, desperately grateful—that ten years of practice had taught her face to be a closed shop.

*BY ORDER OF THE HONOURABLE MAGISTRATE CORVANE:* the notice read, in the fine dense script of the Spire scribes, *a reward of two hundred crowns is offered for information leading to the recovery of a GIRL OF STRANGE SPEECH, aged seven to ten years, dark of hair and eye, mute in the common tongue. Said child may attempt communication by signs or by unlicensed vocalization. She is NOT to be harmed. She is to be brought WHOLE AND SPEAKING to the Magistrate's Office. Informants to apply at any guild-house.*

Two hundred crowns. More than most families on the lane earned in five years. And below the official text, in smaller print, the sentence that turned Elodie's blood to ice water:

*Informants are reminded that harboring is punishable under Statute Seven. Prior offenses will be weighed cumulatively.*

*Whole and speaking.* Not *unharmed.* Whole and speaking, because whatever Corvane wanted from the girl, he wanted her able to give it. The phrase sat in Elodie's mind like a fishhook.

"She's not been through here, then?" the narrow woman asked lightly. Too lightly.

"A girl of strange speech." Elodie shook her head, slow and bored. "Half my customers are strange of speech after the third cup. You'll want the taverns, not the charm shops."

The broad inspector grunted. The narrow woman smiled her butcher's smile and said, "Post the notice where it can be seen," and then they were gone, boots receding down the lane toward the cobbler's at the far end, and Elodie stood in her doorway breathing the cold air and counting to a hundred before she closed the door.

Then she bolted it. Then the shutter. Then she lit every lamp she owned, because the dark suddenly felt like a collaborator.

* * *

Beneath the floorboards, the child had heard everything.

That was the thing Elodie understood only later—that the crawlspace carried sound the way a cupped hand carries water, that every word spoken in the shop above had fallen through the boards like rain. When she finally lifted the trapdoor, the girl was sitting in the dirt with the unlit lantern in her lap and the warding-stone clasped to her chest, and her face was composed, and her eyes were wet.

"I'm sorry," Elodie said, stupidly, uselessly. "I'm sorry. It's over. They're gone. Come up—it's safe."

The girl climbed out on her own, refusing the offered hand with a small firm shake of the head, and stood in the lamplight brushing the earth from her skirt with fast, precise strokes. Then she looked at the reward notice, still crumpled in Elodie's fist, and held out her own hand for it.

Elodie hesitated. Then gave it.

The child read it. Actually read it—her eyes tracking line by line, lips pressed together, and Elodie realized with a lurch that of course the girl could read; whatever she was, wherever she had come from, she was no ignorant stray. When she finished, she did not crumple the paper or tear it. She folded it, once, twice, with terrible neatness, and held it out again.

Then she pointed at the words *WHOLE AND SPEAKING*, and looked up at Elodie, and raised one eyebrow.

It was such a grown-up gesture, such a dry, skeptical, *well?* of an expression, that Elodie did laugh then—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, quickly smothered.

"Yes," she said. "He wants you talking. Which means he knows what you are." She crouched down to the girl's level, careful, the way you approach something wild. "Listen to me. What I'm going to ask you—I need you to understand it even if we can't say it. Can you do that?"

The girl nodded.

"He won't stop looking. Two hundred crowns buys a hundred pairs of eyes on this lane alone. So tomorrow—no, tonight—we get you out of this shop. Somewhere safer. Someone who can help us. Do you understand?"

Another nod. Then, unexpectedly, the girl reached out and caught Elodie's wrist—caught it and turned it, pushing back the sleeve with the confidence of someone who had been waiting all evening to do exactly that.

The sigil-marks were visible in the lamplight. Ten years dormant, they should have been faded scarring, old ink gone brown. Instead they burned: every line of the old guild-script lit along Elodie's forearm like embers under skin, glowing brighter than they had since the night the girl first spoke at her door.

The child studied them. Then she looked up, and for the first time since arriving, her composure cracked—not into fear but into something hungrier, more desperate. She pressed Elodie's marked arm between both her small hands, the way you'd press a wound, the way you'd press a prayer, and spoke.

Not the First Tongue this time—or not entirely. The sounds came out halting, shaped strangely, but Elodie caught fragments, enough: *"You... remember... how... to..."* The girl's brow furrowed with effort, hunting for words she shouldn't have, words that had clearly cost her something to learn. *"Fix... it."*

"I don't know what you mean."

The girl's grip tightened. Her eyes were fierce and wet and certain. *"Fix. It."*

And the marks on Elodie's arms flared so bright they threw shadows on the ceiling, and somewhere out on Crooked Sigil Lane a bell began to ring—the watch-bell, the alarm-bell—and Elodie Yarrow, charm-seller of the third class, liar of ten years' excellent standing, understood that the hiding was over.

Whatever she had been, whatever she had buried, the girl in front of her had just dug it up. And there was nowhere left in Vellharrow to hide a thing that glowed.

She blew out the lamps, all but one.

"We leave by the roof," she said.
