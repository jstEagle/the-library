## 4. First Tongue

The girl woke in a room that smelled of dried lavender and old wax, and for three heartbeats she did not know where she was—only that the ceiling above her was made of human wood, planed and nailed by human hands, and that the silence pressing against her ears was the wrong kind of silence.

In her mother's house beneath the silver birches of the Otherworld, silence had been full. Silence there was a woven thing, threaded through with the hum of roots talking to each other in the dark, with the slow breathing of stones, with the far-off singing of the veil itself—that shimmering boundary her people had guarded since before the first human king put a crown on his head. Even when the singing had begun to fray, even as the thin places widened into wounds, there had always been *sound*. The world had never stopped speaking.

Here, the world said nothing at all.

She lay still beneath the rough blanket and listened to the human woman moving about below—the creak of a stair, the clink of a kettle, the small domestic percussion of a life being lived. The woman had taken her in two nights ago, out of the rain, out of the reach of the things that had followed her through the last thin place. The woman's hands were careful. Her eyes, when they rested on the girl, carried an expression the girl had no word for in either tongue: grief wearing the mask of patience.

The girl did not speak. She could not—not in any way these humans would understand. Her voice had been taken from her at the crossing, poured out like water onto the threshold so that she could pass. That was the price the veil exacted now that it was dying: it let nothing through whole. She had come through carrying the fragment, the shard of the covenant-sigil that the wardens had pressed into her keeping, but it had cost her her words, and it had nearly cost her more.

She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, where the fragment slept. It was warm, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire has gone out. Beneath her hand, under skin and bone, something turned over in its sleep—a slow rotation of light—and she felt it answer the morning the way a flower answers the sun.

Below, the stair creaked again. Nearer this time.

* * *

Elodie Yarrow climbed the stairs to the back room with a tray of bread and honeyed tea, and paused on the top step because the door stood open and the child was sitting up, watching her with those eyes.

They were not human eyes. Elodie had known that from the first moment, rain-lashed and desperate as that night had been. They were the grey-green of deep water over stone, and they held light strangely, as though something behind them glowed rather than merely reflected. But it wasn't the color that unsettled her now. It was the attention. The child looked at Elodie the way Elodie imagined one might look at a locked door—measuring it, wondering what was behind it, whether it would open.

"Good morning," Elodie said, and felt foolish immediately, the way one feels foolish speaking loudly to a foreigner, as if volume could bridge language. "There's bread. And tea. It's—well, it's terrible tea, honestly, it's mostly chicory, but it's hot."

The child tilted her head. Her gaze dropped to the sigil-marks on Elodie's forearm, visible where her sleeve had ridden up—the faded guild-marks every Weaver once bore, burned dull and silent for ten years now, ever since the purge. As the girl looked at them, they *itched*. Not burned, not yet, but a warmth stirred beneath the old scars like a banked coal remembering fire.

"You can see them," Elodie said quietly. She set the tray down on the floor between them, deliberately, the way one might set down food before a wild thing. "Most people can't anymore. Most people look right past."

The child reached out—not for the bread, but toward Elodie's arm, stopping an inch short, asking permission with the pause itself. Elodie, heart hammering against ten years of trained caution, extended her wrist.

Small fingers hovered over the marks. And then the girl began to move them—not touching, but tracing shapes in the air just above Elodie's skin, and with each shape the marks warmed further, and Elodie felt something in the room shift, some pressure changing the way air changes before a storm.

The shapes meant something. Elodie was certain of it. They moved with the cadence of speech, rising and falling, pausing, quickening. It was language—but rendered in gesture, in the grammar of hands, because the girl had no other tongue to give.

"I don't understand," Elodie whispered. "I'm sorry. I don't—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Wait."

She went down to the shop and came back with what she had: a stub of charcoal, a sheet of cheap wrapping paper, the kind she used for parceling charms that had no magic in them. She laid the paper flat and drew, quickly, the only symbol she could think of that might mean anything to a child of the Otherworld: the covenant-sigil, or as much of it as she remembered. Her mentor, Aldous Wren, had taught it to her a hundred times in the years before the purge—had drawn it in spilled tea, in candle smoke, in the frost on windows while inspectors walked the street outside. A circle broken in seven places. Seven mended seams.

The girl went very still when she saw it.

Then she took the charcoal from Elodie's hand with a gentleness that undid something in Elodie's chest, and began to draw.

* * *

This was how they spoke, in those first days: in charcoal and paper, in the space between two languages where meaning had to be built by hand, plank by plank, like a bridge thrown across a gorge in the dark.

The girl drew first her home. Elodie recognized none of it and all of it at once—trees whose trunks spiraled like frozen whirlpools, houses grown rather than built, their walls living wood flowering at the eaves. A river that ran silver even in charcoal. Figures with elongated shadows, drawn standing among the trees, their faces left blank—not from lack of skill, Elodie realized, but because the girl seemed unable or unwilling to give them features. As though their faces were not the point. As though they were known too well to need describing.

Family, then. Or her people.

Then the drawings changed. The trees bent. The river ran black. The blank-faced figures scattered like leaves, and the girl's charcoal strokes grew hard and jagged, tearing the paper in two places. She drew a sky splitting open—no, not splitting, *thinning*, rendered as a great luminous membrane stretched taut until it went translucent, and beyond it, pressing through, shapes that Elodie's eyes slid away from even in charcoal, shapes her mind refused to hold.

"The veil," Elodie breathed. "It's failing."

The girl looked up at her. She didn't nod—she did something better. She touched her own chest, over her heart, and then touched the drawing of the torn sky, and then drew a line connecting the two. A thread. From her heart to the wound in the world.

Elodie sat back on her heels. The shop around them seemed suddenly very small, very flammable, very full of guild-inspectors' boots on cobblestones outside.

"You're carrying something," she said slowly. "Something from the other side. Something that belongs to the veil—to the covenant." Her mentor's voice rose unbidden in her memory, lecturing in the cellar behind the shop while Elodie ground charm-base below: *The covenant isn't a contract, girl, it's a seam. Someone has to hold the needle. Someone always has to hold the needle.*

The girl watched her mouth move, reading what she could of the words, and then did the most extraordinary thing. She closed her eyes, and she *hummed*.

No—not hummed. The sound that came out of her was not a sound Elodie had heard in ten years, and hearing it now, in this shabby back room above a shop of hollow trinkets, brought tears to her eyes before she knew they were coming. It was the First Tongue—not spoken, for the girl had no voice to speak it with, but *sung* in its rawest form, the way it had been sung in the guildhall before the purge, the way Aldous had sung it once, drunk and weeping on the anniversary of everything they'd lost. The melody bypassed meaning entirely and went straight to the marrow. Elodie's sigil-marks blazed—not painfully, but with a heat like sunlight through glass—and for one impossible moment she saw, behind her closed eyelids, images that were not hers:

A hall of living wood. Wardens in grey cloaks. A council gathered around a stone that sang. And the stone cracking—one clean fracture, a shard flying loose, and hands reaching, dozens of hands, and one pair of small hands catching it, closing around it, holding on—

Elodie opened her eyes. The girl was watching her, and the humming had stopped, and her expression was the oldest expression in the world: *Did you see? Did you understand?*

"I saw," Elodie said hoarsely. "I don't know what I saw. But I saw it."

* * *

At night, the dreams came.

Elodie had dreamed ordinary dreams for ten years—dreams of ledgers and inspectors and Aldous's face, which faded a little more each year no matter how fiercely she gripped it. Now the dreams were not hers. She understood that with a dreamer's strange clarity, the way one understands in dreams that the house one stands in is not one's own house. She dreamed in colors that did not exist in Vellharrow: a green that was also a sound, a gold that tasted of cold water. She dreamed of running through a forest that wept, actual tears beading on the bark of the trees, and behind her the sky peeling back like old paint, and in her arms—or in her chest, it was never clear which—a weight of light that grew heavier with every step.

She dreamed of the crossing. Of standing at a thin place in the veil—a riverbank, perhaps, or a place where two hills met, the geography kept deliberately vague even by her dreaming mind—and of the price being named. She could not hear the naming, but she felt it: the sensation of something being poured out of her, and looking down at her own hands afterward and finding them empty in a way that had nothing to do with objects.

Her voice. The veil had taken her voice as toll.

And she dreamed—this was the worst one, the one she woke from with her heart slamming—of being small, smaller than she was, hiding beneath the roots of a spiral tree while shapes moved overhead that her waking mind would not permit her to remember. Shapes that had followed her across. Shapes that were still following her, here, in Vellharrow, patient as rot.

She woke on the fourth night and went upstairs without deciding to, barefoot on cold boards, and found the girl already awake, sitting up in the dark, hugging her knees. Their eyes met in the gloom.

"You dream it too," Elodie whispered. "Now I dream it too. Whatever you carry—it's leaking. Into me. Into the marks."

The girl unfolded herself and patted the blanket beside her. An invitation. Elodie, who had not shared a bed or a confidence or a cause with anyone in ten years, sat down on the edge of it, stiff as a fence post.

The girl took her hand. Small fingers, cool as riverstones. And she pressed Elodie's palm flat against her own chest, over her heart, and held it there.

Beneath the skin, the fragment turned in its sleep. Warmth bloomed outward through Elodie's hand, up her wrist, along the dead channels of her sigil-marks, and for a moment—only a moment—she felt what the girl felt. Not thoughts. Feelings, vast and simple and devastating: the weight of being the only one. The loneliness of carrying a piece of a dying world inside a body too small for it. The terror of the hunters behind and the strangers ahead. And underneath all of it, so faint it was almost not there, a thread of something else—hope, maybe, or its raw material—winding toward Elodie like a root finding water.

*You,* the feeling said, in the wordless way feelings say things. *You are the first door that opened.*

Elodie withdrew her hand. Her eyes were wet. "I don't know how to do this," she told the girl honestly. "I don't know how to protect you. I sell charms that don't work. I've spent ten years being nobody so they'd leave me alone. And you—" she gestured helplessly at the child, at the sleeping light beneath her ribs, at the torn sky in the drawings stacked on the floor— "you're the most dangerous thing in this city right now, and you're sitting on my guest bed eating my terrible chicory tea biscuits."

The girl considered this. Then she picked up the charcoal and the paper one more time.

She did not draw the Otherworld this time. She drew the shop—the counter, the shelves, the crooked little window with its display of useless luck-charms. She drew herself standing behind the counter, and Elodie beside her, and above the shop she drew a sign, and on the sign she drew the covenant-sigil, whole and mended, all seven seams closed, burning gently like a lamp.

Elodie stared at it for a long time.

"That's not a drawing," she said finally. "That's a question. You're asking me if I'll help you mend it."

The girl's grey-green eyes held hers, steady, ancient, six years old.

Outside, somewhere down the street, a whistle blew—two short notes, one long. The patrol signal. And beneath it, fainter, from the direction of Tanner's Row, another sound that Elodie had hoped never to hear again in her lifetime: the crackle of burning wood, and voices shouting, and the bell of the watch-house beginning to ring.

The inspectors had found someone. And they were working their way up the street.
