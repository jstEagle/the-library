## 5. The Last of the Guild

The child slept in the loft above the shop, curled beneath Elodie's own winter cloak, and in sleep she looked like any other girl—small hands, chapped lips, a smudge of soot on one cheek. It was only when she was awake that the strangeness showed, in the way her eyes tracked things that were not there, in the words she mouthed silently against the window glass, syllables that made the frost bloom into patterns no frost should know.

Elodie watched her from the foot of the ladder for a long moment, then pulled on her coat and slipped out through the back.

She had told herself she would go alone. She had told herself a great many things in ten years, and most of them had been lies of the comfortable kind—the kind that let you keep your head down and your shutters closed and your hands busy with harmless tin. But last night, when the child had pressed the guild token into her palm and spoken the First Tongue until Elodie's dormant marks burned like brands beneath her skin, every comfortable lie had caught fire at once.

*Find them,* the burning seemed to say. *You are not the last. You were never the last. You simply stopped looking.*

The dockside quarter smelled of tar and herring brine and old rain, as it always did, but this morning it smelled also of something sweeter and more rotten: cheap spirits spilled across cobblestones, the perfume of men who drank to forget what they'd been. The Saltcrust Tavern leaned at the end of Gullwharf Lane like a drunk leaning on a friend, its sign—a whale painted by someone who had clearly never seen one—swinging in the grey wind off the water.

Bren would be inside. Bren was always inside. That was the whole shape of his life now, as far as anyone knew: the tavern at opening, the tavern at closing, and the fog between.

Elodie pushed open the door.

The interior was dim and blue with pipe smoke, lit by tallow candles that guttered whenever the door breathed. A dozen fishermen sat hunched over their cups, and at the corner table farthest from the hearth sat a man who might once have been broad-shouldered but was now merely wide, folded into himself like a letter someone had given up trying to read.

His hair had gone iron-grey at the temples. His beard was untrimmed. But his hands—his hands were still beautiful, even wrapped around a clay cup, long-fingered and precise, the hands of a scribe, and seeing them Elodie felt something twist behind her ribs.

"Bren," she said.

He didn't look up. "Don't know the name."

"It's me."

"I said I don't know the name." He took a swallow. "Whoever you're looking for, he's not here. Nobody here is anybody."

One of the fishermen snorted. Elodie crossed the room, pulled out the chair opposite him, and sat, setting her palms flat on the sticky table. Up close she could see what the dimness hid: the faded ink stains still ghosting his knuckles, deep in the creases where no amount of washing could reach them. Ten years of ale hadn't drowned them. Some things soak too deep.

"The child speaks the First Tongue," she said quietly.

Bren went very still. Not dramatically—there was no flinch, no spillage—but absolutely, completely still, the way a hare goes still under a hawk's shadow. Even his breathing stopped.

Then he laughed, low and ugly, and finally raised his eyes. They were bloodshot and yellowed and, beneath all of it, terrified.

"Ten years," he said. "Ten years I keep my head down, and you walk in here off the street and say *that word* out loud in a room full of strangers."

"They're not listening."

"Everyone's listening. Everyone's *always* listening. That's how they got Master Aldous." His voice cracked on the name, and he covered it with another drink. "That's how they got everyone."

"Not everyone. Tammas is alive. He forges horseshoes on Cooper's Row and pretends he never held a stylus in his life."

"You've seen him?"

"Once a year, at market. We nod. We don't speak. Like everyone else."

Bren set down his cup. For a moment his hands trembled above the table, and Elodie saw him fight the trembling the way a drowning man fights water. "Why are you telling me this? Why now? Why come dig up corpses?"

"Because the child carries a guild token. Aldous's token. And because—" Elodie hesitated, then reached beneath her collar and drew out the chain she'd worn hidden for a decade, the one bearing her own sigil-mark, dull and cold as river stone. Last night it had burned. This morning it was warm again, faintly, like a banked coal. "My mark woke up, Bren. After ten years of nothing. When she spoke."

He stared at the little pendant. Something moved behind his eyes—longing, mostly, and grief, and underneath both a terrible, hungry hope that he clearly hated himself for feeling.

"No," he said.

"Bren—"

"*No.*" Louder this time. The fishermen glanced over; he dropped his voice to a furious whisper. "You know what they did to Aldous? You weren't there. I was. They made us watch. They made us *sign* the dissolution order first—our own names, our own hands, signing away everything we were—and then they took him into the square and burned his work in front of him, every page, forty years of transcription, and when he tried to stop them they broke his hands. Both of them. A scribe's hands, Elodie. And then—" His throat worked. "And then the rest."

"I know what they did."

"Do you?" His eyes were wet and furious. "Do you know what it's like to sign your name to that? To stand there and do it, because if you didn't, they'd take you too, and someone had to survive to remember the transcriptions, someone had to carry the canon forward in his *head*, because paper burns but—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I signed. I signed and I ran and I drank, and it worked. Being nobody works. It's the only thing that's ever worked."

Elodie reached across the table and took his wrist—not gently, not cruelly, just firmly, the way you hold someone who is about to walk into traffic. Beneath her fingers she felt his pulse hammering.

"She's eight years old," Elodie said. "Maybe nine. She can't speak—not our tongue, not yet. She came to my shop in a storm carrying a dead man's token, and Corvane has posted a reward for her, which means hunters will come, which means that right now, tonight, she is sleeping above my shop while I sit here feeling sorry for myself with the only two people alive who might help me protect her." She leaned closer. "You carried the canon in your head for ten years, Bren. Was it so some day you could watch it die twice?"

For a long time he said nothing. The candle between them guttered and recovered. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the water, and the whale-sign creaked on its bracket.

When Bren finally lowered his hands, his face had changed. The drunkenness was still there—he would be drunk for years, maybe forever—but beneath it, surfacing like something rising through dark water, was the face Elodie remembered from the guildhall: young, sharp, frightened on the day of the purge and resolute anyway.

"Aldous's token," he said hoarsely. "Describe it."

"Bronze. A circle broken by a vertical line, with three dots in the upper quadrant. Worn smooth on one edge, like he thumbed it constantly."

Bren closed his eyes. "The Mediator's Mark," he whispered. "First degree. Only masters carried it." He opened his eyes, and they were clear for the first time since Elodie had entered. "Masters carried it, and apprentices buried their masters' bodies, and I have spent ten years trying to forget which knot we used for the shroud." He exhaled, long and shaking. "Where is she?"

"At my shop. Hidden."

"Hidden badly, I'll wager. You were always a better charmer than a hider." He stood, swaying slightly, then steadied himself against the table with those beautiful ruined hands. "Cooper's Row first. If we're doing this, we do it with Tammas or not at all. He's the only one of us who can still make something real."

* * *

Tammas Vell worked his forge with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and his forearms were magnificent—corded, scarred with spark-burns, blackened to the shoulder. He did not look up when the bell above his door rang, nor when two sets of footsteps crossed his floor. He finished his weld, quenched the iron in the trough with a hiss like a serpent's anger, and only then turned around, tongs still in hand.

He looked at Bren. He looked at Elodie. He looked at the space between them, which was full of ten years of unsaid things.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear. Two guild ghosts walk into my forge after a decade of nodding at each other across a market square like a pair of shy lovers. There's only one reason anyone comes to Cooper's Row wearing that face." He hung the tongs on their hook with deliberate care. "Answer's no. I make hinges now. Hinges and horseshoes and the occasional decorative weathervane. Iron doesn't get people burned."

"Hinges don't save children either," Elodie said.

"Children aren't my problem."

"The child speaks the First Tongue."

The silence that followed was total. The forge ticked as the coals settled. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up. Tammas stood with his back half-turned, and Elodie watched his shoulders rise once, slowly, and fall.

"That's not possible," he said at last. "The First Tongue died with the covenant. That's why the guild existed—to mediate what was left of it. Scraps. Echoes. We spent centuries learning to *whisper* across the veil, and even the whispers stopped when the purges began. The Otherworld closed its doors. There's no one left over there to talk to."

"There's someone over here who talks to it," Elodie said. "In her sleep. In her waking. My sigil-marks woke for the first time in ten years when she spoke. Bren's carrying the canon in his head, whether he wants to or not. And Corvane has posted a reward for 'a girl of strange speech,' which means the magistrate knows about her, which means whatever she is, she matters enough to hang us all."

Tammas finally turned fully around. He was broader than Bren, blunter, a man whose grief had hardened rather than dissolved, and his eyes were the flat grey of unpolished steel. He studied Elodie for a long moment.

"You've seen her work? Her magic, I mean. Not just words."

"She frosted my window into patterns I couldn't read. She made my dead marks burn. And she carries Aldous's master-token, which she shouldn't have, because Aldous died clutching it, and I helped bury what was left of him."

"We buried him," Bren corrected quietly. "All three of us. Don't rewrite it to make yourself feel worse, Elodie. You were there. You dug. We all dug."

"And then we scattered," Tammas said. "Like ash. Like we were *meant* to scatter. You ever wonder about that? The purge didn't kill us—it *dispersed* us. Every survivor sent to a different quarter, watched, listed, forbidden to gather. Corvane didn't want us dead. Dead guilds become martyrs. Scattered guilds become nothing." He picked up a cold piece of scrap iron and turned it over in his fingers, not looking at it. "So why does he want the girl found, if scattering works so well?"

"Because she's not guild," Elodie said. "She's something else. Something the purge wasn't built for."

Tammas set the iron down. He walked to the window, glanced out at the empty street—an old habit, Elodie thought, an old necessary habit—and pulled the shutter closed. The forge-light leapt up the walls, turning the little shop into a cave of orange shadow.

"If I say yes," he said, "it's not like the old days. No guildhall, no charter, no protection. Just three nobodies and a child against the magistrate's whole apparatus. If we're caught, we're not arrested. We're disappeared. You understand that?"

"I understand it."

"And you—" he pointed the tongs at Bren "—you'll need to sober up. Can you?"

Bren laughed, but there was no humor in it. "I've been sober in my head for ten years. The rest of me just hasn't caught up." He rubbed his ink-stained knuckles. "Ask me again when it matters."

"It matters now."

"Then ask me tomorrow, when my hands have stopped shaking, and I'll give you the same answer I'm giving you now, which is yes, damn you both, yes." He sagged onto a stool, suddenly looking every year of his age and several he hadn't earned. "Aldous would have hated this. He'd have said mediation, not confrontation. He'd have wanted to *talk* to Corvane."

"Corvane burned him," Elodie said.

"Yes," Tammas said softly. "Which is why I'm done talking." He pulled a key from around his neck—plain iron, unremarkable—and unlocked a cabinet Elodie had never noticed, built flush into the stone wall behind the forge. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay objects that made her breath catch: a stylus of blackened silver. A spool of thread that shimmered wrong, colors sliding across it like oil on water. A small ledger, its cover stamped with the broken-circle sigil.

"You kept contraband," she whispered. "All this time. In a working forge, with inspectors walking past your windows."

"Best hiding place in Vellharrow," Tammas said grimly. "Nobody looks at a blacksmith's cabinet and thinks *magic*. They think *tools*." He lifted the ledger and held it a moment before handing it to Bren, who received it with both hands, reverently, like a priest receiving scripture. "Guild registry. Names, marks, locations—as of ten years ago. Out of date, but it's a start. If we're going to protect this child, we should know how many of us are left to do the protecting."

Bren opened the ledger with trembling fingers. Pages of names in a dozen hands, columns of sigil-marks drawn in fading ink, annotations in Aldous's tight, precise script. He turned page after page, and with each turn his face grew grimmer.

"So many struck through," he murmured. "Executed. Fled. Unknown." He paused. His finger stopped on an entry near the bottom of a page, and he went pale. "Elodie."

"What?"

He turned the ledger toward her. There, in Aldous's handwriting, was an annotation beside a name she didn't recognize—a name marked with the Mediator's Mark, first degree, same as the token.

*"If the covenant fails, seek the Drowned Chapel beyond the walls. What was broken can be mended, but the price is written in the founding terms. Tell no one. Trust the token-bearer."*

Below it, dated three days before the purge:

*"They are coming for me. I have hidden what matters. Let the others live small lives and stay safe. Do NOT gather. Do NOT reforge. Whatever comes through the veil, the cost of renewal is more than any of you know."*

The forge crackled. Bren's finger trembled on the page.

"He knew," Bren whispered. "Aldous knew what was coming, and he told us to stay apart. Told us *not* to do exactly what we're doing right now."

Elodie stared at the entry, and beneath her collar, her sigil-mark pulsed—once, twice—warm and insistent, like a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

"Then either he was wrong," she said slowly, "or he was protecting us from something worse than Corvane. Either way, the child is here, the token chose my shop, and my mark woke up after ten years of silence. Aldous isn't here to ask. So we decide." She looked at them both—Bren hunched over the ledger like a penitent, Tammas standing rigid by his shuttered window. "Are we gathered, or aren't we?"

Tammas closed the cabinet. Bren closed the ledger. Neither of them answered immediately, and in that silence Elodie heard everything: the fear, the guilt, the decade of small lives lived in whispers.

Then Tammas said, "Someone has to watch the girl tonight. Your shop's the first place they'll look."

"I'll go," Bren said, already reaching for his coat. "Drunk scribes are invisible. Everyone assumes we're harmless." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at them with an expression Elodie hadn't seen on his face in ten years—something old and fierce and almost glad. "Gathered, then. Gods help us. Gathered."

The bell above the door chimed as he left. Tammas began banking his forge, and Elodie stood a moment longer in the orange light, her hand pressed flat against the warmth beneath her collar, thinking of Aldous's warning, of the price written in the founding terms, of a child asleep above a charm shop full of hollow trinkets.

Outside, the first drops of evening rain began to fall on Cooper's Row—and far off, toward the magistrate's spire, a bell tolled once, though no hour matched it.
