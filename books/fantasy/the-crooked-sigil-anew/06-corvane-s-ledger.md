## 6. Corvane's Ledger

The ledger had no name, and that was the first lesson of power: what matters most must never be written where it can be read.

Magistrate Aldous Corvane kept it in a room beneath the Hall of Judgment, behind a door that opened only to his blood—literally, for the lock was a covenant-lock of the old make, and it drank a bead from his thumb each time he turned the key. The room was small, windowless, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with stone. The conduits ran through its walls like veins through marble, thin channels of silvered glass that hummed at the edge of hearing, and along those veins moved something that was not quite light and not quite water. Covenant-power. The raw current of the bargain struck between realms before Vellharrow had a name.

He sat at his desk each night after the Hall emptied, and he counted.

Not coin. Coin was a tool for lesser men, a way of keeping score among people who could not keep score any other way. Corvane counted years. His own, specifically—the ones remaining, measured against the ones purchased—and tonight, as every night, the arithmetic came out short.

He dipped his pen and made the entry in his own private shorthand, a cipher of his own devising, unbreakable even if the room were breached, which it would not be, because no one alive knew the room existed except the three men who maintained the conduits, and two of them had been pensioned into comfortable silence in the provinces, and the third—

The third was due for an accident in the spring. Corvane noted that too, in the same dispassionate hand. There was no cruelty in it. There was no anything in it. That was the discipline. A man who felt things about necessary work was a man who hesitated, and hesitation was how the old guild-masters had died—not at the hands of soldiers but at their own, in the half-second when mercy or doubt got between them and the work.

* * *

Ten years ago, they had called it the Purification.

Corvane remembered standing on the steps of the Hall while the guild-house burned, watching the smoke go up black and greasy over Tanner's Row, and the crowd below him had wept and cheered in equal measure, because crowds always did both when you gave them permission. He had given a speech. He was rather proud of it still. *The age of hidden powers is ended,* he had said. *No more shall unseen hands reach into your cradles and your ledgers and your lungs.* The words had been true, in their way. He simply neglected to mention whose unseen hands would replace them.

The city believed he had outlawed magic out of piety. Let them. Piety was a fine coat; it never wore out, never needed mending, and fit every occasion. The truth was simpler and uglier and far more useful: magic was a resource, and resources left in common were squandered. A hundred petty charm-sellers, each dribbling a thimbleful of covenant-current into love-tokens and luck-knots and cures for colicky infants—it was waste on a scale that offended him the way other men were offended by blasphemy. Every trinket sold in Vellharrow's crooked lanes was a drop of the same wellspring he had spent a decade diverting, drop by patient drop, into his own veins.

For that was the secret under all the secrets: the covenant did not die when the guilds fell. Covenants do not die. They are not living things; they are terms, and terms persist until renegotiated. When the Weavers were scattered and their sigils broken, the current that once flowed openly between the realms did not stop flowing. It pooled. It backed up in the world's hidden plumbing like water behind a dammed river, and Corvane had spent ten years and a fortune in bribes and disappearances tapping that pool, running hair-thin conduits from the veil-places—the old chapel crypts, the sunken shrine beneath the prison, the crack in the world under the Spire itself—up into the walls of his own house.

The effect on his body was subtle, and he monitored it with a physician's rigor. His hair had stopped graying at fifty-two. The tremor in his left hand, inherited from his father along with the estate and the gout, had quieted. Wounds closed a day faster than they should. He slept four hours and woke clear. Small things. Deniable things. But he had watched the ledger long enough to know the pattern: each year bought him another year, and then some. Compounding. He was sixty-one now and had the body of a man of forty-five, and if the curve held—if the conduits held—he might see ninety. Might see a hundred.

Might see the century turn twice.

That was the ambition, though he never spoke it aloud, not even to himself in this room, because words had weight here. But the arithmetic knew. The arithmetic always knew. A man who could live indefinitely did not need heirs, did not need legacy, did not need to be good or loved or remembered. He needed only to keep the taps open and the ledger balanced and the city asleep. Vellharrow thought it had been freed of magic. In truth it had been harvested, quietly, comprehensively, and the harvester intended to go on harvesting until the sun burned out.

There was, of course, a cost. There is always a cost; the guilds had understood that better than anyone, which was precisely why they had to fall. The veil between the realms was thinning. Corvane's surveyors in the outer parishes reported frost in midsummer, birds flying in circles, hedges that bloomed out of season and then died overnight. The Otherworld pressed against its side of the skin of things like a face against wet cloth. He had read the reports with interest but without alarm. Thinning was manageable. Thinning was, in fact, useful—a thinner veil meant a richer pool, meant fuller conduits, meant more years. If the boundary eventually failed altogether, well. He would deal with failure when failure arrived. He had outlived every man who ever told him something could not be done.

What he had not planned for—what no arithmetic had prepared—was a child.

* * *

The report came up from the street-level inspectors just past midnight, delivered by a young man named Hesk who had the particular grayness of face that Corvane liked to cultivate in his subordinates: ambition without imagination. Hesk stood at attention in the office above the ledger-room, holding a folder as though it might bite him, which, in a sense, it might.

"The charm quarter," Hesk said. "Tanner's Row and the lanes off it. A girl, eight or nine years of age, appeared three nights ago. No family claiming her. No record. And she—" he hesitated, and Corvane let him hesitate, because watching men wrestle with their own disbelief was one of the few pleasures left that never dulled—"she speaks, sir. Or makes sounds. The shopkeepers say it isn't any tongue they know. One woman swears she heard it in her grandmother's stories. Old words. Words from before."

Corvane set down his pen.

"Before," he repeated.

"The parish priest was called. He couldn't place it either, but he—" Hesk's throat worked. "He crossed himself, sir. Twice. And then he went home and locked his door, and this morning he sent word asking to be transferred to the coast parishes."

Corvane rose and walked to the window. Below, the Hall of Judgment square lay silvered with frost that should not have been there in this season, spreading from the Spire's shadow in delicate feathered patterns, and beyond it the city hunched under its smoke, ten thousand chimneys breathing, ten thousand sleepers dreaming in warm ignorance of what flowed through the walls around them.

A child speaking the First Tongue.

He knew the term. Of course he knew it. He had spent ten years buying, burning, and burying every text that used it. The First Tongue was not a language in the way Vellharrowan was a language; it was the grammar of the covenant itself, the speech in which the original terms had been struck, the tongue in which the veil had been told to hold. The guild-masters had spoken it in their rites. It was said—by sources now ash—that a speaker of the First Tongue was not merely talking to the boundary between worlds but *addressing* it, commanding it, keying it open or shut.

And a child born to it, raised in it, mute in every ordinary tongue—

"A living key," Corvane murmured.

"Sir?"

"Nothing." He turned back. "The reward. Post it by morning. Fifty crowns for information leading to the recovery of a lost child, girl, eight or nine years, found wandering the charm quarter. Say nothing of the speech. Say nothing of anything. I want her found by my people before the story grows teeth. Understood?"

Hesk blinked. "Fifty crowns is generous for a foundling, Magistrate."

"Fifty crowns is cheap," Corvane said, "for a miracle."

He dismissed the young man and descended alone to the ledger-room, where the conduits hummed in their walls and the cold had teeth. He stood a long moment with his hand flat against the silvered glass, feeling the current move beneath it, slow and deep and patient as groundwater, and for the first time in ten years he felt something adjacent to fear—not fear for himself, but fear for the ledger, for the great compounding sum of purchased years that depended on everything remaining exactly as he had arranged it.

Because a living key changed the arithmetic entirely.

If the girl could speak the covenant open, she could speak it shut. She could collapse the pool he had spent a decade filling. Worse—far worse—she could be taken by others. The Otherworld was pressing at the veil; its wardens and hunters would smell the child the way sharks smell blood across leagues of water. And there were survivors of the purge, surely—there were always survivors, cockroaches of the old order—who would recognize what she was and rally around her, and then the guilds would be reborn not as beggars selling toothless charms but as mediators again, arbiters again, a rival power with a legitimate claim to the very wellspring Corvane had privatized.

Unless he reached her first.

His mind, that old patient machine, began to lay out the board. The reward would bring informants crawling within days. His inspectors would sweep the charm quarter shop by shop—and there was a certain justice in that, he thought, since it was the charm quarter's trade that had forced his hand a decade ago. If the girl surfaced, he would take her gently, publicly, tearfully—a magistrate rescuing a lost orphan, the crowd would weep—and then she would go down into the deep rooms, and he would study her, and he would learn what she could do, and he would find a way to make her do it *for* him. Children could be shaped. He had shaped a city; a child would be simple.

And if shaping failed—well. The covenant demanded renewal, the buried texts said. Renewal demanded sacrifice. He had always assumed the old guild-masters had exaggerated, dressing up their politics in liturgy. But if they hadn't, if the terms were literal—

Then a living key, freely offered, might buy him not years but centuries.

Corvane opened the ledger and, beneath the night's entries, wrote a single line in his private cipher. Then he sat back and looked at it, and the conduits hummed, and somewhere far beneath the city the veil thinned by another hair's breadth, and the frost crept another foot across the square.

The line read: *Find the key. Keep the well.*

By dawn, fifty-crown notices were pasted on every corner of the charm quarter, and in a small shop between a chandler's and a shuttered apothecary, a woman named Elodie Yarrow was hiding a mute child beneath her floorboards, among contraband charms, while inspectors hammered on her neighbor's door.

Neither of them yet knew the other existed.

But the ledger knew. The ledger always knew.
