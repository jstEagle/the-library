## 7. Smoke Over Tanner's Row

Bren smelled the smoke before he saw it.

That was the dockside quarter's one gift to a man who had spent ten years teaching himself not to care: the wind off the harbor carried everything to him first. Tar and brine and fish-rot, yes, but also woodsmoke, lamp oil, and—when the guild-inspectors were burning something they shouldn't be burning, something that fought back—the sharp mineral stink of quench-salt, the stuff they packed around charms to smother whatever stubborn spark of power still lived in them. He'd smelled it once before, ten years ago, from a rooftop on Scribes' Walk, watching the Weaver's Guildhall go up in a column of black that could be seen from the far hills. He had been twenty-six years old and he had done nothing. He had stood there with his master's ledger under his arm—the only thing he'd saved—and he had watched, and he had told himself afterward for a decade that there had been nothing else to do.

He was thirty-six now, and the smell was coming from Tanner's Row, and this time there was a child involved.

He ran.

Not well. Ten years of ale had seen to that. His lungs burned by the second street and his knees complained by the third, and he took the shortcut through the cooper's yard because his body remembered the old escape routes even if his mind had spent a decade trying to drown them. He came out onto Tanner's Row at a stumbling sprint, sweat stinging his eyes, and stopped dead in the mouth of the alley.

The safehouse was a candle-shop front with a cellar that went down two floors and connected, through a bricked-over archway, to the old tannery cisterns. It had been Maren's idea originally—one of her small jokes, hiding the last free scribes of Vellharrow beneath a shop that sold candles, as though light itself would vouch for them. Bren had been drinking in the Saltcrust when Elodie Yarrow found him three days ago, and everything since had happened too fast to think about, which was, he suspected, exactly how she planned it.

Now the candle-shop was an open wound of flame. The roof had gone; fire stood up out of the ruin in orange banners that leaned and snapped in the harbor wind. Sparks wheeled upward like startled birds. And along the front of the building, in a neat line, six inspectors in grey-and-crimson livery worked their way down the row with iron pokers and sacks of quench-salt, prying boards loose, salting doorframes, making certain—not just that the safehouse died, but that nothing of it could ever be used again.

Bren pressed himself into the shadow of a rain barrel and did the arithmetic of cowardice, which was the one sum he had never gotten wrong. Six inspectors. One drunk scribe. No weapons worth the name. Somewhere inside that fire—or already gone, please, already gone—were Elodie, Tammas, and a mute girl who spoke the First Tongue and burned like a lit window in the dark to anyone who knew how to look.

He should run. Every instinct honed over ten careful, shameful years said *run*, get clear, live to drink another night, tell yourself later there was nothing you could do.

Then the cellar doors at the side of the building burst outward, and Tammas came through them like a landslide.

* * *

Bren had forgotten how big Tammas was. He'd been big before the purge—a charm-maker with forearms like mooring rope—but ten years at the forge had turned him into something geological. He came out of the burning doorway carrying a cellar door across his shoulders as a shield, embers sliding off it, and behind him came Elodie with the girl wrapped in her own coat, and behind *them* came two inspectors who learned very quickly what a smith's hammer feels like when it is swung by a man who has stopped being polite about it.

"Left!" Tammas roared, and it wasn't a suggestion. "Down the cooper's lane, Bren, move your arse!"

Bren moved his arse. He grabbed Elodie's sleeve as she staggered past—the girl was a dead weight against her shoulder, half-carried—and hauled them both into the alley. Behind them, Tammas backed out of the doorway with the hammer still swinging, giving ground grudgingly, the way a mountain gives ground to the sea. An inspector lunged; Tammas caught the man's poker mid-swing and simply closed his fist around it until the iron screamed.

"The cisterns," Bren gasped. "Under the tannery—I know the way—"

"I know you know the way," Elodie snapped. She wasn't even breathing hard, or she was hiding it well; there was soot on her cheek and a wildness in her eyes that Bren hadn't seen since before the purge, and underneath the wildness something harder was setting like mortar. "You've been drinking in every tunnel-mouth in this city for ten years, Bren. Tonight it finally counts for something."

They went down through the cooper's yard, past the great upended barrels stacked like sleeping cattle, and Bren shouldered aside the rotten planking that covered the old drain-grate. The drop into the dark was slick with ten years of harbor damp, and he went down it on his back like a man descending into his own grave, arms out to catch the child when Elodie lowered her to him.

She was lighter than he expected. That frightened him more than the fire had.

* * *

The tunnels under Vellharrow were older than the city above them, older than the kingdom, older—some said—than the covenant itself. Storm-drains and smuggler-runs and the buried bones of streets that had existed before some ancient flood, all stitched together by generations of people who needed to move without being seen. Bren had mapped half of them as a young scribe, back when mapping the undercity was part of a guild education, because the Weavers had always understood that any city built on a boundary between worlds needed ways to move quietly along its seams.

His feet remembered. That was the terrible, humbling truth of it: his mind was a fog of ale and grief, but his feet knew these stones the way a pen knows a page. Left at the fork where the brick changed color. Down the stair cut into living rock. Under the low arch where you had to duck or lose your scalp. He led them through the dark with one guttering lantern-stub he'd had the sense to bring, and behind him came the sound of Elodie's breathing and the child's silence, and somewhere far above and behind, muffled by forty feet of earth and stone, the crackle of Tanner's Row burning.

They stopped at last in a chamber where three tunnels met, a round vaulted room that had been a shrine once—you could still see the niches in the walls where offerings had sat, and a worn basin in the floor that might have held water or might have held something else. Bren set the lantern down. Its light barely reached the ceiling.

Tammas caught them up five minutes later, filthy, singed, and bleeding from a gash along his jaw, and dropped onto a stone bench as though his strings had been cut.

"They'll seal the row," he said. "By morning there'll be checkpoints on every street from the harbor to the Guildhall steps. Corvane's not playing anymore."

"Corvane stopped playing ten years ago," Bren said. "We just refused to notice." He looked at Elodie. "How many knew about the candle-shop?"

"Maren. You. Me. Tammas. Two others who are now either running or dead." Her voice was flat. "Someone talked."

"Or someone was followed," Tammas said. "Maren doesn't talk. Not for coin, not for pain. I'd stake my forge on it."

"Your forge is ash by now," Bren said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Tammas looked at him with an expression that had no anger in it at all, only a vast tired agreement, and that was worse.

The child stirred against Elodie's side. She had been silent the whole flight—silent as she had been silent since the night she appeared at the charm-shop door—but now she reached up and touched Elodie's face, two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch something to check whether it was real. Then she looked around the chamber, at the niches, at the basin, and her whole body went still in a different way. Attentive. Like a dog hearing a whistle no human ear can catch.

"She knows this place," Elodie said softly.

"It's a shrine," Bren said. "Pre-purge. Pre-kingdom, maybe. The Others used to—" He stopped. He had spent ten years not saying sentences like that out loud, and the habit sat in his throat like a bone. But Elodie was looking at him, and the child was looking at him, and Tammas was looking at him, and Bren Voss, disgraced guild-scribe, keeper of a ledger nobody had wanted and nobody had burned, heard himself say the rest anyway.

"The Others used to come to places like this. Where the veil wore thin. Before Corvane outlawed the guilds, the Weavers maintained the crossings. We weren't mages—we were *mediators*. Doorkeepers. And every crossing, every rite, every binding we ever worked—" He swallowed. "Every one of them was anchored to a sigil-stone. There were two."

Elodie went very still. "Two."

"The guildhall stone burned with the hall. Everyone knows that story; Corvane made sure of it. But the guild kept a second. Older. The *first* stone, the one the original covenant was cut on, back when the veil was new." Bren heard his own voice steadying, the scribe rising up through the drunk like a drowned man surfacing. "When the purge came, Master Aldous didn't trust the guildhall vaults—he'd seen which way the magistrate's wind was blowing for years. So he moved it. Out beyond the walls, past the tannery flats, to the chapel that flooded when they rerouted the Graywater. The drowned chapel. He hid it below the waterline and he never told the council where. He told me."

"Why you?" Tammas asked.

"Because I was the one who wrote everything down, and Aldous believed paper remembers what men forget." Bren laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He was right, too. That's the joke. I've carried the location in my head for ten years, through every bottle in the Saltcrust, and I told myself I'd forgotten it. Told myself a hundred times. You can't forget a thing like that. You can only refuse to say it."

The lantern hissed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

"And the stone?" Elodie asked. Her hand rested on the child's head, and Bren noticed that the girl had gone to sleep sitting up, the way soldiers and fugitives sleep, trusting the hand more than the ground. "Does it matter? What can one stone do?"

"It's half of everything," Bren said. "It's the anchor-half. The covenant between our realm and theirs—it isn't a wall, Elodie, it never was. It's a seam, and seams are sewn, and the stitching runs through that stone. If the girl carries a fragment of the sigil inside her, like you said—" He shook his head slowly. "Then she's thread, and the stone's the needle's eye. And the veil is tearing regardless, because nobody has tended that seam in ten years, and Corvane has been picking at it from his side the whole time, whether he knows it or not."

"You believe that," Tammas said. Not a question. An observation, almost wonderingly, as though he were seeing Bren clearly for the first time in a decade.

"I believe Aldous," Bren said. "Aldous believed it, and Aldous is dead, and the least I owe him is to stop pretending I don't remember what he told me the night before the guildhall burned." He looked down at his hands. They were shaking. He let them. "He said: *if it ever comes to it, the stone goes back into willing hands or it goes into the water forever.* Those were his words. Willing hands, Bren. He made me swear I'd know the difference."

Silence filled the chamber, thick as the dark.

"So we go beyond the walls," Elodie said at last. It wasn't a question either. It was a decision arriving fully formed, the way her decisions seemed to arrive, and Bren felt something in his chest that he refused to call hope because hope was a luxury for people who hadn't yet paid for anything. "Past the checkpoints. Past whatever hunters Corvane sends. To a flooded chapel full of standing water and ten years of secrets."

"To a flooded chapel," Bren agreed. "Which means going through the Veiled Market, because there's no other way out of this city tonight that doesn't end in irons."

Tammas groaned. "The smugglers."

"The smugglers," Bren confirmed. "Half-blooded Others stranded on this side when the crossings closed. They hate the guild for failing them. They hate Corvane more. Which means they hate us slightly less than they hate him, and in Vellharrow, that passes for friendship."

Above them, faint as memory, the wind shifted and brought the smell of smoke down through some distant grate. Tanner's Row, still burning. Maren's little joke of a safehouse, ash. Bren thought of the ledger in his chest, the one written in a dead man's voice, and of the sleeping child who was a thread looking for a needle's eye, and of the word *willing*, which kept turning over in his mind like a coin he couldn't quite read.

"We rest till the watch changes," he said, standing, joints cracking. "Then we move. And Elodie—" He hesitated, hating the words even as they left him. "Whatever's waiting for us at that chapel. Whatever Aldous hid down there besides the stone. You should know he warned me about that too. He said the stone remembers everyone who touches it. Said it gives back what they carry." His throat tightened. "I never asked him what he meant. I was afraid of the answer. I'm still afraid. But you're the one carrying the most, so if anyone's going to find out—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. In the lantern-light, Elodie Yarrow looked at him with soot on her face and a decade of silence behind her eyes, and nodded once, slow, like a woman accepting a debt she had always known was hers.

Far off in the dark of the tunnels, water dripped into water, counting down toward morning.
