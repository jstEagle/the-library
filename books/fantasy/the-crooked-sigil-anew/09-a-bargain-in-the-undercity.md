## 9. A Bargain in the Undercity

The tunnels beneath Vellharrow smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Tammas Ironhale hated every step of them.

He had spent ten years building a life above ground—straight-backed, sun-warmed, honest. A forge on Cooper's Lane. A name that meant nothing to the inspectors who walked past his door each morning, because Tammas the blacksmith was exactly as dull as he looked: horseshoes, hinges, fire-irons, nothing that whispered. He had buried his guild-sigil under the flagstone of his hearth and told himself, every day for a decade, that burying it had been wisdom rather than cowardice.

Now he was walking back into the dark with a mute child's hand wrapped around two of his fingers, and the dark was walking back into him.

*She trusts you,* Elodie had said when she'd pressed the girl's hand into his at the mouth of the tunnel behind Tanner's Row. *Of all of us, she trusts you.*

He hadn't asked why. He suspected it was because he was the only one of them who didn't look at her like she was a key, or a weapon, or a debt come due. Bren looked at her and saw the guild reborn. Elodie looked at her and saw her dead mentor's token walking around in a child's body. Tammas looked at her and saw a small person with soot on her cheek from crawling through the smoke of Tanner's Row, who liked the way hammer-strokes felt through the floor of a forge, who had fallen asleep against his anvil-stump two nights ago while the adults argued over maps.

That was all. It was enough.

"Stay close," he murmured, though she couldn't hear him in any way that mattered. She squeezed his fingers anyway, as if she'd understood the shape of the words if not their sound. Maybe she had. The girl understood more than any of them gave her credit for; Tammas had watched her watch people, cataloguing them the way he catalogued grain in a piece of oak—reading the rings, counting the years.

Behind them, Bren's lantern threw long shadows along the tunnel walls. The scribe moved badly these days, favoring a hip that the collapse at Tanner's Row had bruised blue-black, but he kept pace, and he kept talking, low and constant, the way he did when fear got its teeth into him.

"The Veiled Market's not what it was," Bren said. "Before the purge, they ran goods up the river and down the coast, no questions. Now they're stranded. Half-bloods, mostly—folk whose mothers or fathers came across before the veil closed, folk who can't go home and can't pass as human either. Corvane's inspectors leave them alone down here because it's cheaper than fighting them."

"And because he finds them useful," Tammas said.

"Everything's useful to Corvane. That's the trouble with the man." Bren ducked under a low arch of brick, and the lantern light slid across carvings in the old masonry—guild-marks, worn nearly smooth by water and time. Tammas's chest tightened at the sight of them. The Weaver's Guild had built half the undercity, back when the city still admitted what it was. These tunnels had been made to carry covenant-power, once. Now they carried sewage and smugglers, which Tammas supposed was at least an honest trade.

The child stopped.

She stood very still in the middle of the passage, head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear. Then she turned and looked at a seam in the wall where no door was, and raised one small hand, palm out, the way you'd greet someone across a table.

"What is it?" Elodie started forward, but Tammas caught her arm.

"Wait." He had seen the girl do this once before, at the forge—a pause, a listening, a greeting aimed at empty air—and an hour later the coals had burned hotter than his bellows could account for. "There are things down here that aren't smugglers. Old things. Things that were here before the veil and stayed after."

"You say that like it should comfort me."

"It shouldn't," Tammas said. "But it should steady you. They're not ours to fight, and they know we know it."

The seam in the wall breathed open—a line of darkness widening into a doorway that had not been there, framed in stone that had been somewhere else—and a figure stepped through.

Tammas had met half-bloods before the purge. Most of them looked almost human: a little too tall, a little too pale, eyes that caught lamplight like a cat's. This one was more than almost. Her skin held a faint grey-green undertone like bronze gone soft with verdigris, and her hair was the white of birch bark, braided close to her skull, and when she smiled—which she did immediately, warmly, showing too many teeth—it did not reach her eyes, which stayed flat and patient as deep water.

"Well," she said. Her voice was smoke and honey. "Three humans and a little sister, wandering my tunnels with a lantern and a guilty conscience. You must want something very badly."

"We want passage," Bren said. "Unseen. From here to the drowned chapel and back."

"Mm." The half-blood circled them slowly, and Tammas noted, with a craftsman's eye, that she made no sound at all—not on stone, not in water. "And you came to me because the streets above have eyes, and the eyes belong to a magistrate who has recently developed an interest in your little sister. Yes? Don't look surprised. News travels faster underground than above. There's a price on her head, and prices make the world honest." She stopped in front of the child and crouched, bringing her face level with the girl's. For a moment the market-smile fell away entirely, and what looked out of those flat eyes was something older and sadder and terribly tired. "Hello, little key," she said softly, in a language that was not Vellharrow's tongue.

Tammas didn't understand the words. But the child did. Her chin lifted, and she answered—one clear syllable, then another, the First Tongue rolling off her tongue like water over stones—and the half-blood went very still.

When she rose, her manner had changed. The warmth was gone. What remained was careful, and care, Tammas knew from long years at the forge, was what dangerous things wore instead of armor.

"My name is Ossra," she said. "I keep the Veiled Market. Come and bargain properly, or don't come at all. I don't deal in corridors."

* * *

The market lived in a cavern where three aqueducts met and died, a vaulted space the size of a chapel, lit by lamps that burned without flame—pale green light that made everyone look drowned. Stalls lined the walls in crooked tiers: a woman selling bottled breath, a man trading in keys that fit locks that no longer existed, a stall hung with coats that Tammas was fairly sure had been worn by people who no longer needed them. The vendors watched the four of them pass with the flat assessment of carrion birds, and the child pressed closer to Tammas's side, and he put a hand on her shoulder and kept walking, because that was what you did in places like this—you walked as though you belonged, or you didn't walk out again.

Ossra led them to a table of black wood set apart from the stalls, beneath a lamp of deeper green. Four stools. She sat last, folding herself down with the unhurried grace of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"Bren of the Weaver's Guild," she said. "Scribe, third circle, expelled—or rather, scattered—in the Year of Ash. Elodie Yarrow, charm-wife of Copperlane, whose shop I have watched for eleven years and whose trinkets I could tell you are not entirely without teeth, whatever she tells her customers." Elodie's face went carefully blank, and Ossra's smile flickered—the real one, briefly, the sad one. "Tammas Ironhale, who was Tammas Emberhand before he paid a cutler in Sallow Street to forget the name. And the little sister, who needs no introduction, since the whole city will have one soon enough."

"You've done your reading," Tammas said.

"I do nothing else." Ossra laced her fingers. "Passage to the drowned chapel. Unseen, unmolested, unremembered—my porters have a gift for being forgotten, it comes with the blood. And passage back, with whatever you find there." Her eyes moved to Bren. "You're looking for the second sigil-stone."

Bren didn't flinch, which cost him something; Tammas saw the effort of it in his jaw. "You know about the stone."

"I know about everything the guild buried, Master Scribe. My mother wove for the Otherworld court before the veil closed. Do you imagine we stranded half-bloods haven't spent ten years piecing together what your guild broke?" There was no anger in it, which somehow made it worse. "We felt it happen, you know. All of us. Like a bone setting wrong. One morning the other side of the sky simply wasn't there anymore, and those of us caught on this side were—" she spread her hands, taking in the cavern, the green lamps, the bottled breath, "—here."

"We didn't break it," Bren said quietly. "It broke. That's why the guild existed—to mend it. And the magistrate made sure we couldn't."

"Yes. Corvane." Ossra said the name the way a surgeon names a tumor. "I've heard your theories. Everyone down here has heard your theories; you've been loud since Tanner's Row burned. So let us skip to the part that interests me." She leaned forward, and the green light pooled in the hollows of her face. "What do you offer?"

Tammas had brought coin. Of course he had—he'd emptied the strongbox under his forge floor, every saved crown of ten careful years, sewn into his coat lining. But watching Ossra's face, he knew coin was a courtesy here, a way of saying *we respect the forms*, and nothing more. Markets like this one traded in weightier stock.

Bren offered the guild's surviving records—Ossra declined them with a small shake of her head. "Paper burns. I've watched a great deal burn lately." Elodie offered the charms she'd begun making again, the ones with teeth, and Ossra examined one with genuine interest before setting it down. "Lovely. Not enough."

Then the child moved.

She climbed onto the stool beside Tammas, straightened, and looked at Ossra across the black table, and spoke. Three syllables of the First Tongue, delivered with the grave certainty of a magistrate pronouncing sentence. Tammas understood none of it, but he felt it pass through the cavern like a change in pressure—lamps guttering, vendors going still, the water in the dead aqueducts shivering though there was no wind.

Ossra had gone white. Grey-green skin doesn't show pallor well, but Tammas would have bet his forge she'd gone white underneath.

"That," Ossra said at last, very evenly, "was not a bargaining chip. That was a promise. Or a threat. With her I honestly cannot tell the difference, and neither, I suspect, can she." She looked at the child for a long moment, and something passed across her face that Tammas recognized, because he'd seen it in his own mirror for ten years: the look of someone meeting a younger version of a grief they'd never finished mourning. "Very well. Passage, both ways, unseen. My price is this: a favor, owed to the Veiled Market, unnamed, uncapped, redeemable at a time of my choosing within one year. Sworn by one of you—not the child, not the scribe. The smith or the charm-wife."

"No," Elodie said immediately.

"Elodie—" Bren began.

"They'll ask her to do something terrible. That's how these bargains work. Unnamed means unwritten means unlimited."

"It means necessary," Ossra said. "Charm-wife, look around you. Every soul in this cavern is stranded between worlds, blamed by one and forgotten by the other, living on smuggled air under a city that pretends we're rats. You want our silence, our porters, our tunnels. You want us to spend the goodwill of ten careful years on your war with a magistrate who has never once thought about us except as vermin with useful hands. And you want to pay in coin." She laughed, short and sharp. "Coin. When what we have wanted, for ten years, is to matter to someone above ground. To be owed to. To hold, just once, a claim on the world that shut its door in our faces."

The silence stretched. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

Tammas looked at the child. She was watching him—not Ossra, not the bargain, him—with those grave dark eyes, and he realized she had put her hand on his forearm, small fingers resting on the burn-scarred skin below his sleeve, and that she had done it deliberately, and that she was waiting.

*A favor, unnamed.* His whole body recoiled from it. Ten years of careful dullness screamed against it. He thought of his forge going cold, of his name on some ledger in this green-lit dark, called in on some future morning for a thing he couldn't yet imagine. He thought of the flagstone under his hearth, and the sigil buried beneath it, and the decade of mornings he'd told himself that survival was the same thing as virtue.

He thought of Tanner's Row burning, and of how he'd run toward the smoke this time instead of away from it, and of how much lighter he'd felt running toward.

"Aye," Tammas said. "One favor. Owed by Tammas Ironhale—Emberhand, if you like, since we're being honest tonight. Within the year. Named by you when you call it." He pulled off his glove and laid his bare hand flat on the black wood. "Sworn on my craft. Break it, and may every hinge I ever hung stick, every shoe I ever set throw, every fire I ever tend go cold."

Ossra studied his hand. Then she placed hers over it—cool, dry, lighter than a human hand should be—and spoke a word in her mother's tongue that made the green lamps bend toward them like grass in wind.

"Done," she said. "And smith—I'll tell you this for free, since you've just made yourself the most interesting man in my cavern. The chapel you're sailing to isn't as drowned as your guild believed. The water went down two winters ago. Something opened the way, and something else has been keeping it." Her flat eyes flicked to the child. "Ask yourselves who's been standing guard over your guild's secrets while you all hid above ground pretending to be furniture."

She rose, the bargain sealed, the audience ended. As they gathered themselves to follow her toward the flooded galleries where her porters waited, she paused beside Tammas and lowered her voice to something only he could hear.

"When I call in my favor, smith—and I will—I suspect you'll be glad of it. The stranded remember their debts better than the free do. It's all we have." A ghost of the true smile. "Mind the third gallery. And mind the warden. He's already in the city. He arrived the night your little sister did, and he's been asking after her in a voice like winter."

Tammas's blood went cold. "A warden. From the other side?"

"From the other side," Ossra confirmed, "come to collect what the broken covenant left lying loose in our world. Sleep fast, smith. You sail at slack tide."
