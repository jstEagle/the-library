## 10. Hunters of Both Realms

The safehouse on Tanner's Row had a smell Bren never bothered to explain and Elodie had stopped asking about—tallow smoke, river rot, and the faint copper tang of old ink soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of scrubbing could draw it out. It was, she supposed, the smell of the guild itself: something written into the bones of a place that refused to be erased.

Three days they had been there, three days of hushed voices and shuttered windows, and the child had begun to fill the silence in ways Elodie both cherished and feared. She drew now constantly—on scraps of sacking, on the whitewashed plaster, once, memorably, on Tammas's forearm while he slept off a night shift at his forge. Her charcoal figures were strange and lovely: towers that grew downward like roots, rivers that flowed uphill, a great tree whose branches were made of doors. And always, always, at the center of every drawing, a small figure with no face, holding a piece of light.

"That's her," Bren said one evening, nodding at the faceless child as he thinned his beer with river water he pretended was wine. "That's how the Others see us, maybe. Faces are for folk who stay put. She's a door-walker. Doors don't have faces."

Elodie didn't answer. She was watching the girl instead—the way her small hands moved over the drawing, sure and quick, the way her eyes flicked up whenever footsteps passed outside, cataloguing them, sorting them: cart-wheel, boot-heel, inspector's measured tread. The child had survived something enormous, whatever it was, and she had survived it by learning to listen the way animals listen.

Which was why, when the girl went suddenly still in the middle of her drawing, charcoal hovering an inch above the sacking, Elodie felt the fear arrive before any reason for it did.

"What is it?" she whispered.

The girl set down the charcoal with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing that might wake. Then she pointed—at the floor first, then the walls, then up through the ceiling toward the street—and made a sound Elodie had never heard from her: a thin, high note, like a struck wire, held and trembling.

Bren set down his cup. Tammas, who had been oiling the hinges of the back hatch, rose without a word and put his eye to the knothole.

"Two of them," he breathed. "Grey coats. Not inspectors' livery—no badges." A pause. "They're not walking like patrols. They're walking like men following a thread."

*Hunters.* The word arrived in Elodie's mind fully formed, cold as a coin left out overnight. Corvane's reward notice had gone up five days ago—a girl of strange speech, a purse heavy enough to buy a house—and money in Vellharrow pulled men out of the woodwork the way rain pulled worms. But grey coats, unbadged, moving like that... those were not opportunists. Those were professionals. Corvane kept a handful of such men, though officially they did not exist: quiet ones, patient ones, who found things because they believed finding was a sacrament.

"We go now," Elodie said. "Out the back, into the tunnels. Bren, you know the way to the drowned chapel quarter better than anyone—"

"I know it drunk," Bren said grimly, already sweeping the child's drawings into the stove. "Sober I know it better."

But even as they moved—even as Tammas lifted the hatch and the smell of the undercity rose up to meet them, wet stone and old salt—the girl caught Elodie's sleeve. She pointed not at the hatch but at the front wall. At the street. Then she pressed her palm flat against her own chest, and her eyes were wide and grave and full of a knowledge Elodie hated her for having.

She wasn't pointing away from danger. She was saying: *it is already here.*

* * *

They came through the roof.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Elodie could only hold fragments: the crackle of the stove fire bending sideways as if a window had opened where there was no window; Bren shouting; Tammas's huge hand shoving her down behind the workbench; and then a light that was not lamplight—not firelight, nothing with heat in it at all—filling the room like water filling a bowl, colorless and total.

When it drained away, a man stood in the middle of the safehouse.

He was tall in a way that seemed less like height than like *attention*, as if he had simply decided to occupy more space than other men did. His coat was the grey of fog over deep water, and it moved wrong—settling after he stopped, the way smoke settles. His face was long, pale, handsome in the manner of a winter branch: beautiful, bare, and indifferent to being looked at. His eyes were the worst part. They were the color of the sky before snow, and they held the particular patience of someone for whom time ran differently.

An Other. Not half-blooded, not stranded, not smuggled through some crack in the veil. A true-born Other, standing in a Vellharrow safehouse, and the veil was supposed to be sealed shut.

"Serevan," the girl said.

Not spoke—she couldn't speak—but the name came out of her anyway, shaped in the First Tongue, and Elodie felt it pass through the room like a current through wire. The warden's gaze dropped to the child, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind that wintry patience—something that looked almost like grief.

"You know my name," Serevan said. His voice was low, resonant, accented in a way Elodie's ear couldn't place—every vowel slightly too pure, as if human speech were a garment he wore reluctantly. He switched to the First Tongue then, and though Elodie understood almost none of it, she understood its music, and she heard the command in it, gentle as a lullaby and absolute as a lock turning: *come here, little one; you belong with your own kind; come home.*

The girl took one step.

"No." Elodie heard her own voice crack across the room, raw and absurd, a shopkeeper's voice raised against a creature out of the old stories. She stepped between them, arms out, feeling exactly as brave and as doomed as a hen spreading wings against a hawk. "No, she doesn't belong anywhere she doesn't choose. She's a child. She's *frightened*. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"Warden," Serevan said, to her rather than about himself, as if correcting a mislabeling. "I am warden of the Ninth March. And you are—" his eyes moved over her, and she felt them like cold fingers, reading her the way she read hallmarks on silver, "—marked. Guild-trained, guild-sworn, guild-abandoned. Your sigil-marks are dead ash under your skin, weaver. You cannot stop me. Do not spend yourself trying."

Tammas came around the workbench with a forging hammer in his fist, and Bren had a knife out that Elodie would have sworn he didn't own a minute ago, and none of it mattered even slightly. Serevan regarded them the way a man regards weather.

"The veil is dying," the warden said, and now the grief was unmistakable, laid open like a wound he'd stopped bothering to dress. "Your side feels it as bad dreams, blighted crops, charms that don't take. Mine feels it as *dying*. Whole marches going dark. Rivers forgetting their names. I crossed what remains of the boundary to find this child because she carries the covenant's fragment, and if she is returned—if the fragment is returned—what is broken can begin to mend." He extended one long hand toward the girl, palm up. An offering. An order. "Come, little key. Your people wait."

And Elodie saw it—the terrible, seductive rightness of him. Because hadn't she said the same thing, in her own heart? That the child was the key, that the covenant could be reforged, that returning what was lost might heal everything?

The girl took another step.

Then she stopped. She looked at Serevan's open hand, and then past it, at Elodie—at Elodie's spread arms, her shaking knees, her ridiculous undefended human body placed between the child and the warden—and something in the girl's face changed. She stepped *back*. She reached out and gripped two of Elodie's fingers in her small hand, and shook her head, once, firmly, in the manner of a person refusing a second helping.

Serevan's hand closed slowly into a fist. "You do not understand what you refuse," he said softly.

"She understands kindness," Elodie said. "Maybe that's more than you do."

What happened next happened fast. Later she would understand that Serevan had never intended violence—that he had, in his alien, patient fashion, intended to let them exhaust themselves arguing and then simply *take* the child, the tide takes a sandcastle. But Tammas swung the hammer, whether in courage or panic Elodie never learned, and the world bent. There was a sound like a bell underwater. The safehouse walls rippled. And when Elodie's vision cleared, the room was empty of everything except the four of them, the scattered drawings, and a single object lying on the floorboards where the warden had stood:

A token. Old pewter, worn smooth, stamped with the interlaced sigil of the Weaver's Guild.

Her mentor's token. The one the child had carried through the veil. The one Elodie had kept in her pocket for ten years like a splinter she couldn't dig out.

Serevan had taken it. Or—or he had left it. In that moment, crouching to pick it up, Elodie couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

"He knows what it is," Bren said hoarsely, staring at her empty hand, at the absence of the thing that had weighed on her for a decade. "He looked at it. He *chose* to leave it."

"Why?" Tammas asked. "Why leave bait with the fish?"

No one answered. Outside, faintly, came the sound of boots—many boots, disciplined boots, moving along Tanner's Row with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly which door they wanted. The grey coats. Corvane's quiet professionals. Drawn, perhaps, by the same wrongness in the air that had marked the warden's crossing.

"Chapel," Elodie said. "Now. Everyone. *Now.*"

* * *

They went down through the undercity with the child strapped to Tammas's broad back, wrapped in sacking like contraband, which in a sense she was. Bren led, sober and sharp-eyed for perhaps the first time in years, calling turns in a low voice: left at the broken sluice-gate, down the ladder with the seventh rung missing, through the crawl where the ceiling kissed your scalp and the dark had weight. Behind them, muffled by stone and distance, came sounds Elodie tried not to translate: the splintering of the safehouse door, shouts, the dull bloom of fire.

They burned it, then. Everything. Every drawing, every scrap of the girl's presence, and probably half of Tanner's Row besides, because Corvane's hunters were thorough men and thorough men preferred ashes to questions.

In a dry pocket beneath an old cistern, they stopped to breathe. Tammas passed around a skin of water. The child drank, wiped her mouth, and then tugged Elodie's sleeve and pointed at her pocket—where the token was not—and made a small, distressed sound.

"It's all right," Elodie said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "It's all right that it's gone."

The girl frowned at her, unconvinced, and drew in the dust with one finger: a circle, a line through it, and then two small figures on either end of the line, pulling.

"A bargain," Elodie translated slowly. "A trade." She looked at the drawing a long moment. "You think he wants something for it. The token for—you?"

The girl nodded. Then, with terrible clarity, she drew a third figure beside the two: taller, robed in scribbled lines, standing apart, watching. And beneath it she scratched the shape Elodie had seen in her drawings since the first night—the fragment of the covenant-sigil, the shard of light she carried inside her.

*Serevan will trade the token back,* Elodie thought, *for the child herself. Or for a piece of her. Or for a promise. And he'll call it fair, because to him it is fair—it's just arithmetic, a key for a key.*

"Bren." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "This chapel of yours. The sigil-stone. How far?"

"Half a night's walk through the tunnels, if the floods haven't shifted. A day if they have."

"And when we reach it—when we touch it—will it be enough? Against him? Against Corvane?" She paused. "Against *both* of them?"

Bren was quiet for a moment, turning his cupless hands around each other, an old habit from drinking days. "I don't know what's enough," he said finally. "I know what our masters used to say, back before. They said the guild never won anything. It only *paid*." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and clear. "Everything worth keeping cost somebody something, Elodie. The covenant most of all. We just weren't told what the price was until the bill came due."

Above them, through fathoms of stone and earth, the city went on with its evening: bells ringing vespers in churches that preached against magic, inspectors stamping their rounds, ordinary people lighting lamps against the dark. And somewhere in all that ordinary noise, two hunters were converging on a mute child—one who served a magistrate who wanted to own the covenant, and one who served a dying realm that wanted to spend her like coin.

Elodie put her hand flat against the child's back and felt her breathing, small and stubborn as a bird's heartbeat.

Ten years she had paid nothing. Ten years of hollow trinkets and averted eyes, of safety bought with silence. Well. The bill had come due at last, and it had a name, and it was sleeping against Tammas's shoulder with charcoal smudged on its cheek.

"Hold on to me," she whispered to the girl, in words the child couldn't understand but somehow did. "Whatever it costs. Hold on."

Then she shouldered her pack, nodded to Bren, and walked with them into the dark, toward the drowned chapel and the price waiting there—while somewhere above, unseen, patient as frost, the warden of the Ninth March turned her mentor's token over in his long fingers and began, quietly, to plan the trade.
