## 11. Serevan's Grief

The warden's tent did not belong to this world, and it knew it.

Serevan had pitched it in the hollow beneath the Widow's Teeth, where the hills of Vellharrow's outskirts folded into shadow and the grass grew gray at the root. The canvas was woven from dusk-moth silk, and it drank the human daylight rather than reflecting it, so that from the outside the tent looked like a hole cut in the world—a place where the afternoon simply stopped. Inside, the light was the light of home: amber and slow, the color of a sun that took its time crossing the sky because it had never once been afraid of night.

He sat cross-legged on the ground-cloth with the child's ribbon across his palms and tried to remember why he had come.

Not why in the way the Council had told him. That part was simple, and simple things held their shape even here, even ten years deep into exile. The covenant was broken. The veil was tearing. His realm was dying by inches—by seasons, now—and the child was the key that had been carried out through the wound, and a key belonged to its lock. He had been given a duty as clean as a drawn blade. Bring her back. Set her in the cradle-stone at the covenant's heart. Let the old words close around her like water closing over a stone dropped into a well.

That was the why he carried in his satchel, written in the First Tongue on vellum that smelled of his mother's house.

But there was another why, the one that lived behind his sternum, and it would not hold still. It shifted every time he looked at the ribbon.

* * *

It had been hers. The child's. He had taken it from her wrist in the orchard-ruins outside Vellharrow, three nights ago, when his hunters had run her to ground in the crooked rows of dead apple trees. She had not screamed. That was the thing he kept returning to, the splinter in his mind he could not work loose. She had not screamed, had not wept, had not begged. She had looked up at him—at him, a warden of the Third Vigil, tall as two men and armored in bark-lacquer and bone—with an expression he had last seen on the faces of the elders in the Hall of Seasons, the year they voted to let the outer provinces go dark rather than spend the reserves to save them.

She had looked at him the way one looks at a thing that has already decided not to understand.

He had bound her wrists, and the ribbon had slipped free, and she had watched it fall into the mud between them without reaching for it. Then Elodie Yarrow had come out of the dark with a dead man's token in her fist and a look on her face that Serevan had no name for in either tongue, and he had traded the child for the token because the token was evidence, because the Council required evidence, because procedure was the last wall standing between himself and the animal fear that had ridden him since the day the sky over home first cracked.

He had told himself it was a fair trade. A relic for a hostage. Order preserved.

He had told himself many things since the covenant broke.

* * *

The tent flap moved, and Vess slid inside.

His second was young—young for the Vigil, which meant barely older than the child—and she carried the exhaustion of this world in her shoulders the way the others carried it in their eyes. This realm's air was thin with magic. Not empty, never empty; he could feel the old covenant humming under the stones of Vellharrow like a struck bell that refused to finish fading. But thin. Thin enough that every hour they spent here, their own reserves bled away into soil that had forgotten how to give anything back.

"The scouts are back," Vess said. She knelt across from him, formal, her hands flat on her thighs. "The woman has gone underground. Literally. There are tunnels beneath the city—old smuggler ways, older guild ways. Our sight doesn't reach down there."

"Then we wait for her to surface."

"She may not surface." Vess hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Serevan had trained her himself, and he heard the weight in it before she spoke. "Warden. There is something else. From the listening posts. The human magistrate—he is moving against them as well. Hunters in black lacquer, paid in coin that smells of stolen power. They nearly had the tunnels tonight. If they take the child first—"

"They will not take her first."

"You cannot know that."

"I know," Serevan said quietly, "that if Corvane's people get their hands on her, she will not be returned anywhere. She will be opened." He had seen the magistrate's conduits once, from a distance, on a night reconnaissance along the river: iron throats sunk into the earth, drinking from the severed veins of the covenant, funneling what leaked through the broken veil into whatever vault Corvane kept beneath his spire. The humans called it piety, outlawing the guilds. Serevan had read enough of their ledgers—stolen, copied, memorized—to know it was farming. They had slaughtered the mediators and then built machines to milk the corpse of the agreement the mediators had tended.

Vess bowed her head. "Then we move faster. The Council's timetable—"

"The Council," Serevan said, "is nine hundred leagues away, sitting in a hall where the moss still grows green, telling itself that one child will fix everything." He heard his own voice sharpen and made himself stop. Made himself breathe. In the amber light, Vess watched him with the careful blankness of a soldier deciding how much of her commander she was permitted to see.

"I have served the Vigil for sixty years," he said, softer now. "I have carried out every order I was given. Do you know what I have learned about orders?"

"That they keep us whole when the work would break us."

"No." He turned the ribbon over in his hands. It was cheap cloth, human-made, dyed the red of rowan berries. Someone in Vellharrow had tied it around a child's wrist. Perhaps the charm-woman. Perhaps someone before her. "Orders are what we use instead of thinking, when thinking would cost too much. And sometimes—" the ribbon's frayed edge caught the light, "—sometimes the cost is the point. Sometimes we are meant to pay it."

* * *

He slept, and the dreams came, and they were not kind.

In the dream he stood in the Hall of Seasons again, but the hall was as it was now—not the warm memory of it but the truth: half the lanterns cold, the great table scarred where panicking clerks had gouged it for splinters of covenant-oak to sell, the moss on the eastern wall gone brown and brittle as old paper. The elders sat in their crescent and did not look at the wall. They looked at maps. On the maps, the dying places were marked in ash-gray, and the ash-gray had been creeping inward for ten years like frost across a window, and every season the elders drew the border of what could be saved a little smaller and called it stewardship.

*We cannot save everything,* the eldest had said, the day they gave Serevan his commission. *But the covenant can be renewed. The key was carried through. Find the key, Warden. Return the key. The rest is arithmetic.*

Arithmetic. As though the child were a number. As though setting her into the cradle-stone were not—

He did not know what it was. That was the rot at the center of his certainty, the thing he circled night after night and could not land on. The renewal rites were old. Older than the Vigil, older than the Council, sealed in the First Tongue in archives only three living wardens could read. He had read them. He had told himself he understood them. But understanding the words and knowing what they meant were different countries, and somewhere between them, the child's face kept appearing—the orchard-ruins, the mud, the terrible patience of her eyes—and asking him a question he could not answer.

*Do you know what you are carrying me back to?*

* * *

Dawn in the human world was a gray, apologetic thing. Serevan stood outside the tent while Vess struck it, watching the light come up over Vellharrow's rooftops like a bruise fading.

The city was uglier than his realm's cities and more alive. That was the paradox he had never resolved in ten years of watching it. Home was beautiful and dying. Here was smoke-grimed, crooked, loud—and everywhere he looked, something stubborn was growing. Weeds through cobbles. Laundry strung between leaning houses. Children running messages through alleys that smelled of tallow and rain. The humans had lost their magic and their guilds and their history, all in one bloody decade, and they had gotten up the next morning and sold fish.

And one of them—a charm-seller with hollow trinkets and a decade of silence in her eyes—had walked into an orchard full of warden steel to buy back a child she had known for days.

He thought of the token she had offered. Her mentor's. He had examined it afterward by moth-light: guild silver, the Weaver's knotwork, worn smooth by a thumb that must have rubbed it for years. The man who had owned it was dead—killed in the purges, or broken by them; the records disagreed. But the woman had carried his token like a coal she was keeping alive, and when the moment came she had given it away without hesitation, because the child mattered more than the remembering.

What did the Council offer? A cradle-stone and arithmetic.

"Warden." Vess stood beside him, the tent struck, the camp already nothing. "The trackers have the tunnel mouths marked. Six entrances. We can net them all by nightfall."

"And when we do?" He said it to the city more than to her. "When we take her back—truly take her, past the veil, into the cradle-chamber—do you know what the rite requires? Have you read the archive-seals, Vess? Truly read them, in the First Tongue, with the commentaries?"

Her silence was answer enough.

"Neither have most of the Council," Serevan said. "They read summaries. Summaries prepared by clerks who read other summaries. Somewhere at the bottom of that chain of polite reductions is a truth, and I do not believe anyone holding power wants to stand next to it." He flexed his hand, feeling the ache of this thin-aired world settle into his knuckles. "The humans hid their truth in a meetinghouse wall. Magistrate Corvane hides his in a ledger. And we—we hide ours in language so old that no one alive remembers it plainly. Every realm buries the price and calls the burial wisdom."

"Then what do we do?" Vess asked, and there it was at last: not the soldier's blankness but the young woman underneath, frightened, far from home, asking her commander a question he had no commission to answer.

Serevan looked at the ribbon coiled in his palm. He should have destroyed it. Evidence, procedure, the clean blade of duty—it belonged in the report-satchel with everything else. Instead he tucked it inside his breastplate, against the skin, where he could feel it with every breath.

"We find the truth before we finish the task," he said. "All of it. Unreduced. If the Council's arithmetic requires a child, I want to see the sum worked in full, in my own reading, before I carry her one step toward that stone. And if the humans' truth or our truth damns the rite—" he met Vess's eyes, "—then I will decide whose side I am on, and it will not be a side. It will be hers."

Vess stared at him. Somewhere across the fields, a church bell rang the hour, indifferent and human, and the sound went through Serevan like a reminder: this world ran on bells and bread and stubbornness, and it had survived the breaking of heaven without asking his realm's permission.

"You understand," Vess said slowly, "that if the Council learns you've said this—"

"The Council," Serevan said, "taught me that duty means carrying the weight. They neglected to mention that some weights are children." He pulled his cloak up against the morning damp. "Come. One of those tunnel mouths opens behind a tannery on the river road. If the charm-woman surfaces anywhere, it will be where the stink hides her. And Vess—if we find her first, we do not take the child. We talk. For once in this miserable decade, we talk."

He set off toward the river with the ribbon warm against his chest and doubt sitting where his certainty used to live—and it was only later, much later, that he would learn how narrow the margin was, and how close behind them Corvane's black-lacquered hunters already rode.
