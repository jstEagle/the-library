## 13. Betrayal at the Veiled Market

The Veiled Market smelled of wet copper and secrets.

Tammas Vane had spent eleven years hammering iron in a forge on Cindermaker's Lane, and he knew metal the way a priest knew scripture—its tempers, its lies, its breaking points. The smuggler-tunnels beneath Vellharrow were held up by iron props older than the guild purge, rusted to the color of dried blood, and every one of them sang a warning to him as they passed: *I am tired. I am tired. Do not trust me.*

He kept his hand on the nearest prop as the others filed past. Bren went first, hood up, shoulders hunched around his guilt like a man carrying a sack of wet sand. Behind him came Serevan, the Otherworld warden, moving through the dark as though darkness were a courtesy paid to him personally rather than a condition of the place. Then Elodie, one hand resting on the child's shoulder, guiding her through the muck. And last, Tammas himself, because someone had to be last, and Tammas had been last for eleven years—the man who closed the shop, banked the fire, watched the road behind them.

"Keep close," murmured Mother Quill, their guide—a half-blooded Other woman with moth-grey skin and eyes like coins left too long in vinegar. She led them down a stair that had not known daylight since before the purge, into the market proper.

It was not a market in any way the magistrates would recognize. There were no stalls, no cries of vendors. Instead, the cavern opened into a warren of hanging lanterns—green glass, each one burning with a light that gave warmth without heat—and between them, veils of dyed silk hung from the ceiling like curtains of rain made solid. Behind each veil, something was sold. Behind one, Tammas glimpsed bottled voices, stoppered in clay jars, murmuring to themselves. Behind another, a man with too many joints was fitting a customer for gloves made of shadow.

The child stopped walking. Her small hand found Tammas's sleeve and gripped it.

"I know," he said quietly. "It's a lot."

She looked up at him. She had learned perhaps forty human words by now, doled out like precious coins, and she spent none of them here. Instead she signed—quick, fluid gestures she'd taught them over the weeks of flight—and what she said was: *The walls are listening.*

Tammas felt the truth of it in his teeth. The whole cavern hummed faintly, a note below hearing, the sound of covenant-power pooled and stagnant, like water behind a dam. Half-blooded Others, stranded when the veil had sealed, living off the leak of it. He understood, in an abstract way, why they'd demanded a favor owed, unnamed, in exchange for passage. Desperate people always wanted hooks in you.

"Mother Quill," Elodie called softly. "The tunnel to the chapel district. You promised us safe passage."

"And you promised payment." Mother Quill's voice was dry as old paper. "A favor, unnamed, owed to the Market. Signed in salt and smoke." She extended a hand whose fingers ended in fine grey filaments, like the legs of dead moths. "The bargain holds. For now."

*For now.* Tammas didn't like the words. He liked even less the way several of the silk veils had gone still—no customers behind them, no sellers, just shapes standing motionless, watching.

They signed. Elodie pressed her palm to the salt-and-smoke sigil Mother Quill drew on the air, and it sank into her skin with a sound like a sigh. The child flinched as though she'd heard something painful.

Then the veils parted, and they were shown the way down.

* * *

The tunnel to the chapel district ran for half a mile beneath the old tannery rows, and it was there, in the dark and the dripping, that Tammas began to count things that did not add up.

First: the lanterns. Every fifty paces, green-glass lanterns marked the route—but after the third one, the light changed. Warmer. Yellower. Human lamplight, oil-fed, not the cold verdant glow of the Market.

Second: the echoes. Their party of five should have made five sets of footsteps. Tammas counted seven. Two extra, soft-soled, keeping perfect time with theirs.

Third—and this was the one that turned his stomach to slag—was the smell. Underneath the wet copper and the river-stink of the tunnels, there was a whiff of something Tammas knew intimately from his forge days: pitch and hemp rope. Fire-starters' kit.

"Bren," he said quietly. "Hold up."

Bren stopped. Ahead, Elodie turned, the child pressed against her side. Serevan's head tilted, listening to whatever Otherworld senses he carried, and Tammas watched the warden's expression go from distant to grim.

"You smell it too," Tammas said.

"The debt has been sold," Serevan said. His voice carried no accusation, only the flatness of a man reporting weather. "I told you this might happen. Corvane does not need to find the Market. The Market finds him. Debts are currency here, and your magistrate is rich enough to buy anything except forgiveness."

"When?" Elodie's voice cracked on the word.

"Soon," Serevan said. "Or now. With debts, there is little difference."

The two extra sets of footsteps stopped pretending to be echoes.

Lanterns bloomed along the tunnel walls—not green but yellow, dozens of them, unfolding out of alcoves Tammas had sworn were empty. Figures stepped into the light wearing veils across their faces, silk the color of bruises, and in their hands were weapons that hurt to look at: blades of black glass, cords of woven shadow, a net strung with bells that made no sound but that Tammas could feel ringing in his back teeth.

At their center stood Mother Quill.

Her moth-grey face was composed, almost apologetic. "The magistrate's men came at dusk," she said. "They did not threaten. They simply produced our ledger—all our debts, all our names, everything we owe to creditors in both realms—and set it on the table beside a purse. Then they said, *sell us the debt of the charm-witch and her companions, or watch it bought by someone who will collect less gently.*" She spread her filament-fingered hands. "We are smugglers, smith. Not martyrs. There is a difference, and it is the difference between being alive tomorrow and not."

"There's always a difference," Tammas said. "That's how they get you. One difference at a time."

"Smith." Something flickered in her vinegared eyes—regret, maybe, or its counterfeit. "Walk to the magistrate's men and no one bleeds. The witch, the scribe, the warden, the child. That is the sale. You are not included in it, if that eases anything. They have no interest in a blacksmith."

"They have an interest in everyone," Tammas said. "That's what a magistrate *is*."

Behind him, he heard Bren draw breath to argue—to plead, probably; Bren pleaded beautifully when he was sober and catastrophically when he wasn't—and Tammas had already done the arithmetic. It was the same arithmetic he'd been doing his whole life, in the forge, in the guild, in the decade of silence: *what can I hold, and for how long?*

An anvil doesn't argue with the hammer. It just stands where it's put and takes the blow so the work can be shaped.

"Elodie," he said, without turning around. "How far to the junction?"

"Two hundred paces," she whispered. "Maybe less. The old cistern channel—it opens into the chapel district cellars."

"And beyond the junction?"

"Collapse points. Bren marked them. The ceilings are rotten limestone. One good—" She stopped. He heard her stop, heard the understanding arrive in her voice like a dropped tool.

"One good strike," Tammas finished. "Yeah."

"No," Elodie said.

"It's a big tunnel," Tammas said. "And they've got bell-nets and shadow-cords, and I've got a forging hammer and eleven years of anger. Somebody has to be the anvil, Ellie. It was always going to be me. I'm the one built for it."

"You are not dying in a tunnel under Tanner's Row—"

"Wasn't planning on it." He rolled his shoulders, and felt the old guild-marks under his shirt warm faintly, the charm-worked scars from another life, another self. "I'm planning on being *loud*. Loud enough that nobody follows you. Loud enough that the roof comes down on the wrong people." He finally turned and grinned at her, the grin he used for customers who balked at prices. "Get the girl to the chapel. Get the stone. Finish it. And when you write the histories—which Bren will insist on doing, badly—give me a good death scene. Make me tall."

"Tammas—"

But the smugglers were already moving, and there was no more time for anything except the work.

* * *

Later—much later, in a cell that smelled of straw and other people's despair—Tammas would try to reconstruct those four minutes and fail. Memory, he decided, was a poor forge: it lost heat at exactly the moments you needed it most. What remained were fragments, struck off like sparks.

The fragment of Serevan moving first—not toward escape but *through* the smugglers, the warden's shadow-cord whipping out to tear a bell-net from the air, buying three seconds of chaos with pure contempt.

The fragment of Bren screaming something—his name, probably, or a curse, or both—and Elodie hauling the child backward toward the junction while the yellow lanterns swayed and the black-glass blades caught the light like fish turning in dark water.

The fragment of Mother Quill, standing apart from her own people, watching him with those coin-dead eyes, and saying—one smuggler to one honest man—"You could still walk away, smith."

"No," Tammas had said. "That's the whole point of me."

And then the main fragment, the one that mattered: Tammas Vane walking forward into the lantern-light with his forging hammer coming off his shoulder, and the first smuggler learning what eleven years at the anvil does to a man's arms.

He fought the way he worked. No flourish, no wasted motion. The hammer took the first blade aside in a shower of black glass; his boot took the second smuggler in the knee, and the crack of it was honest and clean. A shadow-cord wrapped his forearm and burned like a brand, and he roared—not pain, though there was pain, but volume, deliberate, glorious volume—because every eye on him was an eye off Elodie and the child, and he meant to spend every ounce of their attention like a miser spending his last coin.

"COME ON, THEN!" he bellowed, backing toward the junction, hammer swinging, keeping them bunched and cautious in front of him. "TEN YEARS I MADE CHARMS THAT DIDN'T WORK FOR PEOPLE WHO DIDN'T BELIEVE! YOU THINK I'M AFRAID OF A FEW CURTAIN-SALES MEN WITH FANCY KNIVES?"

Someone threw a bell-net. It settled over him and the silence of its ringing went through his skull like ice water, and he tore it off with a sound of rending silk and kept swinging.

Ahead—at the edge of his vision, in the mouth of the junction—he saw them go. Bren first, dragging the child. Elodie last, turning back once, her face a white smudge of grief and fury in the gloom. He met her eyes and nodded, one sharp jerk of his chin, the same nod he'd given her across the guildhall floor ten years ago when the inspectors came and they had all agreed, silently, to live.

*Go,* the nod said. *I've got the work.*

Then he planted his feet at the junction, where the ceiling sagged lowest, where Bren's chalk marks still ghosted the rotten limestone overhead, and he did the only thing an anvil can do.

He stood.

They came at him in earnest then—six, eight, a dozen, the veils torn from their faces, and behind them Mother Quill shouting orders that had the flat finality of a ledger being balanced. A black-glass knife opened his cheek. A cord took his legs and he went down hard, hammer skidding, and a boot found his ribs, and another, and through the red haze he saw a wiry smuggler scrambling up the tunnel wall toward the chalk-marked support with a pry-bar in his hands—they meant to bring the roof down on *him*, seal the junction, trap—

No. That was his job.

Tammas got one hand on his hammer. Got a knee under himself. And with the last full measure of everything his body had ever earned at the forge, he swung upward—not at the smugglers, but at the prop above him, the rusted iron strut singing its tired song, the one weak point in a ceiling already begging to fall.

Iron rang like a bell. Limestone answered.

The junction came down in a thunderclap of dust and white stone, and Tammas Vane, on his knees with his arms over his head, felt the world end above him and was, briefly, gratefully, unconscious of everything.

* * *

He woke to hands.

Rough ones, efficient, binding his wrists with shadow-cord that bit like frostbite. His ears rang with the after-echo of the collapse; his mouth tasted of powdered stone; somewhere very far away, water dripped with idiotic patience. When he could focus, he saw lanterns again—yellow ones, human ones—and beyond them, framed in the broken mouth of the sealed junction, a wall of rubble where the tunnel had been.

No pursuit through that. Not quickly. Maybe not at all.

"Breathing," said a voice above him, disappointed. "The magistrate wants them breathing."

Tammas laughed. It cost him two cracked ribs to do it and he paid gladly. Because they'd wanted him breathing too—that was the joke, the beautiful, stupid joke of it. He'd braced himself to die holding the tunnel, and instead he'd held it *alive*, and somewhere on the far side of that wall of stone, a mute child was carrying a fragment of the oldest covenant in creation toward a drowned chapel, and Elodie Yarrow was with her, and Bren was with them, and not one inspector in Vellharrow knew which way they'd gone.

Sacrifice, his mentor had told him once, long ago, in the guildhall that no longer existed, is only holy when nobody claps. Applause is just another kind of payment, and payment makes it commerce.

Nobody was clapping. There was only the drip of water, the bite of the cords, and the slow, bruised satisfaction settling into Tammas Vane's chest like a banked coal.

They hauled him upright. He let them. An anvil goes where it's put.

But as they marched him up through the smuggler-tunnels toward the gray light of the world above—toward Corvane's spire, though he didn't know that yet—he permitted himself one last look back at the sealed wall of stone.

"Hold," he told it quietly, the way he talked to cooling iron. "Just hold. They don't need long."

And the stone, being honest material, held.

Above, dawn was breaking over Vellharrow, and in a tower of black basalt at the city's heart, a magistrate who never slept was about to learn that the anvil had a name—and that names, in the right hands, could be made to talk.
