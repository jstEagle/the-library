## 15. The Child Chooses

The girl had been counting words the way other children counted stars.

She kept them in a small wooden box at the back of her mind—a box she had built herself, in the first weeks, when the human tongue was nothing but noise shaped like knives and honey. *Bread.* *Cold.* *Stay.* *Elodie.* Each word went into the box, and each night she took them out one by one and turned them over in the dark, learning their weight, their edges, the way some of them were soft as moss and others sharp enough to cut if you held them wrong.

The First Tongue had no need of boxes. In the First Tongue, a thing was what it was, whole and shining, and you could not lie in it even if you wished to. But the human words were crooked little things. They could mean one thing and say another. They could be *yes* and mean *no*. They could be *I'm fine* and mean *I am drowning.*

She was beginning to understand that this was not a flaw in the language. It was a flaw in the people. And she was beginning to love them anyway, which frightened her more than the hunters did.

Tonight the shop smelled of tallow and rain that had come in under the door and refused to leave. The woman called Elodie sat at the workbench with her back curved like a drawn bow, reading a letter by candlelight. The letter had arrived an hour ago, folded into the mouth of a messenger boy who would not come inside, who kept looking up and down the street as though the shadows themselves might be paid to report on him.

The girl watched from the stairs, where she had learned to sit so that the fourth step did not creak.

She could not read the human letters yet—*yet*, said the voice in her head that was becoming Elodie's voice, because everything Elodie said, the girl caught and kept—but she did not need to read it. She could see what it was doing to the woman. Elodie's thumb pressed white against the paper's edge. Her breathing had gone shallow and careful, the way it did when she was holding something terrible very still so it wouldn't wake.

*Corvane,* the letter said. The girl knew that name now. It lived in this house the way damp lived in walls—invisible, everywhere, seeping into every warm thing until the warm things rotted. Corvane had taken Tammas. Corvane wanted the stone. Corvane offered, in his neat cold human letters, to let the child live if the stone was delivered to his spire before the moon waned.

The girl put her hand flat against her own chest, where the fragment of the covenant-sigil slept beneath her skin like a coal banked in ash.

She had known for three days what she was going to do.

* * *

It had come to her in the drowned chapel, though she had not understood it then. When Elodie touched the sigil-stone and wept without sound, the fragment in the girl's chest had flared, and through that flare she had seen—not dreamed, *seen*, the way you see your own hands—the shape of the covenant from its far side.

She saw the first weaving: two realms young and bright, leaning toward each other across a veil thin as morning mist, and the mediators standing between them with their arms open, holding the balance the way a bridge holds two shores. She saw the renewals, generation upon generation, and she saw what each renewal cost.

A life freely given. Not blood. Not death, exactly. Something worse and stranger: a self. A name, a memory, a whole inner country, poured out into the sigil like water into cracked earth, so that the covenant could drink and live and the mediator who gave it walked away hollow—or did not walk away at all.

Her mentor-of-sorts, the dead man whose token she carried—Maren's token, Elodie's teacher—had known. She had felt him in the stone too, folded into its layers like a leaf pressed in a book. He had hidden the truth because he loved his guild too much to ask it of them, and the hiding had broken everything anyway. Ten years of silence. Ten years of the veil thinning like ice in spring. Ten years of her own realm dimming, its rivers forgetting how to run, its wardens growing gray and desperate.

And she had seen, clearest of all, the terms written at the covenant's root in letters older than kingdoms: *willing*. It must be willing. No sacrifice seized would hold; no life taken would seal. That was why Corvane's stolen power leaked and soured, why his conduits were cracking, why his extended life must taste of ash. You could steal magic. You could never steal a covenant.

Which meant the choice was hers. It had always been hers. That was the terrible mercy woven into the thing in her chest.

She was the key. Keys do not get to choose which doors they open—but this one did. This one had been made, somehow, out of a child, and children were the one thing in either realm that both sides had forgotten to account for.

She had spent three days turning it over, the way she turned over her hoarded words.

She thought about her mother, who had pushed her through the last gap in the veil with hands that shook, saying in the First Tongue: *run, grow, remember.* She thought about Serevan, the gray warden, whose grief she had tasted when he held her—that vast slow sorrow of a realm going out like a lamp. She thought about Bren, who laughed like a door banging open, and Tammas, who was in Corvane's hands right now because he had held a tunnel up long enough for her to crawl free. She thought about Elodie most of all: Elodie's rough hands braiding her hair badly, Elodie humming off-key while grinding charm-dust, Elodie standing between her and armed men with nothing but a shopkeeper's stubbornness.

If she gave herself, all of them would be safe. The veil would heal. Both realms would breathe. Tammas would not have died in a cell for nothing. It was clean arithmetic, and the First Tongue loved clean arithmetic.

But there was another voice in her now, a small crooked human voice she had caught from Elodie like a cold caught from a sneeze, and it kept saying: *you are eight years old, and the arithmetic is wrong.*

* * *

On the fourth night, the house was quiet in the particular way that meant everyone was awake and pretending otherwise. Bren snored theatrically in the back room—he snored honestly too, but the theater was for her benefit, a signal that the big harmless man was near. Elodie sat up with the letter and the sigil-stone wrapped in wool beside her, and the candle burned low, and the rain came back as if it had only stepped out for a pipe.

The girl came down the stairs, skipping the fourth step, and stood in the doorway of the workshop.

Elodie looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and exhausted, and when they found the girl they softened in that way that never stopped astonishing her—like a window opening in a wall she'd thought was solid all the way through.

"Hey, sprout," Elodie said quietly. "Couldn't sleep?"

The girl shook her head. Then she nodded. Then she made herself stop dithering, because what she had to say deserved stillness.

She crossed the room and climbed onto the stool across the workbench, the one she always sat in, the one with the wobbly leg Elodie had shimmed with a folded charm-catalogue. She put both hands flat on the bench the way she'd seen Elodie do when she meant business with a customer.

"I have words," she said.

Elodie went very still. The girl had spoken before—small things, fragments, *bread*, *bad dream*, *Bren funny*—each one celebrated like a festival. But something in the girl's posture tonight made Elodie set down the letter and fold her hands and wait, the way you wait at a sickbed.

"My tongue," the girl said carefully, tasting each human word before committing to it, "was locked. When I came through. Fear locks it. Grief locks it." She touched her throat. "Your words are keys. I have been collecting." A ghost of a smile. "I have enough now."

"Enough for what?" Elodie asked, and her voice was gentle, but underneath the gentleness the girl heard the tightening, the drawing-in, the animal readiness of a creature sensing a trap near its young.

"For what I know."

She reached up and pulled the collar of her shirt aside, showing the faint tracery of silver light that lived over her heart, the fragment-sigil, dimmer these days as the veil thinned further, like a star behind smoke. "This is a piece of the covenant. I am a piece of the covenant. I did not choose to be. But I know what it needs now. I saw it in the chapel, through the stone, through—" she searched for the word, found it, "—through Maren. He is in the stone. Part of him. What was left when he—" She didn't have the word for what Maren had done, because she suspected Maren hadn't done it, hadn't given enough, and the failure of it had aged into the stone like a stain.

Elodie's face had gone white. "Sprout—"

"The covenant wants a self," the girl said, pressing on before the lock could close on her tongue again. She had rehearsed this. She would not lose her nerve to Elodie's kindness; kindness was the one weapon she had no defense against. "A whole one. Given freely. Not killed—given. Name and memory and all the rooms inside a person, poured into the sigil so it can drink and hold the veil closed. That is the price. It was always the price. Your guild were not mages. They were the payment, one by one, and no one told them, or someone told them and they chose anyway, and then Corvane broke it so no one would ever have to choose again and he could drink alone."

"How do you—" Elodie's voice cracked. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "You're a child. How do you know this?"

"Because it lives in me," the girl said simply. "Because it has been whispering its terms since before I could speak them. Because I am the key, and a key knows its own lock." She took a breath, and the breath shook, and she hated that it shook, and she made herself keep going. "So I choose. Me. Freely. That is what *freely* means—it means no one can give me, and no one can refuse for me. Only me."

"No," Elodie said.

It came out fast and flat, before the girl had even finished, and the girl had expected that, had planned for it, but hearing it still landed like a slap.

"You don't understand," the girl said, and now her own voice was rising, and the candleflame bent toward her as the fragment stirred, silver light pulsing under her skin. "If I don't, the veil tears. Both realms drown. Serevan's home dies—all of it, the rivers, the singing trees, everyone—and yours burns when what's on the other side comes through hungry. Tammas is in a cell right now. If I give myself, he walks free, everyone walks free, the war never happens, nobody else has to—"

"*No.*" Elodie was on her feet now, palms slammed flat on the workbench, and the whole bench jumped, and the wool-wrapped stone rolled against a jar of charm-dust. "You don't get to do this. Do you hear me? You are *eight years old*. You should be afraid of the dark and angry about bedtime, not— not volunteering yourself like—like a *payment*—"

"It is what I am *for*—"

"You are not *for* anything!" The words tore out of Elodie raw-voiced, and for a moment the girl saw something terrible and wonderful underneath them: ten years of guilt, a decade of silence, a mentor lost, a guild scattered, a woman who had buried her own talent alive and called it survival—all of it rising at once like floodwater. "Nobody is *for* anything. That's the lie. That's the oldest lie in both worlds, that some people exist to be spent so others can be safe. Corvane believes it. The wardens believe it. Maybe my whole rotten guild believed it, God forgive us. But I have watched you for months, little one, and I will tell you what you're for. You're for stealing honey off my shelf. You're for laughing at Bren's terrible jokes. You're for learning words and hoarding them in that little box in your head—you think I don't know about the box? I can *see* you doing it, and it's the most beautiful thing I've ever watched anyone do. You are for *growing up*. That's what you're for. Everything else is negotiable."

The girl was crying. She hadn't meant to. Tears were inefficient and she resented them, but they came anyway, hot and silent, the way she had cried for a year before finding this house.

"If I grow up," she whispered, "there may be nothing to grow up *in*."

"Then we build something worth growing up in," Elodie said. "Or we die trying. But we do it *together*, and nobody gets handed to the fire because it's tidy. Especially not you. Especially—" her voice broke completely, and she came around the bench and knelt, so that her face was level with the girl's, so that her rough charm-maker's hands took the girl's small ones and held them like something rescued from a flood, "—especially not you. Do you understand me? I did not survive ten years of being nobody to let the people I love become *payments*. Whatever the covenant wants, it can learn to want something else. We'll renegotiate. We're mediators, aren't we? All of us. That's the whole point. Nobody stands between two worlds alone—not a guild, not a magistrate, and certainly not a child."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the back room, Bren's theatrical snoring had gone conspicuously quiet; he was listening, and pretending not to, and the girl loved him for both.

Then the girl did the bravest thing she had ever done, braver than fleeing her dying realm, braver than offering herself: she unclenched.

She let the plan she had built fall apart in her hands. She let herself be eight years old. She slid off the stool and into Elodie's arms and buried her face in the woman's shoulder and sobbed, properly, messily, in the ugly human way, and Elodie held her and rocked her and murmured nonsense into her hair—*it's all right, I've got you, I've got you, nobody is spending anybody*—and the fragment-sigil under the girl's ribs pulsed slowly, like something listening, like something reconsidering.

When the crying finally ebbed, the girl pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve and looked at Elodie with red-rimmed, fiercely clear eyes.

"Then we need a different self," she said. "One who chooses. One who is old enough to know what they're losing." She paused, and her new human words arranged themselves into the shape of the thought she had been circling for four days, the thought she had sworn not to say and was saying anyway. "Someone who already decided, months ago, in a shop full of hollow charms, that some things matter more than surviving. Someone who taught me the word *stay*."

Elodie went still.

"Don't," Elodie said, but it was a different *don't* than before—thinner, frightened, the *don't* of someone seeing a road they'd been walking toward all along.

"You read the letter," the girl said softly. "Corvane wants the stone brought to the spire. So we bring it. All of us. And at the top of that spire, when the moment comes, the covenant will take whoever freely gives—and I will fight you with everything I have to make sure it isn't me, and you will fight me to make sure it isn't you, and maybe—" her chin lifted, and for a heartbeat the silver light under her skin flared bright as a struck bell, "—maybe that's the answer. Maybe the covenant doesn't want one sacrifice. Maybe it wants everyone willing, together, and no one spent. Maren never tried that. No one ever tried that."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the magistrate's spire stood black against the storm-light, its summit crowned with the sickly green shimmer of stolen power—and somewhere inside it, Tammas waited, and the moon was waning, and there were perhaps three nights left before Corvane's patience ran out and he sent hunters who did not knock.

Elodie looked at the girl for a long time. Then she looked at the wrapped stone, and at the letter, and at the rain running down the dark glass like the whole sky trying to get in.

"All right," she said at last, and her voice was quiet and terrible and certain. "We go to the spire. All of us. And we offer Corvane a bargain he hasn't imagined." She stood, and began rolling up her sleeves, and the girl saw that her hands had stopped shaking. "Wake Bren. We've got a guild to reforge—and for once, we're going to write the terms ourselves."
