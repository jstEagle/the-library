## 16. Storming the Spire

Dawn came to Vellharrow the color of old iron, and Elodie stood in the mouth of a smuggler's tunnel with soot on her hands and a sigil-stone warm against her breastbone, and she could not remember ever having been this afraid—or this certain.

The tunnel opened behind a collapsed tannery at the foot of Spire Hill, and through its broken archway she could see the magistrate's tower rising against the grey sky like a needle driven through the heart of the city. Corvane's spire. Ten years ago it had been the Guildhall, white stone and open doors, where her mentor had taught her to draw the first simple covenants in chalk on slate. Now it was black-ribbed and windowed with smoked glass, crowned with copper conduits that drank from the sky itself, and every soul in Vellharrow walked wide around its base without quite knowing why.

They knew why now. The Veiled Market had seen to that.

* * *

Two nights before, in the undercity, the freed smugglers had gathered around Tammas's empty forge-chair, and the half-blooded Others had spoken plainly for the first time in a decade of silence.

"Corvane bought our debts," said Vesh, the market-mother, her veils pushed back from a face like weathered riverstone. "He owns us on paper. But paper is not the same as hold." She had laid a hand flat on the table, palm up, showing the faint green shimmer beneath her skin where Other blood sat close to the surface. "He thinks because we were stranded here when the veil closed, we are his. He has forgotten what we were before we were stranded."

"And what were you?" Bren had asked, wary, his scribe's fingers wrapped around a cup he hadn't touched.

Vesh had smiled, and it had not been entirely a kind smile. "We were the ones who carried things across borders, scribe. We have not forgotten how."

So it was decided there, in the dripping dark beneath the city: they would take back the conduit. They would take back Tammas. And they would show Vellharrow—every shuttered window, every bowed head, every charm-seller who had spent ten years selling hollow brass and calling it nothing—that magic had never actually died in this city. It had only been stolen, and hoarded, and hidden in one tall black tower by one frightened, rotting man.

Elodie had looked down at the child then—Wren, they called her now, though her true name lived in the First Tongue where no hunter could reach it—and Wren had looked back with those eyes like deep water and nodded once, gravely, as though the whole plan had been hers all along.

Perhaps it had been. Perhaps it always had been, since the night she'd appeared at Elodie's door speaking words that burned.

* * *

Now, in the grey light, they assembled.

There were more of them than Elodie had dared hope. The Veiled Market had turned out nearly forty—half-bloods and harbor-rats and quiet people who had spent ten years pretending to be only quiet. Some carried knives. Some carried nothing but themselves, which for the Others was weapon enough; when Vesh unbound her veils fully and let her true aspect rise, the air around her bent like heat over coals.

Bren stood at Elodie's left shoulder, ink-stained and shaking, clutching a leather case of chalks and prepared sigil-papers like a soldier clutching a rifle. "I haven't drawn a working covenant in ten years," he kept saying. "Not a real one. My hands—" 

"Your hands remember," Elodie said. "Mine did."

Serevan stood apart, as he always did, the warden's long coat beaded with tunnel-damp, his otherness held around him like a coat of frost. He had come to them three days after the Veiled Market's betrayal, alone, unarmed, and had said only: *My superiors want the child returned so they can abandon your realm cleanly. I no longer believe my superiors serve either realm. I will stand with you.* It was the most words Elodie had heard him string together, and she had chosen to believe him because the alternative was nowhere left to stand.

And Wren—Wren wore the recovered sigil-stone's twin fragment braided into her hair, and she held Elodie's hand, and she was calm in the way that deep water is calm.

"The conduits draw hardest at dawn," Serevan said, nodding toward the spire. Its copper veins already glimmered faintly, drinking the first light. "Corvane's strength peaks then. But so does the pool at its heart—the stolen power gathers thickest, and if we break the siphons while it is full, the release will—" He paused, choosing honesty over comfort. "It will be very bright."

"It'll be visible," Bren corrected quietly. "That's the point, isn't it? Let them see."

Elodie looked out past the tannery ruins, down the crooked streets of Tanner's Row and beyond, where chimney smoke rose straight and ordinary and thousands of people were waking to an ordinary day. Ten years ago they had watched the guilds burn and learned to look away. She remembered looking away herself. She remembered how easy it was.

"No," she said. "The point is the child. The point is the covenant. Everything else—" She touched the stone at her chest. "Everything else is just making sure we live long enough to do it properly."

She squeezed Wren's hand once, let go, and stepped out of the tunnel's shadow into the open street.

* * *

The first ward hit them two hundred yards from the spire's base—a lattice of pale fire strung across Tanner's Row like a fisherman's net, humming with borrowed power. Corvane's work, or rather Corvane's theft made visible. Elodie felt her marks flare hot beneath her sleeves in answer, the old guild-sigils her mentor had inked into her skin a decade ago, dormant so long she'd thought them scar tissue.

"Bren."

"On it." He knelt in the gutter, tore a sigil-paper free, and pressed it to the cobblestones with both palms. His lips moved—not the First Tongue, which belonged to Wren and the Others, but the guild-cant, the mediator's grammar of balance and exchange. The paper flared blue-white. The ward-net shuddered, found itself *answered*, and frayed apart into drifting embers.

Above them, in the spire's smoked windows, lights began to kindle.

"They know," Vesh said, unsurprised. "Good. Saves us knocking."

Then the street filled with inspectors.

Elodie had spent a decade flinching from guild-inspectors—their grey coats, their iron pikes tipped with null-charms that ate warmth out of the air, their faces set in the particular blankness of men paid to forget what they'd once known. There were thirty of them pouring from the spire's gates now, and behind them came something worse: hollow-men, constructs of packed ash and bound servitude, the kind of thing that could only be made by someone who had power and no conscience about spending it.

But the inspectors stopped.

Elodie watched it happen, watched it ripple down their line like wind through wheat. They had been told for ten years that magic was dead, purged, a sickness cut out of the city. And here were forty people walking up Spire Hill wreathed in soft light—here was Vesh with her aspect risen and her shadow moving wrong, here was Serevan whose presence made the null-charms on the pikes whimper and die—and here, small and barefoot and utterly unafraid, was a child whose skin shimmered with the First Tongue written in light.

An inspector dropped his pike. Just let it fall, and put his hands up, and stepped aside. Then another. A few held their ground—one loosed a bolt from a null-crossbow that should have snuffed Bren's shield-spell like a candle—but the bolt struck the air an inch from Wren and simply *unmade itself*, unraveled by proximity to something older and truer than any suppression charm Corvane's gold could buy.

The hollow-men did not hesitate. They never hesitated. They came down the hill like a rockslide, and the battle truly began.

* * *

What Elodie remembered afterward came to her in fragments, the way fire comes back to you: heat, noise, a single image held too long.

Vesh and her smugglers met the hollow-men head-on and *unwove* them—not destroying, mediating, doing with violence what the guild had once done with covenant: taking bound things and releasing them. Each construct burst apart into ordinary ash and a brief, grateful flicker of whatever enslaved spirit had been packed inside. Elodie saw one flicker circle Vesh's head twice, like a moth remembering joy, before it went out.

Bren fought with chalk. He moved through the melee crouched and darting, drawing circles of protection around the wounded, sealing doorways against counter-spells, his hands steady now, his cant ringing out clear and confident, and every time he completed a working he laughed—a wild, disbelieving sound, a man rediscovering a limb he'd been told was amputated.

Serevan was terrible and beautiful. The warden moved like winter given purpose, and where he passed, Corvane's stolen workings simply froze and fell silent. Elodie caught one glimpse of him facing three hollow-men alone, and then deliberately did not watch, because some things a mediator should not witness too closely even when they fight on her side.

And she herself—she fought the way her mentor had taught her, the way she had almost forgotten: not with force but with *turning*. When a binding-spell came shrieking off the spire's upper balcony she caught it on her marks and gave it somewhere else to go, redirecting it into the gutters, into the earth, letting the city drink it. When inspectors' null-fields pressed in she answered them with the sigil-stone's warmth, and the stone sang through her, and she understood suddenly why the guild had called themselves weavers: because that was exactly what it felt like, threads passing through her hands, tension and slack, pattern forming under pressure.

Through all of it, Wren stayed at her side, and the child's presence was the loom on which everything else held together.

* * *

Tammas was on the fourth level.

They found him—Bren shouting his name through a shattered stairwell door—in a chamber lined with copper pipe, chained to a chair with null-fetters that had eaten the color out of his hands. He was thinner than Elodie remembered, grey-faced, one eye swollen shut from questioning, but when he saw them his good eye filled and he laughed, a cracked and rusty sound.

"Took you long enough," he rasped. "Ten years and eleven days."

"We counted differently," Bren said, and his voice broke on it, and he was already cutting the fetters with a severance-sigil drawn directly on the metal, his tears falling onto the chalk and making the line run silver.

Tammas got one arm over Bren's shoulders and one over Elodie's, and as they hauled him upright he looked past them, out the broken wall of the conduit-chamber, and went very still.

"What," he whispered. "What is that."

Elodie followed his gaze and forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

From the chamber's great central pipe—thick as a cathedral column, thrumming with siphoned power—light was bleeding. Not metaphorically. The seams of the conduit glowed white-gold, and through rents in the spire's smoked glass the morning sun struck it, and the whole tower hummed like a struck bell. Ten years of stolen covenant-power, drained from the land and the veil and the guild-stones Corvane had seized, pooled and pressurized and *waiting*. She could feel it pressing against the inside of the pipes like a tide against a dam.

"That's what he's been drinking," Elodie said. "All this time. That's what's kept him alive."

"Then let's stop him drinking," Tammas said, and spat on the floor, and grinned through split lips. "Give me a hammer. Or better—give me the stone. I still know how smiths break things."

* * *

They climbed.

The spire's upper levels were near-empty—Corvane had spent his garrison below, expecting siege, not infiltration—and the higher they went the louder the conduit sang, until Elodie's teeth ached with it and Wren had to press both hands over her ears, though the First Tongue-light under her skin pulsed in time with the hum, answering it, *recognizing* it.

Because that was the truth of it, Elodie realized as they reached the final stair. The power in the pipes was covenant-power. Stolen, perverted, dammed up behind greed—but it was the same current that had once flowed openly between the realms, the same song her mentor had died protecting, the same song Wren carried in her blood. The spire wasn't just a fortress. It was a wound, cauterized shut by a thief who wanted the bleeding all to himself.

They came out onto the crown of the spire, into wind and thin cold light, and there it all was: the great copper crown of conduits branching over the platform like a metal tree, the siphon-nodes glittering, and beyond the balustrade—the city.

Vellharrow spread out below them in the early light, rooftops and bell-towers and the grey ribbon of the river, and it was not empty. Word had run through the streets faster than any inspector could ride it down. People stood in doorways. They crowded the hill's lower slopes. From Tanner's Row to the fish-market, faces were turned upward toward the spire, watching smoke and strange light pour from its crown, watching figures move against the sky.

Watching magic burn openly for the first time in ten years.

Elodie heard the crowd before she understood it: a low sound, thousands strong, neither cheer nor fear—something rawer than either, the sound of a city remembering. Somewhere down in the streets someone began to sing, an old work-song with a guild-refrain in it that everyone knew and no one had dared sing aloud in a decade, and others took it up, badly, off-key, louder.

"They're not running," Bren said. His voice shook. "Elodie—they're *not running*."

"No," she said. Her own eyes were burning. "They're done with that."

Tammas leaned on the balustrade, grey and grinning, and looked down at the singing city, and said quietly, "Worth every day of it."

Which was when the conduits screamed.

* * *

It was Corvane, of course. Cornered things bite. From somewhere within the spire's heart the magistrate threw everything he had left into his stolen machine, and the copper crown above them blazed white, and the siphon-nodes spun up to a pitch that cracked the smoked glass of the cupola. The whole tower lurched. Cracks spidered through the ancient stonework. The pool of stolen power strained at its bindings like a living thing.

"He's going to vent it," Serevan said, materializing out of the wind at Elodie's shoulder, frost riming his voice. "At the city. If he cannot keep the power, he will spend it all at once and call it justice—purge the rebels, drown the lower quarters in raw force. He has the doctrine written. I have read it."

Elodie looked down at the crowds on the slopes—at the singers, the watchers, the ones who had finally stopped looking away—and felt something in her chest go hard and bright as forged iron.

"Then we break it first," she said. "Here. Now. Before he can aim it anywhere."

She drew the sigil-stone from her breast and held it up, and it answered her, blazing, and Wren stepped forward to stand beside her with the light of the First Tongue rising off her skin like steam off summer stone. Below, the crowd's song swelled. Above, the stolen power howled behind its copper veins.

And from the dark of the stairwell behind them, unhurried, came the sound of slow footsteps and a dry voice that had ordered a decade of burnings:

"So. The last of the weavers, come home at last."

Magistrate Corvane stepped out onto the crown of the spire, and in his withered hands he held the child's second name.
