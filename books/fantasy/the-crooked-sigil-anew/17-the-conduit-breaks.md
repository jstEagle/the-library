## 17. The Conduit Breaks

Bren had spent ten years learning to be small, and in a single night he was being asked to be enormous.

The spire's lower galleries rang with the sound of the assault—shouted commands, the crack of Tammas's ironwork prying at doors that had been sealed since the Purge, the wet shriek of something Other moving through the smoke above them. He pressed his back to a pillar of black marble and tried to remember how his hands were supposed to work. They had been steady once, when he was a guild-scribe, when the worst thing they ever did was copy covenant-law onto vellum by candlelight. Now they shook around a scribe's stylus repurposed into something between a tool and a weapon, its silvered tip glowing faintly where Elodie had keyed it to him.

"Second conduit's ahead," Serevan said. The warden moved like water finding its level, gray-robed and terrible, his eyes catching light they shouldn't have caught in a stairwell this dark. "You'll feel it before you see it."

He was right. The sensation came up through the soles of Bren's boots first—a low, wrong hum, like standing on the hide of some vast sleeping animal. Then through his teeth. Then behind his eyes. It was power, but power *pulled* rather than given, drawn out of the world's roots and hoarded, and every step closer made Bren feel like a man walking upstream against a river that wanted to take him apart.

The conduit chamber opened before them: a vaulted rotunda beneath the spire's root, ringed with pipes of hammered copper that ran up into the dark like the ribs of an inverted whale. Along each pipe, sigil-plates had been bolted—guild-work, stolen guild-work, plates Bren recognized from the scriptorium's own shelves—and threaded through them ran a light the color of old bruises. Green-gold. Covenant-light. Ten years of it, siphoned drop by drop from a wound Corvane had cut in the veil and never let heal.

"Gods," Tammas whispered beside him. The blacksmith had come up through the tunnels with them, still bruised from his captivity, his forearms burned where he'd torn free of his irons. He stared up at the pipes the way a man stares at a grave. "That's Maren's plate. Third from the left. She died in the Purge and her work is *here*, feeding him."

"It fed him for ten years." Elodie's voice came from Bren's left, quiet and level in a way he'd learned, over these past weeks, to fear more than shouting. She stood with the child's hand in hers—the girl wrapped in smuggler's wool, watching the bruised light with those too-old eyes. "Every charm I sold hollow. Every gift my mentor gave me, drained before it ever reached the street. All of it running up into that man's veins."

"Then we break it," Bren said, and heard his own voice crack on the second word.

They broke it.

It should have been harder. That was Bren's thought afterward, in the terrible quiet after, when there was nothing left to do but understand what they'd done. It should have required hours of careful unweaving, the way Maren would have insisted, the way the old guild would have worked it—plates eased free one at a time, the flow redirected, the pressure bled off gently like steam from a kettle. There had been no time for gentle. Above them, the battle for the spire's upper floors thundered; freed smugglers held the stairwells against inspectors who fought with the desperation of men whose master's life was their livelihood; and somewhere overhead Corvane himself waited with ten years of stolen power coiled in him like a second heart.

So they broke it fast.

Tammas went first, because Tammas always went first now, as if he owed the world a debt of courage and meant to pay it down in full. His long-handled shears—forge-made, spell-bitten, humming with the little charms Elodie had woken in them—closed around the third pipe, Maren's pipe, and cut.

The sound was not metal parting. The sound was a note, one vast sustained note, as if someone had struck the inside of the world with a hammer. Bren felt it in his sternum, in his molars, in the scar tissue over his heart where he'd taken an inspector's blade ten years ago. The bruised-green light geysered from the severed pipe and hung in the air like a living thing, and for one heartbeat—one shining, impossible heartbeat—it was beautiful. It smelled, absurdly, of his mother's kitchen. Of rain on the scriptorium roof. Of everything the city had been before.

"Again!" Elodie shouted. "All of them—now, before it reroutes!"

Serevan moved through the falling light like a scythe through wheat, his warding-signs flaring, cutting pipes with edges of pure negation. Tammas sheared a second, then a fourth. Bren drove his stylus-blade into a sigil-plate and *unwrote* it, the way he hadn't unwritten anything in ten years, his fingers remembering glyphs his mind had buried alive. One by one the conduits failed, and each failure released another torrent of covenant-power into the chamber, until the air itself was thick with it, until Bren could hardly breathe for the richness of it, until he laughed—actually laughed, wild and ragged—because they'd done it, gods help them, the siphons were breaking and the power was going *home*—

That was when the sky split.

Not here. Not in the chamber. But he felt it—he knew later that everyone in Vellharrow felt it—a sound like fabric tearing above the clouds, and through the rotunda's high vents poured a light that was not any light the human sky had ever made. Violet. Silver. A dawn from underneath.

"No," Serevan said. Bren turned and saw, for the first time, fear on the warden's face—not for himself, but the particular fear of a man watching his own prophecy come true. "No, no—it wasn't meant to release all at once. The veil has thinned for ten years. It is a membrane stretched over a wound, and you have just—"

The floor shuddered. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. And through the vents, drifting down like ash from a fire no one had lit, came petals. Pale, luminous petals that glowed faintly and dissolved when they touched stone, and Bren knew what they were, because his mentor had shown him one preserved in resin forty years ago, back when such things could still cross over: blossoms from the orchards of the Otherworld, which bloomed only under a sky with two moons.

"The veil," Elodie breathed.

Above them, muffled by stone, the sounds of the battle changed. The shouting took on a new register—terror, not fury. Screams began, human screams, and beneath them other voices, voices that resonated in the chest like struck bronze, voices out of stories told to frighten children about what lived beyond the hedge of the world.

They ran for the surface.

* * *

Vellharrow was burning, and it was not only fire.

Bren came up out of the spire's side door into a night that had been replaced with something else entirely. The sky above the city had split along a seam that ran from horizon to zenith, jagged as a lightning scar, and through it poured light—violet, silver, the green-gold of the covenant itself—and shapes. Shapes drifting down slow as snowfall and fast as hawks both, shapes with too many joints and too much grace, shapes that were almost beautiful right up until they weren't. A herd of pale stags with antlers like winter branches picked their way through Tanner's Row, leaving frost where their hooves fell, their eyes ancient and uncomprehending. Something vast and eel-like swam through the air above the harbor, trailing river-water that fell as rain. Small bright things—wights or wisps or whatever the old texts called them—swarmed the gutters like starlings, and where they passed, streetlamps guttered and iron fences wept rust.

And everywhere, people. Pouring out of houses into the streets, faces upturned, some screaming, some kneeling, some—Bren saw it and would carry it all his life—some *laughing*. An old woman in the doorway of a chandlery held her arms out to the falling petals and wept without shame. Two inspector-soldiers stood frozen at their post, halberds forgotten, watching a stag the size of a cart horse drink from the fountain in Coronation Square.

"They're not attacking," Tammas said hoarsely. He stood at Bren's shoulder, shears still in his fists, blood on his knuckles that might have been his own. "Look at them. They're *lost*. Like us. Like—"

"Like things that woke up somewhere new." Serevan stood apart, hood thrown back, his face turned to the torn sky with an expression Bren couldn't read—grief and homesickness and horror braided together. "Ten years the covenant has been failing. The far side of the veil has been starving, dimming, dying by inches. And now the wall is open and everything that survived the starving is coming through toward the light. Toward the *power*." His jaw tightened. "Toward the wound your rite will need to close. If it can close now. If it isn't too wide."

"Elodie," Bren said.

She was already moving. Of course she was. Through the chaos of the square, against the current of fleeing crowds, Elodie Yarrow walked with the child's hand gripped tight in hers, her face lit violet from above, her expression fixed in that terrible calm that Bren had come to understand was the truest sign of how afraid she was. The girl stumbled once on the cobbles—she was so small, gods, she was so small—and Elodie lifted her without breaking stride, settling her on her hip like she'd done it a thousand times.

"We get her clear," Elodie called back, not turning. "Away from the square, away from the spire, away from *him*. Bren—"

"I'm here."

"—the chapel-stone. You carried it?"

"Against my ribs." The sigil-stone, pried from its flooded altar weeks ago, warm as a living thing against his skin ever since. It was warmer now. It was nearly hot.

"Good. Because if the veil is open, then the reforging can't wait for us to be ready. We do it tonight or we lose both worlds tonight."

They made the edge of Coronation Square, and that was where the victory curdled.

Later—much later, in the long years after, when he told the story and retold it and taught it to the guild's new children—Bren would try to explain how it happened. How fast. How quietly, almost. How a night that contained falling moons-petals and weeping soldiers and a sky torn open like a purse could still hold room for one small act of theft, one ordinary cruelty, committed by a man in fine robes walking calmly through miracle and catastrophe alike as though neither concerned him.

Corvane came out of the spire's front doors like a man descending to inspect his gardens.

Bren saw him first across the square—saw the magistrate stop three steps down from his own threshold and lift his face to the broken sky, and watched something move across his features that Bren had no name for. Not awe. Not fear. *Hunger*, maybe, or grief, or the fury of a man who had spent ten years building a private door through the world's wall only to find the whole wall demolished without his permission.

Then Corvane saw the child.

Everything afterward happened in the space between two of Bren's heartbeats. Corvane raised one hand—the left, the one that even at distance Bren could see was wrong, the skin translucent, veined with green-gold light, ten years of stolen covenant showing through him like ink through paper—and spoke a word. One word, in the First Tongue, and the word hit the square like a dropped bell. Every wight in the gutters scattered. The great stag threw up its head. And the crowd between Corvane and Elodie simply—folded, bodies bending aside like wheat, compelled or shoved, Bren never learned which, and the path from the spire doors to Elodie Yarrow lay open and empty for exactly as long as it needed to be.

"Bren—" Elodie started to turn.

Corvane crossed the square faster than a man his age should have been able to move. Bren was already running, already shouting, stylus-blade out and blazing, and he was not slow, he had never been slow, and it did not matter at all. The magistrate's wrong hand closed on the child's wrist and *pulled*, and the girl came off Elodie's hip with a sound that Bren would hear for the rest of his life—not a scream, not yet, just a small wounded breath of surprise, the sound of a child who had believed herself safe.

Elodie lunged. Light erupted from her sigil-marks, white-hot, and met a wall of green-gold force that Corvane threw up without even looking at her, spending power the way a rich man spends coins. The shockwave knocked her flat. Knocked Bren sprawling. Knocked the falling petals into spirals.

Corvane stepped back, the child crushed against his chest with one arm, his ruined hand raised toward the torn sky as if greeting it.

"Look what you've done," the magistrate said, and his voice carried across the whole square, impossibly, the way the First Tongue carried. He sounded, horribly, reasonable. Calm. Almost kind. "You've broken the world open, and now there is nothing between one realm and the next but weather. Except—" and here his grip tightened, and the child made her first real sound, a thin cry, and Bren felt it go through the assembled crowd like a blade through cloth, "—except through *her*. The key walks on her own feet, and I hold the key."

"Let her go." Elodie was on her feet. Blood ran from her hairline where she'd struck the cobbles, and her marks still burned, and her voice did not shake at all. "Whatever you think you're going to do with her, Corvane—whatever you've told yourself—you are holding a child in a collapsing square while both worlds bleed. Let. Her. Go."

"Or you'll reforge the covenant without me?" For the first time, something cracked in the magistrate's composure—a flicker, quickly smoothed, but Bren caught it. Fear. Real fear. "I know what it requires, Mistress Yarrow. I read the same meetinghouse walls you did—I've owned that meetinghouse for ten years. A life freely given. A self surrendered. Do you imagine I'll permit the one currency I cannot mint to change hands while I still breathe?"

Above them, the tear in the sky widened with a sound like ice cracking on a spring lake. More shapes came through. Somewhere across the city, a tower groaned and fell. The stags in the square had begun to keen, high and mournful, as though they understood better than anyone present what was happening to the boundary of the world.

Serevan appeared at Bren's shoulder, silent as fog. "His power is real but finite," the warden murmured, barely audible. "He has been drinking from a dying vein for ten years, and the vein just burst. He cannot hold the child and fight you both and mend the sky. He knows it. Watch him. He is deciding which thing to abandon."

Corvane's eyes moved—across Elodie, across Bren, up to the tearing heavens, down to the child struggling in his grip—and Bren saw the calculation run behind them, cold and quick and rotten to the root.

Then the magistrate smiled, and it was the worst thing Bren saw all night, worse than the split sky, worse than the things falling through it, because it was patient.

"Then let us go where the key belongs," Corvane said. "To the wound itself."

Green-gold light swallowed him and the child whole, and where they had stood there was nothing but a scorched outline on the cobblestones, shaped like a small pair of shoes.

Elodie made a sound Bren had never heard from her—not grief, not rage, something older than either—and the sigil-marks along her arms flared so bright that the nearest falling petals ignited. She looked at Bren, and Bren looked back, and no words were needed, because they both understood the same thing at the same moment:

There was only one place in Vellharrow where a man could stand inside the veil's wound.

The spire.

They ran.
