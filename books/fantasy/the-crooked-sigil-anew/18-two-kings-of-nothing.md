## 18. Two Kings of Nothing

The wind at the top of Corvane's spire did not blow so much as *scream*, tearing up from the wound in the sky and spiraling down around the broken crown of the tower like water finding a drain. Elodie clung to the last standing merlon with both hands, her knuckles white against stone that hummed with stolen power, and looked up at the place where the world had been torn open.

It was not darkness. That was the worst of it. She had expected darkness—a black gash, an absence—but the veil's rupture was the opposite of absence. It was *everything*: a slow churning aurora of colors that had no names in any human tongue, veined through with shapes that moved the way thought moves, formless and vast and curious. Through it she could see the Otherworld looking back. Not armies. Not monsters, though there were those too, wheeling at the edges like gulls over a foundering ship. What she saw most clearly was hunger—the deep, patient hunger of a realm that had been starving for ten years behind a wall its keepers had abandoned.

Below them, Vellharrow burned in a dozen places, and also, for the first time in a decade, *glowed*—lanterns lit without flint, ward-sigils flaring to life on doorways as frightened people reached instinctively for the old protections their grandmothers had whispered about. The conduit-break had loosed everything at once. Magic poured back into the city like blood returning to a numbed limb, and the limb was screaming.

"Elodie!" Bren's voice from the stairwell behind her, ragged with smoke. "The siphons are down! All six—we broke them all!"

She felt it even as he said it. The humming under the stone had changed pitch, the greedy suck of Corvane's machines gone silent, and what remained was rawer, wilder, unclaimed power pooling at the top of the spire like rainwater gathering in a cupped hand.

And in the center of that pool stood the two kings of nothing.

Corvane had climbed to his own roof to meet catastrophe, and he had dressed for it. Even now, with ash streaking his silver-trimmed sleeves and his carefully ordered city unraveling beneath him, Magistrate Aldric Corvane stood straight as a plumb line, one hand resting on the balustrade as if the whole tower were merely an extension of his will. The years he had bought with stolen covenant-power were peeling away from him by the hour—Elodie could see it happening, the way one watches frost retreat across a window—the fine lines around his mouth deepening, the steel-gray at his temples spreading like spilled ink. But his eyes had lost none of their terrible patience. They were fixed on the sky-wound with something close to ownership.

Opposite him, half-turned toward the rift, stood Serevan.

The warden looked worse than he had in the tunnels, worse than he had when he'd held the child and Elodie had traded her mentor's token for her freedom. His bark-grey skin had gone pale as birchwood, and the antlered shadow of his true shape flickered in and out around him like a candle guttering. He had come to Vellharrow as a hunter; he stood now as a man watching his own funeral procession approach from a great distance.

Between them, held fast in Corvane's grip—not by hands but by a lash of raw power wrapped around her small body like a snare—stood the child.

She wasn't struggling. That frightened Elodie more than screaming would have. The girl simply watched the sky with those old, old eyes, and the fragment of the covenant-sigil inside her glowed faintly through her skin, a lantern seen through flesh.

*Hold on,* Elodie thought, not daring to speak it aloud. *I'm coming. Hold on.*

She crossed the roof in the shelter of the parapet, Bren at her shoulder, Serevan turning at the last moment with an expression that was equal parts warning and surrender. The wind tried to peel her off the stones twice. She let it fail.

"Magistrate," she said, and her voice carried farther than it should have, riding the loose magic the way a boat rides a flood. "Let her go."

Corvane turned his head slowly, and smiled. It was, Elodie realized, the first time she had ever actually seen the man smile, and she understood immediately why he avoided doing it in public. There was nothing warm in it. It was the smile of a ledger balancing.

"The charm-seller." He said it almost fondly, the way one names a pest one has finally decided not to crush. "Yarrow, isn't it? I reviewed your file, after the raid. Ten years of selling dead trinkets to gullible widows. Do you know what struck me most in your records? How *carefully* harmless you made yourself. You could have been something, once. Instead you spent a decade pretending to be nothing." His gaze slid past her to the burning city below. "A waste. But then, waste is all this age has been."

"Let. Her. Go."

"In a moment." He raised his free hand toward the rift, and Elodie saw the conduits' remnants still wrapped around his fingers—broken filaments of siphon-work, twitching, trying to drink. "Do you know what you've done tonight, Yarrow? You've broken the dam. Everything I spent ten years containing—rationing, *managing*—" his voice cracked, just slightly, on the word, and beneath the crack Elodie heard something that surprised her: genuine conviction, rotten as its roots were, "—is pouring out uncontrolled. Without me, this ends in a week. The veil collapses entirely. Both realms drown. I am the only person alive who understands the mathematics of what's happening above us."

"You understand theft," Bren growled beside her. "You built machines to milk the covenant while you outlawed anyone who might notice."

"I built *order*," Corvane snapped, and for one unguarded instant the mask slipped entirely, and Elodie saw the thing underneath: not a monster, but a terrified man who had discovered his own mortality and decided the universe should be reorganized to prevent it. "Magic is a flood, girl. Always has been. It drowned the Old Kingdoms. It rotted the guilds from within—I read the archives, I know what your precious Weavers became before the end. Power needs a single hand on it or it devours everything. I was going to be that hand. One realm, ruled rightly, walled against the chaos beyond. That was always the plan. That was always the *only* plan."

"One realm," Elodie repeated quietly. "And the other?"

Corvane shrugged, elegant and obscene. "Sealed. Starved. A problem deferred indefinitely. The Otherworld was dying anyway. I merely declined to die with it."

Beside her, she heard Serevan exhale—a sound like wind through winter branches.

"And there it is," the warden said. "The human heart, laid bare. I thank you for your honesty, magistrate. It saves time." He turned to face the rift, and when he spoke again, his voice was flat with a grief so old it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for generations. "Because my masters have arrived at precisely the same arithmetic from the other side. You heard them in the sky, yes? Those shapes wheeling at the edge? That is the Court of Thorns, come to look at the wound their cowardice helped make. And their judgment is already rendered." His jaw tightened. "Seal the rift permanently. From our side. Let the human realm keep its stolen century of silence. Cut the dying limb away and save the body."

Elodie's stomach dropped. "Cut it—Serevan, if they seal it completely—"

"Then both realms live. Smaller. Barren. Forever divided." He did not look at her. "The covenant was meant to bind the two worlds into one living system, each feeding the other, each checking the other's excesses. My masters have concluded that the system has failed, and that the failure is contagious. They would rather amputate than cure." At last he turned, and his eyes—which had been hard as river-ice when he took the child, when he hunted her through the undercity—were cracked clean through. "They sent me to retrieve the vessel because the vessel is the key either way. She can anchor a sealing as easily as a renewal. The child does not survive either rite. Only which side survives *with* her does."

The wind screamed. Below, somewhere in Tanner's Row, a building collapsed in a bloom of orange sparks, and Elodie heard—faintly, impossibly—the sound of people singing. Ward-songs. Old ones. The city was remembering.

Two answers to the same question, she thought. Two kings of nothing. Corvane would wall one realm and starve the other, ruling a kingdom of ashes with a ledger instead of a heart. The Court of Thorns would seal the wound and abandon half of creation, saving their own skins and calling it wisdom. And between them, a child. Always a child. The smallest piece on the board, asked to pay every debt.

Something in Elodie went very quiet and very clear, the way the air goes before lightning.

"No," she said.

Corvane's eyebrow rose. "'No,'" he echoed, amused. "And what do you propose, shopkeeper? A third option? Some clever compromise?" He gestured at the sky with contempt. "Look at it! The mathematics don't allow for sentiment. There are exactly two stable outcomes—"

"There are three," Elodie said. "There have always been three. You just never counted it, because it isn't in any of your ledgers."

She stepped forward, out of the shelter of the parapet, into the full scream of the wind, and pulled back her sleeve.

Her sigil-marks were awake. They had been waking for weeks—since the night the child first spoke the First Tongue in her doorway—but now, bathed in the loose power pooling atop the spire, they blazed like brands, the old Weaver's script crawling up her forearms in lines of soft gold. Behind her, she heard Bren's breath catch. Ten years ago she had watched these same marks go dark on her mentor's arms as the guild fell, and she had believed they would never light again. She had spent a decade selling charms without teeth, telling herself the ache in her hands was only habit.

It had never been habit. It had been hunger.

"The covenant was never a wall," she said, and the spire seemed to lean in to listen. "It was a *handshake*. That's what the meetinghouse told us—that's what your inspectors burned, Corvane, and it didn't matter, because we found the truth anyway. The Weavers weren't mages. We were mediators. Translators between two languages that need each other to mean anything. Every renewal required a life freely given—not taken, not bargained for, not extorted from a child. *Given*. That's why your machines failed you. That's why the Court's sealing will fail too. You can't take what can only be offered."

Corvane's composure fractured, just visibly. "The original terms are lost. The last renewal died with the guild—"

"They're not lost." Elodie touched the glowing marks on her arm. "My mentor carried them out of the purge in his skin, and he gave them to me in the flooded chapel, along with the price. I know the rite. I know the cost." Her voice did not shake. She was almost proud of that. "And I am telling both of you—magistrate, warden, and whatever thorn-crowned things are watching from that sky—that the third path exists, and I intend to walk it. Renewal. On my terms. A willing sacrifice, freely chosen, anchored by the guild remnant and the vessel *alive*, not spent."

"The sacrifice requires selfhood entire," Serevan said quietly. "Name. Memory. Self. You know this. You would become no one."

"I know what it costs." She thought of the child's drawings—the stick figures, the two worlds holding hands, the small figure with the burning mark who stood in the middle. She thought of her shop, her hollow trinkets, her careful, careful harmlessness. "I've already been no one, warden. For ten years. I simply didn't know it yet."

"Sentiment," Corvane spat. "Madness. You'll fail, and the failure will kill everyone—"

"Then it will be *my* failure," Elodie said, "chosen and paid for—instead of yours, purchased with a child's life and called stewardship. That's the difference between us, magistrate. You want someone else to bear the cost of your safety. I'm offering to bear mine."

The lash of power around the child flickered.

It was small—the barest stutter, Corvane's concentration slipping as the words landed somewhere beneath his armor—and it was enough. Because the girl had been waiting. Elodie watched her small hands rise, watched the covenant-fragment flare bright enough to throw shadows, watched the snare of stolen power *unravel* around her like rope rotting through, and then the child was running—across the ruined rooftop, through the wind, straight into Elodie's arms, and Elodie caught her, staggered, went down to one knee and held on with everything she had.

"It's all right," she murmured into the girl's hair, in human speech, in the First Tongue, in the wordless language of a hand pressed to a small trembling back. "I've got you. Nobody's spending you. Nobody."

Above them, the rift churned. And from within it, Elodie felt attention settle on the rooftop like weather—vast, cold, weighing. The Court of Thorns, listening. Considering.

Serevan dropped to one knee before them both. When he spoke, it was not to Elodie. It was upward, into the screaming aurora, in the First Tongue, and though she caught only fragments, the shape of it was unmistakable: an argument. A warden, defying his court. *The vessel is willing. The mediator is willing. The old terms can be met. Let me witness it before you cut the world in half.*

The sky said nothing. But it did not leave, either. That, Elode decided, would have to count as a beginning.

Corvane stood alone amid the ruins of his ambition, power guttering off his fingers, years falling from his face like leaves past a window. For a long moment he simply stared at her—at the charm-seller with the blazing arms and the child in her embrace—and Elodie watched him arrive, at last, at the end of his own mathematics. Two stable outcomes, he had said. He had never imagined a woman choosing a third at the price of herself.

"This isn't over," he said at last, but there was no weight in it. It sounded like a man reading his own obituary aloud to hear how it fit.

"No," Elodie agreed. She rose, lifting the child onto her hip the way she'd once seen mothers do in the market districts, natural as breathing. "But it's over for you."

Behind her, boots on the stairs: Tammas, soot-blackened, alive, one arm around Bren's shoulders, and behind them a handful of freed smugglers blinking in the open sky-light. The guild remnant, gathered. The anchors, assembled.

The rift pulsed overhead, hungry and waiting, and the wind carried up from the streets below the sound of Vellharrow singing wardsongs it had half-forgotten, as if the whole city knew—as if it had always known—that something was about to be asked, and given, and sealed.

Elodie looked at the child. The child looked back, and nodded, once, gravely, the way one nods to a co-conspirator.

"To the veil's edge, then," Elodie said, and her sigils burned like sunrise. "We have a covenant to reforge."

* * *

They descended the far stair together, the guild remnant and the warden and the child, leaving Corvane alone on his roof with his fading power and his crumbling years. The way down was lit by Elodie's marks, gold light spilling off the walls, and the child walked with one small hand tucked in hers, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the walk itself did not say better.

At the base of the spire, where the street opened toward the old temple district and the thinning place beyond, Serevan stopped and pointed with one long grey finger.

The wound in the sky bent down toward the earth there, a column of churning light touching the rooftops of the ruined chapel quarter, and around its base the air rippled like heat over a forge. Waiting. Hungry. Listening.

Elodie tightened her grip on the child's hand, felt the covenant-fragment pulse in answer to the sigils under her skin, and stepped forward into the light.

Whatever came next, she would pay for it herself.

That was the whole of her plan, and it was enough.
