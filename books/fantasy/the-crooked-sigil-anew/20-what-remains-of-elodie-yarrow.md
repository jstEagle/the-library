## 20. What Remains of Elodie Yarrow

The world ended and began in the same breath.

The child felt it happen from the inside, because she was the inside of it—the hollow place where the covenant had lived, the wound in the veil that had been aching since before she could remember anything but running. The sigil closed around her like two hands meeting, Elodie's name unraveling thread by golden thread into its center, and for one impossible moment the child stood in both worlds at once: the drowned chapel's cold stone beneath her feet and the silver grass of the Otherworld beneath them too, the same ground seen through two skins of sky.

Then the light came down like rain after a long drought, and everything it touched was mended.

She heard the veil heal. That was the strangest part—not saw, not felt, but *heard*, a sound like a great loom completing its final pass, weft crossing warp, the shuttle clicking home. Where the sky over Vellharrow had split open, bleeding strange constellations and worse things, the tear drew itself together with a sigh that moved through the city like wind through wheat. The creatures that had spilled through—the pale hounds, the lantern-eyed wraiths, the slow green things dragging roots behind them—did not flee or fight. They simply turned, all at once, like a flock answering an unspoken call, and walked back through the narrowing seam as it sealed itself to something thinner than a wall and kinder than a door. A boundary now. A membrane. A place where the two worlds could touch without tearing.

The child knew this was what Elodie had wanted. Not a wall—Elodie had said so, once, in the dark under the floorboards, drawing pictures in the dust with a bent nail. Walls made things press against them until they broke. What the covenant needed was a window with a latch.

But the light had taken the price first.

* * *

Afterward there was silence on the spire, and the silence had weight.

The child opened her eyes. The storm Corvane had brewed out of stolen years was gone; the clouds hung ordinary and gray, wrung out, drifting east toward the sea. Below, Vellharrow lay stunned. She could see the whole of it from up here—the crooked streets, the smoke still rising from Tanner's Row, the tiny figures pouring out of doorways and staring upward at a sky that no longer hurt to look at. Somewhere in the crowd, people were beginning to make sounds. At first she thought it was fear. Then she understood it was the opposite: ten years of held breath leaving ten thousand chests at once.

Bren knelt nearby, soot-streaked, his scribe's hands shaking as he pressed them flat against the spire's stones as if checking whether the world was still real. Tammas sat slumped against the shattered remains of Corvane's conduit-engine, one arm cradled wrong against his chest, alive, breathing, his face turned up toward the healed sky with an expression the child had no human word for yet. Serevan stood at the spire's edge, his warden's cloak stirring in the wind, looking not at the city but at the seam in the air where the Otherworld showed through—a ribbon of silver light, thin as a scar, steady now. His long face was wet. He did not wipe it.

And Elodie Yarrow stood in the center of the sigil's fading ring, exactly where the rite had left her.

She was upright. Her eyes were open. The golden threads that had poured out of her were gone, spent, woven into something larger than any of them would ever fully see. Her dark hair moved gently in the wind. Her hands hung loose at her sides, palms forward, the way a person stands when they have set down something very heavy and are not sure their arms know how to hold anything again.

"Elodie." Bren scrambled up, crossed the distance between them, caught her shoulders. "Elodie, it worked. Do you hear me? It *worked*. The veil—it's whole, it's—"

She looked at him.

The child watched Bren's face change, watched hope curdle there in real time, and felt something in her own small chest crack along a seam she hadn't known she had.

There was nothing behind Elodie's eyes. Not emptiness exactly—that would have been simpler, crueler in a clean way. It was more like standing outside a house at night where every lamp has been carried out room by room, and knowing, from the shape of the windows, that the rooms are still there. Still furnished. Still full of everything they ever held. Just—dark. Uninhabited. No one home who knows their own address.

"I'm sorry," Elodie said, and her voice was her voice, warm and low and roughened by smoke, which made it infinitely worse. "Have we met?"

Bren made a sound the child had never heard a grown man make.

"You're hurt," Elodie went on, gently, reasonably, reaching up to touch the cut on his brow with fingers that remembered how to be kind even when they no longer remembered why. "You should sit down. There was a battle, I think—I can see that much. Are the others safe?" Her gaze drifted past him, found Tammas, found Serevan, found the child, and rested on each of them in turn with the polite, distant attention of a stranger taking inventory of a room. "I don't know my name," she said, almost apologetically. "I keep reaching for it and there's just... space."

The child ran to her.

She didn't decide to. Her legs simply took her, across the scorched stone, through the last drifting motes of sigil-light, and she threw her arms around Elodie's waist and pressed her face into the familiar smell of her—charcoal and dried lavender and the faint copper tang of old magic, all of it still there, all of it *her*, and none of it hers anymore.

Elodie's hand came up and settled on the child's hair. Automatic. Tender. Empty.

"There now," she murmured. "There now, little one. You're safe."

She did not know her. She comforted her anyway, the way one comforts any frightened child anywhere, and the child understood, with a clarity that felt like being flayed, that this was what remained: not Elodie Yarrow, not the woman who had hidden her under floorboards and traded her mentor's token and given up everything, but the shape kindness leaves behind when the person is poured out of it.

* * *

They brought her down from the spire slowly, the way you carry something breakable.

Corvane they brought down differently. The child watched from Bren's hip as they led the magistrate through his own ruined hall, and she studied him with the fierce, unsentimental attention of someone who had run from this man's hunters across two worlds and intended never to be afraid of him again.

He had been magnificent an hour ago—robed in siphoned years, his skin smooth as a man half his age, power leaking off him in visible coils. Now the conduits were broken glass around his feet and the debt had come due all at once. She watched it take him as they walked. It moved up his body like frost climbing a window: his hands spotted and knuckled, his spine curled, his thick dark hair going gray at the temples and then white at the roots, decade after stolen decade reclaiming itself in the space of a hundred steps. By the time they reached the doors he was only an old man, tall still, straight-backed by habit rather than strength, his eyes sunken into a face suddenly honest about its seventy-odd years.

He did not struggle. That frightened the child more than struggling would have. He looked out at the crowd gathered in the square below the spire—at the inspectors who had served him, at the shopkeepers whose shops they'd burned, at Bren and Tammas and herself—and his expression was not rage. It was bewilderment, deep and genuine, the look of a steward returning to find his estate burned and honestly unable to understand how anyone could have thought fire was the wrong choice.

"It was falling apart," he said, to no one, to everyone, his voice cracked and papery. "The covenant. The veil. Someone had to *manage* it. You've let a child and a charm-seller decide the fate of two realms." He shook his head slowly. "History will want to know who was actually protecting whom."

"No one was protecting anyone," Bren said quietly. "That was the whole problem. You hoarded instead of mediated. That's not stewardship, Corvane. That's just theft with better handwriting."

They took him to the magistrate's own cells—the irony of it was not lost on anyone—to await trial before whatever government might coalesce from the wreckage of the old one. As they led him away he passed close to the child, and he stopped, and he looked down at her for a long moment. She stared back, unblinking, the way she had learned to stare in the Otherworld, where eye contact among wardens was a language of its own.

"They'll put it right," he said at last, and she could not tell if it was a threat or a prayer. "Someone always thinks they can put it right better than a bargain."

Then he was gone, an ordinary old man shuffling toward an ordinary cell, and the child discovered she felt nothing at all about him, and thought perhaps that was its own kind of victory.

* * *

In the days that followed, the city changed faster than the child would have believed possible.

Magic did not come flooding back—she learned quickly that it didn't work that way, that the new covenant was a window with a latch, not an open gate—but it began, tentatively, to *seep*. Charm-shops reopened with charms that actually charmed. A midwife on Cooper's Lane worked a warming-sigil over a failing newborn while the mother wept and the neighbors stood witness in the doorway, and no guild-inspectors came, because there were no guild-inspectors anymore; the ones who surrendered wept harder than anyone, the child noticed, as though they had been waiting ten years for permission to stop pretending. In the market square, an old woman who had once been a Weaver's apprentice traced a mending-glyph in the air above a cracked wellhead, hesitated, terrified—and then the glyph held, glowing soft as candlelight, and three hundred people cheered loud enough to startle pigeons off the magistrate's roof.

The Veiled Market emerged blinking into legitimacy, its half-blooded smugglers suddenly brokers and ambassadors, strutting through the daylight with their veils pushed back, showing their otherworld-touched faces to anyone who cared to look. Most people looked. Some even bowed.

Serevan came and went through the seam like a man visiting a convalescent relative. The Otherworld was healing too, he told them, in his careful, formal Common Tongue—the silver grass growing back where the blight had crept, the wardens' dying cities steadying. Not saved. But no longer drowning. He always looked at the child when he said it, and then, increasingly, he looked at the blank-eyed woman sitting quietly by the shop window, and his long face did something complicated that the child was only beginning to learn how to read.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that sat in the middle of every celebration like a stone in a shoe.

The woman who had been Elodie Yarrow had come back to the charm shop on Candlemaker's Row because there was nowhere else for her to go, and because Bren and Tammas had agreed, in the exhausted private conference the child had not been meant to overhear, that familiarity might be kinder than novelty. So she swept the shop. She arranged the trinkets on their shelves with an instinct that made Tammas go quiet and turn away. She asked customers sensible questions and gave sensible answers and smiled the exact smile that had sold a decade of harmless charms, and every night she asked, politely, where she slept, and every morning she woke and asked, politely, whose shop this was.

"She remembers *how*," Bren said one evening, his voice low in the back room while the woman watered the window plants—lavender, of course; she had chosen lavender without knowing why. "Everything but the *who*. The craft's all still in her hands. It's only her that got taken out."

"Will it come back?" the child asked.

It was the first time she had asked a question that long in human speech, and both men went still, and Tammas put his big blacksmith's hand on her head, very gently, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking.

"I don't know, little miss," he said. "I truly don't."

* * *

On the seventh evening, the child sat with her in the shop after closing.

This had become their ritual, though the woman didn't know it was a ritual; she only knew that the small girl liked to sit on the counter and watch her work, and she was patient with that, endlessly patient, kind in the general direction of the child without ever aiming the kindness at the person she had been to her. Tonight the woman was restringing a bracelet of river-stones, her fingers moving with a fluency that had nothing to do with memory, and the lamplight caught the fine lines of her face, and the child looked at her and ached in a way that had no words in either of her languages.

"Do you dream?" the child asked.

The woman's fingers paused. "Sometimes," she said. "Strange ones. There's a man in them. Older. He's teaching me something—I can never see what. And there's a floor that opens up, and I'm putting things underneath it, keeping them safe, and I wake up before I see whose hands are doing the keeping." She laughed softly, embarrassed. "Silly. Dreams are just the mind tidying itself. Bren tells me I used to say that all the time."

"You did," the child said. "You say it exactly the same."

The woman looked at her then—really looked, the way she hadn't yet, the way she looked at nothing—and something flickered across her face. Not recognition. The ghost of a shape recognition might someday fit into.

"You speak strangely," she said. "Not wrongly. Strangely. Like the words come from further away than your mouth." She tilted her head. "Who taught you?"

"You did," the child said. "A little. And a warden. And mostly myself, running."

"Running from what?"

"From everyone," the child said. "Until someone hid me under her floor and stopped running with me."

The woman was quiet for a long moment. Outside, Vellharrow's evening bells rang—new bells, recast, ringing freely for the first time in ten years—and the sound washed over the little shop like water. The woman's hand rose, absently, to her own collarbone, where beneath the skin the child could see it, faint as banked embers: the old sigil-marks, the ones that had burned the first rainy night, dimmed now but not dead. Sleeping. Waiting.

"That sounds like a good person," the woman said at last, slowly, as if testing each word for load-bearing strength. "The one who hid you. I hope she's somewhere safe."

The child slid off the counter. She took the woman's hand—the hand that had signed her death-warrant of a bargain, the hand that had braided her hair, the hand that had held a god's-name of golden thread and let it pour away—and she pressed it flat against her own small chest, over her heart, where the fragment of the covenant-sigil still lived.

"She's here," the child said. "You just can't see yourself yet. That's all. Everything else came back. I think you will too."

The woman stared down at their joined hands. Under her skin, so faintly that the child might have imagined it, the ember-marks warmed—one degree, maybe less, the difference between ash and ash-that-isn't-quite-cold.

"I don't know why," the woman said quietly, "but when you do that, I feel like I'm being *remembered*. Isn't that a strange thing to say." She shook her head, smiled her shopkeeper's smile, and reached for the next bracelet. "Now. Tell me your name, little one. You've never told me, and I find I mind that more than I can explain."

The child climbed back onto the counter, settled herself, and looked at the woman who had given up her name so that names could mean something again.

"My name," she said, in the First Tongue first, and then, carefully, in the language of Vellharrow, "is Wren. And tomorrow, if you like, I'll tell you the story of a woman called Elodie Yarrow. From the beginning. As many times as it takes."

Outside, the bells finished ringing, and the veil-light along the horizon glimmered steady as a kept promise, and somewhere far below the floorboards of the world, the old sigil turned over in its sleep and dreamed, perhaps, of coming home.
