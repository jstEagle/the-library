## 21. A Stranger in Her Own Shop

The guildhall smelled of fresh-cut timber and old ghosts.

Bren stood in the doorway of what had been the Weaver's Guild meetinghouse for thirty years, then a granary, then a ruin, and now—Gods help them all—a guildhall again. Carpenters swarmed the rafters like patient birds. Sunlight fell through gaps where the roof was not yet mended, laying bright bars across the floor, and in one of those bars of light sat the child, cross-legged, teaching three apprentices to draw a sigil that would keep damp out of mortar.

She had a name now. They'd given her one at the first gathering: Wren, because she was small and loud when she chose to be and because Elodie—because the woman who had been Elodie Yarrow—had once said, in a story Bren told her over and over, that wrens nested in walls and sang anyway.

Wren looked up as he entered. She didn't wave. She just watched him with those dark, unchildlike eyes that had seen a realm die, and gave him the smallest nod—the nod of a colleague, not a child—and went back to her work.

Bren's chest did the thing it always did now, the tight folding-in, when he thought about how much weight those small shoulders carried. First member of the refounded Weaver's Guild. The living key who had chosen, at ten years old or however old she was, to stay on this side of the veil rather than go home to a dying world, because someone had refused to let her pay for adults' failures.

He crossed the hall, nodded to Tammas—who was arguing with a carpenter about load-bearing walls with the same ferocity he'd once argued about forge-temperatures—and climbed the narrow stair to the rooms above the shop.

* * *

The shop below the rooms had reopened a month ago. It sold charms again, real ones now, mediated through the guild's new covenant—small workings, honest workings, each one anchored by a licensed mediator's mark. The woman upstairs could not make them anymore. But she still swept the floor every morning, arranged the trinkets in their cases with an instinct that made Bren's throat ache, and greeted customers with a smile that was warm and empty as a lamp with no flame.

She was at the window now, the one overlooking Tanner's Row, watching the city rebuild.

Vellharrow had changed since the spire fell. You could see it from here: scaffolding on half the rooftops, the magistrate's tower stripped of its conduits and hung with banners of the provisional council, and everywhere, everywhere, people working magic openly for the first time in ten years. A lamplighter down the street touched each wick with a fire-sigil instead of a taper. Two women on the corner were arguing cheerfully about whether a mending-charm could fix a cracked foundation or only soothe it. Children chalked sigils on the cobbles—not forbidden ones, not dangerous ones, just little charms against rain, against bad luck, against sour milk—and no inspectors came.

There were no inspectors anymore. There was a guild instead, and oaths, and oversight, and Wren at the center of it all like a small dark sun.

The woman turned when Bren came up the stairs. Her face did the thing it always did: a flicker of polite inquiry, the briefest furrow, as if his face were a word in a language she'd almost learned once and forgotten.

"Good morning," she said. "You're early."

"I'm always early," Bren said. "You've told me so. Repeatedly."

"Have I?" She smiled, rueful, self-deprecating. "I seem to have opinions about everything I can't remember."

"You do." He set down the basket he carried—bread, cheese, the good tea from the Veiled Market, which had survived Corvane's fall and rebranded itself, with breathtaking shamelessness, as a legitimate trading concern. "It's one of your best qualities. Opinions. You once told Tammas his hinges were an insult to the concept of doors."

"That sounds like me," she said, and laughed, and the laugh was hers, exactly hers, and it cut him worse than the silence ever had. "Though I don't know how 'me' is supposed to feel. Everyone keeps telling me stories. I'm starting to think you're all making me up."

"Only a little," Bren said. "Mostly we're remembering you badly."

* * *

They had a system, he and Tammas. Unspoken, worked out in the first terrible weeks when the blank-eyed woman had wandered into the street at dawn looking for a shop she didn't know she owned, and Tammas had found her there, and neither of them had slept properly since.

Tammas handled the practical things: keys, ledgers, the names of streets, which door stuck, where the good tea came from. He was patient the way forge-work is patient, hammering the same gentle facts day after day until something took shape. The woman trusted him fastest. Bren thought it was because Tammas never looked at her like she was a ghost. He looked at her like she was a customer who'd misplaced her purse—annoyed, fond, entirely unsentimental.

Bren handled the stories.

It had started as necessity. Doctors—from the Otherworld, actually, gray-robed wardens who had come through the healed veil to study what Elodie had done—told him that memory lost to a sacrifice like hers did not return, but that a person was more than memory. That identity could regrow around the stories others told, like a pearl around grit. So Bren told her stories. Every day. Over breakfast, over tea, walking her through the market while she gripped his arm and stared at a city she had saved and could not recognize.

He told her about the purge, carefully, gently: about the night the inspectors came, about the mentor whose token had arrived in a mute child's hands, about ten years of selling hollow charms and hating himself for the safety of it.

He told her about the rain-lashed night a girl appeared at her door speaking words that burned.

He told her about the drowned chapel, and the flooded memories of a dead man, and the warning written in the guild's oldest wall: *every renewal requires a willing sacrifice of selfhood.*

He told her about the spire, and the storm, and the moment she had walked into the veil's wound and offered up her name like a coin spent gladly.

And every time, she listened the way she listened to everything—attentive, kind, slightly apart, as though hearing about a heroine in a novel whose fate concerned her but did not belong to her.

"That was very brave," she said this morning, when he finished the part about the drowned chapel. "This Elodie. Do you think she knew what it would cost?"

"She knew," Bren said. "Her mentor warned her. She went anyway."

"And you loved her."

It wasn't a question. Bren looked out the window at the lamplighter doing his rounds, and his eyes stung, and he let them.

"Yes," he said. "We all did. She was the sort of person you love without deciding to."

The woman reached across the table and patted his hand—kindly, clumsily, the way you comfort a stranger at a funeral. "She must have been remarkable."

"She was," Bren said. "She is."

* * *

Some days were harder than others.

Hard days were the ones when something surfaced—when she hummed a tune she couldn't name and stopped mid-note, stricken; when she picked up a charm-brush and her hand moved in patterns her mind had never learned; when she called Bren by a name that wasn't his, some ghost-name from a decade of friendship, and then apologized, mortified, for the mistake.

On those days Tammas would appear with some errand, loudly invented, and give her something to do with her hands. And Bren would sit with her in the evening and tell her the story of that particular ghost, whatever it was, stitching it back onto her like a button sewn with the wrong-colored thread—functional, visible, wrong, but holding.

Today, after breakfast, she stood at the counter downstairs arranging a tray of luck-knots, and suddenly said, without turning around:

"The knots go red on the outside. Don't they."

Bren froze on the stair. "What?"

"Red outside, white inside. For luck." Her hands had already begun re-weaving one, quick and sure, fingers moving on knowledge her head didn't own. "I don't know why I know that."

"Elodie taught half the market that," Bren said quietly. "It was the first honest charm she ever sold. Before the guild fell. Red for blood, white for breath. Luck is just life, she used to say, worn on the outside where people can see it."

The woman looked down at the knot in her hands. For a long moment her face was very still, and Bren saw something move behind her eyes—not memory, nothing so kind, but the shape where memory had been, the scar-tissue of it.

"Luck is just life," she repeated. She said it like a woman testing whether a bridge would hold her weight.

Then she smiled at him, and set the knot in the case, and asked him to help with the inventory, and the moment passed, the way all such moments passed: leaving behind not grief exactly, but its cousin. Hope's quieter, sadder sibling.

* * *

In the afternoon, Wren came.

She came most days, officially to inspect the shop's mediation-marks, unofficially because this had been her home before it was anything else, and Bren suspected because of what happened when she and the woman were in the same room.

The woman always lit up when Wren arrived. Not recognition—never that—but something older than recognition, some thread the sacrifice hadn't found to cut. She would abandon her ledger mid-entry, fuss over the girl, press sweets on her, ask about her studies with genuine hunger. And Wren would sit on the high stool behind the counter—her stool, though neither of them knew why it was hers—and let herself be fussed over with a gravity far beyond her years.

Today the woman was showing her a new stock of river-glass charms when Wren said, casually, in the First Tongue:

*"The knot is wrong. White should lead."*

The woman's hands stopped.

She didn't turn around. She stood there with a piece of river-glass in her palm, and Bren, watching from the doorway, saw her lips move—shaping sounds in a language she had never been taught, answering before her mind could stop her mouth:

*"White leads. Always. I—"*

She caught herself. Looked down at the glass. Looked at Wren. Laughed, uncertain, delighted, unsettled. "What language was that? It felt like—it felt like remembering a dream. Like the word for something was right there and then it wasn't."

"It's an old language," Wren said, in Vellharrow speech, solemn as a judge. "Very old. Some people have ears for it."

"Do you think I might?" the woman asked. Eager. Wistful. "Learn it, I mean. It felt like it fit somewhere."

Wren looked at her for a long time. Bren saw the child weighing something—the same look she'd had atop the spire, the same look she'd had in the drowned chapel, ancient and careful and ten years old.

"I think," Wren said finally, "that some things written on a person don't wash off. Even when the person can't read them anymore." She slid off the stool. "I'll teach you. If you want. One word a day."

"Oh," the woman said softly. "Yes. Please."

* * *

That evening, Bren and Tammas walked her home—or walked her to the rooms above the shop, which was home now, though she still sometimes paused at the top of the stairs with the expression of a guest checking which room was hers.

Tammas lit the lamps. The woman puttered, contented, putting away the tea things in places that were slowly becoming hers by habit if not by memory. And when she'd gone to bed, the two men sat in the kitchen with the last of the good tea, and neither spoke for a while.

"She almost answered today," Bren said at last. "In the First Tongue. Did you hear?"

"I heard." Tammas turned his cup. The lamplight caught the burn-scars on his knuckles, old ones, from a forge and from a war nobody talked about. "The doctors say it doesn't come back. What she gave."

"They said the covenant couldn't be reforged, too. They said a lot of things." Bren rubbed his eyes. "One word a day, Wren says. You know what happens if a sigil gets fed even a trickle? It wakes up. It remembers its shape. I've read the old texts—I helped bury them, Gods forgive me, but I read them first. A name isn't ink, Tammas. It's a pattern. And patterns can be redrawn."

Tammas was quiet a long moment. "And if it works? If you're right, and the sigil under her skin starts waking up? What comes back, Bren? All of it? Or just enough to know what's missing?" His voice roughened. "I held her hand through the worst year of her life and she doesn't remember my name without being told. If she wakes halfway—if she spends the rest of her days reaching for something on the other side of a wall—"

"Then we'll be there," Bren said. "Both sides of the wall. That's the whole point of us."

Tammas snorted, but it was a wet sound, and he covered it with his cup.

Upstairs, faintly, they heard the woman moving in her sleep. And beneath it—so soft that later Bren would convince himself he'd imagined it—something else. A hum. Three notes, rising, falling, rising again. The opening phrase of a lullaby that a mentor had sung to a frightened apprentice twenty years ago, in a shop that sold charms without teeth, on a street that had not yet learned to burn.

Tammas heard it too. He set down his cup very carefully.

"Bren," he said. "Go get Wren."
