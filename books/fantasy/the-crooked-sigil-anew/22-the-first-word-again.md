## 22. The First Word Again

The shop smelled of cedar oil and rain.

Wren knew every smell of the charm shop now—the way she knew the creak of its third floorboard, the way she knew which shutter stuck and had to be lifted with a hip, the way she knew that the woman behind the counter would hum three notes of a song she could not name and then stop, puzzled, as though the melody had walked out of her mouth without permission. Wren had watched her do it a hundred times. She counted the notes each time. Always three. Never four.

It was spring again, or close to it. The gutters of Vellharrow ran loud with meltwater, and the veil—thin now, permeable, a membrane instead of a wall—let through light that did not quite belong to this world. It pooled in corners. It made the dust motes drift in slow spirals, like things deciding where to land. The city had learned to live beneath that light the way a person learns to live beside a river: carefully at first, then without thinking, then with love.

Wren stood in the doorway a moment before entering, because she liked to see the woman first from outside, the way a stranger would.

The nameless one was arranging charms on the counter's velvet cloth. Her hands moved with a sureness that surprised people who did not know her. They thought, seeing the blankness in her eyes when they asked about her past, that everything must be blank in her—that she was an empty room. But her hands remembered. Her hands sorted true-iron rings from plated ones without looking. Her hands tied knots in red thread that held. Sometimes, when a customer described an ache or a fear, her fingers would drift to exactly the right shelf, to exactly the right charm, before her mind caught up—and then she would frown faintly, as if she'd heard someone whisper from another room.

Bren said that was Elodie still living in her body, like heat left in a hearthstone hours after the fire went out. Tammas said it was simpler than that: you cannot unmake what a person *did*, only what they *knew*. The knowing was gone. The doing remained.

Wren, who was eleven now by the counting of this world and older than that by the counting of hers, believed both things and neither. What Wren believed was written in the First Tongue, and there were no words in any human language for it yet. She was working on that. She was working on many things.

She pushed open the door. The bell above it rang—a real bell now, guild-forged, its voice carrying a thread of the Otherworld's resonance. Everyone in Vellharrow had bells like it. That was the covenant's new shape: not silence, not flood, but a door left ajar, with rules scratched into the frame on both sides.

"Good morning," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, pleasant the way weather is pleasant. "You're early."

"I'm always early," Wren said.

"You're always early." A small smile touched her mouth, automatic, unexamined. It was one of the strange griefs of loving her: the smiles came easily but arrived from nowhere, like birds landing on a wire. "Mind the wet on the floor. I mopped."

Wren minded the wet. She set down her satchel—the one Bren had given her, too big for her, straps doubled over—and began the morning work that had become her liturgy: sweeping, sorting, refilling the jar of salt that was not quite salt. Through the front window she could see Tanner's Row waking up, the smoke of breakfast fires rising straight in the windless air, and beyond the rooftops, far off, the pale gleam of Corvane's spire. It was being taken apart stone by stone. The magistrate himself sat in a cell below the Hall of Records, an ordinary old man now, his stolen years falling off him like leaves out of season. Wren had seen him once, through the bars, during the trial. He had looked at her for a long time. He had not looked angry. He had looked like a man reading a ledger and finding, at last, that the sums did not balance and never had. She had felt no triumph. Only a cold sort of pity, and the memory of Tammas's ruined hands, and the knowledge that some debts are collected by time itself and need no avengers.

But that was not today's work. Today's work was smaller, and closer, and it frightened her more than spires ever had.

* * *

The morning passed in its ordinary music. A fishwife came in for a charm against gull-theft; the woman sold her one and told her, unprompted, to hang it high, and the fishwife laughed and said how did you know I hang them low, and the woman paused, hand flat on the counter, and said, "I don't know," in a voice with a hairline crack in it. Then the moment passed, the way all such moments passed, dissolving back into the day like sugar into tea.

A boy bought a luck-knot for his sister's wedding. An old man stood a long while over the shelf of mourning-charms and finally bought nothing, and the woman let him stand, and did not hurry him, and something in the gentleness of that made Wren's chest ache.

At midday, when the street emptied for the meal hour, the woman ate bread and honey standing up, the way she always did, as if sitting were a habit belonging to someone else. Wren ate with her. They did not talk much. Talking was not the point of them; it had never been the point of them, even before.

Then the woman wiped her hands, and looked at the afternoon's quiet, and said, "There's a crate in the back I can't shift. Help me?"

Wren's heart began to beat very fast, because there was no crate. There had been no crate for six days running. Every day the woman invented a reason to take her into the back room, and every day Wren invented a reason to refuse, and the two of them circled each other like dancers who had forgotten the steps but remembered the music.

Today, Wren decided, she would stop circling.

"All right," she said.

* * *

The back room was small and warm, lit by one window whose glass had been replaced after the battle—everyone in Vellharrow had some glass replaced after the battle—and it held shelves of jars, bundles of dried herbs, the smell of cedar and old paper. In the corner stood a crate that was, in fact, real, though nothing heavier than feathers inside it. The woman had built the fiction carefully. Wren almost loved her for the clumsiness of it.

They knelt by the crate and pretended to shift it. The woman's sleeve rode up her forearm, and there it was: the sigil, faint as breath on glass, the crooked mark that had once blazed across her skin like a brand of office and now showed only when the light came from the Otherworld side—which, these days, was most of the time, if you knew how to look.

Wren looked.

The woman caught her looking and drew the sleeve down, quick, embarrassed. "Old scar," she said. "I don't remember from what."

"It's not a scar," Wren said.

"No," the woman agreed mildly. "I suppose it isn't."

And there it was—the door, standing open, the way it had stood open for months while Wren stood on the threshold gathering courage the way other children gathered stones.

Because here was the thing Wren had learned in the drowned chapel and the undercity tunnels and atop the broken spire, in the wind between two worlds: that the covenant demanded a life freely given, and Elodie Yarrow had given hers, and the giving had been complete. Name, memories, self—all of it poured into the sigil like water into cracked earth. The rite had taken everything offered. No one had lied about that. No one had promised otherwise. When the light faded and the veil healed and the sky closed its wound, the woman standing in the ruin had turned to them with polite, empty courtesy and asked who they were, and Wren had understood, in the oldest part of herself, in the tongue that predated kingdoms, that a bargain kept is not the same as a bargain undone.

But Wren had also learned something else, in the months since. She had learned it from watching the woman tie knots she should not have known, and hum songs cut off at three notes, and reach for the right shelf before the wrong memory could stop her. She had learned it from Serevan, who visited across the veil twice a season now and spoke of the Otherworld's slow greening with wonder in his gray voice. She had learned it from the sigil-stone itself, which sat in the re-founded guildhall and sang, very softly, to anyone willing to listen.

Some selves cannot be fully erased. Not because the rite failed—but because a self is not only what is remembered. A self is also what is *given*. And Elodie Yarrow had given herself away so completely, so freely, that the giving had become a kind of keeping. The sacrifice had been the largest act of her life. The rite could burn the name off her, but the shape of the burning—that was hers forever.

Wren meant to tell her this. Wren had rehearsed it in human words a hundred times, in the First Tongue a thousand. But kneeling there in the cedar-smelling dark, she found that words of either kind were too small. So she did what she had done the first night, rain-lashed and mute at a stranger's door. She reached out and took the woman's hand.

The woman went still.

Her hand was warm and work-hardened and slightly sticky with honey, and it lay in Wren's two hands like a bird that hadn't decided whether to be afraid. Wren bowed her head over it, the way her mother had taught her—over the hand, over the wrist, over the pulse—and she spoke.

Not human speech. Not yet. She spoke the First Tongue, the language that bound the covenant before there were kingdoms to break it, the language the veil itself was woven in. She spoke quietly, the words falling like water drops into a well:

*I know what you paid. I know what was taken. I am the debt you paid it for, and I have come to say: it was not wasted. Nothing was wasted. Listen.*

The lamp flame leaned. The dust motes stopped their drifting and hung, suspended, attentive. Outside, the meltwater in the gutters seemed to hush. Somewhere far off, in the guildhall across the city, Wren would learn later, the sigil-stone rang once, clear as a struck cup, though no hand had touched it.

And under Wren's fingers, in the woman's wrist, the pulse changed.

It did not quicken. It *answered*. One beat, then a second, slightly offset, like a second voice joining a song—and warmth rose up through the woman's arm, and Wren watched it climb, watched the faint crooked sigil emerge along the forearm like a path appearing under snowmelt, line by line, glowing soft gold, alive.

The woman made a sound. Small. Wounded. Wondering.

"What—" she whispered. Her free hand pressed flat against her own sternum, as though checking that the house of her was still standing. "What is—there's something—"

*Don't be afraid,* Wren said in the First Tongue, and then, because she had promised herself, she said it in the human tongue too, her voice cracking on the human words the way a young bird's wings crack on first flight: "Don't be afraid. It's me. It's only me."

The woman looked at her. And here was the thing Wren would carry for the rest of her long life, the thing she would tell the guild's new members years later when they asked why they did this work: the woman's eyes were still blank—still the eyes of someone with no past, no name, no map of herself. The rite had not been cheated. Nothing had been given back.

But behind the blankness, deep down, like a lamp in a house where all the shutters are closed, something had *turned toward the light.*

"I don't know you," the woman said slowly. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "I don't—I look at you and I don't know you." Her brow furrowed. Her grip on Wren's hand tightened, though, tightened as if the hand had opinions the mind had not approved. "But when you speak that way—when you speak *that*—"

She stopped. Swallowed.

"When you speak that," she said, "it feels like coming home to a house I've never seen."

Wren burst into tears. She was eleven, and she had faced wardens and hunters and the end of two worlds, and she cried now, ugly and hiccupping, and the woman—bewildered, alarmed, moved by an instinct older than her own erasure—pulled her close and held her, one hand cradling the back of Wren's head, and rocked her slightly, and murmured, "Hush, hush, little one, hush," in a cadence that no one had taught her, in a tenderness that had survived the burning of everything else.

They stayed that way a long time. The lamp righted itself. The dust resumed its falling. Out on Tanner's Row, the city went on with its afternoon, bells ringing their double-toned ring, the veil-light pooling gold in the streets, the world remade and ordinary and good.

* * *

That evening, Bren came by with supper, and Tammas with him, walking slowly, leaning on his new cane, complaining cheerfully about the damp in his joints the way he complained about everything, which was to say, with love. They found the woman and Wren sitting together at the counter, not talking, the woman's bare forearm resting on the wood, the sigil still faintly visible, still warm.

Bren stopped in the doorway with the supper basket hanging forgotten from his fist. Tammas said nothing at all, which from Tammas was thunderous.

"It answered her," Wren said. "When I spoke. It answered."

The woman looked around at them—at these men she did not know, who visited her weekly and told her stories of a person called Elodie Yarrow, stories she received the way one receives tales of a stranger, politely, with occasional puzzlement. Now she looked at them differently. Now there was a question in her face that had never been there before.

"The stories you tell me," she said. "About her. About Elodie." Her fingers traced the crooked mark on her arm, slow, wondering. "Tell me the one about the spire again. And—" she hesitated, and Wren saw her gather herself, saw her reach for words the way a swimmer reaches for a rope, "—and afterward. Tell me what happened *afterward.* All of it. Everything you know about her."

Tammas's eyes shone. Bren set down the basket with great care, as though it might break, or as though he might.

"Everything?" he said.

"Everything," the woman said. "I want—" She frowned, searching, and then found it, and the finding lit her face like dawn through a shutter. "I want to know what I'm growing back into."

Outside, the first stars were coming out, and among them, low over the western hills, burned the soft green star that was not a star at all but the nearest gate of the Otherworld, shining steady, shining home. Wren took the woman's hand again—she would take it every day for years, and the woman would let her, and gradually, gradually, the flicker would become a flame, the flame a hearth, the hearth a self—not the old self, never that, but a new weaving on the old loom, crooked sigil and all.

And in the First Tongue, the language of beginnings, Wren said the word her mother had used to open every door, every bargain, every morning of her vanished life.

It meant, roughly: *again.*

The woman smiled—four notes of the song humming out of her, unbidden, before she caught herself and laughed, puzzled and pleased, at whatever had just slipped loose.

"Again," she repeated, in accented but perfect First Tongue, though she could not possibly have known what she was saying.

Some selves cannot be fully erased. Some are only waiting, patient as seeds under snow, for the first word of spring.
