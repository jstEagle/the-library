## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Things

The rain had been falling on the guildhall for three days, and Felix Galloway had catalogued forty-one relics in that time, none of which anyone would ever buy.

He worked by lamplight in the long gallery, where the shelves rose to the smoke-darkened rafters and the dust had its own weather. Item forty-one was a birdcage made of knucklebones, wired together with hair fine as spider silk. The ledger said it had been traded up from the fen-country sixty years ago by a Keeper named Aldous Merrin, who had drowned two years later in a river that did not appear on any map. Felix wrote *provenance uncertain; contents absent* beneath the entry, because the cage was empty and always had been, and yet when he held it near the lamp he could hear—very faintly, very politely—a small sound like a bird shifting its weight.

Impossible things. That was what the Ledgerkeepers dealt in. Or had dealt in, once, when there were buyers in the free cities who wanted bottled thunder and the last breath of a saint and mirrors that remembered faces their owners had lost. Now the cities bought iron and grain and soldiers, and the guildhall of the Ledgerkeepers stood at the crook of the river like an old man everyone had stopped visiting.

Felix set the cage back on its shelf and blew out a breath that fogged the lamp glass.

"You're still doing it wrong," said Corvane from the shadows at the end of the gallery.

Guildmaster Corvane sat where he always sat these days: in the great chair behind the receiving desk, which had been built for a man twice his size and now swallowed him entirely. There was a bottle beside him—not wine, not quite—and a cup he kept forgetting to fill, or filling and forgetting to drink, so that the surface trembled with his pulse. He was watching Felix with eyes that had once been famous for their sharpness and were now famous for nothing at all, since no one came to see them.

"Doing what wrong?" Felix asked.

"The cataloguing." Corvane gestured with two fingers, vague and dismissive. "You write as though the things matter. *Contents absent.* As if absence is a fact worth recording."

"Absence is the only fact most of them have left."

Corvane smiled at that, or something moved across his face that had once been a smile, and he turned his cup a quarter-turn on the desk. "Your master Merrin used to say the same thing. Then he went looking for one of those absences and the river took him. Write less, boy. Live longer."

"I'm not your boy."

"No," Corvane agreed, and looked out the tall window at the rain. "You're the last apprentice I have, which is worse for you."

That was true, and they both let it be true, and the silence settled over the gallery like the dust. Felix went back to his work because work was the only thing that kept the loneliness from pooling around his ankles. He catalogued item forty-two, a key that unlocked nothing but grew warm near lies. Item forty-three, a letter written in a hand that changed depending on who read it. Item forty-four, a jar of darkness labeled *harvested at dusk, use sparingly.*

Forty-four relics in three days. In the old ledgers, the great Keepers had recorded forty-four in an afternoon, and then gone out into the world to trade them. Felix had never known the guild alive. He had been taken in at nine years old, a foundling with ink-stained fingers and no name anyone claimed, and he had loved the hall with the fierce, unrequited love of a child for a dying parent. He knew every shelf, every creaking stair, every ledger in the archive—the Red Ledger of debts, the White of payments, the Grey of things neither given nor taken. And he knew, though he had never been permitted to open it, that somewhere below the archive there was a Black Ledger whose entries even Corvane did not read aloud.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere in the walls, water found a new path and began to drip.

* * *

It was near midnight when the knocking started.

Not knocking, exactly. A sound like knuckles on wood, but slower, more hesitant, the way you'd knock on a door you weren't sure existed. Felix's pen stopped mid-word. Corvane's head came up off his chest, and for a moment—just a moment—the old sharpness was back in his eyes, and Felix saw the man he must have been before the bottle and the empty hall.

"Expecting someone?" Corvane asked.

"No one's expected anything here in ten years."

The knock came again. Three times. Patient. Small.

Felix crossed the gallery floor, past the shelves of impossible things, and put his hand on the door bar. Through the iron grille set into the oak he could see nothing but rain and dark, and then, low down, at the height of the grille's bottom edge, a face. Mud-smeared. Wide-eyed. Very young.

He lifted the bar.

The child fell through the doorway more than walked, catching herself on the frame—she couldn't have been more than eight or nine, though it was hard to tell under the mud. She wore clothes that had once been good, a merchant's daughter's coat maybe, torn at the hem and soaked black with wet. Her feet were bare and bleeding. She looked up at Felix with an expression he recognized from his own childhood mirror: the look of someone who has run until running was the only thing left, and is now waiting to find out if the world will allow her to stop.

"Please," she said.

And then she said something else.

Felix would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what happened next, and he would never manage it, because language was made for describing things, and this was a description of the describer. The syllables came out of the child's mouth—three of them, four, soft and round as river stones—and every piece of ink in the gallery *moved*. He felt it before he heard it: a crawling sensation along his forearms, in his teeth, in the wet paper of his lungs. Behind him, forty-four catalogued relics shivered on their shelves. The knucklebone cage rattled softly, like a bird waking. And in the ledger lying open on the desk, the fresh entry Felix had written for the jar of darkness began to writhe, the letters lifting off the page like insects disturbed under a stone, swimming, rearranging—

The child said one more word, and the ink lay back down.

Silence. Rain. The drip in the wall.

Felix became aware that he was gripping the doorframe hard enough to splinter it, and that Corvane was standing, actually standing, for the first time in months, with the cup forgotten and both hands flat on the desk, staring at the child the way a man stares at a ghost—or at a debt.

"What did she say?" Felix whispered.

"I don't know." But Corvane's voice had gone strange, thin and careful, the voice of a man lying about the weight of something he is carrying. "Get her dry. Get her fed. And Felix—" the guildmaster's eyes flicked to the ledger, to the ink still settling, "—don't write down a single word she says. Not one."

"Why not?"

"Because some words are worth more than we can pay."

The child swayed on her bleeding feet. Felix caught her arm without thinking, steadied her, felt how light she was, how cold, how utterly spent. She looked up at him again, and this time she did not speak the moving language. This time she simply said, in ordinary, exhausted, mud-and-rain Common:

"They took my name. I want it back."

And Felix Galloway—who had spent nineteen years cataloguing absences, who had never once been needed by anything living, who had loved a dead guild alone in the dark—felt something crack open in his chest that was not pain.

"Then you came to the right door," he heard himself say. "I don't know why yet. But you did."

Behind him, Corvane poured the cup full to the brim and drank it down in one motion, and did not pour another, and did not take his eyes off the child.
