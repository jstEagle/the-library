## 2. A Grammar of Souls

Felix did not sleep. He told himself it was the cataloguing—the third shelf of reliquaries needed recounting, and the fourth, because Master Corvane had drunk his way through two inventories' worth of accuracy in as many years—but the truth was simpler and stranger: the child was sleeping in the guildhall's east alcove, on a pallet Felix had made from a horse blanket and an old processional banner, and every time Felix closed his eyes he heard that voice again.

The language had not stopped when Wren slept. It came out in murmurs, small and round and ancient, like river stones being counted by someone who had counted them forever. And each murmured syllable made the ledger ink crawl.

That was the part Felix could not stop thinking about. Not the mud, not the hollow cheeks, not the way the child had appeared at their door at dusk as if washed up by a flood no one else could see. The ink. When Wren spoke—awake or dreaming, it made no difference—the open ledgers on the front desk shivered. Lines of inventory quivered like insects waking. Once, a column of figures had rearranged itself into a shape that looked almost like a letter, though not any letter Felix knew, and then subsided, ashamed, back into arithmetic.

He sat at the long table with a single candle and the guild's oldest reference, *Hollis on Transcription*, and read the same paragraph four times without retaining a word of it. *The transcriber must never assume the text is passive,* Hollis wrote. *All true records exert pressure upon the world. This is why we keep them, and this is why we fear them.*

Outside, rain had begun again, soft and businesslike, ticking against the shutters. The hall smelled of tallow and dust and old vellum, and beneath all of it now, faintly, of river mud.

"You should be asleep."

Corvane stood at the end of the table, holding a cup in one hand and the other hand flat against the wall to steady himself. In daylight—or candlelight—he looked worse than usual: gray at the jowls, eyes sunk into bruised sockets, the great seal ring of the Ledgerkeepers hanging loose on a finger that had grown too thin for it. He had been handsome once, Felix supposed. There were portraits.

"I couldn't," Felix said. "Master, the child—"

"Is a child." Corvane drank. His throat worked once, twice. "Foundlings turn up on doorsteps. It's practically traditional. We feed them, we send for the parish, we forget them. That is what happens next."

"The ink moved."

"Rain damp." He said it too quickly, and they both heard how quickly. Corvane set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way drunk men do when they are trying to prove they are not drunk. "Ink crawls in damp weather. Everyone knows it."

"No one knows it," Felix said. "No one has ever said that."

For a moment the only sound was the rain and the child's low, sleeping murmur from the alcove—and beneath the murmur, so faint Felix might have imagined it, a dry whisper of paper, like a page turning itself over in its sleep.

Corvane crossed the room. He did it badly, weaving slightly, but there was something underneath the weaving, something urgent, and when he reached the desk where the day-ledger lay open he put both hands flat on it as if holding down a lid. "Listen to me, boy." His breath was wine and old paper. "Whatever you think you heard tonight, whatever you think you saw—you will write nothing about it. No entry. No marginal note. Nothing."

"You're telling me not to do my work."

"I am telling you—" Corvane stopped. Felix watched him reach for a lie the way a man reaches for a tool he has dropped in the dark, grasping at several wrong things before finding one. "I am telling you that the guild cannot afford entanglements. We have three clients left. Three. If word gets out that we've taken in some—some *phenomenon*—"

"What is she?" Felix asked quietly. "What did she say?"

And here Corvane looked at him, really looked, and Felix saw something he had never seen in his master's face before, not during the audits, not during the slow years of decline: fear. Genuine, undiluted, animal fear.

"She said *hello*," Corvane said. "That's all. A greeting. In a dead language. Children babble." He picked up his cup, drained it, and walked toward the stairs with the careful dignity of a man descending a ship's ladder in a storm. At the bottom step he paused. "Lock the Black Ledger cabinet before you come up."

Then he was gone, and Felix sat alone with the candle and the whispering paper and the lie still hanging in the air, sour as spilled wine.

*A greeting. In a dead language.*

Which meant Corvane knew the language. Which meant it was not dead to him.

* * *

Morning came gray and rinsed, the rain gone, the light through the high windows the color of pewter. Wren woke early and ate bread and honey with the solemn intensity of a creature who had learned not to trust meals. Felix watched her over the rim of his own cup and tried to see her plainly—a child, seven years old perhaps, maybe eight, thin as a winter branch, with mud still dried in the creases of her knuckles and eyes the deep brown of wet oak. Plainly she was only that. But nothing about her was plain. When she finished eating she looked around the hall—at the shelves, the desks, the cabinets with their little brass plates of classification—and something in her face changed, sharpened, the way a compass needle changes when it finds north.

She slid off the bench and walked to the front desk, where yesterday's ledger still lay open, and stared at it. Then, deliberately, she reached up, took the spare quill from the stand, dipped it in the inkwell, and drew.

Not wrote. Drew. The marks came out of her whole arm, fluid and sure, spirals nested in spirals, a vertical stroke through the center like a spine, small hooks at the terminus of each line like the barbs of feathers. She worked in silence, tongue caught between her teeth, and the ink did not merely sit on the page—it *settled*, sinking into the vellum as if the page had been thirsty for it for a very long time.

Felix stood very still. The candle flames along the hall had all leaned toward her, just slightly, like grass bending under wind.

When she finished, she stepped back and looked up at him, and held out the quill, handle first. An offering. Or a question.

"Wren," Felix said carefully. "Can I—may I keep this?"

She nodded once, gravely, and returned to her bread crusts.

He waited until she was occupied folding the processional banner into what appeared to be a boat. Then he took the page she had drawn on—carefully, as one lifts a wounded bird—and carried it to the reference shelf, and began, hands shaking only a little, the work he had been trained for since he was younger than she was.

Cross-referencing.

It took him most of the morning. The sigils matched nothing in Hollis, nothing in the *Codex of Visible Scripts*, nothing in the water-damaged fragments of the *Pre-Auditorium Glossary*. But three times, as he turned pages, the ink on Wren's drawing stirred—lifted, almost, off the vellum, straining toward some other book in the room like a plant toward light. And three times Felix followed that strain to the same place: the iron-banded cabinet at the hall's far end, the one with the brass plate that read simply *SEQUESTERED*, and beneath it, in older letters nearly worn away, *THE BLACK LEDGER*.

Sealed by guild law. Openable, technically, only by the guildmaster, in the presence of two witnesses, for cause entered into the record.

Felix stood in front of the cabinet for a long time.

He thought about Corvane's face last night. He thought about the way the man's hands had pressed down on the day-ledger, holding it shut. He thought: *he has opened this. Recently. Often.* The lock's brass had been polished bright by handling, while everything around it wore gray.

He thought about duty. Duty was the first thing they taught you: *the record is the guild, and the guild is the record; a Ledgerkeeper who conceals an entry has already betrayed every entry ever made.* He had copied that sentence forty times as a boy. He had believed it the way you believe stone is hard.

And he thought about Wren, folding a banner into a boat, humming to herself in a language that made souls tremble.

His hands, when he finally raised them to the lock, were cold. The key hung on the guildmaster's ring, but Corvane had been drunk for years, and drunk men misplace things, and Felix had found the spare years ago in the false bottom of the map drawer and never told anyone, because telling anyone would have meant admitting he'd looked.

The lock gave with a click that sounded, in the silent hall, like a bone breaking.

Inside, the Black Ledger lay on a bed of oilcloth, massive as a tomb lid, bound in leather gone black and waxy with age. Its clasp was sealed with lead and the impress of the old guildmaster's signet—broken, Felix saw. Broken and resealed, hastily, more than once. The wax bore Corvane's mark now, pressed over the ruins of the old one, again and again, layers upon layers, like scar tissue.

He opened it.

The pages were heavy, dense with entries in scripts that predated the guild's own alphabet—some of them, Felix realized with a lurch, were *the* script, the spirals and spines and feather-barbs, row upon row of them, each entry annotated in newer hands. Names. The entries were names. Not written *about*, the way ordinary records named things, but written *as*—each sigil-cluster hummed faintly under his fingertips, and when he touched one his teeth ached, and behind his eyes he briefly saw a green hillside under a sun he had never stood under, smelled bread baking in a house he had never entered, heard a woman singing in—

He snatched his hand back. Sat down on the floor. Breathed.

*Each entry is a soul,* he thought, and the thought arrived not as revelation but as confirmation, the way you confirm a thing you have known in your body for a long time and refused to know in your head. *This is what the guild actually kept. This is what Corvane drinks about.*

Wren's drawing lay on the floor beside him. He compared it, line by line, to the nearest entry. The match was not exact—the child's hand was young, the spirals looser—but the structure was identical. Same grammar. Same spine-stroke. Same feathered endings. She hadn't been writing gibberish. She had been writing *her own name*, the way a person writes their name on the first page of a new book.

Except her book was missing its first page.

Felix went through the Black Ledger twice, then a third time, slower. Entries ran chronologically backward through decades. Somewhere in the last ten years there should have been a page bearing the child's sigil-cluster, or its close kin. There was nothing. Worse than nothing: there was a gap. Between one entry and the next, three leaves had been cut away with a blade, close and clean, the stubs still visible at the spine like amputated fingers. The cutting had happened recently enough that the surrounding vellum had not yet relaxed to cover the wound.

Someone had sold those pages. Someone had cut a child's name out of the book of souls and traded it away, and resealed the clasp, and layered fresh wax over the crime, and poured wine on top of it, night after night after night.

Felix sat on the cold flagstones with the Black Ledger open across his knees and felt dread arrive—not the sharp dread of danger, but the slow, settling dread of complicity. Because he understood, already, with terrible clarity, what would happen next. He would close the book. He would return it to the cabinet. He would lock it, and hang the key, and say nothing, and go upstairs and eat supper across from the man who had done this, and pour his wine, and keep the guild's accounts, and be—by silence, by habit, by love, if it was love—forfeit.

Every year of his apprenticeship, Corvane had told him the ledgers mattered. *Everything matters, boy, that is the entire point of keeping record.* Felix had thought it was wisdom. Now he heard it for what it was: a confession, repeated nightly, like a prayer to ward off exactly this moment.

Above him, faintly, Wren was singing.

Felix looked down at the cut stubs of the missing pages. Then he did the only thing a Ledgerkeeper could do—the thing his training demanded and his conscience demanded and, God forgive him, his fear also demanded.

He copied.

Quill shaking, candle burning low, he transcribed the neighboring entries, the dates, the auction-marginalia in Corvane's own cramped hand—*sold, court beneath the mountain, price remitted in full, matter concluded*—and beside them he laid Wren's drawing, matching spiral to spiral, barb to barb, building a case in ink the way other people built cases in law. When he finished, he blotted the page, folded it twice, and sewed it into the lining of his coat with thread and a sailmaker's needle, sweating despite the cold, because a record hidden was still a record, and someday, somehow, there would be an audit.

He locked the cabinet. He hung the key. He climbed the stairs past Corvane's snoring and found Wren in the east alcove, asleep at last and truly, her boat-banner tucked around her shoulders, her breath stirring the candle flame without bending it.

Felix knelt beside her and whispered the only sentence of her language he had managed to learn, from listening in the dark: the word for *stay*. He was not certain he pronounced it right.

Her eyes opened anyway—just a slit, just for a moment—and looked at him with an expression far too old for her face, an expression that said, clearly as any ledger entry: *you know now.*

Down in the hall, below the floorboards, the Black Ledger lay shut in its cabinet, and somewhere beyond the mountain, beyond the maps, in a court where souls were coin, someone had just been paid.
