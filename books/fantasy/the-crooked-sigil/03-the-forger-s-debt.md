## 3. The Forger's Debt

The dye-house below Sable's rooms smelled of madder root and old lies, and she preferred it that way. The vats turned all night, wool steeping from grey to rust to a red so deep it looked like something bled rather than brewed. She had chosen the lodging for exactly this reason: no one asked questions about stains in a dyer's quarter. Her hands had been blue at the knuckles for eleven years now—indigo worked into the skin too deep to scrub—and she had learned to let people read them as a tradeswoman's mark instead of what they were: the residue of a hundred forged seals, pressed and pressed again until the ink became her.

She was working by lamplight on commission work—a merchant's bill of lading, honest enough, though she'd improved his hand considerably—when the knock came.

Three knocks, then a pause, then one more. The rhythm of a man who wanted her to know he remembered their arrangements.

Sable did not move. She sat very still over the drying page, listening to the stair creak under a weight she knew too well. Eleven years, and Corvane had climbed these stairs twice. The first time to pay her; the second time to beg. This would be the third, and third times were never good.

"Go away," she said, before the knock could come again.

The door opened anyway. He still had the key she'd cut him, the fool, the sentimental fool, and he stood in the frame with rain running off his coat and the lamplight catching the grey in his beard that hadn't been there last time. Behind him, half-hidden by the door's edge, stood a young man with ink-stained fingers and an expression like a man carrying something breakable, and beside him—

Beside him was a child. Small, mud-caked, hollow-eyed, wrapped in someone else's coat. A child who looked at Sable the way Sable once looked at magistrates: measuring the distance between herself and the door.

"No," Sable said.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"I know you." She set down her pen with exaggerated care, aligning it with the page's edge so her hands had something to do besides shake. "You climb my stairs when the world has gone wrong, and you bring me something impossible, and you say *Sable, you owe me*, and I do it, because I do owe you, and you have spent eleven years rationing that debt like a miser rationing his last coin."

Corvane had the grace to look uncomfortable. Rain dripped off him onto her floorboards. "I wouldn't be here if—"

"If it weren't necessary. Yes. That's what you said about the death record."

*The death record.* Even now, eleven years later, she could feel the wax under her thumb, the way the guildmaster's seal had taken her impression perfectly, the way she'd written a stranger dead who was walking around very much alive. She had never asked who. She had told herself it didn't matter. Some nights it mattered enormously.

Felix cleared his throat. "Mistress Sable. We need a forgery. Of a ledger entry. A specific kind of entry."

"There's no such thing as a specific kind of entry," she snapped. "There are entries, and there are lies, and the difference is whether anyone checks." She looked at Corvane. "What kind of lie is worth climbing my stairs in the rain?"

"The kind beneath the mountain," Corvane said quietly.

And there it was. The name no one said aloud in this city unless they were drunk or desperate or both—the court under the mountain, where the old roads went down and did not come back up, where debts outlived the debtor and names could be bought and sold like sacks of grain. Sable had spent her whole career making paper tell falsehoods, and even she did not touch that place. Paper lied to men. What lay beneath the mountain ate liars.

"You've lost your mind," she said.

"Show her," Corvane said—not to Felix, but to the child.

* * *

Wren stepped forward slowly, the way animals step when they've been kicked too often, and held out one small hand, palm up. In the mud dried on the skin—or perhaps written in the mud; Sable couldn't tell which came first—were marks. Curved strokes, deliberate, each one closing on itself like a hand folding shut.

Sable leaned close. And stopped breathing.

She knew those strokes. Not the language—no living soul spoke it, that was the whole point, that was why it was called the First Tongue—but the *hand*. The particular way the curves hooked at their ends, the slight asymmetry where a right-handed writer lifted the stylus, the little flourish of pride at the terminal of every sigil, as if the writer could not help signing themselves into the work.

She had seen that hand once before, eleven years ago, on the document she had been hired to forge a companion piece for. Not the original—she'd never seen the original—but its *counterfeit*, the decoy the court beneath the mountain circulated among its brokers, the one designed to be recognized. She had copied its lettering for three days straight, learning its shapes well enough to imitate them, hating every hour, feeling like a woman tracing the outline of something with teeth.

"This isn't the child's writing," she heard herself say. Her voice came out strange, flat, distant. "This is court-hand. Vault-hand. Someone wrote *on* this child. Or—" She looked up at Wren's face, at the exhaustion in it, the terrible patience. "Or the child wrote what was done to them. In the only language that takes."

"What does it take?" Felix asked. He was leaning in too, and she saw now how young he was, how badly Corvane had kept him, this apprentice of a dying guild cataloguing relics nobody bought.

"A sale," Sable said. "A transfer of ownership. These aren't words, boy, they're terms. Price, condition, holder." She touched one sigil without thinking and Wren flinched, and she snatched her hand back as if scalded. "Holder," she repeated softly. "Someone holds this child. On paper. In whatever passes for paper down there."

The room was silent except for the drip of rain off Corvane's coat and, far below, the slow turning of the dye-wheel, patient as a heartbeat.

"That's why we need you," Felix said. "We're going to forge an entry. Into their ledger. We swap it for Wren's soul-page, and Wren goes free."

"It doesn't work like that."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've seen their documents." She was standing now without remembering standing, moving to the shelf where she kept the work she was not proud of, the locked box inside the locked chest, and her hands were steady in the way they always went steady at the worst moments, the calm of a body that had decided panic was a luxury. She drew out a folder of yellowed sheets. "Eleven years ago your master paid me to forge a death record. To make it convincing, I studied the counterfeits the mountain-court lets circulate above ground—their broker papers, their bills of sale. I learned their hand. I can copy it in my sleep. I dream in it sometimes." She dropped the folder on the table between them all, and the sigils on Wren's palm seemed to answer the sigils on the page, like two halves of a broken seal finding each other across a room.

Corvane stared at the folder as if it were a snake. "You never told me you kept them."

"You never told me whose death record I forged." She let that sit. Let him feel it. "We all keep our accounts, guildmaster."

"Sable." His voice cracked on her name, old and tired. "I'm asking. Not calling in the debt. Asking."

That was worse. She had braced for the debt; she knew how to hate a debt. An ask, freely made, from a man she'd sworn never to forgive—that slipped under her armor like water under a door.

She looked at the child instead. Wren had not moved, had not spoken—the child had not spoken a word since entering, Felix had murmured, not in any tongue Sable knew—and yet Wren was watching the folder of court-papers with an expression Sable recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone reading their own sentence.

"They sold you," Sable said quietly. Not a question.

Wren's chin dipped. Once.

"And Veyra is buying more of you. Filling her armies with souls she owns outright, so no soldier can desert, no blade can turn." Everyone knew the rumor; no one said it plainly. Saying it plainly made it real.

Sable looked at her hands, blue to the knuckle, hands that had spent thirty years making lies beautiful. Forging a merchant's bill of lading was a trade. Forging a death record was a sin she'd made peace with badly. But forging an entry into the vault of names to steal a child back from the court beneath the mountain—

That was the first honest work anyone had ever offered her.

"One condition," she said.

Corvane tensed. "Name it."

"When this is done—whatever happens—you tell me whose death record I forged. All of it. No more ledgers of yours closed against me." She picked up the folder, squared its edges, felt the old familiar weight of someone else's handwriting settling into her bones. "And the second condition is the child stays near me while I work. I write better when I can see what I'm writing for."

Something moved through the room then—relief, maybe, or fear finally given somewhere to go. Felix exhaled. Corvane closed his eyes briefly, a man setting down a weight he'd carried up many stairs.

Wren only watched her. And then, slowly, the child raised one mud-marked hand and drew, in the air between them, a single sigil—short, simple, closing on itself like a knot tied tight.

Sable didn't know the word. But she knew the shape of it from the broker-papers, from the margins, from every document the court ever sent abroad. It appeared on everything that left that place.

It meant, if the brokers' glosses were true, *held safe*.

"Well," Sable said roughly, turning away to pack her inks before anyone could see her face. "Then we'd best not disappoint the mountain."

Outside, the rain kept falling, and somewhere south, along the sunken roads, Veyra's vanguard was already marching.
