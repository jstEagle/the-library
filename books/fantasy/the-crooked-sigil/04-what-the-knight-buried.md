## 4. What the Knight Buried

Dame Ivetta Crane had buried three things in her life, and she had done it properly each time, because a knight who cannot bury things properly is a knight who has already begun to rot.

The first was her father, in the churchyard at Aldenmere, beneath a stone she had paid for out of her own purse because his debts would not. The second was her oath—she had spoken the words into the ground of the training yard at Veyra's own citadel, quietly, at dusk, when no one but the crows could hear her unsay them. And the third was her name. Not the sound of it; that still worked well enough when called across a courtyard. But everything the name had once meant—the white cloak, the right to judge, the belief that the law was a lamp rather than a leash—she had folded up like a standard after a rout and put it under the earth, and she did not visit the grave.

So when the summons came from the Crown's new Office of Records—*investigate the Ledgerkeepers' guildhall; determine whether sedition or sorcery; report within the week*—Ivetta read it twice and felt the old grave shift beneath her like a plowed field remembering a coffin.

They were sending her because she was expendable and thorough, which was, she had learned, the same thing in Veyra's service.

The rain had followed her all the way from the capital, two days of it, drumming on her hood and turning the river road to porridge. By the time the guildhall rose out of the wet evening—a narrow stone face wedged between a chandler's and a house that had burned and never been rebuilt—her boots had given up pretending to be dry. She stood across the street a while, letting the rain hide her looking.

It was a shabby place for treason. The sign above the door was so weathered she could only guess at the words: *Ledger-*. A single window glowed amber, and behind the glass moved the shadow of someone young, bent over something, patient as a monk.

Ivetta had arrested sorcerers before. They always looked ordinary at the door.

She went in without knocking, which was its own kind of testimony, and found herself in a hall that smelled of tallow, dust, and ink—that last one strangely strong, strangely sweet, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Shelves climbed the walls, stuffed with ledgers whose spines bore titles that made no civilized sense: *Catalogue of Doors That No Longer Open*. *Registry of Second Chances*. *An Index of Promises Kept by the Dead*.

A boy looked up from a desk near the cold hearth. Nineteen, perhaps twenty. Ink to the second knuckle. He had the particular pallor of someone who had been alone with books too long, and he flinched when he saw her cloak—or what remained of it, grey now, the white boiled out.

"Is the guildmaster in?" she asked.

"He's—" The boy swallowed. "He's upstairs. May I say who—"

"Dame Ivetta Crane," she said, and watched the word *Dame* land on him like a dropped anvil. "Office of Records. I'm here to inspect your holdings."

"We don't keep anything illegal."

"No one keeps anything illegal," Ivetta said. "That's what makes it valuable."

* * *

Corvane came down the stairs like a man descending into his own trial, which, to be fair, he was. He was younger than she expected—fifty at most—with a drinker's bloom high on his cheeks and eyes that kept flicking toward the ceiling as though the real conversation were happening one floor above and he was only stalling until he could rejoin it.

"Dame Crane. An honor. We're a small chapter now, mostly clerical work, inheritance disputes, the valuation of—of unusual bequests."

"I'll see the ledgers."

"Of course. Of course." He gestured grandly at the shelves. "Though most are sealed under guild law, you understand. Precedent going back—"

"Guild law ended when the guild stopped existing," Ivetta said. "You have four members. I counted the beds' worth of dust."

Something moved above them. Soft. Quick. A child's footfall, quickly stilled.

Corvane heard it too. She watched him hear it: a tightening around the mouth, a small involuntary glance upward, and then the smooth mask reapplied, badly, like paint over a crack.

"You have a child in this house," Ivetta said.

"A foundling. Temporarily. Until we can locate kin."

"How old?"

"Eight or nine. Perhaps ten. It's difficult to—"

"Bring them down here."

"Dame Crane, I really must insist—"

"*Bring the child down.*"

Her voice did what thirty years of drill had taught it to do: filled the room without rising. Corvane went pale above his wine-bloom. And then, from the stairs, small bare feet on stone, and the child appeared—not dragged, not summoned, simply arriving, the way weather arrives, with mud to the knees and hair like a crow's nest after a storm and eyes that were already watching Ivetta with a steadiness no eight-year-old should possess.

"This is Wren," Felix said softly. "Wren, this lady is—"

Wren looked at her. Only looked. But something passed through the air between them, faint as heat off a stone, and every scar Ivetta owned lit up at once along her back like a rosary being counted.

*I know you,* the look said. *I know what you refused to do.*

She had refused one order, once. One. In the great square at Karst, when Veyra's purge began and the First Tongue speakers were made to kneel in rows. Ivetta had been ordered to cut the cloak-sign from the shoulders of an old woman who taught letters to fisher children, and she had looked at the woman's hands—ink-stained, gentle, holding nothing but a piece of chalk—and she had said *no*, and the world had ended for her in the ordinary way: quietly, administratively, with paperwork.

The woman had knelt anyway. Someone else did the cutting. Ivetta had walked out of the square alive, stripped of rank, and had told herself ever after that walking away was the brave part.

She knew better. Walking away was just the cowardice that takes longer.

"Your name, child?" she asked, and hated how gently it came out.

Wren opened their mouth. And what came out was not any language Ivetta had ever heard, and yet she understood it, the way one understands thunder—as pressure, as meaning arriving ahead of its source:

*Ask her what she buried.*

Ivetta's hand went to her sword hilt purely from reflex. Felix stepped half in front of the child. Corvane made a sound like a cork leaving a bottle.

"What did it say?" the boy demanded. "What did they say?"

"It said nothing," Ivetta lied, and her lie was worse than Corvane's, because hers shook.

* * *

She spent two days in the guildhall, ostensibly cataloguing. Actually she was waiting—for the household's rhythm to show itself, for guilt to do what guilt does, which is leak.

What leaked was this: the child slept in the archive loft. The child drew constantly, in charcoal, on anything—wrapping paper, the margins of a hymnal, once, terribly, on the inside of Felix's forearm while the boy held still and let it happen. And the drawings were not drawings.

On the third morning, while Corvane snored off his bottle upstairs, Ivetta climbed to the loft and found Wren awake, sitting cross-legged among paper scraps like a magpie's hoard. Sigils covered them. Rows of them, repeated, varied, corrected—practiced, the way a musician practices.

Ivetta picked one up. Her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the loft's chill, because she had seen that mark before—in the great square at Karst, chalked on the doorposts of the houses they were going to empty. It was the mark the Office used to flag a First Tongue speaker's dwelling. Except here it wasn't a flag. Here it was *handwriting*. Here it was the child's own hand, fluent, unlabored, the way Ivetta's own hand wrote her mother tongue.

"Wren," she said slowly. "Where did you learn these?"

The child tilted their head. Then spoke again in that language that arrived like weather, and this time the meaning was unmistakable even through the strangeness:

*These are my bones.*

And below it, softer, almost puzzled, in the tone of a child reporting a lost mitten:

*Someone took them out of me and sold them.*

Ivetta sat down heavily on a crate. She thought of the lists that crossed her desk in the last year before her disgrace—requisitions from the Office of Records to the court beneath the mountain, crates marked ARCHIVE, escorts of Veyra's black-cloaks, and always, always the same notation in the margin: *tongue-bearing stock, live transit preferred.*

She had signed some of those requisitions. Routing orders. Nothing more, she'd told herself. Paperwork.

She looked at Wren—at the living, breathing consequence of her routing orders—and the ground where she had buried her oath shifted again, and this time something in it knocked to be let out.

"Boy," she called down the stair, and her voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "*Felix.* Fetch your guildmaster. And fetch every scrap of paper this child has touched. All of it."

* * *

They laid it out on the long table in the hall that evening: forty-one sigil-scraps, the Black Ledger open to its sealed section (Corvane protesting weakly until Ivetta reminded him she could have him chained within the hour), and the requisition copies Ivetta had carried against her own heart for two years, telling herself she kept them as evidence of her own innocence.

They proved the opposite. Laid beside Wren's drawings—the sigils matching, entry by entry, page by page—they proved exactly what Veyra was doing, what the Office of Records existed to do, what the court beneath the mountain was being fed:

Names. Harvested. Bought. Carried down in crates marked ARCHIVE, and burned as fuel for a conquest.

"She isn't collecting speakers," Felix said, his voice thin. "She's collecting the language itself. You can't write an order in the First Tongue unless you own enough of it to—"

"—to make reality obey the signature," Ivetta finished. "Yes. I've seen her sign things. Nothing refuses her. Now I know why."

Corvane sat with his head in his hands. "If you take this testimony back," he said, "they'll burn this hall and everyone in it."

"If I *don't*," Ivetta said, "then everything I refused to do at Karst will have been for nothing, and I might as well kneel next time they ask."

She drew her sword—not against them, but laid it flat on the table, hilt toward the child, in the old gesture of surrender of judgment. It had not been performed in the free cities since before the war. Felix stared at it. Even Corvane lifted his head.

"Ivetta Crane," she said formally, to the room, to the child, to the buried thing finally clawing up out of its grave, "refuses the warrant of the Office of Records, and gives her testimony instead: that Queen Veyra traffics in souls, and that I carried her paper, and that I mean to carry it no farther than it takes to hang her with it."

Silence. Rain on the roof. Then Wren reached out one muddy finger and touched the flat of the blade, and said something short and warm in the First Tongue, and though Ivetta could not translate it, she understood it completely, the way one understands sunlight:

*Welcome back.*

She was still blinking when Felix leaned over the Black Ledger, frowning.

"Dame Crane," he said slowly. "There's something else. This entry—it's Wren's page. It's dated six years ago. And the sale was authorized by..."

He turned the ledger around. At the bottom of the page, in ink gone brown with age, in a hand Ivetta had watched forge inheritance documents for two days:

*Authorized: G. Corvane, Guildmaster, for consideration rendered.*

Above them, a floorboard creaked. He had heard. He had been standing on the stairs the whole time.

And far to the east, past the rain, past the hills, Veyra's vanguard was already saddling.
