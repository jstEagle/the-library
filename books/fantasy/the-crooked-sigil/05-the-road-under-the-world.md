## 5. The Road Under the World

The sunken roads had been built by no one alive, and no one dead would claim them either. They ran beneath the hills like veins beneath skin, vaulted in black stone that sweated even in the driest months, and the lantern-light only reached so far before the dark took it back, as if the dark were owed something.

Felix walked with Wren's small hand closed around two of his fingers and tried not to think about how natural it had come to feel.

Behind them, Sable argued with Dame Ivetta for the fourth time since dawn—or what passed for dawn down here, a grey seepage through the ventilation shafts far above.

"—because knights charge," Sable was saying, "and these roads are not for charging. They're for listening."

"I have listened to you complain for three days. I am very well informed."

"And yet you still walk on my left, where your sword arm does me no good at all."

"Your left is where the danger is, dyer's-ghost, because you refuse to walk anywhere but first."

Felix let it wash over him. Three days ago the bickering had frightened him; now it was almost a comfort, the sound of people who expected to still be together tomorrow. He caught Wren glancing back at them, and the child's mouth curved—the first smile Felix had seen that wasn't borrowed from someone else's face.

Ahead, the road forked where an old waystone leaned like a drunk against its own shadow. The carvings on it had been worn to suggestion, but Wren went stiff all over, fingers tightening on Felix's hand.

"What is it?" he whispered.

Wren didn't answer in words. Wren never answered in words when words failed, which was often, because the child's Common was a handful of nouns and a great deal of pointing. Instead Wren crouched, wetted one finger in the road's eternal damp, and drew on the stone: a line, a hook, a circle broken open like a mouth.

Felix knew enough of the First Tongue now to read it the way one reads smoke—wrongly, but not entirely. *Left. Down. The mouth is open.*

"The left passage," he said. "Downward. And—something hungry?"

Sable stopped arguing mid-sentence. She came forward, looked at the sigil, then at Wren, then at the left-hand dark, and Felix watched her decide to trust a six-year-old's grammar over her own thirty years of roads.

"Left," she said. "Ivetta. Rear guard. Move."

* * *

They made camp that night in a side chamber where the ceiling rose into a hollow like the inside of a bell. Sable counted their supplies with the grim economy of a woman doing arithmetic she already hated: hardtack for five days if they starved politely, seven if they didn't; a skin of oil; Ivetta's rations untouched because the knight claimed she'd fasted worse before sieges and clearly intended to prove it again.

Felix sat with Wren while the others argued about the route—more gently now, he noticed, the way a storm softens toward evening. He'd brought out the copied sigils, his own careful renderings of the pages from the Black Ledger, and Wren traced them with one finger, pronouncing each under their breath in that voice that made the lantern flame lean in to listen.

"That one," Felix said, tapping a hooked character. "What does it do?"

Wren considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate. Then pointed at Felix. Pointed at the sigil. Pointed at Felix again.

"It's... me?"

A nod. Then Wren pressed the sigil flat against Felix's chest, over his heart, and said a word—not aloud, exactly, more a shape the air remembered—and for one impossible moment Felix felt *written*. Felt that somewhere beneath his ribs there was a page, and someone had just underlined a line of him.

He laughed, startled, and Wren laughed too, high and cracked with disuse, and across the chamber Sable and Ivetta both went quiet, looking over at them with expressions Felix couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"You know," Sable said later, when Wren slept against Felix's shoulder like a warm stone, "in the dye-houses we used to say color doesn't belong to anyone until it's chosen. You can mix it, sell it, bleed it into cloth—but it isn't *that* color until somebody looks at it and says *yes, this one*." She fed the lantern another measure of oil. "I used to think it was nonsense. Dyers say a lot of nonsense."

"And now?"

"Now I think Veyra buys souls because she can't stand the idea that belonging might be voluntary." Her jaw tightened. "She wants it inherited. Owned. Written into bloodlines she controls. A child choosing where it belongs—that must look like theft to her."

From the dark, Ivetta's voice: "It did look like theft. To me. Once." A pause long enough to hold a funeral. "I arrested a family in Ashfall for teaching their daughter the First Tongue. I thought I was upholding order." Another pause. "The daughter's name is on Veyra's harvest lists. I checked. Twice."

No one answered that. There was nothing to answer it *with*, and all three of them knew it, and the silence was somehow less terrible than any comfort would have been.

Felix fell asleep with Wren's weight against him and dreamed of pages turning in a book no one was reading.

* * *

The ambush came on the fifth day, at a place where the sunken road crossed a drowned river and the water sang under the stones.

Sable heard it first—a wrongness in the echo, footsteps arriving a half-beat after they should have. She threw up a fist, the old caravan signal, and they froze, four shadows among shadows.

Then the lanterns came. Six of them, spaced with military precision, and between them moved figures in Veyra's grey-and-crimson livery, faces masked in porcelain smooth as eggshell. Name-takers. Felix had seen one once, from a distance, and had told himself the stories were exaggerated.

They were not exaggerated. The masks had no mouths. Where a mouth should be, the porcelain simply continued, blank and serene, and when the lead name-taker spoke, the voice came from somewhere behind the mask, or inside it, or nowhere at all.

"Surrender the speaker," it said. "The usurper's writ extends beneath the hills. The child is inventory."

"Inventory," Sable repeated softly. "That's a new word for a soul."

"Stand aside, forger. Your debt is known to us. Cooperate, and it may be forgiven."

Something changed in Sable's face—Felix saw it happen, saw twenty years of exile and resentment pass through her like weather—and then settle into something harder and cleaner.

"My debts," she said, "are mine to pay. And I choose who I pay them *to*."

The name-taker raised a hand. The lanterns converged.

What followed happened in pieces, the way disasters do. Ivetta's sword clearing its sheath with a sound like a struck bell. Sable hurling the oil skin—not at the men but at the flooded channel beside them, and Ivetta's blade sparking it alight midair so that a ribbon of fire bloomed across the black water, throwing everything into strobing gold. Felix grabbing Wren and running, running, the child light as a bird and burning hot with terror, and behind him the name-takers' voices beginning the *work*—

Because that was the horror of them. They didn't fight with steel first. They fought with names. The lead taker spoke—Ivetta's name, her true one, dredged from some ledger of Veyra's—and Ivetta staggered as though struck, her sword arm dropping, her face going slack with a confusion that was awful to witness, as if someone had reached into her and rearranged the furniture.

"Dame!" Felix screamed. "Dame Ivetta! You refused the purge! You buried your colors rather than serve her—you *chose*—"

And Ivetta, kneeling in the firelight with her own name wrapped around her throat like a hand, looked up at Felix, and something in her eyes reassembled itself. She rose. She said, in a voice like grinding stone: "*I chose.*" And cut the lantern from the lead taker's grip.

But they were six, and armed, and the four of them were tired and half-starved and carrying a child. It was Sable who ended it, in the end—not with craft or courage but with the particular vicious practicality of a woman who had spent her life working out how things were put together. She kicked loose the keystone wedge at the channel's edge—the old floodgate, rusted, forgotten—and the drowned river came through.

It came through *all at once*, a black wall of it, taking the lanterns, taking their supplies, their food, the oil, half their blankets, taking the name-takers screaming into the dark with their porcelain masks filling with river. It nearly took Wren. Felix felt the small hand torn from his, felt the water close over the place where Wren had been, and dove blind into the roaring dark, and found—one hand, small, gripping, gripping—and hauled the child gasping onto the rising stones as the flood spent itself below.

For a long time afterward, none of them spoke. They huddled in a dry alcove, soaked, starving, possessionless. Everything they owned was gone except Ivetta's sword, Sable's knife, the copied sigils sealed in wax inside Felix's shirt, and each other.

"Well," Sable said finally, teeth chattering. "We're very rich in each other."

Ivetta laughed. Actually laughed—a short, rusty, astonished sound, as if the noise had surprised her more than anyone. "You are the worst woman I have ever been exiled alongside."

"And you charge like a knight. We're evenly matched."

Felix looked down at Wren, wrapped in the last dry blanket, and Wren looked back at him and signed—slowly, deliberately, in the grammar of hands the child had been teaching him on the road: *family*. Not the word. The sigil. The one that meant *chosen, kept, written-together*.

Felix signed it back, badly.

It was Ivetta who spotted it, an hour later, when they crept along the flood-drained channel: fresh-cut marks in the stone ahead, official and precise, the sigil of Veyra's quartermasters. And beyond them, carved deep into the mountain's flank where the sunken road met the world again, a gate the size of a cathedral door, standing open, breathing cold air that smelled of ink and iron and old, old names.

Above the gate, in letters that seemed to move when unwatched, ran a single line of First Tongue.

Wren read it aloud without hesitation, fluently, in a whisper that made the stones shiver:

"*All who enter, enter written.*"
