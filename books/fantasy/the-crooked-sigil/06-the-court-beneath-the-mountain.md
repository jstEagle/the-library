## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not open so much as admit them.

Sable had imagined doors—great bronze leaves, a gatehouse, some ceremony of keys. Instead, on the third day of descent along the sunken road, the stone simply stopped pretending to be stone. The walls peeled back like pages turning, and there it was: the court beneath the mountain, lit by a light that came from everywhere and belonged to nothing.

She had spent her life around forgeries, and she knew at once that this light was one. It was gold the way gilt is gold—a skin over darkness, beautiful and cheap and utterly convincing until you looked too long. Then you saw what lay under it: corridors carved from black basalt polished to the sheen of still water, pillars wrapped in chains of script, and everywhere, *everywhere*, writing.

Not inked. Grown. The letters curled through the stone like veins through marble, rising out of the floor and vanishing into ceilings lost in shadow above. Wren made a sound beside her—not a word in any trade-tongue but a note, low and resonant, and the nearest wall of script *answered*. The letters brightened, rippled, dimmed.

"Stop doing that," Felix whispered, gripping the child's shoulder. But his own voice shook with awe. Sable understood. She was a woman who made false things look true for coin, and here was a place where truth itself was written into the bones of the world. Every instinct she owned stood up and howled.

"Stay close," Dame Ivetta said, and her hand rested on the empty scabbard where her sword had been confiscated at the last checkpoint of the sunken road. "And remember—we are pilgrims. We come to petition. Nothing else."

They walked in.

* * *

The market of souls began before they were ready for it.

Sable had heard the stories—all guild children had—but stories are a coward's preparation. Nothing had told her about the *sound*: a murmur like rain on parchment, thousands of voices layered over each other, none of them quite saying anything. Nothing had told her about the smell—cold iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft in a cellar. And nothing, certainly nothing, had told her about the *stalls*.

They lined the great gallery on both sides, merchants seated behind counters of glass so clear it seemed absent, and behind the glass—

People. Or the shape of people. Small ones, fist-sized, glowing faintly, turning slowly as though asleep in warm water. Souls, unbound from flesh, displayed like lamps. A merchant with skin like grey silk caught Sable staring and smiled with too many teeth.

"Lady! Lady, you have an honest face—" he called. "I have a soldier's courage, barely used. I have a mother's tenderness, forty years seasoned. I have—"

"We're not buying," Ivetta said flatly, and pulled them onward.

But Sable looked back, because she could not help it. Behind the merchant's counter, past his wares, hung a ledger of his own—pages of names in columns, and beside each name a small empty hook where its soul-shape had once been displayed. Some hooks held souls. Many were bare. Sold, she thought. Spent. Fed into whatever engine Veyra was building in the free cities' direction. Her stomach turned over, slow and cold.

Wren's small hand found hers. The child did not speak the trade-tongue, but the child had looked at the hooks too, and the grip said everything: *this is where I nearly went.*

"I know," Sable murmured, surprising herself with the softness of it. "I know, little bird. Not today."

* * *

The gate to the inner court was not a gate. It was a threshold of white stone set into the black, wide enough for ten abreast, and standing upon it was a figure that Sable's eyes refused to settle on. Tall. Robed. Its face was a blank page—literally a page, vellum stretched where features should be, and as she watched, lines of script surfaced across it like breath fogging glass, arranged themselves briefly into an expression, and faded again.

"A magistrate's doorwarden," Corvane said quietly. He had not spoken since the market. His voice sounded scraped hollow. "It reads what you carry."

"What we carry?" Felix asked.

"Memories. One true memory is the toll. It takes it at the threshold. You will not miss it—you cannot miss what you no longer remember." Corvane's jaw worked. "I told you this before we came."

"You told us it would cost 'a memory,'" Ivetta said. "You did not tell us it would cost a *true* one. There's a difference, guildmaster, and you know exactly what it is."

Corvane said nothing. Sable filed that silence away, the way she filed everything—the forger's habit, hoarding details against the day they'd be useful.

The doorwarden extended a hand. Where fingers should have been, there were only more script, letters flowing off its wrist and re-forming as digits, endlessly rewriting themselves.

"One at a time," it said. Its voice was many voices, or perhaps none—it arrived inside the skull rather than the ear. "One memory, freely surrendered, wholly true. Falsehoods are weighed here. Liars do not pass. They remain."

"They remain?" Felix asked.

The page-face rippled into something that might have been pity. "As part of the archive."

Sable stepped up first, because if she waited she never would. That was the trick of her whole life, she reflected—doing terrible things quickly, before the part of her that was decent could lodge a formal objection. She'd forged death records the same way. Signed, sealed, done before doubt could find a quill.

The doorwarden's hand hovered over her chest. She felt a warmth there, a pressure like a thumb pressed to the center of her sternum, and then the thing *began to read her*, and she could feel it happening—could feel her own memories surfacing like fish rising in a pond, offered up whether she willed it or not.

Her first forgery, age eleven, a boy's name scratched over in a tenant roll. The dye-house stink of her rooms above the vats. Corvane's face the night he'd begged her to write a dead man living. And beneath all of it, always beneath all of it, warm as a banked coal:

Maren.

Her sister, laughing in the orchard behind their childhood house, hair full of blossoms, holding up two green apples—one sour, one sweet—and making Sable choose blind, and Sable choosing wrong on purpose every time just to hear her crow with triumph. *Again. Again.* Maren, who died of fever at fourteen while Sable was away learning letters. Maren, whose funeral she had missed. Maren, who was the reason Sable wrote false things—because somewhere in her heart she believed, without ever once admitting it, that a person skilled enough with ink could write the world right again.

The doorwarden paused. The script on its face swirled, considering.

"No," Sable said aloud. Her voice cracked like green wood. "No, take something else. Take my hands' memory, take the dye-house, take—"

The warmth flared. Drew. And took.

* * *

She walked through the threshold and stood on the far side of the white stone, and she felt it immediately: a hole. Not pain—worse than pain, pain has edges and this had none. An absence shaped like a person. She knew she had a sister; the fact remained, dry as a ledger line. *Sable, born second. Had a sister. Died young.* But the sister herself was gone—the laugh, the apples, the blossoms in dark hair, all of it lifted out clean, leaving smooth walls where a life had been.

She was crying before she understood why. Her hands came up to her own face as though belonging to someone else.

"Sable." Felix's voice. He had crossed after her, and his eyes were wet too—he'd paid with something, she could see it in him, some room in his gaze now shuttered and dark. "What did it—what did you lose?"

"I don't know," she said, and the sentence was the most obscene thing she had ever spoken. "That's the filth of it. I don't *know*. There's a shape in me screaming and I can't even tell you its name."

"Wren," Felix said softly.

The child had crossed last, with Corvane. Wren looked up at Sable with those old, old eyes and pressed both small hands flat against her tear-streaked cheeks, and spoke—not the trade-tongue, not quite, but something between it and speech, the First Tongue bending toward comfort the way fire bends toward a draft. Sable couldn't understand the words. But she understood the tone, and the tone said: *I see the hole in you. Holes can be filled. I am proof.*

She knelt and pulled the child against her and let herself shake for three breaths. Only three. Then she stood, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and became the forger again, because the work was ahead and grief was a luxury with interest rates she couldn't afford.

"Not a word about this," she told Felix. "When this is done—if any of us is done—I want it written down. All four of us, what we paid. Someone should keep the account."

"That's guild work," Felix said, with the ghost of a smile. "Keeping accounts."

"Then it seems," Sable said, "we're the guild now."

* * *

Beyond the threshold the court proper opened like a cathedral built inside a geode, and Sable forgot, for one full heartbeat, that she had ever lost anything at all.

The vault rose at the center of everything: a tower of white bone-stone spiraling upward into darkness, its surface crawling with names in every script she knew and a hundred she didn't. Around it, tier upon tier, the galleries of the court—magistrates robed in moving text, petitioners clutching contracts, soul-merchants bowing and whispering. And threaded through it all, audible now beneath the murmur, a sound like a vast slow heartbeat: the vault, breathing. Names being taken in. Names being given out.

Somewhere in that white spiral was a page with Wren's name on it. Somewhere in those galleries, Veyra's agents moved among the merchants with purses full of stolen lives.

Ivetta straightened at Sable's side. The disgraced knight put her shoulders back and lifted her chin, wearing her ruined honor like armor one more time.

"The magistrates receive petitions on the third terrace," she said. "I'll need an hour alone near the vault stair. My order's sigil may still open doors here—we helped build this place, before anyone remembers." She glanced at each of them. "Whatever you're forging, forger, make it good. If they catch us, we don't die. We get *filed*."

Sable touched the satchel at her hip, where the Black Ledger's copied pages lay hidden against her ribs, and felt the hole in her memory ache around its absence like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

"Then let's give them something worth filing," she said.

Above them, far above, through leagues of mountain rock, came a sound none of them could truly be hearing yet—but all of them heard it anyway. A tremor. A rhythm. Boots, perhaps. Armies, perhaps. The future arriving early, as it always did, and never politely.

Wren tugged her hand and pointed up the terraces, toward the vault, and spoke one word in the First Tongue that needed no translation.

*Now.*
