## 7. The Vault of Names

Dame Ivetta Marchand had spent twenty years learning that armor was a kind of language. Wear it correctly and doors opened before you knocked; wear it wrongly and the same steel became a cage. So she wore it now—what remained of it. The court beneath the mountain had no use for plate, but it understood *standing*, and standing was something she still carried like a splinter too deep to draw.

The vault's antechamber was carved from bone-white stone veined with silver, and the veins moved. She tried not to watch them. Watching made her think of worms in marrow, and thinking of worms in marrow made her grip the hilt of a sword she had not been allowed to bring past the gate.

*One true memory,* the gatekeeper had demanded, and she had paid. She could already feel the hole where the payment had been taken: a small smooth absence behind her breastbone, shaped like a winter morning and a boy's laughter. She did not know what she had lost. That was the cruelty of it. You paid with something true, and truth, once surrendered, forgot itself on the way down.

"You are admitted," said the magistrate's herald—a tall thing in robes the color of wet slate, its face an oval of polished obsidian. "The Dame Ivetta Marchand, once of the Order of the Brazen Star, who refused the Purge of Ashford." It pronounced the last word the way a jeweler pronounces a flaw. "Your standing admits you to the outer galleries. The inner vault is closed."

"Closed to thieves," Ivetta said. "I am here to audit."

The obsidian face tilted. Somewhere in its depths, light bent wrong.

"Audit," it repeated.

"The court trades in names. Names are currency. Currency requires verification against the ledgers of the surface world—or does the court no longer honor its own covenants?" She let the old command-voice come up out of her chest, the one that had once made grown soldiers weep during drills. "I stood witness at the Treaty of Nine Bells. My testimony is sealed into your founding records. Check me."

A silence. The moving veins in the walls pulsed once, slow as a sleeping heart.

"The Dame is correct," the herald said at last, and there was something almost like distaste in its glassy voice. "Her name carries weight. She may enter the outer galleries under bond of witness. Touch nothing. Read everything."

"I intend to," said Ivetta, and walked through the doors before it could reconsider.

* * *

The outer galleries were shelves.

That was the only honest word for it. Shelves rising into darkness, each slot holding a slender case of smoked glass, and inside each case a page—not paper, not vellum, but something that breathed faintly when she passed, the way a sleeper breathes. Names. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. Some cases were dark and cold, their pages stilled; some flickered with a light like candleflame seen through a hand.

She found Wren's empty slot on the third gallery, exactly where Felix's stolen transcription said it would be. The case was there. The page was gone. In its place sat a receipt written in ink that hurt to look at directly—a bill of sale, countersigned by a seal Ivetta knew from wanted posters nailed to every church door in three provinces: the crowned thorn of Veyra Ascendant.

*Purchased in good faith,* the receipt read. *For purposes of war.*

Ivetta stood very still and let the anger come, because anger was fuel and she had learned to burn clean. A child's soul, filed like a tax. Bought by a usurper to feed engines Ivetta had seen only in refugees' nightmares—engines that spoke with the voices of the harvested, marching on the free cities wearing borrowed mouths.

Below her, through a grate in the floor, she could see the counting hall where Felix and Sable waited, hunched over a traveling desk like two conspirators in a bad play. Which, she supposed, was precisely what they were.

She touched two fingers to the empty case—the old salute, the one for fallen comrades—and went back to work.

* * *

The forgery took four hours, and Ivetta watched all of it from the gallery rail, which meant watching Sable Vane work, which meant watching something between surgery and sacrilege.

Sable wrote the way other people fought. Her pen never hesitated. She had ground the false ink from ash, gall, and—God help them all—a single drop of Wren's blood that Felix had carried wrapped in wax since the sunken roads. The sigils came off her nib in strokes so confident they seemed less drawn than *remembered*: the First Tongue, rendered in a forger's hand, each character a perfect twin of the ones in the Black Ledger that Corvane had shown them by candlelight and sworn he'd never opened.

"She's matching the court's own scribes," Felix murmured beside Ivetta at the rail, his voice frayed thin. "Look at the descender on the third mark. That's not imitation. That's lineage."

"It's theft," Ivetta said.

"It's both. That's what makes it work." He rubbed his eyes. Below, Sable blew across the page without touching it, and the fresh sigils dimmed from wet black to the gray of old writing, aging forty years in forty seconds. "Corvane says every soul-page has a shadow entry—a record of the record. If our forgery sits in the shadow first, the vault will believe the original was misplaced centuries ago and ours is the recovery."

"And if the vault doesn't believe?"

Felix didn't answer. Down in the counting hall, Sable lifted the finished page, and even from the gallery Ivetta felt it—the way one feels a change in weather. The air thickened. The silver veins in the walls pulsed toward the forgery like roots toward water.

"It believes," Ivetta said quietly.

They came down. The swap itself was almost gentle. Sable palmed the receipt, slid the forged shadow-entry into the archive channel, and spoke six syllables of trade-cant that made the empty case accept the new page with a sound like a sigh. The smoked glass warmed. Inside, a page began to breathe—Wren's name, home again, wearing a forged provenance the way a stray wears a borrowed coat.

Above them, throughout the galleries, hundreds of breathing pages rustled at once, though there was no wind.

Ivetta exhaled for the first time in hours. "It's done."

"We're not done until we're above ground," Sable said, capping her ink. But her hands were shaking now that the work was over, and she laughed—a short, disbelieving bark. "Fourteen years I've been told my hands were weapons. First time they've been a rescue."

Felix was grinning like a fool, and Ivetta found, to her horror, that she was too. They had done it. Against a magistrate older than kingdoms, against a vault that ate memories, against Veyra's whole crowned machinery—they had stolen a child's soul back out of the engine of conquest, and no one—

The lights changed.

It happened everywhere at once. The candle-glow in the breathing pages guttered from warm gold to a color Ivetta had no name for, a white like the inside of a wound. Every vein in every wall flared silver. And from somewhere far below—deeper than the galleries, deeper than the counting hall, from whatever root-system fed this whole buried kingdom—came a sound like a great bell being struck underwater.

The obsidian-faced herald reappeared at the end of the gallery, and it was not alone. Behind it stood a dozen shapes in slate-colored robes, and behind *them* stood the magistrate herself, if the rumors were true about what the magistrates looked like: a figure whose outline would not settle, who seemed to be several ages at once, whose face was a ledger open to a page Ivetta could not read.

"Dame Marchand," the herald said. Its voice had lost its dryness. "Your bond of witness is revoked. Stand aside from the thieves."

Ivetta stepped sideways. Deliberately. Until she was standing in front of Felix and Sable rather than beside them.

"There's been a mistake," she said.

"There has indeed." The magistrate spoke, and its voice arrived not through the air but through the bones, through the teeth, through the old treaty-oaths stitched somewhere in Ivetta's memory. "The shadow-entry you planted was accepted. The soul was returned to its case. All of this is within covenant." A pause, vast as a held breath. "But the provenance you forged cites a guarantor. A guildmaster. A man named Corvane, of the Ledgerkeepers, who attested forty years ago to a death that did not occur—and whose living signature now appears on a living claim."

Felix went white. "No. No, that entry was sealed—it was settled—"

"Nothing is settled," said the magistrate. "Everything is *kept*. Your forgery was flawless. Your guarantor was not. When we weighed his old attestation against your new claim, the scales found a lie at the bottom of both—one lie, folded through forty years, and now folded through your work as well. The covenant says a flawed chain taints the claim entire." The magistrate's unsettled face turned toward Sable, toward Felix, toward Ivetta's bare and useless hands. "You did not steal a soul, Dame Marchand's companions. You stole it *badly*. And the court does not punish failure."

The bell struck again, closer now, and the shelves around them shivered.

"It punishes theft. Seize them."

Ivetta moved before thought, the way she had moved at Ashford, the way she had moved the day she chose disgrace over obedience—sword-hand closing on empty air, body turning to put her shoulders between the slate-robed shapes and the two people behind her who had never held anything sharper than a pen. Twelve figures advanced. The breathing pages screamed softly, all together, a sound like wind through a field of throats.

And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, rising through the mountain's roots from the world above: drums. Veyra's drums. Her vanguard had reached the mountain's crown, and the court beneath it had begun, very quietly, to shake.

"Run," Ivetta said, and drew the only weapon left to her—her name, spoken aloud in the tongue of oaths, daring the court to take it.
