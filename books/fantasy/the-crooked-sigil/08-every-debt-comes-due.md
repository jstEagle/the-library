## 8. Every Debt Comes Due

They took Felix's hands first.

That was the courtesy of the court beneath the mountain—courtesy like a knife laid flat on silk. Two name-takers in robes the color of wet slate bound his wrists with cord spun from something that was not hemp and not hair but had the give of both, and when it touched his skin he felt his own name grow quiet inside him, muffled, as though someone had drawn a curtain across a window he hadn't known was open.

The vault of names closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting on a finger.

* * *

The magistrate's hall was not a hall so much as a throat—a long descending chamber of black stone ribbed with veins of pale mineral, lit by lamps that burned without flame, their light the color of memory just before it fades. At its far end, upon a dais of fused ledgers (Felix could see the spines, thousands of them, pressed and melted into a single seat), sat the Magistrate of Accounts.

It had been human once. Perhaps. What remained was a figure of dignified erosion: a face like a coin worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, eyes that were not eyes but two small apertures through which one glimpsed only more ledger-pages turning.

Sable knelt at Felix's left, her forger's fingers twitching against the cords, already composing and discarding lies—I could see them flicker across her face like fish under ice. Ivetta stood at his right because they had not made her kneel; the court remembered what she was, or had been, and courtesy cut both ways. And Corvane—

Corvane stood apart, as he always did, three steps back from everything, his ruined hands folded, watching the Magistrate with an expression Felix had never seen on him before.

Not fear. Recognition.

"The Black Ledger," said the Magistrate, and its voice was the sound of a page turning in an empty room. "You brought it beneath my mountain."

Felix's heart was a fist against his ribs. The ledger hung from the name-taker's grip now, its black cover drinking the lamp-light, and every instinct he had—the guild trained into his bones since he was nine years old—screamed at him to deny it, to deflect, to lie the way Corvane had taught him: beautifully, and too late.

Instead he heard himself say: "You can't read it anymore. Can you?"

The apertures of the Magistrate's eyes stopped turning.

"The First Tongue has left this place," Felix went on, and his voice shook, and he let it, because a trembling voice was a true voice and these creatures knew truth the way sharks knew blood. "The language souls are written in. You trade in names you cannot write. You hoard pages you cannot read. That's why Veyra brings you speakers—that's why she's harvesting them, one by one, to feed your vault. Because without the Tongue, all of this—" he lifted his bound hands toward the dais, the melted ledgers, the throat of the mountain— "is just paper."

Silence. Somewhere far above, faint as a heartbeat through stone, came the first tremor of Veyra's siege-engines.

"You presume," said the Magistrate, "to bargain."

"I presume to *offer*." Felix's mouth had gone dry as ash, but the words kept coming, and he realized with a strange detachment that they were not entirely his own—they were Wren's sigils, translated; they were the grammar of the sealed ledger, which he had copied night after night by candle-gutter until the shapes lived behind his eyelids. "The Black Ledger is written in the First Tongue. Every entry. Every debt. Including—" and here his voice caught, and he forced it through, "—including the sale of a child called Wren. Page and seal and signature. I can read it to you. Word for word. In the Tongue itself."

He had not known, until he said it, whether he could. But the ledger had been whispering to him for days—through the ink that crawled, through the dreams of falling letters—and when he opened his mouth the shapes were there, waiting, patient as old debts.

The Magistrate rose. It took a long time, like a building deciding to stand.

"Read," it said.

* * *

Felix had imagined many versions of this moment on the road down: the revelation as a weapon, clean and bright. He had not imagined how it would feel to speak the words aloud and watch Corvane's face while he did.

"The ninth day of the Fading Month, in the year the river froze twice," Felix read, and the First Tongue moved through the chamber like cold water, making the lamps gutter, making the name-takers sway. "A soul entered into pledge. A child's soul, unwritten, unclaimed, sold by the hand of—" and here the ledger required the name, and the name rose in him unbidden, in the Tongue, unmistakable, "—Corvane, lastmaster of the Ledgerkeepers' guild."

He looked up.

Corvane had not flinched. That was the worst of it. He stood there with his grey beard and his drinker's broken veins and his eyes fixed on Felix with something that was not denial at all—it was relief, terrible and total, the relief of a man who has carried a stone so long that setting it down hurts more than holding it.

"It was eleven years ago," Corvane said, and his voice was steady, which meant he had rehearsed this confession in the dark for a decade. "The guild was dying. You all know it was dying—the wars, the purges, no one buying impossible things when bread cost a silver a loaf. And a man came down from the mountain with an offer. One soul, pledged against our charter. Keep the guild alive, keep the ledgers safe, keep the *Tongue* alive somewhere in the world above—and the court would hold its protection over us." His ruined hands opened. "And then a child walked in my door speaking the First Tongue, and I understood what I had done. Not sold a soul. Sold a *door*. Sold the last living speaker of the language to the very thing that eats them."

"Why didn't you tell us?" Sable's voice cracked like a whip. She was on her feet despite the cords, fury coming off her in waves. "Eleven years, you old liar. Eleven years we starved in that drafty mausoleum keeping your precious ledgers, and you'd already fed a *child* to this—"

"Because if I told you, you would have tried to fix it," Corvane said simply. "And the fixing would have killed you. All of you. And the Tongue would have died with the guild." He turned to the Magistrate. "That was the bargain. My name against the guild's survival. I signed it knowing the day would come when the debt was called. I only asked that it come late."

"It has come," said the Magistrate.

"I know." Corvane smiled, and it was the saddest thing Felix had ever seen, sadder than the empty guildhall, sadder than the ledger's crawling ink. "I've been walking toward this door for eleven years. I only hoped I wouldn't have to bring children with me when I arrived."

* * *

The Magistrate considered. Around the chamber, the name-takers had gone still in a way that was worse than movement—stillness here was attention, and attention here was appetite.

"The pledge stands," the Magistrate said at last. "A soul was sold. A soul is owed. The ledger does not care whose." Its worn-smooth face turned toward Wren, who stood between two name-takers, small and mud-streaked still, even now, even here. "The child's page is in my vault. The child will fill it."

"No," said Corvane.

"The ledger does not care—"

"*Then let the ledger learn.*" And Corvane stepped forward, out of the shadows where he had lived for eleven years, into the full light of the flameless lamps, and Felix saw that his hands had stopped shaking. "You want a name. Take mine. Not pledged—*given*. Freely offered, freely surrendered, witnessed by four and sealed in the Tongue. There isn't a law in your vaults that governs a gift, Magistrate. I've read your ledgers. I helped write some of them, God rot me. Debts bind. Gifts *free*."

The apertures of the Magistrate's eyes began, very slowly, to turn again.

"A name given cannot be held as collateral," it said, in the tone of a creature consulting something older than itself. "It can only be... spent."

"Then spend it," said Corvane.

"Master—" Felix started forward and the cords bit deep. "Master, don't—"

"Felix." Corvane looked at him then, and for the first time in all the years of candle-gutter and catalogued relics and slow alcoholic evenings, he looked at Felix the way a master looks at a master. "You asked me once why the ledgers matter. I lied. Here is the truth: they matter because someone has to remember what things cost. Someone has to write it down so it isn't lost, so it isn't *nothing*. You're that someone now. Not the guild. You." He glanced at Sable, at Ivetta, last and longest at Wren. "Keep each other. That's the whole secret of it. Everything else is bookkeeping."

He turned back to the Magistrate and spoke—not in the trade-tongue of the free cities, but in the First Tongue, halting and rusted, a language he had clearly not spoken aloud in decades, and yet the chamber bent around the words as though the mountain itself were listening:

"I am Corvane, who was called Lastmaster. I give my name for the child's. Witness it. Write it. Let it be the last entry in the Black Ledger that anyone ever sells."

The lamps flared white.

The mountain shuddered—Veyra's engines above, or the court below, or both—and somewhere in the vault of names, Felix felt certain, a page was turning.

* * *

The Magistrate extended one eroded hand, and the cords fell from Felix's wrists as though they had never been tied, and the name-takers drew back, and the great throat of the chamber exhaled a wind that smelled of ink and iron and rain on stone.

"The exchange requires a witness of the Tongue," the Magistrate said. "The child speaks it. The apprentice reads it. Both will attend the rewriting." Its gaze settled on Felix, and in those turning apertures he saw—or thought he saw—something almost like hunger, almost like hope. "And know this, keeper-of-small-ledgers: when a name is spent rather than taken, the vault does not stay shut. Nothing given freely stays contained. Not a name. Not a soul. Not a *guild*."

Above them, the mountain boomed again, closer now, and dust sifted down through the lamp-light like snow falling upward.

Corvane straightened his coat—an old habit, an old vanity—and walked toward the vault doors without looking back.

Felix followed, because there was nothing else in the world to do, and beside him Wren slipped a small mud-crusted hand into his, and spoke one word in the First Tongue that needed no translation at all.

The vault doors opened onto darkness, and the darkness was full of pages.
