## 9. The Rewriting

Wren had never heard their own name.

They knew it the way you know a word in a dream—felt in the ribs before it reached the ears, a shape with no sound attached. It lived somewhere behind Wren's breastbone, folded small as a moth in winter, and all their life it had been silent. Other children had names. They had been called *girl*, *boy*, *mudling*, *it*, *you there*. The name-takers had taken the rest before Wren could walk, and what remained had grown up crooked and quiet, learning to live inside a body that felt borrowed.

Now they stood in the vault of names, and every shelf in the dark was breathing.

That was the thing no one had told them about the vault: it was not silent. Sable had imagined rows of dead ledgers; Ivetta had braced herself for a crypt. But souls were not dead things here—they were *waiting* things—and waiting has a sound like wind through a keyhole, like ten thousand people inhaling at once and never letting go. Wren stood in the aisle between the shelves and felt the whole vault lean toward them, the way grass leans after rain.

"Which one is yours?" Felix whispered. Ink stained their fingers to the second knuckle. They had not slept since the gate took Sable's sister from her memory, and it showed in the tremble of their hands—but their voice was steady, and steady was the only gift Wren needed right now.

Wren didn't answer with words. They couldn't have said it anyway; the First Tongue lived in them whole but unspoken, a river under ice. Instead they closed their eyes and let their feet move. Down the aisle. Past a shelf where a soldier's courage rattled faintly in its vellum. Past a jar of children's laughter that someone had thought was currency. Their hand rose on its own, the way a compass needle rises—not choosing north, only remembering it.

Third shelf. Second row. A slim black book with no title.

The moment their fingers touched the cover, the ice broke.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like the first breath after nearly drowning, like sunlight on skin that had forgotten warmth existed, like being *called home* by someone who had been calling for years. The name came up through Wren's chest the way a spring comes up through stone—unstoppable, ancient, entirely theirs—

"Wren." Felix's hand closed over theirs. "Not yet. Wait."

*Why?*

But Felix wasn't looking at them. Felix was looking up, past the shelves, toward the ceiling of the vault—which was trembling. Fine grit sifted down through the lamplight. Somewhere far above, iron was meeting iron, and the mountain itself groaned like an old man asked to carry too much.

"They're through," Ivetta's voice carried down the stairwell, clipped and calm the way it went calm only when things were worst. "Veyra's sappers breached the upper gallery. We have minutes. Perhaps fewer."

"Then we do it now," Felix said.

"No." Wren surprised themselves by speaking aloud—in trade-tongue, clumsy, but theirs. "You said... you said I should know it first. Before anyone writes it. Before anything." Their hands shook on the black book. "I want to hear it. Once. Just once. Even if the sky falls after."

Felix looked at them for a long moment. Then they did something Wren would remember for the rest of their life: they stepped back. All the way back, out of arm's reach, out of the circle of lamplight, leaving Wren alone with the book and the breathing dark.

"It's yours," Felix said quietly. "It was always yours. No one else gets to open it first."

* * *

Wren opened the ledger.

The pages did not look like pages. They looked like a window held up to a fire—the soul-page glowed from within, letters moving slow as blood through a heart, written in the First Tongue in a hand that was *their own hand*, though younger, though untouched. This was what Veyra's agents had carried down the sunken road six years ago. This was what Corvane had signed away with a shaking pen in a guildhall that smelled of ink and cheap wine: a child's whole self, bound and sold, so that old men could keep a roof over their heads one winter longer.

And there, at the top of the page, in letters that predated every alphabet Wren had ever seen scratched in dirt or carved in fence posts:

Their name.

Wren read it. And reading was speaking, because that was the nature of the First Tongue—to see a true name is already to say it. The syllables rose off the page and into Wren's mouth without permission, without effort, the way water finds the sea.

They spoke it aloud.

The world changed.

Not loudly—that was the strange part. No thunder, no shattering glass. Just a single word spoken by a mud-soaked child in a vault beneath a dying mountain, and yet every soul on every shelf turned toward the sound like flowers toward dawn. The breathing of the vault stuttered, deepened, became something almost like *hope*. Wren felt the name settle into their body the way a keel settles into water—everything suddenly balanced, suddenly steerable, suddenly *theirs*. Their hands were their hands. Their heartbeat was their heartbeat. Six years of borrowed living fell away like a cloak, and underneath it stood someone Wren had never met and had always been.

Then they began to cry, because joy this large has nowhere to go but out through the eyes.

"I heard it," Felix whispered. Their voice cracked clean in half. "Gods above, Wren, I *heard* it. It sounds like—" They stopped, laughed wetly, tried again. "It sounds like morning."

"Write it," Wren managed. "Before they take it twice."

* * *

Felix worked fast, but not carelessly—that was the difference between a Ledgerkeeper and a thief, and Wren watched them honor it even now, even with dust raining from the vault's ceiling and boots audible on the stairwell above. They uncorked the ink Sable had brewed from grave-lichen and gate-water. They laid the forged page—the beautiful lie they'd built in the court's own script—beside the true one. Then they dipped the pen, and paused.

"Say it again," Felix said. "Slowly. As I write. The name needs your voice under my hand or it won't take. That's how the First Tongue works—it's not a language you write. It's a language you *witness*."

So Wren spoke their name, slowly, syllable by syllable, and Felix wrote.

And Wren understood, watching the letters appear, why Veyra wanted First Tongue speakers by the cartload. Each true name was a key. Enough keys and you could unlock anything—armies, cities, the loyalty of men. Veyra wanted to be the only person in the world whose words made things real. She wanted everyone else to be *blank pages.*

Over my dead body, Wren thought, and found they meant it literally, gladly.

The last stroke fell. The ink flared white.

Every lamp in the vault guttered at once—and then relit, burning a color Wren had no word for except *yes*. The soul-page in the black book lifted free of its binding like a leaf off a branch, drifted up, and poured itself into Wren's chest in a ribbon of light. Somewhere far below the floor, Wren felt the vault's foundations shift—not breaking, but *unlocking*. Shelf after shelf, case after case, ten thousand sealed names felt the change in the air the way dogs feel weather.

One seal popped. Then another. Then fifty, a hundred, a thousand—a cascade of soft sounds like ice going out on a river in spring.

"What did you do?" Ivetta shouted from the stairs.

"Nothing!" Felix called back, laughing now, ink-spattered and wild-eyed. "We did nothing! *Wren* happened!"

The vault of names was emptying itself. Souls were rising out of their bindings in threads of pale fire, streaming upward through cracks in the stone, through vents, through the mountain's own broken bones—up toward the battle, up toward the daylight, up toward every name-taker in Veyra's army who was about to discover that the power they held was not theirs and never had been. Wren stood in the middle of the storm with their arms out and their name ringing in their ears, and for the first time in their life they were not afraid of anything at all.

Then the stairwell door burst open, and the fear came back all at once.

Three of Veyra's name-takers stood in the doorway, robed in grey, faces hidden behind porcelain masks scored with stolen sigils. The tallest raised a hand, and Wren felt it immediately—a hook behind the navel, a pulling, a cold voice in the First Tongue snarling *surrender, surrender, surrender*.

But the name-takers had never tried to pull a name that had just been spoken by its own mouth.

The command hit Wren like a wave hits a lighthouse—and broke. The stolen sigils on the masks flickered. One of the three staggered. Another reached for the nearest shelf, desperate, grasping—and the shelf gave them nothing. Every binding within reach was empty. The vault had already given everything away.

"You're too late," Wren said, in the First Tongue, in their own voice, and the words came out with weight behind them, the way words are supposed to come out. "There's nothing left here to take. Only us. And we don't belong to you anymore."

The masked figure tilted its head. And then, in a voice like dry paper tearing, it answered in kind:

"*No.* You misunderstand, little speaker. We weren't sent to stop you." A pause, and beneath the porcelain, something that might have been a smile. "We were sent to ask you a question. From her."

Above them, the mountain groaned again, louder, and a crack ran across the vault ceiling like lightning drawn in stone. Dust fell. The lamps burned that impossible color. And the name-taker stepped forward into the light, and asked:

"The Usurper wants to know—who taught a sold child to speak?"
