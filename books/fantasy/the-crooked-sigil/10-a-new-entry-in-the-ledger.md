## 10. A New Entry in the Ledger

The light that met them at the mountain's mouth was the color of old pewter, and it hurt Felix's eyes after so long in the dark. He had forgotten, in the vault's inkless depths, what daylight did to things—how it made everything honest, stripped of shadow's mercy. It showed the battlefield below for exactly what it was.

Veyra's vanguard lay broken across the valley like a dropped string of black beads. Not shattered by any army Felix could see—there was no army, only the name-takers themselves, hundreds of them, standing amid their own abandoned siege lines with their hoods thrown back and their faces turned toward the sun as if they had never seen it before. Some were weeping. Some were laughing. One woman knelt in the mud holding her own hands, turning them over and over, mouthing something Felix could not hear but understood anyway: *mine, mine, these are mine.*

They had come down the mountain into a world already changing shape around them. The freed name-takers had turned on their masters' soldiers not with fury but with recognition—walking through the ranks, speaking names aloud, and where the names fell true, men dropped their weapons and remembered who they were. A colonel sat on an overturned supply cart, staring at a letter from his daughter as though he'd never read it. A whole company simply walked away, eastward, toward homes most of them had half-forgotten they had.

Wren stood beside Felix at the mountain's threshold, small and mud-colored still, but luminous somehow, the way a lamp is luminous—not brighter than the dark around it, only refusing to be part of it. The child's hand found Felix's without looking. Fingers closed around his. And Felix felt, absurdly, that he was being held up more than holding.

"They're saying their names," Wren said. In the common tongue now; Wren used the First Tongue less since the rewriting, saving it like sweetmeats. "All of them. All at once. Listen."

Felix listened. The wind off the valley carried a sound like rain, or like pages turning—a vast soft susurrus of people remembering themselves. Somewhere in it he thought he heard the magistrate's voice, thin and furious and very far beneath the earth, cursing a ledger that had begun to empty. But that was under the mountain, and under the mountain was not their country anymore.

Sable came up beside him, gray with exhaustion, her forger's fingers stained to the second knuckle with vault-ink that would never wash out entirely. She looked at the broken vanguard, at the walking dead made walking-alive, and spat neatly into the mud.

"Good," she said. "Now someone owes me breakfast."

* * *

They searched three days for Corvane.

Not because they expected to find him. Because they needed, all four of them—Ivetta had caught up with them on the second morning, armor scorched, testimony intact—to walk the places he had been. The guildhall first, its door swinging open at Sable's kick, dust lying over the counting tables like the first snow of a long winter. Then the taverns along Ledger Street, then the little room above the chandlery where he had kept a bed he never slept in and books he never read. Then, at last, the river.

He was nowhere. That was the whole of it. Nowhere, and no one.

It was Ivetta who understood it first, because Ivetta had spent her life learning how memory worked in the bodies of soldiers—the way grief lived in the shoulders, the way a name lived in the spine. She stood in the guildhall's great reading room, where the ledgers had been shelved, and said quietly: "Ask me who trained you, Felix."

"You did. After the purge. When you came to us—" He stopped.

"Before that," she said. "Who ran this guild? Who taught you cataloguing? Who drank himself blind every night in that chair by the window?"

And Felix reached for the answer and found—nothing. Not an absence shaped like a man, which is what grief feels like. A true nothing, smooth as a page scraped clean. He tried to remember Corvane's face and got instead the chair, the window, the smell of sour wine, the weight of a voice—but no voice attached to any mouth. He tried to say the name and his tongue refused it, sliding off *Corvane* like rain off waxed cloth.

"He paid with his name," Felix whispered. "The whole of it. Not just for Wren. For the exchange to hold, the court took—everything. Every record of him. Every memory. We're the only ones who can even try, because we were under the mountain when it happened. Outside the working."

Sable sat down slowly in the chair by the window—the chair, though none of them could say whose chair it had been—and put her head in her ink-stained hands. "Then he's not dead," she said. "Death would be kinder. Death leaves a grave. This leaves nothing to grieve *properly*. Just a shape in the furniture." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I hated him. I hated him for thirty years and now I can't even hate him properly, because there's no one left to be the object of it."

Wren climbed into the chair beside her, into the space between her arm and her ribs, the way children do when they have decided someone belongs to them. "I remember him," Wren said. "A little. His voice sounded like a door closing. But kind. Doors closing can be kind, if they close behind you to keep the cold out."

No one spoke for a long time. Outside, the city bells began to ring—not the alarm-peals of the last weeks but something looser, arrhythmic, the way bells ring when the ringers have stopped following orders and started following joy. Veyra's advance was broken. By nightfall, the criers were already changing their song in the market squares: the Usurper's name-takers had deserted en masse; her armies were dissolving like sugar in water; the free cities, which had spent a year preparing to burn, were instead lighting festival lamps.

Felix stood at the window and watched the lamps come on one by one across the rooftops, and felt the strangest thing: grief and belonging in the same breath, braided so tightly he could not tell which strand was which. They had won. Someone had paid for the winning, and the payment had been designed so that no one would ever know there had been a payer. That was the cruelest economy Felix had ever recorded—and he had catalogued relics made of teeth and prayers sold by the pound.

"We keep him," Felix said finally. "That's our work now. If the world won't remember him, we will. We'll write him back—not his name, we can't, the court owns it—but everything else. What he loved. Why he lied. What it cost him. Every year, on the day the vault emptied, we read it aloud."

Sable looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes, and something moved through her face that might have been the beginning of forgiveness—for Corvane, for herself, for all the debts that had bound them together so long neither of them remembered anymore which of them owed which.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing this guild has ever done."

* * *

They reopened the guildhall on a morning in late spring, when the wisteria on the neighboring wall had gone riotous and the air smelled of beeswax and new paper.

There was no ceremony. Ivetta had suggested one—banners, dignitaries, a speech—and Felix had refused, gently. The old guild had loved ceremony; ceremony was how you dressed up a transaction until it stopped looking like what it was. Instead they simply unlocked the doors and propped them open with the two bronze doorstops Corvane had bought at auction decades ago, and waited to see who would come.

They came. Of course they came. Word travels faster than armies.

A woman arrived first, clutching a scrap of vellum on which her grandmother's soul-page had been recorded before the purges. Her grandmother had died nameless, sold to some broker in Veyra's early years, and the family had carried the shame of it like a stone in the chest. Felix sat with her at the long reading table while Sable verified the entry and Ivetta drafted the petition to the court beneath the mountain—because the court still existed, diminished, furious, bound now by the precedents Felix had forced upon it in the magistrate's own chamber. Names could be returned. There was a process. There was, unbelievably, a *form*.

Then a boy whose brother had been taken by Veyra's harvesters. Then an old priest with three souls in his keeping and no idea how to give them back. Then a farmer, a widow, a pair of twins who spoke between themselves in a language that made the ink crawl in its pots—First Tongue, alive in the world, no longer something to be hunted.

By midsummer the hall smelled of bread as well as ink; refugees slept in the reading room; Wren taught sigils in the mornings to anyone who wished to learn, and learned, in the afternoons, how to be an ordinary child—which was its own difficult curriculum, and Wren approached it with the same fierce fluency that had once carried them through the sunken roads.

The ledgers changed too. Sable ruled out the old columns and drew new ones in her precise forger's hand: not *acquisitions*, but *returns*. Not *holdings*, but *debts outstanding*. At the top of the first new page, in letters Felix had watched her cut with a graver's care, she wrote the guild's new purpose, and beneath it the only motto any of them would ever need:

*What was written can be unwritten. What was stolen can come home.*

On the first anniversary of the vault's emptying, they gathered in the reading room at dusk—Felix, Sable, Ivetta, Wren—and Felix read aloud from the book they had made, the one no court could seize because it existed only in their keeping and their memories. It contained no name, only a life: a man who drank too much and lied badly and loved a guild that did not deserve him, and who gave the only thing he had left so that a child could belong to themselves.

When he finished, Wren lit a candle and set it in the window, facing the river, facing the mountain.

"For the door closing behind us," Wren said, "keeping the cold out."

* * *

It was near midnight, the others long abed, when Felix made the entry himself.

He sat at the master desk with a fresh ledger open before him, the good paper, the ink Sable mixed with vault-black and ordinary lamp-oil so that it would serve both worlds. The hall breathed around him—settling timbers, the tick of cooling candle-wax, Wren's slow sleeping breath from the loft above. He dipped the pen. He thought about what belonged on this page.

*Entry the first,* he wrote, in his careful apprentice's hand, grown surer now than any master's. *Recorded freely, returned freely.*

And then he paused, because the door of the guildhall—which he had locked, he was certain he had locked—stood open to the night, and on the threshold stood a figure in traveling clothes gone gray with road-dust, holding out a scrap of vellum with both hands, the way you offer something fragile, or something holy.

"I was told," the stranger said, in a voice like a door closing, "that this is where lost names are brought home."

Felix rose slowly, heart hammering against his ribs like a bird against glass. The ink on the fresh page shivered, though there was no wind. He looked at the stranger's face—weathered, unfamiliar, utterly unknown—and felt, beneath the impossibility of it, beneath the terror and the wild incandescent hope tangled together in his throat, a recognition deeper than memory.

Some part of him knew that voice. Some part of him had been keeping it all along.

"Yes," Felix said, and reached for the ledger. "Come in. Tell me your name."
