## 1. Charms for the Godless

The fever-charves sold best in the rain.

Silas had learned that in her first year in Greyhollow, when the damp crept through the river districts and settled into lungs like a tenant who never paid. Rain meant mothers with hollow eyes and coins counted twice, rain meant the luck-knots moved too, because desperate people wanted every kind of insurance. She strung her stall under the leaning awning of a shuttered chandler's shop on Vetch Street and waited, hands folded, looking like exactly what she pretended to be: a plain woman selling folk remedies to people too poor or too proud for the physickers' guild.

The charves themselves were honest work. Dried coltsfoot, honey gone slightly crystalline, a whisper of willow bark—recipes older than the Spire, older than the wards that smothered every spark of true magic inside Greyhollow's borders. A woman could sell those openly. What she couldn't sell openly was the rest of it: the way she held each knot of red thread a half-second longer than knotting required, the way she breathed a word into the wool—wordless now, shapeless, a habit worn smooth by seven years of denial—and the way the charms worked better than coltsfoot and honey had any right to explain.

Small workings. That was all she allowed herself. So small the Spire's wards barely noticed, so small even she could pretend they were nothing—a warmth in the thread, a nudge toward luck, a fever eased a night sooner than nature intended. Candles in a gale. But she'd learned the wards here were old and crooked themselves, full of gaps where the Spire's geometry bent wrong, and in those gaps a careful woman could keep one candle lit.

A careful woman. Seven years of being one, and some mornings Silas woke not knowing her own face in the wash-basin's grey reflection.

"Charve," said a voice, and there was Maren Odo, the cooper's widow, holding out two coppers with the shame she always carried about paying. Silas took the coins without ceremony—that was kindness too—and passed over the twist of waxed paper.

"For the little one's cough?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "It's past coughing." The words came out flat, the way people said terrible things when they'd said them to themselves so often the horror had been sanded off. "Physicker says it's in the blood now. Says—" She stopped. Started again. "Says there's nothing."

*Nothing.* The word dropped through Silas like a stone through still water. There was a protocol for this. There had always been a protocol: sympathy, a free charve, an instruction to keep the child warm and let the body do its slow work or fail at it. Seven years of protocols. Seven years of being no one, because Odile's last lesson before she vanished had been written in a girl's blood on a burned waystation floor: *they will look for you, and if they find you, everything I did was for nothing.*

"I'll come round this evening," Silas heard herself say. "See what I can do. No charge."

Maren stared at her. "You're a herb-seller."

"I'm a woman who knows fevers." It wasn't even a lie. That was the cruelest part of her disguise—it was built entirely from true things.

* * *

The Odo house stood on Cobble Lane where the street gave up pretending to be straight, squeezed between a rope-walk and a tannery that made the whole block smell of lime and ruin. Inside it smelled instead of tallow and sickness, that sweetish edge a fever leaves in a room when it has lived there long enough to feel like a resident.

Pell Odo was seven years old and looked five. He lay in the box bed beneath the window, and his breathing had that sound—the wet rattle, the hitching catch, the rhythm of small lungs filling with what they couldn't hold. His mother sat beside him with her hand on his forehead as if she could hold the heat in place by touch alone.

Silas set down her satchel and did the ordinary things first. Cool cloth. Willow tea. She turned the boy's thin wrist and read the pulse the way a physicker would, counting beats against the drip of the eaves outside. Then she sat back on the stool and faced the truth she'd been walking away from since Vetch Street.

She could ease him. Not cure—her gift had never been that, even whole, even at twelve years old before Odile took it. But she could unknit the fever from the blood, pull the heat out strand by strand the way you'd unpick a snarl from wool. She'd done it once before, years ago, for a stranger on a road, and had spent three weeks after listening to every cart that slowed near her door, certain it was witch-finders coming.

Seven years. Seven years of candles in a gale, of luck-knots and half-measures, of watching people die politely around her edges because intervening would cost more than their lives were worth to her invisibility. And here was a child whose breath sounded like water in a broken pipe, and here was the protocol, and here was Silas, hating herself with a familiarity that felt almost like comfort.

"Pell," she said softly. "Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. Grey, glassy, far too old. He nodded, a tiny motion.

"I'm going to help you breathe easier. It might feel strange. Don't be frightened."

She took his hand. Small, hot, damp. And then—looking at Maren, giving her a nod that said *this is only medicine, only craft, nothing to fear*—she reached for the gap in the wards.

It was always like slipping sideways. Greyhollow's suppression lay over the city like a second sky, but the Crooked Spire had been built bent, deliberately, gloriously bent, and its bending left seams. Silas found one the way a blind woman finds a familiar wall, leaned into it, and let herself remember.

The magic came up through her like water through cracked ground. It didn't feel stolen or sealed or diminished; that was the lie she'd told herself for seven years, that what remained was scraps. What remained was *hers*, and it rose glad, rising the way a dog rises when its person comes home, and Silas had to bite down on a sound that was half laugh and half grief.

*Unknit,* she thought, shaping the working with hands that remembered even if the power behind them had been rationed to whispers. *Unknit and carry out.*

Heat began to move. She felt it leave Pell's chest in threads, drawn up through his arm, through her palm, dispersing into the seam between worlds where the wards wouldn't taste it. The rattle in his breathing loosened. Loosened again. On the bed, the boy's shoulders dropped from their hunched guarding, and he sighed—a child's sigh, deep and easy, the sigh of someone surfacing from deep water.

"There," Silas whispered. "There."

And that was when the light happened.

She hadn't meant it. She'd been careless with joy, and joy is the least containable thing a magician carries. Her working flared visible for half a heartbeat—gold-white filaments lifting off the boy's skin like heat-shimmer made solid, curling in the air above the bed, beautiful and utterly unmistakable, nothing any herb or prayer had ever produced.

Maren Odo screamed.

Not loudly. That was worse somehow—the scream came out strangled, clapped behind both hands, her eyes enormous, fixed on the fading glow and then on Silas with an expression Silas knew from stories and had hoped never to see aimed at her: the look of a person realizing the thing under their roof was the thing the bells warned about.

"You're—" Maren's voice shook. "That's spellwork. That's—witch-finders hang—"

"Listen to me." Silas kept her voice low and level while her heart tried to claw its way out through her ribs. "What you saw was fever-breaking. Old remedy-craft. Nothing more."

"That was *not*—"

"Maren." She held the widow's gaze. "Your son can breathe. In three days he'll be asking for supper. Whatever you decide I am, decide it knowing that."

The silence stretched, taut as wire. Below them, out on Cobble Lane, a cart rattled past, and both women flinched at the sound. Somewhere across the city, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, not the alarm. The *finding* bell, the one the witch-finders rang when they swept a district. Probably routine. Probably nowhere near.

Probably.

Maren sank onto the bed's edge. Pell, drifting toward real sleep for the first time in days, mumbled something about supper. When the widow finally spoke, her voice had changed—shame replacing terror, which was its own kind of answer.

"He was dying," she said. "I know what I saw. And he was *dying*, and now he isn't." She wiped her face with her apron. "Get your things and go. And Silas—" She swallowed. "I never saw anything. But don't come back here. Please. For all our sakes."

* * *

Silas walked home the long way, through streets going amber with lamplight, and let herself shake only when she reached the stairs of her own lodging, one hand on the rail.

Nearly. So nearly. Half a heartbeat of visible gold in a city where the Spire's wardens burned people for less, and all because she'd been happy about it. Happy. She pressed her palms against her eyes and laughed, quietly, at the absurdity—seven years invisible, undone by relief over a cooper's son.

But underneath the fear, treacherous and warm, sat the other thing. The feeling of her magic rising glad. Of doing, finally, after seven years of almost-doing, the actual work she'd been born for. The loneliness of her half-lived life had never hurt quite so much as it did tonight, standing in contrast to ten minutes of being whole.

She lit no lamp. Through her narrow window she could see it across the rooftops: the Crooked Spire, black against a sky just beginning to show stars, leaning its impossible lean like a drunkard leaning on air. She'd looked at it every night for seven years with hatred, or indifference, or the practiced blindness of a woman avoiding her own reflection.

Tonight she caught herself studying it. The way its bend seemed wrong in a way she'd never let herself examine. The way, if you looked long enough, the lean almost seemed deliberate. Almost seemed like architecture rather than accident.

Almost seemed like a cage.

Down in the street, the finding bell had stopped. Silas exhaled, told herself the day's danger was done, and began unlacing her boots.

Then the stars went out.

All of them. At once. Not clouded—*gone*, every point of light above Greyhollow snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, leaving a sky blacker than any night she had ever seen.

And from the direction of the border wards, distant and terrible, came a sound like glass breaking—if the glass were a mile wide, and falling forever.
