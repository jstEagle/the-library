## 2. The Night Without Stars

The sound woke her at the hour when even Greyhollow's rats went quiet.

Not a crash. Not thunder. Something worse—something delicate. A crack, high and thin as ice giving way underfoot, that didn't stop. It spread, note after note, each one higher than the last, until it wasn't a sound anymore but a sensation behind Silas Voss's teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the scar tissue over her heart where her magic used to live.

She was out of bed before she understood why, bare feet on cold floorboards, one hand pressed flat against the wall.

*It's the wards,* something in her said. *Something just broke the wards.*

Then every star in the sky went out.

She saw it through the gap in her shutters—saw the whole constellation-strewn dark above Greyhollow simply cease, like a candle pinched between enormous fingers. No fade. No cloud. One heartbeat the stars were there, ancient and indifferent, and the next the sky was a smooth black lid pressing down on the city.

Silas stood very still.

Seven years of practice had taught her to stillness. Seven years of keeping her head down and her hands empty and her gift folded up small beneath her ribs, wrapped in the mental bindings Odile had taught her before Odile walked out of her life and left her alone in a city that hanged people like her. She had gotten so good at being nothing that sometimes she forgot what she was.

But now the thing inside her stirred.

It had never done that. Not once in seven years. She kept it bound the way you'd keep a wild animal sedated—constant, gentle pressure, a discipline so ingrained it felt like breathing. And now, against all her training, it pressed back. It rose against its restraints like a bird throwing itself at cage bars, wings beating, and for one vertiginous moment Silas felt it *reach*, straining toward that broken sound in the night, toward the vanished stars, toward—

Toward home, she thought wildly. It wants to go *home.*

"Stop," she whispered aloud, to herself, to it. "Stop, stop."

It didn't stop. The pressure built, sweet and terrible, and her fingertips prickled with light she refused to look at. She clenched her fist, drove the bindings down, held them down with sweat rolling between her shoulder blades, until finally—finally—the caged thing settled into a low, continuous tremble, like a struck bell still humming.

Outside, Greyhollow began to scream.

* * *

She dressed in the dark because lighting a lamp felt obscene. Wool coat, boots, the knife she'd stopped carrying three years ago and started again last month without admitting why. In the street, doors were banging open along Cobbler's Row, and people poured out in their nightclothes, faces tilted up to the starless sky.

"The Spire," someone said. "Look at the Spire."

Silas looked.

The Crooked Spire stood where it always stood, leaning drunkenly over the Temple District, warped like a melted candle, wrong in the way it had been wrong for two hundred years. Every child in Greyhollow learned the story: the Spire had bent on the night of the Godswar's end, when the last great magicians gave their lives to seal something away. The wards that smothered all spellwork in the city radiated from its crooked crown. That was the bargain of Greyhollow: no magic here, ever, and in exchange, safety.

Except the wards were supposed to be *inside* the border too. And the border—the ring of pale standing stones nine miles out, the first line of the covenant—had just shattered with a sound like breaking glass.

And the Spire was glowing.

Faintly. Wrongly. A sick amber pulse at its tip, slow as a dying man's breath, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming. Silas had lived in its shadow seven years and had never once seen it do anything at all.

"It's falling," a woman near her said. Her voice had gone flat with shock. "The border wards. My husband's a carter—he was on the Salt Road tonight. He said the stones were humming." She looked down at her own hands as if they belonged to someone else. "If the border's gone, then the city wards are all that's left. All that's left between us and whatever's out there."

Whatever's *in here,* Silas thought, and immediately hated herself for thinking it.

Because she knew things about the Godswar that Greyhollow's children weren't taught. She knew the seal hadn't been simple, hadn't been clean, hadn't been only sacrifice. Odile had drunk too much once, years ago, and talked in circles, and some of those circles had contained sentences like *the lock needs a key* and *nothing is sealed for free.* Silas had been nineteen and grieving and hadn't asked. She'd spent seven years not asking.

Now the sky had no stars and the Spire pulsed like a wound and the magic under her ribs wouldn't lie still, and every unasked question came due at once.

A bell began to ring from the Temple District. Then another. Then dozens, overlapping, arrhythmic, urgent—not the measured tolling of services but the frantic clatter of alarm. Witch-finders' bells.

Across the square, torchlight flared. Figures in grey-and-scarlet livery moved through the crowd with the terrible calm of people who had trained their whole lives for this night. Captain Ilsa Renn's witch-finders, going door to door already, though it couldn't have been more than twenty minutes since the sky broke. As if they'd known. As if they'd been *waiting.*

"Ward-tappers!" one shouted, voice cracking across the panic. "Anyone caught working spellcraft, anyone harboring spellcraft, speaks to no one, shelters no one! By order of the Conclave, the city is under curfew! Return to your homes!"

People scattered. People always scattered. But Silas noticed—and hated noticing—that some didn't. Some stood rooted, looking up at the black sky with expressions that weren't fear. An old woman by the fountain with river-mud dried in the creases of her knuckles. Two young men holding hands too tightly, wearing charms around their necks they thought were hidden. The charm-seller's boy from Fishmarket, who sold fever-charves and pretended they were folk remedies, exactly the way a woman named Silas Voss did.

They were looking up at the dead stars like people watching a door open after a long imprisonment.

*Part of you is glad,* said the treacherous voice in her chest, and this time it wasn't the magic talking. It was just her. *The world is ending, and part of you is glad, because if it's ending anyway, then maybe you can stop hiding. Maybe the hiding is over whether you choose it or not.*

She turned for her door. Duty of invisibility: go inside, bar the shutters, be nobody until morning.

She got four steps.

Then she heard it, cutting through the bells and the screaming: a child crying, high and desperate, from the alley behind the chandler's shop. And beneath the crying—a sound Silas knew intimately, a sound like the air tearing, like the world developing a seam.

Ward-work. Someone was doing ward-work in that alley, clumsy and raw and terrified, and the Spire's suppression should have crushed it flat, and it wasn't crushing it flat, which meant the wards were weaker than they'd been an hour ago, which meant—

Which meant everything was coming apart, all at once, everywhere.

Silas stood in the street with her fists clenched and the caged thing in her chest beating its wings, and made the worst decision of her life, or possibly the first honest one.

She ran toward the sound.

* * *

The alley stank of tallow and terror. A girl of maybe twelve stood backed against the chandler's wall, hands raised, and around her hands flickered a warding-shield so badly constructed it was hurting her—the edges fraying, sparks crawling up her wrists, her nose bleeding freely. Facing her, backing her down with a drawn bell-censer swinging slow arcs, was a witch-finder Silas didn't recognize, young, frightened, his voice pitched high:

"—last warning, little witch, put it *down*, the Conclave will show mercy if you—"

The shield guttered. The girl sobbed. The censer swung back.

Silas didn't decide anything. Her body decided for her, seven years of buried instinct detonating all at once. She stepped out of the shadows, dropped her mental bindings for exactly one heartbeat—one, no more—and pushed.

It was like opening a door onto a hurricane. Power roared through her, vast and starving and *joyful*, and she shaped it into the simplest thing she knew: a knot of stillness, a luck-knot the size of a fist, thrown not woven. It hit the censer mid-arc and the bell-curse died with a sound like a snapped string, and the witch-finder stumbled backward staring at his own empty hand.

For one shining second the girl's shield steadied, strengthened, blazed clean white—and Silas felt it all, felt the girl's raw talent brush against hers, felt the night feel *open*, felt the vanished stars like a missing tooth her tongue kept finding—

Then she slammed the bindings closed and staggered, gasping, half-blind with afterimages.

"Run," she rasped at the girl. "Run home. Never do that again."

The girl ran. The witch-finder stared at Silas—at her empty, trembling, ordinary-looking hands—and fumbled for his whistle.

Silas ran the other way, into the dark, with the whole crooked city howling around her and the Spire pulsing overhead like a slow heartbeat, and the caged thing beneath her ribs singing, singing, singing.

She had been invisible for seven years.

It took the sky eleven minutes to end that.
