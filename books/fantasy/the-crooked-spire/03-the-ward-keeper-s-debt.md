## 3. The Ward-Keeper's Debt

They took Tam Ashgrave's badge at noon and his name by nightfall.

The badge he expected. The name was crueler. In Greyhollow, a ward-keeper without a name was worse than a ward-keeper without a post—you couldn't testify, couldn't draw wages, couldn't be arrested properly, which meant anyone could do anything to you and call it civic improvement. The clerk at the Hall of Wards had scratched through *Ashgrave* with the same dull pen he used for dead men's estates.

"Pending inquiry," the clerk said, not looking up.

"The border fell," Tam said. "Eleven miles of it. I maintained that stretch for six years."

"And it fell on your watch." The pen scratched. "Pending inquiry."

He'd stood in the hall while they inventoried his kit—copper rods, chalk satchels, the little brass theodolite his father had left him—and felt something he hadn't let himself feel since the Night Without Stars: not fear, not shame, but a cold, clarifying anger. Because he had maintained that stretch of border. He had walked it every third day, tested every anchor-stone, filed every report. And he knew—he had *known* for months—that the readings were wrong in a way no maintenance error could explain. The wards weren't failing. They were being *unmade*, thread by thread, from somewhere inside the city.

He'd written that in three reports. All three had come back stamped PENDING.

So when they stripped his name, Tam did what disgraced men in Greyhollow have always done. He went into the undercity to find out who had unmade eleven miles of warding and why, and he decided that if the answer got him hanged, at least he'd hang knowing.

* * *

The undercity smelled of tallow and wet stone and, underneath it all, faintly of iron—the old blood-iron of Greyhollow's foundations, leaching up through the brick. Tam moved through it with his hood up and his remaining senses open. Without his rods he had to work the crude way, reading the pressure in the air with his skin, the way miners read for gas.

It was wrong everywhere. That was the thing nobody above ground understood yet. The Spire's wards didn't just smother spellwork; they pressed down on everything, a constant low ceiling over the whole city, and Tam had lived under that ceiling so long he'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. But now the ceiling had cracks in it. Pressure leaked through them like light under a door, and everywhere he walked he could feel it pooling—in cellars, in culverts, in the hollow places where the poor and the hidden made their lives.

And where magic pooled, people gathered. And where people gathered with magic, witch-finders followed.

He found the first bell-scar in a culvert off Tanner's Row: a scorched handprint on the brick, still warm after two days. Witch-finders marked their work. It was doctrine—they wanted the fear to spread faster than the news.

The second scar was fresher, and beside it, someone had dragged a body. The drag-marks went deeper into the tunnels, toward the old cisterns, and Tam should have turned around then. A man with no name and no badge had no business following drag-marks toward witch-finder work.

He followed anyway. His whole life, that had been the flaw in him. His instructors at the Ward Academy had called it initiative when they wanted to flatter him and insubordination when they didn't. Both times they'd been describing the same thing: Tam Ashgrave, walking toward the sound everyone else was walking away from.

The sound led him to the third cistern, and the sound was singing.

* * *

Not singing exactly. Chanting—a woman's voice, low and rapid, in a cadence Tam recognized from musty academy texts and had never once heard spoken aloud. Old-tongue binding-work. The kind of verse that hadn't been legal in Greyhollow since the Spire went up, the kind that got you a bell-curse or a rope depending on the finder's mood.

He came around the cistern wall and stopped.

There were maybe thirty people crammed against the far curve of the cistern—refugees from the border districts, by their look, border-folk clothes, border-folk ash still in their hair. They'd come flooding into the city when the wards fell, and the city had done what Greyhollow always did with refugees: closed its gates behind them and called it safety. No shelter assignments. No ration chits. Just the undercity, and whatever the undercity could spare.

In the center, kneeling over a boy of perhaps eight who was shaking so hard his teeth clicked, was a woman with ink-stained fingers and an expression like someone doing arithmetic she hated.

She wasn't performing. That was the first thing Tam noticed, the thing that kept his hand off the whistle he technically no longer carried. Street hedge-witches performed—big gestures, loud words, eyes flicking to see who watched. This woman worked small and fast and furious, her lips barely moving, her fingers knotting a length of red thread around the boy's wrist with the precision of a surgeon tying off a vessel. The chant wasn't for the crowd. It was for the working.

And the working was *holding*. Under the Spire's ceiling, with the wards supposedly smothering all spellwork in the city, this woman's charm was taking root like a seed in wet ground—because she'd found one of the cracks, one of the leaks, and she was working inside it as neatly as if she'd mapped it.

Tam knew exactly two people who could do that. One was dead. The other had built the Spire.

The boy's shaking slowed. Stopped. The woman sat back on her heels, and for one moment—one unguarded, exhausted moment—her face showed everything: relief, terror, and underneath it a loneliness so complete it looked like weather.

Then she saw Tam, and all of it shut behind doors.

"You're bleeding," she said.

Tam touched his temple and found she was right; he'd cracked it on a low arch somewhere back along the tunnel. "I'm looking for answers," he said. "You're looking for nothing, I hope, because I'm about to ruin your evening."

"I don't know you."

"No. You know my coat, though." He pushed his hood back. In the lamplight the refugee nearest him flinched—ward-keeper grey, even disgraced, still read as authority. "Third shift, eastern border. Or I was. As of today I'm a man with no name, which means arresting you would be volunteer work."

Something shifted in her face—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. "You're Ashgrave," said an old woman near the wall. "You came through the flood camps last spring. You signed the chits yourself when the clerks wouldn't."

"I did."

"They say the border falling is your fault."

"They say a lot of things." Tam crouched, slow, hands visible, the way you approach a spooked animal—or a magician, which amounted to the same thing in Greyhollow. "Here's what I know. Those wards didn't fail. They were unwoven, carefully, from inside the city, over months. I reported it. My reports are sitting in a pending tray, and my name is sitting in a scratch-out, and eleven miles of border is sitting open like a wound. So I'm hunting the who and the why. And you—" he nodded at the red thread on the boy's wrist, at the crack in the air above her head that only trained eyes could see, "—are the most skilled worker I've seen outside the Academy archives. Which makes you either the answer or the door to it."

"And if I'm neither?"

"Then I've wasted an evening, and you keep your secrets, and we both go on being invisible." He held out his hand, palm up. Empty. "I'm not here to arrest you. I can't arrest anyone. That's rather the point."

She studied him the way she'd probably study a suspicious charm—for structure, for flaws, for the trap hidden in the workings. Then, without touching his hand, she said: "Silas Voss. Sells luck-knots off Chandler's Walk. If you repeat that name to a single living soul, I will make your life short and inventive."

"Understood."

"Why did you sign the chits yourself?" It came out almost angry. "Last spring. The clerks wouldn't cross the waterline. You did."

Tam shrugged, uncomfortable. "Chits don't sign themselves."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have. Some things are the duty even when the duty's been taken off you." He heard how it sounded the moment it left his mouth—like a man reciting his own indictment—and Silas's mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile with no humor in it.

"Yes," she said. "I know something about that."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low, sitting apart from the sleeping refugees, voices down among the drip and echo of the cistern. She told him nothing, and told him everything: that the wards had cracks, that she could find them, that there were more workers like her than the Spire would ever admit—hidden people, careful people, people who'd spent years folding themselves small. She named no names. But she drew him a map in spilled lamp-oil on the stone: seven leak-points in the undercity, each one precise, each one matching the anomalies in his own border reports like teeth fitting a jaw.

"This isn't leakage," Tam said quietly, staring at the pattern. "This is drainage. Someone's pulling the wards' power toward a center. Something under the city is drinking."

"Or being fed," Silas said. "Depends which end of the straw you're standing at."

"And you'll help me find it. The source. Why?"

She was silent long enough that the lamp guttered twice.

"Because whoever unwove your border used stolen magic to do it," she said finally. "Old magic. And I think—I *think*—it might have been mine." Her voice didn't shake, but it went flat, the way voices go when feeling has been locked out of the room. "Seven years ago someone took something from me. I've spent seven years pretending that's survivable. Two nights ago the stars went out, and I stopped pretending."

Tam looked at her—at the ink stains, the red thread, the terrible competence of her—and recognized something he'd been carrying himself for six years without naming it: the specific exhaustion of a person who did everything right and was failed by the system anyway, and who kept serving it regardless, because the alternative was admitting the service meant nothing.

Two failures, facing each other across a dying lamp.

"Here's my offer," he said. "Your knowledge of the old workings, the leak-points, the deep structures—I need it, and you need what I am. Witch-finders hunt magicians. They don't hunt ward-keepers. Not yet. I can walk you past checkpoints. I can read the Hall's schedules. I can tell you which bells are real and which are bluff. You get my protection. I get your map. And neither of us asks the other to be sorry for what we failed to protect."

"That's a cold bargain."

"It's the only kind I can afford." He almost smiled. "Warm ones cost more than I have."

Silas looked at her own hands for a long moment. When she spoke again, it was barely above the drip of the cistern.

"There's a waystation on the Marrow Road. Burned, three years back. There's something of mine there—something I should have gone back for a long time ago." She met his eyes. "If I'm right about what it says, your border was only the first lock to fail. Help me get there before the witch-finders work out where the trail leads, and you won't just have your answer, Ashgrave. You'll have the reason the stars went dark."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of brick and stone, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm. The finder's peal, three-and-one, sweeping the districts in sequence. Coming closer.

Tam stood and pulled his hood up.

"Then we'd best move," he said, "before the bells finish counting."
