## 4. What Odile Left Behind

The waystation had been burning for three days before we found it, and it was still burning.

That should have been impossible. The rain had come down in grey sheets since Greyhollow, soaking Tam's coat through and turning the road to porridge. But there the building stood on its knoll above the river ford, black walls outlined in flame that gave no heat and cast no light on the ground around it. Cold fire. Odile's signature, if she'd ever bothered to have one. She'd always said a magician's work should be legible the way handwriting is legible—*let them know me*, she used to say, *and let them be afraid of what they read.*

I stood at the edge of the property with rain running down my neck and felt something I hadn't let myself feel in seven years: recognition. Not of the place. I'd never been here. Of the hand that had written it.

"You knew her," Tam said. It wasn't a question. He'd stopped asking questions somewhere outside the city walls, when I'd finally told him enough truth to buy his silence—the luck-knots, the fever-charms, the sealed thing under my breastbone that strained against its cage every time I breathed too deep. He hadn't arrested me. I still didn't understand why, and I'd stopped expecting to.

"My master," I said. "Odile Vane. Before the Spire went up, before the wards, before any of it. She taught me for four years."

"The woman who built the Crooked Spire." He said it flatly, the way men say things they don't believe. "The woman who vanished the night it was finished."

"She didn't vanish." I started up the muddy track toward the burning door. "She left. There's a difference, and I've spent seven years pretending there isn't."

The cold fire parted around me when I crossed the threshold. I felt it part—a sensation like walking through a curtain of static, like being weighed and measured and passed over. It knew my magic. It had been keyed to it, once, long ago. I didn't understand what that meant yet. I would, soon, and I would give anything to un-know it.

Inside, the waystation was a ruin of ordinary life: a shattered table, a bed with its straw ticking spilled across the floorboards, a child's wooden horse lying on its side with one leg snapped off. Someone had lived here recently. Someone had left in a hurry, or been made to. Scorch marks fanned out from the hearth in patterns no natural fire makes—concentric rings, deliberate, a message written in destruction.

Tam moved through the room like a man who'd done this before, checking corners, testing floorboards. Ward-keeper habits. He found the loose stone behind the hearth in less than a minute.

"Hidden cache," he said. "Standard construction. You want to do the honors, or shall I?"

I wanted to say *you*, because some cowardly part of me already knew what we'd find and wanted someone else's hands to touch it first. But my hands were already moving.

Under the stone: an oilcloth bundle, dry as bone despite everything. Inside it, a journal bound in grey leather that I knew intimately—I'd bought it for her myself, eleven years ago, from a stationer's stall on Cooper's Lane, with coins earned selling charms I wasn't supposed to be able to make. Odile had laughed when I gave it to her. *What would I write in this?* she'd said. *Confessions?*

She'd written confessions.

* * *

We sat on the floor because there was nothing else to sit on, and I read by the light of the cold fire while Tam watched the road through a gap in the shutters. Her handwriting was exactly as I remembered it—sharp, slanted, impatient with its own sentences, crossing out whole lines rather than revising them.

*Day 40. The Spire stands. They cheer it in the streets. Fools. They think it protects them. A wall does not protect you from what is inside the wall.*

I turned the page. My fingers were steady. I remember being surprised by that.

*Vhalor wakes slower than projected. The lock holds. The lock will always hold—it cannot do otherwise—but holding is not the same as sealing. A lock wants a key. Every lock dreams of its key. I have made certain the key can never reach the door.*

"Silas," Tam said quietly. "The bell towers in Greyhollow just started ringing. All of them."

"I know." I did know; I could feel it, faintly, through the soles of my boots, through the sealed place in my chest, a vibration like a struck tuning fork. "Keep reading with me. I need a witness for whatever this is."

He came and sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and read over mine. His breath smelled of cheap tobacco and wet wool. Ordinary smells. I held onto them.

*The covenant requires a living anchor. Not a sacrifice—that is crude, and crude workings fail. An anchor. A life poured into the mechanism, not spent but *set*, the way mortar is set. I searched two years for a vessel with the right resonance. Then I found her. A child in the fen country, eight years old, whose magic came in early and came in vast. The kind of power that happens once a generation, and always to someone who will waste it or be wasted by it.*

*I took it. All of it. She will not remember—memory yields cleanly at that age—and without her gift she will be nothing, invisible, safe. Safe. That is the word I will use. That is the word everyone will use.*

The page blurred. I wiped my face with my sleeve and kept reading, because stopping would have meant understanding, and understanding could wait until the last possible line.

*The anchor lives. She thinks she is powerless. She thinks she was born half-made. Every ward in Greyhollow passes over her because her magic is not hers anymore—it is the Spire's, and the Spire does not flag its own blood. Seven years she has sold luck-knots in the shadow of her own stolen self, and the witch-finders walk past her door.*

*If she ever learns—if she ever comes to the Spire knowing what she is—the lock will recognize her. The key returning to the door. And then there will be a choice, and I have built my whole life on the hope that choice never comes.*

*Her name was Silas. Is Silas. I write it here because nowhere else in the world will anyone write it again. Forgive me, girl. Or don't. Either way, you are the lock, and the lock must hold.*

I closed the journal.

The cold fire crackled soundlessly in the ruined room. Rain hit the shutters. Somewhere far off, the bells were still ringing their alarm, and beneath them, so faint it might have been my pulse, something vast was turning over in its sleep beneath the Crooked Spire.

"Say something," Tam said.

"What is there to say?" My voice came out level. That surprised me too. "I've spent seven years thinking I was broken. Born wrong. A magician's daughter with no magic in a city that burns people like me, and I thought—gods, I thought I was *lucky*. Lucky to be invisible. Lucky to slip through. I built my whole life on being a cracked vessel, and it turns out I'm not cracked. I'm *emptied*. Someone reached into me when I was eight years old and took out everything I was, and used it to build a jail cell, and then wrote down that she hoped I'd never find out, because finding out was the one thing that could undo her work."

"That's why the wards never touch you," Tam said slowly. "At the border collapse, when every hidden magician in Greyhollow lit up like a signal fire—you didn't. I checked the sweep records. Your name isn't in them. I thought you were clever."

"I'm not clever. I'm *keyed*." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "The Spire doesn't hunt me because I'm part of it. I'm the lock, Tam. I'm not a person who lives in the city the Spire protects. I'm a component. A hinge. She didn't even kill me—she did something worse. She made my whole existence into a mechanism and called it mercy."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man handling something fragile. "My grandmother used to say the Spire was crooked because it bowed under the weight of what it carried. Everyone thought it was a drunkard's rhyme."

"It's not a rhyme." I looked at the journal in my lap—at her handwriting, sharp and slanted and patient, patient for eleven years, patient for my whole life. "It's a confession. She's telling me where to go. This whole book is a letter addressed to the day I'd find it. She *wanted* me to follow the trail. Why burn the waystation in cold fire—her handwriting, her signature—unless she wanted it legible? Unless she wanted me reading?"

"Or she's dead and someone else burned it."

"No." I stood. The certainty arrived whole, the way old knowledge arrives—not learned but remembered, rising out of the emptied place inside me like water filling a well. "She's alive. She's dying, maybe. But she's alive, and she's waiting, and she knows what's coming. The wards fell, Tam. The stars went out. The lock is failing, and the lock is *me*, which means the answer isn't in Greyhollow and it isn't in this journal. It's at the top of the Spire, where she built her cage, standing in front of the door I was made to be."

Outside, the cold fire guttered once—all at once, every flame bending toward the north, toward the city, like grass bowing in wind. Like something answering a call.

Tam rose beside me. "Witch-finders will have found this place by morning. Maybe sooner. The bells—"

"Then we're gone before morning." I tucked the journal inside my coat, against my chest, against the hollow where my magic used to live. It fit there as if it had always belonged. "There's a drainage grate two miles back, before the ford. You told me about it your first night—undercity access, pre-Spire construction. You said it like a man reciting an escape route he'd memorized for someone else's benefit."

"I said it like a disgraced ward-keeper who knows all the ways into and out of his own city," Tam said. "Including the ones he was fired for knowing."

"Good." I looked back once at the burning waystation—at the child's wooden horse on the floor, one leg snapped off, and I thought: *that was me. Eight years old, and someone broke me and left me lying in an empty room.* "Because I'm done being invisible, Tam. Whatever I am—lock, key, hinge, girl—I'm done letting other people decide."

We went out into the rain. Behind us the cold fire burned on, patient as its maker, lighting nothing, warming no one.

And far away, beneath the crooked stone, something that spoke in my own childhood voice began, very softly, to say my name.
