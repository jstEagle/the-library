## 5. The Witch-Finder's Bell

The bell found them before the witch-finders did.

Silas heard it first as a wrongness in the air—a thin silver note threading through the market crowd on Cooper's Row, where they'd gone to buy bread and, if Tam had his way, answers. The sound didn't travel so much as *arrive*, blooming in her ears like a needle finding a vein. Around her, shoppers kept haggling over cabbages and candle-tallow, oblivious. But every hidden magician in Greyhollow would have felt it: the Finder's Bell, rung once, singing her name.

*She's here. She's marked. Run.*

"Tam." Her hand closed on his sleeve. "We're made."

He didn't ask how he knew. Seven years of ward-keeping had taught him the city's grammar of alarms, and the Bell was the oldest word in it. He turned slowly, casually, scanning the crowd the way she'd watched him scan a hundred rooms since the border fell—reading faces, reading shadows.

"There," he said quietly. "Green cloak, by the fountain. And the woman selling chestnuts who hasn't sold a chestnut all morning."

Three of them, then. Maybe more. Captain Ilsa Renn would not have come herself for an ordinary runaway. But Silas Voss was not ordinary anymore. She was the thread that unraveled from a burned waystation to a stolen childhood, and Renn knew it.

"The undercity entrance is four streets east," Tam murmured. "We walk. We don't run until we have to."

They walked. Silas counted her heartbeats and tried to make them slow. The crowd pressed warm around them, smelling of wet wool and frying onions, and she thought absurdly: *I have sold luck-knots to half these people. Mrs. Anselm bought three last winter. The cooper's boy wears my fever-charve under his shirt.* All those small transactions of care, and not one of them could help her now. Invisibility had been a shield, but shields only work until someone decides to look.

The bell rang again. Closer. It wasn't sound anymore—it was pressure, a finger pressing directly on the sealed door inside her chest where Odile had locked away everything Silas was. The seal held. But something behind it turned over in its sleep.

"They're converging," Tam said. His voice had gone flat and professional, the voice of a man doing arithmetic with lives. "Renn wants us alive. That's our one advantage—she'll close the net slow rather than risk breaking what she needs."

"And when she has me?"

"Then you tell her nothing, and I find some way to be useful from whatever cell they put me in." He almost smiled. "I've had practice being useless in official buildings."

The joke landed soft and landed anyway, and Silas hated how much she needed it. Four days ago he'd been a stranger holding a warrant. Now his shoulder brushed hers in the crowd and she felt safer for it, and safety was a debt, and debts were how people like her got people like him killed.

The fountain was behind them now. The green cloak had fallen back. For three whole streets, hope was a live thing in her chest.

Then the alley ahead filled with grey coats, and Captain Ilsa Renn stepped out of it like a knife being drawn.

* * *

Tam saw the trap close and did the math in half a second: two exits, both covered, six witch-finders, one captain, and between himself and all of it a woman whose magic was locked behind a seal he still didn't fully understand. Bad odds. The kind of odds he'd spent eleven years learning to survive by never facing.

Renn was smaller than her reputation. Grey hair cropped short, a face like a folded map, and eyes that had already finished deciding about you before you opened your mouth. She carried no weapon he could see. She didn't need one. At her belt hung a small brass bell, dull with age, its clapper wrapped in black cord.

"Silas Voss." Her voice was dry paper. "You've cost me two good keepers and a week of sleep. I'd like that to be the end of it."

"You have the wrong end of it," Silas said. "The Spire isn't what you think. Ask your own records who paid for its foundations. Ask who the lock was forged from."

Something flickered across Renn's face—doubt, quickly killed. "I don't take theology from fugitives."

"It's not theology. It's bookkeeping."

Tam almost laughed. Almost. Because in that half-second of Renn's hesitation, he saw the geometry change: the captain's attention narrowing wholly onto Silas, the flanking finders shifting weight, and at Renn's belt the black-corded bell beginning, very softly, to hum.

He knew what a bell-curse was. Every ward-keeper did; it was in the first-year texts, in the chapter they made you read twice. A bell tuned to a name. Ring it, and the curse walks into the target and begins unmaking—not flesh, not bone, but the softer architecture. Memory. Attachment. Love. The victim doesn't die. The victim simply becomes no one to everyone, and everyone to no one, hollowed out from the inside while the body keeps walking around looking for a self that's gone.

It was meant for Silas. Renn hadn't raised it yet, but the tuning-hum said *Voss* clear as a shout.

There were perhaps nine feet between Tam and the captain. Nine feet, one surprised heartbeat, and a lifetime of failures he'd been carrying like stones in his pockets—the border wards he'd signed off on, the refugees he'd processed and forgotten, the career traded away for a conscience that arrived too late to matter.

He was tired of arriving too late.

He moved. Not toward escape—toward Renn. His shoulder hit her mid-turn, his hand closed on the bell, and the world rang.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. The curse entered through his palm like cold water entering a sleeve, quiet and thorough, and the last thing he registered clearly was Silas screaming his name—and then the scream went strange, because for a moment he couldn't place why the voice mattered. Whose voice? Someone important. Someone he—

Someone he loved?

Did he? The thought slid off him like rain off glass.

Then hands were hauling him backward, Silas's hands, and magic he shouldn't have been able to touch roared up out of her cracked seal and threw the six finders off their feet like dolls. The alley filled with light the color of a struck match. Renn was shouting orders through the glare. And Tam Ashgrave stood in the middle of it holding a dead brass bell and trying very hard to remember why any of this was worth running from.

* * *

The undercity took them the way deep water takes a dropped coin.

Silas half-carried Tam down the flooded stair beneath the tannery, through a door that hadn't opened in years until her leaking magic touched it and remembered it open. Behind them, boots hammered on stone. Ahead, the old drainage galleries ran black and echoing, lit by the sickly glow of lichen that grew, she realized with a start, in patterns too regular to be natural. Old wards. Everything in this city was older and more deliberate than anyone wanted to admit.

"Talk to me," she said. "Tam. Say something."

"I'm—" He frowned, groping. "I'm following you. You seem to know where we're going. Do I know you?"

The question went through her like a hook through cloth.

"You know me," she said. "Four days ago you put a warrant in my face and then tore it up. Your mother's name is Bess. You had a sister who died of lung-rot when you were fourteen, and you became a ward-keeper because the wards failed your street that winter and nobody came. You drink your tea bitter because sweet tea was what they gave you at the funeral." She was reciting things he'd told her by firelight, things she'd pretended not to store away, and her voice cracked on each one. "That's who you are. Hold on to it."

"Bess," he repeated, tasting the name. "Bess. Yes. Yes, that's—" Relief flickered and guttered. "It's going, isn't it. I can feel it going. Like water through fingers."

"Yes." She wouldn't lie to him. "But it goes slow. The curse eats from the edges. Which means we have time, and I am going to spend every hour of it getting you to someone who can cut this thing out of you."

"There's no cutting out a bell-curse, Silas. There's no—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than she meant, hard enough to echo down the gallery. She stopped walking. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock. "Don't tell me what can't be done. I've spent seven years being told what couldn't be done, and it turns out most of it was lies told by a woman who stole my childhood to build a prison. So no. You don't get to hand me a list of impossibilities while you stand there forgetting your sister's face because you stepped in front of a curse that was *mine*."

He looked at her. In the lichen-light his eyes were steady even as whatever lived behind them rearranged itself. "Would you have taken it?" he asked. "If it had been yours?"

"Yes."

"That's why I took it instead." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said most true things. "A man gets one clean choice in his life, maybe. I've wasted mine so far. I didn't want to waste that one."

Silas turned away because her face was doing something she refused to let him see. Guilt sat in her chest like swallowed coal—hot, permanent, hers. First Odile, who had built a god's cage out of her stolen power. Now Tam, unraveling one beloved memory at a time, paying a debt that was never his. Everyone who came near her ended up spending themselves on her behalf, and she was done. Done being the coin other people paid with.

Somewhere above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the Finder's Bell rang again—and this time it was answered by a second bell, and a third, a spreading constellation of silver notes as Renn called in every hunter the Spire had.

They would sweep the undercity by morning. They would bring dogs and diviners and worse.

Tam was squinting at the lichen on the wall, his brow furrowed with the effort of remembering something just out of reach. "These patterns," he said slowly. "I've seen them before. In the Spire's foundation records, the ones they burned after I read them. This isn't drainage architecture, Silas. This is a *door*. And I think—I think there are people down here who've been living behind it for years."

From the darkness ahead, where the gallery bent out of sight, came a light. Then voices. Then, unmistakably, the smell of cooking onions and woodsmoke, impossibly far beneath a starless sky.

And a child's voice, calling out warily into the dark:

"Who's there? I know your step. Charm-seller. Is that you?"

Silas's breath caught. She knew that voice too. She had pulled a fever out of it two winters ago, in a room that smelled of tallow and fear, and sworn afterward never to think of it again.

The boy she'd healed had found the undercity first.
