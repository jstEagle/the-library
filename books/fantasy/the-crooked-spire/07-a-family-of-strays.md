## 7. A Family of Strays

The undercity smelled like wet stone and frying onions, and Tam Ashgrave had never been so grateful for either.

He'd spent eleven years as a ward-keeper walking Greyhollow's upper streets, and he had never once thought about what lived beneath them. The old cisterns, the collapsed wine vaults, the smugglers' galleries that predated the Spire itself. Down here the witch-finders' bells went quiet—not because the law didn't reach, but because the people below had learned, over generations, to make silence a communal art. A knock on a pipe meant sweep coming. A lamp turned blue in a window meant run. Nobody asked why. Everybody obeyed.

Tam sat on an upturned barrel with his back against a wall that wept condensation, and he tried to remember his mother's face.

It was gone.

Not blurred, not faded—*gone*, the way a page is gone after fire, leaving only the knowledge that there had been a page. He knew her name. Maren Ashgrave. He knew she'd sung while kneading bread and that her hands had smelled of lye. But when he reached for her face, his memory closed over the space like water over a dropped stone.

The bell-curse was eating him from the edges inward. Ilsa Renn's bell, swung in the smoke of the waystation ambush, its note passing through Silas and finding him instead—because he'd stepped where he shouldn't have, because he'd made himself a wall between a witch-finder and a fugitive magician, which by every law he'd ever sworn to was exactly backwards.

He flexed his fingers. They still worked. That was something.

"You're doing it again."

Silas Voss crouched in front of him, turning a skewer of onions over a brazier made from a cut-down helmet. Her dark hair was tied back with what looked suspiciously like a luck-knot, the kind she used to sell for two coppers to women who wanted safe childbirth and men who wanted safe dice. Up close, now that he knew what he was looking at, he could see the magic in her the way you could see current under ice—a faint wrongness in the air around her hands, a warmth that had nothing to do with coals.

"Doing what," he said.

"Counting." She handed him a skewer. "You get this look. Like you're taking inventory of yourself and finding shelves empty."

"Your onions are burning."

"My onions are perfect. Eat."

He ate. They were perfect. Somewhere beyond the curtain of sacking that served this cellar as a door, voices were rising and falling—the strange congregation they'd collected in three days of hiding, drawn to them like moths who'd decided the flame was safer than the dark.

* * *

The strays had come one and two at a time.

First had been Wren Halloway, a charm-seller from Tanner's Row whose stall the witch-finders burned on the second night of the starless dark. She arrived with singed eyebrows and a crate of contraband: fever-charves, moon-salts, a jar of preserved grace-light that she claimed was older than the Spire. She took one look at Silas and said, "You're the knot-woman from Gullmarket. Your fever-charve saved my nephew in the winter." And then she'd rolled up her sleeves and started organizing the cellar like a general claiming ground.

Then the deserters. Two of them, then five—ward-keepers and city watch who'd walked away from their posts when the order came down to seal the refugee gates against the panic-flood from the borderlands. Tam knew two of them by name. Neither spat at him, which he'd braced for. One, a big flat-faced man called Odo, clapped him on the shoulder and said, "Heard you took a bell for a witch. Heard wrong, or heard right?" And Tam, who had spent eleven years being exactly the kind of man who would have arrested all of them, said, "Right," and found that the word fit better than he expected.

And on the third evening, past dusk that never quite happened anymore because there were no stars to mark it, a woman came down the smuggler's stair carrying a child.

The child was maybe seven years old, thin as a bundle of kindling, with a fever-bright look in her eyes that Tam recognized because he'd seen it before—in the market square, seven years ago, before he was disgraced, when he'd watched a knot-seller break every law in Greyhollow to save a dying girl and walk away clean.

"Pip," the mother said, hoarse. "Her name is Pip. You healed her. I don't know how, I don't care. The finders came to our street tonight asking after charm-workers. Asking about a woman who does knots." She set the girl down, and the girl looked up at Silas with an expression Tam had no word for except *faith*. "We have nowhere else."

Silas knelt. Took the girl's hands. Said, very quietly, "You're taller."

"I ate," Pip said, with the gravity of a scholar presenting findings.

And Silas Voss—who had spent seven years being nobody, who flinched from touch and slept sitting up and kept her gift buried so deep even she sometimes pretended it wasn't there—laughed. It cracked something open in her face. Tam watched it happen and thought: *this is what the wards were built to smother. Not the power. This.*

* * *

Wren declared it a feast, because Wren declared everything, and because the alternative was sitting in the dark counting days until the bells found them.

They pooled everything. Odo produced army hardtack and a wheel of cheese he refused to explain. The deserters contributed a sack of apples taken, they admitted, from an orchard whose owner had fled north. Wren's grace-light jar became the centerpiece, uncorked, spilling soft gold across the low ceiling so the whole cellar glowed like the inside of a lantern. Someone found a fiddle with two working strings. Someone else found a third string.

Tam sat at the edge of it, as was his habit, and let it wash over him.

Pip had appointed herself his shadow. She sat beside him now, cross-legged, working through an apple with the concentration of a surgeon. "You're sad," she informed him.

"I'm resting my face."

"That's what sad people say." She chewed. Swallowed. "Silas says your remembering is sick. Like my fever was."

He went still. "Does she."

"She says a bell did it. Bells are supposed to be for calling people home." Pip frowned at her apple core, betrayed by theology. "That's stupid. Why would anyone make a bell that makes you forget?"

"Because forgetting is worse than killing," Tam said, before he could stop himself. "Killing ends a person. Forgetting makes everyone around them carry the corpse." He heard his own voice and stopped. Pip was looking at him with those fever-clear eyes.

"Do you remember me?" she asked.

"No," he said honestly. "I met you once, seven years ago, in a crowd. I remember the day. Not you."

"But you remember Silas."

"Yes."

"Good." She nodded firmly, as if a matter of state had been settled. "Start with the important ones. That's what my gran did, when she got muddled. She said, learn the new things fast, so when the old things go there's already something in the shelves." She pointed her apple core at the room—at Wren arguing harmony with the fiddler, at Odo teaching Silas a drinking song that was absolutely not for children, at the grace-light pooling gold on all their faces. "These are new things. Learn them fast."

Tam's throat did something complicated. "You're seven."

"I'm nearly eight." She leaned her head against his arm, briefly, the way children do when affection is a fact and not a negotiation, and then went off to demand a turn with the fiddle.

He sat there and did as he was told. He learned the new things fast. Wren's laugh was a bark, startling, always a half-beat after everyone else's because she liked to be surprised into it. Odo sang flat and didn't care. The deserters had a toast—"to gates that open"—that they said with a bitterness Tam understood completely, because he had stood at sealed gates too, on the other side, following orders, and the shame of it was a stone he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for an organ.

And Silas. He learned Silas.

She had a scar on her left palm she didn't talk about. She hummed when she charmed—old tunes, half-remembered, probably from Odile's teaching, though she didn't know yet that he knew about Odile. She watched Pip the way a person watches a fire they're not sure they're allowed to warm their hands at. When she laughed, she put her hand over her mouth first, an old habit, the reflex of a woman who'd taught herself that visible joy was visible evidence.

He memorized all of it. Shelf by shelf. Because somewhere in the wrecked archive of his head, a bell was still ringing, patient as rot, and he did not know how many nights like this he had left in which to know these people.

* * *

Late—though late meant nothing now, with no stars to keep the hours—Silas came and sat beside him against the weeping wall. The feast had gone soft. Wren was asleep sitting up. Pip was asleep in her mother's lap. The grace-light had burned down to embers of gold.

"Tell me what's left," Silas said.

He looked at her.

"The curse," she said. "Don't do the thing men do where they go quiet and noble and let people find out afterward. Tell me what's left."

Tam turned the skewer stick in his fingers. "My mother's face is gone. My father—I only have his hands. My sister's whole. So far. My first posting, my oath-day, my disgrace—all whole. The last week is..." He searched. "Bright. Strange. Everything from the waystation forward is very bright, like paint still wet. Maybe because it's new. Maybe because the bell eats oldest first and I'm just... noticing the wet paint while the house burns from the back."

Silas was silent a long moment. "There has to be a way to unmake a bell-curse."

"There isn't. I wrote the registry entries on them, once. There's no counter. There's only—" He stopped.

"Only what?"

"Only what the girl said." He nodded toward sleeping Pip. "Learn the new things fast."

Silas pulled her knees up. In the gold light her face was young and old at once. "When this is over," she said, "when we've done whatever it is we're going to do at that Spire—you'll stay. You'll build new memories. On purpose. Even if the old ones go."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it. "Even then."

"Because I've spent seven years being invisible," Silas said, "and it turns out the cure for that isn't magic. It's witnesses. So you're going to witness me, Tam Ashgrave. Whatever's left of you. That's an order."

"A ward-keeper doesn't take orders from a witch."

"A disgraced ward-keeper," Silas said, "takes orders from whoever feeds him burnt onions."

He laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising out of him—and felt, for one suspended moment, almost safe. Almost home. Around them the strays slept and breathed, a small warm nation of the hidden, and above them through forty feet of stone the crooked tower leaned on its stolen lock, and the sky stayed black where stars should be.

Then the sacking door moved.

Everyone was awake instantly—deserters reaching for blades, Wren's hand closing on her crate—but it was only Odo's sentry, a wiry ex-watchman called Brannic, and his face was wrong.

"Boat traffic on the flooded gallery," he said. "One boat. One passenger. Old woman. Half-dead, by the look of her. Says—" he swallowed— "says she needs to speak to her apprentice. Says her name is Odile."

Beside Tam, Silas went white as bone.

"And captain," Brannic added quietly, "there are witch-finder bells on the water behind her. A lot of them."
