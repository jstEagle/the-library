## 8. The Price of the Lock

She came at dawn, when the undercity lamps burned low and the strays were still asleep.

Silas knew before she saw her. The air changed the way it always had in Odile's presence—thinner, attentive, as if the room itself sat up straighter. Seven years since she'd felt that pressure, and her body remembered it like a scar remembers rain.

She was standing at the mouth of the cistern tunnel, leaning on a broken pillar as though she'd been there all night and simply hadn't bothered to announce herself. Gray coat. Gray hair now, where Silas remembered black. She looked like a woman who had been burning from the inside for a long time and had finally let the fire win.

"You've gotten tall," Odile said.

Silas did not move. Behind her, in the alcove where the strays slept, Tam stirred but didn't wake—the bell-curse had taken his light sleep along with his mother's face, and he sank into dreams like a stone into deep water. Good. Let him sleep. He'd given enough.

"You shouldn't be here," Silas said. "The witch-finders are crawling through every sewer in Greyhollow looking for you."

"They're not looking for me." Odile pushed off the pillar and took one step, and Silas saw how the step cost her—a hitch in the hip, a catch in the breath. "They're looking for you. I'm just the rumor they use to keep themselves honest."

"What do you want?"

"To show you something." Odile held out her hand, palm up. In it lay a sliver of white stone, veined with gold, and even across the distance between them Silas felt it pull at the sealed place behind her ribs—the lock inside her, the hollow where her childhood magic used to live. "A piece of the Spire's heartstone. It cracked when the border wards fell. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means your cage is failing."

"It means *our* cage is failing." Odile's voice was gentle, which was worse than cruel. Cruelty, Silas could have fought. Gentleness was the weapon Odile had always used best. "Come with me. There's a choice you have to make, and you won't make it well down here, among strangers, with that boy bleeding memories all over your floor."

"They're not strangers."

"No," Odile said softly. "That's the problem, isn't it?"

* * *

They walked up through the drowned levels while the city above screamed its morning panic. Witch-finder bells rang in triplets—*found, found, found*—and somewhere a mob was burning a charm-seller's stall in Tanner's Row because fear needed feeding and magic was the only meat left. Silas kept her hood up and her hands in her pockets and hated how easily her feet fell into the old rhythm beside her master, matched stride for stride, as if seven years of freedom had been a detour and this was the road home.

Odile led her to the roof of the old tithe-house on Candlemaker's Stair, where they could see the Spire whole against the starless sky. It leaned like a drunk against nothing, its crooked crown scraping clouds that wouldn't form properly anymore. Up close, in the gray light, Silas could see the cracks. Thin gold seams running down the stone like veins opened wrong.

"I'm dying," Odile said, without ceremony. "The lock has been eating me for thirty years. That's what no one tells you about building a prison out of stolen power—it takes a jailer too. I poured myself into the heartstone every year to keep it fed, and now there isn't enough of me left to pour." She turned, and her eyes were the same eyes Silas had trusted at nine years old, at twelve, at fifteen, right up until the night she'd woken in a locked room with a hole in her chest where her magic should have been. "So it falls to you. It was always going to fall to you."

"Say it plainly."

"The lock was forged from your magic. Which means it can only be renewed from your magic. Your life, Silas. All of it. Poured into the heartstone the way I poured mine, except yours will hold for another century instead of another decade." Odile said it the way she'd once taught Silas to bind a fever-charm: clearly, calmly, as if the horror were merely a technicality. "Or—and I confess I never thought I'd say this—you can unmake it. Break the heartstone, free Vhalor, and take your chances with a god that hasn't been awake since before the city had a name. The witch-finders will call it war. They may even be right."

"That's it? Those are my choices?"

"Those are the choices that exist."

"No." Silas heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "Those are the choices *you* built. You stole a child's magic and made a lock out of her, and then you spent thirty years telling yourself it was mercy because the alternative was war. And now you climb out of your grave to offer me the same bargain you made for me when I was nine—except this time you're asking permission." She stepped closer, close enough to see the tremor in Odile's hands, the gold light flickering beneath her skin like embers under ash. "You didn't give me a choice then. Don't dress this one up like a gift."

Odile didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing about her; she never flinched. "If you break the stone, Vhalor wakes, and the first thing it feels will be three hundred years of pain. What do you think a god does with pain?"

"I don't know. But I know what a girl does with it." Silas's throat tightened. "She hides in a crooked little city for seven years selling luck-knots to people who can't afford them, and she tells herself she's safe, and she hates herself for being glad when the world starts ending because at least the ending is *hers*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You did that. You took my magic and you took my name and you left me a life half-lived, and you called it protection."

"Yes," Odile said.

Not *I had no choice*. Not *it was necessary*. Just yes, plain as a confession, and somehow that undid Silas more than any argument could have.

"I watched you," Odile went on, quietly. "After I sealed you. I told myself I'd watch from a distance, that it was safer for everyone. And then I kept watching. The fever-child in Cooper's Lane. The luck-knots. The way you'd stand in doorways at festival time, wanting to dance and not letting yourself." Her breath caught, and gold light leaked from the corner of her mouth when she spoke again. "I built a lock out of a child and then I haunted her for seven years calling it love. There's no word for what that makes me. I've looked."

Silas turned away. Below, the city seethed—bells, smoke, the Spire leaning over all of it like a question nobody wanted answered. Somewhere down in the dark, Tam was sleeping off pieces of his life, and a child she'd healed was keeping watch, and a dozen strays were waking to a meal they'd shared like family. Ten thousand small warm things in a city built to smother them.

"You said those were the only choices," Silas said. "You're wrong. You've been wrong about everything, because you only ever think in locks. Locks and keys and who pays. You never once thought about doors."

"There is no third path, Silas. I've had thirty years to look for one."

"And I've had seven years to learn what you never learned—that magic doesn't have to be taken. It can be *given*." She turned back, and something in her chest, the sealed hollow place, beat against its walls like a fist on a door. "Vhalor doesn't need a jailer. It needs witnesses. It needs names. It needs a city full of small magicians willing to carry a piece of it—not one person sacrificed, but ten thousand people choosing. You never asked anyone to choose. That's why your lock is cracking."

Odile stared at her. And then, slowly, she began to laugh—a dry, broken sound that ended in coughing, gold flecks drifting from her lips like pollen. When she straightened, there was something in her face Silas had never seen there before. It took her a moment to recognize it, because she'd never seen it aimed at herself.

Respect.

"A covenant of gifts," Odile said. "Willing ones. Anchored by names." She shook her head. "It would take a hundred voices. A thousand. No one mage could hold it alone."

"I'm not going to hold it alone."

"The witch-finders will storm the Spire before you finish. Renn has been waiting her whole career for a reason."

"Then we'd better start tonight."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the journal—burned at the edges, swollen with damp, the book Silas had already read and reread in the ashes of the waystation—and pressed it into Silas's hands. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a key of white stone and gold.

"The heartstone's approach," Odile said. "The last thing I ever made that wasn't a cage." Her legs buckled, and Silas caught her—caught her without thinking, the way you catch family—and lowered her to sit against the parapet, propped against the crooked skyline of her own making.

"Silas." Odile's voice was very thin now. "I am not going to ask you to forgive me. I don't get to ask that. But I need you to know—" Gold light pulsed under her skin, faster now, uneven. "—the night I sealed you, you begged me not to. And I told you it was for your own good. It wasn't. It was for mine. I couldn't bear the war, so I made you pay for my peace. That was the price of the lock. Me. Always me. Never brave enough to make it myself."

The words hung in the cold air between them.

And Silas—who had rehearsed hatred for seven years, who had built a whole self out of the absence of this woman—found that the hatred had nowhere left to stand. Not because Odile deserved absolution. Because carrying it was another kind of lock, and Silas was done with locks.

"I forgive you," she said, "for me. Not for you. So you don't get to die clean."

Something like a smile crossed Odile's face. "Wouldn't dream of it." Her eyes drifted up, past Silas, to the Spire leaning against the empty sky. "Go up, then. Before I'm gone and the stone cracks all the way through. And Silas—"

"Yes?"

"When you speak the covenant—" The gold light was rising in her now, spilling out of her like dawn through shutters. "—say my name with the others. Not because I earned it. Because I'm asking."

Below them, across the city, the first crack split the Spire's crown with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and every witch-finder bell in Greyhollow began to ring at once.

Silas stood, key in hand, and started for the stairs.
