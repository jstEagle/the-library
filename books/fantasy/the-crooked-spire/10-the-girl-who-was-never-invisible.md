## 10. The Girl Who Was Never Invisible

**Silas**

The stars came back on a Tuesday, and Greyhollow did not know what to do with them.

I stood in the schoolyard of what had been the tannery on Cobbler's Row—scrubbed, limed, and stubbornly not smelling of hides anymore—and watched people stop in the street to look up. A fishwife dropped her basket. Two boys from the ropewalk stood with their mouths open like the sky was a trick someone was playing on them.

Seven years I'd lived under a dark ceiling and called it normal. Four months since the Spire straightened, stone by groaning stone, until it rose over the city true as a plumb line, and still the whole of Greyhollow flinched at starlight.

"You'll want to close your mouth," Tam said beside me, "or you'll catch flies."

"Look who's talking."

He wasn't looking up. He was looking at me, which was worse, or better—I'd stopped being able to tell the difference somewhere around the third week he'd stayed.

The school opened at dawn bell. No sign out front; Wren had argued for one, painted a crooked spire on a board with a door standing open where its heart should be, but I said no. The children would find it. They always found things. That was rather the point.

By midmorning we had eleven of them sitting on benches that still smelled faintly of oak bark: the fever-girl Maren, whose charm had started all of this and who now appointed herself my deputy with terrifying efficiency; a deserter's twin boys who could make rope move without touching it; a chandler's daughter whose candles burned colors she'd been beaten for, twice. Eleven children who had spent their whole lives learning to hide the thing that made them themselves.

"We don't hide here," I told them, on the first morning. "That's the only rule. Whatever you can do, you do it where everyone can see, and if it goes wrong, we fix it together."

Maren raised her hand. "What if the witch-finders come?"

"They won't." And then, because they deserved better than a lie dressed as comfort: "Some of them might. Captain Renn resigned her commission two weeks ago. She sent a letter asking about a teaching position."

The twins looked horrified. Maren looked delighted.

"She can sweep floors," I said. "Nothing else."

**Tam**

Memory is a strange country when half the map's been burned.

I know there was a mother. I know this because there's a shape in me shaped like a mother-shaped hole, warm-edged, aching. I know there were brothers—the hole has three smaller holes inside it, like nests. I know their names used to live in my mouth because my tongue still reaches for them sometimes, finds nothing, and comes back empty.

The bell-curse took the faces and kept the love. Nobody warns you about that part. It would be easier if it took both.

But here is what I've learned, these four months in Silas Voss's school that isn't called a school: memory isn't only behind you. Odile's journal said the old covenant was built on names spoken aloud, small willing gifts, ten thousand anchors—and Silas says a covenant is just a promise with weight, and a promise is just a memory you choose to keep making.

So I make new ones.

I keep a book now. Small, leather, unremarkable—except every page is a day I refuse to lose. *Today Maren levitated the chalk and blamed the twins. Today the chandler's girl made a candle burn blue and cried because no one hit her.* Today Silas laughed at something I said and didn't pretend she hadn't.

If the curse comes back for the rest—if it eats even the book, even the shape of her—I have decided something, and I wrote it on the first page so I'd find it again:

*Whoever you are when you wake up: stay anyway. She's worth meeting twice.*

* * *

**Silas**

Odile's grave sits beneath the Spire, in the foundation vault where Vhalor's voice once filled the dark with my own childhood. We buried her there because she asked—not forgiveness, exactly. She asked to be where the covenant was made, "so I can listen to it working."

Vhalor had offered to speak at the funeral. I said no. Some griefs want quiet.

But I go down sometimes, on evenings when the school is empty and the stone overhead hums with ten thousand small gifts flowing steady as a tide. I sit on the cold floor with my back against her marker—a plain slab, no title, just the words *SHE BUILT THE CAGE AND THEN HATED HERSELF FOR IT* in Wren's best lettering, because Odile would have hated anything grander and loved the honesty.

"I'm teaching them the way you should have taught me," I told her last night. "Out loud. With mistakes."

The stone didn't answer. But the Spire hummed a half-tone lower, the way it does when Vhalor is listening, and I chose to take that as agreement.

Here is what nobody tells you about getting everything you wanted: you spend months waiting for the bill. Every morning I wake expecting the wards to fail, the stars to gutter, Renn's bell to ring down the street. Every morning the sun comes up ordinary and rude and mine.

The invisibility is gone for good—that was the price, written into the covenant in letters even I can read now. Every hidden magician in Greyhollow glows faintly to anyone who knows how to look, and I glow brightest of all, the anchor-name, the first gift. I cannot walk through a market without children pointing. I cannot lie about what I am ever again.

I thought I'd hate it. Instead, most days, it feels like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying.

* * *

**Tam**

Renn came for her interview on a grey morning, sat across from Silas with her hands folded like a woman awaiting sentencing, and said, "I hunted you for four months. I broke three laws doing it. Why would you let me near children?"

Silas didn't blink. "Because you stopped when you understood. Most people don't stop."

"And if I relapse? If the old training wakes up some night?"

"Then you'll sweep floors until it doesn't," Silas said. "Everyone here is rebuilding from wreckage. You'll fit right in."

I expected Renn to bristle. Instead she looked at the floor for a long moment, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious about it. "The bell-curse," she said. "The one Ashgrave took. I authorized it. I want—" Her jaw worked. "I want to help build whatever this is. That's not penance enough. Nothing is. But it's a start."

Silas slid an application form across the table. "Groundskeeper," she said. "You start tomorrow."

Afterward, in the yard, I asked her why. Really why.

She watched Renn measure out a grave-sized plot for the herb garden—wrong dimensions, entirely too big, and nobody corrected her. "Because Odile spent thirty years believing the world could only be held together by cages," Silas said. "And I spent seven years believing I could only survive by disappearing. Somebody has to prove us both wrong, daily, or the lesson doesn't take."

She glanced at me. "Besides. You remember what you wrote in that book of yours?"

"Every word."

"Liar. You re-read it every night."

Caught. I am apparently transparent to ward-keepers and novelists alike.

"It's a good line, though," she said, softer. "*Stay anyway.* I'm keeping it."

* * *

**Silas**

Winter came early that year, the first honest winter Greyhollow had seen since the Spire went crooked—snow instead of ash-fall, cold instead of that damp, watching chill. The children built a snow-spire in the yard and argued fiercely about whether to make it crooked. Maren settled it by melting it slightly crooked with a breath of heat, "for tradition," and I let it stand.

On the longest night, we held the first public gifting. Ten thousand names already anchored the covenant, but the city had begun adding to it freely—shopkeepers leaving luck-knots on doorposts in broad daylight, the fever-charves sold openly now, labeled honestly. So we gathered in the yard under the returned stars, and one by one, people stepped forward and spoke a name aloud: theirs, or a child's, or a stranger's, and gave a small thing—a warmth, a mending, a moment of courage—into the stone.

When my turn came, I spoke my own name. *Silas Voss.* Full voice, no flinch. The Spire above us rang like a struck bell, gentle, and the snow on its lengthened shadow glittered all the way down Cobbler's Row.

Tam stood beside me, his book open on his palm against the cold, writing by starlight. I didn't read over his shoulder. Whatever he was keeping, it was ours, and it was new, and new was the whole point.

Later, walking home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke instead of fear, he said, "Do you ever miss it? Being invisible?"

I thought about the dying child in a garret, seven years ago, and a charm that worked when it shouldn't have. About the crack in all my careful walls that turned out to be a door.

"No," I said. "I spent my whole life being a rumor. I'd rather be a name."

He nodded, and wrote something else down, and I let him keep it.

Above us the Crooked Spire—straight now, though the city refuses to rename it, and I hope it never does—held the stars in place the way a promise holds a future: not by force, but by being kept.

Somewhere beneath our feet, an old god listened to ten thousand small voices and, for the first time in an age, was not alone.

Neither was I.
