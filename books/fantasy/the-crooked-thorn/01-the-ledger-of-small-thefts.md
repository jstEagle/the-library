## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

There were three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Gilded Thorn, and Odile Voss had broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never write a memory down in plain ink. Rule two: never count what you cannot account for. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, fall in love with anything in inventory.

Odile had broken rule three twice, and both times it had cost them. The first time had been a bottled grief belonging to a drowned fisherman's widow — Odile had kept it on their desk for eleven days before Maretta noticed and made them sell it back to her at cost, which was no profit at all, which was the point. The second time was not a thing in inventory at all, but a person with soot on their knuckles and a dragon's heartbeat under their palm, and that particular theft was still outstanding, accruing interest daily, unpayable by any ledger Odile knew how to keep.

But that was later thinking, and mornings were for earlier things.

The counting room of the Gilded Thorn sat above a chandler's shop on Candlemaker's Row, which everyone agreed was either a joke or an insult, depending on whether you knew what the guild actually sold. Downstairs, Brannic the forger turned out documents so beautiful that two separate governments used them as currency templates. Upstairs, Odile sat at a desk older than the city's official history — which was easy, because the city's official history was only a hundred years old, having been invented wholesale after the country that stood here stopped existing — and counted impossible things.

The morning light came through leaded windows in dusty bars. Odile dipped a pen in ink mixed with powdered moth-wing (rule one) and opened the great ledger to the current page.

*Inventory, autumn audit,* they wrote. *Item: one jar of borrowed courage, vintage unknown, slightly evaporated. Item: seventeen years of a miller's happiness, sold back to him last spring at a markup he thanked us for. Item: the sound of a name nobody remembers saying aloud, provenance disputed. Item: luck, bottled, nine vials, of which four are genuine, three are wishful thinking, and two are actively cursed — see note re: the Alderman.*

The Alderman's curse-vials were a standing problem. He'd bought them to win an election he'd already lost, and now they sat in the strongroom radiating a low, sour misfortune that made the locks stick and gave Pip nosebleeds when he walked past. Odile had flagged them twice for disposal. Twice, the guildmaster had said *not yet* in that voice like a door closing gently.

Which brought Odile, inevitably, to the missing entry.

Page forty-one of the founding ledger — the old book, the one bound in leather gone soft as ear skin, the one with the thorn stamped crooked into the cover — had been cut out. Not torn; cut, with a razor or a very patient knife, close along the spine, so cleanly that if you didn't know the book the way Odile knew it — the way you know a face, the way you know your own handwriting — you'd never notice. But Odile had copied that ledger out by hand at fourteen, punishment for some long-forgotten sin, and they knew that page forty-one had held something. They could feel its absence the way you feel a tooth gone from your mouth: a small wrongness, always touched, never healed.

"Counting again?" Maretta leaned in the doorway with two cups of chicory and the expression of a woman who had fenced feelings for thirty years and found most of them counterfeit. She was broad and silver-streaked and wore her rings on every finger except the ones she needed for picking pockets, and she set one cup on Odile's ledger without apology, directly over the word *courage*.

"You'll smudge the moth-ink."

"You'll smudge yourself into a grave, sitting hunched like that." She dropped into the chair across the desk, which was hers by ancient custom though she owned no part of the guild officially. Nothing about the Thorn was official. That was rather the whole architecture of it. "What's got your face doing arithmetic?"

"Page forty-one."

Maretta's cup paused halfway to her mouth. It was a pause of perhaps half a heartbeat, and Odile would have missed it in anyone else, but Maretta dealt in feelings the way Brannic dealt in seals and signatures, and she could no more hide a flinch from Odile than Odile could hide a ledger line from her.

"You know something," Odile said.

"I know my coffee's getting cold." She drank. "I know the Alderman's vials need moving before they sour the whole strongroom. I know Brannic is forging a bishop this week, which should be fun, because he's never done a bishop and bishops have *opinions* built into the letterforms." She set the cup down. "And I know that some pages are cut out for reasons, little thief, and the reason is usually mercy."

"For whom?"

"That depends entirely on who's asking." Her eyes were kind and absolutely immovable. "You're auditing. Fine. Audit. But don't dig up what the garden's growing over unless you're prepared to find out why we buried it."

She left, taking her coffee and leaving Odile's, which was its own kind of message: *I'm not angry, I'm protecting you, drink something warm while the world stays ordinary.*

Odile drank the chicory. It was terrible. It was home.

* * *

Midmorning brought Brannic, filling the doorway the way he filled every doorway, a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink permanently staining his fingers to the first knuckle and a magnifying lens pushed up into his wild grey hair like a second pair of eyes.

"Voss. Tell me about 1189."

"A year. Long ago. Before the erasure, if you go by the old reckoning."

"Don't be clever, I've had cleverness all morning." He dropped a document on the desk — a bill of lading, gorgeous, flawless, the watermark alone worth more than the chandler's shop downstairs. "Client wants provenance for a coin. Coin's minted here. Minted *in this city*, Voss, with a die-work I've never seen, and I've seen everything. And the date on the die—" he tapped the paper "—is 1189. Which means whoever struck it was minting currency for a kingdom that hasn't existed in a hundred years. Either it's the finest forgery I've ever encountered—"

"—which would wound you professionally—"

"—which would wound me *professionally*, yes, thank you, or it's real. And if it's real, then somebody's spending money from a country that isn't there." He rubbed his stained fingers together, a habit Odile had catalogued years ago as *nervousness, mild*. "Who's the client?"

"Veiled. Paid in advance. Same coinage."

Brannic went quiet in the specific way of a man recalculating. "A whole purse of it?"

"A whole purse."

"And the guildmaster took the job."

"The guildmaster took the job."

He looked at the ceiling, at the floor, at the window, anywhere but at Odile, which was how Brannic prayed. "I've got a bad feeling shaped like page forty-one," he said finally.

"So you do know about page forty-one."

"I know about *nothing*," Brannic said with tremendous dignity, "and I intend to continue knowing nothing until someone tells me otherwise," and he gathered up his beautiful forgery and left, and Odile wrote in the margin of the audit, in moth-ink, in shorthand only they could read: *the family knows more than it says, which means the guildmaster knows more than the family says, which means the ledger is lying to me, which means the ledger has been taught to lie, which is worse.*

Pip appeared sometime after noon. Pip's appearances were less events than weather — you'd look up and there he'd be, sixteen maybe, thin as a candle-wick, silent as the space between bells. He couldn't speak; nobody knew if he'd ever been able to. What Pip could do was touch memories without breaking them, hold a vial of somebody's stolen childhood against his palm and tell you — in gestures, in sketches, in the rare eloquence of his enormous dark eyes — whether it was sweet or spoiled. He was the guild's taster, its quality-control, its conscience with hands.

He set a tray on Odile's desk: bread, hard cheese, an apple, and a folded scrap of paper. On the paper, in Pip's careful pencil, a drawing: a candle. Underneath, a single word, pressed hard enough to dent the page:

*LIT.*

Odile looked up. Pip pointed one long finger toward the door of the counting room, through the anteroom, toward the little shrine-nook where the Thorn kept its household gods — the small, polite, deliberately forgettable alcove where they burned a token candle to whatever powers might be listening, precisely so that those powers would conclude the Thorn worshipped nothing worth noticing. The candle there was supposed to be unlit. It was *always* supposed to be unlit. A lit candle was a prayer, and the Thorn did not pray, because praying was how you got noticed, and being noticed was how you died.

"It's lit?" Odile asked.

Pip nodded. Then, after a moment, he held up his hand, flat, and slowly raised it higher, higher, until his arm strained — *tall*, the gesture said. *Taller than a candle should burn.* His eyes were wide and steady and afraid in the composed way of someone who had already decided to be brave about it and was informing you as a courtesy.

Odile walked the length of the counting room, through the anteroom smelling of Brannic's sealing wax, and stood before the shrine-nook.

The candle was burning.

It was a fat white votive of the kind sold by the dozen in the chandler's shop below, and it had burned down nearly to the socket, which meant it had been burning since before dawn, since before Odile's audit, since — Odile's stomach performed a slow, cold turn — since roughly the hour the veiled client had come and gone with their purse of impossible coin.

And the flame was wrong. Not tall, exactly. *Deep.* It burned the way a well is deep, the way a stairwell going down is deep, drawing the eye in past the point where light should end. The wax around it had melted into shapes that were almost letters and refused to resolve. And on the wall behind the shrine, where the flame threw its shadow, there was a scorch-mark that had not been there yesterday.

It was shaped like a door.

Odile stood very still and did the thing they were best at in all the world, better even than stealing: they observed, and they recorded, and they did not touch.

Someone had prayed here. In the Thorn's own house, under the guildmaster's own roof, someone had lit a candle to an old god — one of the gods of the country that no longer existed, the gods who had gone silent a century ago along with everything else, the gods whose silence was the entire foundation stone upon which the Thorn's invisibility was built.

And the god, apparently, had answered.

The flame flickered, and for half a heartbeat Odile would have sworn — though they were a professional observer and swore nothing lightly — that the scorch-marked door on the wall stood open, just a crack, just wide enough for a draft of air that smelled of salt water and green hills and woodsmoke from hearths a hundred years cold.

Then it was a candle again. Wax and wick and fire, ordinary, stupid, harmless.

Except that it was still burning, and votives didn't burn this long, and somewhere beneath Odile's ribs the auditor's instinct — the instinct that had caught every discrepancy, every forged entry, every beautiful lie in twenty years of ledgers — sat up like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could hear.

*Something has been entered into the world's books,* the instinct said, *and no one has recorded the debit.*

"Pip," Odile said quietly, without turning around. "Go find Maretta. Tell her—" and then Odile stopped, because how did you draw *a god answered a prayer in our house*? How did you sketch that in pencil?

Pip solved it himself. He was already gone, footsteps silent on the stairs, and in his wake he'd left the tray, the apple, and one final folded note Odile hadn't seen him write.

Odile opened it. The pencil line wavered, pressed hard, urgent:

*IT KNOWS WE'RE HERE.*

Behind them, in the shrine-nook, the candle flame stood perfectly straight and perfectly still, as if listening, as if waiting to see what the Gilded Thorn would do next — and down in the counting room, on the great ledger's open page, the moth-ink of Odile's own handwriting began, very slowly, to fade.
