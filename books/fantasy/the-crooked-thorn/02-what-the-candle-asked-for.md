## 2. What the Candle Asked For

The candle had burned for eleven hours before Odile noticed it, which was, by their own accounting, ten hours and forty minutes too long.

They noticed it the way they noticed everything in the Gilded Thorn: sideways, through the ledger, while ostensibly doing something else. Odile Voss did not look directly at problems; looking directly at a problem gave it weight, and weight gave it a place in the world, and things with places in the world could be taxed, seized, or remembered. Better to catch them at the edge of vision, where they were still soft.

So it was that at half past nine in the morning, with a quill behind one ear and three columns of inventory open before them, Odile became aware of a small wrongness on the mantel above the counting room's cold hearth.

A candle. White tallow, thumb-short, guttered down to a puckered pool of wax. And burning — no. Not burning anymore. *Burned.* All the way down, all the way out, in a house where no one lit candles on the mantel because the mantel belonged to the ledger of unclaimed goods, and nothing on the ledger of unclaimed goods was ever to be touched, moved, or *used*.

Odile set down the quill.

The wax had pooled into a shape that made the back of their neck prickle. Not a puddle. A ring — no, not quite a ring. An oval, tall and narrow, wider at the base, like a doorway drawn by someone who had only ever heard doors described.

And beneath it, scorched into the mantel's old oak in a brown so dark it was nearly black: the same shape again. A door. A door with no wall around it, no hinges, no handle. Just the idea of a door, burned into wood by a flame that should not have been hot enough to do anything but warm the room.

"Brannic," Odile said, without raising their voice. In the Thorn you never raised your voice for trouble; trouble was a customer like any other, and customers were greeted politely. "When did we last take inventory of the mantel?"

From the back room came the sound of a pen scratching, then stopping. Brannic Odo emerged wiping ink from his fingers, a big man built like a well-fed bishop, with the careful hands of a forger and the guilty face of one caught mid-forgery. He looked at the mantel. He looked at the candle. His face did something complicated.

"That wasn't there last night," he said.

"I know."

"I mean it *wasn't there*, Odile. I closed up. I checked the seals on the strongroom, I banked the hearth, I walked the whole floor the way you taught me. There was no candle."

"I know," Odile said again, and now they were standing, and now they were looking at it directly, which they hated, which meant it was serious. "That's what worries me. Things that arrive without arriving are either gifts or invoices. And nothing in this city gives gifts."

Behind them, from her corner near the window where she conducted her business of buying and selling what people felt, Maretta said quietly: "It's been answered."

Odile turned. Maretta Quill sat with her tea untouched, which was the second wrongness of the morning, because Maretta's tea was a ritual as fixed as the tides. She was watching the scorch-mark the way sailors watch weather.

"What has?"

"The candle." She rose, crossed the room, and stood over the mantel without touching it. Her fingers hovered above the burned door-shape, close enough to feel — Odile saw her feel it, saw her flinch — whatever lived in the burn. "You know what prayer-candles are. Cheap vessels. You whisper what you want into the wick, you light it, and the wish goes nowhere, because the gods it's addressed to are a hundred years dead or a hundred years gone, depending on which priest is selling you the candle. It's theater. Comfort. We've sold enough of them ourselves to know."

"We don't sell prayer-candles," Odile said. "We sell memory-vials, bottled luck, dream-salt, and the occasional genuine miracle at a markup. Prayer-candles are beneath us."

"Mm." Maretta's mouth curved, but without humor. "Then explain the answer."

Odile looked at the mark again. The door-shape. Burned clean, burned *precise*, in the way that nothing natural burns. Fire makes chaos; this was calligraphy. Someone — something — had written a door onto the mantel of the Gilded Thorn, in a guild whose entire existence depended on remaining unwritten.

"Pip," Odile called.

The apprentice appeared the way Pip always appeared, which was already present. A small person of indeterminate age with watchful eyes and a slate hanging from a cord around their neck. Pip could not speak — had never spoken, in all the years since Maretta had fished them out of a canal — but their slate spoke fluently enough for everyone.

Odile pointed at the candle. "When did this come into the house? Think. Unclaimed goods come in through the night-slot, through Maretta's buyers, through Brannic's contacts, through the drop under the bridge. Somewhere, someone catalogued a white tallow candle, or failed to, and either way you were the last pair of hands on the intake shelf."

Pip took up the chalk. Wrote. Turned the slate:

*Came with the Tuesday lot. No seller. Slot was locked. It was on the step when I opened up, standing upright in its own wax, already lit.*

"Already lit," Odile repeated.

Pip nodded. Then, after a moment, added:

*I didn't touch it. It felt like it was listening.*

The counting room went very quiet. Outside, Grayharbor woke in its usual register of gulls and cart-wheels and the fog-bell at the mole, and none of it mattered, because inside the Gilded Thorn a candle that had delivered itself, lit itself, and listened to itself had burned down to a door-shaped scar, and every instinct Odile possessed was screaming that somewhere in this city, a prayer had just been *paid*.

Prayers, in Odile's long and unsentimental experience, were contracts. And contracts were the Thorn's native country.

"Right," they said briskly, because briskness was armor and they had worn it so long they'd forgotten it wasn't skin. "Maretta, with me. Brannic, seal the counting room — chalk line across the threshold, your best work, and if anyone crosses it before we're back, they'd better be carrying us. Pip—"

Pip was already writing.

*Find who prayed.*

Odile allowed themselves one thin smile. "Find who prayed. Because whoever lit that candle asked an old god for something, and the old god answered, and answers—" they tapped the ledger, that great cracked-leather heart of the guild, "—always come due. I intend to know the terms before the collector arrives at our door."

* * *

The petitioner, when they found her, was dying in a garret over a chandler's shop on Rope-Walk, in a room that smelled of tallow and lilies and something older underneath both, something like stone left out in rain for a hundred years.

Finding her had taken most of the day, and it had taken Maretta, because finding people was Maretta's trade in a way that even Odile's ledgers could not match. Odile tracked transactions. Maretta tracked *feelings* — she bought grief off widows, sold courage to cowards, and could walk down any street in Grayharbor and tell you which windows held despair and which held merely poverty. Despair was common. What she had hunted today was rarer.

"Someone in this city is *radiant*," she had said, striding through the morning fog with her shawl pulled tight. "Not happy. Radiant. The way people glow when a prayer gets answered — terrified and lit up from inside at once. I haven't felt it since I was a girl. It's like walking past an open oven in winter. Follow the warmth."

The warmth had led them up three flights of stairs that complained the whole way, to a door with no lock, because the woman behind it had decided locks were finished with her.

Her name, she told them, before they had given theirs, was Ilse Varn. She was very old in the way that furniture is old — worn smooth, structurally compromised, full of other decades. She lay propped on pillows that had gone flat as her voice, and her hands, folded on the blanket, were translucent at the fingertips, as though she were already partly elsewhere.

She looked at Odile and Maretta in the doorway and said, "You're the thieves. Good. I hoped you'd come before the end. Sit. I can't get up, which will save you the trouble of making me."

"We're not thieves," Odile began, on principle, because the principle mattered even when it was a lie.

"You keep the ledgers of the Gilded Thorn." Ilse Varn's eyes were pale and terribly clear. "Don't waste my hours denying it. I prayed to a god that doesn't exist, and it sent me the guild instead. That tells me everything about how the answering works, and I have had a whole day to think about it, and I would like to spend what time remains talking to someone who understands *accounts*."

Maretta sat. Odile remained standing, in the doorway, in the habit of exits.

"What did you ask for?" Maretta asked gently.

"For my country back." The old woman smiled, and the smile was young, and it was the worst thing in the room. "Just for one hour of it. One hour of Vellichor, before I die. That's all. I didn't ask for much. I was careful. I've been careful for ninety years."

The name landed in the room like a coin dropped down a well. Odile heard it fall and fall and never hit bottom.

There was no such place as Vellichor. There had never been such a place. Every map, every census, every gravestone in every churchyard agreed on this with the perfect unanimity of things that had been arranged. A century ago — more, by some reckonings — the kingdom of Vellichor had ceased, and not in the way kingdoms cease, by conquest or collapse, but in the way a word ceases when everyone agrees to stop saying it. No war. No ruin. The borders simply opened onto other borders, the roads simply led to other roads, and the people who had lived there — those few who remembered being of there — found themselves citizens of nowhere, haunted by a homesickness with no address.

The official histories called it the Quiet Adjustment and devoted four polite paragraphs to it. The unofficial histories did not exist, because unofficial histories require someone willing to write them down, and everyone who might have was either dead or had been paid very well to forget.

The Gilded Thorn had been paid very well to forget. Odile knew this the way you know the load-bearing walls of your own house: without thinking about it, and with a vague dread of what would happen if you ever did.

"There is no Vellichor," Odile said carefully.

"Yes," said Ilse Varn, with enormous satisfaction. "Say it again. Feel how the sentence fights you. Feel how your tongue wants to say *there was*. Ninety years I've carried it. I was six years old when the Adjustment came. I remember the harbor at Vellichor — the *real* harbor, with the crooked lighthouse and the bells that rang underwater at high tide. I remember my mother's kitchen and the smell of the thorn-hedges in autumn. I remember the king's face on the coins. Do you know what it is, to be the only person alive who remembers a whole country? To carry a nation in a head that's failing?" She lifted one translucent hand. "I am a library burning down, masters, and there is no copy."

Maretta had gone very still. Odile, watching her rather than the old woman, saw the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"What did the candle do?" Maretta asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands, in her lap, were not.

"It answered." Ilse Varn laughed, a dry rustle. "I bought the candle from a peddler who swore it was ordinary. I whispered into the wick the way children do. One hour, I said. Give me back one hour of my country and I'll die content. And the flame went green — green, do you understand, green as the sea off the Vellichor coast — and it burned all night, and in the morning the candle was gone from my table and there was a door burned into the wood. A little door. And I swear to you, on whatever's left of me, that in the night I *went through it*. One hour. I stood on the quay. I heard the underwater bells. My mother called me in for supper." Her clear eyes filled, and the tears fell without her seeming to notice. "And then I woke here, in this room, in this country that doesn't exist either, and the door was gone, and the candle was gone, and I thought: *someone will come for the receipt.*"

Silence. Down on Rope-Walk, a chandler's boy cried his wares, and the ordinary sound of commerce seemed suddenly obscene.

"Why the Thorn?" Odile asked at last. "Why send the candle to us?"

Ilse Varn turned her pale gaze on them, and for a moment Odile felt weighed, appraised, entered into a column somewhere.

"Because the guild remembers too," she said. "Whatever you've been paid to forget, you were also paid to *keep*. That's the shape of it, isn't it? A vault doesn't lose what's inside it just because it's locked. I've known about the Thorn since before you were born, ledger-keeper. My family — " she stopped. Started again, quieter. "My family had dealings with yours, once. Long ago. Before the Adjustment. We trusted you with something, and you kept it, and I am the last Varn, and I am dying, and the god answered me, which means the gods are *answering again*, and if that's true—"

She coughed then, a deep wet tearing cough, and Maretta was up and holding a cup of water to her lips, and the moment broke like ice.

"If that's true," Ilse Varn finished, when she could speak, "then the debt comes due. All of them. Every bargain struck before the Adjustment. And your guild, masters, sits on the largest unpaid account in the history of the erased world."

* * *

They walked back through Grayharbor in the blue hour, when the lamps were lit and the fog came in off the water like a tide of milk, and neither of them spoke until the Thorn's street, because streets had ears and this conversation had grown teeth.

It was Maretta who finally stopped, under a lamp at the corner, and turned to face Odile, and Odile saw that her fear had finished curdling into something harder.

"You knew," Maretta said. "Some of it. Don't lie to me, Odile, I buy feelings off strangers for a living, I'll know."

"I knew the Thorn was old," Odile said. "I knew our founding contract predates the Adjustment — it's the one document in the archive written in a hand nobody can read anymore. I knew the guildmaster keeps a sealed page in the master ledger that he checks every year on the same date, and that he goes gray as ash afterward, and that he's done it for forty years. I knew we were paid, once, enormously, by someone, for something, and that the payment was in coin from a mint that officially never existed." They paused. "I did not know the client's name was Varn."

"And now the gods are answering prayers." Maretta wrapped her arms around herself against the fog, or against something else. "Do you understand what that means? The old gods didn't die, Odile. They were *silenced*. Everyone knows the story — the kings bought the silence, the priests wrote it down, the country paid the price. If the silence is breaking—"

"Then every contract the old powers ever signed is live again." Odile heard their own voice go flat and precise, which was what it did when the ground moved. "Every debt. Every promise. Including ours."

"Including ours." Maretta looked up the street toward the unmarked door of the Gilded Thorn, its plain brick face, its utter anonymity, the invisibility that had kept them alive for a hundred years. "And someone lit a candle on our own mantel, in a locked house, addressed to a dead god, and the god used *our* mantel to open a door. That wasn't chance. That was a summons. Or a reminder." She swallowed. "Or a foreclosing."

Above them, far off across the rooftops toward the ruined quarter of the old capital, the evening sky flickered — faintly, briefly, unmistakably — with a red-gold light that was not sunset, not lamplight, not anything the modern city had a name for.

Old light. Hearth-light. The kind of light a very large fire makes when it is trying, and failing, to remember what it once burned for.

Maretta saw it. Odile saw Maretta see it. Neither of them said a word, because there was nothing yet to say, except the thing Ilse Varn had already said for them:

The debt comes due.

And somewhere between the garret on Rope-Walk and the crooked door of the Gilded Thorn, Odile understood — with the sick certainty of a ledger balancing against its keeper — that before this was finished, someone was going to have to go down beneath the ruined capital, to the fireline where the Order of the Veil kept its last secret, and find the one person in Grayharbor who would hate being dragged into this more than anyone alive.

Sable.

Odile stood in the fog a moment longer, telling themselves the tightness in their chest was only dread.

They were almost able to believe it.
