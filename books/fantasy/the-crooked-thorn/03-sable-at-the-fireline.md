## 3. Sable at the Fireline

The dragon's fire had gone the color of old honey.

Sable noticed it first in the way the light fell across the vaulted dark beneath the ruined capital—once it had thrown shadows sharp as sword-blades against the pillars of the Underhall, and now it pooled soft and thick, like light through amber, like something already preserved rather than alive. Sable stood at the edge of the fireline with a rake of blackened iron in both hands and did not move for a long moment, just watching, the way you watch a sick child sleep.

Emberling lay coiled in the great ash-bowl at the center of the hall, wings folded tight against a body grown leaner these last two years, scales that had once burned copper-gold now dulled toward the color of cooling slag. The dragon's breathing came slow and vast, each exhale stirring the embers around her muzzle into brief bright constellations before they sank again.

"Good morning to you too," Sable said quietly.

One eye opened. It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of deep water over fire, and it found Sable with the terrible patience of a creature who had watched two hundred years of keepers come and go and remembered every one of their names, even the ones the Order had struck from its rolls.

"You're hungry," Sable said. "I know."

It was not hunger for food. That was the thing no one outside the fireline understood, the thing Sable had stopped trying to explain to the Order's quartermasters years ago. Dragons did not eat the way other creatures ate. They fed on *anchorage*—on place, on meaning, on the dense accumulated weight of somewhere mattering. A dragon's fire was not fuel. It was memory, combusting.

And Emberling's anchorage was dying.

Sable set down the rake and crossed the ash-field barefoot, as tradition demanded, feeling the warmth come up through the soles—not the fierce heat of the stories, but the gentle residual glow of a hearth banked for the night. The dragon lowered her head until her muzzle rested an arm's length away, and her breath washed over Sable like a tide, smelling of cedar smoke and rain on hot stone and something older underneath, something that had no name in any language still spoken above ground.

"I brought the good coal," Sable said. "Don't tell the quartermaster I paid for it myself."

The dragon made a sound low in her chest, a rumble that was not quite speech and had never needed to be. Sable worked in silence after that, shoveling the white-hot seam-coal into the feeding trough of black iron, laying sprigs of dried rosemary across it because Emberling had liked rosemary since before the Order existed, checking the scale-lines along her throat where the fire came closest to the surface. There were new gray patches there. Sable counted them without wanting to. Eleven last month. Fourteen now.

Fourteen places where the flame was forgetting how to burn.

"Talk to me," Sable murmured, resting a palm flat against the warm armor of her jaw. "Tell me what you're losing."

The mind-link opened like a door into a warm room.

Dragons did not speak in words, exactly. What came through was weather—vast slow pressures of feeling and image—and Sable had spent fifteen years learning to read this particular sky. What came through now was a city. Not this one. A city of white walls and crooked streets, of bridges that sang in the wind, of a river the color of hammered tin, of bells—Sable could almost *hear* the bells—a thousand bells ringing some festival whose name dissolved even as Emberling reached for it.

Then the image guttered, like the fire itself, and behind it Sable felt the thing that frightened them more than anything else in the world: the vast, patient confusion of a creature looking for its home and finding a hole where it should be.

Not ruins. Ruins would have been something. Ruins could be mourned. This was absence—the shape of a country cut cleanly out of the world, edges smooth as a paper doll lifted from its page, and Emberling pressing her ancient face against that smooth edge again and again, unable to understand why there was nothing on the other side.

Sable withdrew from the link before the grief could pull them under, and knelt in the ash with their forehead pressed to the dragon's jaw, breathing, breathing, until their own heart slowed to match hers.

"I'm going to fix it," they said. It was a keeper's vow, half-formed, dangerous. "I don't know how yet. But I'm going to fix it."

The dragon's eye closed. Above them, far up through the broken vaults of the Underhall, morning light fell in dusty columns through the cracks in a capital that everyone agreed had always belonged to someone else.

* * *

The summons came at midday, as summons from the Order of the Veil always did: delivered by a novice who would not meet Sable's eyes, sealed in gray wax stamped with the veiled hand, and smelling faintly of cold iron.

Sable read it standing in the keeper's alcove while the kettle failed to boil properly over the dwindled heat of the fireline's overflow channel. The letter was short. The Order's letters were always short; brevity was a kind of piety to them.

*A god has been answered within the city walls,* it said. *A prayer offered and granted, in the old manner. You will find who prayed. You will find what heard them. You will report to Inquisitor Vane, who arrives within the week.*

Below that, in a different hand—the hand of First Keeper Ossen, Sable's superior, a good man worn thin by thirty years of service—three lines that had clearly been added in haste:

*Sable. They are saying answered prayers mean the gods are waking. They are saying the Veil has waited a century for this excuse. Be careful what you find. Some things are easier to lose twice.*

Sable read the letter three times, then fed it to the overflow channel and watched it curl.

A god answered. Here, in the city that officially did not exist inside the country that officially did not exist, in the capital whose name appeared on no map and whose ruin the Crown had spent a hundred years pretending was merely picturesque geology. The Order of the Veil had one purpose: that nothing erased should be remembered, and nothing remembered should be answered. For a century they had been a quiet organization, mostly clerks, mostly bored, policing smugglers and antiquarians and the occasional fool who dug where he shouldn't.

Answered prayers were not in that ledger. Answered prayers meant the erasure was failing.

Sable thought of Emberling pressing her face against the smooth edge of absence, and felt something turn over in their chest that was not quite hope and much too dangerous to be.

Because here was the thing Sable had told no one, not Ossen, not the other keepers, not the quiet gray novices who brought the letters: for three months now, Emberling's fire had behaved strangely on certain nights. Burning brighter. Leaning, if fire could be said to lean, toward a particular quarter of the city—toward the tangled lanes south of the Ropewalk, where the night markets ran and nothing was quite legal and everything was quite obtainable. Toward, though Sable had only admitted this to themselves in the smallest hours, a particular person who sometimes walked those lanes with ink-stained fingers and a smile like a locked drawer.

Sable banked the fireline, checked Emberling's fourteen gray patches one last time, and went to find their second-best coat.

* * *

The night market on the Ropewalk began at dusk and ended whenever the watch decided to pretend it hadn't seen it, which lately was later and later, as though even the watch's indifference had begun to fray at the edges. Stalls of oiled canvas glowed with witch-lights; a fiddler played something in seven beats that no conservatory would recognize; the air smelled of fried dough, lamp oil, wet wool, and—underneath it all, faint as a rumor—of something sweet and scorched, like sugar left too long in a pan.

Sable smelled it and thought of the letter and kept walking anyway, because duty was a coat one wore whether or not it fit.

Odile Voss was at the memory-seller's stall, as Sable had known they would be. Some part of Sable's mind—the part that was a professional keeper of impossible things and therefore an excellent tracker of them—had known it the way one knows where north is. Odile stood with their head tilted, reading the vial-labels with the intense, unsmiling attention they gave to everything except people, and the witch-light caught the fine bones of their face and turned their eyes briefly gold.

Sable had met them four years ago, when the Thorn had sent a runner to negotiate fire-rights for the guild's winter stores and the runner had been Odile, twenty-two and insolent, who had looked up at the ruined capital's broken crown and said, "So this is where you hide the biggest hearth in the world," and Sable had fallen—slowly, stupidly, over months of night-market meetings and arguments about the ethics of selling luck—irreversibly, inconveniently in love.

They had never said so. Neither of them. It lived instead in the space between meetings, in the length of time Odile let their fingers rest when passing a coin, in the fact that Sable, who reported everything to the Order, reported nothing at all about the Gilded Thorn.

"Those labels are wrong," Sable said, coming up beside them. "Half those vials are mislabeled. That one says 'a summer afternoon' and it's actually somebody's grief."

Odile didn't startle—that was one of the thousand things Sable loved about them; Odile startled at nothing, catalogued everything—but the corner of their mouth moved. "Keeper Halloran. Slumming it."

"Duty." Sable nodded at the stall. "The grief-vial. I can smell the difference. We train for it. Memories have signatures, like fires do."

"And you can read them all?"

"All the ones that burn honestly." Sable glanced sideways. Odile was wearing the gray scarf tonight, the one that made their eyes look storm-colored instead of gold, and had ink on their left index finger, and was watching Sable with that careful merchant's attention that was also, somehow, the most naked look anyone ever gave Sable in this whole hidden city. "You're buying?"

"Selling, actually. Maretta wants stock for the solstice trade." Odile turned a vial in their fingers, and the liquid inside caught the light like trapped dusk. "Which reminds me. Your Order's been sniffing along the Ropewalk asking questions about candles."

The words landed like a dropped coal. Sable kept their face still through fifteen years of practice, but their pulse stumbled. "Candles."

"Somebody lit a prayer candle somewhere in this city," Odile said lightly, too lightly, turning back to the stall as if discussing weather, "and somebody answered. And now everyone from the Veil to the thieves' cant is whispering about it. So." They held up the grief-vial to the seller, haggled it down with two flicks of their fingers and one devastating smile, and then, without looking at Sable, added: "If your Order asks you to find the petitioner—what will you do?"

It was the closest either of them had ever come to saying it outright. *Whose side are you on.* Sable looked at Odile's profile against the witch-light, at the crooked little scar through one eyebrow that they'd never explained, and felt the whole unbearable geometry of their life assemble itself at once: the dragon dying of homesickness below the earth, the Order sharpening above it, and this person, standing in the smoke and fiddle-music, asking to be chosen without daring to ask.

"The same thing I've always done," Sable said quietly. "I'll find whatever I decide is worth finding."

Odile turned then, really turned, and for one breath the market noise seemed to fall away, and Sable saw in their face something unguarded and young and terrified, and thought: *say it, say it now, one of us has to—*

But a bell rang from the Ropewalk's high end—one stroke, then silence, then two—which was not a watch signal and not a market signal but something older, and every seller along the lane went still as hares. The sweetness-and-scorch smell strengthened, just for a heartbeat, and Sable felt it like a hand laid flat against the sternum: recognition. Something answering.

When Sable looked back, Odile's face was shuttered again, merchant-smooth, but their hand had found Sable's sleeve and gripped it—briefly, hard, deliberately—before letting go.

"Go home, Keeper," Odile said softly. "Whatever your Order sends you to find next week—find it slowly."

They melted into the market crowd with the grief-vial and the gray scarf and the ink-stained hand, and Sable stood alone in the middle of the Ropewalk with their heart hammering out a signal no code-book contained.

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone else in all that glittering crowd, a veiled figure in dove-gray silk paused at the edge of the firelight, watching the space where Odile Voss had been—with the patience of someone who had waited a hundred years and could wait a hundred more.

Then the figure dropped a single coin onto the memory-seller's counter, turned, and was gone.

Sable stared at the coin's pale gleam between the seller's fingers. Even from six feet away, even in bad light, they recognized the stamp on its face: a bridge, singing in the wind, over a river the color of hammered tin.

Coin from a mint that had burned a century ago, in a country that had never existed.

Sable touched the ember-warmth in their pocket where the Order's letter had been, and thought of Emberling dreaming of bells, and understood, with a keeper's certainty, that the fire and the theft and the prayer and the woman they loved were all one thread—and that very soon they would be asked to cut it.
