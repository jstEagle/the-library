## 4. The Client with No Face

The Gilded Thorn kept its appointments the way it kept its ledgers: in ink that could be denied.

Odile Voss had written this one into the day-book three nights ago, in the narrow hand they used for matters of consequence — *Client, veiled. Parlor room, dusk bell. No name given; none asked.* Below it, because habit was a kind of prayer and Odile was nothing if not devout about the wrong things: *Payment terms unknown. Risk assessment pending.*

*Pending* was doing honest work in that sentence. It had been doing honest work for three nights while Odile slept badly and woke with the taste of candle-smoke on their tongue, though no candle had been lit in their rooms.

Now the dusk bell rang, and the parlor room was ready.

Odile had dressed for it carefully, which is to say forgettably. A coat the color of wet slate, cut well enough to signal competence and dull enough to signal nothing else. Hair pinned back. Rings left off except the plain band on the right thumb — the one with a hairline seam where a needle of sleeping-venom lived, because even a meeting was a job, and even a job deserved professional courtesy.

Maretta met them at the foot of the stairs, arms crossed, her many earrings catching the lamplight like a school of small suspicious fish.

"You're really going up."

"I'm really going up."

"Without me."

"Without you." Odile adjusted a cuff that did not need adjusting. "You're the fence of feelings, Mare. If I come down needing to sell something, I want you unsullied by the transaction. Deniability is the whole architecture of this house."

"The whole architecture of this house," Maretta said, "is rotting beams and good paint. And you know what I think of our visitor."

"I know exactly what you think. You've told me four times, once in rhyme."

"It rhymed because it's *true*. Truth wants rhythm." She caught Odile's sleeve as they passed, and her voice dropped out of its performance register into the low one she saved for real things. "A client who won't show a face is a client planning to be unrecognizable later. When the Inquisition comes asking — and they *will*, Odile, there are answered prayers in the gutters now, people lighting candles and getting *burns shaped like doors* — I don't want us holding a receipt."

"We're not holding a receipt. We're holding coin."

"Coin minted by a country that doesn't exist," Maretta said. "Which is either a forgery so magnificent Brannic should weep, or it isn't. And if it isn't—" She let go of the sleeve. "Go on, then. But hear me saying it first, so you can't pretend you didn't."

"I never pretend I didn't," Odile said. "I pretend I didn't *listen*. There's a difference, and it's load-bearing."

They climbed the stairs alone.

* * *

The parlor room sat at the top of the Thorn's townhouse, behind two locked doors and a wall that was, on certain evenings, not a wall at all but a very convincing painting of one. Odile had furnished it themselves over eleven years: deep chairs, a cold hearth dressed with unlit candles, a table set between the chairs at a distance calculated to make leaning forward feel like a decision.

The client was already seated.

Veiled, as promised — not in mourning gauze but in something older, a gray silk shot through with threads that moved when the light moved, so that looking at the veil was like looking at water over stones. Hands folded in lap: gloved, still, patient. No jewelry. No scent, which was itself remarkable; every person in the city smelled of something, coal smoke or tallow or the particular sweetness of the memory-tinctures the Thorn sold to those who wanted to feel they'd lived more than they had. This person smelled of nothing at all, as if the air around them had been politely asked to step aside.

"Master Voss," the client said. The voice was a woman's, aged or made old by effort, smooth as a riverbed. "Thank you for seeing me."

"Thank you for coming to us," Odile said, taking the opposite chair. "Though I'll note for the record that we were never given your name, and I'll note further that I didn't ask, which makes us both very careful people."

"Careful people live longer."

"Some do." Odile folded their hands to match hers, a small mirror, a small courtesy. "Others keep careful ledgers and die rich. What can the Gilded Thorn steal for you?"

For a moment the veiled figure said nothing, and the silence had texture, like felt. Then: "The oldest memory in the city."

Odile had prepared for many answers. Wealthy widow wanting back her wedding day. Politician wanting a rival's shame extracted and bottled. Some pious fool wanting proof of godhood now that gods were answering prayers again, badly, dangerously, all over the lower quarters. They had not prepared for this, and they were annoyed at themselves for the way their breath went shallow.

"There are perhaps nine hundred thousand memories in this city," Odile said lightly. "Give or take the dead. 'Oldest' will need defining."

"The oldest *living* memory," the client said. "Held in a living mind. Not bottled, not traded, not copied. Original stock. It has never been taken from its keeper, and it cannot be taken gently — which is why I have come to professionals."

"And what does this memory contain?"

"A country."

The word sat in the room. Odile let it sit. Outside, the dusk bell finished its last stroke, and somewhere below, Pip's feet went padding past the door on some silent errand, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which suddenly felt like a costume.

"You'll understand," Odile said, "that I need more than poetry. Countries are large. Memories are small. Even a life only holds a few square miles of any nation — a street, a kitchen, a harbor in summer. What you're describing sounds less like a theft and more like an amputation."

"Yes," the client said, and something in the veil shifted, as though she had smiled, or as though the silk itself had flinched. "It is an amputation. I am prepared to pay for a clean one."

She reached into her sleeve and set the payment on the table between them.

Odile had seen money do many things. They had watched a single memory-vial buy a man his mother's forgiveness and ruin him with it. They had seen fortunes move through the Thorn's books disguised as shipments of beeswax and hymnals. But they had never seen coin like this.

Gold, but gold with a greenish heart, struck soft and thick. On the face: a crown made of thorns — not the Thorn's own crooked sigil, but a formal heraldic thing, seven points, each point blooming. On the reverse: a walled city on a hill above a river, and above the city, a sun with a face, eyes open.

Odile knew the sun with a face. Every child in the city knew it, the way children know a lullaby without knowing its words. It appeared on old boundary-stones, on the lintels of buildings too ancient to have other owners, in the corner of paintings where the painter had run out of commission and painted what they pleased. Nobody could say what kingdom it belonged to. Scholars called it *the local motif*. Priests called it *decoration*.

The client called it money.

"That's ten years of the Thorn's operating costs," Odile said quietly. "In one purse."

"That is a retainer. The balance on delivery."

"Where did you get currency from a mint that—"

"—no longer exists?" The veil tilted, considering. "I have my sources. As you have yours. That is rather the nature of our profession, Master Voss. We deal in things that officially never happened. I am simply more thorough about it than most."

Odile looked at the coins and thought, with the cold clarity that came upon them sometimes like weather: *This is the missing page.* Three weeks ago, auditing the founding ledgers, they had found a gap — a page razored out at the spine, centuries ago, by a blade fine enough to leave no burr. The guildmaster had shrugged when asked. *Old houses lose pages,* he'd said. *Like coats lose buttons.* But Odile had weighed the neighboring pages, had read the ghost-impressions pressed through from the sheet before, and there had been numbers there. Numbers of the kind used for holdings, not transactions. Something the Thorn owned. Something the Thorn had been keeping.

And here sat a stranger paying in the coinage of nowhere, asking for a memory of everything.

"I'll need time to locate the keeper," Odile said. "And I'll need to know the terms of extraction. What may be taken, what must remain. Whether the keeper survives the loss."

"The keeper survives," the client said. "That much I can promise. Everything else—" the gloved hands opened, palms up, an elegant shrug, "—is negotiation. You are the professionals. I am only the desire."

*Only the desire.* Odile would remember that phrase later, turning it over like a coin, feeling its weight, wondering how anything so light could cost so much.

"When do you require an answer?"

"You have already given it." The client rose, and the gray silk settled around her like water finding its level. "But say it aloud. The old ways prefer it. And the old ways, Master Voss, are waking up. Best to stay on speaking terms."

Odile stood too, because one did not remain seated while a purse of impossible gold sat on the table, and because some animal part of them wanted to be upright, near the door, near height.

"The Gilded Thorn accepts the commission," they said. "The oldest living memory in the city, delivered intact per negotiated terms. Payment as tendered."

"Payment as tendered," the client echoed, and this time the smile was audible. "Then we are agreed, and the agreement is old, and old agreements hold." At the door she paused, one gloved hand on the frame, and said, without turning: "Tell your fence she rhymed correctly. Truth does want rhythm. It's why they took the music out of the country first."

The door closed. The corridor was empty. Odile stood in the parlor room among the unlit candles with a fortune in dead coinage and the absolute certainty that they had just been known — thoroughly, intimately, by someone who had been listening at the foot of their stairs.

* * *

Downstairs, the family was waiting in the counting room, which meant Maretta had told them to be, which meant the argument had already started without Odile, which was traditional.

Brannic had one of the coins under his loupe, pinned to the scarred oak table with two fingers. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink-stained fingers and the gentle, appalled expression he wore whenever forgery offended him — which was often, since most things were forgeries of something.

"This is real," he said, before Odile had fully cleared the doorway. "That's the problem. Sit down. Look at this. Look at the *strike*."

Odile sat. Brannic turned the coin under the lamp.

"Soft strike, high relief, no die-cracks, no filing, no circulation wear worth mentioning. Which means it was minted and then *kept* — hoarded, cased, handled maybe a dozen times in its life. Now look at the alloy." He tapped the greenish heart of the metal. "There's silver in this gold. Deliberate. It's a minting style, Odile. It's a *period* style. I've seen it exactly once, on a seal impression in the Inquisition archive plates — the ones of the border-stones from before the Reconciliation. This coin is a century old at minimum, and it has never spent a night in the ground."

"So it came out of a collection."

"So it came out of a *hoard*," Brannic said. "Out of somebody's private treasury, sealed since before the fires. And here's the part that's going to ruin my evening." He fished a second coin from the purse and set it beside the first, edge to edge. Identical. Die-identical. Same flaw in the sun's left eye, same whisper of doubling on the thorn-crown. "Two coins, one die. Hoards get mixed. Collections get assembled. But a *purse* — a purse is minted together, issued together, spent together. This purse came out of a single strongbox, opened recently, by someone who has been sitting on a dead kingdom's payroll for a hundred years."

"Or someone who robbed such a person," Maretta said from the window, where she'd taken up her post with her arms crossed again, silhouetted against the street-lamp glow.

"Then they robbed a ghost," Brannic said. "The mint that struck these burned in the same year the border-stones went blank. Same season. There are records — bad ones, burned ones, the kind you have to read sideways — but they agree on the shape of it. Kingdom ends, mint burns, coinage melts down or vanishes. Except apparently somebody's didn't."

Pip was at the far end of the table, small and still, arranging the remaining coins from the retainer into a spiral. Odile had long ago stopped wondering whether Pip listened; the child heard everything and reported almost nothing, and the almost was always worth having. Now Pip placed the last coin at the spiral's center, looked up at Odile, and pointed — deliberately, gravely — at the sun with its open eyes.

"I see it," Odile said.

Pip pointed at the coin, then at the ceiling — toward the archive, where the founding ledgers slept — then drew one finger slowly across their own throat.

"No," Odile said. "Not murder. But yes. I think it's the page."

The counting room went quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"You found the gap three weeks ago," Maretta said. "You told me it was nothing. You told me old houses lose pages."

"I lied," Odile said. "I tell lies; it's the family trade. The gap is in the founding book, Mare. First quire, fourth gathering. Whatever was on that page was important enough to remove with a razor and quiet enough that nobody missed it in three hundred years. And tonight a woman wearing a veil full of moving water paid us a decade's income in the currency of a country that officially never existed, and quoted our own private conversations back to us, and told me the old ways are waking up." Odile turned one of the coins over, and over. "The job touches the page. I'd bet the house on it. I'd bet the *ledger* on it."

"Then we refuse," Maretta said flatly. "We return the retainer, we plead a full calendar, we invent a plague in the family, I don't care. Odile — the Order of the Veil is already sniffing at the answered prayers. Sable's lot will be hunting whoever woke the old gods, and you know it, because you know *Sable*, whatever the two of you call it these days. Taking this job is standing in a field during lightning season holding up the tallest thing you own."

"Our invisibility is the tallest thing we own," Odile said. "Yes. I've done the sums."

"Then—"

"But here's the sum I keep arriving at anyway." Odile set the coin down and looked around the table, at Brannic's appalled tenderness, at Maretta's fury wearing fear's clothing, at Pip's open, waiting face. "Someone erased a country. Thoroughly — maps, records, mint, music, the lot. And then someone founded a guild of thieves in its ashes and gave us a founding contract with a page cut out, and for three hundred years we've been stealing memories and bottling luck and telling ourselves we're in the business of *nothing*, of *invisibility*, of *deniability*. And now the prayers are being answered, and the coin is walking around, and a woman with no face knows what we sound like at the bottom of our own stairs." They spread their hands. "We were never invisible, Mare. We were *stored*. Like everything else in the vault. And I want to know who holds our receipt."

"That's curiosity," Maretta said.

"It's the best thing I own."

"It's how thieves die! Curiosity, then greed, then a rope!" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Odile could see her hating it, and pressed on anyway. "Brannic, tell them. Tell them the smart thing is to melt the coin and forget the face."

Brannic was quiet a long moment, turning his loupe over and over in his stained fingers.

"The smart thing," he said at last, heavily, "is to melt the coin and forget the face." He looked up. "But Odie's right about the page. I've been thinking about it for three weeks too, and lying about it, which apparently is also the family trade. Whoever cut that page was protecting us or *from* us, and either way, they decided we were safer ignorant. And I find—" his jaw worked, "—I find I've stopped agreeing to be safe on other people's decisions. Even dead ones'. Especially dead ones'."

"Oh, wonderful," Maretta said to the ceiling. "Wonderful. Two of you. The vote's tied and the child doesn't count."

Pip, without looking up from the coin-spiral, raised one small hand.

"You abstain?"

Pip shook their head. Then they pointed at Odile, and at the coins, and mimed — with terrible, gentle precision — the act of opening a book.

*Read the page,* the gesture said. *Find out.*

Maretta closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fury had banked down to coals, which was worse and also more useful. "Fine. Fine. But we do it my way. Slowly. Brannic traces the coin — quietly, no archives, no Inquisition plate-rooms, use the fences, use the pawn-brokers, use the people who owe us. And Odile—" she stepped close, and lowered her voice so that only the family could hear it, "—you find the keeper of this oldest memory, and you look at them, and you ask yourself one question before you lift a single thing. Ask yourself who else remembers what they remember. Because if the answer is *nobody*—" her voice caught, steadied, "—then it isn't a theft anymore. It's a killing. And I need to know, before we go one step further, that you know the difference."

Odile held her gaze. Around them the counting room ticked and settled, the lamps burned, the impossible coins lay in their neat spiral with the sun's eyes open on every face.

"I know the difference," Odile said. "I've just never had it priced before."

They gathered the retainer, all of it, and carried it down to the vault themselves — past the shelves of bottled luck, past the drawers of borrowed courage, past the iron cabinet where the founding ledgers slept with their one wound in the first quire. At the vault door Odile paused, one coin loose in their palm, and looked a long moment at the little sun with its open eyes.

Somewhere across the city, beneath the ruined capital, a dragon's fire was guttering, and a keeper Odile loved was asking questions of the wrong people for all the right reasons. Somewhere a veiled woman was spending a dead kingdom's wages. And somewhere in the dark between them lay a memory old enough to hold a country entire, waiting in a living head like a lamp in a window.

Odile closed their hand around the coin, felt its century-old edges bite, and went to find out whose window it was.

They did not yet know that the answer would be their own.
