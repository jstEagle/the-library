## 5. Inquisitor Vane

The dragon's fire had gone the color of old honey, and Sable knew what that meant the way a child knows hunger.

Emberling lay coiled in the hollow beneath the ruined capital, in the vaulted dark that three generations of keepers called the fireline—a mile of collapsed colonnades and melted mosaics where the last dragon of the erased kingdom breathed her slow, guttering heat into the stones. The walls still held her warmth like a banked oven. It was the only place left in the country-that-wasn't where you could stand barefoot on tile and feel summer.

Sable knelt at the edge of the great jaw, ladling tallow-and-ash salve along the seam where scale met lip. Emberling's eye rolled toward them, vast and amber, the pupil narrowing to a thread.

*"You smell afraid,"* she said, in the way she said things—not words exactly, but pressure behind the eyes, a taste of smoke at the back of the throat.

"I'm not afraid."

*"Liar. You are always lying beautifully. It is your one talent besides the salve."*

Sable smiled despite themselves and worked the ladle deeper into the crevice. "Something's wrong in the city. People are praying again. And the prayers—" They hesitated. You did not say certain things aloud near a dragon; the air remembered. "The prayers are being answered."

The great eye closed, then opened. Beneath the honey-gold, something flickered that might have been hope or might have been terror. With Emberling it was often both.

*"I dreamed of the shore,"* she said. *"The white shore. There were bells."*

"You dream of it every night."

*"It is closer every night. Do you understand me, keeper? The country is not gone. It is only folded away, like a letter no one has opened. And someone is beginning to unfold it, and I can hear the crease tearing."* Her breath moved through the colonnade, hot enough to lift Sable's hair. *"If they tear it all the way open before I am strong enough, I will burn with it. And if I am not there when it opens—"* She did not finish. Dragons rarely finished their worst sentences.

Sable pressed their forehead briefly against the warm scale above her nostril. "Eat first. Worry after."

She took the offered haunch of salted ox without complaint, which frightened Sable more than anything else. A dragon who ate quietly was a dragon conserving flame.

They were still kneeling there when the bells began.

Not Emberling's bells—not the shore-dream. These were real, ringing from the Order's citadel above ground, and they rang in the pattern Sable had read about in the oldest keeper-manuals but never once heard in nine years of service: three strokes, silence, three strokes. *A visitation.* The Order of the Veil was sending someone down.

Sable wiped their hands, pulled on their coat, and went up the spiral stair with their heart already climbing ahead of them.

* * *

Inquisitor Vane arrived at midmorning, though under the earth morning was a courtesy of lanterns. He came down the great stair flanked by four gray-cloaked acolytes, and he was not what Sable expected, which was itself unsettling. Zealots were supposed to burn. Vane was cold as a ledger line: tall, gray-eyed, precise, his white cassock so clean it seemed to refuse the dust of the fireline on principle. His voice, when he spoke, was soft enough that acolytes leaned in to catch it, and everyone else leaned in too, because softness from an inquisitor is worse than shouting.

"Keepers of the Ember," he said, standing in the mosaic court where the firelight pooled. He did not look at Emberling's distant glow. He looked at the people. That was how Sable knew what kind of inquisitor he was. "Nine of you. Good. Nine witnesses, nine accounts. My name is Vane. I have come about prayers."

No one breathed.

"In the last month, eleven petitions have been answered in this city. Candles that burned without wicks. Sick children healed by ash. A woman who spoke to her drowned father through a well. The Order does not concern itself with fraud—we burn frauds on Tuesdays, it is practically administrative." A thin smile, offered to no one. "We concern ourselves with *answers*. Because answers require gods, and our country's gods were accounted for a century ago. Every god dead, every shrine sealed, every prayer a superstition. That is what the histories say. So either the histories are wrong"—he let the silence stretch until it creaked—"or something is wearing their voices."

He walked slowly along the line of keepers, hands folded behind his back, and stopped in front of Sable last, which felt deliberate.

"You are Keeper Halloran."

"Sable Halloran. Yes."

"Nine years. Longest posting at the fireline in a generation. Devoted." He said *devoted* the way a man says *isolated*. "Tell me, Keeper Halloran. Has your dragon prayed?"

Someone made a small involuntary sound. Sable kept their face still. "Dragons don't pray, Inquisitor. Dragons remember."

"Do they." Vane turned his head slightly, and for the first time looked toward the deep glow at the end of the colonnade, and Sable watched him weigh it—the last living creature older than the erasure, the one witness the Order could neither question nor control. "How interesting. Everything in this country remembers, if you ask it correctly. Wells. Ash. Mosaics." His gaze came back. "And dragons. If any of you hears an answered prayer—if you *smell* one, if you dream one—you will report it to me within a day. Not to your superiors. To me. Those who help the Veil find the source will be remembered kindly. Those who conceal it—" He smiled again, and it reached his eyes, and his eyes were like frost over a well. "Well. We will make examples kindly too."

He turned to go, then paused, as if remembering something trivial.

"There is a shrine two streets from the lamplighters' gate. Someone has been leaving candles there. Fresh wax, every few nights." He said it mildly, conversationally, a man mentioning weather. "We will be visiting it this afternoon. Anyone who wishes to observe the Order's methods is welcome. Education is part of my office."

Then he went back up the stair, and the fireline exhaled all around Sable, forty lungs' worth of held breath.

Old Kessin, who had kept the ember-banks since before Sable was born, gripped their elbow. "Go nowhere near that shrine."

"I wasn't going to."

*"You were going to,"* rumbled Emberling from the deep, and Sable did not bother denying it to either of them.

* * *

Sable went.

They told themselves it was duty—keepers were sworn to record everything, and a shrine burning was data—but duty had never made their hands shake like this. They reached the lamplighters' quarter as the shadows went long, and joined the knot of onlookers gathered at a careful distance, the crowd that always assembled for Order business the way crows assemble for anything.

The shrine was nothing. That was what struck Sable first—it was nothing, a niche in a wall, a shelf, a stub of candle, a saucer of ash. Half the city had shrines like it, little domestic apostasies nobody enforced because nobody believed anymore. Except now somebody had lit candles here and asked for something, and the wax had burned without a wick, and the scorch-mark behind the shelf was shaped—Sable felt their stomach drop—shaped like a door.

Vane stood before it with the patience of a surgeon.

"Who tends this?" he asked the crowd, softly. No answer. He waited. He was very good at waiting; you could feel the whole street leaning away from its own shadow.

Then a woman stepped forward.

Sable knew her by sight—Tamsin, a rag-seller who worked the evening markets, gray-haired, stooped, with a laugh you could hear across a square. Sable had bought ribbon from her. Everyone had bought ribbon from her. She walked to the shrine as if walking to a friend's door and stood beside it with her chin up.

"It's mine," she said. "I've been lighting them for a year. Before any of this started. Just candles. For the ones we lost."

"The ones we lost," Vane repeated. "Lost where?"

Tamsin's mouth worked. Whatever she saw in his face, she chose honesty, which was the bravest stupid thing Sable had ever witnessed. "You know where. Everyone knows where. We just don't say it."

"No," Vane agreed gently. "We don't. And yet the candles answered. Do you know what answered you, woman?"

"Nothing answered. I just—remembered. Is remembering a crime now?"

"Remembering is free," Vane said. "Being *answered* is sedition." He nodded to his acolytes. "Burn it. And her with it—publicly. Let the city learn what happens when it talks to ghosts."

The sound the crowd made was not a gasp. It was smaller and worse than a gasp—the collective intake of people discovering how much they could lose. Two acolytes seized Tamsin's arms. She didn't struggle; she looked stunned, a woman interrupted mid-sentence by the end of the world.

"You can't," she said, almost reasonably. "For candles? For *candles?*"

"For doors," said Vane.

They stacked faggots against the wall while the crowd stood frozen, and Sable understood, with terrible clarity, that they had perhaps ten seconds in which to decide what kind of person they were. Every instinct of survival—the instincts nine years at the fireline had honed—said *stillness*. Say nothing. Remember everything. Report nothing. Keepers survive by being furniture.

But Tamsin had sold them ribbon. Tamsin had laughed across squares.

Sable stepped forward—one step, out of the crowd, into the open—and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect.

"She's a rag-seller, Inquisitor. She sells rags. Whatever door you think she opened, it was an accident of grief, not treason. Burn the shrine. Leave her to the magistrates."

Vane turned and regarded Sable with genuine interest, the way a scholar regards a new specimen. "Keeper Halloran. Come here."

Every part of Sable wanted to run. They walked forward instead.

Vane studied them at arm's length. "You know," he said, conversational again, "the Order's records list your predecessor. Keeper Anwell. Also devoted. Also long-posted. He was retired eight years ago for—sympathies. You strike me as a person with sympathies." He leaned in slightly, and his voice dropped to almost tenderness. "Sympathies are how the doors open, Keeper. Grief. Love. Memory. All of it kindling. I am going to watch you very closely, and I want you to know that I will enjoy it."

Then he straightened, gestured, and the faggots were lit.

Sable would carry the sound of it for the rest of their life. Not screaming—worse, at the end: singing. Tamsin sang something in the old cadences, four notes rising, a lullaby-shape that half the crowd visibly recognized even if no one could name it, and the crowd wept and did not move and did not move and did not move, and Sable stood among them, motionless, memorizing everything, hating their own memorizing, feeling something in their chest harden from fear into something with edges.

When it was done, Vane bowed slightly to the crowd—to the *ash*—and said, "Thank you for attending," and left.

* * *

Emberling smelled the smoke on Sable's clothes before they reached the deep colonnade, and her growl shook grit from the ceiling.

*"Tell me."*

So Sable told her, all of it, kneeling with their forehead against her jaw-scale while she listened, and when they finished she was silent a long time, and her silence had heat in it.

*"They burned her for a candle,"* she said at last. *"While I lie here dying, and they burn the ones who remember me. Sable. Little keeper. Listen. Your Order thinks I am their weapon. Their furnace. Their last sword against the waking. But I am not theirs. I never was theirs. I am the country's. And the country is starting to wake up."*

"Emberling—"

*"Find whoever is unfolding it. Find them before the gray man does. Not for the Order. For us."* Her eye, huge and amber, filled Sable's vision. *"You love someone. I have tasted it in your blood every time you come back from the night market smelling of cardamom and ink. Go to your cardamom-and-ink. Tell them the gray man is hunting. Tell them the fireline is no longer safe for secrets."*

Sable's face went hot. "You're supposed to be a dignified ancient creature."

*"I am dying,"* Emberling said placidly. *"Dying creatures may be honest."*

* * *

The warning couldn't go by letter. Vane's acolytes were already reading the night market's couriers, and letters burn. But the Thorn traded in memory, and memory had channels of its own.

Sable bought the vial from a dealer off Salt Row—an unremarkable thing, thumb-sized, cloudy glass, holding a stolen scrap of some stranger's afternoon: rain on a window, a dog barking twice, bread cooling. Innocuous. Then, in their rooms above the fireline, by candlelight, using the technique every keeper learns for tagging specimens, Sable bent their will into the vial's contents and hid a message inside the memory the way you hide a coin inside a loaf.

*Vane came down. He burns petitioners. A woman died today for a candle—Tamsin, the rag-seller, if Odile ever knew her. He suspects the Thorn. He suspects me. He watches the night market. Do not come Thursday. Do not come anywhere. I mean it. —and also: the scorch-marks are doors. Emberling says the country is folding open. I need to see you and I must not see you and I don't know how to want both things. S.*

They sealed it with plain wax—nothing gilded, nothing traceable—and carried it at dusk to the secondhand stall on Cooper's Lane where a one-eyed woman sold buttons and broken instruments, and left it in the tray of cracked vials marked *memories, common, cheap*, knowing that by midnight it would travel hand to hand through the Thorn's quiet economy until it reached a pair of clever hands that knew how to read between the layers of a stranger's rain-on-glass.

Walking home through the darkening streets, Sable passed the burnt shrine. The scorch-mark on the wall remained, door-shaped, untouched—Vane had burned the petitioner but not the mark, whether from oversight or something colder, Sable couldn't guess. Candle-stubs and ash had already begun accumulating at its base. By morning there would be flowers.

They stood looking at it a long moment.

*Sympathies are how the doors open,* Vane had said. He'd meant it as a threat. Standing in the cold with ash on their boots and a hidden message traveling through the city toward the person they loved most and understood least, Sable finally understood it as something else entirely.

It was instructions.

Somewhere out there, Odile Voss was running ledgers in a safehouse full of stolen memories, and somewhere out there, an inquisitor was sharpening his soft voice, and somewhere beneath it all a dying dragon dreamed of a white shore with bells. Three threads, pulling tight. Sable turned up their collar and walked home through a city that was learning, candle by candle, how to pray with its eyes open.

By Thursday, one of those threads would reach the wrong hands.

Sable just didn't know yet whose.
