## 8. Emberling Dreams

The fireline was quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

Sable Halloran-—no, just Sable, the Order had stripped surnames from its keepers along with everything else soft—moved down the corridor of the undercity with a lantern in one hand and a pail of rendered tallow in the other, counting their own heartbeats the way they'd been taught. One for each step. Two hundred and forty steps from the sealed gate to the first threshold of Emberling's hollow. The count mattered. The count was discipline, and discipline was the only prayer the Order of the Veil permitted.

At step two hundred and forty, they stopped, set down the lantern, and pressed their palm flat against the warm stone.

"Emberling," they said softly. "It's me."

The stone pulsed once beneath their hand, slow as a sleeping heart, and answered in the old way—not with sound but with heat, a wash of it rolling up through Sable's arm, into the chest, settling behind the ribs like a second pulse. *Here,* the heat said. *Here, here, here.*

Sable had been keeper for eleven years. They had learned to read the dragon the way farmers read weather: in pressure changes, in the taste of ash on the tongue, in the particular quality of warmth that came through forty feet of living rock. And for eleven years, that warmth had been steady as a hearth.

Lately it had begun to gutter.

They descended the last stair into the hollow itself, and there Emberling lay coiled in her bed of fused glass and cinder, an island of dull embers in a cathedral of dark. She was not large—not anymore. Once, the histories said, dragons had filled ravines like rivers of bronze; once, Emberling's wingspan had shaded whole market squares in the capital above. But she had been folding herself smaller for decades, joint over joint, scale over scale, the way old people fold into chairs, conserving what could not be replaced.

Her fire-glands showed through the thinning hide at her throat like coals wrapped in parchment. Dull orange. Flickering.

"No," Sable murmured, kneeling beside the great wedge of her skull. Her eye opened—a slit of amber, ancient and unblinking—and found them. "No, none of that. Eyes open is good. Let's have you awake."

*Small-keeper.* Her voice arrived inside Sable's skull the way warmth arrived through stone: without passing through the air at all. *You came at the wrong hour.*

"I came at the hour you called." Sable set the pail down and began, with practiced hands, to work the tallow into the seams of her throat-scales where the skin had gone dry and cracked. It smelled of lanolin and char. "Don't pretend you didn't."

*I did not call.*

"You did. Third watch. A pull on the link, twice, like knocking." Sable kept their voice even, kept their hands moving. "You've been calling every night this week. You think I don't keep a ledger of my own?"

Emberling's nostril flared, exhaling a thread of smoke that curled up toward the vaulted dark and hung there like a question. *I do not remember calling.*

"You don't remember because you were asleep."

*Then I was not calling. I was dreaming.* A pause, long enough that Sable heard the drip of mineral water somewhere deep in the undercity, patient as a clock. *And I do not wish to speak of the dreams.*

"You will," Sable said gently, "because I'm going to ask."

*Insolent small-keeper.*

"Loyal insolent small-keeper. There's a difference, and it's measured in tallow."

The amber eye closed again—but not in dismissal. Sable knew the difference. It was surrender, or something near it. They worked in silence for a while, hands moving along the dragon's jaw, feeling the tremor there that hadn't been there a month ago. The fire-glands beneath Sable's fingertips stuttered like a candle in a draft. Eleven years ago those glands had run hot enough to make the air above Emberling's back shimmer. Now Sable could press two fingers against the thickest of them and hold it there, and feel only a fading warmth, a banked thing, a hearth going out one coal at a time.

The Order's physicians-of-flame had theories. Age. Contamination of the tallow. A wasting sickness of the blood-fire, treatable with minerals and rest. Sable had listened to all of it with the polite blankness the Order trained into its keepers, and had believed none of it. Because Sable had seen dying creatures before, and Emberling was not dying the way flesh dies.

She was dying the way a flame dies when you close off its air.

*Ask, then,* Emberling said at last, so quietly the words were barely more than heat. *Since you will anyway. Ask your questions, small-keeper, and I will lie about half of them.*

"That's very generous."

*I am a generous creature. Everyone says so.*

Sable sat back on their heels, wiped their hands on their apron, and looked up at the vast dim shape of her—the ridge of her spine like a buried mountain range, the folded wings that would no longer open past a quarter-span. Grief rose in them, sudden and specific, the way it always did in this hollow, and they swallowed it down because grief was another thing the Order did not permit, and because Emberling could taste emotion through the link the way Sable tasted salt.

"Why are your fires failing?" Sable asked. "Truly. Not the physicians' answer. Yours."

The silence stretched so long that Sable thought she might simply refuse. Then, low, in the tone of someone confessing to a crime:

*Because I am burning for a country that is not there.*

Sable's hands went still. "Say that again."

*Dragons do not make fire from meat, small-keeper. Your physicians measure my glands and feed me tallow and they understand nothing. Fire is a promise. Every flame is a promise—that there is something worth burning for, some ground beneath it, some hearth it answers to. Mine was lit a thousand years ago to burn for this land. For its kings' oaths and its harvest-fires and its funeral pyres and its kitchen hearths, ten million little flames all answering one another across the valleys, and mine was the great one underneath, the anchor-stone of all of them.* The eye opened again, and this time what moved behind the amber was not age but anguish, raw and bottomless. *And then one day the oaths went out. All of them. Every hearth, every pyre, every candle, all at once, like a breath blown across the world. Not extinguished—*unwritten*. Do you understand the difference? A flame put out leaves smoke. A flame unwritten leaves nothing. Not even the memory of having burned.*

"The Erasure," Sable said. The word came out flat. Every child in the Veil's schools learned it: a century ago, the kingdom of Vhalen—or whatever its true name had been; even the name was gone—had ceased. Not conquered, not destroyed. *Ceased*. Maps redrew themselves overnight. Records rotted into blankness. The borders simply closed like a wound, and the country that had been there became a country that had never been, remembered by no one, mourned by no one, because mourning required a thing to mourn, and there was nothing left to hang the grief on.

Nothing left—except, apparently, a dragon buried beneath its ruins.

"I thought you were hatched after," Sable said slowly. "The records say—"

*The records say what the Order wrote. The Order writes what keeps the Veil tidy.* Smoke curled from her nostrils, bitter-smelling. *I am older than your Order, small-keeper. I am older than the hole in the maps. I burned for that country before it was taken away, and I have burned for its absence ever since, and a flame cannot anchor itself to an absence. So I fold. I sleep. I spend myself as slowly as I can, hoping—I have always been hoping—that the ground I burn for will come back and give me leave to stop.*

"And now?"

*Now the dreams come,* Emberling said, *and in the dreams I can smell the harvest-fires again. And I wake, and I call for it, and there is nothing there to call to. And each time I wake, I have spent a little more of what remains. Grief is expensive, small-keeper. I am running out of coin.*

Sable sat down fully on the glass floor, because their legs had stopped being interested in holding them. Above, faintly, through forty feet of rock, came the muffled sounds of the ruined capital: cart wheels, a bell, someone shouting cheerfully about fish. A city built on top of a grave no one knew was a grave.

"You're killing yourself with homesickness," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

*I prefer to think of it as loyalty,* Emberling said, with a ghost of her old dry humor. *But yes.*

*

That night, the Order sent for Sable.

Not the fireline commandery—they would have sent a runner, informal, smelling of lamp oil. This summons came on vellum, under seal, delivered by a rider in grey whose face Sable never saw clearly, which meant it came from above the commandery. From the Veiled Court itself. The message was four lines long and contained, for Sable's purposes, only one sentence:

*Inquiries continue regarding unauthorized petitions to proscribed divinities. Keepers will report any anomaly observed among the bound flames.*

Anomaly. Sable stood in their narrow cell in the keepers' quarters, holding the vellum, and understood with cold clarity what the Order already suspected: that the gods were stirring, that prayers were being *answered*, and that somewhere in its machinery of ledgers and seals and quiet executions, the Veil had connected the answered prayers to the erased country—to the one thing in the world the Veil had spent a century ensuring stayed erased.

And Emberling, dreaming nightly of harvest-fires, was an anomaly the size of a mountain.

*Report any anomaly.* Sable fed the vellum to their candle and watched it curl and blacken. Reporting meant inquisitors in the undercity, chains on the great skull, and eventually—when her fires failed completely, as they were failing—an official determination that the last dragon had died of natural causes, and a quiet order for the rendering of her body into lamp oil and relic-charms. The Order did not waste.

Not reporting meant treason.

Sable lay on their cot and stared at the ceiling and thought about Odile Voss.

They thought about the night market three weeks ago, the lantern-strung lane behind the spice sellers, the way Odile had leaned against a post with their arms crossed and their mouth doing that thing it did—half smirk, half apology—and said, *"Keeper of the last dragon. You know how that sounds? Like a title somebody invented to keep you busy."* And Sable had laughed, actually laughed, on duty, in uniform, which was a small treason all its own, and Odile's eyes had gone soft and surprised at the sound, as if they'd been trying to earn it for years and hadn't quite believed the strategy would work.

They thought about other nights, fewer, dearer: a rooftop above the tanneries with a bottle of plum wine passed back and forth; Odile's shoulder against theirs, warm through two layers of wool; the conversation that always circled and never landed, both of them too careful, too sworn, too aware that a keeper of the Veil and a ledger-keeper of the Gilded Thorn were standing on opposite sides of a line drawn in something considerably worse than ink.

*"What would you do," Odile had asked once, apropos of nothing, watching the smoke of the city rise, "if your duty and your heart filed conflicting paperwork?"*

And Sable had said, *"The Order trains us to want what we're ordered to want,"* and Odile had looked at them for a long moment with an expression Sable still couldn't file anywhere, and had said, *"That's the saddest sentence anyone's ever said to me, and I fence stolen memories for a living."*

Sable turned over. Slept badly. Dreamed of amber eyes.

*

Three nights later, Emberling woke screaming.

There was no other word for it. Dragons did not scream—Sable had been taught that in their first year, had repeated it like catechism: *a bound flame does not cry out, for a bound flame has nothing left to fear*. And then Sable came down the two hundred and forty steps at a dead run, taking them three at a time with the lantern swinging wild, because the link had torn open like a wound and what poured through it was not words at all but *fire and loss and a name, a name, a name burning on the tip of a tongue that could no longer pronounce it*—

Emberling was thrashing.

Eleven years, and Sable had never seen her move beyond the slow shifting of a sleeper. Now her tail lashed and struck the hollow wall, and the fused glass of her bed cracked like lake ice, and her jaws gaped open and fire came—not the tame pilot-flame of the fireline, but the old fire, the true fire, white-edged and roaring, pouring up at the vaulted ceiling in a column that turned the whole cavern to noon. Heat slammed against Sable's face hard enough to dry their eyes, and the stone overhead began to glow, began visibly to *soften*, and somewhere in Sable's mind a clinical, horrified voice noted that if the flame held, it would breach the foundations of the capital above and bring several districts down into the dark.

But it didn't hold. It couldn't hold. Halfway through the roar, the column gutted, flickered, starved—the flame reaching for something that wasn't there, burning on empty, spending fuel that no longer existed to feed it—and collapsed back into Emberling's throat with a sound like a sob. The white died to orange. The orange died to red. And the great head sank down onto the cracked glass, and the screaming stopped, and what came through the link instead was worse than screaming.

*Gone. It's gone. I saw it and it's gone, why do I keep seeing it, why do they let me see it—*

Sable crossed the burning floor—glass crackling underfoot, air shimmering, their eyebrows crisping at the edges—and pressed themselves against the dragon's jaw, arms spread, palms flat against the hot hide, the way they had done since their first year, the way that meant *here, here, I am here*.

"Emberling. Look at me. Look at me."

*The harvest-fires,* the dragon whispered. *All the valley fires, rowed out like lamps, like stars fallen into the fields. I could smell the barley burning sweet. I could hear the singing, small-keeper, the harvest songs, hundreds of voices—* Her mind shuddered. *And then the dream turned around, the way dreams do, and I saw the border-stone, and the stone was blank. Blank as an egg that will never hatch. And I woke up and I called for the country and the country wasn't—there isn't anything to call TO—*

"I know," Sable said, and their voice broke, and they let it, because there was no one down here to report the breaking. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

*I am the last one who remembers it burning,* Emberling said, and now the anguish had gone quiet and terrible, settled into the marrow of her. *Do you understand? When I die, small-keeper, the last fire that ever answered that land goes out, and no one will ever have burned for it again. Not forgotten—worse. Unremembered by everything except a dying animal underground. What kind of inheritance is that? What kind of heirloom is grief with no name attached?*

And Sable, holding onto the jaw of a dying god-beast in the dark beneath a city that didn't know what it was built on, felt the pieces click together with an almost audible snap.

An inheritance.

Two halves of one inheritance.

Because Sable had spent three weeks doing something the Order would have flayed them for: listening. Listening in the taverns and the night markets, in the pauses between Odile's deflections, in the gossip Maretta's couriers traded like currency. And the story that kept surfacing, wearing different clothes each time, was always the same story underneath: a prayer candle that lit itself. A scorch-mark shaped like a door. A veiled client paying in coins from a mint that had burned a century before it minted anything. And at the center of it all, whispered once, half-drunk, by a fence who should have known better: *the oldest memory in the city*, locked in a pearl, in a house that remembers.

A pearl that held a kingdom entire. A dragon who was the last living recollection of the same.

Memory and flame. Receipt and anchor. The written record of what was erased, and the last living fire that had ever warmed it—one locked in a house that reshaped itself around remembering, one buried beneath the ruins slowly starving on homesickness. Two halves, separated a century ago, both waiting.

Both, Sable realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cooling air, *both currently sought by the same veiled client the Thorn had taken coin from*—and hunted by Inquisitor Vane, whose reputation arrived in cities the way frost arrives, silently and ahead of him.

Emberling's breathing had slowed. The red of her glands had steadied to a sullen ember-glow. Sleep was taking her back down, dragging her under whether she willed it or not, and her mind through the link was going soft and vast and sorrowful, like a sea at dusk.

*Small-keeper,* she murmured, nearly gone. *If someone comes asking after old fires—*

"Who would come asking after old fires?"

*A thief,* said the dragon, on the very edge of dream, with something that might have been amusement or might have been hope. *A thief with careful hands and a crooked smile. If such a person comes... show them the other half.*

Sable knelt alone in the great cooling hollow for a long time afterward, in the smell of char and lanolin, listening to the huge slow heartbeat and thinking about a rooftop above the tanneries, a bottle of plum wine, and a pair of clever hands that had no idea yet what they were holding.

Then they got up, took a blank memory-vial from their kit, and began, carefully, to write.
