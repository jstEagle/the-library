## 9. The Theft

The house on Corvane Street had been watching them for an hour.

Odile felt it the way a locksmith feels a tumbler about to give—not with the eyes, but with the teeth, some animal part of the skull that remembered when houses had teeth. The manor did not breathe, exactly. But its windows shifted in their frames by fractions of an inch when no one stood at them, and the iron gate had swung open before Pip touched it, hospitably, horribly, like a mouth that wanted to be fed.

"Tell me again why we're doing this the polite way," Brannic muttered from the shadow of the garden wall. He was dressed as a coal merchant tonight, all soot-grey wool and a delivery ledger under one arm, and even his disguise looked offended. "I could forge us a writ of entry. I could forge us a *bishop*. Instead we're walking into a building that's been eating burglars since before either of us was born."

"It's not eating anyone," Odile said. They checked the little brass instrument in their palm—a compass whose needle pointed not north but toward value, toward the densest concentration of worth in any given place. Right now it strained against its glass housing, quivering like a held breath, aimed at the dark heart of the manor. "It's *bargaining*. Everything this house does is a bargain. That's why we're not breaking in. We're making a purchase."

"With what money?"

Odile touched their chest, just below the collarbone, where the memory-vials hung sewn into the lining of their coat. Three of them. Small as fingernails, warm as coins held too long.

Brannic saw the gesture and went very still. "Voss."

"The wards are paid in recollection," Odile said lightly, which was how they said things that were not light at all. "Maretta's notes were clear. The house doesn't want blood or gold. It wants to *remember more*. We give it what it wants, it gives us what we came for. Clean trade."

"You have forty-one vials on you right now. Why three?"

"Because three is what the needle says." Odile turned the compass so Brannic could see. The needle had settled now, pointing at them—pointing at Odile's own coat—with the flat insistence of a thing stating a fact.

Brannic swore in three languages, two of them dead. Then he looked past Odile's shoulder at Pip, who stood at the gate with their small hands folded and their face tilted up toward the manor's tallest window, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear. Pip could not speak. Pip had never spoken. But Pip touched memories the way other people touched silk, judging thread-count, finding flaws—and whatever Pip heard in that window made them nod once, slowly, and look back at Odile with eyes that were, for once, afraid.

"They say it's fair," Odile translated quietly.

"They say it's *fair*," Brannic repeated. "Wonderful. A haunted house with honest rates." He spat into the hedge. "Fine. But if you come out of there missing your name, Voss, I'm keeping the coal cart."

The gate let them through without a sound.

* * *

Inside, the House That Remembers was worse than the rumors, because the rumors at least agreed with each other.

Corridors reorganized themselves ahead of their footsteps—not hostilely, but the way water makes room for a stone. Wallpapers bloomed and faded: roses, then wheat, then a pattern of blue-and-gold that made something behind Odile's sternum ache before they could name why. Doors opened onto rooms furnished in decades that hadn't happened yet or hadn't happened anywhere. In one, a table was laid for a dinner party whose chairs still held the shapes of guests long dissolved. In another, snow fell upward past a window that looked out on a harbor Odile had never seen but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face in a dream you've been having for years.

Pip walked close enough to touch Odile's elbow. Their fingers tapped twice against the coat lining—their private shorthand, learned across years of silence. *Slow down. It's testing us.*

"I know," Odile murmured.

It *was* testing them. Every corridor ended in a choice, and every choice was wrong until it wasn't; the house rearranged itself around hesitation like a cat circling a mouse that might yet prove interesting. Twice Odile caught themselves standing in front of a door they had no reason to open, hand already raised, some borrowed longing humming in their blood—*come in, come see, remember with me*—and both times it was Pip's grip on their sleeve that cut the thread.

At the foot of the main stair, the house stopped playing.

The hall opened up vast and candlelit, and the air itself grew thick, syrupy with accumulated years. Every surface gleamed with the patina of ten thousand afternoons. And at the center of it all, on a pedestal of black glass, hovered the pearl.

It was smaller than Odile had imagined. A sphere the size of a plum, opalescent, shot through with veins of color that moved like weather seen from very high up—there, a coastline; there, a mountain range wearing snow like a crown; there, tiny and impossibly detailed, a city with crooked streets and a thousand chimneys breathing smoke. The oldest memory in the city. A country entire, kept alive in one captive grain of light.

The compass in Odile's hand shattered. It simply could not hold a reading that large.

"Well," Odile breathed. "Hello, gorgeous."

They crossed the hall at an ordinary walk, because running would have been an insult, and they both knew it. Up close, the pearl hummed against their trade-sense like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Odile had stolen crowns, confessions, the last words of a drowned king, a widow's grief measured out in exact spoonfuls. They knew precious. This was something else. This was a thing that had been *kept*, faithfully, expensively, by a house that had bent its whole architecture around the keeping.

The pedestal bore an inscription, worn nearly smooth:

*PAID IN FULL. THE INTEREST ACCRUES.*

"Sentimental architecture," Odile muttered, and drew out the three vials.

They had chosen them that afternoon, alone, by candlelight, with Maretta's warning ringing in their ears—*the house will take what it likes if you don't choose first, Voss, it always takes more than it quotes*—and so Odile had selected carefully, deliberately, from among their stored inventory: the taste of a specific winter apple from a market day twenty years gone; the sound of rain on a particular roof in a town they'd left at nineteen; the smell of salt and tar and a stranger's laughter on a dock at dawn, filed away because it had seemed like the beginning of something.

Three small thefts against one great one. Fair arithmetic. They pressed each vial to the black glass in turn, and the glass drank.

The apple went first—a flicker of white frost-light, gone. The rain followed, drumming once against the inside of Odile's skull like a knock at a door they'd never answer now. The third vial, the dock, the dawn, the stranger's laugh—

The pearl flared.

Color poured through the hall like dye through water. For half a heartbeat Odile saw everything: the crooked city, the crowned mountains, ships in that remembered harbor, banners of blue and gold over streets that smelled of bread and brine—

And then it was done. The pearl drifted free of the pedestal and settled into Odile's cupped hands, warm, weightless, obedient. The house exhaled around them. Somewhere above, a clock they hadn't noticed began to chime.

"That's it?" Brannic's voice crackled in their ear, thin through the whisper-wire. "That's the whole price? Three trinkets?"

"That's the whole price," Odile said, and found, oddly, that they were smiling, and could not quite have said why. Something in their chest felt lighter. Airier. Like a room after furniture has been carried out.

Pip took their arm. Tapped twice. *Go. Now.*

* * *

The trouble started at the garden wall, because trouble has a sense of timing that borders on professional.

Odile was over the wall first, pearl secured against their heart in a sling of warded silk, when the night went wrong. Lanterns bloomed in the street beyond the hedge—six of them, then eight, moving in the disciplined paired formation that Odile had hoped never to learn to recognize. White cloaks. Grey masks. The sigil of the Order of the Veil catching lantern-light like a closed eye.

"Vane's people," Brannic hissed from atop the wall. "How—"

"They didn't follow us here," Odile said, mind racing cold and quick. "They were *waiting*. Someone sold the street." There was no time to wonder who. "Pip—"

But Pip was already moving.

Later, Odile would replay it a hundred times and understand it completely and still feel it like a wound: the apprentice slipping over the wall in the opposite direction, toward the lanterns, small and silent as spilled ink. Not fleeing. *Drawing.* They snatched a loose cobblestone from the border-bed and hurled it through the nearest window of the neighboring townhouse, and the crash of it rang through Corvane Street like a starting pistol, and eight white cloaks turned as one toward the noise, and Pip ran, leading them, a hare dragging hounds away from the den.

"Pip!" Brannic lunged for the wall. Odile caught his collar and hauled him back with a strength neither of them knew they had.

"No. No, look—" Through the hedge slats, glimpsed sideways: Pip going over a garden fence, swift and low, the lanterns streaming after. Pip had escaped worse. Pip had escaped *Vane* worse, once, in a chapter of their history they'd never told. "They know these streets better than the Veil does. We go to the drop point and we wait, and we do not make it worse."

"We *wait*—"

"*We wait.*" Odile's voice came out harder than intended, edged with something new and metallic. "Every second we argue, they get further from help and closer to a wall. Move."

They moved. Through drainage cuts and over drying-lines, the pearl a small sun against Odile's ribs, the whisper-wire carrying nothing but breath. At the second canal bridge Odile heard it—distant, brief, unmistakable. A whistle-crack of leather through air, the sound a lash makes when it finds what it's looking for, followed by a silence that was somehow louder than a scream.

Brannic made a sound like a blade being drawn halfway and sheathed.

Then, fainter, retreating: footsteps. Running. Unhurried-hurried, the rhythm of someone who knew exactly which rooftops sloped the right way.

Alive. Probably. Odile clung to *probably* the way a drowning sailor clings to anything.

* * *

The safehouse was a shuttered dyer's shop three districts east, and by the time they reached it, dawn was considering its options. Maretta met them at the door with a lamp and a face like a closed ledger, and her eyes went straight to Odile's chest, to the shape beneath the coat.

"You have it."

"We have it."

"And Pip?"

"Coming. On their own route." Odile stepped inside, and the warmth of the room hit them like a foreign country. Sable rose from the corner by the stove.

Sable had been waiting. That was obvious immediately—the fire built high, tea made and remade, the dragon-keeper's heavy travel cloak still on despite the heat, ash from the fireline ground into the seams of their gloves. They crossed the room in four strides and took Odile's face in both hands, thumbs brushing cheekbones, searching, and the relief in their eyes was so naked it hurt to stand in front of.

"You're bleeding," Sable said.

"I'm not."

"Your shoulder."

Odile looked. A thin line of red soaked through the coat seam where they'd gone through the hedge thorns without noticing. "Hedge," they said. "Cosmetic."

Sable laughed—cracked, unsteady, the laugh of someone who had spent the night imagining the worst—and pulled them close, and Odile stood in the embrace and felt...

Nothing. Not revulsion. Not discomfort. Just a polite, attentive blankness, like being hugged by a well-made coat. Sable's heartbeat against their ear meant nothing. The scar on Sable's knuckle, the one Odile knew the origin of—or had known, surely, at some point—was just a mark on a stranger's hand.

They stepped back. Smoothly. Smiling. "We should wait for Pip."

Something flickered across Sable's face—small, quickly banked, the way a hearth-flame ducks when a door opens. "...Of course," they said, and turned to pour the tea that had gone cold twice already, and Maretta watched the whole exchange over the rim of her lamp with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

* * *

Pip came home an hour past sunrise, walking upright through the front door as if returning from a stroll, which was Pip's way of saying *I am fine* whether or not it was true.

It was not entirely true. The lash had caught them once across the shoulders before they'd lost their pursuers in the tannery district—three parallel welts rising through torn cloth, ugly but clean, the kind of wound that healed with pride attached. Maretta fussed. Brannic swore oaths of revenge so creative that Maretta wrote two of them down. Pip endured it all with their usual grave patience and then crossed the room to where Odile sat cleaning the pearl's silk sling, and tapped their shoulder twice, and pointed at the wound, and pointed at Odile, and tapped twice again.

*I did it for you.*

"I know," Odile said. "I'll square the ledger."

Pip studied them for a long moment. Pip was very good at studying people—it was most of how they spoke—and whatever they saw in Odile's face made them go still. Slowly, they reached out and touched Odile's temple, featherlight, the way they touched vials. Reading.

Their hand dropped away. Their expression, when Odile finally looked up to meet it, was the worst thing in the whole long night: not fear, not anger. *Grief*, precise and terrible, the grief of someone who had opened a book and found pages razored out.

"What?" Odile said. "What is it?"

Pip shook their head. Tapped twice on their own chest—*me, I'm fine*—then turned away and began, methodically, to bandage their own shoulders rather than meet the question.

Across the room, Sable had gone quiet mid-sentence. They were looking at Odile the way a person looks at a familiar door that has suddenly, inexplicably, been painted a different color. When Odile caught them at it, Sable smiled—warm, easy, everything in its place—and the smile fit so perfectly that it could only have been practiced.

"So," Sable said. "You have it. The oldest memory in the city." They came and sat, careful, at the far end of the bench, close enough to talk and not one inch closer, and Odile registered the distance and found it appropriate and did not ask themselves why that word, *appropriate*, should have stung. "What does the client want done with it?"

"Delivery in three nights. Payment on completion." Odile turned the silk sling over in their hands. Beneath their palms, the pearl hummed its endless unfinished note, and somewhere inside it a country breathed. "Coin minted by a kingdom that doesn't exist, delivered by a client who doesn't have a face, for a job that shouldn't be possible. Any other night I'd call that a bad bargain."

"And tonight?"

Tonight Odile's chest felt strangely hollow, three shelves emptied, and they couldn't say what had been on them. Tonight Sable sat a deliberate arm's length away and Odile felt nothing at all about it, which was somehow worse than feeling the wrong thing.

"Tonight," Odile said, "it feels less like a theft than a delivery. Like the pearl's been waiting a hundred years for someone to carry it somewhere." They looked up, and found Sable watching them with an ache they couldn't read and, apparently, no longer had. "Like it knows where it's going."

Maretta set down her lamp. "Voss. Look at me. What did you pay the house?"

"Three vials. Chosen by hand. Apple, rain, and—" Odile paused. Frowned. The list had had three items on it all night, and now, reaching for the third, they found only the smooth place where a fact should be. "And. Hm."

"And what?"

"I'll remember in the morning," Odile said lightly, and smiled, and the smile fit so perfectly that it could only have been practiced, and across the room Pip bowed their head over their bandages like a mourner at a graveside for a person who was still, as far as anyone could prove, alive.

Outside, the bells of the city counted seven, and none of them rang for the country that used to be.
