## 10. Strangers Who Love Each Other

The morning after the theft, Odile Voss woke in the safehouse loft with the particular lightness of a person who has spent something and cannot remember what.

It was a familiar sensation, in its way. Every thief knew it—the hollow where the lifted thing used to sit, the phantom weight of a purse no longer at the hip. But those hollows closed over. This one did not. It sat beneath Odile's ribs like a room with the furniture gone, swept clean, waiting for an occupant who had been told to expect someone and could not recall who.

Downstairs, the smell of frying onions and burnt chicory. Brannic arguing with the stove. Pip sitting cross-legged on the counter, peeling an apple in one unbroken spiral, which was Pip's way of saying *good morning* without saying anything at all. And Sable at the table, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred from old fire-spatter, looking up when Odile came through the door with an expression that made the empty room under Odile's ribs ache in a way they had no vocabulary for.

"You're up," Sable said. "I kept some of the eggs back. You like them—"

"Thank you." Odile took the plate with a small, correct smile. The kind of smile one gave a tradesman. A neighbor. "That's very thoughtful."

Sable went still. Not dramatically—a stillness of the hands first, then the shoulders, then the eyes, as though a cold draft had moved through them one vertebra at a time.

Odile ate the eggs. They were good eggs. They tasted like nothing in particular, because everything had tasted like nothing in particular since the manor, since the wards had drunk their payment out of Odile's skull three swallows at a time. Three memories. Maretta would want to know which three. Odile had checked the ledger of their own head the way one checks a strongbox after a burglary, and found the inventory intact except for gaps so smooth-edged they might have been born there. A childhood afternoon, gone. A name from the early years of the Thorn, gone. And a third gap, shaped like nothing, meaning nothing, that Odile could not even find the edges of.

"Odile." Sable's voice was careful. "Last night. When you came over the wall with the pearl—you looked at me. On the roof. You looked at me like—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Are you hurt?"

"No." Polite. Correct. "Tired. We should inventory the pearl before the wards re-settle around it. I'll be in the counting room."

Odile washed their own plate. That was the thing Sable would turn over for hours afterward, turning it like a coin of doubtful mint: Odile had always left their plate. Always. Some small domestic rebellion, some private joke against order, and now the plate was washed and set to dry and Odile was already halfway to the stairs, and Sable sat at the table holding a love with no address to send it to.

* * *

Sable walked home along the fireline as the sun came up over the ruined capital, and tried to remember what they had done wrong.

That was the shape the morning took, walking the ash-roads above Emberling's cavern with the dragon's failing warmth seeping up through the cracked flagstones like heat from a banked hearth: not *what happened*, but *what did I do*. Lovers quarreled. Sable knew about quarrels; they had grown up among keepers, a whole order of people married to their duty, and they had watched enough marriages crack and mend to know the sound of a quarrel. This was not that. This was worse. This was Odile looking at Sable the way one looks at a stranger who has been introduced twice and whose name keeps sliding away.

*You looked at me like you'd never seen me before,* Sable wanted to say. *And then you thanked me for eggs.*

They descended into the cavern. Emberling lay in the great ash-bowl of her sleeping place, wings folded like a cathedral fallen in on itself, and her fire was lower this morning. Sable felt it before they saw it—the way you feel a cold room before you see the dead hearth. A century ago her flame had lit the underside of the world. Now it guttered in her throat like a candle in a doorway, and every day there was less of it, and every day Sable fed her the only thing that helped, which was company.

"I'm here," Sable said, settling against her foreleg, palm flat on scales gone dull as old coins.

Emberling opened one eye. In the mind-link—that narrow bridge of warmth and image she allowed her keeper—she sent a question that was not words but was unmistakably a question: *the one who smells of ledgers?*

"They were here last night," Sable said. "Briefly."

*Again,* Emberling sent, and the sending carried something almost like reproach. *You let them go again.*

"There was a job. There's always a job." Sable laughed, and it came out wrong. "Except—" They pressed their forehead to the warm scale. "Something's wrong, Em. Something changed and I don't know what. They look at me like I'm a door they've forgotten how to open."

Emberling considered this with the long patience of a creature who had watched empires be founded and forgotten. Then she sent an image, unbidden: the night market, months ago, lantern-light on wet stone, and two figures sharing roasted chestnuts while a pickpocket worked Sable's coat pocket and Odile caught the pickpocket's wrist without ever breaking eye contact with Sable—and both of them laughing, later, splitting the chestnuts three ways because even the thief had seemed lonely.

Sable flinched from the memory the way one flinches from a burned hand.

"That's what's missing," they whispered. "Not the memory itself. The—the weight of it. She looked at me last night and there was no weight. Like all of that was never—" Their voice failed. Above them, far off, the Order's bells rang the hour, and somewhere in the ringing Sable heard Inquisitor Vane's voice from three days past, mild as milk: *Love is a debt, keeper. All debts are paid or burned.* 

Sable stood. Duty said: report the answered prayers, report the pearl if you learn of it, burn what must be burned. Love said: Odile is wrong inside, and I will find out why if I have to read every ledger in that crooked guild myself.

For once—for the first time in a life built of duty—love won without a fight.

* * *

Maretta threw a ledger at Odile's head.

To be fair, it was a slim one, and she threw it underhand, and it hit the counting-room wall beside Odile's ear rather than the ear itself, which by Maretta's standards constituted restraint.

"Three," she said. "You sold *three*."

"Maretta—"

"Don't you *Maretta* me in that reasonable voice. I taught you that voice. I know exactly how much reason is in it." She advanced on the counting room like weather. "The wards at the remembering house take payment in self. Everyone knows that. What everyone also knows, what is written in the first ledger, in the founder's hand, in letters we are apparently all too stupid to read anymore, is that you *itemize before you pay*, Odile. You do not hand a strange house your soul and say *help yourself*."

"It was fast. Pip was bleeding. The wards were closing—"

"Pip would have bled for a week rather than let you do this. Pip would have *died*—" Maretta stopped herself, breathed, pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose the way she did when the numbers refused to balance. "Which three."

"I don't know."

"You don't—" Her voice dropped, which was infinitely more frightening than the throwing. "Say that again slowly."

"The gaps are clean. Smooth. Whatever I paid, the house took the receipt too." Odile turned the memory-pearl over on the desk between them, its depths swirling with the light of a country that no longer existed, and hated how steady their own hands were. "There's a hole shaped like nothing. I can't even find its edges, Mare. How do I miss something I can't remember missing?"

Maretta looked at Odile for a long moment. Then she crossed the room, took Odile's face in both hands—fence-of-feelings, reader of hearts, the woman who could price a stranger's grief at thirty paces—and looked *into* them, the way she looked into everything, hunting the tell, the seam, the false bottom.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you absolute fool. It's Sable, isn't it. Something with Sable."

"What?" The word came out flat. "What about Sable?"

"That's just it, isn't it." Maretta released them and stepped back, and her eyes were wet with a fury that had nowhere righteous to go. "You don't know. You look at that dragon-keeper—who has been half in love with you since the night market, who has been risking the Veil's rope for you since spring—and you feel *nothing*, and you think that's just how things are. You think the politeness is maturity. You think the distance is sense." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "You sold your own heart a piece at a time to get through a door, Odile, and now you're standing in the room wondering why it's cold."

Odile stood very still. The empty place under their ribs ached, that room-with-no-furniture ache, and for the first time it had a direction—it pointed, the way a compass points, toward the fireline, toward scarred forearms and careful eyes and eggs kept warm.

"How do I get it back?" Odile asked. Quietly now. Honestly.

"You don't." Maretta picked up the thrown ledger, smoothed its bent cover with a tenderness she offered to nothing else in the world. "Memories sold to a ward are spent, same as coin. But—" she tapped the pearl, "—that thing holds a kingdom entire. Every hearth, every wedding, every grudge. If a country can be remembered, maybe a piece of one person can be found in it. Maybe. Don't hope yet. Hope's expensive and we're overdrawn."

She was at the door when the knock came—three quick raps, the courier's rhythm, and then a fourth that was wrong, dragging, a rhythm learned in a hurry from someone else's mouth.

Brannic got the door. Beyond him, on the step, stood a boy of perhaps fifteen wearing a Thorn courier's sash, gray-faced, lips split, one eye swollen shut—and behind his shoulder, filling the street, men in the white-and-ash of the Order of the Veil, and at their center a tall figure whose voice Odile had heard described often enough to know it on arrival, mild as milk and cold as the far side of a candle-flame.

"Forgive the intrusion," said Inquisitor Vane. "Your boy was lost. We're returning him." He smiled at the courier with genuine, terrible kindness. "Tell them what you told me."

The boy's ruined mouth moved. "*They have the pearl.*"

Vane's gaze traveled past Brannic, past Maretta, past the counting room and its open ledgers, and settled—Odile would have sworn it settled physically, a weight like a hand—on the memory-pearl gleaming on the desk.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Then we're all finally being honest with one another."
