## 14. Maretta's Price

The pyre-house smelled of tallow and scorched feathers, and Maretta Vos decided, in the first hour of her captivity, that if she was going to die in a room, she would at least redecorate it with her contempt.

They had taken her boots. They had taken the little bone charms sewn into her sleeves—fence-of-feelings though she was, she'd never trusted her trade to anything that couldn't be burned or buried—and they had left her sitting on a chair too small for her hips, in a circle of white ash that Inquisitor Vane apparently believed meant something to her. It did not. Ash meant nothing. Ash was what happened when people like Vane finished talking.

He came in at dusk, carrying two cups of tea.

"You'll notice," he said, setting one within her reach, "that it is good tea. The Order does not stint on comfort for those who cooperate."

"I notice," Maretta said, "that you brought two cups, which means either you're drinking with me or you're very optimistic about how long this takes."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the first honest expression she'd seen on him. He sat across from her, folding himself down onto a stool with the patience of a man who had all the time the world's grief could buy him.

"Tell me where the pearl is kept," he said, "and you walk out of here. Not pardoned—I don't have that power—but *alive*. Free. I give you my word on the Nine Veils."

"The Nine Veils." She let her voice go flat as slate. "You swear oaths on veils. Things whose entire purpose is to hide the truth. Do you hear yourselves?"

"Maretta Voss." He said her name carefully, tasting it. "Fence of feelings. Thirty-one years with the Gilded Thorn. You've sold stolen joy to grieving mothers and bottled courage to deserters. You are not a woman whose outrage will move me."

"No," she agreed. "But I'm a woman whose *prices* might interest you. So let's talk trade, Inquisitor. That's my language. What are you actually offering?"

"Your life."

"Worthless. I've been dying since I was forty; everything past that is found money." She leaned forward, wrists still bound, and smiled the smile that had closed a hundred bad deals before they could open. "Try again."

His jaw tightened. Good. Anger was information; she collected it the way other people collected coins.

"Your freedom, then. Your guild's survival. I am authorized to offer the Thorn clemency if the pearl is surrendered voluntarily."

"*There* it is." She sat back, letting the chair creak. "You don't want me. You want the pearl without breaking your pretty hands prying it loose. Which tells me something wonderful: you're afraid of it. The Order that burns shrines and hangs pray-ers from bridge-posts is afraid of one small memory." She shook her head slowly. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me all day, and you didn't even say it on purpose."

Vane stood. For a moment she thought he would strike her, and she catalogued, coolly, where the pain would land and what she would do with it. But he only picked up both cups of tea—one untouched, hers untouched, the symmetry of it almost funny—and carried them to the door.

"We'll speak again tomorrow," he said.

"Bring better tea," Maretta told his back. "And bring worse lies. These are insulting."

* * *

Three miles beneath the city, in the warm dark under the fireline, Odile Voss paced a groove into the ash floor and called it planning.

"It's suicide," Sable said again, from the mouth of the tunnel. Emberling's failing glow lit them from behind, gilding the edges of their shoulders, their jaw, the stubborn set of their mouth. Odile refused to look directly at that light. It made something ache in their chest—a hollow ache, a missing-tooth ache, an ache with no story attached—and they hated it more than they could say.

"It's arithmetic," Odile said. "Vane has Maretta. Vane has maybe thirty soldiers. We have Brannic, Pip, me, and a dying dragon. If we wait, Maretta dies alone. If we go now, while he thinks fear has us pinned—"

"If you go now, you die *with* her. Three funerals instead of one."

"Two and a half," Odile said. "I'm very good at escaping places."

"You're good at stealing things." Sable came closer, and their voice dropped into that register Odile had no defense against—not because of any memory, but because of some deeper stratum, some sediment of feeling the theft hadn't reached. "Odile. Listen to me. I know keepers. I know what Vane's people can do with fire and wire and patience. Maretta held out against the first day because Maretta could sell sand to the sea. But everyone breaks. And when she breaks, Vane comes here. To the fireline. To Emberling." Their hand found Odile's wrist—not gripping, just resting, a touch light as a moth. "If we run, we abandon her. If we fight, we lose everything. There has to be another door."

"There's always another door. That's rather the family business."

"Then find it *slowly*."

Odile turned at last, and the look between them snagged on something—on the strange polite distance that had grown up between them like frost on glass, on the way Odile now knew Sable's face intimately and remembered nothing of why. They had been told. Maretta had diagnosed it in her furious, grieving way: *you sold the memory of meeting them, you absolute thief, you sold your own heart and didn't even keep the receipt.* Knowing was not the same as remembering. Knowing was reading someone else's ledger entry about your own life.

"How did we meet?" Odile asked abruptly.

Sable went still. "Maretta told you?"

"She told me there was a memory. She wouldn't tell me the shape of it. Said—" Odile's throat tightened unexpectedly. "She said it wasn't hers to give back. That it had to come from you or nowhere."

For a long moment there was only the sound of Emberling breathing, vast and labored, like bellows full of embers.

"The night market," Sable said finally. "The one on the canal, three autumns ago. I was off-duty, out of uniform, buying luck for a friend whose dragon-flight was scheduled. I bought a vial from a stall I thought was yours. It wasn't. It was cut luck—luck that had been stolen twice, thinned down until it was mostly water and wish. My friend drank it and walked into the flight pen and the dragon there—*not* mine, a stranger's beast—panicked. Fire everywhere. And there was this person in a grey coat who shouldn't have been anywhere near the pens, vaulting the fence, shouting at me in trader's cant to *give the beast something true, quickly*, and shoving a memory-vial into my hand. Your own. A real one. First-hand joy, uncut." Sable's mouth curved. "You gave away inventory worth more than my year's wages to save a man you'd never met, because a stranger looked frightened enough."

"What happened to the vial?" Odile whispered.

"I never opened it. I was going to return it. Then the market burned—arson, some turf war—and by the time I tracked you down through three fences and a forger, I'd convinced myself you'd want payment, and you'd convinced yourself you wanted nothing, and we spent two years pretending the debt ran one direction when it ran both." They laughed, low and cracked. "You were going to charge me interest, that first night. You got as far as naming a price and then you stopped, mid-word, and asked me instead whether dragons dream. Nobody had ever asked me that. Nobody had ever asked me *anything* that wasn't about duty."

Odile stood in the warm dark with a stranger's love story in their ears and felt it settle into their chest like water finding old channels—true, and yet not *theirs*, not yet, secondhand where it should have been first. It hurt in the exact place the hollow ache lived. Of course it did. The ache had a shape after all, and the shape was a night market and a thrown vial and two years of careful pretending.

"I believe you," Odile said. "I believe every word. And I hate—" Their voice failed and restarted. "I hate that it's a beautiful story about a person I can't remember being. I want to be angry at the version of me who sold it. I want to shake them. Why would I—"

"Because you were paying for the pearl," Sable said gently. "Because the wards took memories and you fed them three of your own rather than take anything from the rest of us. Because that's who you are, Odile. Who you *were*. The wards didn't change you. They just made you forget the proof."

Odile's eyes stung. They blinked it away, furiously practical, because grief was a luxury and Maretta was on a clock.

"Then help me build the third door," they said. "Not running. Not fighting. Stealing. We steal Maretta out of the pyre-house the way I stole the pearl: sideways, through the seams, wearing the enemy's own assumptions as a coat."

Sable studied them for a long moment—studied them the way keepers study flame, looking for the color that says *still burning*.

"All right," they said at last. "Show me the seams."

* * *

In the pyre-house, on the second day, Vane brought no tea.

He brought a chair for himself this time, and a small iron box, which he set on the floor between them and did not explain. Maretta appreciated theater; she simply wished it weren't so derivative. Every interrogator eventually arrived at the mysterious box.

"You slept well," he observed.

"Your straw is excellent. Five stars."

"Yesterday you told me my offers were insulting. So today I won't make one." He folded his long hands. "Today I'll tell you what happens tomorrow. At dawn, I burn a petitioner in the square. A girl of nineteen who prayed to the Door-God for rain. Whether you are alive to watch depends entirely on whether, tonight, you tell me where the Thorn keeps the pearl. Not for your sake—for hers. Every hour you withhold, the Order finds another believer. I have lists, Maretta Voss. Very long lists."

Maretta looked at him for a long, quiet moment, and inside herself she did the thing she did best in the world: she read him. Not his face—he was trained, his face was a wall. His *wants*. Underneath the zealotry, underneath the lists, there was a hunger so specific it practically had a signature: Vane needed the pearl to be somewhere *seizable*. Somewhere defensible, warded, guarded—a fortress-problem, because a fortress-problem justified his methods. He could not bear for it to be hidden somewhere trivial, somewhere that made the burning pointless.

People always told fence-of-feelings exactly what lie they needed to hear. They just never knew they were telling.

"The safehouse under the Lantern Bridge," Maretta said.

Vane's breath caught. Caught! She nearly laughed.

"You're lying," he said.

"Of course I'm lying. Everything I say is a lie; that's my profession. The question, Inquisitor, is whether this particular lie costs you more to ignore than to test." She tilted her head, all sweet reason. "Here's what I'll give you, freely, as collateral: the Lantern Bridge house is real. It exists. The Thorn used it for years. Whether we still use it—that's the part I'd have to check. Wards get moved. But you have lists, don't you? Check your own records. The house will be on them. Old seizures, old informants. The truth of the address will make the rest plausible, and you know it will, and that's why you're already deciding to believe me."

She watched him decide to believe her. It was the most obscene thing she'd ever witnessed, watching a man choose his own delusion with his whole face.

"Three days," Vane said, standing. "I'll verify quietly. If the address holds—if there's even a trace of ward-sign there—you'll have proven your cooperation, and we'll discuss terms in earnest."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then dawn comes early for the girl in the square." He paused at the door. "Why do it this way? Buy time you cannot possibly spend?"

"Time," Maretta said, "is the only currency I've ever respected. Spend it well."

When he was gone she let her head fall back against the wall and allowed herself, briefly, one shaking breath. Three days. Lantern Bridge had been empty for two years—Brannic had stripped its wards himself—but the Order's own archives would show decades of Thorn activity there, false trails the guild had salted deliberately, back when salted trails were cheap insurance. Vane would verify. Vane would find exactly enough truth to swallow the hook.

And the Thorn would have seventy-two hours to do something about a rescue that Sable called suicide.

* * *

On the third morning, Odile laid the plan out on the floor of Emberling's cavern, using buttons for soldiers, a scrap of red silk for the dragon, and Pip's carved wooden mouse for Maretta.

"Vane believes he's closing on Lantern Bridge," they said. "Which means by tonight he'll have moved half his strength there—either to watch it or to storm it. The pyre-house thins. Meanwhile, the girl he promised to burn—she's real, Sable confirmed it, a petitioner named Iselle. He intends to burn her at dawn regardless, as pressure on Maretta. But here's what he doesn't know." Odile's finger traced the button-lines. "A public burning requires a public square, crowds, the full theatrical apparatus of the Order. And crowds mean pockets. Purses. Sleeves. Doors that open for the right coin and close at the right moment. You don't break into a pyre-house, Brannic—you *buy* one, for eleven minutes."

Brannic grunted, turning a button-soldier between huge fingers. "Eleven minutes to walk a chained woman out of an armed compound."

"Nine, if Pip's work holds." Odile glanced at the apprentice, who sat cross-legged with their slate balanced on their knees, chalk moving in quick sure strokes. Pip wrote: *the guards' relief rotation is 40 min. the west gate guard owes a debt to a fence i know. i can buy his sleep for one hour.* "Pip can put a man to sleep with a touch-memory. One hour, one gate. Maretta walks out dressed as a penitent—Brannic's work; nobody questions a hooded penitent at a burning—and we're gone before the smoke clears."

"And if it fails," Sable said quietly, "we're all of us inside the walls when it fails. With Vane. Who wants Emberling's blood and knows my face."

Odile looked up at them—at the firelight on their face, at the ache-shaped hollow in their own chest that this person somehow filled without trying. Something in Odile steadied. Not memory. Choice.

"Then we make sure it doesn't fail," they said. "Together. That's the whole plan, really. The buttons are decoration."

It was Pip who broke the silence—Pip, who had not spoken in years, whose silence was older than any of them knew. The chalk flew, and then stopped, and Pip looked up at Odile with wide eyes and held up the slate:

*odile. the lantern bridge trap. vane won't just check the house.*

*he'll check the ledger.*

Odile went cold. Of course. The false trail at Lantern Bridge led to a strongroom, and the strongroom's lock answered to a ledger-line, and the ledger—the *real* ledger, the founding contract, the page that named the Thorn custodian of the erased kingdom—was still hidden in the old house's foundations, because moving it had always seemed more dangerous than leaving it.

"He doesn't want the pearl," Odile said slowly. "Or not only. If he finds that page, he doesn't need to burn the kingdom's memory—he can *prove* the Thorn's guilt. Prove a century-old conspiracy. Burn us all with paper instead of fire."

Sable rose, and the great shadow of Emberling stirred behind them, and far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the bells of the city began to ring wrong—quick, urgent, the alarm-pattern that meant soldiers moving before dawn.

"That's not the burning hour," Sable said. "That's Lantern Bridge."

Maretta's three days were already spent.
