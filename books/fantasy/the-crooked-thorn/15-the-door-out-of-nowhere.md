## 15. The Door Out of Nowhere

The fire was going out.

Sable had known it the way you know a fever coming on—before any symptom shows itself, in some older room of the body where weather is felt first. For eleven years they had tended Emberling's flame through every season of its long decline: the bright years when it roared and lit the caverns beneath the ruined capital like a second dawn; the middle years when it burned steady but low, a hearth-fire rather than a sun; and now this, the last season, when the great dragon lay coiled in the dark like a banked oven and her breath came out not as flame at all but as heat-shimmer, as the ghost of flame, as light remembered instead of light made.

Sable knelt at the edge of the ash-circle with their palm flat against the warm stone and counted her heartbeats. That was the oldest trick of the keeper's craft, older than the Order, older than the vows: you learned your dragon's pulse through the rock, because a dragon's heart was too vast and too slow to hear with ears. Emberling's beat came once for every four of Sable's own. When Sable had been sworn in, it had been one to two. The arithmetic of dying was patient. It did not hurry, and it could not be argued with.

"You're up early," Sable said softly. "Or late. I never can tell down here."

Emberling's eye opened—a slow unfolding, like a door in a house that had been shut for a hundred years. It was the color of old gold left too long in water, and it found Sable in the dark with terrible gentleness.

*Little keeper,* said the voice inside Sable's skull, which was not a voice at all but a warmth behind the eyes, a pressure like a hand resting on the crown of the head. *You lie badly. You have been awake since the bell above rang third watch. You were thinking about the thief.*

"I was thinking about inventory," Sable lied, and felt the dragon's amusement move through the link like a current through still water. "And about the thief."

*The one who smells of ink and stolen things.* Emberling's nostrils flared, though there was nothing in the cavern to smell but ash and cold mineral and the faint tallow-stink of Sable's lamp. *You love her.*

"Him," Sable said, out of habit, because Odile Voss wore gender the way they wore coats—changed according to the weather, discarded when inconvenient, always slightly borrowed. "Sometimes. Today, I think, him. He was wearing the grey coat yesterday."

*Him,* Emberling agreed placidly, having never once cared. *You love him, and you have not told the Order, and you have not told him what the Order has asked of you, and now your heart is doing that thing where it runs ahead of your mouth like a child running downhill.*

Sable looked at their hands. Keeper's hands, scarred at the knuckles from raking clinker, stained permanent umber at the fingertips from handling fire-salts. Hands that had been taught, from the first day of novitiate, that everything they touched belonged to the flame and nothing—not comfort, not company, not the specific weight of another person falling asleep against your shoulder in a night market while the lanterns swayed—belonged to them.

"The Order asked me to find whoever is waking the old gods," Sable said. "I told them I was looking. I didn't tell them I'd found them. That's not the same as lying either."

*No,* Emberling said. *It is the same as standing in a doorway during a storm and calling yourself dry.*

Sable laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound that echoed off the vaulted dark. The caverns beneath the capital had been dug by hands that expected to be remembered; the pillars were carved with kings whose names no living tongue could produce, their faces worn smooth as river stones by a century of nobody looking at them. Sable had grown up among those faceless kings. They had never seemed sad before. Lately they seemed like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been cancelled a hundred years ago.

Emberling shifted. The movement took a long time—the way a mountain would take a long time to shrug—and when she had rearranged herself, her head lay close enough that her breath stirred Sable's hair. It smelled of hot stone and, underneath, faintly and terribly, of nothing. Of absence. Of a hearth gone cold in an empty house.

*I want to show you something,* she said. *Before I am too tired to show it properly. Come into the dream with me, little keeper. There is a door, and I have finally remembered where I put it.*

Sable had shared the mind-link with Emberling since their swearing, but they had never gone deep—never past the shallows where images passed like coins across a counter. What the dragon offered now was not coins. It was the whole purse, upended.

"Will it hurt you?"

*Everything hurts me now,* Emberling said, with the mildness of the very old discussing weather. *This will hurt differently. Come.*

Sable set down the lamp, folded their hands in the keeper's posture—open palms, open self—and stepped off the edge of the world.

* * *

They fell through fire that was not burning.

That was the first truth of it: Emberling's dreamscape was made of flame the way other minds were made of memory, and none of it scorched. Sable stood—or floated, or simply was—in a country under a green-gold sky, on a shore where the water came in silver and went out again without ever quite touching the sand. Hills rolled inland, terraced and planted, orchards heavy with fruit that glowed faintly from within. A city rose along the river, white-walled, red-roofed, its spires hung with bells. Smoke rose from chimneys. People moved in the streets below, small as stitches in embroidery, and somewhere a bell was ringing the hour, and the hour had a name.

Sable knew, in the way one knows things in dreams, that none of this existed. Not anymore. Not anywhere. This was Emberling's memory of the kingdom that had been unwritten, and the dragon had carried it alone for a hundred years, turning it over nightly like a miser counting a currency no one else accepted.

*Here,* Emberling said, and the dream tilted, and Sable was suddenly standing on a hillside overlooking the white city, beside a border-stone half-swallowed by gorse. *Look at the stone. Look at what I did.*

The memory sharpened. Sable saw the city—but saw it wrong, saw it the way you see a painting from an angle where the perspective breaks. Behind the white walls there was nothing. Not darkness, not distance: nothing, a flat and absolute absence, like the margin beyond the edge of a map. And the border-stone stood at the seam between the country and the nothing, and around the base of the stone the ground was scorched in a ring, and the scorch-mark—

Sable's breath caught, there in a place where they didn't technically have breath.

The scorch-mark was shaped like a door.

*I burned it,* Emberling said quietly. *On the last day. When they came to tell me my country had been sold—that the rulers had traded the whole of it away to buy the gods' silence, that the deed was signed and the ink dry and the people unwritten—I flew to the border and I breathed my fire on the stone, because I thought—I was young, little keeper, I was arrogant, I thought I could burn the contract. Burn the debt. Keep what was ours.*

She let the silence stand. In the dream, the silver water came in and went out, faithful to a shore that no longer was.

*Fire cannot burn a contract. But fire remembers what it has touched. My flame fell on the border between what is and what was, and it has been smoldering there ever since, all these years, banked down inside me like a coal wrapped in ash. That is why my fire gutters, do you understand? A flame must burn *something*. Mine has been trying, all this century, to burn a hole in the wall of the world—and the wall is thick, and I am only one dragon, and I am tired.*

"Show me," Sable whispered. "Show me the rest."

The dream turned, and Sable saw it: the door in the air, hanging above the border-stone, outlined in ember-light. Through its frame—not glass, not surface, but a shimmering, a hesitation, the way heat wavers above a road in summer—you could see the green-gold sky. The orchards. The red roofs. The kingdom entire, waiting on the far side of its own erasure like a letter that was never delivered but also never returned.

*A door out of nowhere,* Emberling said. *Out of the country that no longer exists. Your thief's client wants the pearl so the kingdom can be restored—but restoration is a slow miracle, and miracles are rationed. The door is faster. The door is *now*. Anyone who walks through it walks out of the erasure. Walks into what was, alive, breathing, real.*

Hope rose in Sable like water rising in a lock, and with it, immediately, the keeper's training raised its hand like a schoolchild: *and the cost.* There was always a cost. Everything in the Order's oldest books said so, in margins written by dead hands.

"What does it cost to light it?"

Emberling was quiet for a long moment. In the dream, the bell in the white city rang an hour that had no name anymore, and the sound of it was the loneliest thing Sable had ever heard.

*A keeper's vow,* she said at last. *The first vow. The one they stopped teaching after the erasure, because there was nothing left to keep. You know the words. Every keeper learns them and prays never to need them. The vow binds the keeper's life to the flame and pours the life through it. The fire takes the vow and makes of it a key. And the keeper—*

"I know what happens to the keeper." Sable's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Ash. The keeper becomes ash. It's in the liturgy. *And the servant of the flame shall be gathered into the flame, and be no more, and be all.*"

*Yes.*

"And how much life does it take? All of it?"

*All of it,* Emberling said gently. *A partial vow lights a lamp. Only a whole life opens a door.*

The dream held them both there, on the hillside, in the green-gold light of a place that had been erased so thoroughly that even grief for it felt contraband. Sable thought about the arithmetic of it, because arithmetic was what they were good at, arithmetic was safe: one keeper's life, weighed against a door. Weighed against Odile walking through it, and Brannic, and Maretta, and Pip, and every soul the Thorn had sheltered, out of the country that had swallowed them invisible. Weighed against Emberling finally being able to stop burning herself hollow against a wall.

It was, Sable thought, the fairest trade they had ever been offered. One life, already sworn to the flame anyway, spent on the only purchase that mattered.

That was the horror of it. Not that the price was high. That it was *reasonable*.

*You are deciding,* Emberling observed. *I can feel you deciding. Stop it.*

"Somebody has to decide."

*Not tonight. Tonight you are going to climb out of my dream, and you are going to go home, and you are going to sleep, and you are going to see your thief, and you are going to tell him—*

"Tell him what?" Sable heard their own voice crack along a fault line they hadn't known was there. "That I've found the way out and the toll is me? He steals memories for a living, Emberling. He knows exactly what a life weighs. He'd never forgive me. Worse—he'd try to find a way around it, some thief's trick, and get himself killed, or worse, get *Pip*—" Sable stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes, here in a place where eyes were only a courtesy. "There's no version of this where I tell him and he lets me do it. And there's no version where I don't do it and everyone I love stays buried in a country that doesn't exist."

*Ah,* said Emberling, very softly. *So it is not duty after all. You keep telling the Order it is duty. But look at what you are actually spending your life on. Not the flame. Not the kingdom. Him. Them. The ragged little family of thieves.* The dragon's warmth moved through the link like a hand smoothing hair. *Duty would have been easier, little keeper. Duty doesn't make you cry.*

"I'm not crying. Dragons don't have tear ducts and neither do I in here."

*Of course not.*

* * *

Sable woke on the ash-circle floor with the lamp burnt to a coin of cold wax and dawn-grey light filtering down the ventilation shafts, and did not remember climbing out of the dream. Emberling slept, or seemed to—her breathing slow as tide, her gold eye shuttered—and Sable sat for a while with their back against a pillar carved with a king whose name was lost, and let the ordinary world reassemble itself around them: the chill, the drip of seep-water, the far-off bell of the Order's morning call.

Then they got up, and took a fresh sheet of the Order's grey paper, and began to prepare.

It had to be done properly. That was the one mercy the liturgy allowed: the vow could not be spoken in haste, could not be stumbled through with an unready heart, or the fire would refuse it and the door would stay shut and the life would be wasted, spent on nothing. So there were preparations. Fasting, first—three days, beginning tomorrow. Then the writing of the testament, the keeper's last ledger-entry, accounting for everything owed and everything forgiven. Then the vigil, one full night, palms open, in the keeper's posture, until the flame itself gave the sign of acceptance.

Sable had watched old Keeper Vashti perform the first two stages once, as a child, before the Order realized what she intended and stopped her by force. Sable had never forgotten the calm of her. She had written her testament the way other people wrote grocery lists.

Sable wrote theirs by lamplight in the keepers' cell, in careful script, and it took less time than expected, because there was so little to account for. No property worth naming. No debts. One secret kept from the Order—one thief, loved, unreported—which Sable wrote down plainly, because a testament was no place for cowardice, and if the Order read it afterward, let them. Let them choke on it.

At the bottom, after the formal lines, Sable hesitated, then added:

*To O.V.: You will be angry. Be angry. Anger means you're alive, and alive is the whole point of the door. Go through it. Take them all with you. Plant something on the other side. I'm sorry I won't see the color of it.—S.*

They read it twice. It was insufficient. It was the most honest thing they had ever written. They folded it, sealed it with the keeper's mark, and addressed it to the Gilded Thorn, to be delivered only upon confirmation of the keeper's death—an instruction the fireline clerks would follow without question, because clerks always did.

Three days of fasting, then the vigil, then the vow. Four days, perhaps five, before the door.

Which meant four days, perhaps five, of Odile. Of the night market and the swaying lanterns and the specific weight of his head against their shoulder. Of pretending, one last time, that they were two people with a future instead of one person with a deadline.

Sable sealed the letter, blew out the lamp, and went to find him—already rehearsing the smile that would hide the arithmetic, already grieving in advance, already, traitorously, hoping he would guess.

He always guessed. That was the trouble with loving a thief.

They never noticed what you hid until it was already gone.
