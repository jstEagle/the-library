## 16. Rescue at the Pyre-House

The pyre-house had once been a granary, back when the city still bothered to feed itself. Now it stood at the edge of the Ashmarket like a blackened tooth, its rafters hung with iron cages instead of grain-sacks, its floors stained dark where the Order of the Veil conducted the business it called purification. Smoke seeped from its eaves even in daylight—not woodsmoke, but something sweeter and worse, the smell of burned prayers.

Odile crouched in the shadow of a collapsed tannery across the square and counted windows for the fourth time. Eleven lit. Three guarded. One, on the topmost floor behind bars of gilded iron, flickering with candlelight that moved too steadily to be anything but an interrogation lamp.

"She's up there," Odile murmured. "Third floor. The cage with the pretty bars."

Beside them, Brannic flexed his enormous hands around the hilt of a fire-axe he'd stolen from the Order's own armory—a detail he'd insisted on, as though there were poetry in burning Vane's house with Vane's tools. His face, usually so carefully blank beneath the forger's stillness, was working now. Three days they'd spent preparing this, three days of Sable arguing it was suicide and Odile refusing to hear it, and Brannic had said almost nothing the whole time except: *She held the door for us. We hold it for her.*

Pip crouched on Odile's other side, small and silent as ever, wearing a courier's coat two sizes too big. In one hand they carried a satchel Odile had packed with the care of a jeweler: six vials, each stoppered with wax, each holding a memory that belonged—by every law of the trade—to someone else.

"You're certain about these?" Brannic asked quietly.

"No." Odile turned one vial in their fingers, watching the light swim inside it like a fish under ice. Inside was a kindness: a guard named Dossel, years ago, carrying a drunk stranger home through the snow rather than letting him freeze. Dossel had sold that memory to pay a debt and had never known what he'd given up, only that some rooms in himself felt cold. "That's the whole point of a gamble, Brannic. If I were certain, it wouldn't cost anything."

"And if it costs too much?"

Odile thought of Maretta's voice through the wall of the safehouse, telling them to run while she bought time. Thought of her laugh, which she deployed like a weapon and meant like a prayer. Thought of the ledger line where her name sat beside debts unpaid and debts forgiven, and how the second column had always been longer.

"Then it costs," Odile said. "We've paid worse for less."

They crossed the square at the change of the watch, moving through the dead ground behind a cart of tallow that Brannic had arranged—or forged the manifest for; with Brannic the distinction was academic. Pip slipped ahead, a shadow among shadows, and did something to the lock of the coal-chute with a bent nail and infinite patience. The chute opened onto darkness that smelled of old fire.

Inside, the pyre-house breathed. That was the wrong word and Odile knew it, but there was no better one for the way the building seemed to inhale around them, rafters groaning, cages swaying on their chains like slow pendulums counting down to something. Most were empty tonight—the Order had emptied them after Maretta's capture, sensing, perhaps, that mercy was not coming and neither was silence. But the stairwells were thick with gray-cloaked interrogators, men and women with ash-rubbed eyes who had learned to stop flinching at screaming.

Odile stopped on the second-floor landing, pressed against stone, and listened to two of them below, talking in the flat voices of the very tired.

"—the woman again tonight?"

"After midnight. Vane wants the location before the third bell."

"He'll have it by the second."

A pause. Then, lower, almost ashamed: "She told him the pearl was buried under the fountain in Vellum Square. He believed her. I don't know why he believes her. She just *laughs* at him."

Something loosened in Odile's chest—fear and pride in equal measure, tangled past separation. *She fed him a false site.* Three days bought. And here was the proof of it: even Vane's own dogs couldn't understand why their master trusted a laughing woman in chains.

*Because you can't burn a memory out of someone who keeps making new ones faster than you take them,* Odile thought. *She is out-stocking you, you fools. She has been doing it all week.*

Pip touched Odile's sleeve and pointed. Two guards on the third-floor landing, backs half-turned. Brannic hefted the axe, and Odile shook their head.

"Not yet. Not first." They drew the first vial from the satchel. "Kindness first. It's how we do everything else."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had come to Odile at four in the morning three days ago, sitting over Maretta's ledgers and not reading them. Every interrogation Odile had ever studied—and the Thorn kept records of everything, including the Order's methods, purchased at obscene cost from a frightened clerk—ran on the same engine: the interrogators had to believe they were instruments, not people. Vane's whole machine depended on men and women who had sawed off the parts of themselves that would object.

But you cannot saw off a memory without leaving the stump. Odile knew that better than anyone alive. Somewhere under the ash in each of those gray cloaks was a person who had once done something decent and had it taken away or traded away or simply buried under the work. And a kindness, returned, was not a gift. It was a debt collector.

So: no assault at the gates. No blood on the stairs, if the gods—who owed the Thorn more than the Thorn owed them, whatever Maretta's ledgers said—were willing to be generous.

Odile stepped out of the shadow of the landing before the guards could turn fully, hands open and empty.

"Don't shout," Odile said pleasantly. "Shouting brings the whole house, and you'll have to explain why you let a stranger get this far. Whereas if you stay quiet—" they uncorked the vial, and the memory rose out of it like heat-shimmer, silver and soft, "—you get to remember something you lost."

The nearer guard—a young man with a burn scar along his jaw—did exactly the wrong thing, which was to breathe in. The memory poured into him like water into cracked ground.

Odile watched it land. Watched the scarred jaw go slack. Watched the young man's eyes lose their ash-glaze and fill, all at once, with snow—Dossel's snow, the drunk stranger's weight on his borrowed shoulders, the absurd tenderness of carrying someone home because the alternative was unthinkable. The guard made a small sound. It might have been a sob. It might have been laughter.

"I carried him," he whispered. "I don't—I carried *someone*. Through the snow. Why do I remember—"

"Because you're the sort of person who carries people," Odile said. "Someone took it from you. I'm giving it back. Now, here's the trade, and it's a fair one: the door to the cells is behind you, the keys are on your belt, and for the next hundred breaths you are going to find that you cannot bring yourself to raise an alarm against someone who reminded you what you are. Go stand somewhere and feel things. It's allowed. It used to be your whole profession's problem."

He went. He actually went—stumbling down the corridor toward the cells, keys already in his shaking hand, weeping openly at whatever other fragments the kindness dislodged behind it.

The second guard reached for a whistle. Odile was faster, and less gentle: the second vial wasn't Dossel's snow but a different memory entirely—one of Odile's own purchases, a woman remembering teaching her small brother to whistle, badly, until the birds complained. The guard inhaled it mid-motion and forgot, for one long golden moment, everything except a summer afternoon and a boy laughing at his terrible birdcalls. When the moment passed, she lowered the whistle as though it had become, in her hand, an obscenity.

"Go home," Odile told her softly. "Tonight. Whatever he's paying you isn't enough for what this house takes."

* * *

It worked eleven times. Odile would count them forever after, the way misers count coins: eleven interrogators met on stairways and landings, in corridors that smelled of char, and eleven vials uncorked, and eleven faces breaking open like locked doors kicked inward. Some wept. Some laughed. One dropped his weapon and sat down on the stairs and refused to move again that night, humming a lullaby no one had sung to him since childhood. Two tried to fight anyway—grief and habit are stubborn things—and those two Brannic handled, gently by his standards, which left them unconscious rather than broken.

Pip moved through it all like a rumor, cutting cage-locks with the bent nail, herding dazed guards into storerooms with little pushes and pointed fingers, communicating entire tactical assessments through eyebrow alone. At the third landing, Pip paused by a cell where a boy of maybe fifteen lay shivering, arrested for petitioning a river-god for rain, and pressed a heel of bread into his hands and a finger to his own lips. The boy stared after them like he'd seen a saint, or a thief. With the Thorn, Odile supposed, the categories had always overlapped.

Then the top floor. The gilded cage.

Maretta hung from her wrists in a way that told Odile everything about the last three days, and her head came up when the lock clicked, and Odile braced themselves for the wit, the armor, the laugh.

What they got was worse. She looked at them with clear, exhausted blue eyes and said, hoarsely:

"Oh, you *idiots.*"

"Maretta—"

"I told you to run. I bought you three days. I had it *scheduled*, Odile, there was a whole—"

"There was a whole plan," Odile agreed, catching her as the manacles released and her legs failed, lowering her down with Brannic's help onto a blanket Pip produced from nowhere. "You're looking at it. How bad?"

"Ribs. Fingers. Nothing that doesn't heal crooked, which suits me." Her mouth twitched toward the laugh and didn't quite make it, and that—that above everything—scared Odile more than any wound. Maretta's voice dropped. "Vane knows the fountain was false. He figured it out an hour ago. He's coming back tonight, Odile. You have minutes, not days."

"Then we spend them walking," Brannic rumbled, gathering her up with a gentleness that would have astonished anyone who hadn't seen him forge love letters for twenty years. "Can you run?"

"I can be *carried*, which is my preferred method of travel and always has been."

They made the coal-chute with ninety seconds to spare. Odile knew it precisely, because at eighty seconds the doors at the front of the pyre-house slammed open hard enough to crack wood, and Inquisitor Vane came in with the wind of his own certainty, and the remaining guards parted before him like water around a hot blade.

From the darkness of the chute, wedged between Brannic's shoulder and the cold iron, Odile watched him pass beneath them. Vane was not large. He did not need to be. There was a quality to his stillness that made the torches seem to lean away, and his eyes—pale, patient, terribly clean—moved up across the rafters, across the swaying cages, across everything, cataloguing.

He stopped beneath the empty gilded cage. He looked at the cut manacle. He looked at it for a long time.

Then, without raising his voice at all—which was somehow worse than shouting—he said:

"The dragon-keeper's beast will burn before the month is out. Tell them that. Tell the thieves that their little family ends where mine begins: at the fireline, with the last of the old world chained and burning, so that nothing erased can ever be remembered again."

And he turned and gave orders in his quiet voice, and the pyre-house swallowed him, and Odile slid down into the dark with Maretta's weight against their chest and a cold spot under their ribs where triumph should have been.

* * *

They ran until the city thinned, then walked until it vanished, then followed the drainage road out along the old canal toward the fireline, where Emberling's dim glow stained the underside of the clouds the color of banked coals. Dawn found them in the ruins of a tollhouse, Maretta splinted and furious and gloriously, viciously alive, eating bread with the concentration of a woman who had decided that chewing was an act of defiance.

Brannic sat against the wall with his axe across his knees and tears running silently down his soot-streaked face, which he made no effort to hide. Pip had curled against Maretta's good side and fallen asleep instantly, utterly, the sleep of the victorious young.

Maretta looked around at all of it—at Brannic crying, at Pip drooling on her splinted arm, at Odile sitting in the doorway keeping watch on nothing—and she laughed, finally, properly, wincing around her broken ribs and not caring at all.

"We won," she said, wondering. "Look at us. Broken, hunted, homeless, and we *won*."

"It's a specialty of ours," Odile said. "The ledger calls it 'profit taken in non-monetary forms.'"

Maretta's laughter faded slowly into something quieter. She studied Odile across the ruined tollhouse, and her eyes, which missed nothing even now, narrowed slightly.

"You gave away pieces of yourself in there too," she said. Not a question. "I saw your face when you uncorked them. That was your own stock, some of it. Odile. What did you spend?"

Odile looked out at the graying sky, at the coal-colored glow above the fireline where Sable was, at the horizon where Vane's threat hung like weather coming.

"Ask me tomorrow," they said. "Tomorrow I'll write it in the ledger, and then it'll be true. Tonight I want to keep believing we got out cheap."

But under their ribs the cold spot pulsed, and somewhere behind their eyes a door stood open onto a room they could no longer enter, and in that room, though they could not have said why, a dragon-keeper's face was smiling at them across a night market full of lanterns—and the smile meant everything, and Odile could not remember why.
