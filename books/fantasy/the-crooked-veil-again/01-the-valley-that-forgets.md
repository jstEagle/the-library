## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats always knew before the storm did.

Orsen watched them drift down from the high pasture in a tight, uneasy knot, ears flat, and set down the mending they'd been pretending to do. Ten years in Greyhollow taught them to read animals better than people. The sky over the western ridge was still blue, insultingly so, but the air had that pressed-in quality, the hush of a held breath, and somewhere beyond the Veil's border the weather was already turning ugly.

"Go on, then," Orsen told the lead doe, a gray-faced creature called Bristle who had outlived three winters she had no right to survive. "Complain about it."

Bristle bleated, unimpressed, and led her sisters into the lean-to.

That was most of Orsen's conversation these days. It was enough. Words were dangerous things; words had been the instrument of their ruin, spoken aloud on an altar-stone with a god listening, and Orsen had learned to keep them small and few and pointed at nothing. *Feed the goats. Mend the fence. Do not think about—*

But thinking was like water in Greyhollow. It found every crack.

They went inside and poured the first drink of the evening at an hour that would have scandalized any decent farmer, not that Greyhollow had ever been accused of decency. The valley sat folded between two ridgelines like a secret kept too long, and nothing of the world's magic could cross its borders. Wards died at the tree line. Charms curdled. Even the old roads forgot where they went, which was precisely why Orsen had chosen it—a place where the past could not follow, because the past, for people like Orsen, traveled on currents the valley refused to carry.

Ten years. Long enough for the scar tissue to feel like skin. Long enough to almost believe it.

Orsen drank and did not almost believe it at all.

* * *

The silence of Veyl was not like ordinary silence. Ordinary silence was empty. This one had weight, texture, the specific gravity of a room after someone has left it—the warmth still in the chair, the shape of absence. For the first year in the valley, Orsen had felt it constantly, the way a tongue returns again and again to a missing tooth. The oath-marks that once crawled up both forearms like black ivy were gone now, or seemed gone, or were simply sleeping beneath the skin in whatever country bound things go to when their binding is severed mid-word.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth Orsen had never said aloud, not even drunk, not even alone: Veyl had not finished.

The rite had been half-spoken when the screaming started. Orsen remembered the altar, the cold iron, the taste of ash and honey that meant a god was near. Remembered the voice—not heard with ears but felt along the bones, ancient and amused and terribly kind—saying *I bind myself to your—*

And then nothing. A cut rope. A door slammed mid-sentence.

Every other sworn servant across the three kingdoms had felt the same severance on the same night. Orsen knew this because for two years afterward, before fleeing here, they had looked for others. Found only graves, mostly, and madmen, and one hedge-priest who wept into his cups and swore the god hadn't abandoned them—it had been *taken*, dragged down somewhere deep, devoured by something older and hungrier—

Orsen had stopped listening around the word *devoured*. Some truths you can't carry and keep walking.

So: goats. Fences. Drink. Ten years of small days stacked like stones over a well, and if you didn't look down the well, you could almost forget what was buried there.

Almost.

* * *

The storm arrived the way storms did in Greyhollow—all at once, as if the valley had been saving up. Wind came down the ridgeline and flattened the grass in long silver strokes, and rain followed hard behind it, drumming on the shingles loud enough to make thought impossible, which Orsen considered a professional courtesy.

They were on their third drink, watching lightning walk the ridge like something searching, when the knock came.

Three blows. Small ones.

Orsen went very still. In ten years, no one had knocked. Maren, the nearest neighbor, shouted from her horse like a sensible woman. Traders came twice a season and honked their cart-horn from the road. Nobody knocked on a door at night, in a storm, in a valley whose whole purpose was to be forgotten—and nobody knocked *softly*, the way you would if you were small and frightened and not entirely sure what waited on the other side.

Lightning lit the window white. In the flash, Orsen saw the yard, the gate swinging, and a shape standing at the threshold—small, swaying, mud to the eyebrows.

Another flash. Gone.

Orsen took down the hatchet from beside the hearth, an act of habit rather than conviction, and opened the door on a wall of rain.

The child stood there, perhaps eight years old, drowning in a cloak far too big, which was the only reason the storm hadn't already taken her. Mud caked her to the knees and higher. Her lips had gone the color of old candle wax, and she was shivering so violently her teeth clicked like a lock trying to turn. But her eyes—her eyes were open and fixed on Orsen with a focus that had nothing childlike in it, a look Orsen recognized because Orsen had seen it in mirrors during the worst years. The look of someone who has run until running is all they are.

She opened her mouth. Rain poured off her chin. And she spoke.

"*Veyl ath-karesh. Veyl torum. Sel veth—*"

The hatchet nearly fell out of Orsen's hand.

Not because the words were strange. Because they weren't. The language came through the storm like a blade coming through cloth, and every syllable landed on scars Orsen had spent a decade pretending were healed. It was the tongue of the first kingdoms, dead a thousand years, surviving in exactly two places: the oldest temples, and the prayers of Veyl. Orsen had knelt on cold stone and repeated those prayers until the cadence lived in the marrow. *Veyl ath-karesh.* God of the crooked veil. *Veyl torum.* God of the quiet door.

No living person spoke it. No living person *could*. Orsen had checked, in another life, in another decade.

The child swayed. Orsen caught her by the shoulder on instinct, and the moment skin met skin through the soaked wool—

The valley hummed.

There was no other word for it. Greyhollow was magic-dead, had been magic-dead since before the first farms, and yet something under the ground—or under the world—answered the child's touch with a low, rising tone Orsen felt in their back teeth, in the hollow of the throat, in the exact places the oath-marks used to live. Dead wards that hadn't stirred in ten years woke along the doorframe and glimmered faintly, like embers breathed upon.

The child looked up at Orsen, and in her eyes, for half a heartbeat, something looked back. Something vast and patient and grieving.

Then she collapsed, and the hum died, and the storm rushed in to fill the silence, and Orsen stood in the doorway holding eighty pounds of freezing, mud-caked, impossible child while the rain tried to wash away everything they'd built.

"No," Orsen said aloud, to the storm, to the child, to whatever was listening. "No. Not again."

The child, unconscious, did not argue. Which was more than the god had ever managed.

* * *

Orsen worked without deciding to work. That was the horror of it—ten years of discipline undone in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Wood stacked on the fire. Wet cloak peeled away. Blankets, broth forced carefully between cracked lips, boots off feet rubbed raw from miles of walking. The girl slept like something felled, breathing slow and ragged, and Orsen sat across from her with the untouched hatchet on the table and the fourth drink going warm and watered beside it.

Forbidden prayers. Orsen turned the phrase over. They'd been forbidden since the Purges, since the crowns decided gods were safer as architecture than as voices. Saying them aloud in certain kingdoms got you a cell, or worse. This child had crossed the Veil—crossed it *on foot*, through wards that killed lesser travelers—in the middle of a storm, speaking the dead tongue like it was her mother's milk.

Nobody taught a child that. Nobody alive *could*.

Which left the answer Orsen had been refusing since the knock: the god hadn't finished its sentence because the god hadn't been able to. And if the hedge-priest's drunken ravings held any truth—if Veyl had been devoured, piece by piece, by something hungry—then a fragment might have fled. Might have hidden. Might have done what desperate things do and burrowed into the smallest, softest shelter it could find.

A temple orphan. An unclaimed child. A vessel.

Orsen looked at the girl's sleeping face, slack and young and utterly ordinary, and felt dread crack open the long-buried thing inside them like frost splitting stone.

Because if the fragment had come here—into the one valley in the world where nothing divine could follow—it hadn't come randomly. Things that flee come *to* shelters. And things that hunt shelters know where they are.

Outside, the lightning had moved on, but the wind kept worrying at the eaves. Orsen rose, shuttered the windows, and paused at the last one, looking north along the dark ridgeline toward the pass. Toward the world.

Far off—too far to be sure, close enough to be afraid—there was a light on the ridge. Then another. Then a line of them, patient and unhurried, descending through the rain like a procession of pale stars that had no business being there.

Torches. Many torches. Moving in formation.

Orsen closed the shutter and stood with their hand flat against the wood, heart hammering the old war-drum rhythm they hadn't heard in ten years.

Behind them, in her sleep, the child began to speak again—soft, fluent, terrible—words in a language older than any crown:

"*Sel veth. Sel karesh. He is coming.*"
