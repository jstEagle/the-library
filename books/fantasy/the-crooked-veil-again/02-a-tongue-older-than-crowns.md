## 2. A Tongue Older Than Crowns

The child slept in the corner by the hearth like something that had learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing about it—curled tight, one hand fisted in the hem of my spare cloak as if it might be taken back at any moment.

I had not slept at all.

I sat at the table with the last of Maren's barley whiskey and watched the fire eat itself down to coals, and I told myself the same lie I'd been telling since the storm first drove her against my door: *she's just a lost child. Feral, maybe. Half-starved. The valley has seen stranger things wash up on its stones.*

It was a good lie. It had kept me company through most of the night. But lies are like goats—they need feeding constantly or they wander off, and this one had already bolted.

Because I knew her words.

Not all of them. Not even many of them. But when she'd stood dripping in my doorway and opened her mouth, what came out was not the crooning of a frightened child, not any of the valley's dialects or the trade-speech of the lowland caravans. It was older. It had a shape to it, a weight, the way a stone has weight even before you lift it. Four syllables, repeated twice, and then a fifth that cracked in the middle like ice on a pond.

*Veyl ath-korren. Veyl ath-korren. Sennu-*

I had heard those syllables exactly once before in my life, spoken into my own mouth by my own tongue, kneeling on cold marble with an oath-blade laid across my palms. The night I bound myself to a god who then went silent mid-word and never spoke again.

I drained the whiskey and did not pour another. My hands were steady enough for now, and I wanted to keep it that way.

* * *

Morning came grey and thin through the shutters. The storm had passed in the night, leaving the valley rinsed and glittering, every stone washed clean, the kind of morning Greyhollow produced to remind you why people stayed here despite the silence. No magic. No wonders. Just weather, and work, and the long peace of a place where nothing extraordinary could follow you.

I fed the goats. I split wood because splitting wood is what I did instead of thinking. And all the while the child watched me from the doorway with those enormous dark eyes, saying nothing—which was strange enough—but watching the way a soldier watches terrain.

When I carried the water bucket up from the stream, she followed me. When I mended the hinge on the goat pen, she sat on the fence rail and studied my hands. She didn't beg. She didn't cry. Somewhere in her short life someone had taught her that crying cost more than it bought, and the knowledge sat in her small body like a stone in a shoe.

Around midday she finally spoke again.

I was at the well, and she was standing beside me, and she looked up at the sky—the empty, ordinary, magic-dead sky—and said six words in that dead language, soft, almost conversational, the way you'd remark on the weather.

Every scar on my body woke up at once.

I don't know how else to describe it. The oath-marks I'd carried for fifteen years—inked sigils along my forearms, my collarbones, the ridge of my spine, marks that had been dead ink since the night Veyl went quiet—*ached*. Not burned, not flared. Ached, deep and dull, the way old breaks ache before rain. I gripped the well rope and breathed through my teeth and felt something turn over in the silence behind my ribs, something that had been lying still so long I'd convinced myself it was a corpse.

And the wards hummed.

Greyhollow had no wards. That was the whole point of the valley—it was a wound in the world where magic couldn't enter, couldn't take root, couldn't whisper. The old ward-stones at the valley mouth were just rocks. I'd leaned against them a hundred times. They were granite and lichen and nothing else.

But standing there at the well with the child's voice still hanging in the air, I heard—felt—a low vibration coming up through the ground, through my boots, through the bones of my feet. Like a struck bell heard through a wall. Like something vast turning in its sleep.

The child looked at me. At my arms, where I'd shoved back my sleeves without meaning to, where the dead ink of the oath-marks had gone dark and glistening as if freshly drawn.

She said another word. One word. Gentle. Almost sorry.

And I understood it—not translated it, *understood* it, the way you understand your own name—and the word was:

*Orsen.*

My knees nearly went.

"Who are you?" I said, and my voice came out wrecked, scraped raw. "What are you? Who sent you?"

She tilted her head. She didn't understand the words—I could see that—but she understood the fear in them, and something in her face closed, the way a door closes, polite and final. She took a step back from the well.

"No," I said, softer. "No, wait. I'm not—" I dragged a hand down my face. Ten years of hiding, ten years of being no one, and here was a mud-caked child speaking the language of a murdered god, and my hands were shaking around a water bucket. "Sit down," I managed. "Please. Just—sit."

She sat on the well's stone lip, prim as a duchess, and waited.

I knelt in the mud in front of her, which my back complained about immediately, and I spoke slowly, carefully, dredging up syllables I hadn't used since the temple, since the binding, since the last night of my old life. My accent would have made a priest weep. I didn't care.

"I knew your god," I said in the old tongue. "Before he went quiet."

Her eyes went wide. Her whole face changed—caution cracking open into something desperate and young, and suddenly she was talking, fast, tumbling over herself, hands flying, and I caught maybe one word in five. But I caught enough.

*Alone.* *Long.* *Dark.* *Running.* *They come.* *They come.* *They come.*

"They?" I said. "Who comes?"

She pressed both hands flat over her chest, over her heart, and then drew one finger across her throat—not a threat, I realized, but a description. What happens to the others. What happened to everyone like her.

Then she pointed north, toward the ridge, toward the pass out of the valley, and made a shape in the air with her fingers: a crown.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the thing I was best at, which was refusing to think, and thinking anyway.

I fixed the pen gate. I hauled hay. I sharpened the old sword that hung above my mantel, the one I'd sworn I'd never need again, and the rhythmic scrape of whetstone on steel steadied me the way prayer used to. The child—Sable, I decided; I needed to call her something, and when I tried the name aloud she'd nodded once, quick, like a bird deciding to trust you—Sable sat on the step and watched the road.

That was what finally finished me. Not the language. Not the humming wards, not the waking scars, not even the word *crown* drawn in the air. It was the way she watched the road. Constantly. Without hope, exactly—more like a rabbit watches a hedgerow. She wasn't looking for rescue. She was looking for the thing that ended rescues.

Nobody teaches a child that. Only hunting does.

So I let myself think it, there in the amber light of late afternoon with the whetstone whispering: the god hadn't died. Dying leaves a different silence. I'd lived inside Veyl's silence for ten years and I knew its texture, its weight, the way a held breath has weight. This was not death. This was *consumption*. Something had been eating my god—one piece, one voice, one vessel at a time—for longer than I'd been hiding, and it had gotten everything except whatever fragment lived behind this child's sternum.

And it was still hungry. And it had a crown now, and soldiers, and all the time in the world.

I looked at Sable on the step, thin wrists, mud-stiff hair, eyes older than her years by several hard lifetimes, and I felt the thing I'd spent a decade drowning rise up in me whole and undeniable. Not duty. Duty I could have argued with. This was simpler and worse: the animal certainty that this child was mine to keep alive, and that I would burn down anything that came for her, god or king or the space between them.

I hated it. I want that on the record, somewhere, in some honest ledger of my life: I hated it with my entire heart. I had paid for my peace in blood and betrayal and ten years of goat shit and cheap whiskey, and I had earned the right to be left alone, and the world had agreed, and now the world was reneging.

The whetstone stopped.

Down the valley, where the road crested the ridge before dropping toward the pass, dust was rising. Not storm dust—storm dust hangs and drifts. This rose in columns, disciplined, spaced. Horses. Many horses, moving at a canter, holding formation.

I set down the blade and crossed the yard quietly and put two fingers under Sable's chin and turned her face away from the ridge, down, toward me.

"In the house," I said in her tongue, clumsy but clear. "Under the floor. Now."

She didn't argue. That frightened me more than the dust did—children who obey instantly have been taught by bad things.

Through the gap in the shutters I watched them come. Eight riders, black armor dulled to matte, no banners. They didn't need banners. On each pauldron, worked in tarnished silver, was the sigil I'd only seen once before, on documents smuggled out of the capital by men who later stopped writing: a crown floating above an empty space where a head should be.

The Hollow King's mark. The crown that wore the man.

They reined up at the valley mouth, at the dead ward-stones, and I saw their leader dismount and lay his bare palm flat against the granite the way you'd check a horse for fever. Listening. And though they were half a mile off and the wind was wrong, I swear I saw him go rigid—saw him snap his head up and look, unerringly, across the whole length of the valley, directly at my farmhouse.

Directly at the floorboards where a child was holding her breath.

He raised one hand. The riders spread out, unhurried, certain, the way wolves spread when the prey is already dead and merely hasn't noticed.

I took the sword down from above the mantel. It was lighter than I remembered, or my arms were stronger than I'd let myself become.

"Sable," I said to the floor, in the tongue of dead kings, "when I tell you, you run for Maren's farm and you don't stop. Whatever you hear behind you—" My throat closed. I swallowed past it. "Whatever you hear, you don't stop."
