## 3. What the Oath Still Owes

The goat-smell wakes her before the light does.

Sable lies still in the dark under a blanket that smells of smoke and old wool and someone else's sweat, and she listens to the house breathe. Houses here are not like the temple. The temple breathed stone — cold, patient, a held breath that never let go. This house breathes wood. It creaks when the wind leans on it. It groans like an old man turning over in his sleep. Somewhere below, the man with the scarred hands is moving around his kitchen, and the sound of him is not frightening.

That is the strangest thing. That it is not frightening.

She does not have words for this yet — not in the tongue he speaks, which slides off her ears like rain off oilcloth, and not even in her own mouth-tongue, the one she was born knowing before the priests took it apart like a stolen clock. What she has is *knowing*, the kind that lives below words, down where the god-fragment coils in her chest like a second heart that beats out of time with hers.

*He is safe,* says the second heart. *This one is safe.*

She has learned to believe it, mostly, because the second heart has never lied to her. Only gone quiet at the worst moments, like now, like always lately, a warmth turned down to embers. But embers are not nothing. Embers mean something is still burning underneath.

She gets up. The floorboards complain. In the small window, the storm from last night is leaving — dragging its grey skirts over the ridge, dripping off the eaves in long threads — and beyond it the valley opens up all green and wet and empty, and Sable presses her forehead to the cold glass and tries to remember how she got here.

That is the trouble with her memory. It comes in pieces, and the pieces come when they want, and some of them she would give back if she could.

* * *

*Piece one:*

A courtyard of white gravel raked into waves, so that walking across it felt like wading through a frozen sea. The Temple of the Quiet Choir, though she didn't know then that "quiet" was a joke the adults told each other with their eyes. She was small. She was carrying someone's laundry, or maybe someone's sins; at that age the two weighed the same.

The other children called her *the Echo* because sometimes, when she slept, she talked in a language nobody taught her, and once — only once, but everyone remembered — the candle flames had leaned toward her bed like flowers toward sun.

Mother Superior Vess found her by the well that day. Vess had hands like folded paper and a voice like honey poured over a knife.

"You hear it too," Vess said. Not asking. "In the night. The singing."

Sable nodded, because lying to Vess was like lying to weather.

"It's a sickness, little Echo." The paper hands cupped her face, gentle as frostbite. "But there are men who can cure it. Important men. And they pay very well for sick children."

*Piece two:*

The night itself, which she keeps in a locked room inside her head and which opens anyway, every time, because locked rooms in her head have learned that locks mean nothing to what's coming.

Candles. A circle of them, and she is standing in the middle in a white shift, shivering, and around the circle stand six priests with their hoods back and their faces arranged into expressions of solemn mercy, the way people look at livestock they're fond of. There is a bowl of water so black it looks cut out of the night sky. There are words. She knows the words. She knows them the way you know your own name — except they aren't hers, they are older, they are *His*, and as the priests chant them the second heart in her chest begins to scream in a language made entirely of *no*.

They are not selling her voice. That is what no one ever understood, not even her, not for years. They are selling what lives *behind* her voice. The vessel goes to the crown; the god-fragment goes with the vessel. A package. A delivery.

And the buyer —

*Piece three:*

The buyer is the part she cannot make herself see straight-on. When she tries, her mind slides away like a hand off wet glass. So she sees it sideways, in flashes:

A throne room tall enough to hold weather. Banners the color of dried blood. Courtiers standing very still, the way rabbits stand still.

A man on the throne who was handsome once, the way a portrait is handsome, paint over canvas over rot.

And the crown. Oh, the crown. It does not sit on his head. It *grows* into it, filigree threading down past his temples like roots into soil, gold becoming skin becoming gold, and where his eyes should be there are two drowned lanterns, lit from beneath, green and drowning, and when he smiles —

When he smiles, the smile belongs to something else. Something that looked at her across that throne room with a hunger so vast and so patient that her knees simply stopped working, and the second heart in her chest went silent for the first time in her life, silent the way a forest goes silent when the wolf enters it, and the thing on the throne opened a mouth with too many teeth, far too many, teeth like a lamprey's, teeth that were not there a moment ago and should not fit in a human jaw, and it said, in the tongue of the first kingdoms, in *her* secret sleeping tongue:

*"There you are. The last little piece. I have been saving room."*

Then: running. Cold floors. Someone screaming and it takes her years to understand the screaming was her. Hands grabbing. The smell of incense and rot-sweetness. And a door, and snow, and —

*Piece four:*

Snow. A valley. Falling down a hillside with briars writing red lines on her legs. Mud to her chin in places. Three days? Four? She doesn't remember eating. She remembers the second heart going quiet again whenever the riders passed on the ridgelines — black riders, banners of dried blood — and going warm again only when the valley swallowed her deeper.

And then a door. And a man with grey in his beard and scars laddering his forearms like ladder-rungs, and when she spoke to him — when the old tongue fell out of her mouth without permission, the way it always does — his face did something terrible and wonderful. It cracked. All the hard years fell away from behind his eyes for just a moment, and what was underneath looked like grief, and hope, and a man remembering he'd buried something alive.

He said a word back to her. One word, rough and rusty, in the dead language.

And the second heart *sang*.

* * *

Now she stands at the window watching him through the floor — watching Orsen Delacroix, though she doesn't know the name yet, will learn it tonight or tomorrow — watching him feed the fire and talk to the goats through the wall like they're colleagues, and she tries to explain to herself why she isn't afraid.

It isn't the scars. It isn't that he gave her bread and didn't ask questions, though he clearly wanted to, questions crowding behind his teeth like sheep at a gate. It's this: when the priests touched her, the second heart flinched. When the thing on the throne looked at her, the second heart died down to nothing. But when Orsen carried her in from the rain, grumbling the whole way in his wrong-tongue, wrapping her in wool that smelled of smoke — the second heart had gone *warm*. Warm the way a stone stays warm hours after the sun leaves it.

Trust, she thinks, is just a warmth you decide to sit beside.

There is a sound outside. Hoofbeats, far off, on the ridge road. Two horses, maybe three, moving slow the way men move when they are looking rather than going.

Orsen stops mid-motion. His head comes up, sharp, a dog hearing a whistle no one else hears. And Sable feels the second heart do something it has almost never done.

It speaks. Clearly. In the old tongue, in words she half-understands and fully obeys, the way you obey a shout of *fire* whether or not you know the word:

*Down. Be small. He comes wearing borrowed faces, and the valley is thinner than it was yesterday.*

She is already moving, dropping below the windowsill, when the hoofbeats stop outside.

A knock. Polite. Three measured raps, patient as a spider.

Through the floorboards Sable watches Orsen's boots go still beside the table. Then, slowly, they turn toward the door. Above her, muffled through wood and distance, a voice calls out — pleasant, reasonable, the voice of a neighbor or a tax-man or any ordinary morning:

"Householder! Open in the name of the Crown. We seek a child."

And inside Sable's chest, the fragment of a devoured god begins, very quietly, to burn.
