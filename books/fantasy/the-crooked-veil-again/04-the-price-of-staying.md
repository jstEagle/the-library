## 4. The Price of Staying

The smoke came first.

Orsen smelled it before dawn, a thin acrid thread woven through the rain that had followed the storm for three days. They stood in the doorway with a milking pail forgotten in one hand, watching the eastern ridge where the Hollis farm sat below the tree line, and waited for the light that would tell them it was only a hearth fire gone wrong.

It came at first light. Not orange—the wrong kind of orange, too steady, too deliberate. A burn line, drawn across the barley field like a signature.

Behind them, small feet padded across the floorboards. Sable pressed into the gap between Orsen's hip and the doorframe, her dark eyes fixed on the same horizon, and she said something in the dead tongue—two syllables, low, the vowels shaped like grief.

"I know," Orsen said, though they didn't. They were learning to guess. "I know."

*They have found the valley's edge,* she might have been saying. *They are coming inward.*

Ten years, Orsen thought. Ten years of goats and rainwater whiskey and pretending the silence in their bones was peace instead of amputation. Ten years believing Greyhollow's dead air was a wall no army could climb, because magic could not enter here, and what hunted Sable—if anything still hunted—would be made of magic.

But soldiers did not need magic to burn a barley field. And whatever wore King Aldric's crown had never needed magic to find people. It only needed patience, and other men's hands.

* * *

By midmorning there were three fires visible from the high pasture: the Hollises' barley, Tam Wicker's barn, and something farther south whose smoke rose black and greasy, the color of a house burning with everything inside it. The valley folk gathered at Maren's gate the way they always gathered when trouble came—not running toward it, not yet running away, just standing together in the mud as if proximity were a kind of armor.

Maren stood at the head of them, sixty years old and built like the stone walls she'd spent a lifetime mending, her gray hair tied back with twine. She had taken Sable in without question the night the storm brought her, fed her, taught her to card wool. She had asked Orsen exactly one question about the child—"Is someone looking for her?"—and when Orsen said yes, she had nodded once and never raised it again.

"Riders on the south road," called Doss Ellery's boy, sliding down off his father's cart. "Six of them. Maybe eight."

"Soldiers?"

"Dressed like it. Black and gold." He swallowed. "Got a banner. Some kind of crown with no king under it."

Orsen felt the words go through them like a nail through wet wood. The sigil of the Hollow King. Not a story after all. Not a nightmare Sable had dragged out of some burned temple in her sleep.

Maren turned, and her eyes found Orsen across the crowd the way a compass finds north. She didn't say *you knew.* She didn't say *what did you bring down on us.* She just looked, and then she looked at Sable, half-hidden behind Orsen's leg, and her jaw set in the way it set before lambing season, before floods, before every hard thing she had ever walked into rather than around.

"All right," Maren said to the crowd, loud enough to end argument before it started. "Doss, get your family down into the root cellars. Anyone with young ones, go with him. The rest of us are farmers, not fighters—we stand where we're seen, all of us together, old and plain and harmless. Nobody gives them a reason."

"And if they want something?" someone asked.

"They'll want nothing we have," Maren said. But her eyes went once more to Sable, and everyone saw it, and nobody said it either.

* * *

The riders came up the valley road at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they already own what they're riding toward. Eight of them, chain mail under black tabards, and the banner snapping above them: a crown floating over emptiness, rendered in thread so fine it seemed to hover even against the sky.

Their captain reined in at Maren's gate. Younger than Orsen expected, with a soft face and hard eyes, the eyes of a man who had been told what he was doing was necessary often enough to believe it.

"We're searching for a child," he said. "A girl, perhaps eight years. Dark-haired. She does not speak any tongue you'd know. She may travel with an adult—a tall stranger, scarred." His gaze moved across the assembled farmers, cataloguing. "She is property of the Crown. Stolen property. The penalty for concealing her is the same as the penalty for theft."

"This is Greyhollow," Maren said evenly. "We don't hold with crowns here, stolen or otherwise. No child's passed through but our own, and those we can account for."

The captain smiled without warmth. "Then you won't mind if we look."

He dismounted. Two of his men followed him through the gate, and the valley folk parted around them the way water parts around a keel—unwilling, unresisting, hating themselves a little for it. Orsen watched the searchers move down the lane toward the barns and understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that this was theater. The captain knew. Something behind that soft face knew exactly which farm held what it wanted, and the searching was ritual, a formality performed so that the burning afterward would feel deserved.

Sable's hand found Orsen's. Her fingers were ice.

She spoke without moving her lips, barely a breath, and this time Orsen understood without trying—the fragment of Veyl in her translating itself directly into the marrow, past language entirely:

*The hollow one sends its hands. It has always sent its hands. You hid where it could not see, but I was never fully hidden. It has been counting my heartbeats for ten years.*

"You knew," Orsen breathed. "All this time—they've known where you were?"

*They knew something lived here that should not. Knowing and reaching are different things. But the Veil thins, mercenary. And now they have stopped sending questions and started sending fire.*

* * *

The search took two hours. It was thorough and it was insulting and it found nothing, because Sable had been in the root cellar beneath Maren's kitchen since before the riders crested the south hill, curled among turnips under a false floor Maren had built during the border wars forty years ago and kept oiled ever since, "just in case the world gets ideas again."

When the captain emerged from the last barn, he was no longer pretending. He stood in Maren's yard and looked at the assembled farmers with something close to regret, the way a man regrets weather.

"The Crown will assume noncompliance," he said. "The farms along the south road will be assessed accordingly. If the child is surrendered within three days, assessments will cease."

"Assessed," Maren repeated.

"Burned," the captain agreed pleasantly. "I'd start packing."

He mounted. The column wheeled. And Orsen, standing at the crowd's edge with Sable's cold fingers locked in theirs, watched the banner recede down the valley road and felt ten years of careful forgetting catch fire along with everything else.

Three days. The entity hadn't come for the valley in a decade because reaching across the Veil was slow, expensive, a siege conducted by proxy—and now the siege had broken surface, because the last vessel had run out of hiding, and hiding had run out of valley.

There was no version of this where Greyhollow stayed safe. There was only versions where more farms burned, where more names got added to the list of debts, where Maren's oiled cellar became a coffin with better ventilation.

Orsen knew what had to happen the way you know a bone is broken. Cleanly. Immediately. Without negotiating.

That evening they told Maren in her kitchen, by lamplight, while Sable slept upstairs in the bed Maren had given her.

"I'm taking her out tomorrow night. Over the western pass, before their pickets settle in."

Maren stirred the pot on her stove. She didn't look up. "Western pass is watched. Everything'll be watched. You'll need a distraction, something loud enough and stupid enough to pull eight soldiers off a road in the dark."

"Maren—"

"Don't." Still not looking up. "Don't do the thing where you decide for me what I'm willing to spend. I buried a husband in the border wars and a son in a bad winter, and I'm sixty-one years old, and I have been waiting ten years for something worth doing with what's left." Now she looked, and her eyes were dry and absolutely certain. "You take the child west. I'll give the valley a bonfire it won't forget. That's not a request, Orsen Delacroix. I've known what you were since the day you staggered in here with those marks on your arms, drinking like a man outrunning his own shadow. I let you hide because hiding was mercy. Mercy's over."

Orsen's throat worked. Every argument they reached for—*you're too old, they'll kill you, it should be me*—was true, and none of it mattered, and Maren's face said she knew all three.

"She calls you the quiet one," Maren said, softer. "In that language of hers. Did you know? The quiet god's keeper. Whatever's left of your god lives in that girl, and it chose you, drunk and half-dead in a storm, and I'll tell you something about gods, mercenary: they don't choose people to save them. They choose people who still owe something."

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Small footsteps, then silence, listening.

Orsen bowed their head. "I'll carry it," they said finally. "Whatever it costs. I'll carry it."

"I know you will." Maren turned back to her pot. "Now eat something. You can't outrun a kingdom hungry."

* * *

They left at moonrise, two days early, because Sable woke screaming at midnight with blood on her lip and a word in her mouth that Orsen recognized from the oldest forbidden prayers: the word for *harvest.*

The western pass was a goat track above the tree line, and they were halfway up it when the valley lit behind them—Maren's barn going up first, then the hayricks, then a ring of brush fires laid in a crescent along the south road, exactly where the soldiers had made their camp. Shouting drifted up through the cold air. Bells. Horses.

And figures moving through the chaos with a torch in each hand and decades of grudge in every swing: Doss Ellery, the Wickers, half the valley, turning Maren's single sacrifice into a general uprising, buying the fugitives every minute they could.

Orsen stood on the switchback with Sable on their shoulders and watched the place that had saved their life burn itself to buy their escape, and understood that invisibility had always been borrowed, and the interest had just come due.

Below, tiny in the firelight, a figure on horseback turned from the chaos and looked up the mountain—at nothing, at darkness, at two specks against the stars—and Orsen felt, unmistakably, across half a mile of cold night air, the attention of something ancient settle on them like a hand.

Sable's fingers tightened in their hair. From her throat came a whisper in two voices at once—one a child's, one older than crowns—and the word was the same in both:

*"Run."*

Orsen ran.
