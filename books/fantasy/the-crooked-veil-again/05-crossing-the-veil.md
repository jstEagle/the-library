## 5. Crossing the Veil

The border of Greyhollow was not a wall or a river or a line drawn in any king's ledger. It was a feeling—a wrongness in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes—that every exile learned to read the way sailors read weather. For ten years Orsen had felt it at their back each morning like a hand pressed flat between the shoulder blades: *stay*. The valley held them the way a scar holds a wound closed.

Now they stood at its edge with a child's cold fingers laced through theirs, and the hand was pushing instead of holding.

Behind them, smoke still rose from the outer farms. Maren's farm. Maren's bones, if the soldiers had left any to burn. Orsen had not let themselves look back on the road—looking back was how you died in stories—but here at the last threshold they allowed it one long breath. The valley lay green and indifferent under a bruised sky, goats scattered on the high pasture where someone else would find them, or no one would. Ten years of quiet, ending in ash and a girl's small hand.

"Orsen." Sable said it in her own tongue, the dead one, but she'd learned the shape of their name in living speech and used it now like a key. She pointed down the slope, past the standing stones that marked the old pilgrim road, toward the world.

"I know," Orsen said. "I know."

They stepped over the stones together.

* * *

Magic did not return gently.

It came like water through a broken dam, like a shout after years of deafness—and Orsen's body remembered everything at once. They went to their knees on the wet grass beyond the border-stones, gasping, as the oath-marks woke.

They had almost forgotten what the marks looked like. Faint silver scarring, once, running from wrist to shoulder like frost ferns grown under skin—the contract written into flesh when a mercenary with nothing left to lose had knelt in a burning temple and sworn herself to Veyl, god of thresholds and unkept promises. In Greyhollow the marks had faded to nothing, ghost-lines visible only in certain light. Now they *ignited*. Light poured up Orsen's arms in branching veins, white-gold and merciless, and every nerve sang with a decade of deferred devotion. It hurt the way a limb hurts when blood returns to it. It hurt like being *claimed*.

Beside them, Sable made a sound—not fear. Recognition.

And then, out of the child's mouth, in a voice that was hers and yet not hers, doubled and deepened like a bell struck underwater, came the first clear word Orsen's god had spoken in ten years:

"*Run.*"

Orsen ran. Hauled Sable up and ran, off the pilgrim road and into the bracken, because ten years ago they had learned what that voice sounded like when it spoke plainly—it did not waste words, and it did not say things twice. The light along their arms dimmed to embers but did not die, pulsing in time with their heartbeat, and above the ridge behind them the air began to *bend*, the way it bent around the Hollow King's hunters when they worked their hollow sorceries.

They ran until the light failed and the hills swallowed them, and only then did Orsen permit themselves to fall against an oak and breathe.

Sable leaned against their side, small chest heaving. Her eyes, when she looked up, were wide and shining. "He's awake," she whispered—in the dead tongue first, then, clumsily, in the trade-speech she was learning word by word from Orsen's patient mouth. "He's *awake*, he wasn't before."

"He was never asleep," Orsen said. Their voice came out rougher than intended. "He was being eaten. Slowly. A bite at a time." They flexed their marked arm; the ember-light guttered, steadied. "And now he knows I'm out here. And so does the thing that ate him."

Sable considered this with the terrible gravity of children. Then she took Orsen's marked hand in both of hers and pressed it flat against her sternum, over her heart, where something ancient stirred beneath the ribs like a fish turning in dark water.

"He says you kept the oath," she translated. "Even silent. Even here. He says—" she frowned, listening inward, "—he says he never stopped counting the days."

Orsen looked away, at the hostile green world, and found their eyes stinging. "Tell him," they managed, "that I counted too."

* * *

They traveled by night for two days, sleeping in culverts and shepherd's ruins, following the old road south toward the river crossings. The marks stayed lit—fainter than that first flooding moment, but present, a low warmth like a banked fire. Orsen hated how much they had missed it. That was the trap of gods, they thought. You could starve yourself of them for ten years and the first taste went straight to the starving place.

On the third night they smelled woodsmoke that didn't belong to any hearth, and saw the checkpoint.

It sat astride the bridge at Corren's Ford—a palisade of black-wood stakes, lanterns hung in a row like hanged men, and a dozen soldiers in the grey-and-bone livery of the Hollow King. Wagons were being turned back. A merchant argued and was struck down for his trouble. And at the center of the crossing, planted in the mud like a trophy, stood a pole bearing a cage of iron bands—and inside the cage, wrapped in oilcloth but unmistakable to anyone who knew what wards looked like, a *resonance bell*. A vessel-finder. It would sing when Sable came within half a mile.

Orsen drew back into the alder thicket and cursed with feeling, quietly, in three languages.

"There's another ford," they murmured. "Two days north. Cold water, bad footing, but no bell."

Sable tugged their sleeve. She was staring not at the checkpoint but at a figure kneeling at its edge—a man in a priest's cassock gone grey with road-dirt, no vestments, no sigil, his hands bound before him with cord while a soldier laughed and shoved him. Even at this distance Orsen caught the words: "*Blasphemer. Preaching the Old Quiet in the king's own march. Let's see how your god saves you.*"

The priest did not beg. He looked up at the soldier with an expression Orsen knew intimately—the flat, tired defiance of a man who has already lost everything worth losing and found it strangely freeing. The soldier raised his boot to put the man face-down in the mud.

The resonance bell rang.

One clean note, silver and wrong, rolling across the ford—and every head turned, not toward the thicket where Orsen and Sable crouched, but toward the *priest*. Because the bell did not find vessels of Veyl alone. It found anything that carried the old current, and this disgraced, bound, mud-caked man lit up like a struck match in the dark.

"Hedge-priest," Orsen breathed, understanding. Unordained, unlicensed, carrying a scrap of the old faith in his bones—probably a relic sewn into his cassock, probably nothing more than a saint's fingerbone and a great deal of stubbornness. Enough to ring a bell tuned for gods.

The soldiers converged on him. And Orsen, who had spent ten years teaching themselves not to act, not to care, not to *burn*—felt the oath-marks flare hot along both arms and heard Sable's breath catch beside them, and understood that the decision had already been made somewhere below thought.

"Stay here," they told the girl. "If I don't come back, follow the stream north. Two days. Don't stop for anything that speaks kindly."

She gripped their wrist. In the doubled voice, soft as a knife going in: "*He says go. He says the fat one sleeps badly.*"

* * *

The fat one slept badly indeed—or rather, the sentry at the palisade's blind corner had drunk most of a flask of something and was nodding over his spear when Orsen's elbow found the base of his skull. Orsen wore his cloak over their own shoulders and walked, unhurried, into the lamplight of the camp, toward the knot of soldiers dragging the hedge-priest toward the interrogation tent.

Four steps. Six. The marks under the borrowed cloak pulsed once, and Orsen thought, absurdly, *not now*—

A lantern guttered. Every flame on the ford dipped at once, all together, like a congregation bowing its head—and in that heartbeat of darkness Orsen moved. Cut the cord. Shoved the priest stumbling into the reeds. Put a knee into the nearest soldier's gut, a fist into the next one's throat, and then simply *ran*, hauling the man by the collar while shouts erupted behind them and the resonance bell tolled wildly, uselessly, singing for everyone and finding no one.

They crashed through the alders. Sable materialized out of the dark exactly where she should not have been, took the priest's other arm without being asked, and the three of them floundered upstream through freezing shallows until the shouting faded and the night folded shut behind them like water over a stone.

* * *

They collapsed in a ruined mill two miles north, breath ragged, soaked to the ribs. The priest lay on his back among the rotting sacking and laughed—a dry, cracked sound, like a door that hadn't opened in years.

"Well," he said. "Either I've died and this is a very muddy heaven, or the Old Quiet has finally taken an interest in me again."

"It's neither," Orsen said, wringing out their cloak. "Keep your voice down."

"Brother Callow." He sat up, wincing, and pushed back his hood: a gaunt face, fifty-odd years, a beard going to salt-and-iron, and eyes that had the particular brightness of a man who has been beaten for his faith often enough to find it funny. His gaze snagged on Orsen's forearm, where the sleeve had ridden up and the marks glowed faintly in the dark like coals under ash. He went very still. "Threshold-marks. Oath-bonded. I haven't seen a living bond since—" He stopped. Recalculated. His eyes moved to Sable, who was watching him with unnerving, ancient calm. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, *you're* the one they're hunting. The little vessel."

"She has a name," Orsen said.

"Sable," the girl supplied, in trade-speech, clearly pleased with herself.

Callow looked from the child to the mercenary and back, and something in his face shifted—the humor settling into something older and more careful. "You pulled me out of a king's checkpoint without knowing me. That's either faith or madness."

"It was tactical," Orsen lied.

"Mm." Callow rubbed his bound wrists, red-raw. "Then let me offer something tactical in return. There's a company of the Hollow Guard moving down from the pass—two hundred heavy horse, not twelve bored sentries. They'll sweep this whole march within the week, and they have *three* bells, and one of them is tuned narrow enough to find that girl through stone and river both." He paused. "You can't outrun that. But I know roads the Guard doesn't, because I preached on them illegally for eleven years. Catacomb ways. Drowned chapels. The paths under the world that the crown paved over and forgot."

Orsen studied him. The marks on their arms pulsed slow and warm, and Orsen had learned—ten years too late, but learned—to trust what the god warmed toward. "Why?" they asked. "What do you get?"

Callow was quiet a moment. When he answered, the flippancy had burned away.

"I watched them harvest a vessel in Vellmark, four years ago. A boy of nine. They carried him into the palace smiling and he was smiling too, right up until the doors closed, because they'd told him he was going to meet his god." His jaw tightened. "I ran. I've been running and preaching to ditches ever since, and telling myself there was nothing a hedge-priest with a stolen relic could do against the thing on the throne." He looked at Sable—at the fragment of the devoured god sleeping behind her eyes. "There isn't. Not alone. So I'm asking to stop being alone."

The mill creaked. Somewhere far south, faint as memory, a bell rang again, sweeping the dark.

Orsen looked at Sable. The girl was already asleep against a beam, utterly trusting, one hand curled around Orsen's marked wrist as though even in dreams she refused to let the bond be severed. Above them, through the broken roof, the stars wheeled over a kingdom that wanted her dead.

"We leave before dawn," Orsen said. "And Brother Callow—if you slow us down, I'll leave you. If you betray us, the god whose faith got you beaten will watch what I do about it."

Callow smiled, tired and true. "That," he said, "is the first sermon I've believed in years."

Far to the south, past the hills, past the ford, past the burning farms and the valley that had forgotten them—something vast and hungry turned its attention north, and every warding-stone along the old road hummed like a plucked string.
