## 6. The God That Went Quiet

Brother Callow had not prayed in four years, and the ruin he'd led them to was as good a place as any to remember why.

The shrine crouched in the crook of two hills like an old animal that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway. Its walls were fieldstone gone soft with moss, its roof a ribcage of blackened beams open to the sky. Rain came through the gaps in slow, deliberate threads, pooling on a floor where the mosaic of Veyl's face had been chipped away tile by tile—deliberately, Callow knew, by men who wore vestments like his own. Only one eye of the god remained, a disc of blue glass no bigger than a coin, staring up out of the rubble with the patience of the drowned.

He set his lantern on the altar slab and watched the light find it.

Behind him, the mercenary Delacroix stood in the broken doorway with rain steaming off his shoulders, one hand never far from the axe at his hip. The child—Sable—was already inside, moving through the ruin with the unselfconscious certainty of someone walking through a memory. She touched the shattered pillars. She pressed her palm flat against the altar stone. And when she passed beneath the empty arch where the god's name had once been carved in letters deep enough to hold candlelight, she stopped, tilted her head back, and said something in that dead tongue—two words, soft as breath.

The blue glass eye flickered.

Callow's knees nearly went. Four years of defrocked, drunken, God-forsaken wandering, and one word from a mud-streaked child made him feel like a novice again, sick with awe.

"You felt that," Delacroix said. Not a question. The man missed nothing; it was exhausting.

"I felt it." Callow lowered himself onto a fallen beam, joints complaining. "Sit down, mercenary. What I have to tell you is long, and you're going to want to be sitting when it stops making sense."

Delacroix didn't sit. He leaned against the doorpost instead, arms crossed, rain dripping from his jaw. Fine. Let him stand. Pride was cheaper than chairs.

* * *

"Before I was thrown out of the Order," Callow began, "I kept the archives at Saint Verren's. Not the public archive—the true one, under the floor, the one we told ourselves didn't exist because existing would have meant admitting what we knew." He turned the lantern's handle slowly, watching shadows wheel across the surviving eye of the god. "You think Veyl went silent. Everyone thinks that. Ten years ago the prayers stopped being answered, the vessels went quiet, the wards crumbled, and the priesthood declared it a mystery and then, very quickly, declared it nothing at all. Mystery is bad for donations."

"The wards in Greyhollow still work," Delacroix said. "Barely."

"Barely. Like a man breathing after the spear's already in him." Callow met the mercenary's eyes. "Veyl did not go silent, Orsen Delacroix. Veyl was eaten."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere in the hills, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"There is a thing older than the first kingdoms," Callow went on. "Older than Veyl, older than the oaths, older than the idea of gods at all. The oldest texts call it the Crownless—not because it lacks a crown, but because crowns are all it has ever wanted. It has no body. It has no voice of its own. It is hunger wearing whatever shape will feed it longest. And ten years ago, it found something better than a shape. It found a king."

"Aldric," Delacroix said quietly.

"Aldric." Saying the name aloud felt like stepping on a grave. "You've seen him. At the Feast, in the capital—you saw what wears him."

"I saw a man mouth the words *kill me* before something smiled through his face." Delacroix's voice stayed level, but his knuckles had gone white around his own arms. "I saw enough."

"Then you saw ninety percent of the story. Here is the last ten percent." Callow stood, because some things could not be said sitting down, and crossed to the altar where Sable stood watching him with those too-old eyes. He crouched to her height, which his back hated, and spoke gently anyway. "Your god didn't abandon you, little one. Understand that first, before anything else. Whatever else I say tonight—that is the truth underneath it."

She studied him. Then she reached out and took his hand, her fingers small and cold and certain, and said a single word in the old tongue. He didn't need translation. Every child raised within reach of a temple learned it eventually: *continue.*

* * *

He told them everything, because they deserved everything, and because he had carried it alone so long it had begun to rot him from the inside.

How the Crownless had come into the world the way all such things do—invited. A dynasty of kings who wanted to live forever, a court sorcerer who promised eternity and delivered appetite instead. How the thing could not simply take a host; it had to be fed, patiently, vessel by vessel, voice by voice. How Veyl, the quiet god, the god of thresholds and small mercies, had stood between the Crownless and the world for a thousand years—not with fire, never with fire, but by scattering itself. By putting pieces of its own voice into mortal children, so that the hunger would have to chase divinity through a hundred small bodies instead of swallowing the source whole.

"And it worked," Callow said. "For centuries it worked. The Crownless gnawed at the edges. It harvested a vessel here, a temple there. Slow work. Deniable work. And then Aldric's line inherited the throne, and Aldric's line had forgotten why their grandfathers built shrines in the middle of nowhere, and the Order—" his voice caught, and he let it, because lying about this was how the rot spread, "—the Order decided the vessels were more useful sold than sheltered. Temple orphans. Children no one would miss. We sold them to the crown by the cartload and called it tithing."

Sable's hand tightened in his. She had been one of those cartloads. He did not look away from her, though every part of him wanted to. She deserved a man who could look at what he'd done.

"Ten years ago, the harvest got efficient. Too efficient. They found a rite—a way to pull the god-fragment out of a vessel whole, without killing the child first. Do you understand what that means?" He looked up at Delacroix. "It means the Crownless stopped eating crumbs. It started eating courses. Vessel after vessel, year after year, each fragment swallowed making the thing in the king stronger and the god weaker. Veyl didn't fall silent, mercenary. Veyl was devoured piece by piece while the men sworn to protect it counted the payment."

"And the children?" Delacroix asked. His voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of a man standing at the edge of something deep. "After the harvesting. The rites you described—the ones that don't kill first."

Callow closed his eyes. "There are always complications. Always spillage. The vessels don't survive the second harvest. There hasn't been a second harvest yet, for any of them." He opened his eyes and forced himself to finish. "Every child your age or near it who ever carried a fragment of Veyl is dead, Orsen. Except one."

All three of them looked at Sable.

She looked back, and she did not cry, and somehow that was worse than crying. She pulled her hand free of Callow's, walked to the mosaic floor, and crouched beside the blue glass eye. With one finger, she traced the empty socket around it—the place where the rest of the god's face used to be—and then she looked up at them both and spoke in the old tongue, a full sentence this time, her voice steady as a plumb line.

"What did she say?" Delacroix asked.

"She said," Callow managed, and had to stop, and start again, "'Then I am what's left. So I will have to be enough.'" He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and awful. "Four years I told myself there was nothing left worth believing in. Four years. And an eight-year-old just out-preached me in a dead language."

* * *

Night settled properly. Delacroix built a small fire in the shrine's center, beneath the hole in the roof, so the smoke rose straight up into the stars like a thread stitching earth to heaven. Sable slept against the altar stone with the mercenary's cloak over her, and even asleep her lips moved sometimes, shaping words none of the living remembered.

Callow sat across the fire from Delacroix and finally asked the question that had been riding his spine since the valley.

"Why did you leave the Veil? Truly. You had ten years of safety. You could have hidden her deeper—there are places in those hills no army has ever found. Instead you walked out into the open world carrying the last vessel of a devoured god, toward the capital, toward the thing that eats gods. Men don't do that out of obligation. Obligation runs out at the first burned farm."

Delacroix fed a stick to the fire. For a long moment the only light on his face was orange and shifting.

"I made an oath," he said at last. "Ten years ago. Mid-oath, the god went silent, and I've spent a decade telling myself that silence released me." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were hard and bright and terribly tired. "It didn't. Silence isn't release, priest. It's debt with the ledger torn out. You still owe. You just can't see how much."

"That's not faith," Callow said. "That's guilt wearing faith's coat."

"Maybe." The ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "But guilt got the girl out of Greyhollow alive. Where was your faith when the carts left Saint Verren's?"

The question landed exactly where it was aimed. Callow took it, because he'd earned it, and let it sit in his chest like a swallowed coal. "My faith died in the archive," he said finally. "Not because I stopped believing Veyl existed. Because I read the harvest ledgers, and I understood that belief without action is just a slower way of selling children." He nodded at the sleeping girl. "So now I act. Badly, late, with bad knees and a forged travel seal. But I act. If that's not faith anymore, then I don't know what to call it, and I've stopped needing to."

The fire cracked. Above them, through the broken roof, the stars turned their slow indifferent wheel.

"It knows she's coming," Delacroix said abruptly. "The Crownless. It's been hunting vessels for ten years—it will have felt her cross the Veil. Every ward between here and the capital will be singing our names."

"Yes."

"And the Feast of Unbinding is in nine days. The whole court gathered. The thing at full strength, on home ground, wearing a king."

"Yes."

Delacroix stared into the fire. "Then we're not bringing her to the Feast to hide her," he said slowly, and Callow heard the terrible arithmetic completing itself behind the man's eyes. "We're bringing her because the fragments the Crownless ate—they're still *in* it, aren't they? Swallowed isn't destroyed. Everything it devoured of Veyl is still inside that thing, trapped, screaming."

Callow felt the old awe stir in his chest again, unwelcome as a scar in cold weather. "That is either the most hopeful thing anyone has said to me in four years," he said, "or the most suicidal."

"Can't it be both?"

And from the altar, without opening her eyes, half-dreaming, Sable answered them in the old tongue—one word, clear as a bell struck in an empty church.

Callow translated it anyway, though neither of them needed him to.

"*Enough.*"

Outside, far off along the ridge road, a horn sounded. Once. Then again—closer.
