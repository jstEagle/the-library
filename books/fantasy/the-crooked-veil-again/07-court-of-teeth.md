## 7. Court of Teeth

The capital of Veylmark had a smell Orsen had spent ten years trying to forget: tallow smoke and wet stone and, beneath it all, the faint sweet rot of incense burned too long in closed rooms. Karavel rose out of the river mist like a jaw of black teeth, its spires crowned with the banners of the Feast of Unbinding—white cloth, unmarked, hanging from every window like the city was in mourning for something no one would name.

Orsen hated how well they remembered it.

"Stop gawking," Callow murmured beside them, hunched into a hedge-priest's grey cloak that fit him like an apology. "Gawking gets pilgrims noticed. Pilgrims get searched."

"I'm not gawking." Orsen adjusted the sword at their hip—a mercenary's blade, honest iron, bought with Maren's coin and sharpened on the road. The disguise sat strangely on their shoulders after a decade of goatskin and silence. "I'm counting exits."

"How many?"

"Four we can use. Six that will kill us."

Callow nodded as if this were comforting. Ahead of them, Sable walked between two hired swords Orsen had paid far too much to trust, her small hand wrapped in one of theirs. She wore a servant's livery dyed feast-white, her dark hair braided flat against her skull, and she moved with a strange, terrible calm—the calm of someone who had been told what was coming and had decided, in the way only children decide things, that she would walk toward it rather than be dragged.

She hadn't spoken since dawn. She didn't need to. The fragment of Veyl inside her hummed against Orsen's oath-marks like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and every step closer to the palace made the sound sharper, more urgent, more *afraid*.

*Run*, it had whispered at the border. It had not stopped whispering since.

The queue at the Gate of Knives stretched half a mile: mercenaries, sellswords, free companies come to swear feast-oaths before the throne and collect the king's coin. The Hollow King paid well for blades, everyone said. Paid better than any crown in living memory. No one asked why a monarch so beloved by his soldiers so rarely let them see his face.

Orsen asked anyway, quietly, while the line crawled. "How many companies are inside tonight?"

"Eleven," Callow said. "Maybe twelve hundred armed men within bowshot of the dais." He paused. "And not one of them would lift a finger if the king began eating someone in front of them. I've seen it. They look at their boots. It's a kind of discipline, the way sheep have discipline."

"You've seen it."

"I served in Karavel for six years before they burned my ordination." Callow's voice stayed level, but his knuckles whitened around his staff. "I saw a chamberlain dragged from the feast hall by nothing at all. Unseen hands. The king watched. Smiled. The court smiled with him because smiling is cheaper than screaming." He glanced sideways at Orsen. "You should know what you're walking into. Not the monster. The *audience*. Monsters are simple. Audiences are why monsters survive."

* * *

Inside the walls, the city had dressed itself in white.

Garlands of bone-white ribbon looped across the streets. Children ran underfoot wearing paper crowns, shrieking, playing at kingship. Roast meat turned on spits in every square, and the smell of it nearly drowned the rot-smell Orsen remembered—but not quite. Under everything, faint as a scar, that sweetness lingered. Incense. Temple incense, the kind burned over vessels.

Over children sold to the crown.

Sable stopped walking. Her hand tightened in Orsen's until the bones ground together.

"What is it?" Orsen crouched, mercenary-blank, a hired sword adjusting a strap.

Her eyes were fixed on a temple banner hanging from a balcony—the old sigil of Veyl, the veiled eye, defaced now, the eye scratched out and replaced with a smooth white oval. A mouth with no features. Below it, in gilt letters fresh enough to gleam:

*THE HUNGER IS FED. THE KINGDOM ENDURES.*

Sable looked up at Orsen and said, in the dead tongue of the first kingdoms, one word. Orsen knew it from prayers their grandmother had whispered and priests had died for. It meant *liar*. It also meant *thief*. In the oldest conjugations, it meant *the thing that eats names*.

"I know," Orsen said in the same tongue, rough from disuse. "I know. We go in, we find the rite-chamber, we do what has to be done. And then we burn that banner ourselves."

Something flickered in the child's face—not quite a smile. Close enough. She walked on.

* * *

The Palace of Aldric did not look like a palace anymore. It looked like a wound that had learned to dress itself.

The outer courts were bright with feast-light, musicians tuning, servants hauling wine. But the deeper the procession of sworn companies moved toward the great hall, the whiter everything became—and the emptier. No guards past the third gate. No servants past the fifth. Just long corridors of pale stone where their footsteps came back wrong, doubled, as if something walked beside each of them matching stride.

Orsen felt the oath-marks wake along their forearms, the old bindings heating under their sleeves like coals breathing. Ten years in Greyhollow had taught them to read that heat. It meant proximity. It meant *him*.

"He knows we're here," Orsen breathed.

"No," Callow whispered back. "He knows *Veyl* is here. There's a difference, and our lives depend on it. The Crownless hunts the god's voice, not the god's servants. To it, you're furniture." His eyes flicked to Sable. "She's the meal."

They passed through the last archway into the feast hall, and Orsen understood, finally, why no one spoke of the Hollow King's face.

The hall could seat a thousand. Tonight it held perhaps four hundred—sworn captains, high lords, temple prelates in white—and every one of them sat arranged around a vast empty floor of polished stone, at the far end of which rose the dais. Torches burned along the walls, but their light seemed to *bend* near the throne, curving away, unwilling.

King Aldric sat upon it.

He had been a broad man once; the stories all agreed on that. A soldier-king who had carried his own shield at the border wars, who laughed too loud and drank too deep and loved his kingdom with the simple ferocity of a man who believed kingdoms could be loved. What sat on the dais now wore that man's frame the way a gallows wears a rope. He was thinner than a king had any right to be. His skin had the grey translucence of candle wax held too near the flame. He was dressed in white from crown to boot, unadorned, and he did not move except to breathe, and even the breathing seemed borrowed.

His eyes were the worst. They were open, and they were lit—faintly, gutteringly, like lanterns drowned in deep water, their glow straining up through fathoms of dark. Someone was behind those eyes. Someone very far down, pressing his face against the inside of the glass.

Orsen had imagined a monster. They had prepared themselves for a monster: teeth, shadow, a presence that would make their marks scream. Instead they found this—a ruined man propped on a throne like a scarecrow in feast clothes, and four hundred subjects pretending with all their might that he was still a king.

The herald cried the names of the companies. One by one, captains knelt and swore their feast-oaths, and the king's head inclined by degrees, precise as clockwork, and the Crownless accepted each oath through his mouth without ever quite moving his lips.

Then their company's turn came, and Orsen walked forward with Sable's hand small and burning in theirs, and the distance to the dais shrank step by step, and the drowned lanterns began to swing toward them.

Toward *her*.

The air changed. The torches guttered in unison, a hundred flames flinching like startled birds. Somewhere in the rafters, something exhaled. Orsen felt the oath-marks blaze hot enough to brand through cloth, and Sable made a small sound—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The way a lamb recognizes the shape of the knife.

Ten steps from the dais, the kneeling captain ahead of them rose and withdrew, and it was their turn, and Orsen sank to one knee with Sable pressed close against their side, and looked up—

—and the king looked down.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The drowned lanterns stared through them, through the hall, through the world, hungering at some horizon only it could see. Orsen's knee ached against stone. Callow's staff trembled faintly somewhere behind them. The whole hall held its breath the way audiences do, cheaply, obediently.

Then the king's face *moved*.

Not the smooth mechanical incline. Something else—muscles firing out of order, dragging the ruin of his mouth into shapes it hadn't made in years. His cracked lips parted. No sound came out. But Orsen was close enough, kneeling in the angle of his shadow, watching his face the way no subject was permitted to watch it, and they saw the words form, syllable by silent syllable, in the last flicker of those drowning eyes:

*Kill me.*

His fingers twitched against the armrest—two of them, curling, the smallest gesture, pointing down at himself. *Here. Now. Please.*

Orsen's hand was on their sword-hilt before thought caught up. One draw. One cut. Twelve hundred witnesses and eleven companies of sworn steel, and the thing wearing him would simply pour itself into whatever vessel stood nearest—it needed a body, Callow had said, it needed a *coat*—and the nearest bodies were Sable, and the four hundred souls of the court, and the world beyond.

But gods, the *pleading* in that face. Ten years Orsen had carried hatred like a whetstone, honing it nightly, and here at the end of it was not a monster to hate but a man begging a stranger for a mercy no one in his kingdom dared give him. Pity arrived like a blade between the ribs, unexpected and deep. *Aldric*, Orsen thought, absurdly, learning his name in the moment of his ruin. *Your name is Aldric. I'll remember it. Someone has to.*

The king's lips shaped one word more—*hurry*—and then the light in his eyes went out like a pinched wick.

And the smile came.

It climbed his face from the chin up, slow and total, a smile with too much width and no warmth at all, and when it reached the eyes the drowned lanterns relit—but the light in them was different now, colder, focused, and it fell entirely, weightily, on the child at Orsen's side.

"There," the Hollow King said, and his voice was a chorus of voices wearing one throat, gentle as a lullaby sung in an empty house. "There is the little bell. We have waited so long for the little bell."

Around the hall, four hundred courtiers turned as one to look at Sable, their faces smooth and attentive and terribly, perfectly calm.

Orsen rose slowly, hand off the sword, heart hammering a war-drum, and smiled the empty smile of a hired blade while every instinct screamed.

The Crownless leaned forward on its stolen throne, savoring, and beckoned with one grey finger.

"Bring her closer."
