## 8. The Unbinding Rite

The cage was iron, but the cold came from inside it.

Sable had learned to tell the difference. Iron cold sat on your skin, like a wet cloak. This other cold lived under her ribs, in the place where the god-voice curled like a sleeping ember, and it grew sharper with every mile the wagon rolled away from Greyhollow and toward the city on the sea.

She didn't cry. Crying made the soldiers look at her, and looking at her made them flinch, and flinching men did stupid things. So she folded her hands in her lap and watched the world through the bars: grey fields, grey roads, grey faces that turned away when the wagon passed, as if sorrow were contagious.

*They sold me once already,* she thought, in the old tongue, because thinking it in the living language hurt too much. *The priests put me in a box of silk instead of iron, but it was the same box.*

She had been small enough then to fit in a temple alcove. She remembered candle-smoke and the soft voices of men deciding her future over her head. *The vessel is ripe. The crown is hungry. It is a holy transaction.* They'd washed her feet. That was the part she hated most — they had washed her feet first, gently, like she was something precious being wrapped for shipping.

Now there were no gentle hands. The soldiers fed her bread through the bars without meeting her eyes, and one of them, a young one with a chipped tooth, had whispered something at night when he thought she slept. *Sorry. Sorry. Sorry.* Three words, over and over, like a prayer he'd forgotten how to finish.

Sable held onto that boy's whisper the way Orsen had taught her to hold a knife: close, careful, ready.

*Orsen will come.* She believed it the way she believed in stone. Not because anyone promised. Because Orsen had stood between her and riders once, an old mercenary with aching knees and a face like a closed door, and had said nothing at all — just stood there, until standing was the whole argument.

But the wagon rolled on, and the god inside her stayed quiet, and Sable began to be afraid that this time, even standing would not be enough.

*

The capital rose out of the morning fog like a jaw full of teeth.

Walls first, black with old rain. Then spires, too many, all leaning like drunkards whispering to each other. Then the palace itself, squatting atop the highest hill, crowned in white stone that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. Sable felt it before she understood it — a pulling sensation behind her eyes, as if the whole city were a wound and the palace the thing sucking at it.

Inside her chest, the ember stirred.

Not words yet. Just warmth against the cold, a turning-over, the way a sleeper shifts when they smell home cooking from far off. Sable pressed both hands flat against her sternum.

*I know,* she answered silently. *I feel it too.*

They took her out of the cage at the gate and bound her wrists with silk. Silk again. Even now, even here, someone wanted her handled like a treasure. A woman in grey robes checked her teeth like a horse trader, murmured something about *ripeness*, and wrote in a ledger.

"You are to be presented clean," the woman said. "The Feast is sacred."

Sable looked at her and spoke in the dead tongue, softly: *You will burn your ledger when you understand what you have written in it.*

The woman stepped back as if struck. The guards laughed, uneasy. Nobody translated. Nobody needed to. Some things crossed every border.

They walked her through halls that got grander and emptier. Servants flattened themselves against walls when she passed. Courtiers watched from doorways with expressions she couldn't read — hunger, or fear, or the terrible blankness of people who had stopped feeling either. And everywhere, everywhere, the cold behind her ribs sharpened into something almost like recognition.

By the time they reached the doors of the throne room, Sable understood what the recognition was.

*It knows I'm coming,* she thought. *It has been setting the table for years.*

*

The doors were taller than trees and opened without being touched.

The throne room swallowed sound. Her footsteps died a half-breath after leaving her boots. Banners hung motionless despite the draft. Court lined both walls in perfect silence, hundreds of them, and not one breathed loudly enough to hear.

And on the dais, beneath a canopy of white bone carved into roses — someone had thought that beautiful, once — sat the king.

Sable had imagined a monster. Teeth and shadow and wrongness, the thing from her nightmares that wore Aldric like a coat. What she saw instead was worse: a man. Tall, gaunt, silver-haired, dressed in mourning-black trimmed with gold. He might have been handsome ten years ago. He might have been kind.

His eyes were lanterns drowned in deep water. Light moved in them sometimes, slow and aimless, like fish circling a wreck.

*He's still in there.* The knowledge arrived not as thought but as ache — the ember in her chest flaring hot, straining toward the throne like a hand toward a barred window. *Part of him. Shreds. He's still in there.*

The Crownless smiled.

It did not move his mouth so much as arrange it, the way wind arranges sand. When he spoke, his voice was layered — one voice speaking, and underneath it, faintly, many others, all slightly out of time, a choir heard through a locked door.

"The last vessel." The drowned lanterns found her. "Do you know how long I have waited? How carefully I have cultivated you? Every temple emptied. Every voice harvested. And here you walk to me on your own feet, wearing your little borrowed flame." He tilted his head. "Veyl always did love hiding its treasures in plain clay."

Sable's knees shook. She let them shake. Fear was honest; she would not lie about it. But she lifted her chin the way Orsen did when facing down something with more power and less sense.

She answered in the old tongue, loud enough to fill the silent hall:

*You ate a god in pieces. You will choke on what's left.*

The court flinched as one body. No one had translated. No one needed to. The king laughed — or the thing in the king laughed, a sound like coins poured into a grave — and rose from the throne, descending the dais step by step, unhurried, savoring.

"Bring the rite," he said. "Let us finish the harvest."

*

They arranged her on the floor before the empty throne. Chalk circles. Silver stakes at four points. Priests in grey who moved with the smoothness of long practice — how many children, Sable wondered, how many voices had these hands unwound — chanting in a corrupted half-tongue, the old prayers run backward like water flowing uphill.

The silk bindings burned away. The chalk lines began to glow a color that wasn't quite light. Cold flooded up through the floor into her bones, and the ember inside her screamed — wordless, high, a struck bell — as hooks of hunger sank into its edges and began to pull.

*This is what happened to the others,* Sable thought, tears finally coming, freezing on her lashes. *This is how it felt. Alone in a room full of people, being unmade while everyone watches.*

And then — through the pain, through the pulling — she felt something else.

A resonance.

Deep beneath the throne room, deeper than chalk, deeper than the palace foundations, something answered the bell-cry of Veyl's fragment. Faint. Frayed. Torn nearly past recognition. But *familiar*. The same note, sung from a broken throat.

*Aldric.*

What remained of the king was still in there. Shredded across years of slow devouring, scattered thin through the Crownless's vast dark hunger — but present. And every shred of him still carried the shape of an oath, sworn long ago at a real altar by a real young man: to guard, to serve, to hold the line against the hunger should it ever wear his crown.

He had sworn it knowing. He had sworn it anyway.

And oaths, Sable realized, with a clarity that cut through terror like dawn through fog — oaths do not die just because the one who swore them is devoured. They wait. They persist like seeds in frozen ground. All they need is a voice to call them awake.

The ember surged against the hooks. Not struggling free — reaching. Pouring itself toward those scattered shreds of Aldric, touching each one, singing to each one the same two syllables in the oldest grammar of the oldest tongue:

*Unbind. Unbind. Unbind.*

The chalk circle flickered.

One of the grey priests faltered mid-chant, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. A silver stake hummed, then rang, then split down the middle with a sound like ice cracking on a spring river.

"What is it doing?" someone shouted from the wall of courtiers. "Someone make it stop—"

The king had stopped smiling.

For one moment — one endless, ringing moment — the drowned lanterns of his eyes cleared, and Aldric looked out of his own face at the small girl on the floor of his stolen hall, and his mouth shaped three words without any voice at all:

*Help me. Please.*

Then the Crownless roared back up through him like floodwater through a broken dam, and every banner in the room snapped straight, and the temperature dropped so hard breath bloomed white from a thousand mouths at once.

"It lies," the layered voice thundered, shaking dust from the rafters. "There is nothing left to save. There is only ME—"

But the circle was already breaking. The shreds of Aldric were waking, answering, gathering — drawn to Veyl's fragment like iron filings to a lodestone, weaving themselves into a single thread of mortal will threaded through the monster's heart. Sable could feel it all now, could feel the rite reversing, the harvest becoming a severing, the devouring becoming a tearing loose.

It needed a price. The old rites always did. Something freely given, freely broken.

She didn't know what. She was eight years old and lying bleeding-light on the floor of hell, holding a god's last ember in her cupped hands and a dying king's last will in her voice, and she did not know what the price would be —

— but the great doors of the throne room slammed open behind her, and she knew the stride of the boots before she knew anything else at all.
