## 9. Where the Hunger Ends

The doors of the throne room closed behind Sable like a mouth swallowing.

Orsen hit them a heartbeat later, shoulder-first, and the iron did not so much as shudder. The sigils carved into their face—hungering mouths, crownless heads, spirals of teeth—glowed a dull amber, drinking the impact. Through the gap beneath, torchlight flickered. Beyond it, he could hear the court: hundreds of voices murmuring in unison, a sound like wind through a field of dry bones.

"Orsen." Callow caught his arm, hedge-priest's robes torn at the hem, blood on his collar that might have been his own or someone else's. "Not that way. The servants' stair—it comes up behind the dais. I served here once, before they took my orders from me. Before I understood what we were feeding."

"How long do we have?"

Callow's face did something complicated. "Until the rite finishes. And the rite is fast. They've practiced it nine times."

Nine vessels. Nine children harvested before Sable. Orsen had spent ten years telling himself the god had simply gone quiet, that silence was a thing gods were allowed. He had built a whole life on that lie, in a valley where magic couldn't reach him, tending goats and drinking to forget. The lie was done now. It had died somewhere on the road, in Callow's ruined shrine, in the king's drowned-lantern eyes mouthing *kill me* across a feast hall.

He drew the sword he'd sworn never to draw again. The oath-marks along his forearm flared white-hot at the touch of the hilt, as if Veyl's fragment in Sable had left an echo in him too—or as if the marks themselves remembered what they were for.

"The stair," Orsen said. "Lead."

* * *

The servants' stair smelled of tallow and old incense, and it climbed through the bones of the palace like a throat. Twice they passed hollowed servants standing motionless in alcoves, faces smooth as unfired clay, and twice Callow pressed a cracked icon of Veyl against his own chest and whispered something that made the things turn away, slow as weather vanes.

"They can't see us," Callow breathed. "The Crownless looks through its people, not with them. But the moment the rite ends—"

"I know." Orsen pushed past him. "Move."

The stair ended at a door of black oak banded in silver, and beyond it, light—the terrible, honeyed light of the Feast of Unbinding, pouring through the throne room's high windows. Orsen put his eye to the seam.

The court stood in perfect ranks, nobles and soldiers and priests, all of them swaying gently in time like kelp in a current. Their eyes were open but nothing looked out of them. On the dais, upon a throne of fused bone and gold, sat King Aldric—and around him the air itself was wrong, bent inward, as though the room were a wound and the throne its deepest point.

Sable knelt before the dais. She was so small. Eight years old and mud-streaked and utterly alone in a hall of puppets, and she was not crying. Her chin was up. Her hands were spread flat on the marble, and from her fingertips thin threads of pale light ran up the steps toward the king, trembling like harp strings.

And inside those threads, Orsen heard it—a voice he had not heard in ten years, worn thin as river glass but unmistakably itself.

*Veyl.*

*Little one,* the god-fragment said through Sable's mouth, in the dead tongue of the first kingdoms, and every scar on Orsen's body ached at once. *I am here. I am still here.*

The Crownless rose.

It did not stand so much as pour upward out of Aldric, a shape of absence wearing the king's outline, taller than any man, crowned in nothing at all—which was somehow worse than any crown. Where its face should have been there was depth, a hunger with geometry, and when it spoke, the whole court spoke with it, a thousand throats in ragged chorus:

**THE LAST VESSEL. THE LAST CRUMB OF THE QUIET GOD. COME HOME TO MY TEETH.**

"No," Sable said—in the old tongue, in plain trade-speech, in both at once—and the threads of light thickened. "You don't get his voice anymore. You don't get *anyone's* voice anymore."

The rite had begun. Orsen could feel it in his teeth, in the oath-marks burning up his arms like brands. Whatever she was doing, she was doing it from inside the enemy's own hall, unbinding the devourer from the devoured, and the Crownless knew it. Its attention swung toward her like a lighthouse beam.

Orsen kicked the door open and walked into the light.

* * *

Later, he would not remember deciding to fight. He would only remember the fighting itself, arriving in pieces:

The nearest guards turning, faces smooth, swords rising in perfect unison. His blade taking the first one's wrist, the second one's knee, moving the way Maren had taught him twenty years ago in another life—"the sword is just a promise kept quickly, boy"—the oath-marks blazing so bright the hollowed soldiers flinched from him as if from the sun.

Callow at his shoulder, swinging a censer like a flail, holy smoke streaming, screaming scripture in a voice cracked with ten years of disgrace. A noblewoman lunging at Orsen with a fruit knife and no expression at all, and Orsen catching her wrist, gentle even now, putting her down unconscious rather than worse, because these people were not enemies—they were hostages wearing their own bodies.

The stairs of the dais. Seven steps. He took them three at a time while the Crownless turned its faceless head and the whole court's murmur became a roar:

**THE BOUND ONE. THE OATH-BREAKER WHO NEVER BROKE. TEN YEARS HIDING IN THE DEAD VALLEY LIKE A DOG UNDER A PORCH.**

"You know me," Orsen said, climbing.

**I KNOW EVERYTHING THAT WAS EVER FED TO ME. YOUR GOD SCREAMED BEAUTIFULLY, MERCENARY. PIECE BY PIECE. WOULD YOU LIKE TO HEAR HOW IT ENDED?**

"No," Orsen said, reaching the top. "But I'll show you how *you* end."

The dais was small, a platform of black stone above the court, close enough now to see everything. Close enough to see Aldric.

The king was still in there. That was the horror of it—that was the thing Orsen would carry forever. The Crownless poured out of him like smoke from a lamp, but the man remained beneath, folded small inside his own body, hands gripping the arms of the bone throne, and his eyes—drowned lanterns, yes, but with a spark guttering at the bottom of each one—found Orsen's face.

His lips moved. No sound came out. But Orsen had watched men die for thirty years and had learned to read the last words they shaped.

*Please.*

Sable knelt between them, threads of light running from her fingers into the king's chest, sweat shining on her small face, the god-fragment inside her singing now—not words anymore, just a note, a single sustained note like the last bell of a drowning temple. The Crownless writhed above them all, its outline fraying where the light touched it, and it screamed without a mouth, and the court screamed with it, and the windows bowed inward in their frames.

**IT CANNOT FINISH THE RITE,** the hunger thundered, and Orsen understood suddenly, horribly, that it was afraid. **THE RITE DEMANDS A PRICE. AN OATH FREELY BROKEN. AND THIS ONE—** the faceless depth swung toward Orsen **—THIS ONE HAS KEPT ITS FILTHY OATH FOR TWENTY YEARS. IT WILL NOT PAY. THEY NEVER PAY.**

Orsen looked down at his arms.

The oath-marks were burning. Every vow he had ever sworn to Veyl was written there in script older than crowns: *protection, service, remembrance, return.* Twenty years of keeping them. Ten years of hiding because he couldn't bear that the god had stopped answering—as if silence were faithfulness, as if keeping promises to a corpse were devotion instead of fear.

The rite demanded an oath freely broken.

And there it was. There was the price. Not blood. Not the child. Not the king.

Him.

"Callow," Orsen said quietly. "Get Sable when it's done. Whatever happens, get her out."

"What are you—" Callow's face went grey. "No. Orsen, the marks are your binding—if you break the oath, you break—"

"Everything I am to the god. Yes." Orsen knelt beside Sable. Her eyes flicked up to his, wide and brown and terrified and trusting, and inside them the fragment of Veyl looked back at him, and for one impossible moment he felt the god's attention settle on him fully for the first time in ten years—felt it *recognize* him.

*I'm sorry,* Orsen said, in the dead tongue, badly, the way you speak to someone you loved and abandoned. *For the valley. For the goats and the drink and the ten years. For thinking silence meant you'd stopped needing me.*

The note in Sable's throat wavered. Warmed. If a worn-thin shard of a devoured god could be said to smile, it smiled.

*You never stopped being mine,* Veyl said. *That was always the problem, little mercenary. Contracts end. This doesn't.*

"Good," Orsen said aloud. "Then this won't hurt it."

He stood. He faced the Crownless, which had gone very still, the whole court holding its breath with it, and he raised his marked arms over his head like a man offering rain to a drought.

"Veyl," he said, and his voice carried to the rafters, to the hollowed thousands, to whatever dark country the god's shredded remainder occupied. "I swore you protection, service, remembrance, return. I kept every word for twenty years. Hear me now, wherever you are: I break them. All of them. Freely. Gladly. Take the binding—all of it, every year, every drop—and let it go where I send it."

The marks screamed.

White fire sheeted off his forearms, and Orsen poured it forward—not striking, not aiming, just *giving*, twenty years of kept promises becoming one magnificent broken one, ten years of guilt transmuted in a single instant into fuel. The light struck the threads between Sable and the king and traveled them like lightning down a wire, straight into the place where the Crownless gripped Aldric's soul with its thousand invisible teeth.

The hunger shrieked—a sound with no sound in it, a tearing heard only in the marrow—and Orsen felt it seize the broken oath and try to swallow that too, try to make his sacrifice into food, and for one black moment the whole world tilted toward its hunger—

—and then Veyl's fragment moved.

The last crumb of the quiet god, the piece that had hidden eight years in a temple orphan's voice, threw itself down the threads after the broken oath, into the Crownless's open depth, and the two annihilations met.

There was no explosion. There was a *silence*—a silence so total that Orsen heard his own heart stumble, heard Callow drop the censer, heard, distantly, a goat bleating in some farmyard miles away. The Crownless unraveled from the edges inward, its outline fraying into motes, its thousand borrowed mouths falling open and empty, and the last thing it did—Orsen would swear this to his grave—was look almost relieved.

Then it was gone. And the court fell.

Hundreds of bodies dropped like cut marionettes, then began, slowly, terribly, to breathe—to sob—to wake. All across the hall, people who had been furniture for years opened their eyes and found themselves kneeling on cold stone with no memory of how long.

On the throne, King Aldric collapsed forward.

Orsen caught him before his skull met the steps. The king weighed nothing, a coat with no one in it, and then—gradually, like frost melting—he weighed everything. He was mortal. He was sixty years old and weeping into Orsen's shoulder with the raw, ugly, human sobs of a man who has just learned what was done with his hands.

"My wife," Aldric gasped. "My daughters. How long—Gods, how *long*—"

"Ten years," Orsen said. "Maybe more. It doesn't matter now. You're the only one in there. You're free."

"And the god?" The king's ruined eyes searched Orsen's face. "The child said—I heard her, inside, there was a voice—"

"Gone." The word cost more than the breaking had. Orsen looked down at his bare forearms, smooth and unmarked, pale where the script had lived for half his life. Nothing. No warmth, no whisper, no ache in the scars. Just a man's arms, in a man's hall, at the end of a war. "They destroyed each other. It's finished."

Sable pushed herself upright on the dais, swaying, grey-faced, drained to the last drop. She looked at the empty air where the Crownless had been. She looked at Orsen's empty arms. And then she crossed the stones and put her small hand in his large one and held on, and said nothing at all, because she didn't need to.

Below, the court was waking in waves, and already someone was shouting for physicians, for the guard, for the queen dowager, and Brother Callow stood amid the fallen with tears cutting tracks through the smoke-grime on his face, laughing and praying in the same breath.

King Aldric gripped Orsen's sleeve with sudden strength. "They will need someone to tell them what happened. Someone who saw. Someone who—" He stopped, swallowed, started again, a king learning the shape of his own voice. "Stay. Both of you. I will give you anything."

Orsen looked at Sable. Sable looked at the great doors, thrown open now onto morning, onto a city stirring unaware that its nightmare had ended—and beyond the city, days north, a green valley where the silence was waiting.

"We'll talk about it," Orsen said. "Over breakfast. I'm told kings eat well."

And far away, in a valley that forgot, a door stood unlocked for the first time in ten years—though no one yet knew why.
