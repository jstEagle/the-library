## 10. Home Is a Door Left Open

The king's letter arrived in Greyhollow on the back of a mule, which Orsen thought was fitting. Kingdoms rebuilt themselves the way farms did—slowly, stubbornly, one load at a time.

*To Orsen Delacroix, once-sworn of Veyl, and to Sable, Voice-Bearer,* it began, in Aldric's own hand, the letters still slightly crooked where his fingers hadn't yet remembered how to hold a quill without shaking.

*The temples want you both. The priests have already begun arguing over who gets to build your statues. I have told them nothing, because I owe you more than stone.*

*The harvest fields in the east are planted again. It took three seasons and most of what remained of the treasury, but the granaries are full and no child goes to sleep counting the wrong kind of teeth. I say this not as accomplishment but as apology—every field I plant is a field the Crownless burned while wearing my hands.*

*I do not ask you to come back. I understand why you won't. But I wanted you to know that the kingdom remembers how to be ruled now, mostly. There are days I forget my own name and stand in the throne room listening for something older than me to answer, and there is nothing there, and that nothing is the finest sound I have ever heard.*

*Tell Sable the gardens in the capital bloom white this year. She'll know what I mean by it.*

*Aldric, King of the Sundered Throne, First of His Name to Remember*

Orsen read it twice by the fire, then folded it into the tin box beneath the floorboards where they kept the things worth keeping: Maren's carved goat whistle, a hedge-priest's cracked prayer bead, a child's first attempt at writing her own name in charcoal on slate.

Sable was out with the goats. She'd be back before dark. She was always back before dark now—not because anyone told her to be, but because she'd learned, somewhere in the wreckage of the last two years, that doors left open were invitations, and someone had finally taught her the difference between an invitation and a trap.

Orsen stepped outside into the long gold light of late summer. The valley lay quiet around the cottage, the way it always did—the particular silence of Greyhollow, magic-dead and memory-soft, where the world's noise couldn't follow them down the switchback roads. Ten years ago that silence had been a wall Orsen hid behind. Now it was just... quiet. The difference mattered more than any word could carry.

Down the slope, Sable was perched on the fence rail, talking to the goats.

That was the thing that still caught in Orsen's chest sometimes, even now. *Talking.* Not the dead tongue of the first kingdoms, not the fragment of a devoured god whispering through a child's throat in words that made old scars ache. Just a girl's voice, high and ordinary and entirely her own, telling a belligerent nanny-goat named Turnip exactly what she thought of her manners.

"—and if you headbutt me one more time I'm telling Orsen, and Orsen will *definitely* make you into stew, don't think they won't—"

"They absolutely will not," Orsen called down. "Stew requires effort."

Sable turned, grinning, mud on her knees and a burr in her hair. Eleven years old now, taller by a hand since the winter, with a gap in her smile where a tooth had finally come in after years of waiting. "Turnip says you're all talk."

"Turnip can say whatever she likes. She's the only one here allowed to lie."

The girl slid off the fence and came up the hill at a run, the way she did everything—full tilt, no hesitation, as though the world had been returned to her and she intended to use every piece of it. She skidded to a stop in front of Orsen and held out her hand. In her palm sat a wildflower, white-petaled, slightly crushed from the journey in a fist.

"From the ridge," she said. "Where we first saw the riders." She said it easily now, the way children learn to carry heavy things lightly when the adults around them stop flinching at the weight. "I thought the ridge should give something back."

Orsen took the flower. Their throat did something complicated and unhelpful. "It did. It gave us you running downhill instead of away."

"That's not poetic."

"I'm a mercenary, not a poet."

"You're a *farmer*," Sable said, with the deep scorn only children can produce, "who used to be a mercenary, who used to be a *sworn blade*, who used to—" She stopped herself, and her voice gentled in a way that always undid Orsen more than any accusation. "Who used to carry too much alone. And now you don't."

"No," Orsen agreed. "Now I don't."

They stood together in the last of the light, and Orsen thought about the letter in the tin box, and the temples, and the statues.

* * *

The envoys had come in spring. Three of them, robed in temple white, riding down into the valley with the careful reverence of people approaching a shrine—which, Orsen supposed, was exactly how they saw the cottage.

They had found Sable watering the garden and dropped to their knees in the dirt.

"Voice-Bearer," the eldest had breathed. "Last vessel of the god Veyl. We have searched—"

"I know," Sable had said. "You've been searching for two years. You sent letters. We sent them back unopened. This is the part where you ignore that and keep talking."

The priest had blinked. Orsen had bitten down hard on a laugh and lost.

"The temples are rebuilt," the man pressed on, recovering. "The rites restored. The god's voice must be housed properly, honored properly—a vessel such as you cannot simply live in a *valley*, tending *goats*. There are ceremonies. There is purpose. There is—"

"A cage," Sable had said.

"Sanctuary."

"I've lived in sanctuary." Her voice hadn't risen at all. That was the frightening thing about her sometimes, the thing that made Orsen's blood run cold with pride: she never raised her voice, because she'd learned young that the people who took everything from her had never needed to shout either. "Sanctuary is where they kept me before they sold my voice to the crown. Sanctuary is where the priests decided I was a door instead of a girl. I know exactly what your word means, and I would like you to leave my garden now, please, before I say something the god would have disapproved of."

They had turned to Orsen then, those white-robed men, seeking the adult, the authority, the sworn blade who would surely see reason.

Orsen had leaned on the garden fence and said, "You heard her. Leave the garden."

"But—the vessel—"

"She's not a vessel. She's Sable. She hates turnips, cheats shamelessly at cards, and cries at sad songs when she thinks nobody's watching. If you come back, I will meet you at the valley border, and I will not be holding a hoe."

They had left. They always left. And every time, Orsen waited for the doubt to come—the old familiar whisper that said *you're keeping her from greatness, from purpose, from the god's work*—and every time, it didn't come. Because Orsen had broken one oath already, the great and terrible one, and learned the truth underneath it:

Belonging was never granted by contract. Not divine, not otherwise. It was chosen. Chosen in sacrifice, chosen in small daily acts, chosen every morning when a girl came running uphill through the goats and a scarred old mercenary pretended not to be glad.

No temple could grant that. No god could. That was rather the point of gods, maybe—or the point of losing them.

* * *

Autumn came early that year, the way it liked to. On the first cold night, Orsen built the fire up big and they played cards at the table, and Sable cheated, and got caught, and argued her case with the full gravity of a barrister, and lost, and demanded a rematch.

Halfway through the second deal, she went quiet.

Orsen looked up. The girl was staring at her own hands, spread flat on the table, and there was something old and far away in her face.

"Sometimes," she said, "I try to remember what it felt like. When the god was in me. And I can't. It's like trying to remember a dream from when I was six." She looked up. "Is that bad? Is that forgetting?"

Orsen set down the cards. Thought carefully. They had gotten better at that—thinking carefully before speaking, a skill ten years of silence and two years of parenthood had jointly hammered into them.

"When I broke the oath," Orsen said, "when I poured everything into severing the Crownless from Aldric—I felt Veyl go. All the way through me, like a bell being struck one last time. And do you know what I felt after? Nothing. Silence." A pause. "And it wasn't empty, Sable. That's the secret nobody tells you. Silence isn't empty. It's just room. Room for whatever you choose to put in it."

Sable considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to all large questions. "What did you put in yours?"

Orsen looked around the cottage—at the tin box under the floorboards, the mended window Maren's brother had helped them fit last spring, the second chair that had been added without discussion or ceremony, the way important things arrive. At the girl across the table with flour still on her sleeve from the morning's baking.

"You," Orsen said. "Mostly you."

Sable's face did something complicated, the way faces do when they're trying very hard not to cry and mostly succeeding. Then she reached across and stole three cards from Orsen's hand while they weren't looking.

"Hey—"

"Cheating," she said, wiping her eyes with her free hand and grinning with the gap-toothed smile. "You said Turnip's the only one allowed to lie. You never said anything about cheating."

* * *

Winter. Snow on the ridge, smoke from the chimney, the goats complaining in their shed. A letter went south in the first supply cart, in Orsen's blunt soldier's hand:

*Your Majesty—we received your letter. We are not coming to the capital. The temples will have to make their statues out of someone else. Tell the gardeners the white flowers were appreciated. Tell yourself, from both of us, that the nothing you hear in the throne room is the sound of a job well done. Guard your sleep. Some hungers leave echoes.*

*P.S. Sable says the next letter should include the recipe for honey cakes. This is not negotiable.*

And on the first truly deep night of winter, when the snow came down soft and endless and the whole valley held its breath, Orsen stood at the window with a cup of something warming and watched the dark.

Somewhere out there, the world went on—kingdoms, temples, the slow repair of everything broken. Somewhere out there, a king slept badly and woke grateful anyway. Somewhere out there, Brother Callow was probably drunk in some respectable tavern, retelling the story of the Feast of Unbinding with himself cast considerably braver than he'd been, and Orsen found they didn't mind at all.

But here, in the valley that forgot, the silence wasn't hiding anymore. It was just home.

Behind them, Sable's voice drifted from the loft where she was supposed to be asleep—murmuring to herself, making up stories the way she did, ordinary words in an ordinary tongue, and every one of them hers.

Orsen finished the cup. Banked the fire. Left the door unlatched, the way they always did now.

A door left open wasn't an invitation to danger anymore. It was just a promise: *whatever comes, it can find us here, and we will be enough.*

Outside, far up on the snow-covered ridge, something glinted—briefly, faintly, gone between one blink and the next. Starlight on ice, Orsen told themselves. Only starlight.

They went to bed anyway with the old knife within reach, just in case the world had one more lesson to teach them about borrowed time.
