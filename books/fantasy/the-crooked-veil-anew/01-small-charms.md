## 1. Small Charms

The trick to selling illegal blessings, my mother taught me before the Concord took her, is to make them look like nothing at all. A blessing should be small enough to swallow without chewing. A charm should look like it was made by accident, by a bored woman with cold fingers and a stall she can't afford to heat.

So that's what I sold, on the third night of Frostwane, in the night market of Vessengard: nothing at all.

Copper rings polished dull so they'd pass for old luck. Knotted threads in colors the Concord hadn't gotten around to outlawing — they'd banned red for protection, blue for safe passage, but green knots were still legal, technically, because the last assessor couldn't decide what green was *for*. I sold a great many green knots. People will pay for anything if you let them believe the law simply hasn't noticed it yet.

"Charms," I called, in the flat voice I saved for crowds. "Charms against ordinary misfortune. No magic in them, sirs, ladies — none whatsoever. The Concord has my word."

That always got a laugh. The joke being, of course, that my word was worth exactly as much as my name, which wasn't mine. Silas Okafor belonged to the Temple of the Quiet Ledger, same as my mother's hands had, same as her mother's spine had, all the way back to the debt that started it — some ancestor who borrowed from the gods when borrowing from gods was still a thing you could do, and left the bill to us like an heirloom. Twelve years I'd been paying it off at four coppers a day plus whatever the temple skimmed for administrative grace. Eleven years, seven months, and change to go. I did the arithmetic every morning the way other people pray.

A woman stopped at my stall, the kind of customer I liked best: tired, respectable, and lying to herself about something. She picked up a green knot and turned it over like it might confess.

"What's this one for?"

"Whatever you need it for," I said. "It's just thread."

She smiled the way people do when you've told them exactly the lie they came to buy. "Just thread," she agreed, and paid me two coppers instead of one, and walked away holding it like a candle flame.

I want to say I felt guilty. Mostly I felt warm, in the way of a fire you know is burning down someone else's house. The blessings worked. That was the trouble with them. I never learned anything grand from my mother — no rites, no true names — but she taught me how to listen under a prayer until you find the part of it that's real, and then you tie that part into a knot, and the knot holds. It isn't magic, not the way the Concord means it. It's more like... plumbing. You're not making the water. You're just the pipe.

The Concord does not distinguish between pipes and floods. The Concord hanged my mother for a flood.

* * *

Frostwane nights in Vessengard are the color of old pewter, and the market glows under it like embers in ash: paper lanterns, fry-bread smoke, the braziers of the tea sellers hissing when snow hit them. I worked the corner near the fountain because the fountain was dry year-round — drained by city ordinance after the Purge, since standing water was said to carry "resonance" — and there's a particular kind of customer who likes buying charms beside a dead fountain. A certain defiance in it. A little private middle finger to the Concord, purchased for one copper.

Business was slow until the beggar came.

He came limping up the Row past midnight, when even the lantern-sellers were packing up, and he didn't stop at my stall so much as collapse against it, folding down onto my crate of unsold rings like his legs had been repossessed. Up close he smelled of wet wool and something underneath it, sweeter and worse, the smell I'd learned at my mother's knee meant *this body is finishing its accounts.*

"You're the charm woman," he said. His voice had gravel and holes in it.

"I'm the thread woman."

"My daughter." He said it the way you'd set something fragile on a counter. "In Corrow Ward. Fever ward, the charity house. She's nine." He fumbled in his coat and produced a coin purse that weighed nothing at all. "I've got three coppers and a button."

"The fever ward doesn't take charms," I said. "Takes donations. Take your three coppers to them."

"They don't take enough." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that I found myself leaning over the stall toward him, which was already a mistake. Rule one of the family business: never touch the dying. They ask for things. Rule two: they ask anyway.

"What do you want?" I asked, because apparently rule two applied.

"Not for me." He pushed the three coppers across my crate. "For her. Just — say something. Over her name. You're one of the ones who can still hear, aren't you? I can tell. You've got the listening face."

I have a face like everyone else's, is what I wanted to say. I have a face the temple owns. But my hands had already taken the coppers, which is the oldest sin in my line, and he was looking at me with the terrible patience of a man who has run out of everything except asking.

"Her name," I said.

"Maret. Maret Oduya."

And here is where I should tell you what I actually did, because it matters, and because I still don't fully understand it. There was no knot. There was no rite. I took his prayer the way I take all of them — reached under the words for the real thing underneath, the raw wire of it — but this time the wire was live. This time, when I closed my hand around *let her live, please, whoever's left, please,* something answered.

Not me. Not my plumbing. Something turned over in the dark under the city, under the dead fountain, under twelve years of silence, and looked at me through the beggar's fevered eyes, and said — not in sound, in the way a bell says things after the striking —

***Heard.***

The word went through me like cold water through a cracked pipe. Every lantern on the Row guttered at once. For half a heartbeat the whole market smelled of woodsmoke and myrrh, which is the smell of temples that haven't existed since before I was born, and somewhere very far away and very close, something enormous exhaled.

Then it was gone, and the beggar was just a beggar again, blinking, three coppers poorer, alive and dying as before.

"What," he whispered, "was that?"

"Thread," I said. My voice came out wrong. "Green thread. Very lucky."

He smiled at me like I'd handed him the moon on credit and limped off toward Corrow Ward, and I sat down hard on my stool and did not sell another thing all night.

Because here's what kept circling me like a moth around a dead lamp: gods are dead. Everyone knows it. The Concord killed them a generation ago — that was the whole point of the Concord, the founding miracle, the one story every temple tells now with straight faces. The gods died of their own excess, or were executed, depending on which pulpit you prefer; either way, prayers go nowhere now, which is why we have ledgers instead. Debts instead of devotions. The Quiet Ledger keeps perfect accounts and answers no one.

Except something had answered. Something old and vast and *hungry for listening*, something that had heard a dying man's three coppers' worth of prayer and said *heard* like it had been waiting thirty years in the dark for anyone to speak to it at all.

I packed my stall with shaking hands. I tied my green knots into a bundle. And I told myself the only sensible lie available: that I was tired, that I'd imagined it, that tomorrow I would go to the temple, pay my four coppers, keep my head down, and never think about the word *heard* again.

* * *

I lasted until the fountain.

I was halfway home along the empty Row, boots crunching frost, when I passed the dry fountain and heard — felt — the word again, fainter, like a bell heard through a wall. *Heard.* And beneath it, patient as sediment, something else. Not words yet. More like the shape a word makes before it decides to exist. Attention. Enormous, ancient attention, settled on me the way a cat settles on the one warm spot in a room.

Every instinct I owned — and I owned good ones, I'd inherited them along with the debt — said *walk away, Silas, walk home, lock your door, be nobody.* Being nobody was the whole of my religion. Twelve years of small charms and smaller compromises, and I had gotten very good at the art of being overlooked.

But I stood at the edge of that dead fountain in the falling snow, and I thought about my mother at the scaffold, and the way she'd looked out at the crowd — not afraid, exactly. Listening. Like she'd finally heard something the rest of us couldn't, right at the end.

"Who's there?" I said aloud, to a fountain, like a madwoman.

The snow stopped falling.

Not everywhere. Just above the fountain basin — a column of flakes hanging in the air, each one lit from within by a light that wasn't light, and in that held breath of a moment I saw what was looking at me: a woman made of hearth-ash and patience, grey as banked coals, with eyes like the last warmth in a room. She was seated in the dry basin as though it were a throne, as though she'd been sitting there since the Purge, as though thirty years were a long afternoon.

She did not speak. She didn't need to. The word arrived the way dawn arrives:

***Veilkeeper.***

Then the snow fell again, all at once, and the basin was empty, and I was alone in Vessengard with a word I didn't understand and a heart going like a drum in a procession.

I ran home. Of course I ran home. I am, whatever else is true, a practical woman.

But I did not sleep. I sat by my window with my bundle of green knots in my lap, watching the snow come down over a city that believed its gods were dead, and I counted the hours until the temple opened, because there was one thing I knew with absolute certainty:

In the morning, I was going to ask the Ledger's priests what a veilkeeper was.

And some cold, clear part of me — the part that had watched my mother die for less than this — already knew that asking would be the second-worst mistake of my life.

The first, presumably, was answering.
