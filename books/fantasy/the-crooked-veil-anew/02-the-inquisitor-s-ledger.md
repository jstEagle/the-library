## 2. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The Concord says miracles don't happen. It says this the way a man with his fingers in his ears says he can't hear you — loudly, repeatedly, and with increasing desperation.

I had been an inquisitor for eleven years, and in that time I had confiscated four hundred and six illegal charms, broken up two smuggling rings, and witnessed exactly zero genuine acts of divinity. This was, I was assured by my superiors, not because miracles were rare but because they were impossible. The gods were dead. The Concord had killed them a generation ago, or sealed them away, or simply outlasted them — the official histories disagreed on the mechanism but never on the conclusion. Faith was a debt that had been called in, and Vessengard had paid.

Which is why, on the morning after the night market miracle, I found myself standing in High Inquisitor Serane's office wondering why she looked so unhappy about it.

"You heard the report," she said. Not a question. Serane's questions always arrived pre-answered; anything else was just her testing your nerve.

"A dying beggar at the Saltmarket stalls," I said. "Prayed to a god with no name. His lung-rot reversed itself. He walked out of the market under his own power. Three witnesses of good standing."

"Four." She slid a paper across her desk. "And one of them swears the beggar spoke the god's name before the healing. Ashen-Mother. Which is interesting, Vale, because Ashen-Mother has no temple, no cult, and no entry in any Concord registry of extinct faiths. She isn't even supposed to be dead properly. She's supposed to be *forgotten*."

"And yet someone remembered her."

"Someone did." Serane leaned back. She was a narrow woman, grey as ledger-paper, with the kind of patience that made you want to confess things just to fill the silence. "I'm giving this to you because you have a reputation for thoroughness, and because thoroughness, in this case, means finding nothing. Understand me? A sick man got better. Happens more often than the physicians like to admit. You will trace the rumor to its source, you will determine it was fraud or coincidence, and you will file a report saying so. Cleanly. Quickly."

"And if it wasn't?"

Something crossed her face then — fast, gone before I could name it. I would spend weeks trying to identify it later, and when I finally did, I would wish I hadn't.

"Then you'll bring it to me," she said, "before you bring it to anyone else. That's an order, Vale, not a courtesy."

I took the assignment the way I took all of them: with a nod and a private inventory of everything wrong with it. I'd gotten good at that inventory over the years. It lived in the back of my skull like a tenant who'd stopped paying rent.

* * *

The Saltmarket by daylight was a different animal than the Saltmarket by night. At night it breathed smoke and spice and the low murmur of commerce conducted in half-voices. By day it was all scrubbed stalls and licensed vendors and the particular blankness of people pretending they hadn't seen anything unusual, thank you, officer, nothing unusual here, ever.

I started with the witnesses. Two of them told me nothing useful and enjoyed telling me very much. The third, a fishwife named Maren Ostrow, looked at my badge and said, "You're going to arrest whoever did this, aren't you?"

"That depends on what they did."

"Healed a dying man. That's what they did. And if that's a crime, then maybe ask yourself who wrote the law." She spat, with impressive accuracy, into the gutter between us. "Stall forty-one. The charm-seller. Little woman, dark skin, eyes like she's already forgiven you for whatever you're about to do. Been there years. Sells trinkets with the temple stamp, legal as bread. But folk say—" Maren lowered her voice, though we were alone "—folk say some of her charms actually work."

"Folk say a lot of things."

"Folk said a dying man stood up last night too," she said. "And here we are."

* * *

Stall forty-one was shuttered when I first came to it, which was either coincidence or the fastest rumor network in Vessengard. I came back the next morning, early, wearing no badge and my least official coat, and found the stall open and its proprietor arranging little wooden tokens on a cloth with the care of a jeweler laying out stones.

Silas Okafor. I knew the name before she gave it to me, though I didn't know yet how well I was going to come to know it. She was smaller than Maren's description suggested, quick-handed, with the unhurried manner of someone who had learned that hurrying attracted attention. Her stall was a study in legality: blessing-tokens stamped with the Concord temple's seal, luck-knots woven from sanctioned thread, prayer-cords blessed by priests whose god was the Concord itself. Nothing heretical. Nothing dangerous. Everything, I thought, precisely boring enough to be a disguise.

"Morning," she said, without looking up. "Tokens are a copper, knots are three, and if you're here about last night, I sold that man nothing but a prayer-cord, which he paid for honestly, and whatever happened after is between him and whatever listened."

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You've been standing there for ten seconds deciding whether to show me the badge in your left pocket or pretend to be a buyer." She looked up then, and yes — the eyes were exactly as advertised. "Save us both the theater. Badge. Please. It's cold and you're making my customers nervous."

I showed her the badge. She examined it with the mild interest of someone reading a menu at a restaurant she couldn't afford.

"Inquisitor Vale," I said. "Concord Office of Inquiry."

"Mm. And what does the Office of Inquiry inquire about today? Because if it's unlicensed blessings, you're wasting your morning. Everything on this cloth has a stamp."

"If it were unlicensed blessings, yes. It isn't. Last night a man named Corvin Tull was dying of lung-rot at the edge of this market. Witnesses say he prayed aloud, and something answered. I'm tracing where the prayer came from."

"And you traced it to me because—?"

"Because you were the nearest charm-seller. Because Maren Ostrow says your charms work. And because," I added, before I could stop myself, "you didn't look surprised when I mentioned him."

For the first time, something moved behind those eyes. Not fear, exactly. More like a door being quietly, firmly closed.

"I look at everyone the same way," she said. "It's called customer service. Now — was there a purchase you wanted to make, Inquisitor, or are you just here to stand in my light?"

I bought a luck-knot. Three coppers. It was, I would reflect much later, the single most significant purchase of my life, and I made it while telling myself it was procedure.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about doubt: it doesn't arrive like a revelation. It accumulates like dust. One day you notice the shelves are grey and you can't remember when they got that way.

My doubts had started small. An old woman burned for a love-charm that turned out to be dried rosemary. A boy of nine questioned about his grandmother's whispered prayers, crying so hard he wet himself while two grown men in Concord grey took notes. Each one had been, individually, defensible. Procedure. The law was the law, and the law existed because magic — real magic, the kind that ate cities — had to be kept from the world, and the only way to keep it out was to treat every ember as a wildfire.

But somewhere in eleven years of embers, I had stopped believing in the wildfire. And I had started noticing that the Concord's founders had burned rather a lot of books along with the witches, and erased rather a lot of maps along with the gods, and that an order built on protecting people spends suspiciously much time protecting *itself*.

I mention this now because what I did next was not the act of a loyal inquisitor, and I would like it on record — my record, the one only I keep — that I knew exactly what I was doing.

I went to the archives.

* * *

The Concord archive beneath the Hall of Judgment holds every record the order has ever deemed worth keeping, organized with the loving precision of people who believe history is a weapon you should store properly. Purge ledgers. Seizure manifests. Confession transcripts. I had spent enough nights down there to know its rhythms, and enough nights down there to know which shelves the archivists dusted least.

The Okafor name appeared twice.

The first entry was mundane: a licensing record, thirty years old. *Okafor, Jemis — scribe, third rank, Temple of the Concord, debts assessed and bound.* Silas's father, presumably. Or mother. The handwriting gave nothing away.

The second entry was from the purge itself, twenty-nine years back, and it was written in the cramped, joyless hand of a field inquisitor whose name had been struck through by someone with a steadier pen:

*Household of Okafor, Ilvaren-descended. Subject family suspected of retaining pre-Concord devotions. Relics seized: seven. Head of household executed under standard writ. Survivors indentured to Temple service, term: until debt discharged.*

Ilvaren-descended.

I sat down on the archive stool and read the entry four times. Every apprentice learned the word Ilvaren once and only once, in their first year, in a lecture titled *Correcting Historical Error*: Ilvaren was a country that had never existed. A cartographic mistake propagated by pre-Concord superstition. There was no such place, had never been such a place, and anyone claiming otherwise was either mad or lying.

Except someone had bothered to write *Ilvaren-descended* in an official purge ledger, and someone else had struck through the inquisitor's name afterward, and neither of those things happens with mistakes.

And underneath the entry, in the margin, in that same struck-through hand, almost too faint to read:

*The door was never meant to be opened from our side. God forgive what we've sealed in with them.*

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in my rooms above the chandlery with the luck-knot on my desk — three coppers of braided sanctioned thread, utterly inert as far as I could tell — and I turned the problem over the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath.

Silas Okafor. Daughter of an executed heretic. Indentured by bloodline into twelve years of temple servitude for a debt her family incurred by praying wrong. And now a miracle had bloomed within ten feet of her stall, and the High Inquisitor herself had assigned me to bury it, gently, quickly, before anyone else looked too hard.

Serane hadn't sent me to find the truth. She'd sent me because she thought I'd find nothing — or because she thought I was loyal enough to find something and keep it in the right drawer. Either way, she had handed the one loose thread to the one inquisitor who'd been quietly pulling threads for years.

Or — and this was the thought that kept me up — she knew exactly what she was doing, and I was the thread.

Dawn came grey over the rooftops. I dressed, pinned on my badge, and looked at myself in the mirror: a tired man in Concord grey with a face that had gotten very good at revealing nothing. Eleven years of practice. My father had called it a waste of an expressive face. My father had been a carpenter and believed wood was more honest than people, and he may have had a point.

I went back to the Saltmarket.

Stall forty-one was open again. She saw me coming from across the row — of course she did — and this time she didn't reach for her customer-service voice. She just watched me approach with the wariness of someone who has done the arithmetic on inquisitors and found the sum always comes out against her.

"You again," she said.

"Me again." I set the ledger page down on her cloth, between the tokens and the knots. Her copy, I mean — I'd had a transcription made, because handing her the original would have been theft of Concord property, and I was not yet a thief. I was merely, I told myself, an inquisitor conducting follow-up inquiries.

She read it. I watched her read it. I watched the moment she reached the word *executed*, and the moment she reached *indentured*, and the moment her hand — steady through everything else — trembled slightly at the struck-out signature at the bottom.

"My father's debt," she said quietly. "Twelve years I've served it. They tell me it's nearly paid. Another year, maybe two, and my name belongs to me again." She looked up. "Why are you showing me this?"

Because the margins of that page contained a sentence about a door, and a country that officially did not exist, and a god with no name who healed a dying man in the Saltmarket two nights ago. Because the Concord had lied to me for eleven years and I needed to know how deep the lie went. Because she was the only person in this city who might be able to answer questions I hadn't figured out how to ask.

"Because," I said, "someone in the Concord wrote that margin note, and then someone else tried to erase it, and I've learned to pay attention to what this order tries to erase. And because yesterday I bought a luck-knot from you, and I'd like to know whether it works."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth. It did not reach her eyes, which had gone somewhere far away and cold.

"It doesn't," she said. "None of them do. They're wood and thread and stamps. I make them honest, Inquisitor, because honest is safe." She folded the ledger transcription with precise care and held it out to me. "Whatever it is you think I am, I'm not. Take your paper and go home."

I didn't take the paper. We stood there a moment longer than was comfortable, two people on opposite sides of a stall, each holding something the other wanted.

Then, behind her, at the edge of the market, a bell began to ring — not the temple bell, not the watch bell, but the thin, urgent clatter of the Concord alarm that I had heard perhaps five times in eleven years, and every one of those times had ended with someone in chains.

Silas went white.

"They're not coming for a luck-knot," I said. "They're coming for you. Back way — now. Move."
