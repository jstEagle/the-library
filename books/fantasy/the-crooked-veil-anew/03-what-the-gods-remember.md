## 3. What the Gods Remember

The dead god came to me on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Tuesdays were for mending, for counting coin, for the small honest drudgery of staying alive. Gods, if they had any manners at all, arrived on feast days or in thunderstorms. Mine arrived while I was darning a sock with a hole the size of a copper piece, and it arrived through my own mouth.

I'd been dreaming, badly, the way you dream when your room is too cold and your stomach is a fist. In the dream I was standing in my grandmother's kitchen — a kitchen that had been confiscated before I was born, along with her name, her books, and eventually her life — and she was stirring something that smelled like woodsmoke and rain. She turned to me and said, *You have your mother's hands,* which was nonsense, because I had never met my mother's hands, only the debt they left behind.

Then she said, in a voice that was not hers, not any voice that fits inside a human throat: ***Wake up, little scribe. The ink is drying without you.***

I woke with ash on my tongue.

Not metaphor. Actual ash, fine and grey, dusting my lips like a lover who'd smoked all night. I sat up in my narrow bed in my narrow room on the top floor of a tenement the Concord called "transitional housing" because "warehouse for people we own" lacked poetry, and I spat into my palm, and the grey gritted there, real as anything.

The lamp by my window was lit. I hadn't lit it. Its flame burned the wrong color — a deep hearth-orange shot through with veins of gold, the way coals look just before they collapse into something you can't undo.

"Hello," said the flame, in the voice from my dream. Or perhaps the voice said hello through the flame; the distinction seemed suddenly unimportant. "You heard me at the market. You are the only one who has heard me in thirty years."

I did what any sensible person does when confronted by an impossible fire speaking in their bedroom. I checked the door lock. Then the window latch. Then, because I was raised Concord-adjacent and paranoia is our true state religion, I pressed my ear to the floorboards and listened for inquisitors.

"Your locks are very thorough," the god said, sounding almost fond. "They will not help."

"Who are you?" My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"I have been called many things. Ashen-Mother serves. Hearthkeeper served better. Once, in the country you have never seen, I had a name so long it took a whole congregation to say it." A pause, and the flame leaned, somehow, the way a person leans across a table. "You sold a blessing three nights ago. To a beggar named Corvo. You told him it was nothing — a trinket, a charm against cold feet. And then you whispered over it anyway. Why?"

"Because he was dying," I said. "And it costs nothing to lie kindly."

"It cost everything," said Ashen-Mother. "Kindness is the oldest magic there is, little scribe. The Concord never managed to outlaw it. They only taught everyone to be ashamed of it." The flame flared, gold threading brighter. "When you whispered over him, you tore a seam in the veil they sewed across the world. And I slipped my finger through, and I found *you*, and I have been pulling ever since."

"I'm not anything," I said. "I'm a debtor. I sell charms. I keep my head down. That's the whole of my ambition, and it's a modest one, and I'd like to get back to it."

"You are a *veilkeeper*," she said, and the word went through me like a nail finding old wood — that sense of fitting somewhere it had already been fitted before. "One of perhaps six living souls born with ears on the far side of the silence. The gods did not die when the Concord burned us out. We were locked away. Behind a door. In a country your maps pretend never existed."

And then she stopped talking to me and started *showing* me, which was considerably worse.

* * *

Visions, I learned, are not like dreams. Dreams let you float at the edges, half-spectator. Visions put you bodily in the middle and hold you there.

I stood on a shore that did not exist. Grey water, grey sky, a coastline of black stone worn smooth as an old coin. Behind me rose hills — green going gold, terraced and dotted with white houses, smoke rising from chimneys in fat contented columns. A country. A whole living country, humming with the sound of bells and goats and someone laughing very far away.

Except the hills were *wrong*. Where they met the sky, they simply stopped, cut off clean as paper, and beyond the cut there was nothing. Not fog. Not distance. Nothing, the way the inside of a closed eye is nothing.

"The veil," Ashen-Mother said, beside me now — and I could see her, or a shape of her, tall and broad and made of the same hearth-light as my lamp, wearing robes the color of banked coals. Her face was kind the way winter is kind: honestly, without apology. "We pulled it closed ourselves, in the last hour, when the burning came. Better to take a country out of the world than watch it fed to pyres. But a door must remain, little scribe, or the locking is a tomb. One door, in the deepest harbor of Ilvaren, and it opens once a century — unless a veilkeeper stands on either side and speaks it open."

"And the Concord knows this."

"The Concord built the lock," she said. "They know exactly where the door is. What they do not know is that the key was never metal." Her coal-bright eyes fixed on me. "The key is hearing. And you, Silas Okafor, are the first ears the world has grown in thirty years."

The vision folded like wet paper. I came back to my body kneeling on cold floorboards, nose bleeding freely onto my one good shirt, the lamp guttered to nothing, and dawn coming up thin and accusing through the frost-feathered glass.

I sat there a long time, holding my bleeding nose and thinking, with great clarity: *No.*

Here is the arithmetic of my life. Twelve years ago, the temple of Saint Verene bought my family's debt — a debt incurred by my grandmother's heresies and my mother's flight — and I had served seven of my contracted fifteen years as a scribe, copying psalms I wasn't allowed to believe, selling charms on the side that weren't allowed to work. Every year of service was a year off my name's leash. Four years remained. Four years, and then I could walk out of Vessengard under my own sky, owing no one, belonging to no one, safe as a stone.

Against that: a god in my lamp, a word like a nail in my chest, and a door at the bottom of a drowned country.

*No,* I thought again. *Absolutely not. Find another orphan.*

But even then — even huddled on my floor with blood drying on my lip — some traitorous part of me had already seen those impossible hills. Had already heard the bells. And knew, the way you know a scar is yours, that I would go. Not today. Today I would be sensible. But someday soon, when the terror wore down to something I could carry.

The Concord, unfortunately, did not grant me the luxury of *someday*.

* * *

They came at the ninth bell, which told me everything. Inquisitors raid at dawn, when people are soft. Ninth bell meant they'd waited, watched, coordinated — meant someone had reported the market miracle, traced the blessing, done the careful work I'd spent twelve years making sure no one would ever need to do on me.

I heard them on the stairs because the building heard them first — every tenement rat in Vessengard goes silent when jackboots start climbing, and the silence climbs ahead of them like a herald. Three floors of quiet, arriving at my door.

There is a back stair in my building. There is always a back stair; poverty builds redundancies. It creaks like a confession but it empties into the alley behind the tannery, and tannery alleys smell so profoundly of rendered horror that even inquisitors hurry through them.

But my hands wouldn't move. Because on my table, where I'd left it after the market, lay the relic — the thing I'd taken, though *stolen* is such a legal word for rescuing something from a burning stall. A hand-sized disc of black stone, older than the Concord, older maybe than stone, carved with a spiral that seemed to turn when you weren't looking directly at it. Ashen-Mother's sigil. The last surviving icon of a god with no temple, and I had stuffed it in my coat pocket like a stolen apple because something in my chest had refused to leave it in the flames.

If I ran without it, they'd find it. They'd melt it, catalog it, and the god who had finally, *finally* found an ear in the world would lose her only thread back.

*This is how it happens,* I remember thinking, shrugging my coat on with shaking hands. *This is how sensible people die — doing one last sensible-adjacent stupid thing.*

I took the relic. Of course I took the relic.

The knocking began — three measured blows, the knock of men authorized to be patient — and I went out the back stair, past Mrs. Havel's door (she looked at me through the gap, saw my face, and closed it again without a word; the poor guard their own), down into the tannery stink, and out into streets that had gone strange and bright with fear.

Behind me, my door splintered. Ahead of me, Vessengard sprawled under its lawful grey sky, four years of debt still pinned to my name, and in my pocket the black disc pulsed once against my ribs — warm, patient, and turning, turning, turning.

*Run east,* breathed a voice that was ash and hearth-smoke and my grandmother's kitchen. *East, little scribe. Toward the water. Toward the country that waits.*

I ran.
